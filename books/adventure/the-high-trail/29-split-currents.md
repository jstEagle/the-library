## 29. Split Currents

The ford at Miller's Bend was a lie the river told twice a year.

I'd crossed it in dry season when it barely wet your bootlaces, and I'd watched it swallow a flatboat whole one spring. Now, with the monsoon's first tantrum still draining off the high country, it sat somewhere between those two moods — brown water moving fast over a gravel bar that might or might not still be there underneath. You couldn't tell by looking. That was the trouble with fords. They were honest only about being dishonest.

"We could go around," Bram said, which was his way of saying we couldn't.

"Going around costs us half a day." I shaded my eyes and studied the far bank, where the trail to Council Rock climbed away through drowned cane. "And Corvus's men are already on the water. Every hour we give them is an hour they use."

Wren stood beside me, arms crossed against the drizzle, doing her best impression of someone who wasn't afraid. She'd gotten better at it since the stowaway days — back then she couldn't have lied with her face if you'd paid her. Now she almost managed it.

Almost.

"So we cross," she said.

"So we cross."

Odile came up from the treeline where she'd been scouting, favoring her left side the way she still did when she forgot anyone was watching. The wound had closed weeks ago; the habit of guarding it hadn't. She crouched at the water's edge, dipped two fingers in, and tasted them like she was checking a soup.

"Fast but not mean," she reported. "Gravel underfoot until midstream, then it drops. Maybe waist-deep on you, chest-deep on Wren."

"On me," Wren said.

I looked at her. She looked back with that chin-up stubbornness she'd inherited from nobody aboard, or maybe from all of us at once, the way kids collect traits from whatever family raises them.

"You're the fastest of us," Odile said mildly. "And the lightest. If the bar gives way, you're the one most likely to make the far bank anyway."

"That's not the argument I want to hear," Wren said.

"It's the true one," I said. "Which is worse, I know."

Bram grunted. He'd been quiet all morning, quieter than usual, and I knew why. We all did. Tomas was somewhere behind us, in the hands of men who worked for Corvus, and every minute we spent arguing about river crossings was a minute the old man spent learning what Corvus's patience felt like. Bram had carried Tomas across a flooded weir on his back three nights ago. He didn't say any of this. He just started unbuckling the pack he wouldn't need on the water and handing things to Odile.

That was the plan, such as it was. It had come to me sometime around the third watch, staring at the ceiling of a ruined barn while rain played percussion on the tin roof, and I'd hated it the moment it arrived fully formed, because good plans are like that. They show up ugly and correct.

We were splitting up.

* * *

I laid it out for them in the barn's lee, with the horses — well, the mules we'd traded for at the last farmstead — stamping and steaming nearby.

"Wren and Odile take the high trail straight to Council Rock. You've got the will's copy, the charter papers, everything that matters on paper. The council convenes day after tomorrow at noon. If we're not there, it doesn't matter — what matters is that testimony gets given, and Wren's the one who can give it."

"And you?" Wren asked.

"Bram and I go back downriver to the weir camp. Tomas knows things no paper can prove. He saw the falsifications happen. He's the difference between the council *hearing* us and the council *believing* us."

"Or the difference between you getting captured too," Wren said.

"There's that."

She turned on me then, and I saw the storm building behind her eyes, the same look she'd worn the day I found her curled among the crates, except now it wasn't fear of me. It was something worse. It was fear *for* me, and she didn't have anywhere to put it.

"You're splitting us up," she said. "First time since—" She stopped. She didn't have to finish. Since the crates. Since the day a sharp-tongued girl with a dead grandfather's name crawled out of my cargo hold and made herself into something I hadn't asked for and couldn't anymore imagine living without.

"I know."

"You don't get to just *know*, Hazel. Give me a reason."

"The reason is arithmetic," I said, and hated how cold it sounded, so I made myself keep going before the cold set in permanently. "Four of us walking into a camp of armed men is four people caught. Two of us moving quiet, at night, with a riverman's tricks — that's a chance. Meanwhile the council won't wait, and Corvus has had twenty years to buy everyone on it. What tips a bought council isn't sentiment. It's a girl standing up in front of them with papers in her hand and nothing left to lose. You heard what Tomas said. The will has to be read *publicly*. That's you. Not me. I'm a boat captain with a ruined name and a talent for reading sandbars. You're the heir."

"I don't want to be the heir. I want—"

"I know what you want."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "Because from here it looks like you're sending me away so you don't have to watch if it goes wrong."

The rain ticked on the tin. Bram studied his boots. Odile, mercifully, said nothing, which from Odile was a full speech.

I took Wren by the shoulders. She let me, stiff as a mast.

"When I found you in that hold," I said, "you told me not to turn back. You said you'd rather drown going forward than rot going home. I've never once regretted believing you. So believe me now: this isn't me sending you away. This is me trusting you with the part of the fight that actually wins it. I'm giving you the hard job, kid. The easy one's mine."

"That's a lie," she said. "Yours is the dangerous one."

"Yes," I agreed. "But it's the simple one. There's a difference."

Something moved in her face — not surrender, never that, but recognition. She was sixteen going on forty, and she understood simple versus dangerous better than most people twice her age. She'd been living the difference since her grandfather died.

"If you're not at Council Rock by noon the day after tomorrow," she said, "I'm telling the council you died heroically and I'm having you canonized. Saint Hazel of the Sandbars. Patron of terrible plans."

"Patron of *difficult* plans."

"Terrible." But her mouth twitched, and that was the victory, small as it was. She pulled away from my hands, swiped at her face with her sleeve in a motion she clearly hoped looked like brushing off rain, and went to help Odile sort the packs.

Bram appeared at my elbow. He didn't look at me. He watched the mules.

"She'll be all right," he said.

"I know."

"You don't."

"No," I admitted. "But saying otherwise out loud doesn't help anything."

He nodded slowly, the way he did, as if agreeing with the shape of a thing rather than its content. "Odile's fever broke, but she shouldn't be marching overland alone."

"She's not alone. She'll have Wren."

"That's what worries me. The girl's got your recklessness and none of your experience."

"Then Odile will teach her some. That's how it works, Bram. That's how it worked on me."

He glanced at me sidelong. "Nobody taught you. That's the problem with you."

* * *

We crossed at first light, because rivers are kindest early, before the day's melt comes down off the hills.

Wren went first, as decided, pole in hand, testing each step. The water took her to the ribs at midstream and I watched her lean into the current with her jaw set, small and fierce and absolutely not going to be moved, and something in my chest did a thing I refused to name. Odile followed a boat-length behind, angled upstream against the push, one hand hovering near the girl's shoulder without ever quite touching — giving her the dignity of crossing alone while making sure she would.

They reached the far bank. Wren turned and raised the pole over her head like a trophy, and even at fifty yards I could see her grinning.

"Show-off!" Bram bellowed.

"Jealous!" she hollered back, and then they were turning, and Odile lifted one hand — not a wave exactly, more a salute between professionals — and the cane swallowed them, and that was it.

First time since the crates that I couldn't turn my head and know where she was.

I stood in the shallows longer than I needed to. Bram waited without comment, because Bram understood that some silences are load-bearing.

"Captain," he said finally. "River's rising."

"Yeah." I waded back. "Let's go steal an old man back from a syndicate."

* * *

Going back downstream toward the weir was like swimming against your own wake. Every landmark we passed we'd passed before, fleeing; now we crept past them in the other direction, and the country looked different wearing its new coat of floodwater. Fields gone to shallow lakes. A chapel with water lapping its second step. A dead cow turning gently in an eddy, which Bram regarded with professional sympathy.

We moved by night and hid by day, which meant a lot of lying in ditches listening to frogs and pretending neither of us was thinking about the girls on the high trail. We failed at pretending. Around noon on the second day, sheltering in a culvert while a patrol skiff growled past on the flooded road below us, Bram said, out of nowhere:

"Council Rock's got a bell. Old one. They ring it when a session opens."

"I know."

"You'll hear it from miles off, if the wind's right."

"I know, Bram."

"Just saying." He picked mud off his sleeve. "If the wind's right."

What he meant was: *if we're not there, you'll know it's started without us.* What he meant was: *what if we fail.* Neither of us said either thing. We lay in the culvert and listened to the patrol pass, and I counted the seconds until the engine faded, and thought about a girl climbing a hill toward a hall full of bought men, and whether the papers in her satchel would weigh enough.

* * *

We reached the weir after dark on the second night, and it was worse than I'd hoped, because hope is a bad surveyor.

Corvus's men had fortified it properly. The old lock-keeper's station sat on the near bank with lantern light leaking through shuttered windows, and a wire fence now ran along the towpath where no fence had been in my father's day. Two skiffs moored below the weir itself, riding the backwash. And there, visible in the lantern glow through a window whose shutters hadn't quite closed — a figure sitting very still in a chair. White-haired. Hands folded. Sitting the way men sit when they've decided that dignity is the last property they own and they intend to defend it.

Tomas.

"He's alive," Bram breathed.

"He's alive." I let that land for a moment. "Now let's work out how to keep him that way."

Here's the thing about armed camps: they're built by people who expect enemies to come at them the way enemies are supposed to. Front door, guns drawn, shouting. Everything about the layout said *we dare you*. The fence faced the towpath. The skiffs guarded the water approach. The lanterns lit every sensible line of attack.

So we wouldn't be sensible.

Upstream of the weir, the old millrace still channeled overflow water — the monsoon had the river running so high that the sluice gates were open wide, spilling a white ribbon of current down the side channel toward the millpond below the camp. Twenty years ago, that channel had fed a timber flume. My father had run timber on this stretch. I'd grown up on stories of it. The flume was long rotted away, but its bed remained: a steep, stone-lined trough running from the high ground above the station down to within thirty yards of the lock-keeper's back wall.

Water ran in it fast enough to kill you.

But it also ran loud enough to hide you.

"We ride the race," I told Bram, crouched in the ferns above the sluice. "Not in it — alongside it, in the flume bed, feet in the shallows at the edges. The noise covers us all the way down. We come out behind the woodpile at the station's rear. There's a cellar hatch. My father used to drink with the lock-keeper; I'd bet the hatch is still as rotten as everything else here."

"And if it's not?"

"Then we improvise, and you remind me later that improvising was my idea."

Bram looked at the roaring race for a long moment. "My father always said rivers are honest."

"Your father never met a millrace."

We went down in the dark and the roar took us, cold water to our knees, stone walls close on either side, the world reduced to white noise and wet rock and Bram's hand on my shoulder keeping us together. It was terrifying and it was perfect. For two hundred yards I was seventeen again, running the flume beds on dares, alive in the way you only are when death is loud and immediate and personal.

The flume spat us out into the millpond's edge, and we stood gasping in the shadow of the woodpile, soaked to the bone, thirty yards from a cellar hatch that was exactly as rotten as I'd promised.

Bram peeled it open like wet cardboard.

* * *

The cellar smelled of lamp oil and potatoes and twenty years of neglect. A ladder led up into the station's kitchen, and above us, voices — two men playing cards, judging by the lazy rhythm of it, the scrape of chairs, the occasional curse at luck. Guarding their prisoner with the enthusiasm of men who'd been told the prisoner was old and half-blind and going nowhere.

Tomas was in the room above, then, not the parlor window I'd seen. Fine. Better, even — kitchens had back doors.

I put my mouth near the ladder and breathed the words upward, barely louder than the race outside: "*Señor Reyes.*"

The card game continued. I tried again. "*Tomas Reyes.*"

A chair scraped. One of the guards laughed at something. Then — faintly, from deeper in the house, a voice like dry paper: "Who says that name?"

"A friend of an enemy," I whispered. "Elias Thornbury's daughter."

Silence. Long enough that I thought I'd imagined the answer. Then footsteps, slow, shuffling, a man navigating a dark he only half trusted his eyes in. The floorboard above us creaked.

"Elias Thornbury," the old voice said, "is twenty years dead to me."

"He's dead to everyone. That's rather the point. His daughter would like to un-dead him. There's a council at Council Rock tomorrow noon, señor, and a will that needs a witness, and a granddaughter who's carrying it up that hill right now with nothing but paper and nerve. Corvus means to bury both. You're the shovel he can't replace."

Another silence. Then, closer, directly overhead, a dry laugh with no joy in it.

"You talk like him. Did you know that? Elias talked like a man selling something he believed in."

"He'd have denied the flattering part."

Above us, a bolt slid. The kitchen door opened — I heard the guards' voices sharpen, one of them rising from his chair — and then Tomas Reyes, half-blind, bitter, twenty years a ghost, walked out of his own captivity through the front room of his captors' quarters, bold as brass, calling out that he wanted tea, that his bladder was acting up, that someone should fetch the doctor, complaining his way across the house while Bram and I stood in the dark beneath the floorboards with our hearts in our teeth.

It was magnificent. It was insane. It was working — until it wasn't.

A guard's voice, suspicious now: "Sit down, old man. Nobody's fetching anything."

"My daughter fetched plenty, before your master's men burned her with the rest of the village. Fetch *that*, boy."

Chairs went over.

"Now," I hissed, and we went up the ladder.

What happened next took eleven seconds and felt like an hour. Bram came out of the hatch like weather, took the first guard's lantern arm and introduced it to the table edge. The second guard had a knife and more sense than the first; he backed toward the door shouting, and I threw the potato crock at his head, which sounds undignified and was, but crocks are heavy and his skull rang like the council bell I'd been thinking about. He sat down among the shards and stayed there.

Tomas stood in the doorway to the front room, one hand braced on the frame, blinking at us through milk-pale eyes.

"Well," he said. "You do move like him too."

"No time," I said. "Back door?"

"Through the scullery. Down to the pond. But the skiffs—"

"—are watching the front water. We're not going by water. We're going by flume."

His face did something complicated. "That race will kill a man my age."

"Not if you go the way the water goes, and not if you argue with it. Bram — carry him if he falls. Señor Reyes, you've spent twenty years being angry at my father. You can spend ten minutes being angry at me while we run. Move."

* * *

We went out through the scullery into the rain, and the alarm rose behind us — a whistle, then a shout, then the particular chaos of armed men discovering their prisoner gone and their dignity with him. The flume took us up into its roar and its darkness, and I shoved Tomas ahead of me with Bram behind, and the water screamed around us and covered everything — our splashing, our breathing, the shouts dying away behind.

Halfway up the race, lights bloomed at the top of the bank. Lantern beams swept the flume's upper reaches, searching the wrong end of it, because no one sane would come *up* the race from the pond side.

No one sane.

A beam swung down and caught us — one blinding instant, white light, three figures clinging in white water — and a voice shouted, and then another voice cut across it, sharp and familiar, ordering the search party down toward the boathouse, *they'll try for the skiffs, go, go* —

Silas Vane's voice.

For one heartbeat, the beam held on us. I stared into it. I'd have sworn I could feel him staring back, weighing something, the way he'd weighed everything since High Landing.

Then the beam swung away, and Vane's voice sent his men crashing off downhill after skiffs we had no intention of reaching, and I stood in the freezing race with an old man's wrist in my grip and understood that I had just seen Silas Vane choose — for ten seconds, for whatever it cost him — not to see us.

"Move," I said, to Tomas, to Bram, to myself. "Move *now*."

We climbed. Behind us the camp dissolved into shouting and lantern-light chasing the water's edge. Ahead, the high ground rose black and dripping toward the ridge road, and beyond it, miles north, a hill where a bell would ring at noon tomorrow, and a girl with paper and nerve was already climbing toward it.

"Hold on, Wren," I said, to the rain, to the dark, to nobody. "Hold on. We're coming."

The river roared below us, keeping its own counsel, keeping its moods — and for the first time in my life, I prayed it would keep mine too.
