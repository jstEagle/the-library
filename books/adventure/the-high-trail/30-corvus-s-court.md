## 30. Corvus's Court

The rain had stopped an hour before we reached Council Rock, which felt less like mercy and more like the river clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

Odile walked beside me, one hand pressed to her bandaged side, moving with that particular stubbornness of hers — the kind that pretends a fever is a rumor started by other people. Her skin was gray under the brown, and every hundred yards she found something fascinating to look at so she could stop walking without admitting she needed to. I let her have her fictions. I'd learned that much from Hazel.

Council Rock rose out of the flooded country like a knuckle. It wasn't a rock, exactly — it was a hill with ambitions, crowned by a ring of old stone buildings the river had never quite managed to drown, connected by bridges and stairways and the kind of architecture that exists only in places where rich men argue about water rights. Banners hung from the council hall, sodden and dripping. Boats crowded the lower landings, flying the colors of every charter house on the Serrano.

And there, at the head of the main dock, flying a pennant I now knew better than my own heartbeat — the black heron on gold — sat Corvus's launch.

"He's here," Odile said, unnecessarily. She was squinting at it the way you squint at a wound to see how bad it is.

"Of course he's here." I kept my voice light, because Odile was watching me for cracks. "He owns half these docks. Where else would he be? Home, counting his money and crying about his granddaughter?"

"Your humor needs work."

"My humor has carried me through worse than this." It hadn't, actually. My humor had mostly gotten me slapped. But Odile smiled anyway, a small crooked thing, and I counted it as payment.

We climbed the long stairway to the council hall, and that was where I saw him.

Tomas Reyes stood between two of Corvus's men at the edge of the plaza, dry under an awning while the rest of us dripped, looking smaller than any man had a right to look. They hadn't bound him. They didn't need to. One of them had a hand resting on the old man's shoulder with the easy familiarity of someone holding a coat they intended to keep. Tomas's eyes swept the crowd — blind-ish, milky, but still working hard enough — and when they passed over me, they stopped.

I don't know what he saw. A soaked sixteen-year-old girl with river mud to her knees and no captain, no crew worth mentioning, and no witness. Whatever it was, his mouth did something complicated, and then Corvus's men turned him gently by the shoulder and walked him up the steps into the hall, and the moment closed over like water.

"Wren." Odile's voice, low. "Don't."

"I wasn't going to do anything."

"You were doing it with your face."

I fixed my face. Inside, I was doing considerably more than doing it with my face. Hazel and Bram were a day behind us, maybe less if the weir went clean, maybe never if it didn't. And Tomas — my witness, the whole spine of our case, the man whose testimony could crack Corvus's charters like river ice — was being escorted into the hall as Corvus's *guest*.

Guests don't get held by the shoulder. Guests get offered chairs.

* * *

The council hall smelled of wet wool, lamp oil, and old arguments. It was a round chamber, tiered, with a semicircle of seats for the nine councilors and open floor below where petitioners stood to be heard. The charter season disputes were already underway; a pair of dredging companies were shouting about silt rights when we entered, and nobody looked at us twice, which was either good luck or very bad luck depending on how you read crowds.

Corvus sat in the visitors' gallery like a man sitting in his own parlor.

He was not what I'd built him as, in the nights I'd spent hating him on the Kestrel. I'd imagined something reptilian, something with too many teeth. Instead he was broad and silver-templed and handsome in the way of men who've been told they're handsome often enough to arrange their faces around it. He wore river gray, understated, expensive. He was leaning toward a councilor — Councilor Amaya Voss, I'd learn later, senior seat, twenty years on the bench — and saying something that made her laugh.

That was the thing about my grandfather's rival. He didn't loom. He *charmed*. He made cruelty feel like hospitality. I watched him hand Councilor Voss some small joke wrapped around some large lie, and I watched her receive it like a gift, and I understood suddenly why my grandfather's will had been written in secret, sealed, hidden, smuggled downriver inside a runaway girl. Because nothing else would have survived this man's smile.

Then he saw me.

It was only a flicker — a half-second adjustment of his expression, surprise smoothed over so fast most people would have missed it. But I'd spent sixteen years learning to read faces across dinner tables where the wrong reading cost you, and I caught it. Surprise. Then recalculation. Then — and this was the part that frightened me — warmth. He inclined his head to me, slightly, the way you greet family at a funeral.

I inclined mine back, because I am my mother's daughter and we do not flinch first.

"Odile," I murmured, "how many exits does this room have?"

"Four. Two are guarded."

"And the other two?"

"Are also guarded, but by men who look bored."

"Bored men make mistakes."

"So do girls who pick fights in council chambers." She steered me toward the petitioner's floor with the hand that wasn't holding her side. "We stand. We listen. We wait for your captain."

"Hazel said testify without her if we had to."

"Hazel says a lot of things when she's charging a weir." Odile's mouth twitched. "But she also taught you to read water. So read the room."

So I read the room. Nine councilors, four of whom took money from Corvus's charter houses if the ledgers Wren-had-memorized-and-Hazel-had-burned-copies-of were anything close to true. Three who owed him favors. Two wild cards: old Councilor Breck, who was rumored to hate everyone equally, and Councilor Voss, currently laughing at whatever Corvus had fed her. The gallery held maybe two hundred people — charter clerks, dock bosses, a scattering of ordinary river folk come to watch the powerful perform.

And scattered through them, wearing plain clothes badly, at least six of Corvus's men.

The dredging dispute ended. The crier called the next matter, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards, because the next matter was ours.

"The question of the Marlowe charters," the crier intoned, "contested claim, brought forward by counsel for the House of Corvus."

*Marlowe*. My grandfather's name, spoken aloud in this hall for the first time in years, and spoken by *them*, folded into their agenda like a leaf pressed into a book. Of course. Of course he'd moved it up the docket. Every day the will stayed unread was a day his charters stood unchallenged — unless he could get the challenge heard and dismissed *now*, quickly, quietly, while the real claimant was still a rumor on the water.

Counsel for the House of Corvus turned out to be a sleek man named Ferris, who spoke for eleven minutes about irregular documentation, unverifiable claims, and the distress caused to legitimate charter holders by "phantom inheritances." He used the word *stability* nine times. I counted.

"There is no verified instrument," Ferris concluded, spreading his hands, "no witness of standing, no claimant properly presented. We ask the council to dismiss this matter and affirm the existing charters, in the interest of the river's peace."

"In the interest of the river's peace," Councilor Breck repeated, in a voice like gravel settling. "That's a new way of saying *in the interest of Mr. Corvus*."

Laughter in the gallery. Corvus laughed too, loudest, and something in the room loosened toward him. That was his whole method, I realized. He made opposition feel rude.

Councilor Voss leaned forward. "Is there anyone present to speak against dismissal?"

And here it was. The silence stretched, and every eye in the gallery began its slow drift, looking for whoever would be fool enough to stand against the House of Corvus. Odile's hand found my arm — not holding me back, I noticed. Just touching. Letting me know she was there.

I stood up.

My legs were shaking. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because from the outside I'm told I looked calm, and the truth is that every joint I owned had gone to water. But I stood, and I said, in a voice that came out steadier than I deserved:

"I'm the claimant."

The hall did that thing halls do — the ripple, the turning, the sudden hunger of two hundred people who'd come for a show and might actually get one. Somewhere in the gallery, one of Corvus's plainclothes men shifted his weight.

"Name yourself," said Councilor Voss.

"Wren Marlowe. Granddaughter of Edric Marlowe." Saying his name out loud hurt in a way I hadn't prepared for. It sounded right in this room, though. Like it belonged to the walls. "And I have a will."

Murmurs. Ferris was already talking over them — "An unverified instrument, presented by an unverified claimant—" — but Councilor Breck cut him off with one raised finger, which I intend to remember fondly for the rest of my life.

"Approach the floor," Voss said.

I walked the length of the chamber feeling like I was crossing a sandbar I couldn't see the edges of. Behind me, Odile stood too, arms crossed, gray-faced and immovable, a one-woman wall of witness. In the gallery, Corvus watched me come with his chin propped on his knuckles, and his eyes were warm, and warm was so much worse than cold.

"Child," he said, before the councilors could speak, and his voice filled the room the way wealth fills a room, effortlessly. "Do you know how long I've prayed for this day? To see Edric's blood in this hall again." He spread his hands to the gallery, the grieving uncle, the keeper of lost things. "She ran away from her guardians in the spring. We've been searching for her for months. Fearing the worst." His voice cracked, just slightly, professionally. "And now she appears, alone, coached, carrying papers no one can authenticate, making claims against the family that raised her."

Every word was a hook dressed as a hug. I watched the gallery absorb it — the poor man, the worried guardian, the sad little runaway. I watched Councilor Voss's expression soften toward him.

And I thought: *Hazel would say something sharp. Bram would say something funny. Odile would say something cutting.*

They weren't here. That was the whole point of this moment. This was the sandbar only I could read.

"Raised me," I said.

The hall quieted. Corvus's smile stayed exactly where it was, but his eyes did the flicker again, the recalculation.

"You say my grandfather's will can't be authenticated," I went on, turning to the councilors, pitching my voice the way Hazel pitched hers against engine noise — low, level, forcing people to lean in. "You're right. Not yet. The witnesses are coming. One of them is being held upstairs right now as your 'guest,' Councilors, and I'd ask you to wonder what kind of guest needs two guards and no chair."

Ferris started to object. Breck waved him silent.

"But you don't need my witnesses to know something's rotten," I said. "You need arithmetic. Ask why a man who claims to love this river fought every survey of the upper Serrano for twenty years. Ask why three independent pilots lost their licenses after refusing to falsify charter papers — ask about a man named Thornbury, whose boat was taken and whose name was dragged through this very hall, if any of you are old enough to remember." Silence, and a few heads in the gallery nodding slowly, old grievances waking up. "Ask why the House of Corvus moved this matter to the top of the docket today, of all days, hoping to bury it before the claimant arrived. You almost made it, sir. I got wet walking fast."

A laugh ran through the gallery — not a big one, but a real one, and laughs are contagious in rooms full of people who've been waiting years for permission.

Corvus rose. Slowly. And I finally saw it, under all the silver and charm — the thing my grandfather must have seen across negotiating tables for thirty years. Something patient and cold, a heron standing in shallow water, perfectly still, waiting for the fish to make its mistake.

"Edric's granddaughter," he said softly. "You have his gift for making enemies."

"I have his gift for counting," I said. "You should try it sometime. Start with the charters."

For one naked moment, the mask slipped entirely, and the whole hall saw what lived underneath. Then he put it back on, bowed to the councilors, and said, magnificently, "The House of Corvus looks forward to examining these... witnesses... whenever they arrive. If they arrive." And he turned and walked out with his men, pausing beside me just long enough to murmur, for my ears alone:

"Your grandfather hid you well, child. Pity he couldn't hide the will as well as he hid you. We'll discuss terms when you're ready to be reasonable. Everyone comes to terms, eventually. Even captains."

Even captains. Even Hazel. He wanted me to hear it. He wanted me lying awake tonight wondering whether the woman who'd thrown his offer off a boarding ladder might, under enough pressure, climb back down it.

He left. The councilors conferred in low voices, and Councilor Voss called the recess, and the hall emptied around us like water finding a drain.

Odile came and stood beside me. For a while neither of us said anything. Down on the river, the sky was building its green-black towers again, and the first far-off thunder rolled up the valley like something enormous clearing its throat.

"You did well," she said at last.

"I testified with no witness, no evidence in hand, and no captain, in front of the man who owns everyone in that room."

"Yes. Well." She shrugged, winced, kept shrugging anyway. "Hazel does it with less complaining."

I laughed — a cracked, exhausted, real laugh — and then the doors banged open at the top of the chamber, and one of Corvus's guards leaned in, scanned the floor, and left again. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that they hadn't followed Corvus out.

They'd stayed for me.

"Odile," I said quietly. "How bored did those exit guards look?"
