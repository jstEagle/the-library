## 31. The Rescue at the Weir

The weir camp sat on a shelf of rock above the flooded river like a tumor that had decided to be architectural — three canvas shelters, a cook fire hissing under an oilskin awning, and a corral of moored skiffs bobbing in the backwash below. Corvus's men had chosen their ground well: one road in, one set of stairs down to the water, and a clear field of fire across the whole shelf.

They'd also chosen it badly, because they'd chosen it next to a river, and rivers are the one thing I have never been stupid about.

"Four guards visible," Bram said, flat on his belly beside me in the wet ferns above the camp. "Probably two more sleeping off watch."

"And Tomas?"

"In the second shelter. They've got him sitting out front sometimes — I watched through the glass for an hour before you got here. Old man, hands tied to a chair leg, drinking tea like he's being paid for it." Bram's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I like him already."

"He hates me."

"You'll get along fine, then."

Below us, the river ran brown and swollen with monsoon runoff, and behind the camp — this was the part I'd been thinking about since we'd spotted the place from the ridge — stood the old navigation lock. A relic from the charter days, built when the council still believed the Serrano could be tamed with concrete. It was derelict, silted half-shut, its gates cranked by hand from a winch house that hadn't seen maintenance since my father was alive. But the upstream gate was closed, and behind it, the lock chamber held a full head of water, backed up all season, pressing against timber that had been rotting quietly for twenty years.

Every structure on a river is either holding the river back or about to stop doing so. The only question worth asking is which, and when.

"There's your weapon," I said.

Bram looked where I was looking. "That's not a weapon. That's a wall."

"That's a promise," I said. "We just have to collect."

* * *

Here's what Corvus's men knew about rivers: how to float armed skiffs on them and shoot people from the decks. Here's what they didn't know: how a lock works, why a weir sings when it's overloaded, or that the winch house's floorboards would take my weight without complaint because they were ironwood, not pine, laid by men who'd expected floods and gotten them.

I'd sent Wren and Odile ahead at dawn — Odile still gray around the mouth but upright, Wren furious about being sent at all — with the map to Council Rock and instructions to walk fast and argue later. Which left me and Bram, crouched in the ferns like a pair of herons waiting for frogs, watching four bored men guard an old man who drank tea with theatrical patience.

"The plan again," Bram said, "in words, so I can object properly."

"I go down to the winch house. You cut the skiff moorings."

"That's not a plan, Hazel. That's a list of things that will each individually get us killed."

"They won't happen individually. That's the point." I traced it in the mud between us. "When I open the lock, everything backed up behind that gate comes down the spill channel at once. It'll look like the river changed its mind. Their skiffs are moored in the backwash — if the moorings are cut first, the surge takes every boat they own straight over the weir and into the gorge. No boats, no pursuit, no radio run downriver for reinforcements. And while every man in that camp is standing with his mouth open watching his livelihood drown, we walk up the stairs, collect Tomas, and walk away."

Bram studied the mud diagram for a long moment. Rain ticked against the leaves above us.

"It's insane," he said finally. "It's exactly the kind of thing your father would've done." He said it without cruelty, which was how I knew he meant it as a compliment. "What do I cut the moorings with?"

"Your knife, and then mine, and then pray there aren't more than two lines per boat."

* * *

Getting down to the winch house was the easy part. The stairs on the river side were slick with moss and abandoned; nobody guarded a ruin. The door wasn't even locked. Inside, the dark smelled of rust and mouse droppings and old machine oil, and the great wooden gears of the gate mechanism loomed up like the ribs of something dead that had refused to finish dying.

The winch was hand-cranked. Of course it was. Twenty years ago, some clerk in Corvus's syndicate had decided manual operation was cheaper than maintenance, and somewhere downstream a man named Thornbury had signed off on the inspection report without arguing, because arguing cost money and money was the thing he never had enough of.

I put both hands on the crank and pushed.

Nothing. Then a groan, deep in the mechanism, like an old man asked to remember something painful. Then movement — an inch, another inch, the crank fighting me the whole way, the gate beginning to lift with a sound like a whale clearing its throat.

Above the noise, faintly, I heard the camp react. A shout. Boots on rock.

Too early. We weren't ready. Bram was still down among the skiffs, and I could hear him now too — a low whistle, casual as Sunday, the signal that said *moorings cut, get out*.

Except the whistle came from the wrong direction, and underneath it came a different sound: a rifle bolt, worked with the unhurried confidence of a man who has done it ten thousand times.

"Hold that thought," a voice called across the water, pleasant as a dockside greeting. "Both of you hold very still."

Silas Vane stepped out onto the weir apron with a rifle slung loose and rain beading on his immaculate collar, and behind him, emerging from the shelters like ants from a kicked hill, came six of Corvus's men with guns of their own.

So much for four guards and two sleepers. So much for the plan.

* * *

They marched us up onto the shelf and sat us on a log near the cook fire, and someone took my knife, and Bram's, and someone else went down and cranked the lock gate shut again, muttering about crazy river women, and for about four minutes it seemed very likely that the story of Hazel Thornbury would end in a shallow grave above a drowned lock, unwitnessed, unremarked, a footnote in somebody else's ledger.

Tomas Reyes sat tied to his chair leg three feet away, drinking his tea.

"You're the daughter," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the daughter."

"He looks like you. Around the eyes. When he was lying to me, especially." He sipped. "You came all this way to get me?"

"Wren needs you. The will needs you. The council convenes in two days."

"Ah." He looked into his cup. "The will. Yes. I've been thinking about the will for twenty years, mostly at night." His eyes came up, milky at the edges but sharp in the center. "And you brought what, five people? Against Corvus's whole river?"

"Against Corvus's whole river, yes."

"Your father would have called that arithmetic."

"My father called a lot of things arithmetic. That's how he ended up ruined."

"No," Tomas said, and there was suddenly nothing mild about him at all. "Your father ended up ruined because he was the only man on this stretch of water who wouldn't sign his name to a lie. I watched him do it. I watched him hand the papers back and say *find another surveyor*, and I watched Corvus smile at him like a friend, and I—" He stopped. The tea trembled slightly in the cup. "I signed. That's the difference between your father and me, girl. He lost everything honest. I kept everything and hated it."

Before I could answer, Vane's voice cut across the camp, smooth as ever, giving orders — search the treeline, check the ridge path, post a man on the stairs. He was good at it. He'd always been good at command; it was the conscience part that had always been optional.

Then he walked over and crouched in front of me, and for a moment it was twenty years ago and we were two young mates on rival boats, competing for the same charter, laughing about it after over bad rum.

"You should have taken my offer," he said quietly.

"You should have made a better one."

"I made the only one I'm allowed to make." His eyes flicked toward the lock, then back. "You opened the upstream gate. Do you know what's behind it?"

"A season of floodwater and twenty years of deferred maintenance."

"Do you know what happens to a rotten gate when a woman with a grudge winds it open and then some idiot shuts it again under full load?" His voice stayed pleasant. His knuckles had gone white on the rifle sling. "It doesn't reseat. It cracks. And when it lets go, everything in that chamber comes down the spill channel at once, and this camp, these skiffs, this shelf of rock — none of it is downstream of that water. None of it is anywhere except in front of it."

I looked at him. He looked at me. Somewhere behind us, deep in the lock chamber, wood groaned — long, low, structural, the sound a ship makes before it makes a decision.

Vane heard it too. Everyone heard it. One of the guards laughed nervously and asked if the old lock always talked to itself, and nobody answered him, and the silence answered instead.

* * *

Here is the thing about Silas Vane that I have spent twenty years refusing to learn: he is a coward, but he is a coward with excellent instincts, and his instincts have always been about survival — his own, mostly, but lately, I think, everyone's.

He stood up. He turned to his men. And he lied to them with the fluency of a lifetime's practice.

"Change of plan," he announced. "Corvus wants the prisoner moved tonight, ahead of the flood crest. We're taking him downriver on the launch — the big skiffs are done, the river's coming up, and the boss doesn't pay for drowned cargo." He jerked his chin at the shelters. "Get him up. Two men on the launch, rest of you break camp and follow the ridge road. Move."

"But the launch—"

"Is fueled and ready, which is more than I can say for your opinions. Move."

They moved. Men are strange animals; give them a confident voice and a deadline and they'll march into almost anything. They hauled Tomas up out of his chair, and he played his part beautifully — protesting, wheezing, demanding his tea — while Vane steered the procession toward the launch ramp with the air of a man conducting an orchestra he despised.

And as they passed me, tied to my log, Silas Vane paused. Just a moment. Just long enough to crouch again, as if checking my knots, and to speak so softly the rain nearly took it.

"Stairway's unguarded in ninety seconds. Lock gate's going to fail within the hour — I shut it under load, that's on me, and I'd rather be on the water than on this rock when it goes." He stood. "You once told me the river keeps its moods, Thornbury. Well. This one's yours."

Then he was walking away, shouting at his men about the tide schedule, and I was working my wrists against knots that — I realized, with a slow, disbelieving warmth spreading through my chest — had been tied by a man who owned a boat and therefore knew exactly how much slack a captive needs to work free in ninety seconds.

"Bram," I whispered.

"I felt it," he whispered back. "He tied mine too. Sloppy. For him."

The groan from the lock chamber came again, longer this time, and under it a new sound: water moving where water shouldn't, finding the crack, insisting.

"Ninety seconds," I said. "Let's not waste them."

* * *

We came off the shelf and down the mossy stairs and along the weir apron in the gray rain, and the launch was just pulling away from the ramp with Tomas aboard, Vane at the wheel, two guards in the bow who spent the entire time looking backward at the lock because the lock was making noises no lock should make. As the launch swung into the current, Tomas twisted around in the stern, found me on the bank, and raised his teacup — he'd kept the teacup, the magnificent stubborn old goat — in a small, dry salute.

Then Vane opened the throttle and the rain closed behind them, and I turned to Bram.

"Now the gate."

"Now the gate," he agreed, and we ran.

The winch house door banged open under my shoulder. I didn't wind this time. There was no need — the gate was already failing, the mechanism shuddering, the whole timber face of it bowed inward like a held breath. I put my hands on the crank anyway, mostly for balance, partly because some superstitious corner of me believed a lock should be opened by a person, not by accident, and I gave it one hard pull.

The gate let go.

Not all at once — first a seam of white water along the bottom edge, then a bulge, then a sound like the sky tearing, and the whole rotten face of it folded outward and the lock chamber emptied itself into the spill channel in one long, thunderous exhalation. The surge hit the backwash pool where the skiffs had been moored and lifted what remained of them — two hulls, splintered, their lines already cut — and carried them over the weir lip and into the gorge, gone, gone, gone. The camp shelf above disappeared behind a wall of spray. From somewhere in the roar I thought I heard shouting, and I chose, generously, to believe it was about the ridge road.

Bram stood beside me in the spray, soaked through, watching the river do our work for us.

"Your father," he shouted over the noise, "would have been insufferable about this."

"He would have been," I shouted back, and found, to my surprise, that I was laughing — really laughing, the kind that hurts, the kind I hadn't managed since before the bank letters started coming. "Twenty years of deferred maintenance, Bram! Corvus's own account books beat his own guns!"

The flood settled into its new channel. The weir sang its high, overloaded note. And downriver, somewhere past the bend, a launch carrying an old witness and a coward with a conscience was running the current toward Council Rock, and every skiff that might have chased it was kindling in the gorge.

I picked up the teacup Tomas had left balanced on the stair rail — deliberately, I was sure of it, a message in porcelain — and turned it over in my hands. On the bottom, scratched into the glaze with something sharp, in a crabbed old surveyor's hand:

*Council Rock. Three days. Bring the girl.*

Bram read it upside down and grinned. "He's enjoying himself."

"He's a Reyes," I said. "They're all insufferable." I tucked the cup inside my coat, where it knocked gently against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and looked upriver, where the rain was thickening and the sky had gone the color of wet slate. "Come on. We've got a ford to cross, a council to reach, and a granddaughter who's about to stand up in front of the most powerful man on this river with nothing but her name and her nerve."

"And us?"

"And us," I said, and started climbing. "Late, wet, and right on time."
