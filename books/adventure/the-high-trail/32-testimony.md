## 32. Testimony

I had rehearsed my speech forty times on the walk over the drowned country, and by the time I reached Council Rock I remembered exactly none of it.

That's the thing about rehearsing. You build this beautiful little structure in your head — opening line here, devastating detail there, a closing that makes old men weep into their beards — and then you walk into an actual hall full of actual people and the whole thing collapses like a sandbar under floodwater.

Council Rock wasn't what I'd imagined either. I'd pictured something grander, maybe — marble, or at least decent wood. What I got was a cavern of black stone carved into the cliff above the river, with benches worn smooth by two hundred years of arguing men and women, and a great natural window at the far end where you could see the water running brown and furious below. The monsoon had arrived early and was making its opinions known against the rock face. Rain came sideways through the entrance in gusts, wetting the front row, who bore it with the resigned expressions of people who had sat in that row before.

The council itself sat on a raised dais of the same black stone: nine chairs, nine people, ranging from a woman sharp as a fishhook to a man so old he appeared to be mostly beard. In the center sat the Speaker — a broad, gray-haired woman named Oduma, whose face gave away nothing, which I would learn was her most dangerous quality.

And there, in the second row of the gallery, dressed in dry clothes that cost more than the Kestrel, sat Corvus.

He looked wonderful. That's what I'll say for him. Silver at his temples, a coat cut close across the shoulders, hands folded like a man waiting patiently for a train. He'd been charming the council for hours, presumably — I could see the evidence in the way some of them angled toward him, the way one younger councilor laughed at something he murmured to the man beside him. Beside him, flanked by two of Corvus's men who were trying very hard to look like ushers, sat Tomas Reyes, half-blind and rigid, wearing the expression of a man attending his own funeral and disapproving of the flowers.

Hazel and Bram weren't there. Odile was — she'd made it up the trail ahead of me, fever-thin but upright, standing at the back with her arms crossed over the bandage at her side like she was holding herself together manually. When she caught my eye she gave me a nod so small most people would have missed it. I didn't miss it. I built a floor out of it and stood on that.

"Wren Alvarado," the clerk called, mispronouncing it slightly, which felt right. Nothing about this day was going to go according to plan, including my own name.

I walked down the aisle. My legs worked, which surprised me. The hall was bigger than it looked from the door, and every footstep echoed off the stone, and somewhere in the gallery someone coughed, and I thought: *Grandfather used to say the river doesn't care how loud you shout.* He said it about sandbars. I decided it applied to councils too.

"State your name properly for the record," said Oduma.

"Wren Alvarado. Granddaughter of Emilio Alvarado, charter-holder of the Serrano, deceased eleven months." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I'm here to contest the succession."

"You're sixteen," said the fishhook woman.

"I'm the heir," I said. "The age seemed relevant to my grandfather when he wrote the will. It didn't seem to bother anyone until now."

A ripple went through the hall. Somewhere behind me, Corvus shifted — just slightly, just enough — and I understood that everything he did from here on would be slight. Small adjustments. A word here, a smile there. He'd spent twenty years moving the river a teaspoon at a time. I resolved to be a flood instead.

"Proceed," said Oduma.

* * *

Here is what I said. I've reconstructed it since, from memory and from Bram's merciless retelling, because at the time it felt less like speaking and more like falling forward very slowly in front of a large audience.

"My grandfather owned four charters on the upper Serrano. Ferry rights, salvage rights, and the winter trade route through the Narrows. Those charters have been in my family for sixty years. Eleven months ago he died, and within six weeks every one of those charters had been transferred to the Meridian Syndicate — to this man." I turned and pointed at Corvus, because Hazel had told me once: *if you're going to accuse a man, look at him while you do it.* "The council has been told this transfer was legal. That my grandfather sold willingly in his final months. That I renounced my claim."

"Which you did," said the fishhook woman, consulting a paper.

"I signed a renunciation," I agreed. "I was fifteen. I signed it in a room with three of his lawyers, two days after burying my grandfather, after being told that if I didn't sign, the estate's debts would become mine and I'd lose the house too. I've since learned the debts were fabricated. The lawyers were paid by the syndicate. And the renunciation isn't worth the ink, because—" and here I took out the copy of the will, the one Tomas had kept hidden in the mission wall for twenty years thinking it was a different document entirely, the irony of which I planned to enjoy later when I had leisure— "because my grandfather executed a new will eight years ago, witnessed and sealed, which supersedes everything."

I held it up. Paper looks so small when it's all you have. It also looks enormous. Both things are true; I've stopped trying to reconcile them.

"The council will want to examine that document," said Oduma, and held out her hand, and for one horrible moment I thought she was going to take it and lose it, or lose it accidentally-on-purpose, and I nearly didn't let go. But she only read it, page by page, while the rain hammered the entrance and the whole hall held its breath, and then she read it again, and then she passed it down the dais.

"The witness seal," she said finally. "Who witnessed?"

"Tomas Reyes," I said. "Charter pilot, thirty-one years on this river. He's in this hall."

Corvus's men moved — not much, just a settling, the way a dog shifts its weight — and Tomas lifted his chin. Whatever they'd told him on the ride here, whatever leash they thought they had, he looked straight at the dais and said, in a voice ruined by decades of engine noise and cigarettes:

"That's my mark. I witnessed it. And I'll say now what I should have said twenty years ago, which is that Emilio Alvarado was the honestest man on this river, and everything that happened to him afterward was theft wearing a suit."

The hall went quiet in a new way. Not the polite quiet of before. The kind of quiet that happens when a room realizes a story it has believed for a long time might be a different story altogether.

* * *

Here is what I said next, and I want to be honest about how hard it was, because it would have been easier to stop. I had the will. I had the witness. A reasonable person would have rested there.

But Hazel had taught me to read water, and the first lesson of reading water is that the surface tells you nothing. The danger is underneath. If I stopped at the will, Corvus would contest the seal, dispute the witnesses, drag the proceedings through appeal after appeal until I was nineteen and tired and rich enough to be bought. He'd done it before. The charters weren't the only thing he'd stolen.

"There's more," I said. "The council keeps records of charter disputes going back how far?"

"Fifty years," said the clerk.

"Then you'll have the file on the *Mariana*, lost in the upper gorge twenty-two years ago, and the pilot who survived it. Nathaniel Thornbury."

I heard it happen behind me before I saw anything: a change in the breathing of the room. Somewhere a bench creaked. And from the back of the hall, Odile went very still, the way water goes still over a deep hole.

"Nathaniel Thornbury was found guilty of falsifying cargo manifests," said the fishhook woman, "and struck from the rolls. The record is clear."

"The record was written by the syndicate's advocates," I said. "I know because I've read the original logbook. Captain Thornbury refused to falsify charter papers for the Meridian Syndicate — papers that would have moved three free charters into Corvus's hands. When he wouldn't do it, they ruined him. They arranged the *Mariana*'s loss, they manufactured the manifest charge, and they put a young advocate named Silas Vane in front of the inquiry to testify that Thornbury had offered to sell him falsified documents. Vane testified under coercion. He has confessed to it. In writing. The confession exists and will reach this council, along with the logbook itself, if I have to carry both here on my back."

"Why?" said the old man with the beard, and his voice was thin but it carried. "Why would a girl burn all this to clear a dead stranger's name?"

"Because he's not a stranger," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because Hazel had also taught me that a helmsman who hides the weather is lying to her crew. "She's the woman whose boat carried me up this river when every other hand on it belonged to him. She's the captain who took a beating from his hired skiffs rather than hand me over. She lost her father to this man's lies and her boat to this man's flood, and she never once — not once, not even when it would have saved her — considered giving me up. So no, I'm not burning all this for a stranger. I'm paying a debt. My grandfather believed debts on this river were sacred. I understand he learned that from the best pilot he ever knew."

Silence. Real silence, except the rain.

I looked at Corvus. He was still smiling, but it had gone from a train-waiting smile to something with more architecture in it, and I watched him do the arithmetic — the will, the witness, the logbook, a confession still inbound — and I watched him decide, briefly, visibly, that he could still talk his way out. That was his whole life, I think. Talking his way out. He'd never met a current he couldn't argue with.

He stood.

"If the council will indulge me," he said, warm as brandy, "the girl has clearly suffered a great deal, and grief makes us all credulous. Documents can be forged. Confessions can be extracted. I have served this river for twenty years, and—"

"You have," said Oduma.

It was the way she said it. Flat. Weighing. Like a woman reading a sandbar.

"—and I would ask that the council weigh testimony against reputation," Corvus finished, "before seizing on the theatricals of a child."

"Weigh it against reputation," Oduma repeated. She looked down the dais at her eight colleagues, and then out at the hall, and then — and I will remember this until I die — she looked directly at me. "Child. You've given us a will, a living witness, and a promise of more. Is there anything else?"

There was. There was one more thing, and I hadn't planned it, because you can't plan the truest sentence you'll ever say. It just comes up the way floodwater comes up through a deck seam, from somewhere below, and all you can do is stand there while it rises.

"Yes," I said. "My grandfather wrote a letter with the will. It's short. Can I read it?"

Oduma nodded.

I unfolded it. His handwriting, cramped and slanting, the handwriting of a man who wrote fast because the river waited for no one.

*"To whoever reads this: I leave the charters to my granddaughter, Wren, because she is the only one of my blood who loves the river more than she fears it. To the council I leave a request. There are men on this river who were broken for telling the truth — Thornbury, Reyes, others whose names I daren't write here. If my will is ever read aloud in your hall, read their names too. Restore what can be restored. The river forgives everything except a lie, and this council should hold itself to the same standard. — E.A."*

I finished. Nobody moved.

Then Oduma said, quietly, "Read them."

So I stood in Council Rock with the rain coming in sideways and read the names. Some I knew from Tomas's stories. Some meant nothing to me and everything to somebody in that hall — I watched an old woman in the third row press her hand flat against her mouth when I read her husband's name, a ferryman struck off the rolls in the same season as Nathaniel Thornbury, dead ten years with the disgrace still nailed to him.

"And Nathaniel Thornbury," I said last. "Pilot of the *Mariana*. Father of Hazel Thornbury, master of the *Kestrel*. Falsely accused. Falsely convicted. Ruined by the man sitting in your second row."

The hall went silent — the deepest silence yet, a silence with weight and grain to it, the silence of two hundred years of black stone deciding what it thought.

And then, from the back, Odile started clapping.

Just her, at first. Slow, deliberate, fever-bright, each clap costing her something at the wound in her side. Then the old woman in the third row joined, and the bearded man, and then the whole gallery, rising, the sound building off the stone like water rising in a lock, and Corvus stood in the middle of it smiling his architectural smile at a room that had stopped believing a single word he'd ever said.

Through it all, Oduma watched me. When the noise finally broke against the ceiling and fell away, she rapped the dais once with her knuckles.

"This council will recess for one hour," she said. "When we return, we will hear the remainder of the evidence — including, I am told, a confession currently traveling to this hall at some speed." Her eyes flicked toward the entrance, toward the rain, toward the drowned trail beyond it. "If it arrives."

* * *

They gave me a side chamber while the council deliberated — a cold little room with a slit window, where Odile found me sitting on the floor with my back against the wall, shaking now that it was over, the way you shake after running rapids.

"You did it," she said. She slid down the wall next to me, wincing. "You stood up in front of the whole river and you did it."

"I threw up a little in my mouth during the fishhook part."

"Noted for the historical record." She bumped her shoulder against mine, gently, minding the bandage. "Hazel's going to be sorry she missed it. She'll pretend she isn't. She'll say something like 'councils are just sandbars with chairs.' But she'll be sorry."

"When does she get here?"

Odile was quiet a moment. Outside, the rain kept up its argument with the mountain.

"They went back for Tomas," she said. "Weir-side camp, armed men, the whole arithmetic. Hazel and Bram. Which means—" she stopped, and I watched her decide not to soften it, because that was the deal on the Kestrel, nobody softened anything, "—which means they're coming down the flooded gorge road with a prisoner and no boat, and Corvus knows by now that his council performance just died in front of him. He won't wait for the verdict. He'll move."

"Move how?"

"However moves fastest." She looked at the slit window, at the gray light and the rain. "Vane's confession is supposed to meet us here. Hazel's supposed to deliver it. Neither of them is here yet, and the hour is nearly up, and Oduma is not a woman who waits twice."

I got up. My legs had stopped shaking, or I'd stopped noticing.

"Then we stall," I said.

"With what?"

"With whatever a sixteen-year-old heir uses to stall a council." I was already at the door. "I've got a will, a witness, and a hall full of people who just learned they spent twenty years believing a lie. Trust me, Odile. I can make an hour feel like a week."

Behind us, down the corridor, the clerk's bell rang the council back into session — and through the rain-hazed entrance, small and late and soaked to the bone, came a figure limping up the trail carrying a leather satchel over his head, and beside him, supporting him, a second figure I knew by the set of her shoulders before I could see her face at all.
