## 35. What the River Keeps

The morning after the verdict, Council Rock smelled like wet stone and fried dough, which is a combination I'd recommend to no one.

I stood at the edge of the great terrace where the river council met — the same terrace where, twelve hours earlier, I had stood in front of forty-odd charter-holders and syndicate men and told them my grandfather's will named me, not Corvus, and watched the hall go so quiet I could hear the river chewing on its banks below. Now the same terrace was crowded with people who wanted things from me. Charters signed. Debts forgiven. A photograph, someone actually asked, of me standing next to the will case like I was a monument and not a sixteen-year-old girl who'd spent most of the last month smelling like bilge.

They could have it. The photograph, I mean. Not the bilge smell. That was mine to keep.

"You're going to be terrible at this," Odile said, appearing at my elbow with two cups of something that claimed to be coffee. She was moving carefully, her bandaged shoulder held stiff against her side, but her color was good and her eyes were sharp as ever. "Being important. You're going to be terrible."

"I'm already terrible," I said. "I told a councilman his hat was stupid. To his face. In front of witnesses."

"Was it stupid?"

"It was shaped like a boat. An ugly one."

"Then you're going to be fine." She pressed a cup into my hand. "Terrible people with power are just honest people with volume."

Below us, the Serrano was still running high and brown and furious, three days past landfall and showing off about it. Whole trees went by like commuters late for work. Somewhere downriver, Corvus's launch was still being searched for, though everyone knew what everyone knew: the river had taken him at the gorge, and the river does not give receipts. They'd found the launch's roof panel snagged in a willow twenty miles down. That was all. A piece of painted wood, floating where he wasn't.

I drank the coffee. It was terrible too. It was turning out to be that kind of morning.

* * *

Hazel Thornbury was not on the terrace. I'd known she wouldn't be before I finished looking for her.

She was down at the waterline, sitting on a bollard with her elbows on her knees, watching the flood go by. She'd been there since dawn, according to Bram, who reported everything to everybody now because apparently surviving a monsoon and an ambush makes you chatty. She hadn't been to the council hall. Hadn't come to the celebration dinner the town threw, where they roasted a pig and toasted Wren of the Serrano, heir of the charters, and I sat at the head of a long table feeling like a coat someone had put on the wrong hook.

I climbed down the switchback stairs to the waterline, coffee in each hand, because if I was going to be terrible at being important, I might as well start by being useful to exactly one person.

"Here." I handed her a cup. "It's awful."

"Everything up here is awful," Hazel said, taking it. "That's how you know you've arrived somewhere important."

She didn't look at me. She looked at the river. Her face had the particular flatness I'd learned meant the opposite of calm — the look she wore running rapids, reading water, doing arithmetic with her whole body while her face pretended to be furniture.

"They're saying the Kestrel's hull might still be sound," I said.

"The keel's sound. There's a difference." A sip of the awful coffee. She grimaced without surprise. "A keel is what a boat believes about herself. Everything else is just what happened to her."

"That's very poetic for a woman who once described my cooking as 'an act of aggression.'"

"It was an act of aggression. You boiled rice into a new element." But the corner of her mouth moved, barely, and then flattened again, and she went back to staring at the brown water like it owed her money.

I sat down on the next bollard. For a while neither of us said anything, which was its own kind of conversation by then. I'd learned that aboard the Kestrel — that silence between the right people isn't empty, it's just full of things neither of you has to say out loud yet.

The flood carried past us: a fence post, half a door, a dead cormorant with its wings spread like it was still trying. Somewhere upstream, whole lives were rearranged. Downstream, whole lives were about to be. The river kept everything and returned nothing, except occasionally, insultingly, a roof panel.

"I owe you a debt," I said finally.

"No, you don't."

"I literally do. There were witnesses. I said it out loud at the council, in front of the man with the boat-shaped hat—"

"Hat-shaped boat," Hazel corrected. "And you don't. You paid your passage. You read charts better than half my crew by the end. You bailed water for six hours straight singing songs that should be illegal." She turned the cup in her hands. "You don't owe me anything, Wren. That's rather the point of everything we just did."

"It's not the point of *this*," I said, and made myself keep going, because I'd rehearsed this on the stairs and if I stopped I'd lose my nerve. "Here's what I'm offering. When the new charters are drawn — and they'll be drawn this week, the council's already drafting — I want the Kestrel to hold one. First priority run, best rates, the route of your choosing. And I want you as partner. Not employee. Partner. Half-share, seat at the table, your name on the paper next to mine."

The silence that followed had actual weight. A heron flew past, low over the flood, absurdly graceful given the circumstances.

"No," Hazel said.

Not cruelly. Not even firmly, really. Just simply, the way you'd tell someone the tide was coming in.

"*No?*"

"No." She looked at me then, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Wren. Think about what you're offering. You're offering me a permanent claim on the river's trade routes, backed by the council, backed by your name. Do you know what my father would have done with that offer? What any Thornbury would do?"

"Take it?"

"He'd have taken it," she agreed. "And in ten years he'd have been Corvus. Maybe a kinder version. Maybe a version who remembered why he started. But the river doesn't care about your intentions when it hands you a current — you either steer or you get steered, and ownership is a current like anything else." She set the cup down on the stone between us. "I spent my whole life thinking the thing that ruined my family was bad luck and one man's treachery. It wasn't. It was wanting too much of the river. My father wanted a route nobody else could hold. Corvus wanted every route there was. Same hunger, different appetites."

"So you're going to do what, exactly? Sit here?"

"I'm going to ask you for something much smaller and much stranger," Hazel said. "And you're going to think it's pride, and it isn't. Or — it is, but it's the right kind."

* * *

What she asked for was this:

Salvage rights. To the wreck.

"To the *wreck*," I repeated. We'd walked up from the waterline by then, along the flooded lower road, past sandbags and a tavern whose ground floor was performing as an aquarium. "Hazel, the Kestrel is in a cove thirty miles upriver, under ten feet of water, behind a gorge that just ate a syndicate boss. You're asking me to sign papers giving you the legal right to... fish her bones?"

"A keel doesn't rot fast," Hazel said. "Cedar heartwood. My father built her frame; the man was stubborn enough that even his lumber refuses to quit. If we can raise her before the wood takes too much water, before the planking springs for good—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Started again, quieter. "I'm not asking for a charter. I'm not asking for a fleet or a monopoly or a name on a wall. I'm asking for the right to go get my boat."

"She's not—"

"She is," Hazel said. "I know what she is. I know she's kindling with delusions. I know the bank technically owned her until yesterday, and the bank's claim drowned with everything else Corvus touched, and the council will need to sort the wreckage of his holdings for months. Which is exactly why I need it in writing now, from you, before some salvage crew with a crane and no sentiment gets to her first." She glanced sideways at me. "You offered me half the river. I'm asking for one sunken boat and permission to make a fool of myself trying to save her."

"That's not strange," I said. "That's the least strange thing anyone's asked me for since the verdict. A man this morning asked me to bless his fishing net."

"Did you?"

"I touched it. I have no idea what I did. He seemed satisfied."

We walked a while longer. The flood had brought the river up into the streets, and everywhere people were dealing with it in the way river people deal with anything — hauling, cursing, laughing at intervals that didn't quite match the situation. A boy punted a washtub full of chickens through what used to be a bakery. Nobody remarked on it.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Hazel said, "is the night we grounded on the sandbar in the narrows. With Vane's skiffs behind us. Remember?"

"I remember thinking we were going to die, and then not dying, and being confused about both."

"You were scared. Everyone was. Bram was praying to a god he insists he doesn't believe in, Odile was loading the rifle with her left hand because her right was busy holding the boat together, and you—" she shook her head, almost smiling, "—you were at the rail, white as foam, asking me how I knew the bar was there. And I told you: every sandbar has a mood. You learn to read it or you die."

"And?"

"And I've been sitting on that bollard since dawn realizing I never taught you the rest of it. What to do after you read it. How to take a boat over a bar at flood stage, how to feel her tell you she's unhappy before she tells you loudly. All the things my father taught me, that I swore I'd never pass on because passing them on meant admitting they mattered." She stopped walking. Turned to face me fully, and the flatness was gone from her face, replaced by something rawer and harder to look at. "I lost the Kestrel, Wren. Whatever the council rules, whatever the insurance says, that boat as she was is gone. And I grieved my father twice in one month — once for the lie I believed, once for the truth. I don't have another grief in me right now. So I'm not going to borrow yours. I'm not going to stand next to you while you build something enormous and slowly become a person who owns things. I'm going to go pull a dead boat out of a cove with my own hands, and if she floats again, she floats small. One boat. Honest cargo. The route I choose."

"And if I want to ride along sometime?" I asked. "On the honest cargo runs. Just as crew. Just as ballast with opinions."

Something in her face cracked open — not a smile, more like the ice going out of a river in spring, that first groaning shift where the whole surface admits it's been wrong all winter.

"Ballast with opinions," she said. "God help me. You'd fit right in."

* * *

We drew up the papers that afternoon in the council annex, which was a tent, because the annex had flooded, because of course it had. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs wrote out the salvage grant while I dictated terms and Hazel stood at the window pretending not to listen and listening to every word.

I gave her more than she asked for. She couldn't stop me — I was, temporarily and terribly, important. Salvage rights to the hull, yes, but also first claim on any materials raised from the cove, and a council writ of passage through the gorge checkpoints while the water was still high, and — this was the part that made her turn around from the window — a line stating that any vessel rebuilt from the Kestrel's remains would carry free passage on all Wren-of-the-Serrano charter routes, in perpetuity, for the vessel and her crew.

"You didn't have to do that," Hazel said.

"I know," I said. "That's why I did."

The clerk read it back. Hazel signed. I signed. The clerk stamped it with the council seal, which was a brass thing shaped like a keystone, and handed Hazel her copy, and she held it the way she'd held the awful coffee — like something she wasn't sure she deserved and was going to drink anyway.

"There's one problem," Bram said, from the tent flap, where he'd been eavesdropping with the subtlety of a landslide. He came in dripping, grinning, carrying a length of rope over one shoulder like a trophy. "Thirty miles of flooded gorge. No fuel reserve. One wounded deckhand—" he tipped his chin at Odile, who'd appeared beside him and looked ready to argue the word 'wounded' on principle, "—and a boat that's currently a submarine. How exactly are we getting to this cove?"

"We," Hazel said.

"Well, I'm not letting you go alone. You drown without supervision. It's documented."

"I don't drown," Hazel said. "I get dramatically rescued. Different thing entirely."

Odile rolled her eyes toward the tent ceiling. "So we're really doing this. We won. We beat the syndicate, we freed the river, the girl's rich, and tomorrow morning the four of us are going to row thirty miles upriver through debris fields to dig a boat out of the mud."

"Yes," Hazel said.

There was a pause. Then Odile shrugged with her good shoulder and picked up the other end of Bram's rope. "Good. I was worried the answer would be something sensible."

I looked around the tent — at the clerk shaking his head, at Bram coiling rope he clearly intended to use, at Odile testing her bad arm like a gambler checking a marked deck, at Hazel folding the salvage writ into her jacket over her heart, actually over her heart, like she didn't even notice she'd done it.

Nobody had voted. Nobody needed to. Some crews vote below decks; ours had apparently graduated to voting by showing up with rope.

"One condition," I said.

Hazel looked at me warily. "Wren."

"I'm coming. Before you say no — I'm not asking as your partner, since you turned that down, and I'm not asking as cargo, since I'm legally nobody's cargo anymore. I'm asking as crew. I bailed for six hours. I earned a shovel."

The wariness stayed, but underneath it something else surfaced, and I recognized it, because I'd seen it before — the night she pulled me out of a crate and decided, visibly, reluctantly, irreversibly, that I was hers to look after.

"Bring gloves," she said. "And don't sing."

"Bram sings."

"Bram sings *badly*. You sing *aggressively*. There's a difference and the fish know it."

Outside the tent, the flood roared on through the streets of Council Rock, indifferent and enormous, keeping what it keeps. But tomorrow we'd go up into it after the one thing it couldn't digest — a cedar keel with a family's whole stubborn history in her spine — and I caught myself grinning like an idiot at the thought of mud and cold water and hard work, and understood, finally, that this was what winning felt like.

Not the terrace. Not the toast. This. A rope over Bram's shoulder and a wrong boat-shaped hat and a captain asking for her wreck back.

The river keeps its moods. Tomorrow, we'd go teach one of them a lesson.
