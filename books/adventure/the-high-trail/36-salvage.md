## 36. Salvage

I've hauled drowned things out of rivers before. Deadfall. A piano once — don't ask. A man in a Sunday suit who I chose not to look at too closely. The river gives back what it takes when it feels like it, and it never feels like it on your schedule.

But I'd never gone back for something that mattered.

We came down from Council Rock with the verdict still ringing in our ears and the whole valley talking about us like we were a story instead of people. Wren had a charter now, paper and seal and everything. Corvus was in irons awaiting the magistrate's barge. Vane was somewhere being ruined in public, which by all accounts he was doing with a strange kind of grace, like a man finally allowed to put down a weight he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten which shoulder it sat on.

And Hazel Thornbury, who could have asked Wren for anything — a new boat, a fleet, her father's name carved into every dock from here to the sea — had asked for salvage rights to a wreck.

"Your keel," she'd said at the council, "is still down there in that cove."

Not *my* keel. Not *the Kestrel's* keel. *Your*. Like she already knew what she was going to do with it before she said it out loud. That's Hazel all over. She decides things three days before she tells anyone, including herself.

So on the fourth morning after the flood, five of us stood on the bank above the cove where the old Kestrel lay beached and broken, and I looked at what the river had left of her, and I'll be honest with you: my first thought was that we were mad.

* * *

She didn't look like a boat anymore. She looked like a ribcage.

The monsoon had shoved her sideways onto the shingle, half out of the water, and then dropped the water level so fast it was like the river had changed its mind about the whole affair. Her starboard side was stove in along eight feet of planking where the debris had hit. The wheelhouse was crushed flat as a stepped-on hat. Mud had filled her to the gunwales and baked in the sun since, so she wore a skin of cracked brown clay like something excavated rather than something built.

Odile stood beside me with her arm still bandaged, her color back but not all of it, and said nothing for a long time. Odile not saying anything is louder than most people shouting.

"The keel," Hazel said finally. She'd waded out to the hull and was running her hand along the bottom seam, feeling through the mud with her fingertips the way other people read braille. "Feel this."

I waded out. The water came to my knees, cold and quick with snowmelt off the high country. Under the silt, under the clay, my knuckles found the line of the keel timber, and I walked its length with my hand on it, and it ran true. No warp. No crack. One long piece of seasoned ironwood that some dead shipwright had chosen with more care than he probably spent on his own coffin.

"Sound as a sermon," I said.

"There you go." Hazel straightened up, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen since before the bank started sending letters. Not hope exactly. Hope is cheap; I've seen hope on men at card tables. This was more like intent. "Everything else is furniture. Furniture can be replaced."

"That's a lot of furniture," Odile said.

"Yes," Hazel agreed cheerfully, in the tone of a woman who has decided not to hear an objection. "Wren, how much does a charter payment run?"

"First one clears next week." Wren was crouched at the waterline pulling river grass out of a scupper like she was grooming a horse. She'd barely left the wreck since we arrived. I think she felt she owed the boat something. Boats don't keep score, but try telling her that. "Enough for materials. Not enough for a yard."

"We don't need a yard." Hazel looked up the riverbank, at the shelf of flat ground above the cove, the willows, the old ferry road that hadn't seen a ferry in twenty years. "We need timber, tools, and hands. Two of those we can buy. The third—"

"—comes free if you feed them," I finished.

Hazel smiled at me. It was a dangerous smile. I've known her eleven years and I have learned to check my pockets when she smiles like that.

* * *

Here is a thing I learned about rivers during this voyage, and I learned it the hard way, with splinters: everybody on a river owes somebody else on the river.

It took Hazel two days to work the ferry road. She went hut to hut, landing to landing, and she didn't ask for charity — Hazel would sooner swim the Serrano naked than ask for charity — she offered wages, such as they were, and meals, such as they were, and something else besides, though it took me a while to figure out what it was.

Respect. Plain and unadorned. She went to the widow Okonkwo, who runs the fish traps upstream, and she didn't say *help me save my boat.* She said, *your husband helped lay the deck on a packet steamer in '09 and I've always wanted to know how he did the scarf joints, because nobody does them right anymore.* And the widow Okonkwo, who hadn't spoken to a Thornbury in twenty years on principle, talked for four hours and showed up the next morning with her late husband's adzes wrapped in oilcloth.

That was the pattern. The dredger crew from the bend, whose captain Hazel had once talked off a sandbar in fog without charging him — they came with block and tackle. The two brothers who run the sawmill skiff, whose father had been blacklisted by Corvus's syndicate for undercutting charter rates — they came with lumber at cost and wouldn't take even that until Hazel made them. A boy of maybe fourteen showed up the third day with nothing but a hammer and stayed six weeks. Nobody ever learned his name; we called him Rivet, because that was mostly what he did.

By the end of the first week there were thirty people on the riverbank. By the second week, fifty. They came before dawn and worked past dark, and the sound of it carried across the water — hammering, sawing, the ring of the caulking mallet, someone always singing, usually badly — and I want to tell you what that sounded like from the middle of the cove at dusk, with the fire going and the smell of cut oak and hot pitch, because I have tried to describe it to people since and never gotten it right.

It sounded like a heartbeat. A big slow one, beating in wood.

* * *

Now, raising a hull is a science, and I am not a scientist. But I'm strong, and I don't complain much, and between Hazel's reading of the water and Odile's rigging sense and Tomas Reyes — who turned up in week two, half-blind, insufferable, and indispensable — we managed it.

Tomas had been a shipwright before Corvus got to him. Before my time, before any of ours. He'd built boats on this stretch of river for thirty years, and then he'd watched his name get dragged through the council mud alongside Elias Thornbury's, and he'd gone up a cliff and shut the door. Twenty years of silence. He'd come down for the testimony, and then, to everyone's surprise including his own, he hadn't gone back up.

"You're setting that crib wrong," he told me on day nine, standing at the water's edge with his clouded eyes pointed slightly to the left of the hull, which is where he points everything. "You'll crush the garboard strake."

"It's holding fine."

"It's holding fine *now.* Put another foot of water under her and that crib leg goes through her bottom like a knife through pudding. Move it aft. Aft, boy, I said aft, are your ears decorative?"

He called everyone *boy*, including the widow Okonkwo, who was seventy if she was a day, and who laughed every single time. That was the thing about Tomas. He was a bitter old man with a heart like a closed fist, and the flood of hands on that riverbank pried it open one finger at a time, and none of us ever mentioned it, because mentioning it would have sent him back up the cliff.

He rebuilt her lines from memory. That's the part that still gets me. The plans were gone — lost with everything else, or maybe never written down at all, because the old builders kept their lines in their heads the way priests keep prayers. So Tomas stood on the bank with his bad eyes and his good hands and he measured the wreck with a knotted cord and a spirit level, muttering dimensions to Odile, who wrote them in chalk on a board, and by the tenth day he had the whole hull drawn out full-size on the gravel, the way they used to do it, the mold lofts of the old days, and people came from three landings away just to walk around it and touch it.

"She was a good design," he admitted one evening, grudging, to nobody in particular. "Elias Thornbury had an eye. Damn fool in every other particular, but an eye."

Hazel was standing close enough to hear. She didn't say anything. She just looked at the water for a while, and I pretended not to see her wipe her face, because that's what family does.

* * *

The day we floated her — well, floated the bones of her — was the twelfth of the month, and I remember it because it rained, lightly, the first soft rain since the monsoon broke, as if the sky had decided to apologize.

We'd patched the worst of the breaches with green timber and canvas and pitch, enough to make her watertight-ish, which is a word I invented that day and stand by. We dug her free of the shingle by hand, forty people with shovels and levers and a great deal of profanity, and we greased the ways with tallow the widow Okonkwo rendered from fish fat, which smelled like the inside of a cat, and at noon Hazel gave the order and we pulled.

She came off the bank grudging, groaning, mud streaming off her in sheets, and then all at once she was swimming, light and high and wrong-seeming, like a bird with half its feathers, and the whole riverbank cheered so loud the herons went up off the reeds in a white sheet.

Hazel stood at what remained of her wheel — Tomas had salvaged the hub and one spoke — and she didn't cheer. She just held that one spoke and let the current carry the hull a hundred yards down the cove and turn her, gently, broadside to the bank, and I watched her mouth move and couldn't tell if she was praying or apologizing. With Hazel it's often both.

Then she looked up and shouted, "Right! She floats. Now somebody find me a rudder, because I refuse to steer her with a shovel again."

And the bank roared laughing, and the work went on.

* * *

Rebuilding is slower than building. Nobody tells you that. Every plank we salvaged had to be judged — keep, burn, or mourn — and Hazel judged each one herself, which slowed everything down and was also, I understood eventually, the entire point. She wasn't repairing a boat. She was deciding what to carry forward.

The keel, obviously. The stem post, scarred but honest. Deck beams, half of them. The engine — Bram's miracle of the season, that — we winched her out of the flooded bilge after three days of pumping, and she turned over on the fifth pull, coughed rust and river, and caught, and the noise she made was so ugly and so alive that Rivet hugged the cylinder block and had to be peeled off.

What burned went into a pile at the top of the bank, and on the last night of the frame work, Hazel lit it. We stood around the fire of the Kestrel's own dead wood, and it smelled like tar and old voyages, and nobody spoke for a long time, and then Odile said quietly, "To the old girl," and we drank whatever was in the jug, and that was the whole ceremony. Some things don't need more words than that.

The new planking went on pale against the old, oak and teak from the mill brothers, bright as bone, so that for a few weeks the Kestrel wore stripes — her history and her future alternating plank by plank, visible to anybody who cared to read her. People did care to read her. Pilots coming downriver would slow as they passed the cove, and some would raise a hand, and once a whole packet crew lined their rail and took their hats off, which embarrassed Hazel so badly she ordered everyone back to work and then stood watching them out of sight for ten minutes.

Wren painted the name herself. Refused help, refused advice, refused even company, and came down off the bank at dusk with white paint to her elbows and the letters finished: KESTREL, same as before, but bigger, and with a small bird worked into the K's tail that hadn't been there before.

"A kestrel hangs in the wind," she told Hazel, defensive, before anyone could comment. "It doesn't fight the wind. It uses it. Grandfather told me that."

Hazel looked at the name a long moment. "Your grandfather," she said, "would have liked this boat."

"He would have liked *you,*" Wren said, and then went red as fresh pitch and stormed off to sand something, and Hazel let her go, smiling at the water.

* * *

There's one more thing, and then I'll let you get on with your day.

On the last afternoon — the day before launch, with the decks caulked and the wheelhouse framed and the paint drying — I found Hazel alone aboard, sitting on the foredeck with her legs hanging over the pale new planking, looking at the river. I sat down next to her. Neither of us said anything for a while, which is the kind of conversation I'm best at.

"Eleven years ago," she said eventually, "you signed on to a sinking boat with a captain who was one bad season from ruin. Why?"

I thought about lying, because it's traditional. Then I thought better of it.

"Because you read water like nobody I ever saw," I said. "And because you paid late but you always paid. And because when the deckhand's kid got fevered at Miller's Bend you turned the boat around in the night and didn't bill anybody. People notice things like that, Captain. Even the ones who pretend not to."

"Hm." She turned a splinter of old teak over in her fingers. "I signed the boat over to all of you yesterday. Papers are in the wheelhouse drawer. Equal shares. Mine's no bigger than yours."

"I know. Odile told me. You're terrible at keeping secrets from Odile."

"I know." She flicked the splinter into the current and watched it go. "Bram. If I'd lost her — if we'd lost her, back in the gorge, or at the handoff, or any of the hundred times — would you have stayed?"

I considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I'd had weeks of hammering to think about it.

"No," I said. "I'd have followed you upriver on foot, and I'd have complained the entire way, and I'd have done it for any boat you stood on. It was never the Kestrel, Captain. It was the helm."

She didn't answer. But she bumped her shoulder against mine, which from Hazel is a sonnet.

Down the bank, the evening shift was lighting lamps, and the hammering had stopped, and the smell of the widow Okonkwo's stew was drifting across the water, and the new Kestrel rocked gently at her moorings with her striped hull and her white name catching the last of the light.

Tomorrow we'd launch her properly. Tomorrow there'd be speeches, God help us, because Wren had invited the council and the council loved speeches. Tomorrow the river would take her back and decide, as rivers do, whether to forgive.

But tonight she just floated. Sound keel, scarred sides, many hands in her timbers.

Tomorrow, though — tomorrow, Odile came running up the bank waving a telegram, and the look on her face said the river had already sent us our next mood.
