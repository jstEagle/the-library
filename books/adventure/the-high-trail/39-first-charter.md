## 39. First Charter

The manifest for the new Kestrel's maiden run read like a love letter to honesty: two hundred sacks of rice, forty bolts of undyed cotton, a crate of carpenter's tools bound for the mission at Serrano Bend, and one passenger traveling free of charge.

The passenger was Tomas Reyes, and he was currently arguing with Odile about whether he was allowed to carry his own bag aboard.

"I'm old," he said, "not furniture."

"You're cargo," Odile said. "Manifest says so. Free passenger, one each, handle with care."

"Then I'll handle myself with care."

They'd been at it since dawn. I stood at the top of the gangplank watching them circle each other like two herons disputing a fish, and I thought: this is what winning looks like. Nobody tells you that. In the stories my grandfather used to read me, victory was trumpets and banners and a hall going silent when you spoke. In real life it was an old man who'd spent twenty years hiding in a cliffside mission being bullied about his luggage by a woman with a healing bullet wound, on a boat built from the bones of a drowned ship, on a morning that smelled like rain that hadn't decided yet.

I wouldn't have traded it for the trumpets.

"Tell her," Tomas said to me.

"He's cargo," I said. "Handle with care."

Tomas muttered something in the old river dialect that I suspected was not a blessing, but he surrendered the bag to Bram, who took it with the gravity of a man accepting a sword. Bram had been different since the floating market — quieter in some ways, lighter in others, as if trading away his father's compass ring had settled some account he'd been carrying longer than any of us knew. He still wore the raw band of skin where the ring used to sit. He caught me looking at it once and said, "It bought medicine. That's a better use than pointing north." Then he'd gone back to coiling line like nothing had been said at all.

That was Bram. Everything important happened just under the surface, where the current runs.

* * *

Hazel found me at the wheel before we cast off. She didn't say anything at first — she never did, not about the things that mattered — just stood beside me and watched my hands on the spokes the way she'd watched them every day for weeks now, through drills and squalls and one memorable afternoon when I'd put us aground on a sandbar so gently that Odile called it "parking."

"Nervous?" Hazel asked.

"Terrified."

"Good. The ones who aren't terrified are the ones who run you up on rocks." She nodded at the water ahead, brown-green and slow in the morning light, deceptively gentle. "Read it to me."

I looked. This was the ritual, and I knew my lines. "Glassy over the left third — that's the deep channel, but it's lying, because there's a snag shelf under it from the flood. The riffle marks are honest — they're telling me where the bar's moved. And the color change off the point means fresh water pushing out, so the current's running harder than it looks." I paused. "And the birds are wrong."

"The birds?"

"There were herons on that spit yesterday. Today they're further down. Something's changed under the water, or they know something I don't."

Hazel was quiet long enough that I started composing apologies. Then she said, "Your father would've missed the birds," and I understood that this was the highest compliment in her entire language, and that I would be living off it for a month.

She wasn't my father. Nobody was, anymore — that was the whole shape of my life now, the empty space where family used to be, filled in with these particular people: a captain who taught by silence, a cook-navigator who taught by insult, a deckhand who taught by example, and an old man going home who taught, mostly, by asking questions nobody wanted asked. But somewhere between the stowaway discovery and the night we bailed water and sang until our voices broke, the space had stopped feeling empty. It had started feeling occupied. Claimed. Like a berth with your name chalked above it.

"Wheel's yours," Hazel said, and stepped back, and did not take it again all day.

* * *

We cast off at mid-morning with half the riverbank settlement waving. Word had gotten around after the salvage — word always got around on the river faster than boats could carry it — and people came down to watch the new Kestrel take the water. Some of them had helped build her. There was a woman who'd donated planking salvaged from her own flooded boathouse, and a boy of maybe ten who'd hammered more nails than anyone and had demanded, as payment, to be allowed to touch the wheel someday. Hazel had told him: learn to read water first. He'd looked at her like she'd handed him a treasure map.

The boat herself rode well. That surprised no one more than it surprised me. When we'd raised the old hull from the cove, everyone had assumed the keel would be rotten through, that we were performing a funeral, not a rescue. But the keel was sound — her father's keel, laid by Hazel's father's hands, or at least supervised by them — and everything built atop it had been rebuilt by ours. She was scarred. You could see the seams where new timber met old, pale wood against dark, like a body that had healed crooked but healed. Odile had wanted to sand and paint until the scars vanished. Hazel had refused.

"Let them show," she'd said. "A boat without scars has either never worked or is lying about it."

So we kept them. The name went on fresh — *Kestrel*, in white letters tall enough to read across a mile of water — but underneath, if you got close, you could see the ghost of the old paint, the old dents, the whole history of her, which was also the history of us.

Tomas stood at the rail as we pulled away from the bank, watching the settlement shrink behind us. His face did something complicated.

"You're going home," I said, coming to stand beside him.

"I am." He turned the words over like stones. "Twenty years I told myself the mission was home. It was a very convincing lie. Comfortable. Stone walls hold the weather out and the guilt in." He glanced at me. "You ever tell yourself a lie that comfortable, girl, you let somebody knock it down. That's what crew is for."

"I'll remember that."

"Do." He looked forward then, upriver, toward Serrano Bend, toward the valley he'd fled and the life he'd lost. "My brother kept the orchard, last I heard. If he still lives, there will be questions. Twenty years of questions." A pause. "I find I'm looking forward to them. Isn't that a strange thing."

"It's not strange," I said. "It's the bravest thing I've heard all week, and I watched Hazel throw a man off a boarding ladder."

That earned a dry sound from him that might have been a laugh. "Ah, yes. The ladder. I've heard the story four times. It grows in the telling."

"Everything does, on this river. That's how it stays alive."

* * *

The first day ran clean. I held the wheel through the morning reach while Hazel sat on the cargo hatch pretending to check manifests and actually watching me with an expression she thought I couldn't see. Odile ran the galley and produced a lunch that tasted like celebration even though it was just rice and river fish, because Odile had a theory that food was ninety percent context, and today the context was everything.

In the afternoon the wind came around and brought the smell of green water and far-off rain, and I felt the old clench in my chest — monsoon season wasn't done with us, might never be done with us — but the sky stayed only dramatic, not dangerous, piling up silver towers along the western hills like scenery painted by someone showing off. Bram named the clouds for us as they formed. He had names for all of them. "That one's a liar," he said, pointing at a towering white mass. "Looks like weather, means nothing. That low gray one there, that's the honest one. It'll give you exactly what it says." I was starting to understand that Bram had learned to read the sky the way Hazel read water and Odile read people: completely, and without mercy.

Near dusk we made the first lock of the route, and here the maiden charter met its first test, because locks cost money and money was a language I was still learning to speak without flinching.

Before — before the council, before the verdict, before Corvus's charters were voided and his empire cracked open like a dam in flood — every toll on this river had been his toll, collected by his men, at his prices, for his benefit. Now the lock-keeper was a council appointee, a lean woman named Ferro who'd taken the job three weeks ago and still couldn't quite believe it. She waved us through at the charter rate, then leaned over the lock wall as the water lifted us and said, "First boat through on the new papers. You know that? First honest boat through this lock in nineteen years."

Hazel touched her cap brim. "Hope we set a good precedent."

"You already did," Ferro said. "Whole river's talking about Council Rock. Whole river's talking about *you*." Her eyes flicked to me, and I felt the look land like a hand on my shoulder — heavy, curious, a little awed. The girl whose testimony broke the syndicate. I still didn't know how to be that girl. Most days I felt like a fraud wearing my own name.

But the lock gates opened onto open water, and there was no time to feel like anything except a helmsman, because the evening current was tricky and the light was going gold and Hazel had taught me that golden hour lies worst of all — everything looks calm, everything looks easy, and the river uses exactly that hour to rearrange itself.

* * *

Second morning, Tomas came to the wheelhouse while I had the con, and asked if he could sit.

"It's your boat's maiden run," he said. "Seems right the old man should suffer through it with you."

He sat on the bench by the chart table and didn't talk for a long while, which I'd learned was his way of talking. Then: "You knew my grandfather."

The wheel was suddenly very loud in my hands. "I — a little. I knew of him. I grew up on stories about him. Half the river's stories are about Elias Vane, one way or another."

"And what do the stories say?"

"That he owned everything. That he was fair until he wasn't. That he built the trade routes and broke the men who crossed him." I hesitated. "That he died rich and alone, and the will he left tore the river apart before it was even read."

Tomas nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected rather than hoped. "Elias was my friend once. Before the charters, before the falsifying, before he asked me to do a thing I wouldn't do and ruined me for refusing." He rubbed his clouded eyes. "People think power corrupts like rot corrupts wood — slowly, from inside. It doesn't. It corrupts like floodwater. One bad season, one breach, and everything you built is underwater and you're standing on the roof telling yourself you're still king of the house." He looked at me. "You testified against his empire. His blood, standing in front of the council, tearing down everything he made. Did you hesitate?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty. "No. And I've wondered if that makes me a bad granddaughter."

"It makes you a good granddaughter," Tomas said. "The best thing you can inherit from a man like that is the courage to refuse him. He never found anyone brave enough to refuse him until the end. You refused him at sixteen." He smiled, and it transformed his ruined face into something almost young. "Elias would have hated it. And in whatever room in hell or heaven he's sitting in, I promise you, he respects it."

I had to look at the water for a while after that. The riffles off the starboard point needed reading anyway. That was my story and I was sticking to it.

* * *

We made Serrano Bend on the fourth afternoon, and I saw Tomas's home before Tomas did, because I was at the wheel and he was below decks pretending not to pack, which meant repacking the same bag for the sixth time.

The bend came around slow, the way the big bends do, and the valley opened up beyond it like a held breath released: terraced orchards climbing the slopes, gone wild and overgrown in places but unmistakably alive, and a stone mission house with a red tile roof, and a dock — small, private, half-rotten — where a figure was already standing. Even at distance I could see he was old, and broad, and holding a boat hook like he'd been holding it for hours, like he'd known somehow, the way family knows.

"Tomas," Odile called down the hatch. "Somebody's waiting."

He came up the steps two at a time, which for a man his age was somewhere between heroic and suicidal, and stood at the rail, and went absolutely still.

"That's Marta's boy," he said. "That's — that's little Renzo. He can't be little Renzo. Little Renzo is nine years old."

"Little Renzo is fifty," Odile said gently, "same as the rest of us, older than we look."

The Kestrel slid in toward the dock and I brought her alongside soft as a leaf landing, the way Hazel had drilled into me — *the boat should arrive like a rumor, not an announcement* — and the man on the dock caught the heaving line Bram threw, and looked up, and his face did what faces do when twenty years ends in an afternoon.

Tomas didn't move. I don't think he could. So the man on the dock — Renzo, little Renzo, fifty years old with his uncle's shoulders — climbed down onto the gunwale and stepped aboard and embraced him right there on our deck, among the rice sacks and the tool crates, and Tomas made a sound I will keep until I die, a sound like a door unlocking after two decades of rust.

The rest of us found urgent business elsewhere. Even Odile, who misses nothing, developed a sudden fascination with the cargo lashings.

Later, bags ashore, goodbyes said in the river fashion — brief, gruff, everything important compressed into a grip of the forearm — Tomas stood on the dock and looked up at me at the rail.

"Girl," he said. "Wren." He corrected himself, and I felt it land somewhere permanent. "When you get to be old, and you've hidden long enough, and somebody finally drags you out into the light — make sure it's people like these." He gestured at the boat, at all of us. "Make sure it costs them something. That's how you know it's real." Then he turned and walked up the dock toward his nephew, and he didn't look back, because men like Tomas never do, and because looking back is for people who aren't sure.

We were sure. We cast off into the long gold evening, and the valley folded itself away behind us, and I stood at the wheel with the river running honest beneath the hull and thought: legacy isn't what you leave behind. It's what you set walking forward, on legs that aren't yours, toward places you'll never see.

* * *

Hazel came up as the stars came out. She didn't ask about Tomas. She'd heard everything; she always heard everything. Instead she leaned on the rail beside the wheelhouse and said, "Serrano Bend charter paid double rate. Ferro waived the lock fee. Cargo fees from the mission run are already pledged against the bank note." She said it flatly, like reading a list, but I'd learned to hear what lived under her lists. "At this pace, the debt clears by season's end."

"Good," I said. "Because I've been thinking."

"That's dangerous."

"Shut up and listen." I kept my eyes on the water, because this was easier to say to the river than to her face. "Wren charters. That's what the council gave me — the grandfather's routes, the free-trade licenses, all of it. I could run them myself. I could buy a boat, hire a crew, be my own captain by winter." Now I made myself turn. "Instead I want to sign the charter rights over to this boat. To the Kestrel. All of them. Not to you — to the boat, to the crew, the way you signed her over to all of us. Because a charter isn't paper. It's a promise that goods and people move honestly on this river. And this boat keeps promises better than any paper I've ever seen."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Out on the black water, a night heron called once, twice, and got no answer.

"You understand what you're giving up," she said finally. "Independence. Your own command. You're sixteen and you're choosing to share a wheel instead of taking your own."

"I'm choosing the right wheel," I said. "There's a difference. Ask the herons."

Something moved through her face then — not quite a smile, something older and less finished than a smile, the expression of a woman watching a debt get paid that she never expected to see collected. "The crew votes on it," she said. "That's the rule. Everything goes to vote."

"I know. I counted. We have the numbers."

"Do you." And there it was, the actual smile, small and crooked and entirely hers. "Sixteen years old and you counted votes before making the offer. Corvus blood."

"Refused-Corvus blood," I said. "Get it right."

She laughed — actually laughed, short and rough, a sound I could count on one hand having heard — and pushed off the rail and headed for the hatch. At the door she paused.

"Wren. Tomorrow we run the Narrows Reach. Fast water, new boat, your hands on the wheel the whole way through." She said it like a sentence in a contract. "Sleep well."

And she went below, and I stood alone at the wheel under all those patient stars, with the river talking its endless language under the hull, and I realized my heart was pounding like a drum at a festival, and that I couldn't wait for morning, and that this — this exact feeling, fear and joy braided into one line — was probably what Hazel had felt her whole life, every single day, and had never once told anyone.

The river keeps its moods. Tomorrow, for twelve miles of white water, some of them would be mine.
