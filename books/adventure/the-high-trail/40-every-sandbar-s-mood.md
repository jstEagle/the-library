## 40. Every Sandbar's Mood

The river woke me before the sun did.

That's not poetry. That's the Kestrel's hull talking to me through the deck plates — a low, conversational shudder I've known since I was nine years old and my father first let me stand at the wheel with his hands loose around mine like a cage made of calluses. Every boat has a voice. The old Kestrel used to grumble. This one *sings*, which is either the new keel or the fact that for the first time in eleven years, nobody aboard owes anybody anything.

I lay in my bunk a while longer anyway. Old habits. A captain who springs out of bed at dawn on a day with no debts is a captain who hasn't learned what peace costs.

Through the bulkhead I could hear the sounds that have replaced my alarm clock: Bram's boots on the foredeck, heavy as dropped cargo, followed by the clank of him pretending he wasn't already up. Odile's kettle going on the galley stove — she still refuses to use the electric coil I installed last month, calls it "a lie you plug into the wall." And underneath it all, from the wheelhouse, a low murmur: Wren, reading the water aloud to herself the way I taught her. *Sandbar shifting left. Eddy's lazy this morning. Current's got opinions.*

The girl narrates the river like it's a gossip she's fond of. I never told her to do that. She just started, somewhere between the flooded side channel and Council Rock, and now I can't imagine the wheelhouse without the sound.

I got up. Put on the coat — same coat, patched at both elbows now, because some things you keep even when you could replace them, especially when the patches were sewn by someone who hums while she works and pretends she doesn't care if you wear it.

Then I went up to see what mood the river was in.

* * *

Here is what I know about sandbars, and I know it the way you know your own pulse.

A sandbar is not a thing. It's an argument — between current and sediment, between the fast water that wants to carry everything downstream and the slow water that wants to set everything down. Some mornings the argument goes one way, some mornings the other. The bar below Miller's Bend, the one that nearly ate us in chapter one of my life — well, the one that nearly ate us every season of my life — had spent the winter migrating forty yards east. Any pilot who ran on last year's chart would put her bow right into it and spend the afternoon explaining herself to passing barges.

I read it from two hundred yards out. The boil line told me where the fast water sheared off the slow. The color told me the bar was silted shallow — tea-brown over tan, no green depth to it. The birds told me the rest: herons standing where they shouldn't be able to stand, which means sand just under the surface, which means the river had moved its furniture again while we were away.

"Port ten," I said, though there was nobody to say it to but the wheelhouse and the girl already turning the wheel before I finished.

"Already on it," Wren said. "You're slow this morning, Captain."

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference."

"Odile says you're just old."

"Odile says a lot of things for a woman who lost to me at cards twice last week."

"Three times," Wren said, grinning. "She told me to tell you it was three."

The Kestrel slid past the bar with six feet under her keel and room to spare, and I felt that old specific pleasure — the one that used to be the only payment I got some months — of a boat answering her helm exactly as asked. Except now it came with witnesses. Now it came with a crew who noticed, who'd learned enough to notice, and that changed the flavor of it entirely. Skill hoarded is just survival. Skill shared is something else. I hadn't found the word yet. I was working on it.

We were running light. Honest cargo — machine parts for the mill at Corben's Landing, dry goods for the mission upstream, and Tomas Reyes riding passenger in the cabin Odile had fixed up with an actual mattress, because half-blind old men who spent twenty years hiding in cliffside missions have earned mattresses. He was coming home. His home was a burned-out charter office in a town that had forgotten him, and he wanted to see it before the council's survey crews got there, and so we were taking him, free of charge, because some fares you don't charge.

That was the whole manifest. No sealed crates. No nervous brokers. No armed men watching our dock with the patience of vultures who've been paid by the hour.

It felt strange. It felt like wearing a shirt with nothing hidden in the pockets.

* * *

Bram found me at the rail after we cleared the bend.

He does that — appears alongside without announcing himself, a man built like a dock piling moving with the silence of something much smaller. He handed me coffee without asking if I wanted it, which is the highest form of intimacy Bram knows, and leaned on the rail beside me, and for a while we just watched the water go by, which is the second-highest.

"You slept," he said.

"Observant."

"You didn't sleep for eleven years. I kept count." He blew on his coffee. "Bank sent a letter yesterday. Forwarded from the old office."

My stomach did a thing it hasn't done since the monsoon. Eleven years of letters from that bank, and every single one of them had arrived like a hook behind the ribs. Even now, even knowing —

"It's a receipt," Bram said, watching my face and enjoying it, the great lout. "Paid in full. They stamped it and everything. Odile framed it. It's in the galley next to the kettle."

"In the *galley*."

"She says debt should hang where you cook, so you remember what feeds you."

"That woman has a proverb for everything and half of them are invented."

"Half?" Bram smiled into his mug. "She'll want to know which half."

I looked downriver, toward Corben's Landing, toward the bank I'd walked into a hundred times with my hat in my hands and my father's name like a stone in my mouth. Paid in full. Wren's first charter payment — the girl had signed it over without blinking, and when I tried to refuse she'd looked at me with her grandfather's stubborn jaw and said, *You didn't haul me up a river through a monsoon so I could watch you drown in paperwork instead.* So the debt died quietly, in ink, the way it had lived. No drama. Just a stamp.

Eleven years. My father's boat mortgaged to the rivets, and me mortgaged with it, running humiliating freight downriver past people who used to nod to my father and now nodded to the syndicate's colors instead. I used to think the debt was the worst thing the river ever did to my family. Then I learned what Corvus did, and then I learned what my father *didn't* do — didn't falsify, didn't sign, didn't sell — and I understood that the debt had been the kindest of it. A debt you can pay. A reputation you have to rebuild plank by plank, in public, with witnesses.

We'd rebuilt it. Not me alone — never alone again — but the crew, and the council, and a soaked, ruined man named Vane who stood up in front of everyone who mattered and burned himself down to light the truth. Last I heard he was working salvage out of Low Marsh, alone, refusing help with a politeness that fooled nobody. Odile sends him bread by the mail barge. He's never once acknowledged it. She keeps sending it. That's Odile.

"Bram," I said.

"Captain."

"The boat's yours."

He turned. Not startled — Bram doesn't startle — but attentive, the way he gets when the current changes under him.

"Yours, and Odile's, and Wren's, and mine. Equal shares. The papers are drawn up; they're in the wheelhouse drawer. I signed last night." I took a sip of coffee to give my hands something official to do. "I'm telling you before I tell the others because you'll pretend you don't care, and I wanted to watch you pretend."

He was quiet a long moment. The river talked instead — that low conversation against the hull, the one I'd woken to.

"My father owned his boat outright," Bram finally said. "Forty years. Died owing nobody, and the boat went to a nephew who sold it inside a season." He looked at me. "Ownership isn't the paper, Hazel. It's who's aboard when the storm comes."

"I know," I said. "That's why all four names are on it."

He nodded once, slow, and then — because he's Bram, and sentiment between us has always traveled by cargo rather than letter — he said, "Your elbow patch is crooked," and went forward to insult the anchor winch into better behavior.

I wore the crooked patch all day.

* * *

*

Midday, we stopped at the shallows above Corben's Landing where the channel braids, and I let Wren take her solo run.

This is new too. Three weeks new. I sit in the stern with my hands folded like a grandmother at church and I let a sixteen-year-old thread the Kestrel through water that has humbled pilots with thirty years on it, and I don't touch the wheel, and I don't shout, and every time she reads a boil line correctly a small firework goes off somewhere behind my sternum that I have decided is professional pride and absolutely nothing else.

She came through the third braid a little wide — the eddy at the willow point pushed her, the way it pushes everyone — corrected late, corrected *well*, and brought us through with the hull barely whispering. Then she killed the throttle, spun the wheel, and looked back at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"Well?" she said. "Say something critical so I know you're paying attention."

"You came into the braid half a boat wide."

"I know. The willow eddy—"

"—pushed you starboard, and you fought it instead of leaning into it and letting it sling you. Next time, use the river's bad manners. Don't apologize for them."

Wren stared at me. Then she laughed — that full, unguarded laugh she still rationed carefully, like fuel after the narrows — and threw the throttle forward, and the Kestrel surged on toward home waters.

Her grandfather's will had named her heir to the river charters. The council had confirmed it. On paper, Wren Calloway owns more of this river than anyone alive. In practice, she'd spent the entire inheritance negotiation doing something no one expected: she'd chartered most of the docks back to the families who'd worked them under Corvus's thumb, at rates they could actually pay, keeping only the Kestrel's route and the council seat for herself. When the syndicate men protested, she quoted her grandfather's will back at them — *the river is held in trust for those who work it* — and dared them to argue with a dead man in front of the council.

She's sixteen. She terrifies me. I've never been prouder of anyone in my life.

"Tomas wants to steer through the Narrows," she called back. "He says he ran them blind in '61."

"He ran them drunk in '61 and put a scow on the rocks."

"He says that's a slander."

"He told me the story himself, the night we sheltered in the side channel. He also claims he taught your grandfather everything he knew."

"Did he?"

"No. But I let him believe it. It costs nothing and he glows for an hour." I stood and stretched. "Let him take the wheel through the flat water below the mission. The Narrows are mine until he's had his eyes seen to."

"Yes, Captain," she said, and saluted with two fingers, sloppy, perfect.

* * *

Evening found us tied up at the old Thornbury mooring — the one my father leased, the one the syndicate seized, the one the council returned to us along with an apology so bureaucratic it might as well have been written in Latin. The new dock pilings Bram and I drove last month stood straight in the amber water. The shed needed paint. The winch needed Bram. Everything needed something, and for the first time I could look at a list of needs without feeling the cold arithmetic of *which ones can we afford* pressing down behind my eyes.

Odile cooked on the aft deck. She cooks there now whenever the weather allows — says a galley is for bad days and a deck is for good ones, and she intends to have more good ones than the river can take away. Rice and river fish and the green beans from the planter boxes Wren built along the rail, because the girl decided a boat should grow some of its own food and dared anyone to find that sentimental.

Tomas sat in the good chair, wrapped in a blanket despite the mild evening, telling Wren about the flood of '58 with the usual embellishments. Odile listened with half an ear and corrected him with the other half. Bram carved — he carves now, little river birds, gives them away to children at every landing, denies it fiercely if asked.

And I sat at the rail with my coffee gone cold and watched the light come down the water the way it does here, long and gold and forgiving, and I thought about sandbars.

Because that's how this whole life started, or at least how I understood it starting. Sandbars. Their moods. My father teaching me that the river doesn't hate you and doesn't love you, it just argues with itself, and your whole job is to listen to the argument and pick a side at the right moment. For years I thought that was a lonely philosophy — one woman, one wheel, one reading of the water, and if I read it wrong, I drowned alone with my name.

But sitting there, with the four of them making their small noises behind me — the kettle, the knife, the laugh, the old man's embellished history — I understood what had actually changed. Not the river. The river keeps its moods. It will keep arguing with itself long after every one of us is sediment, and some morning it will move a bar and catch some other pilot napping, indifferent as weather.

What changed was that I stopped listening alone.

There's a girl at my wheel who hears things in the water I'd stopped hearing. There's a man forward who reads a hull's stress the way I read a boil line. There's a woman amidships who can smell a storm six hours out and a fever before the skin shows it. There's an old man in the chair who carries twenty years of this river's memory in a head going soft at the edges. Five readings of the same water. Five chances to pick the right side of the argument.

The herons stood on the invisible bar below Miller's Bend, patient as accountants. Somewhere upstream, the monsoon-fed current was already rearranging the channel for next season, setting down new sand, cutting new lies. Let it. We'd be out here reading it. All of us.

"Hazel," Wren called from the wheelhouse door. "Tomorrow's chart. You want to look tonight or trust me?"

I looked at her — silhouetted against the cabin lamp, one hand on the frame, waiting, certain of the answer and asking anyway, because that's the ritual and rituals are how a crew becomes a family becomes a legend becomes, someday, a story some other drowning captain tells herself on a cold morning.

"Show me tonight," I said. "And tomorrow morning, we'll read it together."

She grinned and disappeared inside. Behind me, Odile said something about beans, Bram denied carving again, Tomas demanded someone admire his steering, and the river went on arguing with itself in the dark, keeping its moods, keeping its secrets, keeping its own counsel the way it always had and always would.

But no longer mine alone.
