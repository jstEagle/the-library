## 1. Miles, Not Years

I measure my life in distance. Not years, not birthdays, not the anniversaries that other men carve into their doorframes. Distance. It's a cleaner arithmetic. A man can lie about how old he feels, but the flats don't lie about how far you've walked across them, and neither do your feet, which keep their own honest ledger in blisters and bone-ache.

Three years ago I stopped counting days and started counting miles, and if you'd asked me why, I'd have told you it was ranger discipline, the habit of a professional, and I'd have been lying the way I'm paid to lie, smoothly and with a straight back. The truth is that the day I stopped counting days was the day Ilsa Vey walked out of my life with a satchel full of stolen documents and left nothing behind but a trail signature I could have drawn blindfolded.

That's the thing about tracking a thief for three years. At some point it stops being a manhunt and becomes a conversation. A long, one-sided, increasingly bitter conversation conducted in boot prints and campfire ash, across eleven hundred miles of salt and scrub and dead riverbed. She talks. I listen. I never get to answer.

* * *

The salt flats south of Vell were doing what salt flats do, which is nothing, magnificently. Heat shimmer turned the horizon into a rumor. My dune-crawler had died two days back with a sound like a resigned sigh — a cracked fuel line, fixable in Calloway, unfixable here — so I was walking, which suited me. Walking is how you read country. From a crawler cab you get the summary. On foot you get the full text, every comma of it.

The text that morning said: *someone came through here four days ago, alone, moving fast, and knew exactly what they were doing.*

I'd picked up the sign at the edge of the flats where the hardpan gives way to salt crust, the kind of transition zone most trackers hate because it holds nothing. But this trail held everything, if you knew the handwriting. A walker who rolls from heel to toe to spare the crust. A stride a hair too long for comfort, the stride of someone who'd learned pace on long patrols and never unlearned it. Weight slightly favoring the left leg — an old injury, healed crooked, compensated for so thoroughly that most trackers would miss it entirely.

I didn't miss it. I'd spent three years missing nothing about it.

Ilsa Vey.

I crouched and pressed two fingers into a print. The crust edges had already begun to crumble and round. Four days, maybe five. She was ahead of me and pulling away, but not by much, and not by choice — nobody crosses the Vell flats at a dead run. You cross them at the pace that keeps you alive, and the pace that keeps you alive is the pace I walk.

So it was a conversation again. She'd say something with her feet, and I'd answer with mine, and the country between us would carry the words.

I stood, worked the stiffness out of my knee, and followed.

* * *

You want to know what chasing your former partner for three years does to a person? It makes you an expert in things you never wanted expertise in. I know her water discipline — she rations at exactly two liters a day in flats heat, same as me, because we trained together and the training sticks like a scar. I know her fire habit — she burns dry scrub only, small and hot, and scatters the ash with a boot-heel sweep, and the sweep goes counterclockwise, and I have stood over a dozen of those counterclockwise circles in a dozen bad moods. I know that she camps on rises, never in hollows, that she prefers dawn departures, that she once went four days on half rations to keep weight off a wounded shoulder and lied to me about it with such conviction that I only found out when the medic did.

I know all of this the way you know a song you hate. Completely. Involuntarily. Humming it in your sleep.

The ranger command in Fort Calloway calls it a priority pursuit. Three years of priority pursuit. Other rangers had rotated off it, then onto it, then off again; I never did. When the new district chief suggested, gently, in the way chiefs suggest things, that perhaps my objectivity had been compromised by duration, I told him my objectivity was the only thing keeping the pursuit alive, and that was true, though not in the way he heard it. My objectivity was a wall I'd built between me and a door I didn't trust myself not to open.

Because here's what the file doesn't say, what no report says: she didn't take money. She didn't take the payroll, the rations, the medicine cache, any of the things a thief takes when a thief is a thief. She took documents. Sealed ones. From a records office I still can't talk about, on a night when I was twenty miles away on patrol, which some part of me has always suspected was not a coincidence. She left my bedroll untouched. She left my mother's ring — the one I kept in my kit because I'm a sentimental man pretending not to be — exactly where it was.

Thieves don't do that. Thieves take the ring.

So for three years I've been chasing a thief who committed the one crime I couldn't make sense of, and the not-making-sense of it has been the heaviest thing I've carried, heavier than the pack, heavier than the rifle, heavier than the miles.

* * *

The second day on her trail the flats gave way to the broken country north of Vell — shattered salt pans, ribs of red stone, the bones of a landscape that had tried to be something else and failed. Her sign improved, which is to say it became more deliberately difficult. She'd walked a dry wash for two miles on bare rock. She'd doubled back along her own line twice, once laying false prints with an exaggerated roll that fooled nobody who'd spent three years learning the real one.

That's when I started talking back. Out loud, to nobody, the way you do when the country is empty enough to keep your secrets.

"Counterclockwise," I said, standing over her cold camp from the night before. The ash circle, the boot sweep, the fire ring built from stones she'd chosen for heat retention because she'd once lectured me for an hour about heat retention and I'd pretended to be asleep through all of it. "Very funny."

The country said nothing. It usually doesn't.

I made camp on the rise she'd rejected — no, that's not true, I made camp on the rise she'd used two nights before, because I'm not proud, and I sat where she'd sat, and I ate the same ration she'd eaten, and I thought about how the distance between us had narrowed from eleven hundred miles to maybe thirty, and how thirty miles of flats is one long day's walk, and how I might actually catch her this time, and what I would do when I did.

The file says arrest. The oath says arrest. Three years of reports I'd filed in my own neat hand said arrest, detain, transfer to Calloway, let the magistrates sort it out.

But my hands, when I imagined it, did something else. They hesitated. And a ranger whose hands hesitate is a ranger who should turn in his badge, so I didn't imagine it anymore. I just walked.

* * *

The orders caught me on the third day, which is the kind of timing the universe finds funny.

I'd topped a rise of red stone in the late afternoon when the relay beacon on my pack strap began its ugly buzzing — the long-range burst, priority-coded, the kind that doesn't get sent to rangers who are resting comfortably. I found shade in the lee of the stone, thumbed the decoder, and read.

**PRIORITY — DISTRICT COMMAND CALLOWAY TO RANGER OKAFOR A. — TERMINATE CURRENT PURSUIT. REPORT FORT CALLOWAY WITHIN SIX DAYS. ESCORT ASSIGNMENT. UPRIVER CONVOY. MERIDIAN EXPEDITION RESUPPLY. FURTHER BRIEFING ON ARRIVAL. ACKNOWLEDGE.**

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the last line of the burst had a secondary header I almost missed, the kind of routing flag that means someone above district command had asked for me by name.

*Meridian.* I knew the name the way everyone in the territory knew it — the big survey expedition, the one with family money behind it, the one that had gone up into the flooded canyons of the High Wreck and come back with nothing but silence. Three weeks of silence, now. The supply runs had gone up on schedule; the answers hadn't come down. Fort Calloway's mess hall had a betting pool on it. The smart money said the river had taken them. The sentimental money said equipment failure. Nobody's money said anything worth betting on.

And they wanted me — me, mid-pursuit, mid-conversation, thirty miles from the closest I'd been to her in three years — to walk away and go play escort for a boatload of canned peaches and radio parts.

I should have refused. I've refused assignments before; it's a right the ranger charter grants and the command structure resents. I had a pursuit file with my name on it and three years of my life invested in it and a trail under my boots that was, for the first time ever, warm.

I sat against the red stone and looked at the flats stretching north, where her prints went on without me, counterclockwise ash and long stride and weight off the left leg, and I thought: *she'll cross the pans by tomorrow night. Then she hits the caravan road and I lose her for a month, maybe two, maybe forever, and the conversation ends mid-sentence again.*

Then I looked at the orders. *Requested by name.* Somebody with weight wanted Ansel Okafor on that convoy, and in my experience, when people with weight want a specific ranger for a specific job, the job is never just the job.

I thought about that for a long time while the sun went down and the stone gave back its heat and the flats turned the color of an old bruise.

Then I did the arithmetic I always do. Not days. Miles. Fort Calloway was ninety miles southeast. Her trail went north. The two lines diverged, and I had six days to cover mine, and every mile I walked toward Calloway was a mile of the conversation I'd never get back.

I keyed the beacon. "Calloway, Okafor. Acknowledged. En route." My voice came out level, which surprised me, because nothing about me felt level. "Advise: current pursuit suspended, not terminated. Subject trail active."

The reply came back in the flat pulse of the relay: **NOTED. SUSPENSION LOGGED. HURRY, RANGER.**

*Hurry, ranger.* As if hurry were a thing you could order, like rain.

* * *

I broke camp in the dark and walked southeast, and I'll be honest with you, since the flats aren't listening and you might be: I looked back. Twice. The second time was for the ring I don't carry anymore — no, that's wrong, I still carry it, buried at the bottom of my kit where it's been for three years, because I'm a sentimental man pretending not to be, and because some superstitious corner of me believes that the day I leave it behind is the day I stop being a man who expects her to come back.

The first time was for the trail. Her prints, already fading in the wind off the pans, going north while I went south, and I stood there like an idiot letting the distance grow, measuring it, because that's what I do, that's all I do, I measure.

Then I turned and walked, and I didn't look back again, and I made forty miles before the sun stood up, which is a good day's work by anyone's ledger.

* * *

Here is the thing I figured out somewhere around mile sixty, in the flat gray hour before dawn on the fourth day, walking on a caravan road with the salt dust rising off my boots like smoke: I was glad of the orders.

Not happy. Not relieved. Glad in the way a man is glad of a storm that gives him permission to stop pretending the weather is fine. Three years of chasing her had worn me down to a shape that fit nothing — too loyal to quit, too honest to lie about why, too stubborn to catch her, or maybe too careful, or maybe — and this is the thought I'd been stepping around for a thousand miles like a hole in the road — maybe not trying as hard as I told myself I was.

Because if I caught her, it ended. The conversation, the pursuit, the whole architecture of purpose I'd built out of her absence. If I caught her, I'd have to arrest her, and if I arrested her, I'd have to stop wondering, and the wondering was the last part of her I still had. The documents she stole, the reason she ran, the door I wasn't supposed to open — as long as she was ahead of me on the trail, all of it stayed a question. And questions, I can live on questions. It's answers that kill you.

So yes. I was glad of the orders. I told myself the Meridian job would take six weeks, eight at the outside, and that her trail would keep — trails keep, that's the whole point of trails, that's why rangers exist — and I told myself that the itch between my shoulder blades, the one that said *you're walking the wrong way*, was just the old injury talking, the one I got falling down a scree slope the same year she left, the year I started measuring my life in miles.

The salt dust rose off the road. The sun came up the color of brass. Somewhere north of me, a woman with a crooked-healed leg and a counterclockwise boot sweep was walking her own line through the empty country, and I hoped — I genuinely, unprofessionally hoped — that she'd find whatever it was she was looking for, and that it wouldn't be the kind of answer that kills you.

* * *

I reached Fort Calloway on the evening of the fifth day, a day ahead of orders, because I'd stopped sleeping more than four hours a night somewhere around the third year of the pursuit and never quite started again.

The fort sat on its bluff above the river the way it always did, squat and certain, mud-brick walls the color of dried blood, the relay mast stitching the sky with its little threads of priority traffic. The gate sentry knew me and waved me through without the usual ceremony, which stung a little — three years of priority pursuit and I rate a wave.

The duty officer was a round, damp man named Pell who looked at my trail dust and my face and made the wise decision to skip the pleasantries. He handed me a folder with a Serevan seal on it, heavy paper, the kind of stationery that costs more than a ranger's monthly pay.

"Convoy assembles tomorrow, dawn," Pell said. "Eleven vehicles, two rafts, forty-one souls counting drivers. You're senior escort. The Lady Serevan's office wanted — " he consulted the folder, though he clearly had it memorized — "a ranger with upriver experience and a clean disciplinary record."

"Clean record," I said. "Three-year pursuit, no result. That reads as clean?"

Pell looked at me with the mild pity of a man who processes paperwork for a living. "It reads as *persistent*, Ranger. Upstairs, that's the same thing." He slid a second sheet across the desk. "There's a rider on your contract. Serevan office standard for this run. You'll want to read it before you sign."

I read it. I'm going to be honest: I read it twice, and both times my eye snagged on the same clause, the one about *discretion regarding all cargo, correspondence, and expedition matters observed or inferred in the course of duty*, and the compensation schedule attached to it, which was double my pay, and which nobody doubles a ranger's pay for hauling canned peaches.

"Upriver experience," I said slowly. "And what exactly is it you think is wrong with the Meridian, that a clean record and a discretion clause are the qualifications?"

Pell closed the folder. "I process paperwork, Ranger. What's wrong with the Meridian is above my desk. But — " and here he lowered his voice, which I appreciated, because it meant he was human — "the betting pool's been suspended. Command's orders. Apparently the odds had gotten *indecent*."

I signed the contract, because I had nowhere else to be and because the alternative was the north road and a trail gone cold and a conversation I wasn't sure I could survive resuming. The ink was still wet when the duty clerk came in with the evening traffic, and one sheet of it, routing-flagged, priority-coded, caught my eye on the way to Pell's tray.

It was a field report fragment. Salvage notice, upriver station. A supply raft recovered from the canyon water, hull breached, cargo clawed open — the clerk's word, *clawed*, underlined by whoever had routed it — from the *inside*.

And beneath the salvage notice, in the station log's cramped hand, a line that had nothing to do with rafts and everything to do with the arithmetic I do instead of living:

*Unidentified tracks on the gravel bar below the wreck site. Single walker. Unusual gait noted — weight favoring left leg.*

I stood there with the contract in my hand and the salt dust of five days still on my boots, and the fort's evening noise went distant and thin, and I did the only arithmetic that mattered.

Ninety miles of river between me and that gravel bar. And somewhere ahead of me, going where I was going, walking with a stride I could draw blindfolded, was Ilsa Vey.

"Hurry, ranger," Pell said, not looking up.

I was already moving.
