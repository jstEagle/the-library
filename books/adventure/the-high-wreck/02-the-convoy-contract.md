## 2. The Convoy Contract

Fort Calloway smelled of wet canvas, gun oil, and the particular despair of a place built to be temporary that had outlasted everyone who remembered it was supposed to be temporary. I'd been walking toward it for two days when the relay mast finally rose over the ridge like a needle stitching the sky to the earth, and I felt the familiar tightening in my chest — not dread exactly. More like the sensation of putting on boots you'd outgrown.

Rangers didn't come to Calloway by choice. We came because the river ran through it, and the river ran where it pleased, and everything else in this territory — charters, borders, careers — bent around that fact like reeds.

The gate sergeant knew me. Most of them did; my face had been on the wanted circulars long enough, in the wrong way, as the man hunting the thief rather than being one. He waved me through with the weary courtesy of a man who'd stopped expecting anything interesting to happen at his post.

"Okafor. They said you'd come in from the flats."

"They said right."

"Dispatch wants you. Second floor. And Okafor—" he lowered his voice, which told me everything before he said it, "—there's a lady upstairs. A *lady* lady. Not the usual quartermaster variety."

"I've met ladies," I said.

"Not like this one."

He wasn't wrong, but I'll get to that.

* * *

The dispatch office occupied what had once been a surveyor's map room, back when Fort Calloway had ambitions. The long table still bore the grooves where drafting instruments had been pinned, and the walls still held the old charts of the High Wreck — the drowned city rendered in faded ink, its towers drawn as they'd been before the rivers were rerouted and the canyons filled. Pre-collapse cartography. The kind of paper that belonged behind glass in some family's private collection, not nailed to a fort wall where sun and soldiers could get at it.

Lady Serevan stood at that table with her back to me, studying the Wreck the way other people studied enemies or lovers: with total attention and something withheld.

She didn't turn when the door opened. "Ansel Okafor."

"Lady Serevan."

"You walk quietly for a large man."

"Desert habit. Loud men die thirsty."

That earned me half a smile, delivered over her shoulder, rationed like good whiskey. She was perhaps fifty, dressed in traveling clothes worth more than my annual pay, and she wore her authority the way the canyon walls wore water stains — visibly, patiently, as the record of long pressure. Her hair was iron-grey and cut practically, and her hands, when she finally turned and offered one, were a working woman's hands despite the rings. That surprised me. I'd expected softness.

"You tracked Vey across the salt," she said. Not a question.

"I tracked a thief across the salt. The name came later."

"The name came three years ago." She let that sit. "You filed eleven reports on Ilsa Vey. Every one meticulous. Every one noting the same gait analysis, the same left-foot drag on hardpan, the same preference for night travel along drainage lines. You know her trail signature better than anyone in the service."

"It's my job to know signatures."

"It's your job to catch her." She turned fully then, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't file under any of my usual categories. Pity, maybe. Or appraisal. With women like Serevan those were often the same thing. "And yet here you are, recalled from a pursuit you've chased for a thousand miles, because I asked for you by name."

"I assumed the requisition went through channels."

"Nothing goes through channels anymore, Ranger. Channels are how information drowns." She gestured at the old maps. "Do you know what the Meridian Expedition is?"

"Academics. Survey work upriver. Dr. Sen's outfit. They went quiet six weeks ago."

"They went silent four weeks ago. The first two weeks of silence we called bad weather." Her mouth thinned. "Now we call it something else. I want you to take a convoy upriver. Resupply. Medical staff. Radio equipment. Bring them home if they need bringing."

"That's a rescue mission."

"That is a *resupply* mission, and I will thank you to keep the vocabulary straight in your reports." Something flickered across her face — fast, gone, like a fish turning under ice. "The Meridian holds my family's charter. Do you understand what that means?"

"Everyone understands what charters mean, Lady. They're why the borders are where they are."

"Then you understand that if the Meridian fails — if Dr. Sen comes back with nothing, or doesn't come back at all — my family's claim to these territories weakens. There are five other families who would carve up our holdings within the year. There are courts. There are precedents." She laid one hand flat on the old map, fingers spread across the drowned towers. "My grandfather bought this expedition with thirty years of political capital. If it dies in those canyons, it dies taking my house with it."

"So this is about your charter."

"This is about *certainty*." She said the word like she was setting a stone in a wall. "I have invested too much to fund ambiguity. Which brings me to the delicate part, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide what kind of man you are today."

I should have walked out then. In the years since, I've replayed that moment often enough to know it: the door was eight feet away, the corridor beyond it led to a canteen and a bunk and a career that, whatever else you said about it, had never asked me to choose between my oath and my curiosity. Instead I folded my arms and waited, because she'd known about the reports, and the reports meant someone had been reading my files, and I wanted to know how much of my life was already in her keeping.

"Double pay," she said. "Standard hazard rates on top. And a condition."

"There it is."

"Don't be naive, Ranger, it doesn't suit you. The condition is discretion. Whatever the Meridian found down there — surveys, artifacts, correspondence, nothing — does not leave your reports. You will log distances, weather, losses if there are losses. You will not log discoveries. When the convoy returns, you will brief me privately before you brief your own commandant. Those are the terms."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then another ranger takes the contract, and you go back to chasing ghosts across the salt, and neither of us thinks less of the other." She smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "But you won't refuse. Not because of the money. Because Ilsa Vey went upriver ahead of you, Ranger, and I know you know it."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below us a gate slammed, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a heartbeat.

"How—" I started, and stopped, because the question didn't matter. What mattered was that she'd said it plainly, deliberately, watching my face while she did it. She hadn't mentioned Vey in any of the briefing documents. She'd saved it for the moment when my answer would cost me the most.

"You've read my personnel file," I said instead.

"I've read everything. Your file is the least interesting thing I've read this month." She came around the table, and up close I could see the fine lines around her eyes, the map of a woman who had spent decades making decisions that other people paid for. "Three years ago your partner stole something from a Meridian precursor dig and vanished. The service called it treasure. You wrote in your final report — I quote — 'the item taken appears to be documentary in nature, value unknown.' Value unknown. You were the only investigator in this territory who understood what she actually took, and nobody listened to you, and it has eaten at you ever since. Am I wrong?"

"You're not wrong."

"Do you imagine I don't know what that's like? To be the only one who sees the shape of a thing?" For a moment — just a moment — something almost human moved behind the politics. Then it was gone, packed away with the rest of her inventory. "I am offering you a chance to finish what you started. Find her. Find out what she knows. And bring me back a clean report. Double pay, Okafor. Half of it is for the river. The other half is for your silence."

"And if what she took turns out to matter more than your charter?"

Something crossed her face then that I would spend a long time failing to interpret correctly. It might have been amusement. It might have been fear. Looking back from where I stand now, I think it was recognition — the look of a woman hearing, from an unexpected direction, the first faint echo of the thing that would eventually break her house in half.

"Then we shall both have interesting problems," she said. "Sign here."

* * *

I signed. Of course I signed. Let no one tell you that corruption arrives wearing a villain's face; mine arrived carrying double pay and the only honest lead on Ilsa Vey I'd had in three years, and I took it with both hands and told myself the lie that every compromised man tells: *I can control this. I'll take her money and do the right thing anyway.*

The convoy assembled over the next two days in the lower yard, and I threw myself into the work the way you throw yourself into cold water — all at once, so you don't have time to think. Six supply rafts, twin-hulled, rated for the upper canyon narrows. Two months of rations, medical stores, a crate of new radio components that Tam — the radio operator, nineteen years old, all elbows and enthusiasm — handled like relics. Eleven drivers and deckhands, most of them river-born, none of them asking questions, which should have been my first warning. River people always ask questions. It's how they stay alive.

Dr. Sen's last transmission had come through six weeks prior: routine position report, all hands accounted for, extending survey into the deep canyons. Then nothing. The official assessment was equipment failure. The unofficial assessment, passed around the canteen in low voices, was that the High Wreck ate expeditions the way quicksand ate cattle — slowly, and without caring.

On the second evening, Lady Serevan came down to the yard herself. No entourage, no banner, just her and a single aide, walking the line of rafts with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing troops she intended to survive longer than.

"Ranger." She stopped beside my raft, where I was checking lashings I'd already checked twice. "A word."

"Lady."

"My envoy will follow you upriver in ten days. A supply skiff, light crew. He carries my letters to Dr. Sen and my instructions regarding cargo priority." She watched me coil a rope, and I made myself do it slowly, correctly, because her eyes on my hands made them want to shake. "If the situation ashore is... disordered, you will defer to him on matters of extraction."

"Extraction of what?"

"People. Documents. Whatever warrants extraction." She paused. "You have objections."

"I have a rule, Lady. I don't defer blind. If your envoy wants command decisions, he can carry a ranger's commission or swim alongside."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the river spoke for her — that low, constant mutter of water against hull that never stops, never cares, never agrees or disagrees. Then: "Your file says you're insubordinate."

"My file says worse than that."

"Yes. It also says you've never lost a person under your protection." She turned to go, then stopped, and what she said next she said quietly, without looking back, and I have wondered ever since whether it was a warning or a confession. "The Meridian found something in those canyons, Ranger. I don't know what. My grandfather's archives suggest my family has known the deep canyons held *something* for three generations. We sent the expedition to find it. I need you to understand that whatever you feel about the terms of your contract — the silence, the envoy, all of it — the alternative to my family controlling that discovery is four other families fighting a war over it. Sometimes the lesser evil is simply the evil that employs you."

She walked away across the yard, small and straight-backed against the bulk of the fort, and I stood there holding a coiled rope and feeling the ground shift under assumptions I'd built my whole career on.

* * *

We cast off at dawn on the third day. The river took us north between walls of red stone, and the fort shrank behind us until it was a smudge, and then a memory, and then nothing.

Tam found me at the stern rail around midmorning. He had the look of a boy who'd volunteered for adventure and was only now doing the arithmetic on what that meant.

"Ranger Okafor? Sir. Question, sir."

"Ask it."

"The silence. From the Meridian camp." He gripped the rail, knuckles pale. "Six weeks is a long time. But their transmitter was new spec, military surplus. Those things don't just fail. I checked the maintenance logs before we loaded — Dr. Sen filed a full diagnostic six days before the last transmission. Everything green."

"So the radio works."

"So the radio *worked*, sir. Somebody would have to make it stop." He said it lightly, the way young people say terrible things, testing the weight of them. "Anyway. I'll keep calling on channel nine, every watch. That's standard."

"Keep calling," I said.

He nodded and headed forward, and I stayed at the rail and watched the canyon walls slide past, close enough in the narrows to touch, older than every charter and every family and every border they'd drawn across this country. Somewhere upstream, past the drowned towers, Ilsa Vey was alive — I felt it the way you feel weather coming, in the ache of old scars — and I was sailing toward her under contract to a woman whose family had known the deep canyons held secrets for three generations.

Double pay. Half for the river, half for the silence.

I'd taken the money. Now I had to find out what it had bought — and whether I could live in the house it built.

By evening the canyon had narrowed until the walls pressed close and the light lasted only minutes after sunset, and Tam's voice crackled out over channel nine into the gathering dark, calling the Meridian, calling the Meridian, calling the Meridian.

The river answered. Nothing else did.
