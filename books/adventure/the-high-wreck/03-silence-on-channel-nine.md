## 3. Silence on Channel Nine

My mother wanted me to be a bridge engineer. She said bridges were honest work — you build them, people cross them, everybody lives. Instead I became a radio operator on a river convoy, which is the kind of job where you spend your days talking to people who aren't answering, and by day four of the Meridian run I had started to understand that this was not a coincidence so much as a career.

"Channel Nine, Meridian camp, Meridian camp, this is supply convoy *Calloway's Mercy*, six souls and forty crates of everything you're running out of, requesting status check, over."

Static. The good kind at first — river static, soft as sand poured over glass — and then the bad kind underneath it, the hiss that sounds like something breathing in a room where nothing should be breathing.

I'd made that call eleven times since dawn. I kept a tally in the margin of my logbook, because Ranger Okafor had told me to log every attempt with timestamps, and because keeping tallies is what I do instead of panicking. It's a system. It works until it doesn't.

"Nothing?" asked Wrenn, our lead driver, from the pilothouse of the lead skiff. She had a voice like gravel settling and a face that suggested she'd personally audited every disappointment life had to offer and found most of them adequate.

"Nothing," I said. "Eleven for eleven."

"Could be their set's down."

"Could be." I looked out at the canyon walls rising on either side of us, red stone going purple in the late light, close enough now that I could have thrown a bottle at either one and hit geology worth studying. "Could also be they're all dead."

Wrenn snorted. "Cheerful."

"I'm a radio operator. Cheerfulness is above my pay grade. So is pessimism, technically, but nobody checks."

The truth was I'd been uneasy since the second morning, when we passed the last relay buoy and entered the Narrows proper. Upstream of that point, Channel Nine was supposed to be clean — the expedition had repeaters strung along the rim, expensive ones, the kind Lady Serevan's money bought. Clean channels don't go quiet. Dead men's channels do.

* * *

Ranger Okafor came aft to my station just before dusk, moving the way he always moved, which is to say like a man who has measured every deck plate on the boat and found none of them trustworthy. He was not what I'd expected from a desert ranger. I'd expected weathered and laconic; he was both, but there was a precision to him, an economy, like he rationed his own gestures. He stood at the rail of the cargo skiff and watched the water for a while before he spoke.

"How many attempts today?"

"Fourteen total. Eleven today, three yesterday evening."

"And the last confirmed contact with the camp?"

"Nine days ago. Routine traffic. Dr. Sen's assistant logged a resupply confirmation and complained about the coffee." I hesitated. "There was one thing after that."

Okafor turned. He had a way of turning that made you feel like a document under review.

"The night of the sixth," I said. "I was on the midnight watch. I got a reply."

Wrenn had laughed at me when I reported it. Okafor did not laugh. "You didn't put it in your log."

"I put it in my log."

"You didn't put it in the *official* log."

"There are two logs," I admitted. "There's the one I file, and there's the one I keep so that when somebody asks me later what really happened, I can say it without lying. My father was a court clerk. He taught me that the official record is a story people agree on, and the other record is what happened. You want to survive institutions, keep both."

Something moved across the ranger's face — not quite a smile, more like recognition taking a lap around his features before deciding not to stay. "What did the voice say?"

"A woman. Compressed transmission, low power, which means handheld or a damaged set. No callsign, no protocol. Just—" I could hear it still, if I let myself, which I tried not to. "She said, 'Turn back.' Twice. Then the channel went dead and stayed dead."

"Signal direction?"

"That's the thing." I pulled out my notebook, the unofficial one, its pages soft with river damp. "Bearing came from downstream of the camp. From between us and them. Like someone was transmitting from the water."

Okafor looked at the river. The river looked back, brown and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets at a steady three knots.

"Log it officially too," he said finally. "Timestamped. Word for word."

"If I do that, Wrenn will have me checked for sunstroke."

"If you do that and anything happens to this convoy, your log is the only reason anyone will know where to start looking." He said it mildly, the way you'd comment on the weather, but the weather he was commenting on was a landslide. "Official records exist for the day things go wrong. Until then they're just paperwork. Afterward, they're scripture."

I wrote it down. Both logs. Identical entries, for once. It felt like an omen, though I didn't yet know of what.

* * *

We tied up that night against a shelf of rock below a ruined aqueduct — pre-collapse masonry, older than the families, older than the charters, arches marching off into the dark like the ribs of something enormous that lay down here to die and never finished. We ran no lights beyond the galley lamp. Okafor's orders. He posted watches in pairs and told the drivers to sleep with their boots on, and nobody argued, because when a man who rations his gestures spends three of them posting guards, you take the hint.

I had the midnight-to-four watch again, which was becoming a theme, sharing it with Ferro, the deckhand, who was fifty, missing two fingers, and constitutionally incapable of silence for longer than ninety seconds.

"Third run I've made up this river," Ferro said, for perhaps the fourth time. "Never seen it this quiet. Birds gone. Bats gone. You notice? Canyon like this should be *loud* after dark. Frogs, insects, the whole orchestra." He gestured with his nine remaining fingers at the profound, pressurized silence pressing down on the water. "It's like the river's holding its breath."

"Maybe it heard about our payroll," I said. "Even the wildlife's worried about getting paid."

Ferro chuckled, then stopped chuckling, because that was when the raft came around the bend.

I saw it before he did — or rather I heard it, a wrongness in the water-sound, a dragging, wallowing noise that didn't match current or wind. Then the galley lamp caught it: a supply raft, expedition-pattern, riding low and listing hard to port, turning slowly in an eddy like something deciding whether to sink on purpose.

"*Ahoy*!" Ferro bellowed, and his voice cracked the silence like a rifle shot, and nothing answered, which was answer enough.

Okafor was awake before Ferro finished shouting. I will swear to my grave that man slept like a knife balanced on its edge. He had a line rigged and the raft alongside inside of four minutes, and he went aboard it alone, telling the rest of us — politely, in the tone of a man who would physically prevent alternatives — to stay on the skiff.

We watched him move across the raft's deck with a hand lantern held low. Cargo netting, slashed. Water barrels, punctured — deliberately, all of them, a neat row of holes. And the raft itself—

"Merciful saints," Ferro whispered beside me.

The deck planks were torn open from beneath. Not broken — *peeled*, pried up from below with tremendous force, splinters driven upward, as if something had come through the hull from the river side and then gone back down. Or as if someone locked inside the cargo hold had decided the ceiling was the door.

"Clawed open from inside," Wrenn said flatly, behind us. She'd come forward without any of us hearing her, which was its own small horror. "That's what the ranger means when he says stay on the skiff."

Okafor spent ten minutes aboard. When he came back he carried three things: a waterlogged expedition manifest, a boot — one boot, expedition-issue, size twelve, and I will not describe what was in it — and a length of green synthetic cord, frayed at both ends, which he coiled and stowed without explanation but with a care that suggested it explained itself to him.

"One man's gear," he said. "Raft launched from the camp nine, maybe ten days ago. Single occupant. Somebody left in a hurry, or was sent." He paused. "The hull damage happened after launch. Days after, judging by the weathering on the breaks."

"So something attacked the raft midriver," Wrenn said.

"Or someone." Okafor looked at the black water sliding past the hull. "River cats won't peel planking. Crocs don't pry. Whatever did this had hands, or patience, or both."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The aqueduct loomed over us, its arches full of dark.

"Meridian camp is thirty miles upstream," I said, mostly to hear a human voice. "If people are leaving in rafts, and the rafts come back like this—"

"Then we're delivering supplies to a fire we can't see yet," Wrenn finished. She rubbed her jaw. "Options?"

"We go on," Okafor said. "Contract says Meridian. Contract doesn't have a clause for spooky rafts." He glanced at me. "Operator. First light, you raise them again. Every hour on the hour. And add a new call sign pattern — alternate channels, Nine and Twelve. If anyone downriver of us is listening, I'd rather they chase a pattern than a habit."

"Yes, sir."

"And Tam." He used my name, which he hadn't done before. It landed heavier than I expected, like he'd been saving it. "The woman on the sixth. If she answers again — anything, a click, a carrier wave, breathing — you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Understood?"

"Understood." And then, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone: "Sir, do you think she was warning us? Or warning them off? 'Turn back' cuts both ways."

For a long moment the only sound was the river worrying the hull, and somewhere far off, one bird — a single cry, the first in two days, brief as a struck match.

"I think," Okafor said, "that whoever she is, she's been on this river longer than we have, and she knows something we don't. That makes her either the most dangerous person in this canyon or the only useful one. Possibly both." He settled his hat lower. "Get some sleep, operator. Tomorrow the canyon gets narrower."

He was right about the canyon. He was right about everything, that week, which is the worst part, looking back. The canyon narrowed, the walls leaned in like conspirators, and the channel stayed silent — fourteen calls, fifteen, sixteen, my tally climbing in the margins of two notebooks that were, for the first time, saying exactly the same thing.

And on the fifth morning, twenty minutes after dawn, with mist standing on the water in gray columns and the drowned towers of the High Wreck just beginning to show themselves far upstream like teeth breaking the surface — the carrier light on my set flickered.

Not Channel Nine. Channel Twelve. Low power. A woman's voice, compressed, stripped of everything but the words, arriving out of the mist like a debt collector:

*"Turn back."*

A pause long enough for my heart to attempt an escape.

Then, softer, almost lost under the static — three words I hadn't heard before, three words that turned dread into something with a name and a deadline:

*"They're coming."*

* * *

I brought it to Okafor first, as ordered, my hands steadier than I deserved. He listened to me repeat it twice, wrote it down himself in a small book I hadn't seen before, and then looked upstream at those pale towers rising out of the floodwater, and said the sentence that I would carry out of the canyon along with everything else:

"Change of plan, operator. We're not turning back." His jaw set like poured concrete. "Now we're delivering a warning."
