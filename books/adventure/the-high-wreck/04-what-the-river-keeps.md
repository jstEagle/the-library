## 4. What the River Keeps

I've spent three years learning to read a woman's footprints the way other men read letters from home. It's not a skill I recommend. It pays nothing, it sleeps badly, and it has a way of making every gravel bar in the territory look like a message addressed specifically to me.

But there it was.

We'd made camp on a wide bend where the river slowed and dropped its cargo of stone — a crescent of pale gravel maybe forty yards across, fringed with reeds that stood taller than Tam and his radio mast combined. I'd walked it at dusk out of habit, checking for sign before we bedded down, and found nothing but the usual: heron tracks, the convoy's own churned mud, a snake skin some creature had grown tired of wearing.

Then the moon came up over the canyon rim, low and white as a lamp held close to a page, and the gravel bar lit up like something confessed.

Boot prints. Six of them in a neat line, heel-to-toe, coming out of the water.

Not wading prints — *exiting* prints. The kind you leave when you've swum a river in the dark and climbed out on the far side, moving quiet, moving deliberate. The tread was a civilian pattern, worn nearly smooth at the ball. But the gait — that was what stopped my breath. A long stride, slightly rolled off the outside of the left foot, weight carried forward like a person perpetually about to break into a run. I'd charted that stride across four hundred miles of salt and sandstone. I could have picked it out of a lineup of ten thousand feet.

"Ilsa," I said, to nobody, because saying it out loud was the only way to make the word behave.

The convoy slept behind me. Kessler's drivers snored in their tarps. Tam had finally gone down after two days of hailing Channel Nine into static, curled around his set like a boy sleeping beside a dog he didn't trust. The sentry — I'd assigned him myself — was watching the upstream approach, which is exactly where an enemy would come from, and exactly where Ilsa Vey would never be caught dead approaching from.

I should have woken Kessler. That's the regulation answer, and I want it noted that I thought of it. Waking Kessler meant six men, lanterns, noise, a search grid, and by morning either a prisoner or a story the whole camp would chew on for weeks. It also meant explaining why I could identify one set of boot prints on sight, and I found I wasn't ready to have that conversation with anyone, least of all myself.

So I did the other thing. The thing I'd been doing for three years, alone, at night, telling myself it was duty.

I took my rifle, my knife, and my lantern unlit, and I followed her.

* * *

The reeds along the bank grew in stands, thick as a crowd, and the ground between them was black silt that took a print like wax takes a seal. She'd gone north along the waterline, keeping to the wet margin where footing was soft but sound didn't carry. Professional. Three years ago she'd been good; whatever she'd been doing since, it had made her better.

I tracked her the old way, the way they teach you at Calloway and then spend the rest of your career apologizing for: slow, low, reading the country ahead instead of the ground behind. The moon helped. So did the fact that she wasn't hiding from me — couldn't have been, because she didn't know I existed yet — and so moved at the pace of someone running errands rather than someone being hunted.

That stung more than I expected. Somewhere behind my ribs, a voice I didn't much like observed that she was moving through my territory, inside my cordon, past my sleeping convoy, entirely without urgency. As if I were weather. As if I were a thing that happened to other people.

The trail left the river at a place where a side channel cut back into the cane, and here she'd paused — both feet planted, facing upstream, standing long enough for the silt to hold her full weight twice. Looking at something. I raised my head above the reed line and saw what she'd seen.

Firelight. Far upriver, around the next bend, a smudge of orange against the canyon wall. Too small for a camp. Too steady for a signal fire. Somebody out there in the dark between us and the Meridian, burning fuel they'd carried in on their backs, and my convoy — my eleven souls and forty tons of resupply — camped an hour's walk below it, fat and blind and trusting the river to keep them company.

She'd seen it first. She'd stood here and looked at it, and then she'd turned downstream. Toward us. Toward me.

I felt hope arrive in my chest like a bird hitting glass — sudden, bright, and immediately painful. Because there were only two reasons Ilsa Vey would turn toward a ranger convoy in the middle of the night, and one of them was that she needed help, and the other was that she wanted me to see that fire too.

Three years ago I'd have known which. Three years ago I'd known which hand she'd deal from before she finished shuffling. Now I stood in the reeds with my heart going like a pump handle and understood that I no longer knew her at all — that the woman in my memory and the woman making these prints might share nothing but a stride and a name, and that I'd crossed a desert chasing the memory while the actual person went somewhere I couldn't follow.

Keep up, Okafor. The trail was freshening.

* * *

She doubled back through the cane in a pattern I recognized because I'd taught it to her — a lazy S that looked like wandering and was actually a listening move, letting her hear anything following before it closed. I gave the move its due respect and swung wide, losing the prints for ten minutes, finding them again where she'd cut down to the water and walked the shallows for thirty yards to kill her trail. Killing a trail from *me*. Which meant that somewhere in her calculations, rangers featured. Which meant she knew the convoy had one aboard.

Or she just assumed. Any competent runner assumes. I told myself that. The night was long enough already without adding meaning to mud.

I caught up to her at the mouth of a dry wash where the reeds thinned and the stars came down almost to the ground. She was crouched at the edge of the open, silhouetted against the gravel, and for one absurd moment I was fifteen feet away from Ilsa Vey in the dark and every speech I'd rehearsed across three years of salt flats evaporated at once. *You're under arrest* sat in my throat next to *where did you go*, and neither would come out, and meanwhile she rose, unhurried, and did not run.

That was the worst part. She didn't run.

She moved upstream along the wash at a walk, and I followed at fifty yards like a man trailing his own ghost, and I want to say I had a plan. I had a rifle and jurisdiction and three years of grievance, and none of it amounted to a plan, because plans require knowing what you want, and what I wanted was standing right in front of me refusing to be either fugitive or partner, just a shape in the dark walking away at a pace I could have matched forever.

Then she stopped at a leaning slab of limestone where the wash narrowed, knelt, and did something with her hands at the base of it. Deliberate. Unhurried. The movements of a person leaving something rather than taking it.

And then she was gone — into a crack in the canyon wall I wouldn't have found in daylight, moving with the particular silence of someone who has practiced disappearing until it stopped being a skill and became a temperament. One moment the night had a shape in it. Then it didn't.

I stood in the wash with my pulse hammering and counted to a hundred, because rushing a possible ambush is how rangers end up as cautionary tales, and then I went to the slab.

She'd left a flare.

A ranger-issue emergency flare, the short red kind we carry in our coat lining, the kind that burns hot enough to read a map by and bright enough to be seen ten miles off. Standard stock, serial numbers filed — no, not filed. Scratched. Scratched with a fine point, in the old habit she'd had of marking her gear so nobody could claim it, a tiny V worked into the metal where the serial used to be.

Unspent. Left deliberately, tucked into a cleft where moonlight would find it, angled so that anyone tracking her couldn't miss it.

I held it and the canyon held its breath and I tried to think like a ranger instead of like a man.

A flare is a message. That's the whole point of flares — they're language compressed into fire. And this one said several things at once, depending on how you read it. Read one: *I know you're following me.* Read two: *I'm not hiding, I'm choosing.* Read three, the one that sat coldest in my stomach: *I could have burned your position to the sky tonight, called every gun on this river down onto your sleeping convoy, and I didn't.*

Because that's what a spent flare would have done. Fired from the wash, it would have lit our camp like a stage, shown anyone watching — anyone tending that fire upriver — exactly where forty tons of resupply lay undefended. She'd carried the flare out of the water, through the reeds, up the wash, and chosen not to use it. Chosen to leave it for me instead, like a note pinned to a door.

*I could hurt you. I'm telling you I won't. Ask yourself who would.*

Hope, which had been a bird hitting glass, now sharpened into something with edges. Because the arithmetic was ugly and simple: if Ilsa Vey was warning me off, then there was something on this river worth being warned off of. If she was telling me she wasn't my enemy, then my enemy was someone else — someone whose fire burned upstream of my convoy, someone who had answered Tam's radio once in a woman's voice to say only *turn back*.

Unless the voice had been hers. Unless the flare was misdirection from the same woman who'd whispered a warning down a dead channel, playing both ends of the river against the middle. I could build that story too. Three years of practice had taught me I could build any story about Ilsa Vey I liked, given enough dark and enough wanting.

I stood there longer than a professional should have, turning the flare in my fingers, feeling the scratched V catch the pad of my thumb like a small insistent heartbeat.

Then I pocketed it, marked the slab with a scratch of my own — a straight line, ranger shorthand for *found, noted, moving on* — and turned back toward camp at a jog, because dawn was coming and with it decisions I'd need to make sober, in front of witnesses, wearing my badge.

Behind me the river kept everything it keeps: her prints filling slowly with seep water, the fire upriver burning its patient orange, and the shape of a woman folded into a crack in the world, watching me go, or not watching, or already a mile gone.

At the edge of the reeds I stopped once, because there was one more thing the moon had been saving. Fresh prints crossing mine where I'd passed twenty minutes earlier — her prints, heading back toward the water, laid over my own outbound trail like a signature over a letter.

She'd circled back. She'd stood where I'd stood, read my line in the silt the way I'd read hers, and gone down to the river unhurried, at a walk.

Two people who knew each other's stride better than their own faces, trading footprints in the dark because neither could manage words.

Upstream, around the bend, the fire flickered — and then, deliberate as a wink, it went out.
