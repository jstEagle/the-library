## 5. The Drowned City

I had been awake for three days when the towers finally came out of the water, and I remember thinking, with the particular clarity of the sleep-starved, that I had never seen anything so beautiful or so much like a graveyard.

The canyon opened around a bend like a throat deciding to swallow. One moment we were poling through brown water between walls of rock streaked white with old flood lines; the next, the walls fell away and there they were — spires of pre-collapse concrete rising forty, sixty, eighty feet out of the river, their upper floors furred with moss and gull nests, their lower stories drowned so long ago that the water had made peace with them. Boats could pass through second-story windows. Fish schooled in what had once been a municipal courthouse. A crane arm still jutted from one tower's flank, rusted to the color of dried blood, frozen mid-lift over cargo no one would ever unload.

"God," said Tam, beside me on the lead skiff, radio handset hanging forgotten against his chest. He was nineteen and had spent the entire trip upriver talking — to me, to the river, to his radio, to God — and now he had nothing. "It's real."

"It was always real," I said. "That was rather the point."

I am Dr. Amara Sen, chief of the Meridian Expedition, and for twenty-two days before that morning I had commanded eleven exhausted people guarding a hole in the ground with everything I had left. But I'm getting ahead of myself. That morning I was still crouched in the watch post we'd rigged on the fourth floor of the customs tower, peering through a gap in the shutters with a pair of binoculars held together with wire, watching three supply skiffs come up the channel flying Fort Calloway pennants.

Relief is a strange animal. It doesn't arrive clean. It arrives mixed with suspicion, exhaustion, and the low animal knowledge that anything that looks like rescue might be something else wearing rescue's clothes.

"Count them," said Doran Hale behind me. He'd materialized the way he always did, silent on a floor that groaned under anyone else's weight. Our security chief was a big man gone lean on ration stress, gray at the temples, hands that looked like they'd done work no university ever taught. He'd told us he'd run river security for a mining concern before signing on with the expedition. In three weeks I had learned not to ask him questions he answered too smoothly.

"Three skiffs," I said. "Maybe fifteen people. Cargo tarped."

"Fifteen." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *fifteen problems*. "And Calloway sent them through the narrows this late in the season? The monsoon's—"

"I know when the monsoon is, Doran."

He didn't apologize. He never apologized; he just recalibrated, like a man adjusting instruments. "Someone wanted this convoy here badly enough to risk it. Question is who, and why now."

That was the question that had kept me awake more than the dead did. We had sent our last transmission requesting resupply six weeks ago, before the vault, before everything. Since then: silence from us by choice, silence from them inexplicably. And now, unbidden, an armed convoy comes up a dying river into a drowned city where, as far as anyone at Fort Calloway officially knew, nothing had happened at all.

*They know something,* I thought, watching the lead skiff's wake unspool behind it. *Or someone does.*

* * *

Getting down to the water took time, because getting anywhere in the High Wreck took time. Our camp was strung between four towers on catwalks and cable ferries we'd built in the first week, when we realized the floodwater between buildings was deep enough to drown a man and slow enough to grow things in it. Algae mats. Leeches. Once, God help me, a snake the length of a canoe, which Ovidio swore was harmless and which I have chosen to believe was harmless because the alternative meant it was living near my bunk.

By the time I reached the landing stage — a raft platform lashed to the flooded loading dock of what had been a riverfront hotel — the convoy was idling fifty meters out, engines down to a mutter. Cautious. Good. Cautious meant someone over there had read the water correctly, and the water in the Wreck had teeth: submerged fences, rebar thickets, the occasional floating corpse of a building section that would gut a hull.

I counted heads again. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Rangers' green on two of them — actual desert rangers, which surprised me; Calloway didn't spend rangers on supply runs. The rest were contract boatmen and laborers, and one slight figure at the radio rig on the lead skiff who couldn't have been older than my graduate students.

"Hoy the camp!" The call came across the water in a voice trained to carry — a ranger's voice, low and rough. "Meridian Expedition! This is Ranger Ansel Okafor, Fort Calloway escort. We've got your resupply. Requesting approach to your landing!"

Resupply. Two words, and every person on my landing stage went still. Twenty-two days of short rations and longer nights, and here was the sentence we'd been starving toward.

Beside me, Priya Nair — our archivist, sharp as a blade and twice as likely to cut you — let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Thank God. Thank *God*, Amara."

"Yes," I said. And then, because I am apparently incapable of gratitude without audit: "Hold positions. Nobody boards until I say."

Priya stared at me. "You're joking."

"I'm a scientist. Joking is above my pay grade." I raised my own voice across the water. "Ranger Okafor! Amara Sen, expedition chief. You're welcome here — but I'll be plain with you, because the river's already plain enough. My people are frightened, we've had deaths, and I don't know yet who I can trust. Before you land: your weapons stay aboard. All of them. Yours included."

The pause that followed lasted long enough for me to hear the water slapping the pilings and Priya's disapproving breathing.

Then the ranger's voice again, unhurried: "Understood, Doctor. Weapons stay aboard. We're coming in slow."

*Just like that.* No argument, no negotiation, no wounded pride. I filed that away. Men who gave up their guns that easily either had nothing to hide or were very confident about the ones you couldn't see.

* * *

They came alongside one skiff at a time, and I watched them the way I watched everything now — which is to say, looking for the lie in it.

The ranger in charge came off the gangplank first. Tall, dark-skinned, desert-weathered to leather, moving with the particular economy of a man who had learned that every wasted motion is a motion you might need back later. He stopped at the edge of my platform, took in the camp strung overhead — the cables, the watch posts, the laundry line where our last intact uniforms hung like surrender flags — and something crossed his face that wasn't surprise. Recognition, maybe. Of a kind.

"Dr. Sen." He offered no hand. Smart. "Ansel Okafor. Fort Calloway assigned me to escort your convoy up. We've got grain, fuel cells, medical stores, radio parts—" he glanced at the slight young man wrestling crates down the plank, "—and a lot of questions nobody at the fort would answer."

"How poetic," I said. "We have answers nobody here will enjoy giving."

His eyes moved over my people. I knew what he saw: eleven gaunt figures in patched expedition gear, armed with a museum's worth of scavenged tools, standing on a drowned dock under a crane that hadn't worked since before his grandfather was born. He saw the graves, too. We'd had to put them somewhere, and the only dry ground was the old hotel roof garden, so we'd buried our dead among ornamental hibiscus that had gone feral decades ago. Two mounds. Fresh.

"Deaths," he said quietly. Not a question. He'd read the garden the way I'd read his skiffs.

"Accidents," I said, and heard how the word sounded, and hated it.

"Sure," said Okafor, in a tone that agreed with nothing.

Behind him, his people were unloading under Hale's supervision — and I caught the moment when Doran Hale and Ansel Okafor looked at each other for the first time. It was brief. It was polite. It was two professionals taking each other's measure, and I have spent thirty years reading faces in lecture halls and dig sites, and I will tell you what I saw: Hale smiled like a man meeting a colleague. Okafor smiled like a man meeting a locked door.

*Later,* I told myself. *One crisis at a time.*

* * *

We got the cargo up to the main platform by midmorning, and I will confess that watching my people eat fresh bread — actual bread, leavened, baked at Fort Calloway and somehow not entirely stale — was the closest I have come to religious experience since I stopped believing in anything worth believing. Ovidio cried a little and pretended it was the wind. Priya ate her share and half of mine while lecturing me about caloric deficits. Even the defector-adjacent tension in camp loosened its grip for an hour.

For an hour.

Then Ranger Okafor finished his circuit of the camp — and he had made a circuit, I noticed, casual as a tourist, touching nothing, missing nothing — and found me on the observation deck of the archive tower, where I went to think and to count the boats on the river and to not sleep.

"You want to tell me about those accidents?" he asked. No preamble. Rangers, I was learning, considered preambles a waste of daylight.

"I want to tell you that you're a guest, Ranger, and guests get fed, not debriefed."

"Ma'am, I've been shot at on three rivers and lied to by four governments. You're doing both at once and only managing one." He leaned on the rail beside me, gazing out at the drowned skyline with what looked like genuine feeling. "It's a hell of a place. How long has the expedition been down here?"

"Fourteen months. Survey started eighteen months before that."

"And the fort lost contact when?"

"We went quiet six weeks ago. Deliberately."

That got his full attention. He turned, and I saw the intelligence in him plainly now, banked but enormous, like a fire in a stove. "Deliberately. Why would an expedition that needed resupply go dark?"

*Because we found something,* I thought. *Because two of my people are dead. Because I don't know if the killer is still standing in my camp, smiling at your boatmen.*

What I said was: "Because we found something."

He waited. He was good at waiting. I understood suddenly that this was a man who had built an entire life out of waiting — on trails, on suspects, maybe on something else entirely, some absence he carried the way I carried mine.

"Dr. Sen," he said at last, "I was ordered to protect this convoy and whatever it finds. Lady Serevan herself signed the orders. She also paid me double to keep my mouth shut about what I see down here." His jaw tightened. "So understand me: I'm not your enemy. But I've been paid by the same family whose charter you're working under, and I don't like not knowing what I'm standing inside of. Give me something true."

There it was. The name. Serevan. Spoken by her own hired ranger with the careful neutrality of a man handling something venomous.

I looked out at the water. Somewhere beneath that brown surface lay streets, offices, archives — a whole civilization's paperwork, drowned and preserved, patient as sediment. Somewhere below my feet, behind a bulkhead door we'd sealed with our own hands, lay the thing we had found. The thing people had died for. The thing I had not yet decided whether to trust to anyone — least of all a man on Serevan's payroll, however honest his eyes.

"The accidents," I said slowly, "were not accidents. Both victims were killed with a blade most people have never seen outside a ranger armory. Narrow profile, single-edged, angled entry. My security chief says bandits. Bandits don't climb forty feet of tower at night past three sentries."

Okafor had gone very still. "Show me the wounds."

"There's nothing left to show. River burials. But I described them to a field surgeon's manual and the description matches military close-work." I turned to face him fully. "Here is my situation, Ranger, and I'll give it to you straight because you asked for true. Eleven survivors. One killer, possibly still among us. A discovery downstairs that certain families would burn this whole canyon to bury. And now an armed convoy arrives, uninvited, six weeks after we went silent, sent by the patron whose family name is written all over the documents I'm trying to protect. So yes. Your weapons stayed aboard. And they'll stay aboard until I know whether you're the rescue or the second wave."

The silence between us stretched. Below us, the camp went about its business — crates hauled, bread distributed, Tam the radio boy already elbow-deep in our dead transmitter with the reverence of a priest. On the river, light moved across the water and lit the drowned windows gold, floor after floor, like the city turning its lights on for company after eighty years alone.

"You think one of mine did it," Okafor said at last. Not accusing. Testing.

"I think someone with ranger training did it. And you're the first ranger I've met in three weeks."

"That's fair," he said. And then, to my lasting surprise: "I'd want the same, in your place. Keep the weapons aboard. Keep me close, though — closer than comfortable. If it's ranger work, you want a ranger looking at it. I'll start with your dead man's effects, if you'll allow it."

"You'll start tomorrow," I said. "Tonight you eat, and my people sleep knowing the bread isn't a dream."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was something underneath it, some grief worn smooth as river stone, and I recognized it because I owned several myself.

"One condition of mine, Doctor." He nodded toward the archive tower's base, toward the sealed bulkhead none of his people had been within fifty meters of. "Whatever you found down there — whoever it threatens — somebody at Fort Calloway already knows it exists. Convoys don't get sent blind into monsoon country. Somebody paid for this trip before you ever went quiet. When you're ready to talk about what's in that vault, I'd rather hear it from you than find out the hard way."

*I'll bet you would,* I thought. And beneath the thought, colder: *And so would she.*

That night I stood my usual watch on the customs tower and watched the convoy's cookfire glow on the landing stage, and I made the decision I had been circling for three weeks: I would show him. Not everything. Not yet. But the shape of it — because a man who surrenders his weapons without blinking, who reads graves off a rooftop garden, who asks for truth like it's rations — that man was either my best ally or my most dangerous problem, and either way I could not afford to leave him guessing.

Below me, at the edge of the firelight, I saw Doran Hale standing apart from the others, looking north up the canyon, toward the narrows, toward Calloway, toward whatever was coming down that river that only he seemed to expect.

He was smiling.

I had known Doran Hale for fourteen months, shared rations and storms and the deaths of friends with him, and in all that time I had never once seen him smile at nothing.

I went down to warn the others.
