## 6. Terms of Survival

The standoff lasted long enough for me to count the survivors' rifles twice and arrive at the same unwelcome number both times.

Eleven of them on the rope bridge and the lower catwalk, spread out with a discipline that no academic field team should have. Eleven rifles against our nine, plus whatever was behind them in the camp strung between the drowned towers — and they had the high ground, the water, and three weeks of something bad living in their eyes. Our side had Tam, who was seventeen and holding his radio like a shield, and four drivers who were paid by the mile, not the firefight.

"Nobody fires," said the woman at the center of the bridge.

Dr. Amara Sen was not what I'd expected from an expedition chief who'd gone silent for a month. I'd pictured someone hollowed out, half-mad with river fever. Instead she came down the catwalk steps with her hands visible and her voice level, a small woman in a patched survey jacket, gray at the temples, moving like someone who had decided exactly how much fear she could afford and spent none of it.

"You're Okafor," she said, when I'd waded the last stretch to the gravel shelf below the camp. "Fort Calloway's manifest says you're escorting resupply. The manifest also says eleven personnel. I count nine."

"Two turned back at the narrows. Fever."

"Fever." She looked at me the way you look at a receipt that doesn't add up. "Convenient season for it."

"It usually is."

Behind her, the camp told its own story if you knew how to read it. Tarps lashed down against weather that hadn't come yet. Cooking smoke thin — rationing. A signal mast stripped bare, every wire coiled and hung like laundry. And everywhere, everywhere, the look of people who had stopped trusting the person standing next to them. I'd seen it before, in border posts after a traitor was found, in mining camps after a strikebreaker was strung up. It has a smell. Fear and woodsmoke and not quite enough food.

"We brought grain, medical stores, fuel cells, ammunition," I said. "And orders to establish comms relay back downriver."

"And I have terms before any of that gets distributed." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to; her people had gone still around her, listening the way soldiers listen. "No one leaves this camp. Not your convoy, not my expedition. Nobody goes downriver until I know what happened to Rennick and Cole."

* * *

She laid it out in her command tent, which was really a survey shelter pitched on a platform welded between two drowned towers, forty feet above black water. Through the canvas wall I could hear the river working at the old city's bones, patient as a debt collector.

Sen spread a hand-drawn map of the dig site on her table. Three weeks ago, she said, a dive team working the flooded sublevels of the customs house had found a sealed chamber. Archive Vault, they were calling it. Pre-collapse municipal records, administrative seals, land documentation — thousands of documents preserved in anaerobic silt.

"You understand what that means," she said. "A complete paper record of the settlement era. Charters, grants, surveys. The founding documents of every family claim on this territory."

"I understand it means history."

"I understand it means dynamite," she said flatly. "But yes. History too."

Nine days after the vault was opened, a man named Rennick went down to check a mooring line at night and didn't come back. They found him at dawn snagged in the reeds downstream. Drowning, the verdict was. Except Rennick had swum competitively, and there was water in his lungs but bruising on his throat that Sen herself had documented and then been talked into softening, because the expedition couldn't afford panic and the patron's envoy — more on him later, I already suspected — had strongly suggested accidents happen on rivers.

Then, six days ago, Cole. Fell from the catwalk during a storm. Except the rail he fell from showed fresh cuts in the welds, and Cole was the expedition's best climber, and he'd been found with his hands torn open from trying to hold onto something that wasn't there anymore.

"Two dead in fifteen days," Sen said. "After three years without a single fatality. And since the deaths, my own people won't sleep in the same shelter two nights running. They rotate. Like animals who can smell something circling."

"You think it's one of yours."

"I think whoever did it knew our routines, our watch rotations, which mooring lines sag and which catwalk rails are load-bearing. That's not a stranger. That's family." She pressed her palms flat on the table. "So here are my terms, Ranger. Your convoy's weapons come under joint guard. Your radios stay off until I say otherwise — because if word of the vault goes out on an open channel, we may all die for it. And nobody leaves. When I know who killed my people, you can carry your grain home and tell Fort Calloway whatever keeps you employed."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can explain to Lady Serevan why her investment is sitting in a canyon guarded by corpses." Something moved behind her eyes when she said Serevan's name — not gratitude. Obligation, maybe, worn like wet rope. "I know why you're really here, Ranger. I'm not stupid. A ranger detachment doesn't get assigned to a supply run because the families care whether academics eat well. You're here because of what we found. So let's stop pretending we're on opposite sides of anything except the truth."

I gave her the terms. What else was there? Nine rifles against eleven, on her ground, over her dead. But I made her take them with my eyes open, and I watched hers while I did it, and hers stayed open too. I've trusted worse people on thinner ground.

* * *

They took me to see the bodies.

Not because I asked — though I would have — but because Sen wanted a professional eye, and I was the closest thing to law enforcement within four hundred miles. Rennick lay in a tarp-lined storage locker chilled by river water piped through coils, which is a clever trick I intend to steal if I live long enough. Cole was beside him under a second tarp, and nobody had washed either body, which told me Sen had been waiting for someone like me.

Rennick first. Big man, diver's shoulders, thirty-some years old. Bruising across the throat, consistent with a forearm bar or a garrote attempt that failed and became drowning. Defensive marks on his forearms. And then, low on his right side, mostly hidden where the bloat had begun —

Three wounds. Narrow, deep, slightly downward-angled, close together.

I stood over him for a long time.

"Problem?" Sen asked.

"Maybe." I pulled on a glove and touched the edge of the nearest wound. Clean entry. No tearing. A blade ground narrow and stiff, driven with a short punch rather than a slash. "What kind of knives does your expedition carry?"

"Survey blades. Utility work. Why?"

"Because these weren't made by a utility blade." I straightened up. "There's a pattern. Three-point cluster, low ribs, angled toward the liver. It's taught. It's taught in exactly one curriculum I know of — the ranger close-quarters course at Calloway, module four, sentry removal. We call it the tailor's tack, because you leave three stitches." I heard my own voice go flat and distant, the way it does when my mouth is buying time for my stomach. "It's not in any civilian manual. It's not in military manuals either. Rangers keep it proprietary because it's ugly and it works."

Sen was quiet a moment. "You're saying a ranger killed my diver."

"I'm saying somebody trained as one did. Or trained by one." I moved to Cole's body and lifted the tarp. The fall had done its work — broken collarbone, shattered hip — but there, along the jaw, a small cut I might have missed if I hadn't been looking. Shallow. A warning cut, or a finishing stroke that flinched. Same blade geometry. Same economy.

Three years I'd been reading trails for one particular set of habits. Boot gait favoring the left knee on descents. Knots tied left-over-right. And blade work — I'd seen what Ilsa Vey could do with a knife, once, in a training yard at Calloway, before she was my partner, back when we were both young enough to think competence was character. She'd learned the tailor's tack from the same instructors I had. She'd been better at it than me.

My chest did something complicated that I filed away to examine never.

"There's a member of your vault detail," I said carefully, "who isn't on your roster. My drivers mentioned a woman they saw on the catwalks last night. Tall, dark hair, moves like she owns the ground."

Something flickered across Sen's face — recognition, and behind it, guilt. "Vey," she said. "Ilsa Vey. She came to us eight months ago with survey credentials and a reference I couldn't verify and didn't press hard enough on. She's good. She's been running the vault catalog from day one." Her jaw tightened. "You know her."

"I know her."

"Then you'll want to know she hasn't left the vault detail since Cole died. Hasn't slept more than two hours at a stretch. Whatever else she is, she's been guarding those documents like a mother wolf." Sen met my eyes. "Does that make her innocent or just careful?"

"With rangers?" I said. "Careful is how the guilty ones survive."

* * *

I walked the camp after that, doing what I do, which is counting things until the things confess.

Counted the mooring lines: two replaced recently, splices fresh. Counted the catwalk rail Cole fell from: the cut welds had been filed smooth afterward, which meant patience, which meant premeditation, which meant the killer had come back to tidy up a scene in a storm, which takes nerve or madness. Counted the watch rotations posted on the mess board and noticed they'd been rewritten in the last week — new pairings, strangers paired together, trust rationed like the food.

Tam found me at the rail overlooking the customs house, where the vault entrance yawned at the waterline, a black rectangle breathing cold air. He'd carried a crate of medical stores up three platforms without being asked and now hovered beside me with the specific agony of a seventeen-year-old who has a question and knows it's a stupid one.

"Sir? The woman. Vey. Is she a prisoner here?"

"Why?"

"The others talk about her like one. But she stands watches nobody assigns her. And yesterday I saw her at the water checking a line, and she checked it the way you check things, sir. Left hand, then right, tug twice." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Like the way you checked our raft lines coming upriver."

Kids notice everything except when to shut up. I put a hand on his shoulder, which was becoming a habit I'd have to answer for eventually.

"Get some sleep, Tam."

"Yes sir." He got four steps away before turning back. "Sir — if somebody here killed those men, and we're all locked in together until she figures it out—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. The math finished itself: a killer locked in a box with us, with the monsoon building somewhere north, with nowhere for anyone to run except into black water.

That night I sat my own watch on the highest platform, above the camp, where I could see everything and be seen by nothing. The canyon held its breath around the drowned towers. Somewhere below, in a sealed vault full of paper that could unmake a nation, Ilsa Vey was awake — I'd bet the pension I wouldn't live to collect on it — keeping her own kind of watch.

Two dead men downstairs wearing my profession's signature. A partner-turned-thief guarding the evidence she'd stolen, or hiding among the people she'd helped kill, and I genuinely could not yet tell you which. A patron upriver whose money smelled like a leash. And a saboteur, patient and skilled, sleeping somewhere within fifty feet of me.

Sen thought she was protecting her people by locking the gates.

She was right about one thing, though. Nobody was leaving.

Around midnight, a shape crossed the lower catwalk — quick, quiet, left-knee-favoring, heading for the vault entrance with a lantern shuttered to a thread. I didn't follow. Not yet. I watched her go, and I made myself a promise shaped like a question:

Tomorrow, Ilsa Vey and I were going to have a conversation three years overdue, and only one of us was going to walk away from it free.

I just hadn't decided which one.
