## 7. Ilsa

Three years is a long time to rehearse a reunion. I'd done it in salt flats and river camps and the inside of a drowned archive where the only audience was a dead surveyor's ghost. In most versions I was witty. In a few I was cruel. In one — my favorite, the one I returned to on bad nights — I said nothing at all, just handed him the documents and let them do the talking.

In none of the versions did I step out of a shadow in front of eleven armed strangers with mud to my knees and say, "Hello, Ansel," in a voice that cracked like green wood.

But that's what happened.

The standoff at the camp perimeter had gone on for the better part of an hour — Dr. Sen's people with their scavenged rifles, Ansel's convoy with their ranger-issue ones, and between them the flooded street of a city that had been dead for two centuries and apparently couldn't resist one more corpse. Amara Sen had demanded their weapons. Ansel had refused. Tam, the radio kid, was doing that thing young people do when they're terrified and trying to look operational, which is holding a clipboard.

And I was standing behind a collapsed colonnade thinking, *well. Sooner or later.*

I chose sooner, mostly because my leg had gone numb and partly because I've never been good at waiting for a thing I dread. That's how I got into this line of work, if you want the honest biography. Not ideology first. Impatience.

"Hello, Ansel."

The sound he made wasn't a word. It was the noise a man makes when a floor gives way — that half-second of freefall before the body understands it has to start swimming. His hand went to his sidearm, which was correct procedure, and then stopped, which was not.

"You're dead," he said.

"I get that a lot lately." I stepped fully into the gray dawn light so he could see all of me — the scarred jaw, the shorter hair, the ranger's emergency flare still clipped to my belt because some jokes you keep even when they stop being funny. "Mostly by people who wanted me dead. You I expected better from."

"Ilsa." He said it the way he used to say it across campfires, back when we were partners and the worst thing either of us stole was sleep. Then his face did something complicated — grief and fury fighting for the same real estate — and settled, as it always did, on duty. "You're under arrest."

"There he is," I said to Sen's people. "Told you. Three years, and that's still the whole speech."

* * *

Here's what the camp knew about me before that moment: nothing useful. I'd come upriver eight months ago under a different name, with forged survey credentials and a genuine talent for reading pre-collapse hydrology, and I'd joined the Meridian Expedition as a site technician. I was good at it. I liked it. For about five weeks I almost forgot I was lying for a living.

Then we found the Vault.

After that, everything I'd built — the false name, the false history, the small warm friendships with people like Odile in the mapping tent who taught me card games — became scaffolding around a single purpose. And purposes like that don't leave room for card games.

Sen didn't know who I was. Nobody did, except now Ansel, whose face was doing arithmetic I could watch: *the thief from the salt flats, the trail signature, three years of chasing, and she's here, she's been here, she led us here without knowing it.*

Actually, I hadn't led him anywhere. The river had. But I'll take credit; nobody else is offering any.

He walked me — not arrested, not yet, though his hand never left the holster strap — to the edge of the camp, past the sandbagged approach to the Archive Vault, to a stretch of drowned boulevard where a stone balustrade ran out into black water and the towers stood up out of the flood like the ribs of something that had swallowed a civilization.

"Talk," he said.

"That's my line. I've been practicing."

"Ilsa."

"Fine." I sat on the balustrade, which was stupid, because if he decided to arrest me I'd have to stand up again and my knee would complain. "You want to know why I vanished. You want to know why I let you chase me across half a territory for three years instead of writing you a letter. And you want to know what's down there—" I nodded at the vault entrance, at the two exhausted academics guarding it with rifles older than they were, "—that's worth all of it."

"I know what's worth it," he said. "Whatever you stole."

"And there it is." I laughed, and it came out bitter, which I hadn't planned. "Ansel Okafor. Best tracker in the southern reaches. Reads a trail like scripture. And after three years he still thinks I'm a thief."

"Aren't you?"

"Yes," I said. "Just not of what you think."

* * *

Let me tell you what's in the vault, since no one's told you yet and you've been patient.

When the old world came apart — and it came apart slowly, the way rot does, not the way fire does — the great families didn't just survive it. They *wrote* it. They wrote the peace, the borders, the charters, the whole legal skeleton we all live inside now. Every acre of the territory is held under family charter. Every charter traces its authority back to land grants supposedly issued in the last days of the collapse, grants that legitimize everything above them. Your fort. My gallows, if they ever catch me. The road you patrol. All of it stands on paper signed two hundred years ago.

Except it isn't two hundred years old.

The Meridian Expedition found a sealed records annex — a surveyor's office, pre-collapse, flooded but intact, the paper preserved in cold anoxic water like fruit in syrup. And in it, the working files of a man named Ede Voss, senior territorial surveyor, who spent the years after the collapse watching the families manufacture their own legitimacy. Draft charters. Ink analysis in Voss's own hand — he tested them, Ansel, he *tested* them, iron-gall pigment on paper stock that didn't exist until forty years after the dates on the signatures. And then the thing that made me sit down on a wet floor and laugh until Odile thought I'd lost my mind:

Voss's confession. Signed. Dated. He helped forge six of the nine great charters — he says so, in his own words, with his own hand — because the alternative was his family starving, and then he spent the rest of his life documenting exactly what he'd done, sealed it all in tin, and left it for whoever came after. A dead man's apology, addressed to the future.

We're the future, Ansel. We're who he wrote to.

"So the charters are forged," Ansel said, when I finished. He'd listened without moving, which I remembered about him — the patience, the terrible patience. "That's your evidence."

"That's the evidence. Singular. There's one set of originals, waterproofed, catalogued, sitting in that vault. And copies that I made, because I'm not an idiot, and distributed to—" I stopped myself. Old habit. "To safe places."

"To people."

"To people," I admitted.

"And the thefts. The three years. The warrants." His voice stayed level but I could hear the load-bearing strain in it, the way you hear ice creak. "The Serevan assay office. The Calloway records annex. The magistrate's strongroom at Redford. That was you. Stealing proof."

"Stealing proof," I agreed. "Badly, twice. The Redford job was a disaster, if it helps."

"It doesn't help."

"No," I said. "It wouldn't."

* * *

Here's the part I hadn't rehearsed well enough, because rehearsal can't prepare you for a man looking at you like that.

"Why not tell me?" he asked. "Three years ago. Before you ran. You could have—"

"Could have what? Shown you a stolen document and asked you to commit treason?" I stood up, and my knee complained, and I ignored it. "You'd have done your duty, Ansel. It's the best thing about you and it's the reason I couldn't stay. You'd have arrested me. You'd have hated yourself while you did it, and you'd have done it anyway, and I'd have spent the rest of my life in a family prison while the papers rotted in an evidence locker and the Serevans of the world went on owning the ground under everyone's feet. I ran because the only other option was making you choose. And I loved you too much to make you choose."

The word landed between us like a dropped tool. I watched him decide whether to pick it up.

"You loved me," he said finally, "and you let me spend three years thinking you were dead. Or worse."

"Worse was likelier. Dead was kinder."

"That's not—" He turned away, looked at the water, at the towers, anywhere but me. "Do you know what I did? After? I worked your trail for fourteen months. Fourteen months, Ilsa. Salt flats, border towns, the ferry at Kess. I told myself it was duty. Everyone told me it was duty. And every mile I told myself the same lie, that I was chasing a fugitive, when what I was actually doing was—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Every mile was a letter I couldn't send."

I had rehearsed cruelty for this moment. I had rehearsed wit. I had not rehearsed the truth arriving on its own schedule, the way it does, uninvited and punctual.

"I read your reports," I said quietly. "The ones that went public. You described my gait once — weight forward, favoring the left on long descents. Do you know what that means? It means you knew my body better than anyone alive, and you put it in an official document so other men could hunt it." I laughed, and it broke in the middle. "I slept with that report under my bed for a year. Like a love letter. I'm aware of how that sounds."

"How does it sound to you?"

"Like two people who were very good at their jobs and very bad at everything else."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he used to across a morning fire, before either of us had anything to hide except our feelings — and for a moment neither of us was a ranger or a thief or a traitor or a patriot. We were just tired, in a drowned city, with a storm building somewhere over the canyon rim and killers probably already on the river.

"I'm still going to have to arrest you," he said, but his hand hadn't moved toward the cuffs, and we both heard what that meant.

"Not yet," I said. "Not today. Because here's your problem, partner: you can't arrest me without taking the evidence into custody. And the moment the evidence goes into official custody, it goes up the chain — to Fort Calloway, to the territorial magistrates, to Lady Serevan's friends, to every family whose charter burns the day those documents go public. Your oath is a fine thing, Ansel. But it's stapled to their forgery. Arrest me and you bury Voss a second time."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is also a document somebody wrote, and I've seen the drafts."

* * *

We stood there a while longer, saying nothing, which is a language we'd always been fluent in. Below us the flood moved slow and brown through the streets of the dead city, carrying somebody's supply crate and a flowering branch and, presumably, the occasional secret nobody wanted kept.

It was Tam who broke it — the radio kid, coming along the balustrade at a jog, clipboard and all, with the particular urgency of someone who hasn't yet learned which news keeps.

"Dr. Sen wants everyone at the central tent," he said. "Both parties. She says—" He glanced at me, uncomfortable. "She says given recent revelations, the expedition's security arrangements need review. Also there's a skiff coming upriver. Flying no colors. They're asking for Lady Serevan's interests by name."

Ansel and I looked at each other.

"Serevan," he said.

"They always know," I said. "That's the thing people never understand about the families. They're not rich because they own land. They own land because they're rich in knowing. Someone told them what we found before we found it." I pushed off the balustrade and started toward the central tent, and after a moment — I felt it more than saw it, the way you feel weather change — he fell into step beside me. Close enough to grab me. Close enough, maybe, to mean something else.

"For the record," he said quietly, "I'm not done being angry at you."

"Good," I said. "Anger looks good on you. Always did. Just aim it at the right people this time."

Behind us, far down the canyon, I heard the first engine note of the skiff, thin and patient as a wasp finding a window.
