## 8. The Saboteur's Trail

I've read a lot of dead men in my time. It sounds like a grim joke when I say it that way, but a ranger learns to treat a corpse as a document — the most honest one you'll ever get, because the dead can't lie about where they've been. The river had kept Dr. Pellam Osei for eleven days before it gave him back, and even waterlogged and bloated, he still had plenty to say.

I sat with him on a tarp outside the medical tent while the morning fog burned off the floodwater, and I listened.

The wound was in his left flank, angled upward and inward. One thrust. Whoever did it knew exactly where to put a blade and how deep to push — not a slashing panic, not a fumble in the dark, but the kind of economical violence you learn doing close work on boats, where there's no room for a wide swing and no time for a second try. I turned the body gently and found the entry point clean, no hesitation marks. Somebody had knelt beside this man, maybe spoken to him, maybe helped him up from a crouch on a wet deck — and then put a knife into him with the precision of a man gutting fish.

Academics don't kill like that. Archivists don't kill like that. I've spent three years reading trails left by people who do.

* * *

Sen gave me the run of the camp, which was either trust or desperation, and by the second day I'd stopped trying to tell the difference. She was running an expedition on fumes — two dead, morale like wet paper, and a convoy of armed strangers camped in her living room. She walked me through the deaths herself, because she wanted me to see that she had nothing to hide.

"Pellam first," she said, standing at the rail of the walkway strung between two drowned towers. Below us, the brown water slid past the upper windows of a building that had been a bank once, or a temple, hard to say. "He went down to check a mooring line at night. We found the line cut the next morning, and him three days later, six miles downstream."

"And the second?"

"Yusuf Kade." Her voice went flat in the way voices go flat when someone is holding grief down with both hands. "Fell from the survey platform during a storm. Except Yusuf had climbed that platform four hundred times, and his harness clip was found unseated. He always double-checked. Always."

"Somebody opened it."

"Somebody who could reach it without being seen." She looked at me then, and her eyes were red-rimmed but level. "Ranger Okafor, I've spent twenty years digging holes with people I'd trust with my life. I don't believe one of them is a murderer. Convince me otherwise."

"I'll try," I said. "But I'd rather convince you than arrest somebody."

"Then start with what you know."

What I knew filled three pages of field notes by the second evening. The mooring line hadn't been cut — it had been sawed partway through over several days, a patient job done in increments so the fraying would look natural. The knife work on Osei said military or paramilitary training, river theater specifically: a low crouch thrust, delivered from below the victim's sightline, the kind of technique taught to boarding parties and riverine interdiction units. And the harness — I examined the clip myself — had been worked open with a thin tool, carefully, by someone who understood rigging well enough to know which failure would look like user error.

Add it up and you got a profile: trained killer, boat handler, patient, physically capable of moving around a camp built on ropes and walkways without anyone hearing him. That narrowed things considerably, because the Meridian Expedition was forty-one souls, and thirty-eight of them were people who got excited about pottery sherds.

* * *

I started with the credentials, because credentials are where liars live.

Every expedition member's file was in Sen's records tent — thin paper dossiers, funding-era paperwork, the usual academic archaeology of a person reduced to their publications. I went through them the way I go through a suspected poacher's gear: looking not for what's there but for what isn't.

Most of the files were fat with detail. Field schools, co-authored papers, grant applications with the desperate hopeful tone of people begging institutions for table scraps. Tam, the kid radio operator riding with my convoy, would have fit right in.

Doran Hale's file was nine pages long and told me almost nothing.

Security consultant. Previous contracts listed as a security consultancy in the coastal cities, references given as initials and a comm code that routed to a dead channel. No field school, obviously — security men don't need them — but also no unit history, no service record, nothing verifiable at all. In my world, a man with no paper trail is a man who paid good money to have one erased, and nobody spends that kind of money unless they're hiding from somebody.

And there was one more thing. I laid out the expedition's personnel roster next to Hale's contract dates and did the arithmetic every ranger learns to do: Hale had joined the Meridian eight weeks before departure. Late. Every other core member had been with Sen for a year or more. He'd arrived late, credentialed thinly, and taken charge of security — which meant weapons storage, watch rotations, and access to every locked space in the camp.

Including, I noted, the vault detail's equipment lockers.

I didn't like where the arithmetic pointed. I liked it less because I'd spent three evenings eating dinner within arm's reach of the man.

* * *

Hale was easy to find because Doran Hale was always exactly where he said he'd be, doing exactly what he claimed to be doing, with the unhurried competence of a man who has never once scrambled to cover a lie. That was the thing about him that kept snagging in my brain. Guilty people develop texture. They get busy, evasive, helpful in weird directions. Hale just was — solid, gray-bearded, dry-humored, checking the tie-downs on the supply rafts as if the end of the world were a scheduling problem.

"Ranger." He nodded at me without straightening up from a knot he was testing. "Come to help, or come to suspect? You've got the walk of a man doing the second one. No offense meant."

"No offense taken. Which answer would you give me?"

"The true one, same as always." He finished the knot, stood, and wiped his hands on his thighs. Up close he was bigger than he seemed at a distance — heavy through the shoulders, the build of a man who'd carried loads on boats for years. "You're investigating Osei and Kade. Sen told the senior staff this morning. Fair enough. What do you want from me?"

"Your service history."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt, I decided later. Calculation. The difference matters; I've seen both, and they wear different faces.

"Classified," he said mildly.

"For whom?"

"A client. Corporate security work doesn't leave pretty paper trails, Ranger. Half my career is under seal, and the other half is under different seals. You want references, call the numbers. Oh — wait." His mouth twisted. "The relay's down and we're trapped in a canyon with killers. Terrible timing on your part."

There it was again — the joke deployed precisely where a defensive man would have gotten angry. I circled him the way I'd circle a claim jumper, keeping my voice bored.

"You joined the expedition eight weeks before launch. Everyone else signed on a year out."

"Security contracts are short. Academics plan forever; security men plan until the money clears." He shrugged. "Ask Sen. She'll confirm I came recommended."

"By whom?"

"A firm. The firm's name won't mean anything to you, and its principals prefer anonymity. That's not sinister, Ranger, that's just business among people with enemies." He folded his arms. "Now. My turn. Where were you the night Pellam died?"

That landed harder than he probably intended, because I genuinely didn't have an answer he'd like. I'd been on the gravel bar chasing a ghost, and the only person who could corroborate my whereabouts was the ghost.

"Asleep," I said. "Like everyone claims to be."

"Mm." He studied me for a moment, and something in his face shifted — softened, maybe, or settled. "Let me save us both some time. You've read my file, you've done the math, and I'm the only man in this camp with a soldier's hands. I know how this looks. So here's what I'll give you: my knife, freely surrendered, for comparison against anything you think matches. Full cooperation, no lawyers, no stonewalling. Because I want the same thing you want — whoever killed those men caught before he kills again. And Ranger?" He leaned in slightly, and his voice dropped to something quiet and real. "Whoever it is, they're very good. Better than your average academic. Possibly better than you."

"I doubt that," I said, and took his knife.

It was ranger-issue. Of course it was. Half the security contractors in the borderlands carried them; they're good knives. It proved nothing except that the universe enjoys irony.

* * *

That night I sat in my tent with the evidence spread around me like a bad hand of cards, and I made myself play devil's advocate against my own case.

Against Hale: late arrival, thin credentials, military bearing, access, physical capability. Airtight circumstantially, and worth exactly nothing in court. Any defense advocate would shred it in an hour. Plenty of legitimate contractors look exactly like Hale looks.

For Hale: he'd surrendered his weapon without being asked twice. He'd volunteered cooperation before I pressed. Guilty men of his caliber don't do that — they stall, they lawyer up, they create distance. And his alibi, when I'd checked it obliquely with two expedition members, held: he'd been repairing the water purifier intake the night Osei died, and both the cook and Tam's counterpart in the expedition had seen him there, elbow-deep in pump machinery, cursing creatively. Sabotage of a mooring line takes ten minutes. The pump repair took four hours, witnessed.

Which left me exactly nowhere, and worse than nowhere, because now I had to consider the possibility that the killer was one of the thirty-eight academics after all — someone whose file was fat and friendly and false in ways I hadn't thought to check. Or someone in my own convoy. Or—

Or the person I'd been refusing to put on the list since the moment I arrived.

Ilsa Vey had appeared out of the vault detail's shadow like she'd never left. Alive, unrepentant, three years of silence sitting between us like a loaded weapon. She was agile, she was clever, she knew the camp's rhythms better than any newcomer, and she had — I forced myself to write it in my notes, because honesty is the only discipline that keeps an investigator honest — she had been present near both death sites within the window. I'd confirmed it myself, cross-checking watch logs.

My hand shook writing that last line. Not because I believed it. Because I could feel how badly I *wanted* not to believe it, and wanting is the enemy of knowing.

A ranger's rule, older than the service itself: follow the trail where it goes, not where you wish it went. So I made myself sit with the question I'd been dodging. If the trail led to Ilsa Vey, would I follow it?

I wrote the answer down and immediately regretted it.

Before I could decide whether to tear the page out, the tent flap moved and Sen ducked inside, holding a lamp and an expression I didn't like.

"We found something," she said. "On the survey platform. Under the clip mechanism of Yusuf's harness. Someone left it there — recently, deliberately, where we couldn't miss it."

She set the object down among my scattered notes: a small metal tag, military-stamped, the kind that gets riveted to equipment crates. The stamping read a lot number, a batch date, and a contractor's mark.

The contractor's mark was Serevan Holdings.

Lady Serevan — patron of this expedition, payer of my doubled salary, owner of the charter that the vault's contents could unravel. Her mark, on the piece of hardware used to kill Yusuf Kade.

"Ranger," Sen said quietly, "what exactly did we dig up down here?"

Outside, far off across the black water, a light blinked twice from the direction of the envoy's skiff — steady, deliberate, answered a moment later by something upstream.

Somebody in this canyon wasn't waiting for the monsoon.
