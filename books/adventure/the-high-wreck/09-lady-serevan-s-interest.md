## 9. Lady Serevan's Interest

I had been chief scientist of the Meridian Expedition for two years and eleven months, and in that time I had learned that the desert gives you three kinds of visitors: those who want something you found, those who want something you know, and those who want you dead. The skiff that came upriver on the fourth morning after our convoy arrived was carrying representatives of all three categories at once. It just hadn't sorted itself out yet.

Tam spotted it first — or rather, heard it first, because the boy had taken to sleeping with one ear on the repaired radio like a farmer sleeping beside a sick animal. He came down the ladder from the radio platform two rungs at a time, which I have repeatedly told him is how expedition members end up as cautionary anecdotes.

"Boat," he said. "Single engine, coming slow. Flying white."

White meant parley, in river law older than any of the charters we'd found rotting sweetly in the vault below us. White meant *we come to talk*. It also, historically, meant *we come to talk before we do the other thing*, but I am by training an optimist about material evidence and a pessimist about people, so I put on my cleanest shirt — the one without the vault dust ground into the elbows — and went down to the waterline.

* * *

The skiff was beautiful. That was the first thing wrong with it. Everything else on this river looked like it had been salvaged twice and sunk once; this vessel gleamed with fresh varnish, its lines drawn for speed and comfort in equal measure, the kind of boat that cost more than my entire expedition budget for a fiscal quarter. It carried six crew in matching grey fatigues with no insignia, which is itself an insignia if you know how to read cloth, and one passenger who stepped onto our gravel landing with the unhurried grace of someone who has never once been kept waiting.

She introduced herself as Mistress Corvel, personal envoy to Lady Serevan, patron of the Meridian Expedition. She was perhaps fifty, silver-haired, dressed in travelling linen that managed to look both practical and ruinously expensive. She shook my hand with exactly the right pressure. Everything about her was calibrated to exactly the right anything.

"Dr. Sen," she said. "Your reputation precedes you. Your monograph on post-collapse hydrology is still cited."

"It's out of date," I said. "The river keeps revising it."

"And yet the river keeps inviting scholars back." She smiled. "May we speak somewhere private? Lady Serevan sends her warmest regards and considerable concern. You've been silent for weeks. She feared the worst."

We had been silent for three weeks because our radios had been sabotaged by a man whose credentials didn't check out, and because the things we'd found made silence feel like prudence. I did not say this. I led her up the switchback path to the main tent, past the sentry posts Okafor had established without anyone asking him to, past Vey's people pretending to sort survey equipment while watching Corvel with the flat attention of cats watching a bird that has landed too close.

Okafor fell into step behind us. He hadn't been invited. Nobody invited Ansel Okafor anywhere; he simply concluded that his presence was required, and history had not yet offered a counterargument.

* * *

In the main tent, Corvel accepted tea she did not drink, which told me everything before she said a word. People who drink your tea are telling you they trust your water. People who don't are telling you they've read reports on you.

"Lady Serevan is naturally eager to hear about your progress," Corvel began. "The investment has been substantial. The families take a long view of their commitments."

"The families," I said. Not a question. She'd said it herself, casually: *the families*, plural, the way one says *the weather*.

"The consortium of charter holders who underwrote the expedition, yes." Her eyes moved over the tent — the charts, the sample trays, the crate stencilled ARCHIVE VAULT: CATALOGUE IN PROGRESS that I really should have covered. "Speaking of progress. We understand you've recovered documents. Pre-collapse materials."

"We've recovered a great many things," I said. "Potsherds mostly. Pottery is ninety percent of archaeology and zero percent of what patrons hope for."

"Mistress Sen." She set down her untouched cup. "I will be direct, because the river narrows upstream and neither of us enjoys wasted time. Lady Serevan wishes to know whether documents of a legal character — grants, surveys, confirmations of title — were among the recovery. If so, she requests that they be catalogued separately, sealed against moisture, and prepared for transport under trusted escort. In exchange, the Serevan charter will extend extraction priority to select personnel of your choosing. Yourself first, of course. And your senior staff. Before the monsoon."

I felt Okafor go very still behind me. I have spent decades reading strata; I can read silence almost as well now.

"Select personnel," I repeated. "And the others?"

"The others would follow when conditions permitted."

"When conditions permitted," I said, "meaning when the canyon flooded twenty feet and the question resolved itself."

Corvel's expression did not change, which was itself an answer of remarkable eloquence. "Dr. Sen. The monsoon is not a moral actor. It does not punish the wicked and spare the just. Lady Serevan is offering to save the people you care most about. What happens to the rest is the river's business, not hers, and certainly not mine."

"And the documents?"

"Would travel with the select personnel. Under seal. For study at the family archive in Calloway."

Where they would be studied the way inconvenient letters are studied: by fire. I thought of the vault below us, of the surveyor's confession with its honest, exhausted signature, of the charters copied out in ink that chemistry could date to within a decade — a decade some eighty years *after* the collapse they claimed to predate. I thought of the two graves on the ridge, and the man whose blade had opened them, and how Doran Hale stood at the perimeter right now wearing the face of a man unjustly suspected, convincingly, damnably convincingly.

Here was the thing I understood in that moment, the thing that rearranged the furniture of my understanding and never moved back:

Lady Serevan had asked about legal documents. Not treasure. Not relics. Not the pre-collapse technology every patron salivates over. *Legal* documents. Which meant she knew what the Archive Vault held before we ever cut the seal. Which meant the expedition had not been an act of scholarly patronage. It had been a search warrant issued by the people being searched for.

We had not discovered anything. We had been *sent*.

* * *

I am, as I say, a pessimist about people, so it took me only a moment to arrive there. But I confess my hands were not entirely steady when I poured myself more tea, because there is a difference between suspecting you are a tool and hearing the tool's handle described aloud.

"You'll forgive me, Mistress Corvel," I said, "but the expedition's findings belong to the Meridian Institute and its full roster, per the charter Lady Serevan signed. All personnel share equally in extraction risk and recovery credit. That was the contract."

"Contracts," Corvel said gently, "are instruments of stable times. The rains are coming, Doctor. Rivers rise. Papers drown. History is written by whoever's copy survives." She rose, smoothing linen that needed no smoothing. "Lady Serevan's offer stands until the water rises. I'll remain aboard the skiff — my guards will assist with camp security in the interim. Consider them a courtesy."

Her guards. Six armed professionals with no insignia, staying. A courtesy, the way a wolf at the door is a weather report.

"They're not needed," I said.

"Nothing is needed," she agreed pleasantly, "until suddenly it is. Good day, Dr. Sen. Do think quickly. Patience is a virtue in short supply among the people I answer to."

She left the way she'd come, unhurried, and I watched through the tent flap as her guards distributed themselves around our camp with the quiet competence of people who had done this in other camps, at other sites, and had never once been mentioned afterward.

Okafor spoke for the first time. "You caught it."

"She said *legal*," I said.

"I caught that too." He was looking at the crate. "Three years I chased a thief across the flats thinking she stole gold or charter seals or something worth the chase. Turns out half the country's aristocracy already knew what was down here and just wanted it kept down here." His jaw worked. "My report's going to need more pages than I own."

"Your report," I said, "is going to need a better audience than the people who wrote the forgeries."

* * *

That evening I convened the senior staff, which these days resembled a war council more than a faculty meeting, and laid out Corvel's offer with the precision I use for stratigraphy: layer by layer, no interpretation until the record is complete.

Sen the scientist laid out the facts. Then Sen the human being stood up inside herself and said what she actually thought.

"They knew," I told them. "Serevan funded a two-year expedition to a drowned city on the speculation that a pre-collapse archive might contain land records. No patron funds potsherd-hunting at that scale. She funded it because she needed to know whether the proof survived — and if it did, to control it. We were never colleagues to her. We were a drill bit. And now the drill has struck something, and the drilling company has arrived to cap the well."

Vey sat at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, and said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and certain in a way I envied. "The originals don't leave this canyon under Serevan's seal. If they go upriver, they die upriver. I've seen family archives. There's a room where paper goes and doesn't come out."

"And if they stay," said Okonjo, our logistics lead, grey-faced, "they drown with us. Or worse — the guards take them off our bodies. Either way the truth dies here."

"Not either way," Vey said. "There's a third way. It's harder and it's ours."

But she didn't say what the third way was, and I noticed Okafor looking at her with the particular expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the totals of.

I called the vote anyway, because I am constitutionally incapable of not calling votes. Refuse the extraction offer: unanimous except Okonjo, who abstained with the air of a man planning to revisit the question when the water got higher. Demand the guards withdraw: unanimous, and futile — Corvel's reply, delivered by a courteous lieutenant an hour later, was that the guards would withdraw "when the security situation permits," a situation that, coincidentally, never permits.

So we dug in. Okafor reorganized the watches to include — and quietly to observe — the envoy's men. Tam rigged the one working channel to listen rather than speak. Vey and her vault detail moved the most critical originals into a cache only three people could find.

And I went down alone to the vault entrance and sat with my lantern among the shelves of drowned empire, looking at the surveyor's confession, and thought about the word *patronage*, and what it has always meant, which is: *I will pay for your dream, and keep whatever wakes up.*

Above me, through forty feet of rock and floodwater, the guards changed shift in perfect silence, and the river rose a finger's width against the pilings, patient as its owners.

Somewhere upriver, I understood now, were boats faster than Corvel's skiff and men less courteous than her guards. The conspiracy wasn't coming.

It had been here the whole time, drinking tea it didn't trust.
