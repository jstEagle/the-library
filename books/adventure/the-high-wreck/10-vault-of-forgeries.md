## 10. Vault of Forgeries

There's an art to leading a man through a drowned city in the dark, and I've had three years to practice it. You don't take the straight route — the straight route is where people watch you. You take the flooded stairwells and the collapsed arcades, the places where the water runs black and the only sounds are your own breathing and whatever the river is doing under the surface, which is always something.

Ansel followed me without a word for the first twenty minutes, which was its own kind of conversation. Ansel Okafor has never needed many words. He's a man who says things with his boots. *I'm here*, said the left one. *I haven't decided whether to arrest you yet*, said the right.

I could have told him everything on the walk. I'd rehearsed it, actually, back when he was still just boot prints on a gravel bar and not a tall shape with a rifle following me through the reeds at midnight. I'd rehearsed it the way you rehearse a confession or a proposal — both of those being, in my experience, the same genre of speech. But rehearsal is a coward's craft, and besides, some things you can't say above water. Some things need stone around them. Old stone, dead stone, stone that's been keeping secrets since before either of our grandmothers learned to forge anything.

"Here," I said finally, stopping at a seam in the wall of what had once been — according to Sen's surveys — the Hall of Registries. A government building. The irony is not lost on me; nothing ever is. Irony and I are old partners too.

Ansel ran his hand along the seam. "This wasn't on any map."

"No."

"Sen's maps are thorough."

"I know," I said. "I drew half of them." Which was true, and also the reason the seam wasn't on them. Three years ago, when I still signed my name to things, I'd found this crack during a flood-season survey and filed it as rubble. It isn't rubble. It's a door that opens inward, down a staircase the original builders carved below the waterline because they understood something about governments: that eventually, someone would need to hide the receipts.

The stairs went down forty-one steps. I counted them every time. Not out of superstition — well, fine, out of superstition — but because counting is what keeps your hands busy when they want to shake.

At the bottom, I lit the lamp I'd cached behind a loose block, and Ansel saw the vault for the first time.

* * *

It's not dramatic, if you're wondering. No gold, no crowns, no skeleton king on a throne. The High Wreck has plenty of drama standing up top — towers like drowned fingers, water running through ballrooms at chest height — but down here it's just a room. Square, dry, dry being the miracle, cut into bedrock that predates the collapse by centuries. Shelves along three walls. Sealed cylinders of fired clay, ranked like soldiers. And on the fourth wall, carved deep enough to survive anything short of the world ending twice:

**HEREIN LIES WHAT WAS OWED.**

"They were archivists," I said. "Before the collapse. Real ones, not the ceremonial kind the families keep now. This was their failsafe. Their insurance."

"Against what?"

"Against exactly this." I pulled the first cylinder from its cradle and cracked the wax seal, and the smell came out — old paper, mineral oil, tallow. "Against someone deciding, a hundred years from now, that the past was negotiable."

Ansel took the roll I handed him and unrolled it with the care of a man handling evidence, which is precisely how his hands work even when his heart hasn't caught up. I watched him read. I'd been waiting three years to watch him read.

The document was a land grant. Meridian Basin, north bank, four hundred square miles of the best riverfront in the territory — the founding grant of the Serevan charter, the parchment their entire dynasty hangs from. Except this one was the original. And the seal on it didn't match. And the date was wrong by eleven years. And the signature of the granting magistrate belonged to a man who, per the registry two shelves over, had died eight months before he supposedly signed it.

I gave him time. He needed less than I expected.

"This is—" He stopped. Started again. "The Serevan grant is the anchor charter. Every boundary survey, every ranger patrol line, every water rights ruling in the basin traces back to—"

"To this," I said, tapping the cylinder. "Which is forged. And here's the confession that proves it's not just forged, it's *knowingly* forged."

I set the second document on the shelf between us. It's the one I sleep next to, when I sleep down here, which is most nights. A surveyor's statement, signed and thumbprinted, dated nine years after the collapse. His name was Ede Maro. He was the man hired by the families' grandfathers to produce authentic-looking originals for grants that had never existed, and somewhere between the second forgery and the seventh, his conscience woke up and started screaming. He copied everything. Every fake, every backdated seal, every payment record. Then he hid it all in the one place he figured pride would never let anyone look: beneath the Hall of Registries itself, in the city the families had written off as drowned and worthless.

"He died believing no one would find it," I said. "He was almost right. It took me two years and a very patient flood season."

Ansel hadn't touched the confession yet. He was still holding the grant, and his hands — steady hands, ranger hands, hands I used to watch field-strip a rifle in the dark — were not steady anymore.

"Ilsa." My name came out strange in his mouth. Stripped down. "How long have you known?"

"How long have I been down here, or how long have I known what this means? Because those answers are three weeks and about six days respectively, and the six days have been considerably worse."

"You stole from the Serevan archive in Calloway. That's why you ran. You'd already found the trail that led here."

"I found a copy," I corrected. "A modern forgery of a forgery, which is almost funny if you're the kind of person who laughs at anything. The families keep duplicates of their own lies, did you know that? Backup charters. Insurance on the insurance. I stole one so I'd have proof of what the *originals* should look like, and then I came here to find the truth underneath it." I shrugged, and hated how casual it sounded, and kept talking anyway, because the alternative was saying the true thing, which was: *and I ran because you would have had to arrest me, and I couldn't survive that, and I didn't think you could either.* "So yes. Technically I'm a thief. I've made peace with the word."

"Have you." He set the grant down very carefully, aligning its edge with the shelf, and there it was — the thing I'd been dreading and craving in equal measure, the moment the badge in his head started arguing with the man. "You understand what you're holding. You understand what this does. Every border I've ever walked — the whole patrol grid, the water courts, the land registries — it's all built on these documents. If they fall—"

"Then the borders were never real," I said. "And neither was the law that drew them. Ansel. Look at me." He did, and I wished he hadn't, because his eyes were doing the thing they do when he's losing an argument with himself, which is going flat and bright at the same time. "Every eviction you ever enforced. Every family thrown off land their great-grandparents actually worked, because some charter says the Serevans owned it since before the collapse. We enforced that. Together. I stamped the surveys. You walked the lines. We were the muscle on a lie."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness has never once entered into it. That's rather the point."

He turned away from me then, which I let him do, because a man needs a wall to look at when his world rearranges itself. He looked at the carved words instead. HEREIN LIES WHAT WAS OWED.

"Maro," he said quietly. "Ede Maro. There's a registry office in Calloway with his name on a plaque. Founder's honor roll. The families *honored* him."

"He confessed to fraud and they hung his name in the lobby anyway. Either they never read his file, or—" I spread my hands. "Or they did, and decided a dead honest man makes better decoration than a living inconvenient one."

That got a sound out of him that was almost a laugh. Almost. Ansel's laughs have always lived right next door to other things.

* * *

We worked through the vault for the better part of two hours, and I'll tell you what it did to me, watching him: it finished something. For three years I'd carried this like a coal in my chest — the knowledge, the proof, the certainty — and I'd told myself the weight was the responsibility of it, the nation-shaking import, all the grand words Sen uses. But standing in the lamplight watching Ansel Okafor read a dead surveyor's confession with his jaw set like a man reading his own death warrant, I understood the coal had never been abstract at all. It was him. It had always been him. I'd stolen the future of the country and all I wanted was to hand it to one specific man and see if he could still look at me afterward.

"The ink," he said at last, holding a charter page up to the lamp. "This is modern. Iron-gall with the additive they use in Calloway — the families' own chancery formula. You can't buy this outside the Registry district."

"Now you're doing my job."

"Somebody had to make sure you weren't forging the forgeries." He said it dry, and I loved him for it, and hated myself for loving him for it, and catalogued none of that on my face because Ilsa Vey doesn't do inventory out loud. "The copies in the family archives were made recently. Within the last decade. Someone knew the originals were down here."

"Someone found Maro's cache before me," I said. "Or heard about it. Copied what they needed, resealed the rest, and left it for whoever came next. Which raises the question I've been chewing on for six days: if a family already knows, why leave the evidence intact?"

Ansel was quiet long enough that the lamp fluttered.

"Because destroying it admits it exists," he said slowly. "Better to know where the body's buried and stand on the grave. If the families sent people down here—" He stopped. I watched the ranger arithmetic finish itself behind his eyes, the same arithmetic that had made him check Doran Hale's credentials twice. "Ilsa. Who else knows you come down here?"

"Nobody. I move at night. I—"

The lamp guttered again, and this time it wasn't the flame.

It was the air. A draft, faint but wrong, coming down the staircase we'd climbed forty-one steps ago — carrying with it the smell of the upper camp: woodsmoke, wet rope, and under it, barely, tobacco. Nobody in the expedition smokes. Nobody except the man whose credentials don't check out.

Ansel's hand closed over mine on the lamp handle, and I felt it through his glove: he'd smelled it too.

"Forty-one steps," he murmured, easing the flame low. "Count them with me. Quietly."

We went up together into the dark, toward the smoke and the footsteps above, and every step I thought: *so much for telling him gently.*
