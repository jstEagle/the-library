## 11. Hale Moves

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in a waterproof notebook because I learned early that paper outlasts pride in this country — that I knew something was wrong before anyone else did. Not because I'm clever. Because I was bored.

Boredom is a radio operator's professional disease. You spend your watch listening to static the way a shepherd listens to wind, and after enough hours you stop hearing noise and start hearing *texture*. That night, three nights after the envoy's skiff came upriver with its polite questions and its polite guards, the texture of Channel Nine changed.

Not much. A hum underneath the carrier wave, like someone breathing on glass. It had been there since sundown, faint as a hairline crack.

I logged it. Then I told myself it was atmospheric interference from the storm building over the canyon rim, which was true the way "the knife slipped" is true of most murders.

* * *

The camp at the High Wreck ran on three things: diesel for the generators, fuel for the convoy's engines, and the radio link back downriver to Fort Calloway. We had plenty of the first, barely enough of the second, and exactly one working set for the third — the convoy's long-range rig mounted in the lead truck, because the expedition's own mast had been struck by lightning in the spring and Dr. Sen's budget hadn't stretched to a new one. Lady Serevan's money went to shovels and salaries, not spare parts. That should have told us something about her priorities, but you can say that about a lot of things in hindsight, and hindsight is a smug passenger who never once paid for gas.

So when I climbed into the lead truck that morning to run the scheduled check-in with Calloway, and the set came up dead as a drowned man's phone, I did what any operator does first. I checked the power feed. Clean. Checked the antenna lead. Clean. Checked the fuses. Clean.

Then I opened the access panel, and my stomach performed a small, cold maneuver.

Somebody had been inside the radio. Not broken into — *surgical*. The final amplifier stage had been pulled and its coupling capacitors lifted out, all four of them, neat as teeth extracted by a careful dentist. Without those, the transmitter would receive fine and transmit nothing but carrier whisper. To anyone who didn't open the panel, the radio looked alive. To anyone listening on the far end, we'd simply gone quiet.

You don't do that in a fit of rage. You do it so that nobody notices until it matters.

I sat there holding the panel in both hands like it might explain itself, and behind me someone said, "Problem, Tam?"

Doran Hale. Expedition security chief. Big man, gray at the temples, hands like dock rope, and a face so honest it could sell insurance to a priest. He'd been friendly to me since the convoy arrived — asked about my home valley, remembered my sister's name, which no adult outside my family has ever managed. I liked him. That's worth writing down too, because everything that happened next happened partly *because* people liked him.

"Couple of caps gone," I said. "Vibration damage, maybe."

"Vibration," he said, nodding slowly. "Roads up here will shake the soul out of anything." He handed me a canteen of water, which was a kind gesture, and stood there while I worked, which was not, though I didn't know that yet either. "How long to fix?"

"Days. If we had spares. Which we don't."

"Well," Doran Hale said, and he smiled, and his smile was the warmest thing in that canyon, "good thing nothing's going wrong, then."

* * *

The fuel cache went up forty minutes past midnight.

I was on watch in the comms tent, which is a canvas coffin with a folding chair in it, when the night turned orange. No explosion, not at first — just a light through the canvas, wrong-colored, and then the heat, and then everyone in camp screaming the word *fuel* in six different accents.

The cache sat downstream of the main camp, tucked under an overhang where the convoy's drums had been stacked: diesel for the trucks, petrol for the generator reserve, the lot. Someone had breached two drums and laid fire low along the ground, so the whole stack caught as one slow, greedy bloom instead of blowing. By the time we got buckets and sand moving, the overhang was an oven and the rock above it was raining sparks.

Nobody died. One of the drivers, Okonjo, took burns across his forearms beating a path clear so the flames wouldn't jump to the nearest tent line, and he'll carry those scars, but he lived. The fuel did not live. The fuel burned with a sound like applause.

I remember standing in the crowd watching it, my notebook in my hand because I take the damn thing everywhere now, and thinking: *first the radio, then this.* Two failures in one day. Ranger Okafor says coincidence is a luxury item, and he was already pushing through the crowd toward the fire with his face doing something I'd never seen on it before — not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. He was counting possibilities, and none of them were good.

Dr. Sen got the camp organized in about ninety seconds flat, which is her gift and her curse; she organizes grief the way she catalogs pottery shards. Ansel Okafor got himself organized differently. He organized himself toward the overhang's edge, where the ground was soft, and knelt, and read it.

I watched him read the ground for a long time. He didn't say anything for an hour.

* * *

Here is what the fire gave us besides smoke and ruin: it gave us proof of intent. Sabotage doesn't burn itself. And it gave us a timeline, because whoever set it had to move through camp between the last watch change and midnight, past four posted sentries, without being challenged.

Which meant the saboteur either had sentry eyes or knew the watch rotation.

Ansel called a meeting in the survey tent at dawn. Everyone came, including the envoy's guards, who came because they were invited and because not coming would have been a confession. Envoy Corvane himself did not come — he sent a note saying he was indisposed, which in envoy language means "I am watching this from a comfortable distance and taking notes."

Sen opened with the facts. Radio dead. Fuel gone. "We are now cut off," she said, "until either the monsoon passes or someone comes looking for us. Neither of those is a rescue plan. Both are a schedule for dying." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse.

Then Okafor spoke, and what he said rearranged the room.

"The fire was set by someone who walks heel-first," he said. "Heavy, deliberate stride, size twelve boot, left foot favoring the outside edge — an old ankle injury, badly healed. The same boot made prints at the radio truck two days ago, in the mud after the rain. I matched them this morning." He let that sit. "It also matches the stride pattern of the man who killed Ferro and Adeyemi. I've been comparing tracks around both sites since we arrived."

Every head turned. Every head turned the same direction, the way iron filings obey.

Doran Hale stood among us with his big honest hands loose at his sides, and he said, calmly, "Those are my boots, ranger. I've said before my file won't verify. You want to know why? Because before this job I did contract security work for interests that don't file paperwork. That's not a crime. Ask your questions. I've got nothing to hide."

And God help me, the room *believed* him. I watched belief move through the tent like warmth through a cold house. He answered everything — where he'd been during each watch change (asleep, alone, sorry), why his credentials were sealed (client confidentiality), why he'd lied on his intake form (who doesn't). He was convincing the way a riverbed is convincing: solid-looking, until the flood.

Okafor didn't arrest him. Later I understood why — an arrest with no functioning radio, no fuel, and the envoy's armed men in camp would have meant a trial by whoever held the guns, and the envoy's guns outnumbered ours. But at the time it looked like doubt. It looked like the ranger wasn't sure.

Hale was confined to quarters anyway, gently, "pending investigation," with two of Sen's people outside his tent who were mostly there to be seen. He thanked them for their professionalism.

That should have been the end of the day's bad news. It was not even the middle.

* * *

Because while the camp was burning diesel and burning trust, somebody emptied the vault copies.

Understand the arrangement: Dr. Sen kept photographic duplicates of the Archive Vault documents — the grants, the charters, the surveyor's confession — in a locked case in her own quarters, on the theory that the originals stayed hidden and the copies stayed close. The originals, Ilsa Vey's hiding place for them, I never knew and still don't. But the copies were real, and they were leverage, and everybody in that canyon knew roughly where they slept at night.

In the confusion of the fire — and it *was* confusion, genuine chaos, buckets and shouting and Okonjo screaming — someone entered Sen's quarters, opened the case with a key or skill, and took every sheet.

We found the case empty an hour after dawn, and Sen's face when she saw it is something I'll keep a long time. Not shock. Worse than shock. Recalculation. She had built her whole defense of those papers on proximity, and proximity had failed her in front of her own people.

The envoy's guards were missing from the morning count.

All four of them. Gone between midnight and first light, along with Envoy Corvane's note — a fresh one, pinned to his own empty tent flap, apologizing for departing "ahead of the weather" and wishing the expedition "every success in its important work." The man had signed it. He'd signed his getaway note. That's either arrogance or a message, and with these families I've learned it's usually both.

They'd taken a raft and half our remaining rope and gone downstream into the throat of the canyon, carrying photographs of the truth about every ruling charter in the nation.

"They're not running to Serevan," Ilsa Vey said. She was standing at the water's edge, arms crossed, watching the empty river with an expression I'd call professional disappointment if it weren't so personal. "Serevan's envoy wouldn't need to steal copies — she'd ask, and Sen would stall, and that would be that. These men moved like they're on someone else's clock. Someone who wants the evidence *gone*, not delivered. Someone who'd rather burn the truth than own it."

"Who else is there?" Sen demanded. "Who else knows?"

Ilsa was quiet a moment. When she answered, she didn't look at any of us; she looked upstream, at the drowned towers sticking out of the brown water like the fingers of something patient.

"When I found the vault," she said, "it had been found before. There were older tool marks on the seals. Fresh oil on the hinge pins — months old, not decades. Somebody from the families came down here within the last year, saw what was inside, and sealed it back up and told no one. Because telling anyone makes it real. They didn't send an envoy to negotiate. They sent a cleaner to erase. And Hale—"

"Hale is theirs," Okafor finished. He'd come up beside us without a sound, the way rangers do. "Not Serevan's. Not the envoy's. He was embedded before the expedition ever launched. The sabotage, the theft — he's not covering for a patron. He's executing a burial."

"Can we catch them?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

Okafor looked at the river, then at the sky — at the wall of cloud stacking up over the canyon rim, purple-black, swollen, the monsoon arriving early and hungry — and he did the arithmetic again, the same arithmetic he'd done at the fire.

"No," he said. "Not downstream. Not with no fuel and that coming." He nodded at the storm. Then he turned, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something that scared me more than the fire had, because it was hope and hope in that canyon was starting to feel like a loaded weapon. "But they're not the only ones who can use that river. And we've got eleven days, maybe less, before this valley goes under. So we'd better decide what we're willing to drown for."

Behind us, from the direction of Hale's guarded tent, came a shout — short, alarmed, cut off mid-word.

By the time we reached it, the guards were unconscious, tied with their own bootlaces, and Doran Hale was gone, vanished into the maze of flooded streets below the camp, taking with him the only map of the tunnels and whatever orders he'd been keeping folded behind that honest smile.

The first drops of rain hit the canvas as I wrote it all down.

*End of Chapter 11.*
