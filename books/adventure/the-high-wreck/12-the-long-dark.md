## 12. The Long Dark

The clouds came in like a debt collector.

I'd watched monsoon fronts build over the canyon rim before — from the comfortable distance of Fort Calloway, mostly, where they were a weather report and a reason to double-check the drainage. From inside the High Wreck, three hundred feet down between drowned towers with the river rising under you, they were something else entirely. The sky over the eastern rim had gone the color of a bruise going bad, stacked in shelves, and the air had that thick, charged stillness that meant the whole atmosphere was holding its breath.

"Six hours," I said to Sen, who stood beside me on the camp's highest platform with her arms crossed against the wind that hadn't arrived yet. "Maybe eight if the front stalls on the rim. After that, nobody's flying anything in or out for weeks."

"Weeks," she repeated, the way a surgeon repeats the name of a complication she'd hoped not to see.

"With comms down, we can't even tell anyone we're here."

"I know." She turned and looked at her camp — what was left of it. Forty-one people when the Meridian Expedition had been an expedition. Thirty-three now, counting the convoy's eleven, minus the two dead, minus Hale, who was currently sitting in the old survey office with Tam's wire around his wrists and a ranger watching him who didn't trust rangers anymore either. "Then we stop thinking about rescue and start thinking about arithmetic."

Arithmetic. That was Dr. Amara Sen all over. The world could be ending and she'd have it itemized by lunchtime.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing exactly that.

The lower camp was a loss already written; everyone knew it but nobody said it out loud. When the river came up twenty feet — and it would come up more than that, the flood marks on the tower walls went back centuries and some of them were high — everything below the second platform would go under. So we moved. Rations first: the convoy's crates hauled up switchback stairs cut into the tower's flank, cases of hardtack and dried protein and water purification stock passed hand to hand along a human chain that stretched four hundred feet of vertical. I put the convoy drivers on the heavy loads and the academics on the count, because academics count beautifully and lift badly, and because giving frightened people a job is half of keeping them alive.

The other half is a wall and a reason to stand behind it.

Defenses were harder. We weren't defending against the storm — you don't defend against weather, you just get above it and hold on. We were defending against the gunboat crew that Tam's defector had told us was ten days out as of yesterday, which meant eight days now, which meant they'd arrive after the flood peaked, into a canyon full of wreckage and a camp that might or might not still exist. Or they'd arrive early, running ahead of the storm, which was worse, because professionals run ahead of storms all the time and amateurs drown.

So: chokepoints. The stair was the only way up from the waterline, and I had the drivers collapse two of its spans with pry bars, leaving gaps you'd need rope and nerve to cross. The envoy's guards — the ones Serevan's man had left behind, six of them, armed and polite in the way of people following orders they haven't been given yet — watched us do it without comment. Their officer, a narrow woman named Kess, asked me if I expected them to climb.

"I expect whoever comes next to climb," I said. "If it's your side, I'm sure you'll manage."

She almost smiled. Almost. There was a whole negotiation happening in the space around that almost, and neither of us wanted to have it out loud.

Rations came next. Sen sat at a folding table with a grease pencil and a slate and did her cataloging, and I'll say this for the woman: she did it honestly. Not by rank, not by usefulness to the expedition, not by how much someone's family might pay. She sorted them by need — injured first, then the ones whose heads had started to go soft from fear, then everyone else — and she read the list aloud so no one could whisper later that they'd been weighted differently. It took her four hours. When she finished, one of the older surveyors, a man named Okonjo with hands like tree roots, said quietly, "You've just decided who lives," and Sen said, "No. I've decided who eats first. Those aren't the same thing, and I intend to keep them from becoming the same thing."

I believed her. Mostly. Long sieges have a way of making those two lists converge whether you want them to or not.

By dusk the sky over the rim had gone fully dark hours ahead of schedule, lit from underneath by sheet lightning that never quite reached the ground, like a storm rehearsing backstage. The wind arrived at last, coming down the canyon in gusts that smelled of wet stone and far-off rain, and the river below us changed its voice — deeper, rougher, a sound like something large turning over in its sleep.

Which left the night watch. And the night watch, as arranged by fate or my own bad judgment, put me on the high platform from midnight to dawn with exactly one other person.

* * *

"You did this on purpose," Ilsa said.

"I organize the watch roster by competence," I said. "You're competent. I'm competent. The math does itself."

"The math," she said, "is a liar."

We sat on the platform's east edge with our backs against a stanchion, forty feet above the new waterline, sharing a blanket that smelled of engine oil because it had spent its life under a convoy seat. Below us the High Wreck moved in the rising current — black towers, black water, the occasional groan of a submerged structure shifting its grip on the mud after however many centuries of holding still. Lightning walked along the rim, silent at this distance, strobing the canyon white and then leaving us blinder than before.

Three years I'd imagined talking to her again. In every version I'd had the right words loaded and ready. Sitting there with her shoulder a hand's width from mine, I had nothing.

She solved it, the way she always had, by going first.

"You want to know why I ran."

"I know why you ran," I said. "You stole the Calloway ledger and disappeared. It's not complicated."

"It's exactly complicated." She pulled the blanket higher, not for warmth — the night was warm enough, pre-storm warm, the air thick as wool — but maybe for something to do with her hands. "That night in Meridian Flats. You remember it."

"I remember everything about it. It's a curse."

"I'd lifted the ledger off the tax assessor's clerk. Clean job, thirty seconds, I was already three streets gone when I saw you come around the corner on patrol." Her voice stayed level, but I felt her go still beside me, the particular stillness of someone standing at an edge. "And I looked at you, Ansel, and I thought: he sees me, and his oath makes him take me in. Not because he wants to. Because he has to. Because that's who he is — the man who does what he's sworn to do even when it costs him. It's the best thing about you. So I ran. Not from you. From the choice you'd have had to make."

"That's a stupid reason."

"It's the truest one I had."

Lightning strobed again. In the flash I caught her profile, jaw set, eyes on the water, refusing to look at me while she said it, and I understood suddenly that this confession had cost her more than any vault job ever had.

"If you'd stopped," I said slowly, working it through like a knot, "if you'd let me catch you —"

"You'd have arrested me. And it would have broken something in you that doesn't grow back. I'd seen it happen to other rangers. Good ones." A pause. "Or you wouldn't have. And then you'd have been me, and one of us should get to sleep at night."

"And instead you let me spend three years chasing a ghost and calling it duty."

"Yes," she said simply. "I'm not claiming it was kind."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half a cough, but it was a laugh. "You know what I told myself? Every trail that matched your gait, every warehouse hit with your signature on it — I told myself I was bringing you home. That's the word I used. Home. Like you were a lost dog and not a felon."

"Were you lying?"

"To myself? Constantly. It's my best skill after tracking." I turned to look at her, and this time she turned too, and the lightning chose that moment to hold — a long flicker, not a strobe, enough light to see her face properly for perhaps two seconds. Two seconds is a long time when you're paying attention. "I wasn't chasing you to arrest you, Ilsa. Somewhere in the last year, I stopped knowing what I'd do if I caught you. That scared me more than the theft did."

"I know," she said. "I read your reports."

"You — what?"

"Your incident summaries are filed where anyone with a forged clerk's seal can access them. Yours are very well written. Also very sad, if you know how to read between the lines." The corner of her mouth moved. "Page forty-one of the Vey file. Your own addendum, requesting the case stay open past the statute review. No justification given. Just 'pending.' For fourteen months."

"I had reasons."

"You had grief wearing a badge," she said, gently, which was somehow worse than if she'd said it cruelly. "I'm sorry. I am. I've been sorry for three years, and sorry doesn't unrun anything."

Below us, the river hit something and boomed, a deep concussion we felt through the platform. Neither of us flinched. We'd both been shot at enough that thunder had lost its authority.

"Why the vault?" I asked. "Of all the jobs in the world. You could have robbed the Mint. You could have stolen Lady Serevan's actual teeth."

"Because I found out what was down here by accident, three years ago, planning a different job. A broker mentioned a pre-collapse surveyor's cache in the flooded canyons, thought it was bullion." She shook her head. "It wasn't bullion. It was the surveyor's field records — the original grants, the boundary surveys, the works. And once I understood what they proved, I couldn't un-know it. Every charter, Ansel. Serevan, Calloway, the Ondris family, all of them — drawn up fifty years after the collapse by men who'd never seen the land they were dividing. Forged signatures on top of forged seals, and the whole legal order of the country built on top like a house on a trapdoor." Her hands had closed into fists somewhere during this, knuckles pale in the lightning. "I spent three years getting close enough to touch it. You think I ran from you? I ran *toward* this. You were just — collateral. The part I hated."

"The part you hated," I repeated.

"The only part," she said, and her voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough.

I reached over and took her hand. It was cold, and calloused in new places since I'd last held it, and it closed around mine like it had been waiting three years for permission. Neither of us said anything for a while. The storm talked enough for both of us — wind moaning through the drowned towers, rain finally starting somewhere above the rim, a sound like applause from an enormous distant audience.

"When this is over," I said eventually, "whatever this turns out to be — the families, the kill team, the flood — I'm not arresting you."

"Ansel —"

"I mean it. Whatever happens with the documents, whatever Sen decides, whatever the country does with the truth — you and I are square. The Vey file closes tomorrow. I'm writing it tonight, actually. Three years, one line: subject deceased, presumed, river." I shrugged. "It's even plausible. Half this canyon's going into the water."

"That's obstruction," she said. "That's falsifying a federal record. That's —"

"The end of my career, yes. I've done the arithmetic. Sen taught me." I squeezed her hand. "Turns out I measure my life in miles, not years. And every mile I walked chasing you was a mile I'd walk again, so I'm done pretending the destination was an arrest."

She didn't cry. Ilsa Vey doesn't cry where witnesses can see, and I suspect the category includes rivers, thunderstorms, and me. But she leaned into me until her head found the hollow of my shoulder, and she stayed there through two more waves of lightning, and when she finally spoke her voice had gone quiet and practical, which from her was the equivalent of another person sobbing.

"The originals are in the tin in the survey office. The copies Hale's people grabbed were decoys — good ones, but decoys. Sen knows. Nobody else does."

"You're telling me this now?"

"I'm telling you because if I die in this storm, somebody besides me needs to know where the truth sleeps." She tilted her head back to look up at me. "And because I trust you. Which took me three years and a war zone to admit, so don't make it weird."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

* * *

An hour before dawn, the storm arrived for real.

It didn't build. It landed — a wall of rain moving down the canyon like a curtain being drawn across the world, and beneath it the river's voice climbed from a boom to a roar. I got the watch rotation moving, got the last of the gear lashed, got everyone above the second platform and counted twice. Sen stood at the tally board adding figures by lamplight, lips moving, doing her arithmetic against the water.

Ilsa and I took the east rail together, watching the flood come up the tower walls, foot by patient foot, erasing the lower camp the way it had erased the city once before.

"There," she said suddenly, and pointed downstream.

Lights. Two of them, low on the water, sweeping — running ahead of the storm after all, professionals to the last, and closing fast on a canyon that was busy trying to drown everything in it.

Eight days early. The kill team had arrived.
