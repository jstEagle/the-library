## 13. Turn Back

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in a waterproof notebook because paper doesn't crash — that I never believed in ghosts. I believed in signal propagation, antenna gain, and the fact that if you key a transmitter, somebody somewhere either hears you or they don't. Ghosts are for people who can't read a waveform.

Then Channel Nine said *turn back* in a woman's voice, and I spent eleven days trying to make her into weather.

Here's what I had. The Meridian Expedition's radio suite was state of the art three years ago, which means it's a museum piece now, and half of it drowned during the spring flood and got resurrected by Dr. Sen with solder and stubbornness. Our long-range set could reach Fort Calloway on a good day. On a bad day it reached the inside of its own cabinet. So when the convoy came upriver and Ansel Okafor's operator — that was me, transferred at Calloway because nobody else could keep the old girl breathing — started calling the camp on every frequency we had, silence wasn't strange. Silence was Tuesday.

The voice was the strange part.

It happened on the fourth night in the canyon. I'd been sweeping bands out of habit more than hope, and there she was: clean carrier, strong signal, close — closer than Calloway by a factor I didn't like thinking about. A woman's voice, calm as a lullaby, four words. *Turn back. Turn back now.* Then gone, like she'd never spent the electricity.

I told Ansel. He asked me three questions — direction finding, signal strength drift, any modulation artifacts — and then he looked at me the way rangers look at things, which is to say like the thing might be lying.

"Could be a bounce off the canyon wall," I said. "Could be."

"You don't think it is."

"I think bounces smear," I said. "That was dry. That was someone standing in a room with good acoustics, talking into a decent mic, maybe two klicks away. Maybe less."

He wrote it down. Rangers write everything down; it's how they pray.

* * *

After Hale burned the fuel cache and killed our radios — both of them, the long-range set and the convoy's backup, cut and salted with a competence that still makes my teeth ache — I inherited a junk pile. That's the thing about being the youngest person in a camp full of academics: when everything breaks, you become the most important person alive, and when anything works, you go back to being the kid who eats too fast.

So I sat in the comms tent with a dead man's toolkit and a living woman's grudge against Doran Hale, and I took apart everything he'd touched.

Hale was thorough. He'd pulled the driver boards from both transmitters, cracked the crystals — you do that with a quick twist, they die quiet — and slashed the antenna feedlines high up where the damage reads as storm wear. What he hadn't counted on, because Hale thought in terms of hardware and not history, was the Meridian's original survey cache. Three years of expedition gear, boxed and stacked in a flooded storeroom under the east tower. Academics pack like they're afraid the supply chain will end, because for them it usually does. Down in the mud and mildew I found a field transceiver, the little backpack kind surveyors use for tagging grid points, rated for maybe fifteen watts, and a second one with a cracked case that I cannibalized for parts.

Fifteen watts is a whisper. But a whisper on the right frequency, repeated patiently, will cross a desert.

I got the first set breathing on day two. It would receive beautifully and transmit like a man gargling gravel. Day three, I found the problem — a cooked final amplifier stage — and solved it by robbing the second unit blind. By dusk I had one working radio. One. Fifteen watts, a dipole strung between two drowned towers, and a receiver that drifted so badly I had to retune it every twenty minutes like feeding a sick animal.

One channel. One chance. I didn't know yet what the chance was for. I just knew that a camp full of people who were going to die in a canyon deserved better than silence, and I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a working transmitter idle.

* * *

The transmission I went hunting was our own ghost.

I'd recorded the *turn back* call on the convoy's log deck before Hale torched the fuel store — the recorder survived; it was in my tent, being used as a pillow, because I'm sentimental about machines that talk to me. I played it back maybe forty times. Woman, mid-range voice, no accent I could place, slight compression on the audio the way you get from a noise-canceling mic in a windy spot. And underneath the words, so faint you'd miss it if you weren't the kind of person who falls asleep listening to static:

A data burst. Tucked under the voice like a knife under a coat.

Not speech. Data. Somebody had transmitted a packet, and then spoken over it, or spliced the voice over it afterward. And the packet had structure — I could hear the framing tones, the little handshake chirps at the head and tail. Whoever sent it wasn't warning us. They were reporting to someone. The *turn back* was theater laid over telemetry.

I sat with that for a long time while the rain started testing the canyon rim, fat drops ticking on the tent canvas like fingers counting us.

If the envoy's party had a technician — and they did, a small gray woman named Rhee who handled their skiff's radio and mostly kept her eyes on her hands — then the packet probably went home to the families. Which meant the families had known where we were, what we'd found, and possibly how many of us were left, since before we knew ourselves.

Which meant the kill team Ansel kept half-expecting wasn't coming.

It was already on the river.

* * *

I decoded what I could. Not the payload — the payload was encrypted, compressed, sealed up tighter than Lady Serevan's smile — but the header told stories. Routing flags. A priority code. A timestamp format I recognized from the family relay network manuals I'd studied back at Calloway, because I'm the kind of person who reads manuals for fun and this is why nobody invites me places.

Priority flag: immediate. Destination prefix: not Serevan's house code.

I checked twice. Three times. The routing pointed somewhere else entirely — a house I won't name here, because I'm writing this in a notebook that could get searched, and because naming them feels like pointing a gun I haven't fired yet. Let's say: not Serevan. Another family. Older money, river money, the kind that owns the locks and tolls on the lower waterway.

Hale wasn't Serevan's man. Everyone in camp had assumed the saboteur answered to our patron, because she was the patron and patrons are the obvious monsters. Wrong monster. Someone else had planted him, and someone else was collecting reports through Rhee's skiff rig, and the priority flag said *immediate*, and immediate on a river in monsoon season means days, not weeks.

I brought it to Ansel at dusk, when the light in the canyon goes bronze and everyone pretends they're not counting the hours until dark.

He listened without interrupting, which is his best quality and his most unnerving one. Then: "Show me the header."

I showed him. He read it the way he reads trails — slowly, twice, letting the ground talk.

"Rhee," he said finally. "The technician. She made that transmission?"

"Her skiff's rig matches the compression profile. And she's the only one in the envoy's party who touches radios." I hesitated. "There's something else. The voice on the recording — I've been running it against voice samples all week. Rhee does the camp's daily check-in announcements sometimes. Same pitch floor. Same breath pattern before plosives. I'm ninety percent sure the *turn back* voice is hers."

Ansel looked at me for a moment. "You're telling me the woman who warned us to turn back is the enemy's radio operator."

"I'm telling you the warning was real even if the messenger wasn't. You don't splice a fake warning over your own encrypted report unless part of you wants it heard." I shrugged, feeling suddenly very young and very sure at the same time, which is a combination that has gotten me in trouble my whole life. "People are bad at only doing one thing with their mouths."

He almost smiled. Almost. With Ansel that's a parade.

* * *

Getting to Rhee was the hard part, and I'll be honest about how it happened, because the notebook demands honesty and so does whatever's left of my conscience.

We didn't scheme. We didn't run an operation. Ansel simply walked into the envoy's compound at midmorning — unarmed, alone, hands visible, very ranger — and asked to speak with their communications technician about a technical matter. The guards looked at the envoy's man Voss, and Voss, who by then understood that his leverage was evaporating with the river level and the barometer both, waved it through with the weary air of a man watching a card game he'd already lost.

I came along carrying the log deck and the transceiver, because Ansel said, "Bring your evidence," and I said, "You mean my pillow?" and he said, "Yes," and that was the whole plan.

Rhee met us in the skiff bay, among the coiled lines and the smell of bilge. Up close she was smaller than I'd imagined, early thirties, hands stained with solder flux like mine, eyes that did the arithmetic of a situation before her mouth committed to anything. She looked at the log deck. She looked at me. She looked at Ansel.

"You decoded the header," she said. Not a question.

"Routing flags and priority code," I said. "Payload's still sealed. Your compression's good, by the way. The voice splice was better."

Something moved behind her face — pride, maybe, arriving at the worst possible party. "It should be. I trained on Calloway relay equipment before they bought me." She said *bought* the way you'd say *shanghaied*. "What do you want?"

"The truth," Ansel said. "And then your help."

"And if I give you neither?"

"Then I walk out of here and tell Dr. Sen what's in that header, and she tells the camp, and the camp decides what to do about the woman who's been reporting their positions to the people sending killers upriver." He said it without heat, which is what made it land. Ansel doesn't threaten. He forecasts.

Rhee sat down on a supply crate like her knees had filed for independence. For a while the only sound was rain starting in earnest on the skiff canopy, drumming like applause for a disaster.

"My name isn't Rhee," she said finally. "It's Rhee, but it wasn't first. I was indentured to House Vael at fourteen — technical bond, seven-year term, renewed twice because renewal is cheaper than freedom and they make sure of the arithmetic. I maintain their river relays. Eight months ago they reassigned me to Serevan's expedition support detail and told me to send daily position reports and weekly inventory summaries. I thought it was insurance. Patrons insure their investments." Her laugh was terrible. "Then the reports started requiring content descriptions. Then casualty counts. Then, three weeks ago, a new template. Estimated resistance. Defensible positions. Personnel who could be turned."

She looked up at us. "I've been transmitting a targeting package. Piece by piece. For weeks. I told myself it was just data. You know what data is? Data is what you call a thing after you've decided not to feel it."

"The kill team," Ansel said quietly.

"Left the lower locks nine days ago. Two gunboats, twenty-two personnel, family retainers with river-combat ratings. In this water, ten days to the canyon mouth. Maybe eight, if the flood surge helps them the way it helps everything else get downstream." She rubbed her flux-stained hands together. "They're coming to recover the documents and leave no witnesses. The template says 'no witnesses.' I've read it forty times. I keep hoping it'll say something else."

"Why warn us?" I asked. "The *turn back* call. That was you."

For the first time she looked directly at me, and I saw the thing I'd heard buried under the encryption — a person drowning politely, in a language of protocol, hoping someone would notice the splash.

"Because I fixed your convoy's spare antenna bracket at Calloway," she said. "You were the operator who thanked me. Nobody thanks technicians." She swallowed. "And because when I keyed that transmission, I put the warning under the report on purpose. If anyone ever decoded it, I wanted the record to show I tried. I wanted there to be a record."

"There's a record," I said. "I'm the record."

* * *

She defected that afternoon, and it was less dramatic than you'd hope and more terrifying than you'd think. No running through rain, no gunshots. She simply walked out of the envoy's compound carrying her personal tool roll and a portable relay unit the size of a bread loaf, and Voss watched her go and did nothing, because Voss had done his own arithmetic and concluded that a technician who'd rather die with the expedition than transmit one more targeting package was worth more to him alive as proof of his own reasonableness. Or maybe he was just tired. Conspirators get tired. It's the one mercy of the profession.

The portable relay changed everything, and I mean that with the specific reverence of a person whose entire soul lives in radio.

It was a family-issue burst unit — short-range by design, meant for skiff-to-shore work, but with a power amp that could push thirty watts and a frequency agility that made my salvaged museum piece look like two tin cans on a string. Better: it had a directional mode. I could aim it up-canyon at the high ridge above the north tower, bounce off the rock face, and throw a signal clear over the canyon walls toward the Calloway Gap relay station — sixty miles as the crow flies, and crows don't have to care about canyon shadowing.

One channel. One window. The ridge geometry gave us maybe a two-hour propagation window at dawn, when atmospheric ducting off the rising storm cells would carry the bounce. After that, the monsoon's ionospheric chaos would shred anything we sent.

"So we get one shot," Ansel said, when I laid it out for him and Sen and Ilsa Vey around the map table that night, rain hammering the tower overhead like the sky practicing for what was coming.

"One shot," I agreed. "Thirty seconds of burst transmission, compressed tight. Or five minutes of voice if the duct holds, but I wouldn't bet the camp on a duct."

"What do we send?" Sen asked. She'd been cataloging supplies all day — I'd watched her do it, that grim patient arithmetic of who can be saved — and her voice had the flatness of a woman conserving everything, including hope.

"That," said Ilsa Vey, "is the wrong question."

Everyone looked at her. She stood at the edge of the lamplight with the tin case under her arm — the originals, the forged charters, the surveyor's confession, the whole rotten foundation of the nation in a waterproof box — and she smiled the way people smile when they've been waiting a long time for a door to open.

"The right question," she said, "is what do we send *first* — because whoever hears it is going to come down this canyon to shut us up, and we have about ten days to decide whether we're the message or the messengers."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere downriver, past the dark bends and the drowned towers, ten days became nine.

I picked up the handset and started tuning.
