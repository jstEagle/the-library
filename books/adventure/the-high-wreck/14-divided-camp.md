## 14. Divided Camp

I have spent thirty-one years teaching graduate students that evidence is a conversation with the dead. You excavate carefully. You document. You publish, and the dead get to speak.

Standing in the flooded nave of what had once been a customs house — the Meridian camp's new command post since the storm clouds started stacking up over the canyon rim like a debt coming due — I discovered that my own field had never once considered what happens when the dead demand to be heard *now*, at gunpoint, by people who would very much prefer they stay quiet.

"We broadcast tonight," I said. "The full archive. Surveyor's confession, the charter comparisons, everything. The relay mast on Tower Nine is still standing. Tam says he can rig a burst transmission off the convoy's spare radio."

Around the table — a door laid across two supply crates, which is the universal furniture of every expedition I've ever run — eleven faces looked back at me. Eleven, because we were down from nineteen, and because two of those faces belonged to Lady Serevan's envoy's guards, who stood by the doorway radiating the particular patience of men being paid to listen.

Ansel Okafor sat across from me, arms folded, saying nothing in the way rangers say nothing — loudly.

And Ilsa Vey said: "No."

* * *

Let me tell you about Ilsa Vey as I knew her before this week, because I have learned that everything about her requires context the way a wound requires dressing.

She joined the Meridian Expedition eighteen months ago under the name Veyra Ilsev, presenting credentials as an archival surveyor that were, I now understand, forged with genuine artistry. She was the best field archivist I ever employed. She could date a document by its watermark shadow alone. She catalogued four hundred linear meters of pre-collapse municipal records in the drowned district without complaint, without error, and without once telling anyone where she'd trained — which should have been my first warning, because academics cannot resist telling you where they trained. It is our entire personality.

When she said "no" to broadcasting, she said it the way she catalogued: precisely, without drama, and with the absolute confidence of someone who has already done the math.

"A premature signal dies with us," she said. "One burst transmission from a jury-rigged radio, through monsoon interference, on a frequency the families' people already monitor? At best it's static. At best, Doctor Sen, it's a *location marker*. We tell them exactly where the originals are, and ten days later the kill team arrives and digs them out of whatever's left of us."

"The kill team may already be on the river," I said. "Tam decoded the—"

"Then every hour we spend arguing is an hour they're gaining." She leaned forward. "You don't publish from inside the archive fire. You carry the documents out first. That's not cowardice, Amara. That's *methodology*."

It was the first time she'd used my first name. I want it noted that it did not work on me.

"Methodology," I repeated. "Three of my people are dead. Doran Hale burned our fuel and cut our radios, and his friends—" I nodded toward the guards at the door, who graciously pretended not to be discussed "—are holding copies of the vault inventory right now. And you want to *walk out*. Downriver. Past the gunboats."

"I want the truth to survive," Ilsa said. "You want it to be *loud*. Those are different objectives, and only one of them is achievable."

* * *

The camp split within six hours, which I record here as a matter of professional shame. I have managed field teams through cave-ins, flash floods, and one memorable incident involving a scorpion and the deputy director's sleeping bag. I believed myself competent at holding people together.

I was wrong. What I had never managed was a team with a genuinely unanswerable question at its center, because teams fracture along fault lines you can't see until the pressure finds them.

The broadcasters gathered around me: Tam, naturally — the boy had decoded one transmission and now spoke of frequencies the way converts speak of scripture — along with Okonkwo from geology, the Vashti sisters from the survey detail, and young Prewitt, who was twenty-three and had the specific courage of someone who has not yet been shot at. Six of us. Broadcast tonight, tell the world, dare the families to explain why the truth needed killing.

The carriers gathered around Ilsa: Ansel Okafor, whose silence I initially misread as neutrality; Rennick, the convoy's senior driver, a gray man who trusted gray outcomes; and four others whose names I will protect even now, because what happened next was not their fault so much as their fear.

Eleven people, minus two hired watchers, minus Hale locked in the old ferry office under Ansel's padlock, divided five and four into philosophies of survival. The mathematics of a drowning camp.

"You should know," Ilsa told me privately, on the walkway between towers where the river muttered below us like a colleague reviewing your grant application, "that I don't think you're wrong. I think you're early. There's a difference, and the difference is a body count."

"And if we wait and the kill team arrives first?"

"Then Ansel gets us out the way he got the convoy up here. He's good at impossible rivers." She paused, and something moved behind her face that I recognized from faculty meetings — the look of someone deciding how much truth a situation can hold. "He's better at them than he knows. I've watched him work for three years. From a distance. It's a long story involving a warrant."

"I read ranger files for a living, Ms. Vey. Or whatever your name is."

"My name is Ilsa." She said it simply, and I understood that the other name had been armor, and this was her without it. "Amara. The documents are real. The charters are forged. Every family seat in the Republic stands on paper that was inked forty years after the collapse by a surveyor who confessed to it in writing and then died rich. If we die carrying that truth out, it dies with us, because the families will make sure no one ever believes a dead thief's ghost story. But if it reaches the coast — if it reaches *print* — then it doesn't matter what happens to any of us. Truth published is truth immortal. That's your own doctrine. You taught it to me."

"I taught it to Veyra Ilsev," I said. "I haven't decided yet who I'm talking to."

She almost smiled. "Fair."

* * *

The raft went missing at 0300, and I know the hour because I was awake, because everyone was awake, because a camp waiting for a kill team does not sleep so much as lie down in shifts and rehearse its nightmares.

Rennick was gone. So were two of the carriers — Sefu and Brandt, convoy hands who had eaten our food and guarded our perimeter for a week. So was the longboat from the convoy's lead skiff, and so, according to the breathless accounting of young Prewitt, who had drawn the worst watch shift in human history, were the originals.

Not the copies. The *originals*. The vault tin. Which had been, until that moment, secured in the command post under a guard rotation that included — I checked the roster three times before I stopped hoping I'd misread it — Rennick.

"They're running the midriver channel," Prewitt gasped. "Past the spire line. I heard the oar-lock. I *heard* it—"

Ansel Okafor was moving before Prewitt finished the sentence. I watched him go, and I want to describe it accurately, because later accounts made it sound heroic and it was not heroic. It was *efficient*. He took a paddle, a knife, and a signal flare, woke the two youngest Vashti sister at the second tower's mooring — the expedition's last working skiff — and was on the water in under four minutes, in the dark, in a rising current, without raising his voice once.

Ilsa stood beside me on the walkway. She did not say anything. Neither did I. There is a specific silence between people who both understand that someone they need is doing something dangerous, and it is louder than argument.

We heard the fight before we saw it.

* * *

I will render the account as Tam reconstructed it from Ansel's own debrief, cross-checked against what the Vashti sisters saw, because I was not there, and a scholar who invents her sources is no better than a surveyor who forges her grants.

The longboat had the current and a head start but was overloaded — three rowers and a tin chest that rode badly, shipping water. Ansel and the Vashtis cut the angle across the spire line, where the drowned rooftops broke the current into eddies, and came alongside in the slack water behind the Customs Spire.

What followed took ninety seconds and sounded, from shore, like a barrel of tools falling down a staircase.

Sefu swung first — an oar, two-handed, the honest weapon of a desperate man. Ansel took it on his forearm, we later found the bruise ran elbow to shoulder, and went over the gunwale into the boat rather than away from it, which is the single most tactically insane thing a person can do in a boarding action and also the only thing that works, because suddenly there were four people in a twelve-foot boat and one of them was a federal ranger with a knife and absolutely no interest in a fair fight.

Brandt went into the river almost immediately — swept clear, caught by the Vashtis' throwline, hauled half-drowned into the skiff. Rennick fought the way gray men fight: economically, viciously, aiming for joints. He put a knee into Ansel's ribs that we would later confirm cracked two of them, and got a hand on the tiller, and for perhaps ten seconds the longboat spun wild in the current with all four combatants aboard and the tin chest sliding across the thwart like a puck on ice.

Ilsa, watching from shore, told me afterward that she had begun calculating the current's speed against the downstream rapids, and had arrived at a number she refused to say aloud.

Then Ansel did the thing that ended it. He let go of the fight entirely — released Rennick's collar, dropped his knife hand — and put both arms around the tin chest, and simply held it, chin tucked, absorbing blows, while the boat slewed broadside into the current and the Vashtis' skiff came around and hooked the longboat's stern line.

Rennick struck him twice more across the shoulders and back. Ansel held the chest. That was the whole tactic. Not victory — *custody*. He was not fighting three men. He was guarding one object, and he made himself the object's wall.

Sefu quit when the skiff's bow line went taut and escape became arithmetic instead of hope. Rennick did not quit; Rennick had to be put down, and was — a short, ugly exchange ending with Ansel's elbow across his jaw and the man slumping into the bilge water, breathing, disarmed, done.

Total elapsed time, per Tam's stopwatch, which he had been running since the first shout: four minutes, eleven seconds. Casualties: two cracked ribs, one lacerated forearm, one broken oar, and whatever remained of the camp's ability to pretend it was unified.

They brought them back in the gray before dawn. I stood on the walkway and watched the skiff come through the spire line with the longboat in tow, Ansel sitting in the stern cradling the tin chest like a child, blood on his shirt that might have been his or might not have been, and I thought: *there is the difference between my discipline and his.* I study what survives the water. He decides what does.

* * *

We convened again at sunrise, and I did something I am not proud of and will not undo.

I put it to a vote.

Senates vote. Academics vote. Expeditions in mortal danger, I have now learned, should probably not, but at 0600 that morning, with Rennick zip-tied beside Hale in the ferry office and Sefu weeping openly into his hands, I could see no legitimate authority left in me to decide alone. My expedition had tried to steal its own discovery. My authority had become a fiction maintained by one ranger's sore ribs. Better, I reasoned, to distribute the fiction.

The question: broadcast now, or carry out?

Broadcast: seven votes. Carry: four. Ilsa voted carry, obviously. Ansel abstained — raised his hand, then put it down, and said, "I enforce the outcome. I don't pick it." Which I have decided was either integrity or evasion, and I have further decided never to ask him which.

So we broadcast. Tam worked through the morning rigging the burst transmitter to the Tower Nine relay mast while the rain began — the true rain, the front edge of the monsoon, fat drops the size of coins striking the floodwater like small applause. At 1140 he sent the archive: the confession, the charter scans, the vault manifest, compressed into ninety seconds of shrieking data aimed at the coastal relay net.

Then we waited for the world to answer.

It answered at 1306, and not the way anyone wanted. Tam came running down from the mast white as paper salt, radio handset in hand, and played us the return transmission through the speaker so we all heard it together, the way bad news deserves.

A voice. Calm, male, professionally courteous, with the flat accent of the family retainers' corps:

*"Meridian Expedition, this is vessel Coriander, operating under private contract. We acknowledge your transmission. We are entering the canyon. Do not attempt to leave. Repeat: do not attempt to leave. Assistance is arriving."*

Assistance. Arriving.

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at Ansel. Ansel was already counting gunboats in his head — I could see him doing it, the way you watch a man count exits in a burning room.

"Well," I said, into the rain, into the silence, into the wreckage of my careful vote. "At least now everyone agrees there's a problem."

Nobody laughed. But nobody left, either. And in a divided camp, on the first day of the monsoon, with killers on the river, I have decided to count that as unity — the kind you build, not the kind you're given.

The rain kept falling. Downstream, around the bend past the spire line, the Coriander was coming.
