## 15. The Kill Team Arrives

The river told us before the radio did.

I was on the lower watch platform at first light, drinking coffee that Tam had made with the enthusiasm of a man who believed caffeine was a moral position, when I noticed the water. The Meridian's stretch of the canyon ran slow and green in the mornings, glass-still except for the seep-lines from the drowned towers. But downriver, past the last bend, the surface had gone wrong. A long seam of ripples, moving against the current.

Wake wash. Something was pushing upstream, and it was pushing hard.

"Tam," I said. "Kill the coffee. Kill the fires. Get Sen."

He looked at the water, then at me, and I'll give him this — he didn't waste a second on *are you sure*. Three weeks in a flooded canyon will teach a person to read rivers the way priests read scripture. He was gone before my cup hit the planks.

* * *

By the time the camp was awake, I'd counted the wakes. Two. Running wide of each other, disciplined, the way boats move when the men aboard them have done this before and don't need to discuss it. Not raiders. Raiders come loud, drunk on their own courage, hugging the center of the channel where the water's fastest. These two hugged the shadows of the canyon walls, one high on the north side, one low on the south, and they were pacing each other like a pair of hands closing.

"Gunboats," I told Sen, Ilsa, and Hale in the survey tent. "Twin screws, shallow draft, armored wheelhouse if I had to guess. They'll have a mounted gun apiece and crew of six to eight."

"You can tell all that from ripples?" Hale said.

"I can tell it from the ripples and from the fact that nobody flies colors on a river at dawn unless they're hoping to be identified. These boats aren't hoping anything."

Sen's face had gone the gray of old paper. "The families."

"The families," Ilsa agreed. She was standing at the tent flap with her arms crossed, watching the water like it owed her money. "Ten days out, the technician said. She was off by three. Or they hurried."

"Or she lied," Hale said, and there it was again — that flat, reasonable voice, the voice of a man offering you a perfectly sound explanation for a thing that made no sense. "A defector appears the same week the boats do. Convenient timing."

"Everything about you is convenient, Doran," Ilsa said pleasantly. "That's what makes you so hard to trust."

Sen cut across it with a hand. "Options, Ranger."

I laid it out the way I'd have laid it out for a green patrol, because the only thing worse than bad news is bad news delivered badly. "We can't outrun them — the convoy's rafts are cargo-haulers, and half our fuel burned with the cache. We can't hide — this camp is strung between two towers like laundry. What we can do is make this ground expensive. They want the vault and the people who've seen it. Every hour they spend fighting for it is an hour the storm gets closer, and they know it. Professionals don't like weather they can't schedule around."

"So we hold," Sen said.

"So we hold. And we get the noncombatants up. There are galleries in the north tower, dry above forty feet. Move everyone who can't fight, move the originals with them, and don't tell me where exactly — not even me. Fewer people who know, fewer people who can be made to say."

She nodded and went to do it, and that was the last easy hour we had.

* * *

They came at midmorning, when the light was flat and the shadows short, which told me their commander had studied this canyon or had someone study it for him. No dawn assault, no theatrical night attack. Midmorning, when the water was easiest to read and a defender's eyes were tired from watching since first light.

The lead boat came around the bend at cruising speed, unhurried, flying nothing. Gray hull, low profile, a shielded mount on the bow with a tarp over it that nobody had bothered to remove because nobody aboard expected to need surprise. The second boat held back a quarter mile, sitting in the current like a held breath.

I had twelve people with rifles, of whom maybe five had ever pointed one at anything that shot back. I had the convoy's lead driver, Okonkwo — a big, gentle man who'd hauled freight up this river for nine years and knew every rock in it — positioned at the raft barricade with a signal mirror. I had the defector, Rhee, the envoy's technician who'd bought us our one working channel with her defection, tucked in the radio shack in the south tower because she was the only soul in camp who could keep that frequency alive.

And I had a plan, which was that we would not fire first, and that if they fired first we would make them pay for the courtesy.

The lead boat slowed a hundred meters off the camp. A man stepped to the bow rail. He wasn't in uniform — that was the detail I'd remember afterward, the thing that made my stomach settle into that cold, certain place it goes when a fight is truly coming. He wore river clothes, good ones, the kind of plain expensive that family money buys when it doesn't want to be seen spending. No colors. No insignia. No demand broadcast over a loudhailer.

He simply raised a hand, palm out, and held it there.

"Cease watch," I murmured to the others on the platform. "Nobody moves. Nobody looks armed."

For a long minute, nothing. The river ran. The drowned towers dripped. The man at the bow stood with his hand raised like a man hailing a ferry, patient as stone.

Then the tarp came off the bow gun, and it was not a tarp coming off a gun. It was choreography. Two crewmen, four seconds, no wasted motion, the weapon swung and locked and leveled at the raft barricade in the time it took me to finish a breath. And the second boat — I saw it in my peripheral vision, the way you see the thing you were afraid of — had drifted wide and was bringing its own mount to bear on the south tower.

On the radio shack.

On Rhee.

"Down!" I shouted, and the world went to noise.

* * *

I have been shot at before. I want to be honest about that, because a man who writes about this work — and I do, in reports, in the flat language reports demand — can start to believe he's seen everything. But I had never been shot at by professionals. Bandits shoot to scare and to take. Militia shoot to be seen shooting. These people shot the way a surgeon cuts: the minimum necessary, exactly placed, no more.

The first burst walked across the raft barricade and took it apart. Not destroyed it — *dissected* it, the mooring lines first, then the lashings, the rafts sagging apart into their component logs with a terrible unhurried grace. Okonkwo was moving along the barricade when the second burst came, and I saw him spin and go down into the shallows, and I heard myself make a sound I don't want to remember making.

"Okonkwo's hit — south tower, they're on the radio shack —" Tam's voice in my ear, high and cracking and still, God love him, transmitting.

I went down the platform ladder faster than was good for my continued health and ran the bank with Ilsa at my shoulder. She hadn't been at the briefing's end; she'd simply appeared, the way she always did, rifle already in hand, and I was past the point where I could pretend that didn't steady me.

"South tower's lost," she shouted over the gunfire. "Rhee's in it."

"Then Rhee's our problem. The barricade is theirs to waste ammunition on."

The south tower's ground floor was a flooded lobby from the old world — marble gone soft, doors hanging off hinges, water waist-deep and the color of tea. I went in low and Ilsa went high, up a collapsed staircase that gave her an angle, and that old partnership was still in our bodies somewhere, three years of silence notwithstanding. We moved like we'd never stopped.

Rhee was at the radio, and she was still working. That's what I saw first: this small, sharp-faced woman, thirty at most, crouched behind the set with the handset jammed to her ear and her free hand still turning the dial, because the transmission she'd started hadn't finished and she was going to finish it. Bullets were coming through the tower's old windows in long, searching sweeps, chewing the masonry above her head into powder, and she was turning a dial.

"Ranger!" she saw me and her face did something complicated — relief and apology in the same twitch. "The burst — I'm sending the burst — their channel codes, everything I have —"

"Come with me. Now."

"Thirty seconds. It has to complete or it's nothing."

The shooting paused. I registered that pause the way prey registers a held breath, and I understood it a half-second before it ended: they weren't searching. They were adjusting. Somewhere on that gray boat a man with a calm voice was saying something like *ground floor, behind the equipment, adjust left*, because they had a spotter and he was good, and we were standing in a fishbowl.

"Rhee —"

The burst came through the south windows then, and it was not a sweep. It was three rounds, spaced, placed. The first took the radio's power unit and killed the transmission at twenty-eight seconds. The second took Rhee through the chest.

I got to her. I'll say that for whatever it's worth, which is nothing: I got to her, and I held pressure on a wound that pressure has never once in history helped, and she looked up at me with an expression that wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. She was calculating whether twenty-eight seconds had been enough, and she couldn't finish the sum, and then she wasn't calculating anything at all.

Ilsa's rifle spoke from above me — two shots, fast — and the spotter's window across the water went dark behind a falling body or a ducking one, and then we were moving, because staying in a fishbowl with a dead technician and a live radio was how you joined her.

* * *

They didn't press the assault.

That was the worst of it, in a way. We spent the rest of the day braced for them to come ashore, and they didn't. The gunboats withdrew to the bend, anchored bow-and-stern in the current, and settled in. Toward evening, a cookfire's smoke rose from the lead boat's deck, thin and domestic. Someone laughed over there. I heard it carry across the water in the still air, and it did more damage than the bullets had.

"They're not storming us," I said to Sen on the north platform, "because they don't have to. They've cut the river. Nothing comes up from Calloway that they don't allow, and nothing goes down that they don't inspect. They have food, fuel, armor, and patience. We have a camp full of academics, a barricade in splinters, and a monsoon that's going to arrive sometime in the next two weeks and kill everybody in this canyon regardless of whose side they're on."

"Then we negotiate."

"With the people who just shot a woman turning a radio dial? Sen, that wasn't a battle. That was a demonstration. They showed us exactly what they can do, exactly how little of it they need, and then they sat down to dinner." I looked at the cookfire smoke. "They're not here to fight us. They're here to wait for us to understand our situation."

Upstream, the canyon ran dark and empty, a hundred miles of drowned country between us and Fort Calloway, every mile of it now a corridor owned by two gray boats. Retreat wasn't cut off in the way a road is cut off. It was cut off the way air is cut off — quietly, completely, and with no visible hand.

Ilsa found me at dusk on the platform's edge. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did I. The river ran brown and indifferent between us and the enemy's fires.

"Okonkwo?" she asked finally.

"Alive. Through the shoulder and the hip. Tam's keeping the wound clean, and the man's too stubborn to die, but he's done driving anything for a season."

"And the burst?"

"Twenty-eight seconds of it went out. Rhee thought it needed thirty." I turned the words over and found they tasted like a bet placed on a dead woman's arithmetic. "Maybe the families' people caught enough of it to know it was sent. Maybe that's worse than them not knowing."

Ilsa was quiet a moment longer. Then she said, "Ansel. They knew about the boats before we did. Rhee knew the timeline, and she was wrong by a week — or she was right, and the boats moved early because *we* moved. Someone told them the convoy reached the Wreck."

I had already been there. I'd been there since the cookfire smoke, turning it over the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. A working radio, one channel, and a defector who arrived with the kill team on her heels like a shadow attached to a body. Either the families had excellent intelligence, or someone in this camp had a second channel, a second loyalty, a voice I hadn't heard yet.

Below us, on the gray lead boat, a lantern was lit and hung at the bow rail. Not a signal. An announcement. *We are here. We are not leaving. Take your time.*

Professionals, I thought. They'd already won the part of this that could be won by waiting.

Which meant the part that remained — the part with the vault, the originals, the eleven people I'd promised to get home — would be won some other way. Or not at all.

The lantern burned. The first thunder of the monsoon season rolled somewhere far to the south, too far to see, close enough to count on.

And in the radio shack, on a dead set with a hole in its power unit, a transmission sat unfinished at twenty-eight seconds — waiting, like the rest of us, to find out if it had been enough.
