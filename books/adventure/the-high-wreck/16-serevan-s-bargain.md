## 16. Serevan's Bargain

The envoy's name was Corvane, and he wore white the way other men wore armor.

He'd arrived on that supply skiff days ago with his two guards and his lacquered case of questions, and in all that time he hadn't raised his voice once. That was the thing about family men — the retainers, the lawyers, the well-bred knives. They never had to shout. The shouting was what the gunboats were for, and we'd already met those.

Now he stood at the edge of Dr. Sen's command tent with rain beginning to tick against the canvas like a patient finger, and he made his offer the way a man lays a winning hand down flat, so everyone can see it and no one can pretend they didn't.

"Terms," he said. "The woman Vey, and the original documents from the vault. Delivered to my skiff by dawn tomorrow. In exchange: safe passage downriver for every remaining member of this expedition before landfall. My principals will guarantee it under charter seal." He smiled, small and correct. "You have my word."

"The word of House Serevan," Sen said.

"The word of House Serevan," Corvane agreed pleasantly, "which has held for four generations, which is rather more than can be said for most things in this canyon."

I'll give him this much: he wasn't wrong about that either.

* * *

We crowded into the command tent afterward — Sen, Tam, Hale's surviving loyalists watching from the corners with their arms folded like men at an auction, and me standing where I always stood, near the flap, counting exits. Old habit. Ranger habit. The habit I was about to burn down along with everything else.

Sen spread the map of the lower canyon on her table and didn't look up when she spoke.

"Eleven survivors," she said. "Two wounded. One radio channel that works when it feels like it. And a storm coming that will put twenty feet of water through this camp inside a day." She tapped the flood line someone had penciled across the map, low and honest. "Corvane's skiff draws too little to sink. His guarantee is probably real — the families keep their bargains; it's cheaper than breaking them publicly."

"You're considering it," I said.

"I'm doing arithmetic, Ranger." Now she looked up, and her eyes had the flat shine of a woman who'd already done the arithmetic three times and hated the answer each time. "Ilsa Vey for ten lives. Paper for people. It's not even a hard equation."

"It's a hard equation," I said, "if you've read the paper."

The tent went quiet in that particular way tents go quiet — everyone suddenly very interested in their boots, their canteens, the rain.

Tam broke it, because Tam was nineteen and hadn't yet learned to let hard moments lie there. "What's in the documents? Really? Everyone keeps saying *evidence* like it's—"

"It's forged land grants," I said.

And there it was. Out of my mouth. Three years of chasing a thief, one career built on never saying more than the report required, and I'd just spent it in a wet tent full of witnesses.

Sen went very still. "Explain."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about oaths, since I'm apparently the expert now. They don't feel like chains when you take them. They feel like a coat. They fit your shoulders, they keep off the weather, and after enough years you forget you're wearing one until somebody asks you to take it off in public.

I took mine at seventeen, salt-flat station, a magistrate who smelled of gin reading the words while I repeated them. *To hold the borders as drawn. To serve the charters as law.* Every ranger since the collapse had said those words, because the charters *were* the law — the great families' grants were the skeleton the whole country hung on. Who owned the water rights, who owned the river tolls, who could settle where. You didn't patrol land so much as you patrolled a story, and the story said the Serevans and the Vosses and the rest had earned their ground fair and square in the years after everything fell apart.

Ilsa Vey had spent three years proving the story was written backwards. That the grants came first and the earning was invented later. That the surveyor whose signature anchored half the charters had signed them decades after his own death date, in ink that hadn't existed when he supposedly dipped his pen.

I knew all of it now. I'd stood in the vault. I'd held the tin box with the confession inside, the surveyor's own hand shaking across the page: *I falsified the boundaries at the families' request. God forgive me, I measured lies.*

So I told them. All of it. Standing in Sen's tent with the rain getting serious overhead, I laid out the vault the way I'd lay out a crime scene — which, I suppose, is exactly what it was. The sealed records. The modern ink on ancient parchment. The confession in its waterproofed tin. The charters copied, not granted. Forged after the collapse, by the families, for the families, and enforced ever since by people like me with rifles and badges and coats we forgot we were wearing.

"Every border I've ever walked," I said, "is a line somebody drew to steal. And I've been arresting people for crossing it."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. If somebody had laughed I could have stopped.

Hale's man Brakk — the one who'd tried to raft the originals out midriver and lost the argument to my boot and my temper — shifted in the corner. "Ranger, you understand what you're saying? You're saying the charter government's illegitimate. Out loud. In front of—" he nodded toward the tent wall, beyond which Corvane's guards were doubtlessly listening with excellent posture, "—*them*."

"I know what I'm saying."

"Do you?" Brakk's voice climbed. "Because what I hear is a man burning his own warrant to protect a thief he's sweet on."

There was a version of me, three years younger, who'd have hit him. The current model just looked at him until he found something else to inspect.

"The arrest I'm owed," I said, "is three years old and it's the only clean thing left in my ledger. And I'm telling you I'm not making it. Not for Corvane's bargain. Not for the charter. Not because the alternative costs me everything I spent my life building." I looked at Sen. "The documents are real. That's the whole point of them. Real things don't stop being real because handing them over is inconvenient."

Sen studied me for a long moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere out in the dark, the river kept up its work on the drowned city, patient as erosion.

"You realize," she said finally, quietly, "that if I accept Corvane's terms anyway, you've just made yourself worthless to both sides."

"Yeah," I said. "It's a strange feeling. I recommend it."

* * *

She didn't decide that night. She was better than that — she wanted to be, at least, and wanting counts for something in a woman who'd lost six of her people to a saboteur and a gunboat and was being asked to spend a seventh.

But I watched her do the arithmetic again over breakfast, cold rice and river water, and I watched Corvane make his rounds of the camp with that unhurried white-clad grace, speaking to the wounded, speaking to the frightened, speaking in that voice that made surrender sound like housekeeping. *Dawn tomorrow. Safe passage. Charter seal.* He didn't threaten anyone. He didn't need to. The monsoon was his threat; he was just the man holding the umbrella.

I found Ilsa at the north watch, where she'd posted herself with the field glasses and the expression of a woman counting gunboats that weren't there yet but would be.

"Well," she said, without turning. "Word travels. The ranger burned his oath in front of witnesses."

"News travels fast when there's nothing else to do in a siege."

"Brakk's telling anyone who'll listen that you did it for love." She handed me the glasses. "Which is embarrassing for you."

"Devastating." I scanned the lower canyon. Grey water, grey rock, the towers of the High Wreck standing out of the flood like the ribs of something that had died standing up. "Is it true?"

She was quiet long enough that I lowered the glasses.

"You know it is," she said. "That's the part that used to make you so angry. Not the thefts. That I was right about the why of them."

"I'm not angry anymore."

"No," she agreed softly. "You look worse than angry. You look free. It's very unbecoming on a lawman."

I almost smiled. Downriver, lightning stitched the horizon without sound — the storm still hours out, gathering its arguments. "Sen hasn't decided."

"She will. She's a scientist; she'll choose the survivable number." Ilsa turned to face me fully, and the rain had gotten into her hair and she didn't care, and neither did I, and there was a moment there that belonged entirely to us and to nobody's cause. Then she ruined it, gently, the way she always did. "Unless you give her another option."

"What option? We've got one working radio channel, a raft, and a storm."

"We've got a raft," she said, "a storm, and a man who just discovered he doesn't work for these people anymore." Her eyes were bright and terrible and familiar. "Ansel. When the families trace that anonymous burst you're planning — and they will — what do you think happens to whoever's holding the originals?"

"I try not to plan that far ahead. It's a ranger discipline."

"It's cowardice with good posture." She took my hand, and hers was cold from the watch, and I held on. "Come with me. Tomorrow night. The rapids, the towers, all of it — I need a navigator who shoots straight, and there's exactly one of those in this canyon and he's standing here letting me talk him into suicide."

"That's not how I'd phrase the offer."

"How would you phrase it?"

I looked downriver, where Corvane's skiff sat at anchor with its cargo of polite menace. I looked at the tent where Sen was choosing between eleven people and one truth. I thought about the surveyor's shaking hand, and the coat I'd been wearing since I was seventeen, and how light my shoulders had felt since I'd taken it off in front of witnesses.

"I'd phrase it," I said, "as the first honest job offer I've had in three years."

Behind us, the camp bell began to ring — Sen's signal, the all-hands call, the sound that meant a decision had been made. From the tone of it, fast and urgent and wrong, I already knew which way she'd gone.

Ilsa squeezed my hand once and let go. "Then we'd better go steal your arrest back," she said, "before dawn does it for us."
