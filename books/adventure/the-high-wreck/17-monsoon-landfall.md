## 17. Monsoon Landfall

The rain came the way bad news comes — first a rumor on the horizon, then all at once, everywhere, and too late to do anything about it.

I'd been watching the canyon rim for two days. You learn to read weather in my line of work, or rather in my former line of work, or rather in whatever it is you call a life spent reading other people's property lines and deciding which ones were lies. Storms have a signature. This one had been building like a debt: heat stacking on heat, the air gone thick and sweet, birds flying low and stupid. Then, an hour past dawn, the rim simply disappeared behind a gray wall, and the first drops hit the floodwater hard enough to bounce.

"Twenty feet," Ansel said beside me. He wasn't guessing. He'd stood at this rail of drowned towers before, three monsoons ago, back when he was still the kind of ranger who filed honest reports. "Maybe more if the upper gorge funnels right."

"And we're standing in the funnel."

"We're standing in the funnel," he agreed.

There was a time when that flat calm of his would have infuriated me. It still did, a little. But three years of running had taught me the difference between a man who's calm because he doesn't understand the danger and a man who's calm because he's already done the arithmetic and decided to spend himself anyway. Ansel Okafor had always been the second kind. It was the thing I loved about him, and the thing that made leaving him the hardest theft I ever committed.

Below us, the lower camp was already drowning.

* * *

You have to understand what the Meridian camp looked like by then: not a camp anymore but a besieged town strung across three towers of the High Wreck, walkways and rope bridges sagging between them, the Archive Vault sealed behind a door that had waited four hundred years for someone with my particular set of sins. The originals — the surveyor's confession, the forged charters, the whole rotten genealogy of the ruling families written in ink that hadn't dried yet when the men who signed it were alive — sat in waterproof tins in a crate that Dr. Sen had weighed, cataloged, and argued over for six days straight.

And now the river was coming up to collect them.

The lower camp — the supply dock, the fuel cache Hale had burned, the mess tent, the sick bay — sat twelve feet above the waterline on a shelf of collapsed masonry. By nine in the morning it sat eight feet above it. By ten, Tam was shouting from the radio tower that the gauge stake had vanished entirely, which meant the water was somewhere north of the numbers anyone had bothered to carve.

We evacuated downward people upward, which is the kind of sentence exhaustion writes. Eleven survivors from the convoy plus Sen's skeleton crew of academics, hauling supplies up switchback stairs carved into a tower that had been a customs house before the collapse, before the floods, before anyone decided that who owned what should be settled by paper instead of blood. The envoy's remaining guards helped, which told me everything: professionals don't panic, and they especially don't panic when their employer's kill team is moored downstream and the water is doing half their work for them.

Because that was the other arithmetic. Two gunboats had come up the canyon flying no colors. The first exchange had killed the defector technician — Mara, her name was Mara, she'd lasted four days as one of us and I still caught myself listening for her voice on the radio — and wounded Joss, the convoy's lead driver, through the shoulder. One gunboat had grounded itself in the rising water two nights ago, its crew scattered or dead; the other had pulled back to a mooring under the far cliff, patient as a spider, waiting out the storm the way the families waited out everything.

Sen found me on the stairwell, gray-faced under her sun hat, holding the strap of a pack like it was the last honest thing in the world.

"The vault copies are in the tin with the originals," she said. "If the lower vault floods—"

"It won't. It's above the old high-water line. Whoever built it knew this river." I'd spent enough nights in that vault by then to know its builders had feared exactly two things: discovery and water. They'd engineered against both. "The question is whether we're still here when the water stops."

She looked at me the way she looked at bad survey data — measuring how much of it she could trust. "Ansel says you can run the gorge."

"Ansel says a lot of things."

"He also says you surveyed these channels."

Three years ago, another life, another name on another expedition's payroll. I'd mapped the High Wreck's flooded streets in a dry season, when the towers stood forty feet taller out of the water and the current between them was a rumor instead of a fact. "I surveyed them drunk on celebration once and sober twice," I said. "Sober counts for more. But the river won't be the river I mapped. It'll be twenty feet of new river wearing the old one's clothes."

"That's either a yes or a very elaborate no."

"It's a yes," I said, "if the raft holds and the gunboat doesn't."

Her jaw tightened. She'd done her own arithmetic, and hers included the same term mine did: every hour we sat here, the families' options narrowed, and desperate institutions do their worst work when cornered. The envoy had offered terms two days ago — Ilsa Vey and the originals, everyone else walks free — and Sen had almost taken them. Would have, maybe, if Ansel hadn't stood up in front of God and the assembled camp and burned his own oath to ash, telling them all what was really in those tins. After that, no deal was possible, because after that, everyone understood that walking free meant walking free into a country still owned by liars.

"They'll come for the tins during the storm," Sen said. "When our watch rotation breaks down."

"I know."

"You're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

She nodded once, and something passed between us that I didn't have words for then and still don't — the look of a woman handing a stranger her life's work and trusting the stranger more than the weather. "Bring it to light," she said. "That's all. Not to me. To light."

* * *

The raft was a crime against naval architecture, and I say that with affection, because I helped build it.

Two survey pontoons from the expedition stores, lashed to a frame of walkway timber with cable and prayer. A scavenged outboard from the convoy's wrecked skiff, mounted crooked, fed by a fuel line that Ansel had spliced with the same hands that could field-strip a ranger carbine blindfolded. The tins strapped down center-frame under a tarp, double-bagged, because the surveyor's confession was the only document in four hundred years worth dying wet for.

Tam came down to the launch point — what remained of the dock, knee-deep and rising — carrying a coil of line and an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"The gorge narrows at the third tower," Tam said. "Mara showed me the charts before—" Tam stopped. Started again. "Before. There's an eddy behind the customs house spire. If you get flipped, aim for the eddy."

"If we get flipped," I said, "aim for breathing."

"I decoded her warning. I got the radio working. Let me be useful one more time." Tam shoved the line into my hands and didn't wait for thanks, which was wise, because I didn't have any that would survive the trip.

Ansel checked the lashings a fourth time. He was soaked through already, hair plastered flat, and he looked — I want to get this right, because I've had three years and a flood to think about it — he looked like a man arriving. Not happy, not afraid. Arrived. Somewhere between Fort Calloway and this drowning dock he'd stopped being the ranger chasing a thief and become something else, something that had chosen its side with open eyes, and the strange mercy of it was that he'd chosen mine without asking me to stop being who I was.

"When we clear the third tower," he said, "the current does the steering. We just argue with it less than the gunboat will."

"And if it argues harder?"

"Then we win on points." He put his hand on the tarp, over the tins, briefly, like a blessing or a promise. "Ready?"

"No," I said. "Push us off."

* * *

The first hundred yards were almost gentle, and I want to record that honestly, because what came after deserves the contrast.

Then the canyon took us.

Twenty feet of new water turned the streets I'd mapped into corridors, and the corridors into chutes, and the chutes into something that had never had a name because no one had ever been fool enough to ride it. The current grabbed the raft like a dog shaking a rat. The crooked outboard screamed and did approximately nothing. Water came aboard in solid green sheets, and the towers went by on either side close enough to touch — windows streaming, ledges shaggy with four centuries of silt, a stone face carved above some drowned doorway staring down at us with the patience of the truly dead.

"Third tower!" Ansel shouted, pointing left, where the channel bent around a mass of fallen masonry and narrowed to half its width. The water there was standing up in ridges. I'd seen that texture in survey photos of rapids I'd sworn never to run.

"I see it!"

"Ed dy behind the spire — if we go over—"

"We're not going over."

The river heard me and laughed. I felt it laugh — a long roll under the hull, the whole raft lifting, tilting, the tarp snapping loose at one corner — and then the third tower was on us and the world became white noise and brown water and Ansel's hand locked around my arm, and we went through.

I will not pretend I remember it clearly. What I remember is sound, a sound like the collapse of something enormous happening over and over; what I remember is cold; what I remember is the raft striking something soft — a submerged awning, a fishing net, the ghost of some pre-collapse market stall — and slewing sideways, and the spire of the customs house sliding past close enough to scrape paint, and then suddenly, absurdly, quiet. The eddy. Tam's eddy, a slow circular room of water tucked behind the masonry while the main current roared past three feet away, going wherever rivers go when they're in a hurry to drown someone else.

We sat in the spinning calm, both of us breathing like bellows, and Ansel started laughing. Actually laughing, bent over the thwart, soaked and half-drowned and laughing.

"What," I managed.

"Win on points," he said. "Told you."

I hit him with the bail bucket. It seemed proportionate.

* * *

The gunboat found us at the fifth tower.

I heard it before I saw it — that diesel cough carrying wrong over the water, doubled by the canyon walls — and then it came around the bend downstream with its spotlight swinging through the rain-hammered murk like a blind man's cane. Its crew had survived the storm better than its mooring had; they'd backed off into a side channel when the flood peaked, and now the water was dropping toward something navigable and they were hungry again.

"There," Ansel said, not shouting, which somehow was worse.

The spotlight found us. Pinned us. In the white circle we must have looked exactly like what we were: two people on garbage, guarding a box.

"Take the line," I said, and pointed upstream-left, where the channel split around a drowned granary. "Left fork. It looks worse."

"It is worse."

"It's worse for them worse. Their draft is too deep for the left fork unless they slow down, and if they slow down we gain thirty seconds, and thirty seconds is the gap at the weir."

He looked at me — one second, two — and I watched him decide to trust me with his life for the second time in three years, and I felt the weight of that like ballast. "Left fork," he said, and swung the tiller.

The gunboat captain, whoever he was, was good. I'll grant the families that much: they hire well. He didn't follow us into the fork. He cut across to the right channel, wide and safe, betting he could outrun our detour and catch us at the confluence below the granary. It was the smart play. It was the play I'd have made.

Which is why I'd picked the fork.

The left channel was a chute — steep, narrow, choked with debris riding the flood — and it dropped us through ninety seconds of pure violence that I navigated mostly by screaming directions and partly by memory of a map drawn by a younger woman who'd had no idea she was rehearsing for this. We came out of the chute sideways, spun, corrected, and shot past the confluence a full boat-length ahead of the gunboat, whose bow was just then grinding into a submerged wall with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

They backed off. They'd be around. Good hunters always are.

But we'd bought the weir.

* * *

The weir was the old flood-barrier below the granary district — a pre-collapse spillway, mostly intact, with a gap in the middle where four centuries had eaten the gates away. Above it, the water piled up in a smooth, glassy hump that looked, deceptively, like nothing at all. Below it, the river fell eight feet into churning foam and kept falling as the flood drained toward the lower gorge.

Every instinct says throttle down at a drop like that. Every instinct is wrong. You hit it straight, hit it fast, and let the boat fly.

"Ansel." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Straight down the throat. Don't lift. Don't fight it. Whatever happens on the way down—"

"I know what happens on the way down."

"—hold the tins, not me."

He looked at me then, rain and spray and the last gray light, and he smiled, and it was the smile from the salt flats, from the academy, from the night before everything went wrong — the smile of a man who has finally stopped measuring his life in miles.

"Hold the tins," he said. "Both of them."

We went over.

The world ended and restarted. There was a moment airborne — I know there was, because I saw the drowned city spread out below us, towers and rooftops and the whole silver-gray riot of the flooded streets, beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful — and then the impact tore the breath out of me and the raft buried its nose and the tarp ripped free entirely and we were down in the foam, shipping water, the outboard drowning, the tins—

The tins held. Ansel held the tins, both arms locked around the crate, and I bailed with the bucket and screamed at the engine until it caught, coughed, caught again, and pushed us out of the boil and into the long, fast, comparatively sane rush of the lower gorge.

Behind us, the gunboat's spotlight swept the weir crest, back and forth, hunting. They wouldn't run the drop. Not at night, not at this water level, not with their hull. They'd go around — the portage trail on the east bank, an hour lost, maybe two.

An hour. Maybe two. And then eleven miles of gorge between us and the delta, and the delta beyond it, and the relay station at Calloway Gap beyond that, and the truth beyond that, if the river let us keep it.

I looked at the crate in Ansel's arms. Water had gotten in somewhere — the corner tin was weeping a thin brown thread, and my stomach dropped through the raft's floor — but the inner bags were sealed, the surveyor's confession sleeping inside in its oilcloth shroud, four hundred years patient, one more night patient.

"How far?" Ansel asked.

"To the delta? If the raft holds, if the fuel holds, if they don't find the portage shortcut—" I wiped rain out of my eyes and made myself do the arithmetic honestly, because he'd earned honesty. "Dawn. Maybe."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

I looked at him. He was looking at the crate.

"Then it holds," I said, "because we don't have another plan, and I refuse to have drowned for a box of lies about land."

He laughed again, that low exhausted sound, and steered us into the dark.

The rain kept falling. The river kept rising. And somewhere behind us, patient as the families themselves, the spotlight swept the weir one last time and went out — which was worse than seeing it, somehow, because a hunter who turns off his light has stopped looking and started knowing.

Eleven miles to the delta. Dawn, maybe.

The river had other ideas. I could hear them coming up the gorge behind us — a second engine, deeper-throated, running the shallows where no gunboat had any business being — and I put my hand on the crate and my shoulder against Ansel's back and told myself the oldest lie in the thief's catechism:

*Almost there.*
