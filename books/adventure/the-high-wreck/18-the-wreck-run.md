## 18. The Wreck Run

The monsoon didn't arrive so much as it fell on us like a verdict.

I'd heard storms described my whole life — the old rangers talked about rain the way farmers talk about weather, patient and seasonal. This was different. This was the sky giving up all at once, a gray wall of water marching down the canyon with a sound like the world's largest crowd stamping its feet. The river rose under our raft as we pushed off from the drowned loading dock, and I felt the water take an interest in us. Not hostility. Interest. The way a cat is interested in something small.

"Hold the line!" Ilsa shouted over the roar, hauling on the steering oar. "We go through the towers, not around!"

"Around" was the sensible option — swing wide past the drowned city's edge where the water opened into brown chaos but at least there was nothing to hit. But around meant open water, and open water meant the gunboat behind us could run us down at leisure. Through the towers meant threading a submerged city that had spent two hundred years sharpening itself into knives.

"Through," I agreed, because apparently three years of chasing this woman had taught me nothing about avoiding bad ideas.

The first tower rose out of the flood on our left, a black finger of pre-collapse concrete furred with moss, water peeling off it in white sheets. Ilsa leaned into the oar and we swung close enough that I could have touched it. Below us — I knew without looking, because I'd seen the survey charts in her kit — the tower's base flared outward, a foundation wider than a house, and somewhere near it the roof of a street-level arcade waited just under the surface to open our hull like a letter.

"Left channel," she called. "Thirty meters, then hard right past the spire."

"What spire?"

"The one you're about to see."

She was right. It came up out of the brown water dead ahead, a church spire or a comm tower or something the old world had built to reach for heaven, snapped off halfway and leaning like a drunk against its own reflection. Ilsa took us past it so close that spray off the current wrapping the stonework hit me in the face, cold as a slap.

Behind us, the gunboat.

It had survived the flood's opening act better than we had — heavier, engine-driven, a family retainer's vessel built for exactly this kind of work. Its spotlight swept the water ahead of us, a white column punching through the rain, and every time it found us I felt the light land on my back like a hand. There were men on it. I couldn't count them through the rain, but I could hear the engine working, patient, relentless, closing the gap we kept buying back with Ilsa's navigation and my increasingly sincere prayers.

"They're gaining," I said.

"They're gaining *straight-line*." She grinned at me over her shoulder, and even soaked to the bone, even with the world ending around us, that grin did what it always did — made me feel like the smartest thing I could do was follow her. "This isn't their river. It's mine. I surveyed these streets, Ansel. I know what's under the water."

"You surveyed them dry."

"Dry enough to remember where the buildings aren't."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing for your life through a drowned city: it is beautiful.

The rain hammered down and the towers stood up out of the flood on either side of us, dozens of them now, a forest of black stone with the water combing through their bases. Windows stared out from twenty feet above the surface, dark rectangles full of nothing. Somewhere below our keel were streets where people had lived, argued, sold fruit, raised children — a whole city that the river had taken and never given back, and here we were using its bones to run from men paid to bury a lie.

I thought about the documents lashed under the tarp at my feet. Half of what had come out of that vault. The other half was already gone — lost when the flood took the lower camp, scattered into water that would keep them forever. What remained was the core: the surveyor's confession, sealed in tin, and the charters' comparison sets. Enough to break five families or not enough to break anything, depending entirely on whether we lived through the next hour.

A rifle cracked from behind us. The round went wide — shooting from a moving boat in a monsoon at a moving target is a prayer with gunpowder in it — but the sound of it put iron in my spine.

"Ansel." Ilsa's voice changed, flattened into the tone she used for facts. "The narrows ahead. Two hundred meters of street canyon between the customs house and the exchange tower. Water runs fast through there, and there's a strainer — a fence, old market fencing, stretched across at the far end. We thread the narrows, then cut hard left before the fence."

"And if we don't cut hard left?"

"We become part of the fence."

"Wonderful."

"It gets better." She pointed with her chin. "The narrows are narrow enough that they can't follow us side by side. They have to come through single file, slow, in the dark, in a place where one mistake kills them. Or they go around, lose five minutes, and we're gone."

"So we win by being willing to die faster than they are."

"That's been your whole career, hasn't it?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and because the narrows arrived.

* * *

The walls closed in.

Not stone — the sides of the street canyon were building faces, sheer and windowless, rising fifteen feet on either side, and the river funneled between them with a fury the open water hadn't shown. Our raft accelerated like it had been fired from something. The bow lifted. Spray came over the front in solid sheets and I crouched low over the tarp-wrapped tin, one arm hooked through the lashing, and watched Ilsa read the water.

She wasn't steering anymore. She was *negotiating*. Small corrections, constant, her body swaying with currents I couldn't see, reading boils and eddies on the surface the way Tam read code. Behind us the gunboat's spotlight swung wildly off the canyon walls, losing us, finding us, losing us again — and in the strobing half-light I saw the exchange tower slide past on our right, saw the customs house loom on the left, saw the water ahead begin to smooth and slacken as the street opened out.

"Fence!" Ilsa screamed. "Hard left! NOW!"

She threw her whole weight on the oar. The raft slewed. And the market fence came up out of the water on our right like the spine of some drowned animal — a row of rusted posts with wire strung between them, just breaking the surface, the river dragging debris against it in a ragged brown beard.

We cleared it. Barely. The portside pontoon kissed a post with a sound like a knuckle cracking and I felt the whole craft shudder, but we held together, and we shot out into open flooded avenue beyond, and for one glorious second I thought we'd done it.

Then the spotlight found us again.

They'd gone around. Of course they'd gone around — professionals don't gamble on strainers, they spend fuel instead of blood. The gunboat swung into the avenue a quarter mile back, engine howling, and this time there was no trick water between us. Just straight, fast, open flood, and a boat that was faster than ours.

"They'll run us down before the bend," Ilsa said. No fear in it. Just arithmetic.

"Then we stop being prey." I unslung my rifle and checked the action, water beading on the receiver. "That spotlight. It's their eyes. In this rain, without it, they're blind — and blind men don't chase."

"You want to shoot out a searchlight from a raft, in a storm, at range."

"I want to *try*."

She laughed — actually laughed, wild and bright, the sound torn away downstream by the wind. "There he is. There's the man who arrested me twice and missed both times."

"Once. The second time was a mutual retreat."

"Steady, then." She held the raft true, fighting the crosscurrent, giving me the flattest platform she could. "On the swell. Three, two—"

I fired on two, because the swell came early and I've never trusted counting.

The round sparked off the housing. A miss — but close enough that the beam flinched, jinking wildly across the water, and I heard shouting carry faintly over the rain. They knew. They knew a ranger was shooting back, and professionals respect that enough to get careful, and careful men in a flood make mistakes.

The swell lifted us again. I exhaled half a breath, let the rest out slow, and fired.

The spotlight died.

One moment the white column was sweeping toward us and the next the night ahead was just rain and black water and the ghost-gray shapes of towers, and behind us the gunboat's engine note changed — confusion, hesitation, the sound of men whose plan had a hole punched in it.

"There," Ilsa breathed. "Now we—"

The spire came out of nowhere.

The same leaning spire — I'm sure of it, though I'll never swear to it — the broken tower we'd threaded past at the start, its base hidden now beneath the risen water, its tip no longer breaking the surface where Ilsa remembered it standing. The flood had swallowed another six feet while we ran, and the rock that had shown itself an hour ago was a shipwrecker's secret now.

The raft hit it at speed.

* * *

I have been thrown from horses, blown off a ridge by a dust devil, and once dropped through a rotten roof into a cellar full of feral cats. None of it prepared me for the physics of a pontoon raft meeting submerged masonry at twelve knots.

The world rotated. That's the only honest way to say it. The water, the rain, the tin box, Ilsa's shout, the distant engine — all of it spun around me like water going down a drain, and then the water closed over my head and the roar became a hum and everything was brown and silent and full of debris.

I surfaced gasping, one hand still locked on the lashing ring of the tin box, which floated — blessedly, miraculously floated — beside me. The charters' comparison sets were in oilcloth inside it. The confession was in the tin proper. I hugged the box to my chest and got my boots under me and found bottom, waist-deep, on some submerged rooftop, and looked around for Ilsa.

"Ilsa!"

Rain. Wind. The groan of stressed structures somewhere in the dark.

"*ILSA!*"

"Here." Weak. Off to my left. "Here, I'm — hold on—"

I waded toward the voice, box under one arm, and found her clinging to a piece of our own pontoon, the foam block torn free of its frame, using it as a float. Her face was gray in the lightning-flash. When I hauled her onto the rooftop beside me she coughed up half the river and shook so hard I could feel it through my hands.

"The gunboat?" she managed.

I looked. The avenue behind us was empty — and then the lightning came again, a full second of silver daylight, and I saw it. Grounded. Hard aground on the spire's shoulder, canted, its bow crumpled, its spotlight hanging by its wiring and swinging like a hanged man. Men were moving on its deck, small and furious, and one of them was pointing — not at us. At the water. At the floating wreckage of our raft, and at the things coming loose from under the tarp.

Papers. Oilcloth packets, splitting, shedding pages into the flood.

Half the documents. Gone. I watched them go — white flecks on black water, swirling into the current, sucked down past the drowned windows, into the streets where the old surveyor had walked and lied and finally confessed. The river taking its tithe. The river keeping what it keeps.

"No," Ilsa whispered. She'd seen it too. She tried to rise, to go after them, and I caught her arm.

"They're gone. The ones that matter are here." I lifted the tin. "The confession. The core comparisons. It's enough."

"It's *half*—"

"It's enough," I said again, and didn't tell her I was only guessing.

The lightning faded. In the dark, the grounded gunboat's engine revved uselessly, backing water, tearing its own hull worse. Nobody was chasing anybody tonight. Downstream, the flood carried our lost half of the truth into the deep canyons, and upstream behind us the camp, the survivors, Sen, Tam — all of it receded with every minute we stood on this drowned roof with one tin box and each other.

Ilsa sat down hard on the wet concrete, shivering, laughing, maybe crying, possibly all three. "We won," she said, like she was checking.

"We won," I confirmed.

"We lost half of it."

"The river's a partner now. It gets a share."

She looked up at me, hair plastered flat, eyes catching the next flash of lightning, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we'd earned the silence.

Then she frowned, and her gaze went past my shoulder, downstream, to where the water ran fastest — and I turned and followed it, and saw what she saw.

Downstream, maybe half a mile, where the canyon bent: lights. Moving. Not the gunboat. Smaller, faster, riding high — two skiffs running without running lights until the lightning betrayed them, cutting out of a side channel we hadn't known existed, heading downriver.

Heading the same direction we needed to go.

Toward Calloway Gap. Toward the relay station. Toward whatever route the families had kept in reserve all along — because of course they had. You don't send one boat to do a burial.

Ilsa saw it too. I felt her go still beside me, the laughter draining out of her, replaced by something colder and older — the look she used to wear across stakeouts, back when we were partners, back before everything.

"How fast can you walk?" she asked.

"On this leg? Define walk."

"Ansel." Her hand found the tin box between us, gripped it. "If those skiffs reach the Gap first, they control the relay. They control every word that leaves this canyon."

The rain kept falling. The river kept rising. And somewhere downstream, in the dark, the second wave was already ahead of us.
