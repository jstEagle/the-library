## 19. What Survives the Water

I died twice before noon. The first time was in the river, when the gunboat's wake folded over me and the world went brown and roaring and full of other people's lost property — a boot, a chart case, somebody's entire life in a satchel spinning past like a slow bird. The second time was on the gravel bar where Ansel dragged me, when my body decided that nearly drowning wasn't enough of a story for one day and added a fever to it.

I want it noted, for the record I will never file, that I did not panic about the tin.

Everyone panics about the wrong things. Ansel panicked about me — I could feel it in his hands, the way they moved too fast over my arms, checking for breaks, checking for breath, as if I were a piece of equipment he'd been issued and couldn't afford to lose. And I panicked about the tin box strapped under my jacket, because inside it was a dead surveyor's confession, signed and dated two hundred years ago, and that piece of paper was worth more than every living person in this canyon put together. More than me. Possibly more than him, though I'd have fought anyone who said so out loud.

"Talk to me," Ansel said. He had me propped against a fallen tower stump, some pre-collapse foundation poking out of the mud like a rotten tooth. Rain came down in ropes. "Ilsa. Talk."

"You're dripping on the documents," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone who'd been drinking river water and regretting it. "Two centuries of provenance, and you're going to ruin it with ranger sweat."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh, which is how I knew he was scared. Ansel doesn't laugh when he's fine. He laughs when he's terrified and managing it, and he'd been doing it all day — through the rapids, through the capsize, through the half-mile swim downstream with one arm around my ribs and the other holding a raft line that no longer attached to anything.

"The tin held," he said. "That's what matters."

"The tin held." I put my hand flat on my own sternum, feeling the box's outline through soaked cloth. It was there. It was warm from my skin, which felt obscene somehow — evidence shouldn't be body temperature. Evidence should be cold and institutional and kept in buildings with guards. Instead it had spent three years in a thief's coat pocket and now rode against the heart of a woman with a fever, and if that isn't how history actually gets made, then I've read too many books.

* * *

Here is what the tin contained, in case the fever takes me and someone else has to finish this:

One sealed document tube, military surplus, waterproof gasket intact. Inside: forty-one sheets of vellum and paper, dry as bone. Land grants. Originals. The founding charters of the Serevan, Calloway, and Marchetti families, each one bearing seals and signatures and dates from the first years after the collapse — and each one written in ink that did not exist until sixty years later. Ilsa Vey, who once catalogued the Meridian Expedition's chemical supplies for six months before she decided cataloguing was a coward's job, knows her inks. Iron gall browns. Aniline blacks. The past does not write itself in aniline black.

And beneath the grants, wrapped in oilcloth: the confession. A surveyor named Edmun Roache, who mapped the boundary lines the families still patrol with guns, and who wrote down, near the end of his life, that he had drawn those lines wherever the families told him to draw them, that the "ancient grants" were drafted in his cousin's back room over four winters, and that every border, every charter, every legal inheritance propping up the ruling houses of this country rested on paper he had forged while drunk on rice wine and fear. Signed. Dated. Witnessed by a priest whose name matched a grave marker Ansel found in the vault himself.

Forty-one sheets. One confession. That's the whole revolution. It fits in a shoebox. Most days I think that's the funniest thing I know, and some days — like today, shivering on a gravel bar while the monsoon rehearsed its opening act overhead — I think it's the saddest.

* * *

Ansel built a shelter out of raft frame and torn tarpaulin while I watched the rain and hallucinated pleasantly. I saw my mother, who has been dead eleven years, standing at the edge of the water telling me I never could float. She was right. I never could. I learned to swim in reservoirs, badly, and I have always trusted boats more than water, which is why I run cons on land and let other people do the river work.

"You're burning up," Ansel said. His hand on my forehead was cool as stone. That's the fever talking; he's not cool, he's a furnace of a man, runs hot even in winter, used to complain that the desert never gave him an excuse to be cold.

"It's a mild immolation," I said. "I'll walk it off."

"You'll walk nothing. We're moving at first light, and you're riding on my back."

"I'm not being carried through a canyon like a sack of—"

"You capsized a raft, swam a flood-current river, and spent twenty minutes underwater. You're being carried, and you can spend your energy complaining, which I know is your favorite exercise."

I wanted to argue. I genuinely did. But the gravel was soft and the rain had found a rhythm and somewhere upstream a gunboat was either sunk or hunting us, and arguing with Ansel Okafor about carrying capacity struck me suddenly as the least efficient use of my remaining hours. So I said, "Fine. But if you drop me I haunt you specifically, not generally. Personally. Forever."

"Noted," he said, and pulled the tarp tighter, and in the dark under the tarp I listened to him inventory our supplies out loud — two flares, one wet, one dry; a knife; a compass; three days of pressed ration bars that had survived the river better than we had; the tin; and a pistol with nine rounds, which he counted twice, because counting is how he prays.

* * *

Fever dreams are honest in a way waking life refuses to be. That night I dreamed of the vault.

Not the real vault — the real vault was cold and smelled of mineral dust and old iron, and I loved it the way you love a church you don't believe in. The dream vault was my grandmother's house, which stood outside a town that no longer exists, flooded for a reservoir project authorized under a Marchetti water charter. In the dream the house was underwater but dry inside, and my grandmother sat at her table doing what she did every evening of her life: reading land records aloud, tracing boundaries with a cracked thumbnail, muttering about who owned what and who had stolen it from whom. She was the one who taught me that every map is an argument. I was seven. I thought she meant it as poetry. It took me twenty years and one forged charter to understand she'd meant it as an indictment.

In the dream she looked up at me and said, "You brought it home, then."

"It's not mine," I said. "It's everyone's."

"That's what home means, girl," she said, and poured tea into cups that filled with river water, and I woke up with Ansel's hand on my shoulder saying my name in the careful voice people use on the sick and the armed.

* * *

We moved at first light, or what passes for first light under a monsoon sky, which is to say the dark turned gray and resentful. He carried me. I hated it and I didn't. There's a particular humiliation in being carried by a man you left three years ago, and a particular intimacy in it too, and the two feelings sit in the chest together like passengers who refuse to acknowledge each other.

The canyon had changed overnight. The river was up six feet and rising, brown and muscular, chewing at the banks. Towers that had stood clear of the water two days ago were islands now. Somewhere behind us, the lower camp was gone — drowned or abandoned, Sen's people scattered up the slopes, and I made myself not think about the ones who hadn't made the slopes. Eleven survivors, Ansel had said. Eleven. Out of thirty-four. I did the arithmetic anyway, because I'm a thief and thieves count, and then I made myself stop.

"How far to Calloway Gap?" I asked, my mouth against his shoulder so the words wouldn't blow away.

"Four days. Three if the weather holds, five if it doesn't." A pause. "The relay station's manned. Ranger auxiliary. Old man named Petrosian, half-deaf, completely loyal to the service."

"So we walk into a ranger station carrying the evidence that ends the rangers' employers."

"We walk into a station with a transmitter," he said. "What gets transmitted is a separate question."

I went quiet. Because here was the thing I'd been not-saying since the rapids, since the vault, since three years ago on the salt flats when I walked away from him rather than make him choose: Ansel Okafor is the most lawful man I have ever known, and I say that with love and with contempt, because they're the same thing with him. His oath isn't a costume. It's a spine. And somewhere between the vault and the gunboats, something in that spine had shifted — I'd seen it when he told Sen the truth in front of witnesses, seen his own face when he heard himself do it — but a shift isn't a break, and four days is a long time for a man to decide which way his spine bends.

"You could take it from me," I said. "While I sleep. Take the tin, transmit it yourself, file it as recovered evidence. Clean hands. Your name on the discovery instead of a thief's. They'd probably give you a commendation."

His stride didn't change. For a hundred yards, nothing but rain and his breathing and the river gnawing the bank below us.

"Ilsa," he finally said, "if I wanted your tin, I've had four days of opportunities and a knife."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes, either." He adjusted my weight on his back, which was his way of closing a subject. "Ask me again at the Gap."

* * *

We holed up that second night in the shell of a pre-collapse structure — a stairwell, actually, rising out of the floodwater like the ribcage of something enormous, the steps above the waterline dry enough to sleep on. Ansel rigged the tarp again. My fever climbed in the afternoon and broke in the evening, and in the sweating aftermath I was weak as wet paper and clearer-headed than I'd been in days, which is a trade fevers sometimes offer and you should never accept gladly.

He fed me half a ration bar, soaking-soft, tasting of nothing and everything. Then he sat across from me on the stairs with the pistol across his knees, watching the water, and I watched him watch the water, and the tenderness in the scene was so thick I could have cut blocks of it and sold it.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I slept for three years," I said. "I ran. That was the sleeping part. Waking up is this."

He didn't answer right away. Below us the river slid past, carrying branches, a dead goat, a section of roof. The High Wreck stood around us in the rain, its drowned streets holding their secrets under twenty feet of new water, patient as archives.

"When we get to the Gap," he said at last, "Petrosian will log us. Two names, incoming from the Wreck. That log goes to Fort Calloway. Serevan reads it within the week."

"So we don't use names."

"So we don't use names." He glanced back at me. "And after the transmission — whatever gets transmitted — you go south. Downriver. There's a fisherman at Bend Nine who owes me; he'll take you to the coast without questions. Ilsa Vey disappears. Permanently this time."

"And you?"

"I file my report." His jaw worked. "Equipment failure. Storm losses. Nothing recovered."

"And the tin?"

He was quiet long enough that the rain got loud. When he spoke, his voice had the flat care of a man walking on ice he doesn't trust.

"The tin goes with you. The transmission comes from an anonymous burst, routed through the relay's emergency channel, timed so the families trace it back to a station that will already have logged two unidentified transients heading south. By the time anyone connects anything, the documents are in three newspapers and a courthouse, and I'm a ranger who lost a convoy to weather." He looked at me fully then, and his eyes in the dark were steady and furious and grieving all at once. "I burn the oath, Ilsa. I burn it, and what's left afterward — I don't know what's left. But you take the proof and you get it out, because you're the one who's spent three years learning how to survive without a name, and I'm the one who can't."

I reached out and took his hand. It was scarred and rope-burned and shaking slightly, and I held it the way you hold something you once dropped and never forgave yourself for dropping.

"For the record," I said, "this is the most criminal thing you've ever done."

"I know."

"And you're doing it for a country, not for me."

"I'm doing it for both," he said. "I've stopped pretending those are different."

* * *

On the third day the fever came back, meaner.

It started as a chill at dawn, in the gray hour when we broke camp, and by midmorning I was shivering so hard my teeth chattered the ration bar into crumbs. The world went soft at the edges. Ansel talked to me constantly — directions, observations, insults, fragments of ranger radio protocol recited like scripture — keeping me tethered to the waking world by the simple method of refusing to shut up, which for Ansel constituted heroism of the highest order.

At one point I opened my eyes and we were stopped and he had me laid out on the tarp and he was kneeling over me with that terrible controlled face, the one he wore in the fight at midriver, the one that means he's calculating odds and doesn't like any of them.

"Talk," he said.

"Turn back," I said, and laughed, or tried to. Tam's mystery voice. The whole canyon had been trying to tell us from the beginning. "Everyone warned us. The river, the radio. Turn back. Nobody listens in this family."

"We're not turning back."

"No," I agreed. "We're terrible at it."

He got me up. He got me moving. I remember pieces: his shoulders under my arms, the rhythm of his boots in mud, the towers sliding past on our left like a procession of mourners. I remember telling him things I don't fully remember telling him — about my grandmother, about the salt flats, about the night I ran, that I'd run because watching him arrest me would have killed something in me that three years of thieving hadn't managed to touch. I remember him saying, "I know. I figured it out in the reeds. You left the flare so I'd know you were alive and so I'd know you weren't running from me. Both at once. Very you."

"Everything I do is very me," I mumbled. "It's my defining feature."

By late afternoon the fever broke again, harder, and I came back to myself slung across his back with the tin digging into both our ribs, and the canyon had narrowed ahead into a throat of red rock, and far up that throat, barely visible through the rain-haze, a thin antenna mast leaned against the sky.

"Calloway Gap," Ansel said.

"How far?"

"Half a day. Maybe less." He shifted my weight and started walking, and his voice when he continued was quiet, almost swallowed by the river noise. "There's a problem."

Of course there was. There is always a problem; problems are the native fauna of this canyon.

"Petrosian logs everyone," he said. "But there's a second skiff moored below the station. Family colors. Someone's already there."

I lifted my head off his shoulder and looked at the mast, at the thin thread of smoke rising beside it, at the small dark shape of a boat that had no business being at a lonely relay station in the teeth of a monsoon.

"Well," I said, and patted the tin against my chest like a heartbeat. "Then we'd better decide quickly what kind of anonymous we're going to be."
