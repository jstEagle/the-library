## 20. Calloway Gap

The relay station at Calloway Gap was built by optimists and maintained by no one. It sat on a shelf of rock above the river like a tooth the country had forgotten to finish pushing out — a squat concrete box, a mast leaning at an angle that suggested prayer more than engineering, and a rain gauge full of last year's leaves. The ranger corps had decommissioned it a decade ago and then, in the way of bureaucracies everywhere, never quite gotten around to removing the radio.

I'd been carrying Ilsa for two days.

Not carrying, exactly. She could walk, in the way that a man with a broken axle can still drive — badly, dangerously, with noises you didn't want to hear. The fever had come on the second morning after the wreck run, when we woke on the gravel downstream with half our documents gone to the river and the other half welded to our skin by cold. By noon she was shivering in eighty-degree heat. By evening she was arguing with people who weren't there, mostly me, mostly about things I hadn't done yet, which felt unfair.

The tin rode against my chest the whole way, strapped under my jacket where it had ridden since the rapids. Inside: the surveyor's confession, signed and dated forty years before any charter the great families claimed to hold, along with the comparison sheets Ilsa had spent three years assembling. Half the originals were silt somewhere below the Wreck now. This was what survived. It would have to be enough, because there was no second vault, no second expedition, and no second monsoon coming to cover another attempt.

I got her up the switchback trail to the station as the light went copper behind the western rim. She managed the last hundred yards on her own feet, which I pretended not to notice mattered to her, and then she folded onto the operator's cot like something surrendered.

* * *

The station's interior smelled of dust and mouse and old varnish. A desk. A chair with three legs and one opinion. A hand-crank generator bolted to the floor, its handle worn bright by hands long dead. And the radio itself — a ranger-issue longwave set, model older than Tam, who had been born suspicious of everything and had every reason to continue.

I cranked the generator first, out of habit, and was rewarded with a stutter of light from the standby lamp before the belt slipped off the flywheel. Of course. Nothing in this country worked on the first try; that was practically the national religion.

While I wrestled the belt back on, Ilsa surfaced from the fever enough to watch me with one eye.

"You're going to send it," she said. Not a question. Her voice was gravel and rust.

"I'm going to think about sending it."

"That's what you call thinking? You've been staring at that radio like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money," I said. "Everything in this country owes somebody money. That's rather the point of the documents."

She laughed, then coughed, then laughed again through the coughing, which was worse to hear than either alone. "Ansel. Listen to me." She pushed herself up on one elbow, and even feverish, even gray-lipped, she had that look she used to get in the field when she'd found the thing everyone else had walked past. "If you transmit, they'll trace it inside a day. Maybe less. The families have listeners on every band between here and the coast. You know that."

"I know that."

"They'll trace it here, to this station, logged under your ranger code if you're stupid enough to use it, and then everything you did — letting me go, losing the gunboat, all of it — becomes conspiracy instead of negligence. Negligence gets you a desk. Conspiracy gets you a hole in the salt flats."

"Your bedside manner has really deteriorated since the academy."

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. You're always serious. It's one of the four things I love about you, and I've been trying very hard for three years not to count them."

That stopped her. Outside, the wind came down the Gap and made the mast hum a low note, like the building clearing its throat before saying something regrettable.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I'd been doing since the rapids, while I carried her and the tin and tried not to think about it, which is to say I'd been doing nothing else:

Option one. I transmit the evidence myself, tonight, on my own credentials. Clean signal, strong signal, full documentation attached. Every paper in the country has it by morning. And by the following morning, Lady Serevan's lawyers have traced the transmission to this station, this date, this ranger, and the story stops being about forged charters and starts being about a rogue officer with a personal connection to a known thief. The truth arrives wearing my face, and the families are very good at making faces the whole story. They've been doing it for forty years. It's why the charters held.

Option two. I let Ilsa take the tin downriver to the coastal papers herself, once the fever breaks. She vanishes — she's good at vanishing, it's the fifth thing — and the evidence surfaces clean, unattributable, deniable. My report stays a lie but a survivable one: equipment failure, storm losses, nothing recovered. Eleven survivors home. No career, but a life, and the truth loose in the world anyway.

Option two was obviously correct. Option two was what a smart man does.

The problem with option two was that it required Ilsa to travel four hundred miles of monsoon-swollen river, alone, hunted, with a fever that had already put her on the cot, carrying the only copy of the only proof that a nation had been lied to since its founding. If she died in the reeds somewhere below the Gap, the tin went into the water with her, and forty years of forgery slept soundly forever.

And the problem with option one was that it required me to be a martyr, and I have never trusted martyrs. In my experience they're usually just people who found a way to make their deaths more convenient than their choices.

So I lay awake on the station floor listening to Ilsa breathe — too fast, too shallow — and did what rangers do when the map runs out.

I looked for third options.

* * *

She was better by midnight. Not well — the fever had backed off to a dull burn, the way a fire backs off when it's decided to eat the house slowly instead of quickly — but lucid, sitting up against the wall with my jacket over her shoulders, watching me work on the generator with the expression of a woman cataloguing exits.

"The belt's shot," she said.

"The belt's tired. There's a difference."

"There's a spare in the maintenance locker. Behind the flares. There always was — Okafor, don't give me that look, I ran supply logistics out of Calloway for two years, I know what's in these lockers better than the quartermaster did."

I opened the locker. Behind the flare canisters, wrapped in waxed cloth gone brittle with age, was a drive belt, perished but intact. Ilsa Vey, three years a fugitive, still knew the ranger inventory better than I did. There was probably a lesson in that. I declined to learn it.

With the belt seated, the generator turned over willingly, and the standby lamp burned steady amber. The radio came up with a hiss like the river, patient and enormous.

I tuned across the bands while she watched. Static, static, a fishing cooperative's weather loop out of Meridian Port, static, and — faint, disciplined, ugly — a pattern of carrier tones that didn't belong to any civilian service. Family traffic. Serevan colors, if colors meant anything on a wavelength. They were already listening. They were always already listening; that was the whole architecture of the country.

"See?" Ilsa said quietly. "They're right there. Waiting for someone to be brave."

"Then we won't be brave," I said. "We'll be something else."

* * *

What we were, in the end, was thorough.

I spent an hour writing out the transmission package by hand first — the confession transcribed, the charter comparisons itemized, the chain of custody laid out in dry ranger prose that would bore a magistrate and terrify a lawyer. Then Ilsa, sweating but steady, checked every line against the documents themselves, because if we were going to do this, we were going to do it without a single error for them to hang doubt on.

Then I broke the package apart.

Not the content — the *shape*. I encrypted it in fragments using the old ranger field cipher, the one taught to every scout since before the collapse of the first republic, the one the families' cryptographers certainly knew. That was deliberate. I wanted it readable. What I didn't want was for it to look *sent*. So I didn't send it.

I scattered it.

Every relay station between Calloway Gap and the coast had a dead-letter buffer — a storage register where undeliverable traffic queued when a receiving station went dark. Most of those buffers flushed after ninety days. Some didn't flush at all; nobody had audited the network in a decade, because auditing wasn't glamorous and didn't pay. I keyed fragments of the package into the buffers at six stations across the band, each fragment addressed to a station that no longer existed, each one timestamped weeks in the past, each one buried among genuine garbage — failed weather reports, lost patrol check-ins, the sediment of a decade's neglect.

Anyone tracing live transmissions would find nothing, because there were none. Anyone sweeping the buffers later would find six harmless-looking fragments addressed to ghosts — unless someone knew to ask for them, in order, with the key phrase Ilsa and I agreed on, which I will not write down here or anywhere.

It wasn't elegant. It was ranger work: slow, unglamorous, built on the assumption that the system is lazy and you should build accordingly.

"There," I said, powering down. "No broadcast. No trace. Just six pieces of trash sitting in dead mailboxes, waiting for somebody who knows the knock."

Ilsa stared at me. "How long have you been planning that?"

"Since the rapids. Mostly while you were telling me I'd stolen your boots."

"I never said that."

"You said it twice. Very firmly. To a heron."

Something crossed her face that was almost the old laugh, and then it settled into something quieter and harder to name. "You understand what you've done, though. Even this. If it surfaces and they trace the fragments, the buffers, the timestamps — they'll know it was a ranger. They'll narrow it. It might take a year, but they'll come for whoever did this, and there aren't many rangers who could."

"There's exactly one," I said. "Me. And I've decided I can live with being suspected. It's being certain they can't abide. As long as my report stays clean, they've got nothing but a shape in the static."

"And your report?"

"Will say equipment failure, storm losses, nothing recovered." I heard myself say it and was surprised at how easy it was. Three years I'd imagined confronting her, arresting her, the speech I'd give. Instead I was co-authoring a lie with her at one in the morning in a dead relay station, and it felt like the most honest work I'd ever done.

* * *

But the tin still had to go downriver, and so did she, and dawn was coming up the Gap like a decision neither of us wanted to make.

I stood at the station door with the tin in my hands and the fever-heat still coming off her behind me, and I will admit — since the report won't — that for about four minutes I considered keeping it. Transmitting it myself, loud and clear and signed. Letting the truth arrive wearing my face after all, consequences be damned, martyrdom and all.

I didn't, and not because I was wise. Because I'd finally understood something about the difference between us. Ilsa stole the proof because she believed the truth needed a thief — someone outside the walls, someone the institution couldn't touch or predict. If I delivered it, it became an institutional act, contestable, containable, one ranger's word against forty years of charters. If she delivered it, it stayed what it needed to be: a leak. A crack. Water finding the flaw in the dam.

You can court-martial a ranger. You can't court-martial a rumor.

"Downstream," I said, "there's a fisherman's landing at Kiln Bend, thirty-one miles. Old man Sorrel runs it. He owes me from the flood two years back — I pulled his grandson out of a strainer. He doesn't ask questions and he owns the fastest skiff on this stretch. He'll run you to the delta if the water lets him."

"And from the delta?"

"From the delta, you're a ghost again. Your specialty." I held out the tin. "Coastal papers. Sen's testimony if she makes it out — and she will, she's tougher than both of us. Time it for the rains. Let it break when the roads are mud and the families can't move."

She took the tin. Her fingers were still hot. "And you?"

"I go back to Fort Calloway with eleven survivors and a report full of weather."

"Ansel—"

"Don't." I said it gently. "Don't make it a scene. Scenes leave marks, and marks get read. You taught me that, or I taught you, I honestly don't remember anymore." I looked at her — really looked, the way I hadn't let myself since the night she vanished, because looking had felt like losing. Fever-flushed, hollow-eyed, three years older and exactly the same. "When it breaks. When the charters fall. Don't come find me. Don't send word. Just — exist somewhere, loudly, in a way I can read about."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the best one I've ever had. My plans have historically involved arrest warrants."

She laughed — properly this time, the real one, the one I'd measured every other laugh against for three years — and then she kissed me, briefly, like signing something, and stepped out onto the trail with the tin under her arm.

At the switchback she stopped, turned, and threw something small and bright through the morning air. I caught it without looking. A flare. Unspent. Ranger issue, hers, the same make as the one she'd left on the gravel bar a lifetime ago upstream.

"For the report," she called down. "In case you need to mark where you found nothing."

Then she was gone down the trail, and the Gap swallowed her, and I stood there holding a flare and a clean lie and feeling lighter than a man in my position had any right to feel.

* * *

I gave her until the sun cleared the eastern rim — an hour, maybe a little more — before I touched the radio again.

One last piece of business. Not the evidence; that was sleeping in dead letter queues, patient as sediment. This was different. This was a single data burst, high power, wide band, three seconds long, transmitting nothing but noise — except the noise was structured, and the structure was a signature, and the signature said: *it exists, it moved, look for it.*

I keyed it at 0600 sharp, from the Calloway Gap frequency, and then I stripped the transmitter's tuning capacitor with a screwdriver so the burst couldn't be repeated or precisely characterized. Let their direction-finders chew on that. By the time they triangulated the source, the source would be a broken radio in an abandoned station and a ranger a hundred miles away filing paperwork.

They'd trace it. Of course they'd trace it — the families were patient, and rich, and mean, and they would pull this thread for months. But they'd be pulling it backward, toward a ghost signal, while the actual truth traveled downriver in a tin box on a fisherman's skiff, timed to surface with the first clean week after the rains.

Sacrifice without martyrdom. Ruin without a corpse. I was rather proud of it, in the way a man is proud of a bridge that holds just barely, and only once.

I climbed down from the Gap as the heat came on, and turned north toward Fort Calloway, and eleven survivors, and the report I'd already begun composing in my head — *equipment failure, storm losses, nothing recovered* — each phrase polished smooth as river stone.

Behind me, the broken radio ticked as it cooled, like something counting.

Ahead, four hundred miles of bad road, one fort, and a woman named Serevan who read reports the way sharks read water.

I walked toward all of it smiling, which worried me more than anything else that had happened all year.
