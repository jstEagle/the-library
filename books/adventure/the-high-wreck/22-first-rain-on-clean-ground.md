## 22. First Rain on Clean Ground

They took my rank in a room with good acoustics, which I suppose was a kindness. It meant everyone heard the sentence clearly: stripped of commission, retained as a civilian consultant, no charges filed. The tribunal officer read it out the way you'd read a grocery list, and Lady Serevan sat in the gallery with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she'd paid for.

I want to be fair to her here, because history won't be. She didn't have me shot. That was on the table — I know it was, because Doran Hale's surviving associates told me so later, in the flat tones of men describing weather that had almost killed them. Serevan could have buried me in the same grave as the Meridian Expedition and nobody upriver would have asked twice. Instead she let the tribunal strip my badge, and when I walked past her afterward, she said only one thing.

"You wrote nothing in your report, Ranger Okafor. Nothing at all."

"Equipment failure," I said. "Storm losses. Nothing recovered."

"Yes," she said. "I've read poetry before."

She didn't press. That was the bargain neither of us named aloud: her silence for mine, her family's ruin held off by inquiry committees and procedural fog while the rest of the country burned through the truth like dry grass through a spark. She was going down slow, and she knew I knew, and there was something almost dignified in how she chose not to make either of us say it.

The charters broke eleven days after I filed the report.

* * *

You'd think a thing like that comes all at once — headlines, sirens, the great families dragged into the square. It doesn't. Truth moves like water finding grade. First it was the coastal papers, Dr. Sen's name above the fold beside scans of the surveyor's confession, that poor dead man's handwriting laid out in facsimile so every reader could watch him admit what he'd done a century ago: copied, altered, backdated. Then the wire services picked it up. Then the provincial archives started "voluntarily reviewing" their holdings, which is bureaucratic language for lawyers sprinting ahead of subpoenas.

The Serevan charter was suspended pending inquiry on a Tuesday. I remember because I was eating eggs at the Fort Calloway mess — they still let me eat there, though I no longer rated a seat at the command table — and Tam came sliding across the bench with his radio handset still in one hand, eyes enormous.

"It's suspended," he said. "The whole charter. They're talking about land commissions. Redistribution hearings." He said *redistribution* the way you'd say *miracle*, carefully, afraid of jinxing it.

Tam had come through the High Wreck with a cracked rib and a decoded transmission and the particular hardness that gets forged into people who hear a woman's voice say *turn back* on a dead channel and live to understand why. He'd testified at my tribunal, actually, unprompted, until the panel cut him off. Afterward he'd bought me a drink at the fort canteen and asked, very seriously, whether I regretted it.

"The report?" I'd said.

"Any of it. The raft. Letting her go. All of it."

I thought about the question for the length of a swallow. "I regret the men we couldn't bring home," I said. "The rest of it was arithmetic."

He'd nodded like that settled something for him, which I suspect it did. Young people need the world to be solvable. Some days I envy them that.

Eleven survivors. That's what I brought down from the canyons, and I count it as the only number that ever mattered in the whole affair. Not the documents — those were never mine to keep. Not the charter — that was always going to fall, one way or another; the families had been building on rot for a hundred years and rain finds rot eventually. Eleven people, breathing, fed, delivered to their families with the story I gave them rehearsed on the raft ride downstream: equipment failure, storm losses, nothing recovered.

Sen hadn't needed rehearsing. Amara Sen stood in front of the coastal press two weeks after landfall and told the whole thing straight, names and dates and body counts, her voice level as a surveyor's line, and when a reporter asked why she hadn't come forward sooner, she said, "Because a ranger bought us time with his career, and I intend to spend it loudly." I listened to the broadcast in my quarters and felt something I don't have a clean word for. Pride wearing grief's coat, maybe.

* * *

So: weeks later. Rains finished early this year, the tail of the monsoon dragging itself east, and the salt flats west of Calloway were drying into that blinding white mirror I'd spent three years learning to read. I went out because I always went out. A man doesn't stop being a tracker because a tribunal took his tin star. The flats don't care about credentials anyway. They just lie there under the sun, keeping their records better than any archive vault ever did.

I wasn't looking for anything. That's what I told myself, and it was even mostly true.

Three years I'd measured my life in distance covered. Miles, not years — that was the joke I made, the private ledger where I kept myself instead of a calendar. Forty miles a day across hardpan, twenty through dune sea, and somewhere in all those miles a woman's trail signature stitched through the record of my days like a second heartbeat. You learn a person's gait if you follow it long enough. Ilsa Vey walked like she was arguing with the ground and expected to win — weight forward, stride long, the left boot striking fractionally heavier than the right from an old ankle break she never let slow her down. I could pick that signature out of a herd of cattle. I had picked it out of a gravel bar in a drowned city at midnight, and everything since had grown from that single print like a crack through glass.

That morning the flats were perfect. Recent wind had swept them clean, then a light rain had set the surface just enough to hold a print without slumping it, and the light was low and raking, the kind that turns a depression into a shadow and a shadow into a signature.

I found the tracks nine miles out from the Gap road, heading north.

Left heavy. Right light. Long stride, argumentative. Fresh — hours old, edges still crumb-sharp, no insect traffic through the walls. My heart did something unprofessional, some arrhythmic leap that a tribunal would certainly have found grounds for further discipline.

Beside the third print, planted upright in the salt crust, was a flare.

Unspent. Ranger issue, emergency pattern, the red-and-white casing faded from years in a pack but intact. The same kind I'd found on the gravel bar outside the High Wreck, the night I'd followed her prints into the reeds and lost her and understood, standing alone in the dark with an unburned flare in my hand, that she'd left it for me deliberately. A message in the only language two rangers share: *I'm alive. I'm choosing to be found or not found, and I'm telling you which.*

On the gravel bar it had meant *not yet.*

This one — I turned it over. Scratched into the casing, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking, three words in her quick slanted hand:

*NORTH. CATCH UP.*

* * *

Here's the thing about oaths. Everyone assumes they shatter — one grand moment, one burning bridge, one choice between loyalty and truth, and after that you're free or damned. But I burned my oath in front of witnesses months ago, standing in a flooded camp with a gunboat waiting on the river, and what I learned is that oaths don't shatter. They compost. They break down into something richer and stranger, and things grow in the ruins that would never have taken root in the clean ground.

I'd told Dr. Sen the truth about the charters with Hale's gunboat crew listening through the walls, and I'd done it knowing it ended me. And it had — ended the ranger, anyway. What it started was harder to name. A man who files a report that says nothing, while the truth surfaces anyway on someone else's courage. A man stripped of rank but not of the only skill that ever mattered to him, walking a salt flat with the sun in his teeth, reading the ground the way other men read scripture.

And a woman, three years gone and three months touched, somewhere north of me with a head start of hours and a gait I could follow through the end of the world.

I thought about the night in the Long Dark, both of us on watch with the storm building over the canyon rim, when she finally told me why she ran. Because I'd have had to arrest her. Because she loved me too much to make me choose, and too honestly to stay and lie about why she wouldn't. I'd called it cowardice for three years. Standing in that flooded dark, watching lightning walk the rim of the Wreck, I'd finally called it what it was — the same arithmetic I'd used on the raft. Regret the losses. Do the math on what survives.

She'd chosen to survive. So had I. Now the ground was telling me the sums were still open.

I crouched over the prints a while longer than any professional needed to. Checked the stride against my memory, confirmed what my heart already knew — no pack drag, good pace, someone moving well and unhurried, someone who knew exactly who would find these tracks and how long it would take him. She'd even timed it. Left camp, by my estimate, midmorning. Knew I walked the flats after the rains. Knew I'd be nine miles out by early afternoon, because she'd known my patterns longer than anyone alive, including me.

An unspent flare and three scratched words. That's the whole love letter. Rangers aren't poets. We're readers.

I stood up. My knee — the one that had taken the gunboat's rail during the wreck run — announced its opinion, and I ignored it, the way I'd been ignoring it since the canyon. Somewhere south behind me, Fort Calloway was holding its morning muster without me, a formation one man smaller, and nobody in it would miss me by nightfall except maybe Tam, who'd wave. Somewhere south beyond that, the inquiry boards were sharpening their questions for Lady Serevan, and the land commissions were printing maps of a country that suddenly belonged to everybody again, and Amara Sen was somewhere being loud about all of it, bless her.

None of that needed a ranger. The nation had doctors of history and coastal newspapers and redistribution hearings for the work ahead. What the north had was a woman walking like an argument, getting away from me at a rate of roughly four miles an hour, and a head start I intended to spend the rest of my life failing to close, if I was lucky.

If I was lucky. Three years ago I'd have called that a contradiction. Chasing Ilsa Vey had been duty, and duty was supposed to taste like iron and end badly. This — boots on fresh rain-set salt, sun climbing, a trail pointing north toward country I'd never surveyed — this tasted like the first hour of a shift you actually wanted.

I smiled. Out loud, alone on a white flat under a washed sky, grinning like an idiot at some boot prints, and I'm not ashamed to say it took a while to stop.

Then I squared my hat, set my compass to north out of pure ceremony — the tracks were direction enough — and followed.

The miles came easy. For the first time in my life, I stopped counting them.
