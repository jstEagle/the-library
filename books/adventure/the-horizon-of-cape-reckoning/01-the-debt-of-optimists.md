## 1. The Debt of Optimists

There is a particular kind of fool who looks at a black wall of cloud rolling off the ocean and thinks, *that will photograph beautifully.* I am that fool, and I have made a modest living off it for eleven years, which by the accounting standards of Cape Reckoning counts as either talent or a slow-motion suicide attempt. The jury remains out. The jury has, in fact, been drinking.

The squall came in that morning like it had somewhere to be. I'd watched it building since dawn from the window above my kitchen — a bruise on the horizon, swelling, going the color of a bad decision. By nine I had the camera bag packed, the tripod over my shoulder, and my boots laced with the particular urgency of a man who knows exactly how long a storm gives you before it stops being a subject and starts being an executioner.

The cliffs south of the harbor are the best vantage on the cape. Forty meters of iron-stained rock dropping into water the gray of old dishwater, wind coming up the face hard enough to lean into like a friend who owes you money — which, I note for accuracy's sake, describes everyone in my life these days.

I found my spot, planted the tripod legs into the turf, and waited. This is the part people don't understand about storm photography. They think it's about the storm. It isn't. It's about the waiting, the way a fisherman waits, reading the water, feeling the pressure drop against your eardrums until they ache like a held breath. The storm does what it wants. Your job is to be standing in exactly the right place when it decides to show you something no one has ever seen before and will never see again.

That's the honest appeal of it, anyway. There's also the fact that when you're staring down a storm, you're not thinking about anything else. Not the ledger in the drawer. Not the letters with the red stamps. Not the number that lives in your chest now like a second heartbeat, except this one counts backward.

Four thousand two hundred and sixty pounds. That was the number as of Tuesday. Interest adds roughly eleven pounds a day, so by the time I got home tonight it would be four thousand two hundred seventy-one, give or take Voss's rounding habits, which always round up because God loves optimism but Voss loves compound interest more.

The first gust hit at 9:40. I felt it come up the cliff face through my boots before I heard it in the grass, and I dropped behind the tripod and started shooting.

Here is what I can tell you about storms, having chased them along this coast since I was nineteen years old with my father's borrowed camera and my mother's borrowed despair: they have personalities. This one was a brawler. No elegance to it, no long spiraling approach — just a front of rain and wind shouldering in off the water like a drunk looking for his coat. But ten minutes in, it did something. A waterspout, small, maybe two hundred meters out, spinning up off the wave-tops white as bone and holding there, rotating, alive, for perhaps ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds is an eternity if you're ready and nothing at all if you're not. I was ready. My hands knew the dials without my eyes. I fired forty frames and in three of them — I could see it even on the little screen, even with rain hammering the glass — in three of them the spout stood against the darkening sky with the sun breaking through behind it, and the whole thing looked less like weather than like a door.

A door. Christ. Even my metaphors were in debt.

*Marlowe's Maritime Journal* wanted six images for a feature on the cape's storm season, and their editor, a woman named Petra who communicated exclusively in deadlines and disappointment, paid eighty pounds per usable frame. Three frames of spout plus whatever else survived the edit might mean two hundred fifty, maybe three hundred pounds. Against four thousand two hundred seventy-one. Against the boat payment due Friday. Against the mooring fees, the harbor dues, the fuel bill I'd been carrying since March like a stone in my boot.

I shot the squall's whole passage anyway. Two hours of it, soaked to the skin, grinning like an idiot every time the light did something impossible. Because here's the other true thing, the thing I don't say out loud: when I'm out there with the wind trying to take the camera out of my hands, I'm good. Not good-for-a-debt-ridden-coastal-freelancer good. Actually good. People pay for my storms. Magazines put them on covers. There was a year — I was twenty-six — when a gallery in the city hung four of my prints and a man with a scarf bought one for more money than my father made in a season, and I thought, *this is it, this is the life, the debt was just the toll.*

The debt was not the toll. The debt was the road.

By noon the squall had spent itself against the headland and moved inland to ruin someone else's afternoon, and I packed up and walked back along the cliff path with wet trousers and the warm, specific satisfaction of work done well. For approximately twenty minutes, I was a happy man. I want that on the record. Whatever happened next, for twenty minutes on the cliff path above Cape Reckoning, Ines Marchetti was happy.

The harbor came into view below me and something was wrong with it, the way a room is wrong when furniture has been moved in the dark. It took me a hundred meters to identify the problem: the *Kestrel* wasn't where I'd left her.

My boat. Twelve meters of converted fishing trawler, diesel engine held together by prayer and a mechanic named Salvo who charged me in beer, cabin big enough for a bunk, a table, and all my failures arranged chronologically. I'd left her on my mooring at dawn with the canvas cover lashed down. Now she sat tied up at the commercial quay beside the fish-processing shed, and standing on her deck with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a cathedral was Mr. Tobias Voss.

Voss. Let me describe him properly, because he deserves it. Voss is a large man gone soft in the way of men who've never once needed to hurry — broad, pink-faced, with small blue eyes set close together and a smile that arrives a half-second before any reason for it. He dresses well. He speaks quietly. He has never, in the four years I've known him, raised his voice or his hand, because he has never needed to. The law and the tide do his raising for him. He is, and I say this with professional respect for craftsmanship wherever I find it, the most cheerful man I have ever feared.

"Ines!" he called down, arms spreading wide as I came down the quay ladder. "There he is! The storm artist himself. Wonderful morning for it, I hear. Waterspout, did you get it? Tell me you got the spout."

"Give her back, Toby."

"Her." He pressed a hand to his chest, wounded. "You hear that? *Her.* Four years of missed payments and he still talks about the boat like she's family." He shook his head fondly. "Come aboard, come aboard. Mind the gull mess, they've no manners."

I climbed up onto the deck of my own impounded vessel, dripping, smelling of salt and ozone, and Voss received me like a host. That's the thing about him — he makes impounding feel like hospitality.

"I'll come to the point," he said, "because I know you're eager to get back under your canvas." He patted the wheel housing gently, the way you'd touch a horse. "Friday. Two thousand, and I release her today, and we forget the unpleasantness. Or—" he spread those soft hands, "—we go to the magistrate Monday morning, and he sells her at auction, and you and I both watch strangers carry pieces of her away. And after costs and my principal, there may even be a little left over. Toward the rest."

"The rest" being another two thousand two hundred seventy-one pounds, secured against precisely nothing, because Voss had stopped requiring collateral from me around year two, when it became clear that I myself was the collateral.

"Toby, I'll have the two thousand. I've got a commission—"

"Marlowe's Journal." He nodded sagely. "Petra Kessler. Eighty a frame, yes? She told me herself, over lunch, while I was telling her about your situation. Lovely woman. Very sympathetic." He smiled his half-second-early smile. "So let us do arithmetic together, you and I, because I know how you love numbers. Best case, three hundred pounds by Thursday. You need two thousand. That leaves a gap of seventeen hundred pounds in four days." He clicked his tongue. "Even your skies don't pay that fast."

The rain had gotten into my collar and was running down my spine, cold as a finger. On the quay below, fishermen went about their morning with the elaborate unconcern of people pretending not to watch. Everyone on this coast knows Voss's voice. Everyone knows what it means when a boat moves to the commercial quay.

"You could take the camera," I heard myself say. "The gear's worth—"

"The gear is worth eight hundred and would fetch me four, and then what would you be?" He said it kindly. Genuinely kindly, which was the horror of it. "Ines Marchetti without a camera is a man waiting to die politely. I've no use for that. I lent to you because you're talented, and I've stayed patient because you're stubborn, and stubbornness eventually pays or it doesn't, but either way it's interesting." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register he probably used at church. "But Friday is real. The magistrate is real. So here is my advice, free of charge, which is rare from me: sell me something true by Friday, or accept that the sea keeps what the sea keeps."

He said it lightly. He couldn't have known — no one could have known yet — that the sea was listening, and had opinions, and was already sending someone up the coast toward my door with salt water in his lungs and a folded paper against his heart.

But I didn't know that either. All I knew was the number, ticking upward in my chest — eleven pounds a day, and Friday coming down the channel like weather.

"Monday," Voss said cheerfully, stepping past me onto the quay, pausing to touch my shoulder with one soft hand. "If it helps — and I think it does — you're not the worst off I've seen this month. There's a prospector gone missing up the coast, owed money to worse men than me. Men who don't smile." He chuckled at some private joke. "Count yourself lucky you're only mine."

Then he walked away whistling, a fat man with a light step, and left me standing on my own deck, which was no longer mine, holding a camera bag full of photographs of a door in the sky that opened onto nothing at all.

I stood there a long time. Long enough that the gulls got bored of me. Long enough that the tide turned beneath the hull and the mooring lines creaked their slow arithmetic, load and load and load.

Three days. Seventeen hundred pounds.

People ask — the ones who ask anything — why I don't leave. Why I don't take the camera and catch the ferry south and start over somewhere the debts can't follow. And the answer is stupid, and it's true, and it's the same answer Aldous Wren would give me two nights later with his blood on my floorboards, though neither of us would know we were giving it:

Because everything I am is nailed to this coastline. Because the storms here know my name. Because a man can survive owing money, but he cannot survive owing himself an apology.

I climbed down off the *Kestrel*, walked the length of the quay past the windows that weren't watching me, and went home to count what I had, which was thirty-eight pounds, a bottle of bad whisky, and a skill for standing in exactly the right place when the sky decides to show you something.

That night the fog came in off the water the way it does at Cape Reckoning — not arriving, just thickening, as if the sea exhaled — and I sat at my kitchen table doing sums that refused to sum, and I heard, very faintly under the foghorn's long complaint, footsteps on the shingle below my door.

Heavy ones. Uneven. Stopping.

Then a weight falling against wood, and a voice, barely carried through the fog, saying words I would spend the rest of this story paying for:

"Marchetti. They told me — Marchetti. Please be home."
