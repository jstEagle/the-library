## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at half past two in the morning, which is the hour when knocks stop being knocks and start being omens.

I was awake anyway. I'd been awake since Voss's men towed the *Pelican* out of her berth that afternoon, and sleep and I had been circling each other like dogs deciding whether to fight. My cottage sits at the top of Harrow Lane, close enough to the water that on a rough night you can hear the sea chewing at the seawall, and it was a rough night — a proper Cape Reckoning fog, the kind that comes off the water thick as wool and swallows the harbor light whole. I'd been lying there doing arithmetic. It's what debtors do instead of dreaming. Four hundred and twelve pounds still owed, plus interest accruing at a rate Voss described as "friendly," plus the impound fees now stacking up against my boat like snow on a roof. Every sum ended the same way: nowhere.

So when the knock came — three blows, weak ones, more suggestion than demand — I lay still and listened.

Then it came again, and this time there was a sound underneath it. A sliding. Something heavy going down against my door the way a sack of grain goes down when the rope gives.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is rare currency in this town and I've spent most of mine. The smart move was to stay in bed. Cape Reckoning has a population of people who learned long ago that doors opened after midnight tend to cost money, and I was a man with no money and less sense about spending what little I had left. Voss would pay for information. Voss paid for everything; it was practically his religion. And whoever was dying on my doorstep at half past two was almost certainly worth something to somebody, because nobody pursues a man worth nothing.

I got up anyway. I've thought about that a great deal since, and the best explanation I can offer is this: some part of me — the part that chases storms, the part that stands on cliffs photographing weather that could kill me because it's beautiful — has never once done the arithmetic. That part heard a man falling down and stood up before the rest of me could object.

I opened the door and he fell into my kitchen.

* * *

He was soaked through, which told me he'd come up from the water side, along the rocks below the point where there's no path unless you make one. He was old — seventy if he was a day, maybe older, the kind of old the coast manufactures out of salt and stubbornness. His left arm hung wrong, cradled against his chest, and there was blood on his shirt that wasn't all his own, though some of it clearly was. His face was gray as the fog outside. But his eyes were open, and they were the sharpest thing about him, pale and quick, taking inventory of my kitchen, my face, my hands, in about a second and a half.

"Wrong house," he said.

"You're welcome," I said.

"I said wrong house." He tried to push himself up off my floor and failed, which seemed to offend him more than the pain did. "I'm looking for the Marchetti place."

"This is the Marchetti place."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not relief exactly. Recalculation. "Ines Marchetti?"

"That depends who's asking and what they're offering."

That got a sound out of him that might have been a laugh if laughs were made of gravel. "Still alive, then. Good." And then he fainted, which struck me as poor manners given how the conversation had been going.

I got him onto the floor by the stove, because getting him onto the bed would have required stairs and strength I didn't have, and I built up the fire and did what you do for a drowned cat: stripped off the wet, wrapped him in blankets, checked the arm. Not broken, I didn't think. Dislocated, maybe, or torn deep in the shoulder. There was a gash above his ear that had bled a good deal and stopped, which is either good news or very bad news depending on what's underneath. His hands told me things. Rope-callused, scarred across the knuckles, nails blackened — a working man's hands, but not a fisherman's. Fishermen's hands are uniform in their ruin. These were hands that had dug, hauled, pried, and fought, in roughly that order.

A prospector, then. Or a salvager. On this coast those words mean nearly the same thing and both mean trouble.

While the fire took hold, I went to the window and looked out at the lane. Fog everywhere, pressing against the glass like something wanting in. No lanterns moving. No voices. But I stood there longer than I needed to, because I'd heard something else under the wind on my way back from the window — or thought I had. Out on the water, faint, an engine running low and patient. The kind of engine a boat runs when it isn't going anywhere in particular and wants everyone to know it could.

Calloway boats idle like that. Everyone in the harbor knows the sound. You hear it outside your window at night and you check your mooring lines twice in the morning.

I closed the curtain and went back to my guest.

* * *

He woke near dawn, all at once, the way soldiers wake — no surfacing, just arrival. His hand shot out and caught my wrist before I knew he was conscious, and his grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in leather, and for one clear moment I understood that this old man had killed people, possibly recently, possibly with these hands.

"Easy," I said. "You're in my kitchen. Nobody's here but us."

His eyes did their inventory again — stove, curtains, my face — and the grip loosened. "Marchetti."

"Ines. And you are bleeding on my floor, so I'd appreciate a name to curse properly."

"Aldous Wren." He said it flatly, like a man reading his own headstone. Then, watching me: "The name mean anything?"

"It means nothing to me."

"Good." He relaxed a fraction. "Keep it that way."

I fed him broth because it was all I had, and he ate like a man who understood food as fuel rather than pleasure, methodically, without comment. Between spoonfuls he watched me work — I was rewrapping his shoulder, and I'll admit my technique owed more to enthusiasm than training — and after a while he said, "You know who's looking for me."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it.

"Calloway," he said anyway. "Three of them came off a tender at Gull Rock tonight. I put one in the water." He touched his ribs and winced. "They returned the favor. With interest."

"The Calloway fleet doesn't chase old men up cliffs at night for nothing," I said. "What did you take from them?"

"Nothing." For the first time he smiled, and it transformed his face from grim to wolfish. "That's the beauty of it. I didn't take anything. I *found* something. There's a difference, and it's the difference between theft and discovery, and Calloway Salvage has never once in forty years understood the difference."

I tied off the bandage and sat back. Outside, the fog was turning from black to the color of dirty milk, which meant dawn was considering its options. Somewhere down the lane a gull started up its usual complaint.

"Look," I said. "Wren. Aldous Wren. I don't know what you found and I don't want to know. What I know is this: I owe four hundred and twelve pounds to Dominic Voss, my boat is impounded at his dock, and Voss would pay real money to hand a wounded prospector to the Calloway fleet. Real money. Enough money that I might see the *Pelican* again before Christmas." I held up my hands. "So understand what I'm telling you when I say you can stay until you can walk. And then you have to go somewhere that isn't here."

He looked at me for a long time. Old men look at you differently; they've already read every book you're pretending to write.

"You could name your price," he said. "Right now. I'd pay it."

"I don't want your money."

"Everyone wants money. Money's the only honest want there is."

"Not tonight it isn't." I stood and carried the bowl to the basin, mostly so I wouldn't have to keep looking at him. "Tonight you were drowning on my doorstep and I opened the door. That's the whole story. Don't make it complicated."

Behind me, quiet as fog itself: "Your boat. The *Pelican*. She's the storm-chaser. The one that photographs weather."

"She was. Now she's collateral."

"Shame. Fine hull. I watched her work the squall off the point last week. Came closer to that wall cloud than any sane man would." A pause. "Sane men don't find anything worth finding, Marchetti."

I turned around. He was watching me with those quick pale eyes, and there was something in his face I couldn't read yet — appraisal, maybe. A man measuring timber.

"Sleep," I said. "If anyone knocks, you're my uncle and you're drunk."

"I've never been drunk in my life."

"Then lie convincingly."

* * *

They came at midday, which surprised me. I'd expected night again — men like that prefer darkness the way honest men prefer daylight — but perhaps they figured a wounded man wouldn't move twice in one day. Perhaps they were right. Perhaps that was the plan all along, and I was simply too slow to see it coming.

Two of them, walking up Harrow Lane like they owned the cobblestones, which in every way that mattered they did. Calloway men wear a particular look: clean-shaven, well-fed, jackets cut for movement, eyes that price everything including you. The tall one knocked on my door with a politeness that made my skin crawl.

I stepped out and pulled the door shut behind me. "Morning."

"Afternoon," he said pleasantly. "Looking for a relative of yours. Older gentleman. Been in the water, by the look of reports. Might have come up this way."

"Haven't seen him."

"Might have." He produced a card and held it out. On it, embossed: T. HALLORAN, RECOVERY OPERATIONS, CALLOWAY SALVAGE & MARINE. Underneath, handwritten, a figure that made my stomach drop through the floorboards and land somewhere in the harbor mud. "For information leading to recovery. Paid same day. Mr. Voss can vouch for our arrangements — we understand you two have a relationship."

Voss. Of course. Voss had already sold my name, probably before Halloran's boots hit the dock. That's what creditors sell when they run short of patience: you. Your address, your habits, your desperation, itemized.

"He's not here," I said.

"No?" Halloran smiled. He had the smile of a man who'd never once needed a fist. "Mind if we look? Only takes a minute."

"Mind if you have a warrant?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts over something large passing beneath. "Mr. Marchetti. We're not police. We're not bound by police manners. But we are reasonable, and we'd rather be reasonable than thorough, if you follow me."

"I follow you perfectly," I said. "And he's not here."

Halloran looked at me for a long five seconds. Behind him, his partner drifted casually toward the side of the cottage, toward the window of the room where an old man lay breathing shallow under my only spare blanket, and I felt my heart go strange and slow, the way it does when a wave stands up taller than you thought the sea could make.

Then the partner stopped. Cocked his head. And out of the fog down the lane came the sound of Odile Brandt's dog — the ugliest animal in Cape Reckoning, a creature composed entirely of noise and malice — losing its mind at something. At them, as it happened, because Odile was coming up the path with a basket of mending and no idea she was about to save a stranger's life, and the dog announced her approach like the end of days.

Halloran glanced back. Considered. Two witnesses now, and a lane filling with the ordinary machinery of a small town's afternoon.

"Another time," he said, and touched his hat brim, and they walked back down the way they'd come, unhurried, certain, the certainty of men who believe the world is a ledger and every account settles eventually.

I stood on my step until they were gone, and then I went inside and sat down on the floor next to Aldous Wren, because my legs had opinions about standing.

"They'll be back," he said.

"I know."

"Sooner than later. And they won't knock."

"I know that too."

He was quiet a while. The fire ticked. Down in the harbor, faint through the fog, I could hear that patient engine idling, going nowhere, going everywhere, waiting.

"Marchetti," Wren said at last, and there was something different in his voice now, something that had settled while I'd been out front gambling both our lives on a locked door and a neighbor's ugly dog. "How much do you actually owe Voss?"

"Four hundred and twelve pounds. Why?"

"And your boat's impounded. And you're a man who sails into storms for photographs because nobody sane will pay you to sail into them for anything better." He pushed himself up against the wall, wincing, until he was sitting upright, and looked at me with those pale sharp eyes, and I saw that whatever he'd been calculating since he fell through my door, he'd finished. "I think it's time you and I talked about the Meridian."

I didn't ask what the Meridian was. Some part of me already knew that once I asked, the door would be open — really open, the way it had been at half past two in the morning — and that whatever came through it next, I would not be able to send away.

But I asked anyway.
