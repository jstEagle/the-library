## 4. Six Tides

Wren died the way the fog came in — without announcement, without drama, just a slow thickening of absence until you realized the room had changed.

I'd dozed in the chair by his bed, and when I woke at first light the kettle was cold and he wasn't breathing. His face had settled into something almost peaceful, which felt like a personal insult. Men like Wren weren't supposed to get peaceful endings. They were supposed to go out mid-swear-word, mid-bargain, mid-scheme, leaving debts and grudges scattered behind them like chum. Instead he'd slipped off between one tide and the next, quiet as a man settling a tab.

I sat there for a while with my hand on his wrist, feeling nothing arrive where a pulse used to be, and I thought about how little it takes to make a stranger into a responsibility. Two nights. One fever. One oath sworn at three in the morning to a man whose grip was stronger than his lungs. That's all it took. Now there was a body in my bed, a map in my oilskin pouch, and a promise sitting on my chest like ballast.

"Damn you," I told him, without heat. He'd have appreciated that. He'd struck me, in our one long feverish night, as a man who collected curses the way other men collected stamps.

The fog outside was doing its Cape Reckoning impression — the whole world shrunk down to forty feet of gray wool and the sound of water going about its business below the cliffs. Somewhere out there, gulls were arguing over something dead. The harbor would be waking up. Voss's runners would be waking up. And somewhere in the back rooms of the Calloway fleet's offices, men who ironed their shirts were probably already awake too, because men who iron their shirts never sleep properly.

I made tea because tea is what you do when there's nothing else to do, and I drank it standing at the window looking at nothing, and then I got to work on the business of the dead.

* * *

Aldous Wren weighed less than he should have. I wrapped him in his own oilskin — the good one, patched at the elbows, salt-stiffened — and carried him down the cliff path before the fog lifted enough for anyone to see a storm chaser hauling a corpse toward the old chapel yard above Gullwater Cove. The path knew me. Twenty years of chasing weather along these cliffs will teach your feet things your head never learns, and my feet took us down the switchbacks while my head ran numbers it didn't want to run.

Father Anselm owed me. Three years back I'd photographed the storm damage to the chapel roof for the insurance claim, and I'd done it for free, and I'd also not mentioned the collection box money that went missing the same week a certain priest developed a fondness for a certain brand of whisky. Not blackmail, exactly. More like an understanding between two men who'd each seen the other's ledger.

"He came ashore two nights ago," I said. "Storm took him. Exposure. He had no people."

Anselm looked at the bundle, then at me, then at the bundle again. "No people."

"No people that anyone will miss quickly."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you're going to bury him quietly, under a stone that says something kind but vague, and you're not going to enter him in the parish register until I tell you to."

"That," said Anselm, "is a sin."

"So is the whisky. We've discussed it."

He sighed the sigh of a man whose sins were all secondhand, and took the body from me with more gentleness than I expected, and that was the last kindness Aldous Wren received on this earth: a drunk priest lowering him into ground he'd never seen, witnessed by a man who owed him everything and couldn't say so.

I walked back up the cliff path and unfolded the map.

* * *

Here's what I knew by noon, sitting at my kitchen table with the map weighted down by a tin cup and every object in my flat that had any heft:

The Meridian had gone down forty-one years ago in the Serrated Reach, past the cape, in waters so treacherous that the charts still carried warnings written in the handwriting of men who'd died verifying them. Officially she'd been carrying timber and machine parts. Unofficially — according to a dying prospector who'd spent eleven years and most of his blood finding the truth — she'd been carrying a strongroom's worth of gold bullion and company papers, and she hadn't gone down by accident.

Wren's map was drawn from memory and soundings, annotated in a hand that got shakier as it went, the way a life does. It showed a channel through the Reach that only filled deep enough to dive twice a day at spring tides, and it showed a window: six tides, maybe seven if the weather held merciful, before the channel silted shut for the season. After that, the Meridian might as well be on the moon.

Six tides. I counted them out on my fingers like a child learning arithmetic, and the arithmetic came back ugly. Tide tables don't negotiate. The first usable tide was tomorrow morning. The last one closed four days after that. Which meant I had roughly seventy-two hours to do something that sane people would take a season to attempt.

And I had no boat. Voss had impounded the *Kestrel* — my boat, my home-away-from-home, my one asset besides a camera and a reputation for being lucky near storms — against a debt that had grown teeth over five years of interest. And I had no crew, because crews cost wages, and my entire liquid fortune at that moment consisted of eleven dollars and some coins in a jar labeled BOAT, which Voss had presumably already spent in his imagination.

I laid the map flat and stared at it until the lines started swimming, and I'll be honest with you, whoever you are reading this: for about ten minutes I tried very hard to find the version of myself that folded it back up and put it in a drawer. The version that let the Calloway fleet have their gold, let the dead stay dead, let Mara Wren — whoever and wherever she was — go on not knowing her father had thought of her at the end. That version of me lived longer, probably. That version didn't end up nearly drowned in a storm, though I didn't know that yet, so I can't claim credit for refusing him on those grounds.

What I refused him on was simpler. Wren had looked at me — a stranger, a debtor, a man with nothing — and decided I was the safest place in the world to put his daughter's name. You don't get chosen for things like that often. Maybe once. And you don't get to be unworthy of it afterward and still look yourself in the mirror while you shave.

So. Six tides. One map. No boat, no crew, no money, and a promise.

In my experience, that's actually a respectable starting position. It's the men who start with boats and money who turn out to owe the sea something.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of tar, tea, and the particular staleness of a room where decisions are avoided professionally. Harbormaster Quill was a small damp man built like a bollard, and he greeted me with the wary warmth of someone who'd been paid recently to be friendly to me and hadn't quite reconciled it with his conscience.

"Ines Marchetti." He said my name like he was checking it against a list. He probably was. "Heard you had a rough night. Storm damage?"

"A guest overstayed. How's the fleet?"

"Calloway?" Quill's teacup paused halfway to his mouth, which told me everything before his words did. "Provisioning. Three tenders and the *Corvus* taking on coal and water since dawn. Fresh rigging going aboard. They've chartered the pilotage rights for the outer reach, too — bought up every licensed chart copy in the shop."

"Bought up charts," I said. "For a salvage run they announced... when?"

Quill set the cup down and became very interested in a paperwork tray. "They filed notice yesterday evening. Salvage survey, Serrated Reach, spring-tide window. All proper and legal."

Filed yesterday evening. Wren had come stumbling to my door the night before that, half-dead, pursued. Which meant either the fleet had gotten hold of a fragment of the map through channels I didn't want to imagine, or they'd been hunting Wren long enough to reconstruct his route from his movements. Either way, the conclusion was the same, and Quill's guilty teacup confirmed it: they had enough of the picture to sail, and they'd sail within two days, and they'd bring cranes and divers and lawyers and all the patient brutal machinery of a company that had been winning arguments with the ocean for forty years.

"They'll want divers on site by the third tide," I said, mostly to myself.

"They're saying the fourth," Quill offered, helpfully, and then seemed to realize he'd helped, and frowned.

"Quill." I leaned on his counter. "My boat. The *Kestrel*. Voss holds paper on her. If I settled part of the arrears — say, a substantial gesture — could you release her today?"

"I could release her any day the lien holder consents." He met my eyes, finally, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "You'd need to talk to Mr. Voss."

Everyone always did. That was the architecture of the whole town, really: whatever door you needed open, the key hung around Voss's neck.

I thanked him and walked out into the fog-thinned afternoon feeling the shape of the trap with new clarity. The fleet had the map fragment. The fleet had money, ships, and time to spare. I had a promise, a deadline measured in tides, and a creditor who owned my hull and would cheerfully sell my corpse if the margin worked.

There was exactly one move available, and it was the move I least wanted to make, and I made it anyway, because the tide tables don't care what you want.

* * *

Voss received me in his office above the chandlery, which was furnished like a church and staffed like one too — silent, watchful, faintly disapproving. He was pouring coffee when I came in, and he poured a second cup without asking, which was his way of reminding you he already knew why you'd come.

"Marchetti." He smiled. Voss's smile was famous along the cape; it reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I heard you lost a houseguest. Terrible thing, exposure. Dangerous season."

"The season's fine. The company was dangerous."

"Mm." He slid the second cup across the desk. "You want the boat."

"I want the boat."

"You can't afford the boat."

"I know what I can afford. I'm here to find out what you want."

Voss leaned back and laced his fingers over his waistcoat, and for a moment he just looked at me with that terrible cheerful attention, like a man reading a book he'd already enjoyed once. Then he said, lightly, the way you'd discuss weather:

"There are men coming into this harbor within two days who will pay a great deal of money for information. Old charts. Soundings. Where a certain survey should begin." He tapped the desk once. "You're a clever man, Marchetti. Clever men hear things. If you happened to hear anything useful — anything at all — I'd consider a portion of the debt settled. Generously. Perhaps even the boat released, unencumbered, as a token of goodwill between friends."

The coffee steamed between us. Outside the window, gulls wheeled over the masts of the Calloway tenders, black against gray.

"What information," I said carefully, "do you imagine I might have?"

"None whatsoever." He beamed. "That's the beauty of it. You'd simply be listening. A man with your ears, in a town like this — things drift in on the fog. And if nothing drifts, well." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "No harm done. The debt remains. The boat remains mine. Life goes on, tide after tide, as it always does."

It was, I'll admit, a masterful offer. He wasn't asking me to betray anyone outright. He was asking me to leave a door open, and doors left open swing on their own. And God help me, I stood there and felt the pull of it — the *Kestrel* freed, the ledger zeroed, five years of Voss's cheerful voice gone from my life like a tooth pulled. All it would cost was my silence, and silence is the cheapest currency there is, which is exactly why it buys so much ruin.

"I'll keep my ears open," I said.

"Good man." He beamed wider.

"But I sail tomorrow, and I won't be back before the week's out. So if anything drifts in on the fog, it'll drift in late."

Something flickered behind the smile — quick, professional, gone. "Sail where?"

"Fishing," I said. "The fishing's been poor inshore. A man has to range further."

Voss laughed, warm and easy, and waved me toward the door like a host ending a pleasant call. But as I reached it, his voice followed me, light as dust:

"Range carefully, Marchetti. The Reach eats careless boats. And it would grieve me — genuinely grieve me — to lose such an interesting debtor."

I went down the stairs into the street, and my hands were steady, and my heart was not.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did that evening, at my table, with the map and the tide tables and the jar of coins:

One boat, impounded, releasable only through miracle or fraud. One crew, consisting of nobody. One wage bill, payable in eleven dollars and gratitude. Four days until the channel silts. Two days until the Calloway fleet sails with cranes and lawyers and every advantage the world offers to men who iron their shirts.

Against which: one map drawn by a dying man who trusted me. One promise, sworn at three in the morning, that the salvage goes to Mara Wren and to no fleet, no bank, no creditor wearing a waistcoat and a smile.

I wrote a list of names on the back of an old invoice. People the harbor had chewed up and spat out. A pilot nobody would insure. A mechanic nobody would hire. A deckhand nobody would sail with. Damaged goods, every one of us — and that, I realized, staring at the list, was precisely the qualification. You can't buy loyalty with wages you don't have. But you can offer damaged people something rarer than money: a reason, and a place on a boat that's going somewhere that matters.

Eleven dollars. Six tides. One promise.

I picked up the list, put on my coat, and went out into the fog to see about a crew.
