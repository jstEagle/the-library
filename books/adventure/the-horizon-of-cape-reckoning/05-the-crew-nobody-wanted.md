## 5. The Crew Nobody Wanted

*Ines*

There is a particular art to asking people for favors they cannot afford to give you, and I had mastered it the way a drowning man masters swimming — technically, desperately, and with no style points awarded.

Wren's body was three days cold in the harbor chapel's cheapest drawer, and I had spent those days doing arithmetic that never came out kind. The *Kestrel* needed an engine man who understood her moods. She needed a pilot for the Serrated Reach, because I had sailed it twice and lived both times mostly out of luck, and luck is not a crew member you can put on the articles. And she needed hands — strong ones — because salvage diving is not a solo enterprise unless you want your solo enterprise to end at the bottom of a channel with your name misspelled on a plaque.

I could pay none of them. This I made clear up front, partly out of honesty and partly because it filtered out anyone with sense, of whom I needed none.

First, Odile Ferrand.

You could find her most evenings at the Anchor & Chain, sitting at the far end of the bar where the light didn't reach, drinking slowly enough that the bartender left her alone and long enough that everyone knew she was drinking on purpose. Ten years ago she had piloted half the merchant fleet of Cape Reckoning through weather that made grown captains weep into their charts. Then the *Corvina* went down in the Reach with eleven souls aboard, the inquiry found "pilot error," and Odile's license went into the same water as the ship. She'd been ashore ever since, a woman who knew every rock between here and the open sea by its first name and was forbidden to introduce herself.

She watched me cross the room with the expression of someone who has already read the last page of my book and found it disappointing.

"Marchetti." She said my name like a diagnosis. "Voss's boy."

"I'm nobody's boy. That's rather the point of my visit."

"You're going to ask me to pilot something illegal through the Reach."

"I'm going to ask you to pilot something *urgent* through the Reach. Whether it's illegal depends on which lawyer Mara Wren hires, and she hasn't hired me yet either."

That landed. I saw it land — a small flicker behind her eyes, the name *Wren* doing work that my charm never could. Everyone in port had heard by now that old Aldous Wren had died in a storm chaser's doorway, and everyone had drawn their own conclusions about what he'd been carrying. The Calloway fleet's tenders had been provisioning loudly enough to make their intentions legible from space.

"The Reach ate the *Corvina*," she said quietly.

"The Reach ate the *Corvina* because her pilot was drunk and following a company schedule instead of his own eyes. You weren't drunk. You were scapegoated by men whose insurance premiums mattered more than your name." I set the tide tables on the bar between us, folded to the right page. "Six tides, Odile. After that the channel silts until spring, and whatever Aldous Wren promised his daughter stays down there with the fish. Calloway gets it, or nobody does. I'm offering you the chance to make it nobody-or-me instead of nobody-or-them."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I sail the Reach myself, die badly, and you get to drink here knowing you could have saved us all and chose the porter instead."

The bartender, a sentimental man, pretended to polish a glass that had been clean since the previous administration.

Odile looked at the tide tables for a long time. Then she looked at me, and what I saw in her face wasn't greed or adventure or even redemption — it was simpler and sadder than any of those. It was the look of a woman being offered her own hands back.

"One condition," she said. "When we come back — *if* we come back — you stand in front of the harbor board and tell them exactly who brought your boat through the Reach alive."

"Twice, if you're good."

"I'm better than good. I'm the best pilot this coast ever threw away." She drained her glass and stood, and I noticed for the first time how carefully she stood, like someone relearning the weight of decisions. "Get me a boat worth the trouble, Marchetti."

*Finch*

The thing about boatyards is that nobody looks at the person under the tarp unless the engine is making noise, and engines are always making noise somewhere, so a girl can live inside a boatyard for weeks if she keeps her head down and her hands busy. I'd slept in the sail loft of Harrow & Sons for nineteen nights when Marchetti found me, which meant I'd been sloppy, which meant I was tired, which meant I hated him a little before he even said anything.

He didn't grab my ankle and drag me out, which is what the yard boss would have done. He crouched down next to the engine I was rebuilding — a Kestrel-class auxiliary, temperamental as a cat in rain — and just watched my hands for a while.

"That injector timing's off," he said finally.

"It's off *on purpose*. She runs rich cold and lean hot, so you split the difference and let her warm up before you ask her anything. Whoever owned this boat before you didn't understand her."

"She was my father's. Then the bank's. Now mine, in the way that a hostage is yours." He sat down properly on the deck plates, which nobody did, because deck plates were dirty and important people didn't sit on dirty things. "Finch, right? That's what the yard calls you."

"That's what I call me. The yard doesn't call me anything. The yard would call me trespasser if it caught me."

"Why Finch?"

"Because finches are small and quick and nobody expects much of them, and then one day one builds a nest in your mailbox and you realize it outsmarted you." I wiped my hands and finally looked at him straight. He had the look I'd learned to read in the year since I'd run — the look of a man carrying more debt than money, and I don't mean the kind Voss held paper on. "You're going to offer me work. You can't pay. It's dangerous. And you need somebody who can keep this engine alive past the point where any sane mechanic would've drowned her."

Something moved across his face — surprise, maybe, or the specific discomfort of a man whose pitch has been stolen out of his mouth.

"How'd you know?"

"Because everybody who comes through this yard wants the same three things and only ever has money for two. And because you've got salt to your knees and harbor mud on your boots and you walked past the yard office to get to the sail loft, which means you already knew I was here and wanted to talk before witnesses." I stood up. My whole body ached in the way it ached when I'd been sleeping on canvas too long, and I thought about the nineteen nights, and the winter coming, and the yard boss's nephew who'd started asking questions. "What's the cargo?"

"Wreck salvage. The *Meridian*."

I kept my face still, which took everything I had. The *Meridian*. Granddad's map. The story Mama used to tell before she stopped telling stories, before the drinking, before I stopped being a daughter and became a rumor with grease under its nails. Old Wren dead in some stranger's doorway, and this stranger standing in front of me holding the other half of a story I'd been reading alone my whole life.

"Fine," I said, before my voice could betray me. "But I'm not crew. I'm the reason there'll *be* a crew. Remember that when the engine quits at the worst moment, because it will, and the difference between living and dying out there is whether you trust the person swearing at it."

"What do you swear at it?"

"Affectionately," I said, and shook his hand, and felt the map in my head grow one degree warmer.

*Bram*

They blacklisted me from every crew in the harbor after the *Petrel*, and the funny part — the part I sometimes told myself late at night when the loft above the chandlery got too quiet — was that the blacklist wasn't even a lie. It was true that the *Petrel*'s cargo had gone down instead of her people. It was true that I had disobeyed orders. What the blacklist left out was the shape of the choice: forty tons of timber in the hold, four men in the water, and a winch that could save one or the other before the sea closed its account. I saved the men. The company lost the timber, and companies forgive a lot of things, but they do not forgive losing money to save lives. It sets a precedent.

So I hauled freight on the docks, which my shoulders were built for and my heart was not, and I kept my head down, and I tried to be small, which for a man my size is a full-time occupation.

Marchetti found me at the freight yard on the morning of the fourth day. I'd heard he was asking around — a debt-ridden dreamer chasing a dead man's treasure against the Calloway fleet, which was the kind of story dockworkers tell each other for laughs, except nobody was laughing, because everyone could see the Calloway tenders taking on dive gear and guns both.

"I need a strong back," he said, looking up at me. He was a small man, quick, with the restless eyes of someone who photographs storms for a living and therefore respects them.

"You need a strong back that nobody else will hire," I said. "Otherwise you wouldn't be hiring me."

"I need a strong back that won't drop a winch load on a drowning man to save cargo. Yes. Both of those things are true, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because you've been lied about enough."

I put down the crate I was carrying. It weighed perhaps eighty pounds; I noticed its weight the way you notice a feather, which is to say I didn't. "Who told you about the *Petrel*?"

"Odile Ferrand. She said you were the only man in this port who ever made the right call and paid for it, and that if I was going to ask people to risk their necks for a promise, I should start with someone who understands what promises cost." He shifted his weight, uncomfortable, honest. "I can't pay you. If we find what Wren says is down there, there might be shares. There might not be. There will definitely be Calloway ships trying to stop us."

I looked at him for a while. Around us the freight yard went on with its clanking indifference, cranes swinging loads worth more than any of our lives, and I thought about how many years I had spent being careful, and what careful had bought me: a loft, a bad mattress, and a silence I filled with the memory of four men coughing seawater onto my deck.

"My mother used to say God gives big hands to people who are supposed to hold on to things," I said. "I'd like to hold on to something worth holding."

"That's a yes?"

"That's a yes. But I'll tell you now what I told the *Petrel*'s mate, and it's why we're having this conversation from opposite sides of a blacklist: if it comes down to cargo or people, I choose people. Every time. You should know that before we're in a position where it matters."

Marchetti smiled, and it was a tired smile but a real one. "Bram, that's not a warning. That's the job description."

* * *

*Ines*

By evening the *Kestrel* had a crew, which felt less like an accomplishment than like a dare I'd accepted on behalf of everyone I'd recruited. We stood in her small cabin — Odile studying the Reach charts with the frown of a woman finding errors in an old letter from a friend, Finch elbow-deep in the engine compartment issuing creative blasphemies, Bram folding himself into a space built for smaller men with the patience of furniture arranging itself — and I laid out the truth, because they'd earned it.

"Here's what we have. A map, six tides, a boat that runs on stubbornness, and no money. Here's what's against us. The Calloway fleet, the weather, the Reach, the silting channel, and the fact that every one of you has a reason to be somewhere safer." I paused. "Questions?"

"Yeah," Finch said, without emerging from the engine. "Who leaks? Because a crew this desperate is a crew that can be bought, and I've been bought before, and it doesn't feel like betrayal at the time. Feels like survival."

Nobody answered that. Odile traced the channel line with one finger and said nothing at all, which from her was a full paragraph. Bram looked at me with those patient eyes and asked the only question that mattered:

"When do we sail?"

"Tide after next. Night tide, dark moon." I looked around the cabin at the three of them — a disgraced pilot, a runaway, a blacklisted giant, and a debtor with a dead man's oath in his pocket — and felt something I hadn't felt since before Voss bought my paper. Not hope exactly. Hope's louder cousin. Purpose. "Tide after next, then. Sleep while you can."

It was nearly midnight when I doused the lamp, and I was still awake when I heard the knock — soft, deliberate, three raps that did not sound like any of my crew.

Through the porthole, in the lamplight of the quay, I saw a familiar cheerful face, and my stomach dropped the way it always did, the way it had learned to, like a stone down a well.

Voss had come aboard.
