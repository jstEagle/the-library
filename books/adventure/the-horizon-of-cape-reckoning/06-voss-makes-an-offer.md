## 6. Voss Makes an Offer

Voss came to my door on the third morning after Wren died, carrying a paper bag of pastries and wearing the expression of a man arriving at a christening.

That was the thing about Voss. He never arrived like a creditor. Creditors knock like they're doing you a favor by not kicking the door in. Voss knocked twice, pleasant, and when I opened up he was already smiling, holding out the bag like we were neighbors who'd fallen out of the habit of borrowing sugar.

"Marchetti." He said my name the way he said all names, with a warmth that had been calibrated to the decimal. "You look terrible."

"I've been busy."

"Busy." He stepped past me into the shack without being invited, which was his way everywhere — into rooms, into ledgers, into lives. He set the pastries on my table, right next to Wren's map, and I watched his eyes flick toward it for exactly as long as it took to seem accidental. "Grieving, I hear. The old man in your spare room. Terrible business. The harbor's full of stories about him."

"The harbor's full of stories about everything. Half of them are true."

"And the other half are worth more." He sat down at my table, uninvited again, and helped himself to a pastry. Almond. I knew because he made a small sound of appreciation that I'm sure he'd practiced in a mirror. "I'll be honest with you, Ines. That's always been our arrangement, hasn't it? You lie to me about when you'll pay, I pretend to believe you, and every so often we're both honest for thirty seconds and it's exhausting for everyone."

I leaned against the wall by the stove. There wasn't a chair I wanted him to see me take. "Thirty seconds started now, then."

"Good." He brushed sugar from his fingers, one at a time, unhurried. "Here's my honesty: I know Aldous Wren died in this house. I know he gave you something before he went. And I know what Calloway is paying for information about the *Meridian* — because Calloway told me, over dinner, two nights ago, while you were sitting here deciding whether to bury an old man or burn him."

The room got very quiet. Outside, the gulls were working the tide line, screaming their idiot heads off about nothing, the way gulls do.

"You've been busy too," I said.

"I'm a busy man. It's why I can afford the pastries." He opened his coat — not dramatically, nothing Voss did was dramatic; everything he did was *reasonable* — and took out a folded paper, laying it flat on the table beside the map. He didn't cover the map. He didn't need to. "Your debt. Principal, interest, penalties, the impound fees on your boat, the storage accruing daily at the yard. Everything. Zeroed."

I didn't touch it. My hands stayed where they were, and I was proud of them later, but in the moment I honestly couldn't feel them. "And in exchange?"

"A name and a chart reference. Where Wren's map leads. That's all. You don't have to sail anywhere. You don't have to dive anything. You don't have to risk your neck in the Serrated Reach, which, let's be honest, would kill you inside a week with the crew you'd be able to afford — which is no crew." He smiled again, softer now, the smile of a man offering to carry your groceries upstairs. "You hand me a piece of paper, Ines. One piece of paper. And you walk out of here free. No more Voss. No more impound yard. Your boat back on the water by Friday. You could chase storms again instead of hiding from them."

* * *

Here's what I want to be clear about, since I'm the one telling this: the offer was good.

Not good in the way a trap is good — not baited, not obvious. Good the way dry land is good to a drowning man. I owed Voss eleven thousand four hundred marks, and the number grew every day like something with a heartbeat. My boat sat in his impound yard under canvas, and every morning I walked past it on the way to nowhere, and every morning the canvas looked more like a shroud. I hadn't paid rent on the shack in two months. I'd been photographing squalls for a client who paid late and complained early, and the client was the only reason I'd eaten that week.

Freedom on paper. That's what he was selling. Not money — paper. A ledger line struck through. A life returned to zero, and zero, after years underwater, looks a lot like heaven.

And all it cost was an oath to a dead man.

Wren had lasted nine hours in my spare room. Long enough for fever to burn through him like a tide going out, long enough to press a grease-stained map into my hands with fingers that shook, long enough to make me swear — hand over hand, his grip stronger than a dying man's had any right to be — that whatever the *Meridian* held would go to his daughter Mara, and to no fleet, no bank, no creditor circling either of our names. I'd sworn it at three in the morning to a stranger I'd known for one night, and I'd sworn it easily, the way you promise things at three in the morning, when the cost is invisible and the dying man's eyes are the only light in the room.

Now the cost was sitting at my table eating an almond pastry.

"Ines." Voss's voice dropped into something almost gentle. Almost. "Look at your position honestly. You're going to say no, because you've got a romantic streak and a guilty conscience, and I respect both — genuinely, they're the only interesting things about you. But run the arithmetic. If you refuse, I take your boat apart for scrap and sell the hull, and you still owe me, and Calloway finds the wreck anyway, because they have half the map already and patience I assure you you don't have. The salvage goes to Calloway either way. The only question is whether you drown with it or eat breakfast tomorrow without flinching at the mail."

"That's not arithmetic," I said. "That's just you talking."

"It's both. The best arguments usually are." He tapped the paper once, lightly, then stood and buttoned his coat. "I'm not asking for an answer today. I'm a reasonable man. Take until tomorrow's tide. But Ines—" and here he paused at the door, and for one unguarded second the cheerfulness thinned and I saw the actual machinery underneath, cold and patient and very old, "—the daughter doesn't know you exist. The old man is ash. Nobody living knows what you swore except you. Whatever you think you're protecting, understand that it protects no one. It's just weight. And men who carry weight they don't have to, they sink. I've watched it happen for thirty years. It's the most reliable thing I know."

Then the cheerfulness came back up like a shopkeeper's shutter, and he touched his hat brim, and he was gone, leaving the pastries and the paper and the smell of almonds behind him.

* * *

I sat with it for a long time.

The shack has one window that faces the water, and I watched the tide come in over the flats the way I do when I can't think — reading it, measuring it, letting the water do the deciding for a while. Six tides. That's what the tables said, once I'd finally worked through Wren's scrawl and Odile's corrections: six tides before the channel to the wreck site silts shut for the season. Five now. The first one was already turning somewhere out past the light, indifferent to me, to Voss, to promises.

Five tides. And I had no boat, no crew, no money, and a debt that would follow me into the water if I drowned wearing it.

I picked up Voss's paper. It was beautifully done, I'll give him that. Heavy stock, real ink, the total written in a fair hand with a flourish on the final digit. Freedom, itemized. I read it twice, and both times my eyes went to the same place: the bottom, where there was no signature yet, just a blank space waiting for mine.

An oath is a strange kind of property. Nobody can repossess it. Nobody can impound it or auction it or accrue interest against it. It sits in you like ballast, and it makes the boat ride low, and there is no honest way to jettison it — only dishonest ones, and those sink you slower and deeper.

I thought about Wren's hands. Not the shaking ones from the end — the earlier ones, when he'd first gripped my wrist to stop me putting him out into the rain. Strong. Sea-cured. The hands of a man who'd spent forty years pulling things out of the ground and the water and giving most of it away to people who never thanked him. He hadn't asked me for shelter. He'd asked me for a direction. *Mara*, he'd said. *It goes to Mara.* Like he was handing me a compass bearing and trusting me to hold the course.

And I thought about what Voss had said, the true part buried in the sales pitch: nobody living knew what I'd sworn. I could tear up the map tonight, sign Voss's paper tomorrow, and walk down to the impound yard Friday and put my hand on my own bow rail again, and no ghost would ever raise a hand to stop me.

That was the temptation, if you want the truth of it. Not the money. The silence. The fact that I could break my word completely and get away clean.

I fed the paper to the stove.

It caught slowly — heavy stock, real ink — and I stood there watching my freedom curl and blacken, feeling precisely nothing heroic about it. No swell of resolve. No music. Just a man burning a document in a cold shack because the alternative was becoming someone he couldn't sail with. Voss had been wrong about one thing: the weight wasn't pointless. Weight is what keeps a hull upright in a crosswind. Every sailor knows it. Light boats flip.

The last corner wouldn't catch, so I had to push it into the coals with the poker, and even then it held its shape, a black lace rectangle with one legible word floating in the char: *zeroed*. Then that went too.

The pastries I ate. I'm not a saint, and Voss buys from the good bakery on Harrow Street, and I figured wasting them would be sentimentality in the wrong direction.

* * *

Near dark, I walked down to the impound yard.

I hadn't planned to. My feet decided, the way feet do. The gate chain was locked but the fence by the drainage ditch has been decorative for years, and I slipped through and crossed the gravel to where my boat sat under her canvas, and I pulled the canvas back just enough to lay my palm flat against the deck.

She was cold. Three weeks cold. But she was mine, or she would be, or — I stood there in the dark doing the dangerous arithmetic Voss had warned me about, the kind that isn't arithmetic at all.

Five tides. A silted channel. A fleet with deep pockets and half a map. A debt of eleven thousand marks and a body I couldn't mortgage.

What I needed was a pilot crazy enough to run the Reach from memory, because the charts were wrong and always had been. What I needed was an engineer who could keep a dying engine alive on prayer and stolen parts. What I needed was muscle that didn't ask questions, and luck, and weather, and a miracle with a tide table.

What I needed, in other words, was everybody in this port that nobody else would hire.

I pulled the canvas back down and patted her once, the way you'd touch a sleeping dog, and went over the fence again with the taste of almond still in my mouth and the first drops of evening rain starting to spot the dust.

There was a woman at the end of the pier who used to pilot ships through the Serrated Reach before they took her license. They said she drank. They said worse. I'd seen her thread a tug through fog so thick the foghorn sounded lost, and I'd seen what they did to her afterward, and I remembered thinking at the time: *there but for a different kind of drowning go I.*

Tomorrow's tide was at 6:14. I intended to be standing at her door when it turned.
