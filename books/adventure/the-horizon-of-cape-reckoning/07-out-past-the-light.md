## 7. Out Past the Light

I have run the Serrated Reach in my sleep for eleven years, which is fortunate, because tonight I am running it mostly asleep.

Not literally. A pilot who sleeps at the wheel ends up as a story told in harbor bars, and I have been enough of a story already. But there is a state you fall into after enough nights on dark water — half memory, half instinct, your hands knowing the wheel before your eyes know the rocks. The Reach is a graveyard of pilots who trusted their eyes. Fog comes off that water like a lie off a lawyer, and by the time you see the teeth of the Serrations it's too late to do anything but apologize to God.

So I run it from inside. Counting seconds. Feeling the set of the current through the soles of my boots. Listening to the way the swell changes its mind against stone.

The boat is called the *Kestrel*, and she was never meant to carry this much hope.

Behind me in the cockpit sit three people who should not be here. Ines Marchetti, who owes more money than most small nations and has just bet his last asset — himself — on a dead man's map. Finch, seventeen years old, all elbows and grease, currently down in the engine room having what sounds like a religious argument with a diesel engine that has not run this long since before I was born. And Bram, who takes up an entire bench seat and half of another, holding the tiller steady with two fingers like he's petting something nervous.

We left the harbor twenty minutes ago with no lights, no clearance, and no right to expect anything but ruin. Which, if you think about it, is the traditional way to begin an adventure.

* * *

Let me tell you how I came to be here, because it wasn't wages. Ines couldn't pay wages. He made that clear in the first thirty seconds, standing in the doorway of my rented room above the chandlery with rain running off his coat like he'd brought the weather in with him.

"I need a pilot for the Serrated Reach," he said. "Night passage. No lights."

"Nobody runs the Reach at night."

"You did. Twice. Before."

I looked at him for a long moment. "Before" is a word with knives in it. Before the inquiry. Before they took my ticket. Before the *Corvina* went aground with forty souls aboard and Odile Fenn's name dragged through every tribunal on the coast, though the official finding — weather, chart error, an act of the sea — somehow never quite reached the men who'd ordered her out with faulty soundings.

"They say you're the best," Ines said. "They also say you're finished."

"They're right twice."

"So am I." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, the kind that doesn't expect to be believed but offers itself anyway. "Wreck of the Meridian. Six tides. I can't pay you. If we find her, you get a share. If we don't, you've lost nothing but a night's sleep."

I had plenty of sleep. What I didn't have was a reason to use it. You'd be surprised how heavy a room gets when there's nothing waiting outside it but your own reputation.

"What's really out there?" I asked.

"A promise," he said. And then, because he could see I wanted more: "A dying man asked me for something, and I said yes before I understood the price. Now I'm paying it. That's all."

I've been lied to by better men with smoother stories. But there was something in the way he said *that's all* — like it was everything — that made me reach for my seabag instead of my skepticism.

Besides. Nobody else would take me. When nobody wants you, you go where you're pointed, even if the pointing finger belongs to a drowning man.

* * *

The harbor mouth is the first test. Cape Reckoning's breakwater runs out in two arms like a crab's claw, and between them the tide was making — four knots of confused water where the ebb fights the groundswell. We came out on the tail of the ebb, engines at bare steerage-way, and I'll admit my heart was going harder than the Kestrel's.

Because there she was. Sitting just outside the claw, riding her anchor light like she owned the night: a Calloway spotter, low and black, one of those purpose-built hulls with a radar mast tall as a gallows. Calloway Salvage keeps three of them working the cape, and every skipper in port knows their job isn't salvage. It's watching. Calloway doesn't chase wrecks; Calloway waits for other people to find them and then arrives with lawyers and guns and the full weight of a company that has never once lost an argument it cared about.

If that spotter's watch saw us, saw a lightless hull sliding out past the breakwater at two in the morning, we'd be boarded inside ten minutes. Impounded. And Ines Marchetti would spend the rest of his short, indebted life explaining himself to men who bill by the hour.

"Kill the engine," I whispered.

Finch's head appeared through the companionway, eyes enormous. "You want me to kill it? It just started liking me!"

"Coast it. We ride the ebb through the gap."

"That's insane."

"Yes," I said. "Quietly."

She killed the engine. The silence that followed was total — that particular harbor silence where you can hear your own blood and the creak of the hull and, somewhere behind us, the town clock striking two with the confidence of a thing that has never once been wrong about anything.

We drifted. The ebb took us, and the Kestrel — bless her rotten old heart — sat into the current like a leaf deciding to trust the river. The spotter lay two hundred yards off our port bow, close enough that I could see the ember of a cigarette on her deck. Someone up there was bored, cold, and thinking about his bunk. Nobody watches well when they're thinking about their bunk.

Bram leaned close, his voice a vibration more than a sound. "Should I pray?"

"Do you pray?"

"No. But I'm large. It might help."

It might have, at that. There are things a man that size does to the physics of a situation just by existing in it.

The current carried us past the spotter's stern at maybe eighty yards. I counted the yards because counting kept me sane. Eighty. Seventy. The cigarette ember moved along the rail, paused, moved again. Sixty. Finch had both hands pressed flat against the deck as if she could hold us down by will alone. Fifty.

And then the wind shifted.

Just a breath of it, off the land, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and tar — and with it, our smell. Diesel, bilge, four unwashed bodies. Any watchman worth his salt knows that smell means a boat under way, and this one, whoever he was, had been doing this long enough to have a nose.

Forty yards. The cigarette stopped moving.

I put my hand over Finch's mouth before she gasped, and we drifted, and drifted, and the ember stayed fixed on the rail like a red eye deciding whether to open all the way —

— and then the town clock struck the half hour, distant and absurd, and somewhere aboard the spotter a door banged, and voices laughed about something, and the ember dropped to the deck and died under a boot heel.

Thirty yards. Twenty. Ten. Clear.

Ines let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since chapter one of his life. Bram patted the deck twice, gently, the way you'd thank a horse. And Finch turned to me in the dark with an expression I'd learn to know well — fury and delight fighting for the same face.

"Never again," she whispered.

"Twice more, minimum," I said. "But welcome aboard."

* * *

The Serrated Reach begins where the charts stop being useful.

Officially, the charts show eleven hazards between the harbor and open water. Unofficially, there are nineteen, because the sea has been building new ones faster than the hydrographic office can be embarrassed into updating. The locals call them the Teeth. Each one is a reef ridge that shows only on the lowest spring tides, and between them run channels that shift with every storm, some wide enough for a freighter, some barely wide enough for regret.

By day, with a following sea and good light, a careful skipper can thread them. By night, in fog, with a borrowed boat and a crew of strangers?

You navigate by what the water tells you. The swell stands up and steepens over shallow rock — you feel the boat's motion change from a roll to a hitch, a stutter in the rhythm, and you turn before the stutter becomes a crash. Current makes eddies off the Teeth, little whirls and slicks visible even in starlight if you know to look for the smooth patches where the water is being dragged. And sound: breakers have a voice, low and continuous, distinct from surf on sand. You listen for the voice of each Tooth and you give it its distance.

I stood at the wheel and ran it the way my father taught me, back when I still had a name anyone would print without the word *disgraced* attached. Feel first. Eyes second. Charts never.

"Starboard ten," I said.

Bram eased the tiller. Behind us the engine muttered in its sleep, Finch down below coaxing it through the night like a nurse walking a fever ward.

"Midships. Steady."

The first Tooth passed to port, unseen but heard — a long low sigh of water worrying stone, forty yards off. Then the second. Then the third, closer than I liked, close enough that the swell under us hitched and stuttered and Ines gripped the rail and looked at me with a question in his face.

"Trust the count," I told him.

"I do."

"Liar," I said, kindly. "But thank you."

He laughed — a short, surprised laugh, the first I'd heard from him that wasn't aimed at a creditor. And I understood something then about why a man like Ines Marchetti collects people like us. It isn't charity. It's recognition. He looks at a disgraced pilot and a runaway mechanic and a blacklisted giant and sees the same thing he sees in the mirror: someone the world priced too low and sold anyway.

* * *

Somewhere around the fifth Tooth, the engine died.

Not dramatically. It coughed, stumbled, caught, coughed again, and then quit with a mechanical sigh that in the silence of the Reach sounded like a verdict. We were in the widest channel — the Long Gut, a quarter mile of navigable water between the Widow-Maker and the Old Man's Teeth — but the current there runs three knots against you on the flood, and we were making the passage on the last of the ebb with nothing underneath us but momentum and prayer.

"Finch!" Ines called down the companionway.

"I KNOW," came the reply, followed by a metallic bang, a curse in two languages, and another bang. "There's a piece of — somebody's patched this fuel line with a sardine tin. A SARDINE TIN. Who DOES that?"

"The previous owner," Ines called back, "was an optimist."

"Well he's DEAD now and so are WE—"

The engine caught. Died. Caught again, ragged, missing on one cylinder, but running. And Finch came up the companionway covered in grease from forehead to chin like a raccoon that had lost a fight with an oil pan, and announced to the night at large:

"She'll hold. She's ugly but she'll hold. Don't ask me how."

I have worked with engineers who held tickets from three navies. None of them ever spoke to an engine the way that girl does — scolding it, flattering it, threatening it, loving it in the furious way you love something that keeps letting you down. She'd stowed away on coasters since she was fourteen, they told me later. Ran away from whatever she was running from, and taught herself machines in the dark of engine rooms because machines, unlike people, always tell you exactly what's wrong with them.

"Finch," I said, "when this is over, come work for me."

"You don't have a boat."

"I'm acquiring ambitions."

* * *

The last hour of the Reach is the worst, because it's the part where you start believing you've made it. More pilots have died in the final mile than in the first five, drunk on relief, easing off the count a tooth too early. The Old Man — the largest of the Teeth, a reef the size of a church — sits square across the exit channel, and the only way past him is a gap called the Needle's Eye, fifty yards of water that bends twice like a question mark.

I took us through it at slack water, when the current rests and the rocks lose their argument for a few merciful minutes. Slow. Silent. The Kestrel's bow swinging through the first bend with ten feet to spare on either side, black stone rising wet on both quarters, and Bram steering with the tenderness of a man threading a needle while wearing boxing gloves.

Then the second bend. Then open water — actual open water, the long swell of the outer sea coming in under us clean and unbroken for the first time in hours — and the cape falling away astern with its lighthouse sweeping its blind white arm across the sky.

Nobody cheered. We were too tired, and cheering felt like tempting something. But Ines put his hand on my shoulder, and Bram sat down heavily and laughed once, deep in his chest, like a door closing on a warm room. And Finch slid down the companionway to argue with the engine some more, which I suspect is how she says thank you.

Dawn came up gray-gold behind us, lighting the water ahead in a long road toward the horizon. Six tides, I thought. One spent. Five left, and a fleet behind us, and a storm somewhere out in the deep gathering its skirts.

I stood at the wheel and let the exhilaration move through me and out, because I've learned not to keep it. Exhilaration kept too long turns to recklessness, and recklessness in the Reach gets people drowned. Better to feel it fully and let it go, the way you'd return a borrowed knife.

Ahead: open water, a wreck, a fortune, a promise.

Behind: a harbor full of people who would sell us all for pocket change.

And somewhere between the two, the rest of our lives, sailing toward us at four knots.

I checked the compass, settled the Kestrel on her heading, and began, quietly, to count.
