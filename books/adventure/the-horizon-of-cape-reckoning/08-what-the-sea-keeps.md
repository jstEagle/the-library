## 8. What the Sea Keeps

The wind died an hour after dawn, and the sea went flat as poured pewter.

I have never trusted a becalmed sea. Give me weather, give me work — give me something to fight or fix. But a dead calm is the ocean holding its breath, and in my experience it only ever breathes out again when it's good and ready to ruin somebody's day.

Bram Oduya does not fill silence with talk. That was the first thing Ines learned about me, back at the hiring hall when he asked if I could handle a deck and I said yes and nothing else for three days. Odile had told him my history in two sentences — *blacklisted from every crew on this coast, don't ask why* — and he hadn't asked. He'd just looked up at me, all six foot six of him folded into his little debt-crippled boat, and said, "Can you start tomorrow?"

So when the sails went slack and there was nothing left to do but wait, I waited. I coiled line that didn't need coiling. I checked the bilge twice. I sat at the bow with my knees up around my ears like a man riding a barrel, and I watched the water do nothing.

It was Finch who broke first. She always breaks first. Nineteen years old, grease to the elbows, a mouth like a loose winch.

"This is torture," she announced, lying flat on the cabin top with her cap over her face. "Actual torture. There are laws against this."

"There are laws against most things you enjoy," Odile said from the helm, though there was no helming to do. She had her charts out anyway, studying them the way other people study old letters.

"I'm going to die of boredom and they'll bury me at sea and it'll be your fault, Marchetti."

Ines was at the rail with his camera, photographing nothing — the flat light, the empty horizon, the particular gray of a sky that couldn't decide. He did that a lot. I think the camera was how he prayed: point it at something terrible enough and maybe the world would explain itself.

"Nobody's dying," he said mildly. "We're drifting. It's different."

"It's the same thing but slower."

He laughed, and I watched something happen in his shoulders — a loosening I hadn't seen since we cleared the cape. Three days out from harbor, running from Calloway's spotter boats and Voss's interest, and only now, with the wind gone and nothing to outrun, did the man finally unclench.

The calm held through the afternoon. We ate cold rice and tinned fish, and Odile taught Finch a card game that was mostly cheating, and Finch lost spectacularly while accusing everyone else of it. The sun came down orange and enormous, and still no wind, and by full dark the sea had gone glass-smooth, holding the stars so completely that it looked like we were floating in the sky itself.

That was when Ines brought out the bottle.

Not liquor — I want to be clear about that, because what followed might sound like drink-talk otherwise. It was plum wine, cheap, sweet, the kind of thing you buy at a harbor stall because the woman selling it has kind eyes. He'd been carrying it since port. He poured four tin cups without asking anyone, which was its own kind of question, and set them on the deck boards between us.

"We're becalmed," he said. "Which means nobody's working. Which means—" he hesitated, and I saw him decide, the way you see a man step off a ledge, "—which means we should probably know who we're dying next to."

"That's morbid," Finch said.

"That's honest." He picked up a cup. "I'll go first, since it's my boat and my bad idea. You all know about the debts. What you don't know is how I got them." He turned the cup in his fingers. "My father chased storms. Not for money — for glory, or science, or whatever it is men chase when they can't admit they're chasing their fathers. He died doing it. And I inherited his gear, his obsession, and his creditors, because it turns out glory doesn't service a loan. Every photograph I've ever sold went straight into a hole I didn't dig. That's why I took Wren's map. Not just the promise. Partly I thought — salvage, debt paid, done." He shrugged. "And partly because a dying man asked me, and I've never once in my life managed to say no to that."

Silence. Then Odile, unexpectedly, spoke.

"I ran a ship aground," she said.

Finch sat up so fast her cap fell off. "You *what?*"

"The *Corvane*, eleven years ago. Forty-one souls aboard." Odile's voice was level, the way it always was, but her hands were very still on the chart case. "Fog on the Serrated Reach. My instruments were wrong — sabotaged, I found out later, by the shipping company, because a pilot who reports faulty equipment slows down their schedule. I trusted the instruments. Thirty-nine people lived. Two didn't." She looked at the dark water. "The company settled quietly. I got the blame publicly. Every crew on this coast knows my name as the one word you don't put on your roster." She picked up her cup at last. "I sail with you, Marchetti, because you're the first captain in eleven years who asked me to navigate instead of asking me to disappear."

Finch was quiet for a long moment, which for Finch was roughly geological time. Then she said, "I don't got a tragedy. I got a stepfather who drank and a mom who chose him. I fixed his truck so it wouldn't start and left with forty bucks and a wrench. That's it. That's the whole sad story." She grinned, but it didn't hold. "Except — I keep thinking about the old man. Wren. How he grabbed my wrist when we loaded him off at the cove. Said I had his brother's hands. I didn't tell you guys that." She looked down at her palms. "Maybe I got a tragedy after all. Just haven't finished reading it yet."

They all looked at me then. All three of them, in the starlight, waiting.

I am not a man who talks easily. Words come out of me the way ballast comes out of a flooded hull — necessary, slow, and only under real pressure. But the wine was sweet and the night was windless and these three people had handed me their worst nights like coins into a hat, and it seemed suddenly like the rudest thing in the world to keep mine.

"My wife's name was Adaeze," I said.

Finch stopped fidgeting immediately. Even Odile went stiller than still.

"We had a daughter. Nia. She was six." I looked at my hands, big as boat fenders, useless for anything delicate except the two small things I had loved most in the world. "We lived upcoast, in Merrow Point. I worked the freight runs for the Halcyon Consolidated line. Good money, steady work, and the company owned half the town, so you didn't ask questions about cargo. You loaded what they gave you and you sailed it where they said."

I hadn't spoken some of this aloud in nine years. It came out of me in pieces, rusted shut and then giving way all at once.

"There was a shipment. Chemical drums, they told us. Sealed, stenciled, ordinary. They wanted it moved fast, ahead of a scheduled inspection, and they wanted it moved on a passenger ferry route — shared hold, because it was cheaper than chartering. I flagged it. I was a loader, not an inspector, but I flagged it. Told my foreman the manifest weights didn't match the drum sizes. He told me the company had done the math and the math was fine." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it goes when I'm carrying something too heavy to feel. "The math was fine right up until the ferry *Kestrel* caught fire in the channel with two hundred people aboard. My wife and daughter were on it. Coming home from her mother's."

Nobody moved. The sea made no sound at all.

"The inquiry found a fuel leak. The company paid settlements. My settlement came with a clause — sign it, take the money, never speak the cargo's name. I signed it, because Nia's grave needed a stone and stones cost money, and then I found out the blacklisting wasn't even the company's doing. It was worse than that. The other crews just... decided a man who asks questions is bad luck. Companies can be bought. Superstition is free." I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I let it. "So. That's why I sail with you, Captain. Because Calloway is Halcyon with a new coat of paint, and because somewhere in that wreck there are papers, and papers are the one thing I know how to believe in. Cargo tells the truth eventually. Always. It just usually tells it too late."

Ines reached over and refilled my cup. His hand was not quite steady, and I pretended not to notice, because that's what family does — no. That's what *crew* does. Family was a word I was saving for later, and didn't know yet I was saving it.

"The documents," he said quietly. "Wren's salvage. If there's proof in that strongroom—"

"Then it's worth more than the gold," I said. "Gold spends. Paper convicts."

Odile nodded slowly. "And Calloway knows it. Whatever's down there, they're not chasing treasure."

"No," Ines said. "They're chasing witnesses."

The words sat among us like a fourth crew member. Finch broke the quiet the only way Finch knows how — she leaned her head against my arm, all ninety pounds of her, and said, "For the record, Bram, if a company ever tries to make you move bad cargo again, I'll sabotage the engine. Slowest voyage in maritime history. They'll beg us to stop asking questions."

"You'd wreck our own boat?"

"I'd wreck *anyone's* boat. It's a gift."

And I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since before the *Kestrel*, a big rolling thing that startled gulls out of the rigging somewhere above us — and the others joined in, and the sound went out over that flat black water and came back changed, warmer, like it had been somewhere and returned.

Later, much later, when Finch was asleep in the cockpit and Odile had the midnight watch and Ines stood at the bow with his camera pointed at stars he couldn't possibly capture, I stayed sitting where I was. The wine was gone. The calm held.

I thought about Adaeze, and I didn't hurt the way I usually hurt. It was more like showing her a photograph. *Look,* I wanted to say. *Look who I'm sailing with. A captain who keeps promises to dead men. A pilot the sea forgave before people did. A kid with a wrench and a grudge. Look. I found some.*

The tide turned sometime before dawn. I felt it before I saw it — a long shudder through the hull, the water beginning to remember which way it belonged. By first light there was a breeze, faint and easterly, and Odile woke everyone with the news, and we raised sail and began to move.

It was Finch who spotted it, mid-morning, squinting ahead through the rigging.

"Hey. Hey, Captain." Her voice had gone strange. "What's that buoy doing out here? Middle of nowhere, no channel marker for thirty miles?"

I climbed up beside her and looked, and my stomach went cold and slow, the way it does before bad news arrives officially.

A mooring buoy rode the swell ahead of us, fresh-painted, flying a pennant. And tied alongside it, riding at anchor with the patience of a spider, was a boat.

Calloway colors.

* * *

Ines was beside me at the rail before I'd finished climbing down, and I watched the certainty drain out of his face and harden into something else.

"They're ahead of us," he said. "They shouldn't be ahead of us."

Odile was already at the charts, measuring with her thumb, her lips moving through the arithmetic. When she looked up, her answer was in her face before her mouth caught up with it.

"Captain," she said quietly. "Nobody finds this waypoint early by luck. Somebody told them when we sailed."

Behind us, Finch had gone very still by the engine hatch, one hand resting on it the way you rest a hand on a friend's shoulder.

And the wind, faithful and indifferent, carried us straight toward the buoy.
