## 9. The First Tide Turns

The first waypoint was a rock called the Gull's Tooth, which is the kind of name sailors give to rocks that have eaten better men than them. It stood off the northern shoulder of the Reach like a rotten molar, white with guano and black with old storm scars, and according to Wren's map it was where we were supposed to confirm we were still honest — because if the bearing from the Tooth to the second waypoint didn't match the map's margin note, then either Wren had lied to me with his dying breath or the sea had moved, and I honestly didn't know which possibility I liked less.

We made the Tooth on the morning of the second day, under a sky the color of dishwater, with the engine running at a temperature Finch described as "enthusiastic" and Odile described as "a lawsuit waiting to happen."

"She's fine," Finch said, for the fourth time since dawn, her head buried in the engine housing up to her shoulders. Her voice came out echoing slightly, like she was talking to us from inside a barrel. "She just runs hot when she's happy."

"Everything runs hot when it's about to fail," Odile said from the wheel. "That's practically the definition."

"That's pessimism."

"That's experience."

I stood at the bow with the chart case under my arm and let them argue, because arguing was how this crew said good morning, and I'd learned in two days at sea that as long as they were bickering, everything was probably fine. It was silence I'd started to worry about.

Bram was beside me at the rail, drinking tea out of a paint can because we owned exactly three mugs and he'd surrendered his to Finch without discussion on the first night. He was watching the water the way he always watched the water — patiently, like it owed him an apology and he was willing to wait for it.

"You see it?" he asked.

I did. Off the starboard bow, maybe half a mile short of the Tooth, riding the swell where no honest boat had any business riding: a buoy. Not a navigation buoy. A working buoy — a heavy steel drum with a mast, a radar reflector, and a flag snapping in the wind, anchored dead center over the water Wren's map called the First Watching Place.

My stomach did something complicated.

"That's not charted," Bram said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about buoys: nobody anchors one by accident. A navigation buoy is placed by the harbor authority after committees meet. A fishing buoy marks pots, and pots don't sit in forty fathoms of open channel. And a salvage buoy — a steel drum with a radar reflector and a company flag — marks one thing only: *we were here first, and we know exactly what's under you.*

Calloway flew a gray-and-green pennant. The flag on the drum was gray-and-green.

Odile cut the engine and we drifted, and the four of us gathered at the rail like mourners, looking at the thing.

"They're ahead of us," Odile said. No drama in it. She read water the way priests read scripture — flatly, with total conviction. "Not just ahead. Ahead by enough to have surveyed, weighed anchor, and planted hardware. That takes a day. Maybe more."

"We left two nights ago," I said. "They weren't provisioning to leave for another day after that. Voss told me so himself, the smug bastard, mostly because he thought it would hurt."

"It would appear," Odile said, "that somebody told them otherwise."

The wind picked at the flag. Gray and green. Gray and green.

Finch had come up from the engine well and gone very quiet, which for Finch was roughly equivalent to another person screaming. She stood with her arms crossed, fourteen years old and trying to look twenty-five, staring at the buoy with an expression I couldn't read, and I want to be honest here: I noticed that I couldn't read it, and I noticed that I minded.

Because there was a shorter list than usual of people who knew when we'd sailed. There was me. There was Odile, who'd plotted the route. There was Bram, who'd helped load stores. And there was Finch, who'd known the departure time down to the minute because she'd been the one who swore she could get the engine to turn over by then, and she had, barely, arriving at the dock with grease on her face and something tucked away behind her eyes that I'd been too busy feeling heroic to examine.

And there was the harbormaster, but the harbormaster was a fat, comfortable man who'd known my father, and I wasn't ready to think about him yet. Suspicion is a coward. It always goes for the strangest person in the room first.

"Could be coincidence," Bram said.

"Could it?" Odile asked.

"Calloway's been surveying this coast for months," Bram said. "Maybe they found the same waypoint. Wren's map isn't the only map in the world."

"Wren's map is the only map that matters," Odile said, "because it's the only one that knows where the Meridian actually is. And that buoy—" she nodded at the drum, rocking gently in the swell, smug as a banker "—is sitting within fifty yards of the coordinates on the page I have memorized. Coincidence doesn't navigate, Bram. People do."

Nobody said anything. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, water slapped the hull in that patient rhythm the sea uses when it wants you to know it's not going anywhere.

Finch spoke first, and her voice came out wrong — too controlled, the way voices do when someone has rehearsed being calm. "So what does that mean? For us?"

"It means," I said, "that either Calloway got lucky, or somebody on this boat sold us."

* * *

I'm not proud of how the next hour went. I'll tell it straight anyway, because a man who edits his own failures is just lying with extra steps.

We went below, into the cabin, all four of us, which was already a mistake — the cabin was built for two and a dog, and with four people in it the air went thick immediately, and thick air makes bad rooms, and bad rooms make worse decisions.

I laid the map out on the table. Wren's map, ink on waxed paper, his handwriting going shaky toward the edges where the fever had gotten into it. I put my finger on the waypoint and I said, "This buoy is on top of our route. Someone gave them our schedule. There are four people on this boat who knew it."

"And one of them is fourteen," Odile said.

I didn't ask her to say it. She said it anyway, because Odile had spent thirty years piloting other people's ships through other people's disasters and had apparently concluded that tact was a luxury the Serrated Reach didn't allow.

"I'm not accusing anyone," I said, which was a lie, and everyone knew it was a lie, and the knowing sat down at the table with us like a fifth passenger.

"Good," Finch said. "Because I didn't do anything."

"I know you didn't—"

"Do you?" She turned on me, and there it was, whatever had been living behind her eyes since the dock, finally out in the light. "Do you actually? Because you've been watching me since we cleared the breakwater. Both of you." She flicked a glance at Odile. "You think I haven't noticed? You think I don't know what it looks like when people decide you're the weak point? I've been the weak point my whole life. I know the weather."

"Finch—" Bram started, gentle as ever.

"No." She held up a hand. "You want to ask, so ask. Go on. Ask me why a fifteen-year-old mechanic with no last name she'll admit to signs onto a suicide run for wages nobody's paying. Ask me what I was doing on the docks the night Wren died. Ask me why I already knew how to fix your piece-of-junk engine before I ever saw it." Her voice cracked on the last one, just slightly, and she hated that I heard it. "Ask. Since we're all being honest."

The cabin was very quiet. Outside, the gulls were working the Gull's Tooth, screaming their idiot heads off about nothing.

I should have handled it well. I'd like to tell you I did. But I was running on two hours of sleep and a promise I'd made to a dying man, and the buoy was out there flying Calloway colors on top of my future, and so instead of the wise captain I read about in novels — the one who says the right thing at the right moment — I said:

"Why *did* you know the engine?"

Wrong question. Wrongest possible question. Her face closed like a shop shutter.

"Because I grew up on boats," she said flatly. "Any other inquiries, Captain?"

"Finch." I rubbed my face. "I'm not your enemy."

"No," she said. "You're just the guy who decides whether I eat. Same thing, smaller portions."

She shoved past Bram and went up on deck, and the cabin door banged, and the three of us sat there in the wreckage of the conversation.

"Well," Odile said.

"Don't," I said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say 'I told you so' with your whole face."

"My face," Odile said, "is a professional instrument, and it says only what needs saying." She looked at the map, at the shaky ink, at the coordinates she'd memorized and I'd bet my life on. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing right now — the girl didn't leak us. I've watched liars for thirty years. Liars relax when the accusation lands wrong. She got angrier. Angry means cornered, and cornered means innocent, usually. Usually." She stood. "But she *is* hiding something, Ines. You'd have to be blind not to smell it. And whatever it is, it's bigger than a stolen engine part."

She went up after Finch. Bram stayed, filling the doorway, holding his paint-can tea.

"You know what I think?" he said.

"I genuinely don't."

"I think you're asking the wrong question too." He lowered himself carefully onto the bench — the boat groaned when Bram sat anywhere, a sound I'd stopped noticing and now found oddly comforting. "You're asking who told Calloway. That's a small question. Small questions make small enemies." He blew on his tea. "Big question is: how did Calloway know where to look at all? Wren ran from them for weeks. He hid that map inside a dying man's coat. If they knew the waypoints, they never needed to chase him at all."

I stared at him. Bram has this way of saying enormous things in a mild voice, like he's commenting on the weather, and it takes a moment for the enormity to land.

"They had a fragment," I said slowly. "Wren said they had a fragment. Part of the map. He stole it back off them years ago, that's why they wanted him—"

"A fragment shows *some* waypoints," Bram said. "Maybe not all. Maybe not the right ones. So they plant a buoy on every likely spot and wait to see which boat comes sniffing." He shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Or somebody gave them the whole thing. Either way — that buoy isn't a message to their fleet, Captain. It's a message to *you*. They're saying: we're watching the door."

* * *

I went up on deck to apologize, because whatever else I was — debtor, fool, captain of a rust-bucket with a temperamental engine — I wasn't going to be the kind of man who accused a kid and then sulked below decks about it.

Finch was at the stern rail, hugging her elbows against the wind, watching the buoy fall away behind us as Odile took us around the Gull's Tooth at a respectful distance. I stood next to her. Neither of us said anything for a while. The cliffs of the Reach slid past, red and black strata like the coast had been cut open.

"I was wrong," I said finally. "The way I asked. Accusing you in front of everyone like that — that was cowardice wearing a captain's hat. I'm sorry."

She didn't look at me. "You're not sorry I'm angry. You're sorry the crew saw it."

"Both things can be true."

"Yeah," she said. "That's what my mother used to say. Right before she left."

Ouch. Deliberately, precisely, professionally ouch. I let it sit.

"Here's what I'll give you," she said eventually, still watching the water. "I didn't call Calloway. I've never spoken to a Calloway man in my life, and if I ever do, I'll bite him. What I *will* say is that I knew more about this trip than I told you, and I had reasons, and they're mine, and you're not getting them today." She finally turned and looked at me, chin up, daring me to push. "Can you sail with that? Or do I jump at the Tooth and swim home?"

I thought about it. Honestly thought about it, out there in the wind, with the gray-and-green flag shrinking astern and Wren's promise folded in oilskin against my ribs.

Every person on this boat was hiding something. Odile's disgrace — whatever tribunal had stripped her papers, she'd never said more than "the finding was political" in a voice that ended conversations. Bram's blacklist, and the family-shaped absence underneath it that he touched only in the dark hours. My own debts, stacked like cordwood, and the dying man in my bed whose last request I hadn't fully understood when I made it. We were a crew assembled entirely out of locked rooms. Of course Finch had one too. Of course hers had a kid's whole life behind the door.

"Sail with it," I said. "For now. But Finch — when you're ready to open that room, I'd rather it was to me than to Calloway. Whatever's in there, I'd rather hear it from you than find it out the hard way."

Something moved behind her eyes — the same something from the dock, the thing I'd been too busy to look at. She nodded once, sharp, and that was the whole treaty. Signed in wind and salt, no witnesses.

* * *

We cleared the Tooth at noon and turned south along the outer line, and that's when Odile, glassing the horizon out of pure professional habit, went rigid at the wheel.

"Ines."

"What?"

"How many ships does the Calloway survey fleet run out of this port?"

"Three big, two tenders. Why?"

"Because I count smoke," she said, "and I count four."

I took the glasses. Far off to the southeast, right at the edge where sea gives up and becomes sky, four smudges of funnel smoke crawled in formation, heading the same direction we were. Heading, unless I badly missed my guess, for the second waypoint.

Ahead of schedule. Planted buoys. Four ships where there should have been two.

Somebody hadn't just leaked our departure time. Somebody had handed Calloway the whole playbook — and the only question left was whether the leak was worth money or worth more than money.

Behind me, I heard Finch come quietly down into the engine well, and I heard her turn the lock on the tool locker, the little brass click carrying through the deck like a confession.

Or like preparation. With Finch, that week, I couldn't yet tell the difference.
