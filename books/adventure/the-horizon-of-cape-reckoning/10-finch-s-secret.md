## 10. Finch's Secret

There are three ways to be caught lying on a boat, and I'd like to report that I chose the dignified one, but the truth is Ines Marchetti just walked into the engine room at dawn and said my name in a voice I hadn't heard him use before.

"Finch."

Not *hey, Finch, the coolant's doing that thing again.* Not *Finch, hand me the wrench before Odile kills us both.* Just my name, set down between us like a coin on a bar.

I was elbow-deep in the seawater pump, which meant I couldn't run, which was probably not an accident. He'd waited until I had both hands full of engine. That's the kind of man he was turning into — the kind who waits until your hands are full.

"Morning," I said, going for breezy and landing somewhere around guilty.

"Sit with me a minute."

"I'm working."

"You've been working since three." He crouched down so we were level, which was worse than standing over me. Standing over me I could have handled. "The Calloway buoy was anchored at the first waypoint. Exactly the first waypoint. Not near it, not searching for it — on it, like someone drew them a picture."

"Could be a coincidence. Buoys go places."

"Odile says the fleet left port two days after we did but arrived ahead of us. Bram says the spotter boat that nearly caught us at the light turned toward us before we cleared the breakwater, like it knew where to look. And I keep thinking about a girl who showed up out of nowhere with no papers, no last name, and a very good reason to know what a map to the Meridian looked like when she saw one."

The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above us, gulls were having an argument about nothing, the way gulls do. I wiped my hands on a rag that was already more grease than cloth, mostly to have something to do with them.

"So ask me," I said.

"I'm asking."

*Are you the leak?* is what he wasn't saying, because Ines Marchetti, for all his debts and his drowning eyes, didn't have cruelty in him. He was asking the question sideways, gently, giving me every exit in the world, and somehow that made it harder than if he'd just thrown me overboard and been done with it.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. You want the whole thing?"

"The whole thing."

I sat down on the deck plates because my legs had opinions about standing, and I told him.

* * *

My mother's name was Lila Wren, and she spent her whole life making sure nobody said the second part out loud.

You have to understand what Aldous Wren was to people like my mother — not a father, exactly. A weather system. A story that blew through every few years, leaving things behind: a carved bird here, a fistful of coins there, a baby named Lila with his exact stubborn chin. My mother grew up in a boarding house in Saltmarsh with a woman who wasn't quite her grandmother telling her stories about a man who could find gold in a rock and couldn't find his way back to his own front door.

He came when she was nine. He came when she was fourteen. He came once when I was six, sat me on his knee, taught me to read tide tables upside down, called me *little compass*, and then he was gone again, and my mother cried for a week and then never spoke of him again except to say, when she'd had enough drink in her, *that man owes this family a fortune, and he doesn't even know it.*

She died two winters ago. Fever, cheap doctor, the usual arithmetic of being poor. And I stayed in Saltmarsh another year hating him for it, until the winter a man came through town asking questions about Aldous Wren's family. Asking whether there was anyone. A daughter. A granddaughter. Anyone who might come forward about his affairs.

I should have known what that meant — that somebody wanted to find his kin either to help them or to make sure they couldn't interfere — but I was seventeen and grieving and stupid with hope, so I packed a bag and followed the questions south, all the way to Cape Reckoning, looking for a grandfather I half-hated and wholly needed to be real.

By the time I got there, he was dying in some debt-ridden storm chaser's house, and I never got the chance.

That part I still haven't forgiven anybody for. Including myself, for being slow.

"So you found him?" Ines asked quietly.

"No." The word came out flat. "I got to Cape Reckoning and started asking around, careful, you know? Too careful, maybe. By the time I heard about an old man holed up with the Marchetti fellow, he was dead and you were provisioning a boat and the whole harbor was talking about a map." I made myself look at him. "I signed on to your crew because I recognized the way you were folding that chart. I used to watch him fold charts exactly like that. Same creases. Same little ritual, tucking the corners in. I stood in the fish market and watched you do it and I thought — that's his map. That's my grandfather's map, and this stranger is carrying it to wherever he died trying to get."

Ines didn't say anything for a while. The engine ticked. Above us the gulls kept on about whatever gulls find worth arguing over.

"Why hide it?" he finally said. "Why not tell me the first day?"

"Because I'd watched men follow that old man their whole lives wanting what he carried. Because the minute I said *Wren*, I stopped being a mechanic and became a claimant, and claims get bought and sold and drowned." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Because Voss would pay for a Wren the way he'd pay for a map. Because I've been nobody my whole life and nobody doesn't get thrown off boats."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Finch. Nobody on this boat is nobody."

"Yeah, well." I picked at the rag. "Easy to say from inside the family you built in four days."

Something moved across his face at that — something that had teeth in it — and he let it pass.

"You're not the leak," he said. It wasn't a question anymore. It was him setting down a weight.

"No. I'm not the leak." And then, because keeping secrets is how we got here: "But I know who is."

* * *

Here's the thing about harbormasters: they see everything and are seen by no one. They know which boat left at which hour, whose hold was heavy, whose crew drank too much, whose debts smell like blood in the water. Harbormaster Quill had waved us out past the breakwater himself, lantern raised, smiling his soft gray smile, and I'd thought nothing of it because I was busy holding the engine together with prayer and baling wire.

But I'd spent two months in that harbor before signing onto this voyage. Two months of sleeping in boathouses and doing repair work under the table, and small harbors talk, and I listen, because listening is free and everything else costs.

"Quill keeps a ledger nobody official knows about," I told Ines. We'd gone up on deck; dawn was coming up the color of old pewter, and Odile was at the wheel pretending very hard not to eavesdrop, which fooled neither of us. "Second ledger. Debts, favors, information. Voss owns half of it. Everyone knows Voss owns half of it and everyone pretends they don't, because the other half of Quill's business is selling silence about the first half."

"He sold our departure time." Ines said it like he was testing the weight of it.

"He sold the departure time, the heading, and probably your crew list. He'd have sold your grandmother's recipe if you had one." I hugged my knees against the chill coming off the water. "I heard two longshoremen joking about it the night before we sailed. One said Quill had come into money lately. The other said Quill always comes into money whenever somebody rich wants to know something. I didn't put it together until the buoy. Until now. I'm sorry. I should've—"

"You were seventeen and keeping your own secret," Ines said. "You couldn't carry mine too."

"It's not an excuse."

"No," he agreed. "It's an explanation. Different animal." He looked out at the gray line of the horizon for a long moment. "Voss paid Quill. Voss paid Quill with money he expects to get back out of my hide, or out of that wreck. Which means every mile we sail, we're sailing on Voss's credit too."

"Then we sail faster," Odile said from the wheel, not turning around. "And next port, somebody has a conversation with Quill."

"Somebody with better manners than me," Bram rumbled from the bow, where he'd appeared sometime during the confession like a large, gentle piece of scenery deciding to move. He'd been listening the whole time. Of course he had. Bram listened the way rocks listen — patiently, permanently. "Though my manners improve considerably when children lie awake nights afraid of being thrown off boats."

I felt my face go hot. "I'm not a child."

"You're seventeen," Bram said. "On this boat, that makes you the child. That's just mathematics."

And Ines — Ines crossed the deck and sat down next to me against the rail, shoulder almost touching mine, and said, "Wren."

"What?"

"Aldous Wren. He talked about a daughter in Saltmarsh. Once. When the fever had him. He said her name like an apology." His voice went rough at the edges. "He never mentioned a granddaughter. But he was out of time, and dying men ration their regrets." He turned and looked at me straight on, and his eyes were the same storm-gray as the horizon behind him. "He gave me a promise to keep. Turns out he gave me the person to keep it for, and she was fixing my engine the whole time."

I want to tell you I said something worthy of the moment. Something about legacy, or forgiveness, or the tide that brings everything home eventually. What I actually said was:

"He folded charts corner-in, right? You noticed that too?"

And Ines laughed — really laughed, the first time since Wren died — and said, "Corner-in. Drove me mad the first week."

So there we sat, the debtor and the runaway, grandchildren of the same dead man in every way that counted, watching the sun try to burn through the pewter, while a gentle giant pretended not to cry at the bow and a disgraced pilot steered us toward a wreck that was starting to feel less like treasure and more like inheritance.

* * *

We held a sort of funeral for my secrecy that afternoon. Odile insisted. She said secrets on a small boat are ballast — fine until the storm, and deadly in it — so she made me stand in the middle of the deck and tell the whole story again, start to finish, to everybody, including the parts I'd skipped for Ines. The boarding house in Saltmarsh. The fever. The man asking questions about Wren's family, and how I'd run toward him instead of away, because grief makes you stupid in specific directions.

Bram cried twice. Big silent tears, like rain on a mountain, no drama about it.

Odile, when I finished, just nodded and said, "My license, my fault, my pride, my penance. Your name, your blood, your hope. See? Now we're all carrying each other's luggage. That's what a crew is."

"That's not what a crew is," I said. "That's what a family is."

Odile smiled at the horizon. "On this boat, same thing."

Later, near dusk, Ines found me in the engine room again. He didn't crouch this time. He handed me something folded — a chart, corner tucked in, creases worn soft as cloth.

It wasn't Wren's map. It was a copy of Wren's map, redrawn in Ines's own hand, with additions — soundings Odile had taken, notes on the channel, the waypoints marked in fresh ink. And in the bottom margin, in small careful letters, a new line:

*For Mara Wren, from her father, by way of his granddaughter and her crew.*

"I redrew it after he died," Ines said. "Never knew why. Felt like it needed a living hand on it." He tapped the margin. "Now I know why. When we deliver it, you deliver it. Family hands it to family."

I held the chart like it might bolt. My grandfather's coastline, my crew's corrections, my name in the margin of a promise.

"Get some sleep," Ines said, heading for the ladder. "Long night coming. Barometer's falling."

I looked at the brass gauge on the bulkhead and felt the hair on my arms lift. It had fallen four points since noon, and outside, far off to the southwest, the sky had begun assembling something dark and patient, the way it does before it decides to be a storm.

I slept with the chart under my jacket, one hand on the engine, and dreamed of a man folding maps corner-in, waiting somewhere out past the light for us to bring his promise home.
