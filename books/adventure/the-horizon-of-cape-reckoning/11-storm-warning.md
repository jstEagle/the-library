## 11. Storm Warning

I have been reading weather for thirty years, which means I have spent thirty years being wrong in increasingly creative ways. The sea forgives nothing and forgets less, and every pilot who ever told you she could read the sky was lying to you or to herself. But there are days — rare days, maybe four or five in a decade — when the air itself seems to lean toward you and whisper what's coming, and on the fourth morning out from Cape Reckoning, the air leaned in close and told me something I did not want to hear.

The barometer had been falling since midnight. Not falling the way it does before ordinary weather, in polite little increments you can ignore while you drink your coffee. Falling the way a man falls when the deck tilts under him — fast, and with intent. By dawn it had dropped twenty points. By the time Finch came scrambling up from the engine room with her hair full of grease and her face full of a question she didn't know how to ask, it had dropped thirty.

"Odile." She said my name the way people say *fire*. "The glass is doing something I've only read about."

"Show me."

The barometer hung in the wheelhouse where I'd mounted it the first day, because a boat without a barometer is a boat with a death wish, and I had spent enough of my life flirting with death without inviting it aboard formally. The needle sat at the bottom of the dial like a dog that had been kicked once too often and had decided, on reflection, to stay down.

I tapped the glass. The needle didn't so much as tremble.

"When did it start?"

"Sometime in the night. I noticed it around two, thought the mechanism was sticking. It wasn't."

No. It wasn't. I went out onto the deck and looked south, past the bow, past the gray water running gray-white with wind catspaws that hadn't existed an hour ago, and there it was. The sky over the southern horizon had organized itself. That's the only word for it. Clouds don't just happen; they *decide*, and this one had decided, and it was stacking itself up into a long dark shelf with the particular bruised underbelly that every pilot on this coast learns to dread before she learns to read.

"Finch," I said. "Go get Ines."

"He's sleeping."

"Finch. Go get Ines."

She went.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about storms on this coast, and I'll tell it to you the way my father told it to me, sitting on a crate in the pilot's office at Serrated Reach, rolling cigarettes he never smoked because he said the ritual was the point, not the tobacco.

The cape bends storms. That's not a figure of speech. The headland juts out into the shipping lanes like a jaw, and the warm water of the southern current meets the cold water of the deep channel right off the point, and when a low-pressure system comes down from the northwest, the cape takes it by the shoulders and turns it — sometimes out to sea, sometimes straight into the coast, and no computer model on earth has ever gotten the prediction right more often than the old pilots flipping a coin. The harbormaster's office at Cape Reckoning kept a ledger of named storms going back a hundred and forty years. Half the names were the pilots who'd called them. The other half were the pilots who'd died underestimating them.

I had been one of the pilots. Before. Before the incident, before the inquiry, before my license went into a drawer somewhere and my name went onto a list of people nobody would trust with a rowboat. That's a story for another chapter, as they say, and it's not a chapter I intend to write. What matters is this: I knew storms the way you know a relative who did time. With love. With fear. With the understanding that they are capable of anything.

Ines came up the companionway in under a minute, which told me he hadn't really been sleeping either. None of us had been really sleeping since the waypoint, since the Calloway buoy, since the question of *who told them* had been sitting at our table like a guest who wouldn't leave. He looked at the barometer, then at the southern sky, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd been doing since dawn.

"How fast is it moving?"

"North-northeast, maybe fourteen knots. It'll bend at the cape. The only question is which way."

"And if it bends in?"

"Then it comes down the channel ahead of us and sits on the wreck site like a lid." I pulled the chart across the wheelhouse table and weighted the corners with mugs. "Two days of heavy sea. The window closes anyway when the channel silts. Lose two tides to shelter and we arrive with maybe hours to work, if the Calloway fleet doesn't beat us there outright."

Ines stared at the chart. I watched his finger trace the coastline — the long sweep of the outer shore, the Serrated Reach behind us, and then, jutting out ahead, the spur of rock and shoal that the charts called the Outer Passage and that the pilots called, when they called it anything at all, the Devil's Corridor.

"You're thinking the outer passage," he said.

"I'm thinking about it the way a man thinks about jumping off a cliff because the bridge is out. Yes."

* * *

I called the crew together at the rail, because some conversations you have standing up, where anyone can walk away.

The storm was visible now to everyone, a gray wall in the south with lightning flickering inside it like something alive turning over in its sleep. The wind had come around and was pushing at our starboard quarter with the persistent shove of a thing testing whether we'd move. The *Kestrel's Wake* — Ines's boat, our boat now, though none of us had earned the word — rode the swell with the stubborn grace of a vessel that was better built than her owner's finances had ever suggested.

Ines laid it out plainly, which I'll give him credit for. He never dressed a bad choice up as a good one. Shelter in the lee of Gull Rock, lose two tides, arrive at the wreck with the channel silting under us and Calloway possibly already there. Or run the outer passage, threading between the storm's edge and the shoals, gaining a day and a half, betting all our lives on my ability to read water that would be trying to kill us.

"Odile," he said. "It's your call. I mean that. Nobody else on this boat has the experience to make it, and I won't pretend otherwise."

That, too, I'll give him credit for. Most captains would have decided and then pretended to consult.

Bram spoke first, which surprised no one. Bram always spoke first when it was time to say the simple thing. "I've been in weather," he said. "Not this weather. But I've been in weather where the choice was made for me, and people died because a man in an office decided the cargo was worth the risk." He looked at the southern sky with an expression I couldn't read and didn't need to. "I'd rather choose. Even wrong. I'd rather it be us choosing."

Finch had her arms crossed and her jaw set, which on her meant she'd already decided and was waiting for permission to say so. "The engine will hold," she said. "I can't promise it'll hold *well*. But if we're asking it to do something insane, I'd rather be there when it does than sitting at anchor wondering."

"And you, Odile?" Ines said.

And there it was. The question I'd been avoiding since dawn.

Here is the thing about fear that nobody tells you when you're young: it doesn't go away with experience. It gets more specific. When I was twenty I was afraid of everything at once, a general dread, weather as an undifferentiated menace. Now I was afraid of exact things. I was afraid of the moment when the wind came around the cape's shoulder and the sea stood up in shapes I'd seen before and buried people I'd known. I was afraid of being wrong in the specific way I'd been wrong before, the way that had cost me my name. I was afraid — and this was the fear underneath all the others, the one I'd carried out of the inquiry like a splinter too deep to dig — of being the one who made the call that killed the crew.

But.

But Aldous Wren had died in Ines's house with a map in his fist and a daughter he'd never made peace with, and he had trusted a stranger with the only thing he had left, which was the truth about where the Meridian lay. And the Calloway fleet — the same fleet whose flag I'd once saluted, whose practices I'd once defended at an inquiry because I was young and stupid and flattered — was racing us to that wreck with money and guns and no promise at all. And if we sheltered, and if they arrived first, and if they pulled whatever was in that strongroom up out of the dark, then Aldous Wren's last act on this earth would have been a joke told at his own expense.

Fear is a good advisor and a terrible captain. My father said that too. He said a lot of things, most of them while rolling cigarettes he didn't smoke.

"We run it," I said. "The outer passage. But we do it my way."

"Which is?"

"Which is that I don't sleep, I don't eat anything I can't hold in one hand, and when I tell you to do something, you do it before you finish understanding it. The storm's edge will give us a corridor of maybe six miles between the worst of the weather and the shoal water. The corridor will move. We move with it. If I say come about, we come about. If I say drop sail, we drop sail. If I say—" I stopped, because the next part was the part that mattered. "If I say we've lost it, if the corridor closes, we run north for the lee of the Faro Rocks and we shelter and we lose the tides and we live. The promise doesn't get kept by dead people. Wren needed us *alive* more than he needed us *fast*. Remember that."

Ines nodded slowly. "And if the corridor holds?"

"Then we come out the far side a day and a half ahead of Calloway, and we'll have earned it."

Finch was grinning. Bram had put his hand flat on the rail, the way he did, as if steadying the boat by touching it. And Ines — Ines was looking at the storm the way I had seen him look at it once before, back in the harbor, when a squall had come over the cliffs and he'd stood on his deck with his camera and his face had gone young and hungry. That was the thing about Ines Marchetti that I understood before I trusted it: he loved weather the way other men loved women or drink or God. Recklessly, and with his whole chest.

"Storm chaser," I said to him. "You've chased them your whole life. Ever sailed into one?"

"Never had a reason to," he said. "Now I do."

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and I will tell you that preparation is the only honest prayer a sailor ever says.

Finch stripped down and rebuilt the engine's fuel system with the tenderness of a girl grooming a difficult horse. She came up twice to check the barometer and went back down twice without a word, because the needle had stopped falling and started *lying* — hovering, wavering, the way it does in the dead calm eye-wall of an approaching system. Bram lashed everything that could be lashed and threw overboard, with genuine regret, a third of our fresh water in casks too heavy to secure, keeping the rest in the bilge where it would ride low and steady. Ines went over the rigging line by line, and I watched him find a chafed halyard that would have parted in the first hour and replace it, and I thought: *this man has been poor so long that he maintains his boat better than he maintains himself, and thank God for it.*

I stood at the wheel and watched the sky.

By evening the storm had a name. The coastal radio, crackling through static, gave it out in the flat voice they use for such things — a woman's name, one of the old-fashioned ones, the kind the weather service pulls from a list compiled before anyone thought about what it means to name a thing that kills. I won't repeat it here. Names are for pets and children. What was coming was neither.

The barometer read the lowest I'd seen in twenty years at sea.

We ate a hot meal — Bram insisted, and Bram was right, because you fight weather on a full stomach or not at all — and then Ines stood at the rail beside me while the first outer bands of wind came across the water and began to comb the sea into long white lines.

"Odile," he said. "The inquiry. What happened. You don't have to tell me."

"No," I said. "I don't."

"Will you, someday?"

"Maybe. If we live."

He laughed — a short, real laugh, the first I'd heard from him since the buoy. "That's the best offer I've had all year."

"Low bar," I said. "You owe a loan shark your boat and your name, you're racing a salvage fleet to a wreck with a crew of people nobody else would hire, and you've promised a dead man's fortune to a daughter who may not want it. The bar is on the seafloor, Ines."

"Then we'll dig it up," he said, "along with everything else."

I turned back to the wheel. Behind us, the storm's leading edge had reached the horizon's midpoint, and the light had gone that particular green-gray that old pilots call *the color of the last decision*. The wind was rising in long, steady breaths now, each one stronger than the last, like something enormous waking up and remembering what it was for.

I put the *Kestrel's Wake* on the heading I'd chosen in my head a hundred times since dawn — south by southeast, straight at the dark, straight for the corridor between the storm's rage and the shoals' patience — and I said the only prayer I've ever believed in, which is the checklist.

"Engine room," I called down. "Full ahead. We're going through."

And from below, Finch's voice came up through the deck plates, thin and clear and absolutely certain:

"Hold on, then."

The first real gust hit us before her words had finished echoing, and the horizon began, very slowly, to disappear.
