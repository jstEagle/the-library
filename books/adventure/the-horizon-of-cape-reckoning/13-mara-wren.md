## 13. Mara Wren

The judge ruled against me on a Tuesday, in the flat tone of a man reading out the price of eggs.

"Salvage rights revert to Calloway Maritime pending documentation of prior claim," he said, and then he took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, which was as close as the law of this coast ever came to an apology.

I said "Thank you, Your Honor," because I am my mother's daughter even when my father's blood is screaming, and I gathered my papers with hands that did not shake until they were safely under the table where no one could bill me for it.

Three years. Three years of depositions and docket numbers and men in gray suits explaining, with great patience, that the *Meridian* had been lost with all hands in '61 due to weather, that weather is nobody's fault, that fault requires proof, and proof requires documents that went to the bottom of the sea along with eleven sailors and whatever else Calloway didn't want found. Three years of watching their lawyers smile at me the way you'd smile at a child insisting there's something under the bed.

There was something under the bed. There always had been. But the law doesn't look under beds unless you can afford the flashlight, and Calloway owned every flashlight on this coast.

* * *

My office — I use the word generously; it was a rented room above a chandlery with a view of a wall — smelled of cold coffee and old paper when I got back that afternoon. I had a system for days like this one. It involved a bottle of rye kept behind the maritime almanacs, which is fitting, because both are for navigating waters you'd rather not be in.

The telegram was waiting on my desk. That alone should have warned me. Nobody telegrams a losing lawyer unless they want something or someone has died.

**HAVE WORD OF CREW SAILED FROM CAPE RECKONING STOP PURPOSE SALVAGE MERIDIAN STOP THEY SEEK MARA WREN STOP SENDER DYING PROSPECTOR ALDOUS WREN STOP REPEAT ALDOUS WREN STOP**

I read it four times.

The first time, I read it as fraud. This coast produces con men the way tide pools produce crabs — small, quick, and armored. A dead man's name attached to a treasure hunt is bait older than the telegraph office itself. I have seen grieving widows parted from savings by less.

The second time, I read it as a mistake. There were Wrens scattered up and down the littoral like dropped stitches. Aldous could be any Aldous. My father had been dead to me for nineteen years anyway, which is a long enough time for a man to become a rumor about himself.

The third time, I noticed the operator's mark at the bottom — a notation that the sender had paid for delivery confirmation twice, insisting on the repetition of the name. Dying men are precise about names. They know exactly how much time each word costs them.

The fourth time, I put the telegram down and sat very still and did not think about my father at all, and thought about nothing else for the better part of an hour.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Aldous Wren, or believed I knew, which on this coast amounts to the same currency:

He left when I was nine. Not dramatically — no slammed doors, no note pinned with a knife. He simply went out on a survey contract one spring and the contract ended and he didn't come home. My mother waited a year, then two, then stopped setting his place at the table, then stopped talking about him except in the past tense, and finally not even that. She raised me on her own wages as a ship's chandler's clerk, taught me contracts before cursive because, she said, "one of these will feed you, girl, and it isn't your handwriting."

She died believing he was alive somewhere, or perhaps just unable to prove otherwise. Hope, in our family, is a stubborn tenant who won't leave even after the roof caves in.

And here is what I suspected, in the cellar of myself where I keep things I don't serve to guests:

That "survey contract" had been prospecting. That he'd gone looking for something worth more than coming home. That somewhere between my ninth birthday and my twenty-eighth, a man chose a wreck over a wife and daughter, and then spent the rest of his life too ashamed or too obsessed to choose again.

If some stranger had told me all this over drinks, I'd have bought him nothing but contempt. But the telegram hadn't come from a stranger. It had come from a dying man spending his last coins to spell my name correctly, twice, to a woman who had spent three years and most of her money trying to burn down the company that owned this coast.

Skeptical? Yes. Sentimental? God, no. But I will tell you what I am, and what I have been since the day the first Calloway lawyer called my claim "a daughter's grief dressed up in legal language":

I am a woman who does not waste ammunition. And if there was even a chance — one chance in a hundred, one in a thousand — that my father had found the proof I needed, and had sent it to me instead of selling it, then I would cross this water if I had to swim it.

* * *

I took the telegram to Tam Osei, who runs the harbor office on this side and who owes me for getting his nephew out of a contract dispute with a fish cannery that made Calloway's lawyers look like amateurs. Tam read it twice, whistled through the gap in his teeth, and looked at me with the particular pity harbor men reserve for people about to do something expensive.

"Cape Reckoning's four days' sail in anything honest," he said. "Two and a half in the *Kestrel*, and the *Kestrel*'s skipper is a pirate with paperwork."

"How much?"

"More than you have."

"I have a little."

"You have a reputation and a losing record." He tapped the telegram. "And now you have this. Which means somebody on that side of the water thinks you're worth finding. Use that."

"What are you suggesting?"

"I'm suggesting you stop thinking like a lawyer and start thinking like a salvage man. You don't pay full fare for passage. You offer a share contingent on outcome. The *Kestrel*'s skipper — her name's Ilse Vantol — has hated Calloway since they blacklisted her husband's boat. She'll take your paper. She'll also take a cut of whatever you're after, so decide right now how much of your heart you're willing to sign away."

I decided in about four seconds, which tells you something about the state of my heart that year.

* * *

Ilse Vantol turned out to be fifty-ish, iron-gray, with forearms like dock rope and the handshake of a woman who has hauled lines in weather that would make a bishop swear. She read my proposed charter agreement standing up, in the wind, without a coat, while I shivered and pretended not to.

"Contingent share," she said. "Against Calloway. In open water." She smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "You're either desperate or suicidal."

"Both, historically."

"And this crew that's already sailed — friends of yours?"

"No." I heard how it sounded and made myself say the rest plainly, because lying to the person steering your hull is a poor survival strategy. "I've never met them. A man I haven't seen in nineteen years apparently sent them. I'm chasing a maybe across open ocean in storm season."

Vantol folded the agreement and put it inside her coat, next to her heart, which I suspect is where she keeps everything she intends to hold against you later.

"My late husband used to say the best cargo is the kind you believe in," she said. "Worst cargo too. Come aboard at dawn tide. Bring warm clothes and whatever evidence you've got, because if we run into Calloway out there, I want to know exactly what kind of trouble we're carrying."

* * *

I packed that night like a lawyer and not like a daughter, because I have learned to keep those two women in separate rooms. Deposition transcripts. The Meridian's original charter filings, which I had copied at ruinous expense. Correspondence from a retired harbormaster who once hinted, drunk, that Calloway's insurance payout on the *Meridian* had been suspiciously prompt. Everything I'd spent three years assembling into a case that kept collapsing like wet sand.

At the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, I put the only thing my father ever gave me that wasn't absence: a brass compass with my initials scratched inside the case. I was eight. He let me think I'd stolen it, which he must have known was a kindness, because taking a gift meant admitting he might leave and gifts could follow you.

Nineteen years I'd carried the thing without opening the case. That night I opened it. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with the idiot confidence of all instruments, and I closed it again before I could start talking to it.

* * *

Dawn tide. Gray light, gray water, gulls arguing over the harbor like creditors. The *Kestrel* was a hundred-foot former pilot cutter, all engine and attitude, and Vantol's crew of four looked me over with the frank appraisal of people deciding whether I'd be ballast or cargo.

We cleared the headland at seven. By nine the cape behind us was a smudge, and by ten it was a memory, and I stood at the stern rail doing the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do.

Four days to Cape Reckoning. The telegram was five days old. If this crew — whoever they were, however many, sailing on whatever leaky conviction my father had purchased with his last breath — had left when he died, they were already ahead of me. Ahead of me and sailing toward a wreck that Calloway had spent sixty years pretending didn't exist, which meant Calloway's salvage fleet would be sailing toward it too, faster and better armed, the moment anyone whispered the word *Meridian* in the wrong ear.

I was not racing to a reunion. Let's be clear-eyed about that; it's the one virtue my profession beat into me. I was racing to a crime scene. Whatever my father had found down there, the moment it surfaced, every shark on this coast would turn toward the blood — and among the sharks would be the men whose lawyers had smiled at me across a courtroom for three years.

Unless. Unless the dying prospector hadn't just sent a crew. Unless he'd sent proof. Documents travel badly through water but they survive it beautifully, sealed and boxed, and a strongroom is a strongroom whether it sits under a courthouse or forty fathoms of green sea.

Hope, I told myself firmly, is not evidence. Hope is the thing you feel when the evidence runs out.

Then I went below, spread my transcripts across the chart table, and began annotating them by lantern light as the *Kestrel* ran east into the long swells — because hope may not be evidence, but I have never once lost a case by being overprepared, and I do not intend to start with the only one that ever mattered.

Somewhere out past that horizon, strangers were carrying my father's last promise through weather I didn't want to imagine, toward a fleet I couldn't outfight and a deadline I couldn't see.

I intended to arrive before any of them did.

* * *

It was near midnight of the second day, running with a quartering swell, when the lookout sang out a light on the starboard bow — a single white lamp, riding high, moving fast, closing.

Vantol was beside me at the rail before I finished buttoning my coat. She trained her glass on it for a long moment, and I watched her jaw tighten around whatever she saw.

"Fishing smack's running lights," she said slowly. "Except fishing smacks don't run dark-and-single-lamp at twelve knots in open water, and they don't fly—" she lowered the glass, and handed it to me without looking away from the light— "that."

I raised the glass. The lamp bobbed, steadied, and beneath it, catching the moon, I could just make out the shape on the hull: the long white C inside a ring.

Calloway colors. Running alone, running fast, running straight at us — or rather, straight past us, on a bearing I recognized instantly, because I'd drawn it myself a hundred times in the margins of my case notes.

The bearing of Cape Reckoning.

"They know," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "They're already going."

Vantol took back the glass. For a moment neither of us spoke, and the only sounds were the sea hitting the hull and the engine turning over beneath our feet like a held breath.

"Well, counselor," she said at last, and reached for the engine telegraph. "You wanted to intercept somebody. Looks like somebody's decided to intercept us. Helm's yours to advise — talk fast."
