## 14. Betrayal at Anchor

The second waypoint was a drowned forest, which sounds like poetry until you're looking for it.

Aldous Wren's map marked it as a circle with a cross through the middle and a single word in his cramped, feverish hand: *STUMPS*. Two hundred years ago the sea had swallowed a pine grove whole, and now at low tide the black tips of dead trees broke the surface like the knuckles of a hand reaching up to complain about the accommodations. Odile threaded us between them at half throttle, calling depths from memory of a chart nobody had printed since before Finch was born.

"Three fathoms," she said. "Two and a half. There — hard to port."

Finch leaned out over the rail, one hand on the engine hatch, squinting at the water. "You know this by heart?"

"I knew a man who ran aground here once." A pause. "He's part of the reef now."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Anchor down."

We dropped hook in a pocket of deep water ringed by stumps, sheltered enough from the long swell that the boat only shrugged instead of shuddering. The tide tables gave us four hours here before we'd need to move again — time to check the bearings against Wren's notes, eat something that didn't come from a can if Bram's fishing luck held, and sleep in shifts. Three tides spent. Three left, after this one, before the channel silted shut and the Meridian became a rumor forever.

I remember thinking, standing at the wheel while the others moved below, that things were going almost well.

There's a particular kind of silence the sea makes when something is wrong. Not an absence of sound — an absence of the *right* sound. Gulls gone quiet. The water flattening in a way that has nothing to do with wind. I'd spent ten years chasing storms and I could read the sky like other men read their debts, but I'd never learned to hear that silence until it was too late to matter.

Bram heard it first. He was at the galley stove when he went still, wooden spoon halfway to his mouth, head cocked like a dog hearing a whistle no human can catch.

"Ines," he said. "Engine."

"I don't hear anything."

"No. You don't."

And then I did — or rather I heard what wasn't there. No gulls. No fish breaking the surface in the lee of the stumps. Just our own idle burbling against the hull, and beyond it, coming around the drowned forest from the northeast, a low gray shape with a Calloway house flag hanging limp off its stern staff.

A tender. Forty feet of corporate efficiency, twin diesels idling down as it slid into our anchorage like it owned the water, which in every way that counted, it did.

* * *

Odile came up the companionway first, moving without hurry, which is her way with everything except navigation. She looked at the tender, then at me, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the situation hadn't already said better.

Finch appeared behind her, wrench already in her fist, knuckles white on the handle. "Do we run?"

"On three hours' sleep and half our fuel?" Odile shook her head. "They'd run us down before we cleared the stumps. That's why they came here. This anchorage is a trap with scenery."

The tender swung alongside, close enough that I could see the man at its rail, and my stomach did something complicated and humiliating, because of course it was him. Of course.

Voss stood with his hands clasped behind his back like a tourist admiring the view, wearing a canvas deck coat that probably cost more than my boat, which, to be fair, he also technically owned. He was smiling. Voss was always smiling. It was the most frightening thing about him — the genuine warmth of it, the way he seemed to actually *like* people, right up until he took everything they had. In all the years he'd been squeezing interest out of me I'd never once seen him angry. Anger would have required him to lose something first.

"Marchetti!" he called across the gap of water. "You look terrible. Salt air agrees with some men. With you it just rearranges the misery."

"What do you want, Voss?"

"To talk! Is that a crime? Although—" he glanced upward, mock-thoughtful, "—I suppose it depends who's asking. Shall I come aboard? Your crew looks nervous. The big one especially."

Bram had come up on deck by then, filling the cockpit the way weather fills a horizon, and he was staring at Voss with an expression I'd never seen on him. Bram's face was built for gentleness; even his anger usually looked apologetic. This was something else. This was stillness.

"Don't let him aboard," Bram said quietly. It wasn't fear in his voice. It was recognition.

But there was nowhere to go, and we both knew it, so when Voss stepped across onto my deck — *my* deck, though the paperwork said otherwise — all I could do was stand between him and my crew and wait to find out what this cost.

* * *

He walked around the cockpit like a prospective buyer, trailing one finger along the rail, peering into the cabin hatch. "She's held up better than I expected. Honestly. When you pulled out of harbor I gave you a week before the Reach ate you." He turned back to me, hands in his pockets, radiating good cheer. "The harbormaster gave me your departure time, your heading, and a very flattering description of your pilot. Cost me a fortune. Worth every coin. Do you know what I paid him?"

"I don't care."

"You should care. It was less than you owe me." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd mention the weather. "That's the thing about debt, Marchetti. It prices everything. Every friendship, every loyalty, every promise. Everything has a number, and I own the calculator."

Behind me I heard Finch shift her weight, and Odile say, very softly, "Steady."

"So here's where we are." Voss produced a folded paper from inside his coat and held it up between two fingers like a magician about to make something disappear. "Your position. Your speed. Your waypoints, extrapolated. The Calloway fleet knows exactly where you're going and they'll be there tomorrow, maybe the day after. They have divers, cranes, and a legal department that eats salvage claimants for breakfast." He smiled wider. "You have a leaky boat, a stolen map, and a teenager with a wrench."

"She's nineteen," Finch said.

"Congratulations on the birthday." He never even looked at her. His eyes stayed on mine, warm and patient and bottomless. "So. Last chance, offered sincerely, because despite appearances I respect you, Marchetti. You're the only debtor I've ever had who kept running after it stopped being rational. Give me Wren's chart. The original, the bearings, all of it. Walk away from this, and I walk away from you." He tapped my chest with the folded paper, gently, almost affectionately. "Every coin you owe me. Gone. Clean slate. You walk back into Cape Reckoning a free man for the first time in six years."

The words landed in me like hooks finding flesh.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I've got left that isn't mortgaged: for one full second, I wanted it. Six years. Six years of Voss's cheerful voice on my dock lines, of choosing which bill starved this month, of photographing storms for men who paid in promises. Freedom, handed over like change from a purchase. My body actually leaned toward it before my mind caught up.

And then I thought of Aldous Wren dying on my floor, holding my hand with fingers gone gray, making me swear on the only currency he had left. *Not the fleets. Not the creditors. Mara.* A promise given too easily and kept too dearly, and here was its price tag, itemized, discounted for cash.

"The answer's no," I said.

Voss sighed, and for just a moment the smile flickered — not into anger, but into something almost like disappointment, a teacher watching a promising student fail the final. "No negotiation? No counteroffer? Come on. Everyone has a number."

"Not me. Not anymore."

"Mm." He refolded the paper slowly. "You know what I admire about you? Nothing. But I'm fascinated by you, which is nearly as useful." He glanced past my shoulder at the others, cataloguing them the way he'd catalogue assets. "The pilot — disgraced, unlicensed, unemployable, sails with you because nobody else will have her. The mechanic — a runaway, technically a minor until last month, no family worth mentioning. And him." His gaze settled on Bram, and something in it changed, sharpened, went from appraisal to intent. "Him I've been hearing about. Blacklisted from every crew in the Reach. Big fellow. Gentle, they say. Strong as a crane and twice as careful."

"Bram stays on my boat," I said, and my voice came out flat in a way I didn't recognize.

"Oh, I agree entirely." Voss smiled. "In fact, I insist on it."

It happened fast, the way bad things do — no warning, no wind shift to read. Two men rose from the tender's wheelhouse where they'd been waiting out of sight, and they were across the gap and onto my deck before Odile finished saying *behind you*, and Bram — Bram who could have broken both of them like driftwood, who had once lifted a stalled winch off a trapped deckhand single-handedly — didn't fight. That's what I keep returning to, in the dark watches since. He saw them coming and he *chose*. He put his hands up, slow and open, and he looked at me over their shoulders and said:

"Sail, Ines."

"Bram—"

"They need me more than you do right now." He said it calmly, terribly calmly, while the men took his arms, and he didn't resist, because resistance would have meant blood on my deck and a hostage situation none of us could win, and Bram understood arithmetic better than anyone ever gave him credit for. "Voss wants insurance. Fine. Be expensive to keep alive. That's my whole job now." And then, quieter, just for me, as they turned him toward the tender: "Take care of them. Take care of *it*. Promise him."

I couldn't speak. I nodded like an idiot, rage and helplessness tangled in my throat so tight I couldn't tell which was choking me.

Voss paused at the rail on his way back, and for a moment his cheerfulness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was worse than any threat: simple, patient certainty. "Three tides, Marchetti. The fleet will be at the wreck before you are, and you'll be short-handed, and short-supplied, and alone. Bring me the chart and I return your giant. Refuse, and..." He shrugged, spreading his hands. "Well. Insurance policies lapse. Accidents happen to valuable property. Ask anyone whose boat I own."

Then he stepped down onto his tender, and the two crewmen followed with Bram between them, and the gray hull backed away through the drowned forest, and the gulls came back all at once like nothing had happened, and I stood at my own rail with my fists gripping wood until my knuckles burned.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Odille — Odile — took the wheel without being asked and held us steady in the anchorage, eyes forward, jaw working. Finch sat down on the deck planks, hard, the wrench clattering beside her, and she was crying without any noise at all, tears just falling straight off her chin, because she was nineteen and she'd known Bram for nine days and grief doesn't check how long you've loved someone before it collects.

"He didn't fight," she said finally. "Why didn't he fight?"

"Because fighting would've gotten one of you hurt." I crouched down in front of her. "Because he's stronger than all of us put together, and he used it to hold still. That's the hardest thing a strong man can do."

"We can't just leave him."

"We're not leaving him. We're going to come back for him." I heard myself say it and realized I believed it, which surprised me, because belief has been in short supply aboard this vessel. "Voss thinks he took insurance. He made a mistake. He took the reason we can't stop."

Odile spoke from the wheel without turning. "The tide won't wait for our feelings, Ines. We move in forty minutes or we lose this window, and if we lose this window, Bram's captivity buys nothing but time for Calloway to strip that wreck to the rivets." A pause. "I'm sorry. Someone has to say it."

"I know." I stood. "Get the chart. Both of you. We work the bearings now."

And here is the thing I couldn't explain to them yet, the thing that sat in my chest like ballast as we raised anchor and slipped out through the black stumps with the light going bronze and rotten along the west: Voss had made his offer and I had refused it, and he'd taken Bram, and I should have felt broken. Instead I felt something I didn't have a name for. Something with weight and edges. Rage, yes — rage like a storm cell building past the horizon, patient, organized, taking on structure. And under it, steadying it, the oath. *Mara. Not the fleets. Not the creditors.*

Wren had made me swear before I understood the cost. Now I understood it, and I swore it anyway, again, silently, to the drowned forest and the closing day.

Two tides left. One friend held hostage. One promise that was starting to look less like treasure and more like a debt of a different kind — the kind you can't buy your way out of, only pay.

Ahead, the channel waited, silting hour by hour. Behind us, a gray tender carried the gentlest man I'd ever known toward whatever Voss called justice.

I put the wheel over and aimed us at both.
