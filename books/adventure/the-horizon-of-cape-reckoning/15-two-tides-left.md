## 15. Two Tides Left

They put me in the flagship's hold, which I suppose was meant as an insult but felt more like a compliment. A man my size doesn't fit in a brig. They'd have had to build the cell around me, and Calloway's people don't build anything they can't bill for.

So they gave me a cot that groaned like a dying gull, a bucket, and a lantern bolted to the bulkhead, and they gave me Mr. Quill.

That was his actual name, or close enough to it — a narrow man with narrow hands and the patient eyes of someone who'd learned early that other people's discomfort was a renewable resource. He came down twice a day, sat on an upturned crate, folded those hands, and asked me questions in the tone of a man asking about weather.

"Where does Marchetti keep the map?"

"Who else has seen it?"

"Does he know what the Meridian actually carries?"

I answered all of them with the same expression I use for watching paint dry, which is a skill my mother taught me before she taught me anything else. *A big man who talks too fast, Bram,* she'd say, kneading dough the size of her own head, *is a small man wearing a costume.* She'd have liked Quill, in the way you like a snake you've been warned about.

The thing about being interrogated by professionals is that they're not really interested in your answers. Not at first. They're interested in your silences — where you flinch, where you settle, where your eyes go when a question lands near something true. So I gave them nothing to read. I let my face go flat as a millpond and I thought about bread.

That sounds like a joke. It isn't. Bread is how I survived the years after the *Persephone* went down with my family aboard and the company sent a lawyer instead of a diver. You learn to fill the long dark hours with something warm and rising, something you can shape with your hands even when your hands are tied. I'd built a hundred loaves in my head before Quill finished his first day of work on me.

But here is what nobody tells you about being the hostage: you are not useless. You are intelligence operating behind enemy lines, and the enemy lines happen to be made of steel plates, riveted seams, and men who talk too much around a big quiet fellow because they assume the ears come empty with the size.

I listened.

* * *

By the second morning I knew three things worth knowing.

First: the flagship was called the *Iron Meridian* — some clerk's idea of wit, naming their pride after the wreck they meant to strip — and she was old under her fresh paint. I could hear it in her bones. When the swell came through, her frames spoke to each other in the language of tired timber and corroded fasteners, and there was a note in the starboard quarter, just aft of the engine room, that said *we have been patched and patched badly.*

Second: the crew hated Voss. Not feared him — hated him. Fear keeps a ship running; hatred keeps a ship ready. The bosun drank alone and talked to himself, and what he said to himself was that Voss had promised salvage shares and delivered nothing but deductions. The young one on watch — Ferro, they called him, all elbows and eagerness — had a brother who'd drowned working Calloway's northern grounds, and no one had ever told him why the weather warning hadn't gone out until the boats were already loaded.

Third: they did not know where Ines was. This was the best news of all, and I carried it around inside me like a coal in a closed fist. Twice Quill asked me questions that assumed we'd taken the inner channel, and twice I let him believe it. Let them search the wrong water. Every hour they spent hunting the Serrated Reach was an hour my people spent getting free of it.

I say "my people" now. That happened somewhere between the night stories and the moment Voss's men dragged me over the rail with Bram-sized ropes. You don't get to choose when a thing becomes true. You only notice afterward that it already was.

Quill came down that evening with his crate and his folded hands, and for the first time he didn't open with a question. He opened with silence, letting it sit between us like a third passenger.

"You're a difficult man," he said finally.

"I'm a patient man," I said. My first words in two days. I wanted them to sound rusty. They did.

"Patience runs out."

"Not mine." I looked at my hands, which could have closed around his skull like a walnut shell, and let him see me looking, and let him see that I wouldn't. "You've got me trussed up on a ship full of men who don't want to be here, chasing a man who doesn't quit, into water that kills pilots with twenty years' experience. And you think I'm the one whose patience is going to run out first?"

Something moved behind his narrow eyes. Recalculation. Good. A man who recalculates is a man who can be made to doubt, and a doubting man asks different questions than a confident one.

"The tide window closes," Quill said. "Your friends will lose the channel. Then the wreck is worthless, and Marchetti traded a hostage for nothing."

I smiled at that. I couldn't help it. He leaned back half an inch, which for Quill was the same as another man falling out of his chair.

"You've never watched Ines Marchetti race a deadline," I said. "He once photographed a waterspout from inside it because the client wanted drama. He doesn't lose windows. He climbs through them."

"He'll lose you instead."

And there it was — the real question under all the others, the one they'd been circling since they took me. Not *where is the map*, but *what will they pay*. Voss's whole arithmetic ran on the assumption that everyone has a price and every loyalty has a breaking weight, same as cargo. He'd bought the harbormaster cheap. He'd bought judges cheaper. He genuinely believed that given enough pressure, any man becomes a lever.

What he couldn't model — what men like him never can — is that some of us aren't levers. We're ballast. We sit low in the hull and we keep everything upright, and if you throw us overboard you don't gain speed. You just roll.

"They won't trade," I said, and it wasn't bravado. It was bookkeeping. "Ines swore an oath to a dying man, and I watched him swear it. I know what it cost him. He's not going to spend Mara Wren's future buying me back."

"So you're content to drown."

"I'm content," I said, "to be exactly where I am. Think about what that means."

He thought about it longer than he wanted to. Then he left, taking his lantern-light with him, and I lay back on the groaning cot and got back to work.

Because that was the fourth thing I'd learned, the one I was saving: the secondary channel. There was a chart tube lashed in the chartroom above, and Ferro the elbowy watchman had unrolled it within sight of the companionway while complaining to the bosun about being sent to survey "the blowhole passage" — a fissure in the cave system's seaward wall, marked obsolete, silted, forgotten by everyone except the hydrographic office and, apparently, Finch, who could read an engineering drawing the way priests read scripture. If the Meridian's cave had a throat, this was its second lung.

I memorized the bearing Ferro grumbled. I memorized the depth soundings he read aloud wrong and the bosun corrected. I filed it all away next to the soft seam aft of the engine room and the bosun's grievance ledger and the dead brother of the eager watchman, and I understood that I was building something. Not a plan, exactly. Plans need legs, and mine were currently decorative.

An arsenal. That's what I was building. A hostage's arsenal, made entirely out of things other people had said within earshot of a large quiet man.

Patience isn't doing nothing. Patience is loading a weapon very slowly.

* * *

Sixty miles north and one whole world away, my people were losing a war against mud.

I know this the way you know things about family — later, from the telling, stitched together from what Odile said and what Ines admitted and what Finch never stopped bragging about. But I give it to you now because I lived those hours in parallel, below decks on the enemy's steel, timing my breathing to a tide I couldn't see and trusting three exhausted people to outrun it.

They'd come out of the storm with the boat hurt and the stores soaked — half their fresh water brackish, most of the food either spoiled or sacrificed overboard to save the hull. Odile had brought them through the outer passage on nerve and memory, and then she'd done the arithmetic every pilot dreads: the channel to the Meridian would begin silting with the turn of the fifth tide, and they had lost so much time to weather that the margin was down to hours, not tides.

So they ran the shallows. At night. With a wounded engine.

Finch worked the machinery like a surgeon with dirty instruments and no anesthetic — patching a fuel line with waxed canvas and hose clamps, hand-feeding oil to a pump that wanted to seize, talking to the engine in the low constant voice other people reserve for frightened animals. Twice she went into the bilge up to her shoulders in cold water to clear the intake. She was sixteen and running on tea and fury, and the engine ran because she refused, personally and specifically, to permit it to stop.

Odile stood the helm for thirty-one straight hours. She'd been stripped of her ticket for a mistake that killed no one and ruined her anyway, and here in the dark, threading unmarked water by the feel of the swell against the keel, she was more pilot than she'd ever been with papers. She read the sea the way I read dough — touch, weight, the small lies in the surface. Where the water went glassy-smooth over a brown stain, she turned hard before the sandbar announced itself. Where the current kinked against the wind, she found the deep lane hiding underneath.

And Ines — Ines stood beside her with the tide tables gone soft from spray, dividing hours by fractions, feeding the numbers to Finch and Odile and pretending he wasn't terrified. He was. He told me later that terror and focus feel identical from the inside; the difference is only whether you let it steer. Down a man. Short on everything. Racing sludge. And still, when Odile faltered at the worst narrows, it was Ines who put his hand on the wheel beside hers and said, "You already made this crossing once tonight. Trust the woman who did it."

She made it. Of course she made it.

They anchored in the lee of the last marker rock with the fifth tide draining out beneath them and the channel behind them turning, minute by minute, into a brown ribbon of soup. Ahead lay the final approach — a stretch the charts called impassable at anything less than high water, which meant one attempt, at the sixth tide's peak, or never.

One shot. No reserves. No me.

Finch sat on the deck that night eating the last of the hardtack and said, quietly, without drama, "We get him back or we die trying, and either way I'm fine with it." Odile, cleaning her compass with a silk rag like it was a relic, said nothing at all, which from Odile was a psalm.

And Ines looked south toward the fleet lights on the horizon and made the decision that would break Voss's arithmetic in half: they wouldn't hide and wait for the sixth tide. They'd move early, into the silting channel, trusting Odile to find water where the charts said there was none — because the fleet would expect them to wait, and because waiting meant leaving me another twelve hours in Voss's hands, and because Ines Marchetti does not leave people in Voss's hands.

* * *

On the morning of the fifth tide, Quill came down to my hold with news he thought was a knife.

"We've found them," he said. "Or their wake has. Running the channel ahead of schedule. Desperate." He unfolded his hands, which was new. "Voss is pleased. A desperate man makes mistakes."

I nodded slowly, the way a big man nods, letting the silence do the work. Inside, something enormous and quiet was cheering. Running the channel early meant they weren't desperate. It meant they were coming for me first and the wreck second, and that inversion — that beautiful, reckless inversion — was the single most valuable piece of cargo anyone had ever loaded onto any ship I'd sailed.

"Nothing to say?" Quill asked.

"Just admiring the view," I said, and gestured at the bulkhead, the bucket, the cot.

He left. The lock turned. And I settled back against the steel, put my ear to the plating, and listened to the flagship's tired bones complain about the sea — counting her weaknesses like beads on a string, one soft seam, one bitter bosun, one grieving watchman, one forgotten passage bearing south-southeast — while somewhere behind me, through sixty miles of silt and storm-water, a boat full of castoffs came on.

Two tides left.

Hold on, I thought, at the steel, at the whole ship, at the world. I'm mapping us a way out.
