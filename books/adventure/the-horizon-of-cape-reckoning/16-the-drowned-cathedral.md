## 16. The Drowned Cathedral

The tide table said we had one left, and the sea said the same thing in its own handwriting — a long green trough rolling in from the southwest, the kind of swell that doesn't break so much as *consider* breaking. Odile stood at the wheel with her jaw set like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"One tide," she said. "Maybe less if the swell keeps building."

"Then we'd better be quick about being amazed," I said.

We'd been searching since dawn, running Odile's memory of Wren's map against the coastline the way you'd run a key along a row of locks. The map had shown a headland shaped like a broken tooth, and below it, a seam in the rock — an entrance that only existed at low water, when the tide pulled back far enough to bare the cave mouth like a secret told too loudly. Wren had drawn it with a drunkard's hand and a surveyor's eye, which is to say every line wobbled but every line was true.

Finch spotted it first. She was up in the bow with Bram's borrowed binoculars, and she went very still in that way teenagers do when they're trying not to look excited, which never works.

"There. In the cliff. There's a — it's dark, but it's *wrong* dark, like a doorway."

She was right. A seam of blackness maybe eight meters wide, low against the water, half-veiled by kelp and the kind of shadow that swallows sound. As we closed on it, the swell lifted us and dropped us, lifted and dropped, and each time we rose I caught a glimpse of what waited inside: a vaulted darkness going back further than any light I owned could follow.

Odile brought us alongside and held us there with the engine breathing in short, careful pulses. She looked at the entrance for a long moment.

"I've sailed this coast my whole life," she said quietly. "I've been past this headland two hundred times. There's no charted cave here."

"That's because nobody who found it came back to file the paperwork," Finch said.

"Or nobody found it at all," Bram said. "Wren did."

"Wren died in my doorway," I reminded them, and the cheerfulness drained out of the cockpit like bilge through a scupper. "Let's go see what he bought with his life."

* * *

Getting the boat inside was Odile's work, and I won't pretend I understood all of it. She timed the swell, counted something under her breath, and then committed us to the gap between one wave and the next with the calm of a woman threading a needle during an earthquake. The rock walls slid past close enough to touch. Then the swell released us and we were inside, and the engine noise fell away into a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse disagreeing with me.

I have chased storms across three oceans. I have stood on cliffs while lightning stitched the sky together over Cape Reckoning, and I have photographed the inside of a waterspout from forty meters away with hands that wouldn't stop shaking until Tuesday. None of it prepared me for the cave.

Our running lights caught the ceiling first — stone ribs arching up thirty meters, hung with pale rootless growth that glowed faintly where the light touched it, like the coals of a fire banked a century ago. The water beneath us was clear as glass and deep as a sin. And down there, visible in pieces as our lights swept across it, lay the Meridian.

She sat upright on the bottom, settled into the silt the way a tired old woman settles into a chair she's earned the right to sit in. Her masts had gone but her spars lay alongside her like fallen trees in a forest. Her hull was furred with decades of growth, white and grey and green, and where the light caught her rails and her deck structures, they gleamed — brass and bronze surviving under the slime, holding their shapes the way names hold their shapes on gravestones.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Even Finch, who has an opinion about everything including things she hasn't seen yet, said nothing.

"A cathedral," Bram said finally, and his voice came out hushed, which from a man built like a lighthouse is a strange and moving thing. "That's what it looks like. Like somebody drowned a church."

"The Meridian was a salvage ship herself, once," Odile said. "Before Calloway bought her. Before she went down with a hold full of what she'd pulled up." She shook her head slowly. "They say she was the finest hull on this coast. Look at her. Forty years down there and she's still proud."

"She's waiting," Finch said, and then looked embarrassed, as though she'd said something childish. But she hadn't. That's exactly what she looked like. Waiting.

I killed the engine and let us drift, and in the silence the cave made its own sounds — water ticking against stone somewhere back in the dark, the slow respiration of the tide breathing through passages we couldn't see. Somewhere in that drowned dark was a strongroom, and in the strongroom was whatever Aldous Wren had spent his last days crawling toward, and in whatever that was was Mara Wren's inheritance and my one chance at dying solvent.

"We dive now," I said. "While the tide's still out and the entrance is honest. When it turns, this whole place changes its mind."

* * *

I'll tell you the truth about diving in near-zero visibility: it is not an adventure. It is a negotiation conducted in the dark with your own fear, and your fear drives a hard bargain.

The water inside the cave was clear near the surface and turned to soup below ten meters, where the silt the storm had stirred hung suspended like the ghost of the seabed. We went down on a shot line tied to a rock spur near the Meridian's stern, Finch and I, because Bram was too broad for the narrow gaps in the wreck and Odile refused to leave the boat unattended with the fleet possibly behind us. Finch had insisted on coming. She'd also insisted on rigging a second line — a guide line, clipped off every few meters, so that even blind we could find our way home by hand.

"Rule one," she'd said on the surface, with the bossy authority of someone who'd clearly read every book in the harbor library's small diving section twice. "If you get separated, you stop. You don't search. You go up the line. Dead divers find nothing."

"You've been talking to Odile."

"I've been talking to everybody. It's called learning."

Below fifteen meters, the world became a brown void with suggestions in it. My torch beam bounced back at me off the suspended silt like light off fog, and I navigated by feel — the guide line in my left hand, the wreck's hull looming out of nothing on my right, barnacled steel cold and solid as truth. Once, a shape drifted past that might have been a fish or might have been my imagination filing a complaint. I chose not to investigate.

Finch's glove tapped mine twice: *stop*. Ahead, barely, the outline of a hatchway. The Meridian's aft cabin structure, and below it, according to the plans Odile had dredged out of a harbor archive at three in the morning with a smile and a forged signature, the strongroom.

Going through the hatch was like being swallowed. The silt inside the wreck was worse — decades of it, lying in soft drifts that exploded into total blindness at the smallest disturbance. We moved like men walking on eggs in a dark room, finning gently, touching nothing we didn't have to. The corridor ran forward into blackness. Doors gaped on either side, rooms that had been cabins once, and I tried not to think about the fact that people had lived here, eaten here, argued about cards here, right up until the moment the ship decided to become a grave.

Wren's map had marked the strongroom with a single X and, underneath it, in letters that had taken effort, the word *PROOF*.

Not *gold*. Not *salvage*. *Proof.*

At the time, feverish and dying, he'd probably just meant proof of the wreck's position — coordinates, documentation, the boring paper that makes treasure legally real. That's what I told myself as we felt our way down the companionway stairs, following the line, counting doorframes.

The strongroom door was steel, and it was open. Not broken — *open*, swung wide on oiled hinges after forty years, which meant someone had opened it recently enough that the mechanism still worked. Wren. He'd gotten this far. He'd stood in this room, and then he'd crawled back out and across the water and up the coast to die in the doorway of a stranger who owed everyone money.

My torch found the interior. Shelving lined the walls, and on the shelves sat the mundane archaeology of a shipping concern: ledgers swollen with water into shapes like loaves of bad bread, a signal lamp, a sextant case furred green. And in the center of the floor, a strongbox, its lid thrown back, empty except for—

Except for a canvas packet, weighted down with a shackle pin, sitting exactly where a dying man had placed it to be found. On top of it, protected in oilcloth, a letter. My name was on it. Well — not my name. It said *TO THE ONE WHO CAME*. Which, given the competition, meant me.

I tucked the packet into my dive bag without opening it. Some things you read in air, or not at all.

It was on the way out that I saw the rest.

The strongroom's shelving wasn't all rot and ruin. Along the lowest shelf, sealed in waxed canvas with a care that had cost somebody dearly, were more packets — flat, rectangular, dense. Documents. I pulled one free and worked it into my torchlight, and even through the fog of silt and forty years, I could read the letterhead.

*Calloway Consolidated Marine — Office of the Director — Private & Confidential.*

The pages inside were a voyage report. Dated forty-one years ago. The ink had faded to the color of old bruises but the words held, and I read them twice because the first time my mind refused to accept them.

It was an insurance scheme laid out in plain administrative prose. The Meridian's owners — Calloway — had insured her cargo twice over through two different underwriters, using two different manifests. The report discussed, in the tone men use for discussing grain prices, the most advantageous method of *realizing the asset*. There was a diagram. There was a list of crew, annotated in a margin with a note about severance costs. There was a signature at the bottom that I recognized, because that name was still stamped on every crate and contract in the port of Cape Reckoning.

Old Man Calloway himself. Ordering his own ship sunk. Ordering his own crew written off as a line item.

The Meridian hadn't been lost. She'd been *spent*.

I hung there in the black water inside a dead ship, breathing through a regulator, and felt the awe drain out of me like warmth out of wet cloth. A minute ago I'd been standing — floating — in a cathedral. Now I was standing in evidence. Every beautiful thing down here, the arches and the gleaming rails and the patient hull, was a crime scene. The men who'd done this hadn't just murdered a shipload of sailors. They'd built a company on top of the bodies, and the company was still standing, and it was coming here, and Voss was its errand boy, and I was holding the papers that could burn the whole edifice down.

Finch's glove found my arm. Two taps. *What's wrong?*

I couldn't explain underwater. I took one more packet, and another, stuffing them into the bag beside Wren's letter, and gave her the thumbs-up for ascent, and we rose together through the brown void along the line, hand over hand, out of the drowned church and into the grey-green light of the cave.

* * *

On the surface, I read the letter first, because Wren had earned that much.

It was short. His handwriting had deteriorated from the map's confident wobble into something scraped out by a failing hand, but every word was deliberate.

*If you are reading this, you kept your word, and I am sorry for what it costs you. The gold is real and it is hers — my Mara's — and no one else's. But the papers matter more than the gold, and I say that knowing what gold is worth, because I watched them sink that ship and I was too much of a coward to say so for forty years. I signed their manifests. I kept their secrets. I took their money. Everything I lost afterward, I earned. Give her both. Let her choose what to do with them. She's the only one of my blood with the spine for it. Don't tell her I said that. Tell her I thought about her every day, and that the map was the truest thing I ever drew.*

I read it twice. The second time my eyes weren't entirely reliable.

Finch hovered at my elbow, dripping, saying nothing, which for Finch was practically a eulogy. Finally she pointed at the Calloway letterhead poking out of my bag.

"What is it?"

"It's the reason Old Man Calloway's grandson is going to want us dead," I said. "More than he already does."

I told them. All of it, standing in the cockpit with the cave arching overhead and the Meridian waiting below us in her green dark. Odile listened with her arms folded. Bram listened with his fists slowly closing. Finch listened the way young people listen to news that rearranges the furniture of the world — completely, and with visible cost.

"So the fleet isn't coming for treasure," Odile said at last.

"The fleet is coming to make sure this stays buried," I said. "Voss probably doesn't know what's in these papers. He knows what they're worth to Calloway, which is more than gold. Which means—" I stopped, because the arithmetic finished itself, and I didn't like the answer.

"Means what?" Finch asked.

"Means they won't trade for it. They'll kill for it. And means that Bram—" My throat tightened around his name. "Bram isn't collateral anymore. He's a loose end."

Above us, through the cave mouth, the light had changed. The swell was building. The tide was turning.

And far out beyond the headland, small and patient as a shark's fin, a sail had appeared.
