## 17. The Fleet Arrives

**Ines**

The tide had begun to fall, and the sea was draining out of the cave like a confession being withdrawn.

I stood on the Meridian's tilted deck with a diver's lamp in one hand and a satchel of documents against my chest, and I listened to the water drop an inch, then another, and I understood that every inch was a door closing. The cave mouth that had swallowed us at high water was already showing its teeth — black rock fangs emerging one by one from the swell, narrowing the entrance to something a boat our size could thread only if the sea were feeling charitable.

The sea was not feeling charitable. The sea never had been, not once in my life, and I'd stopped expecting it around the time Voss bought my first loan.

"Ines." Finch's voice came down through the broken skylight above the strongroom, thin and echoey, the way voices get in drowned places. "You need to see this. Now."

I climbed. My hands knew the way by now; three days inside the wreck will teach your body a cathedral's geography better than any map. Aldous Wren's map had been good — better than good, it had been a love letter written in pencil and seawater — but even he hadn't drawn the half of it. The Meridian lay on her beam ends in forty feet of green dark, masts snapped, hull furred with a century of growth, and when our lights swept through her corridors it was like walking through the ribcage of something enormous and patient.

Finch crouched at the cave mouth, binoculars pressed to her eyes, her whole body gone still in the way she went still before saying something true.

"How many?" I asked.

"That's the thing," she said. "Count them yourself."

I took the glasses. Out past the fangs of rock, beyond the line where the grey water met the greyer sky, there were lights. Not one light. Six. Running lamps, spread across two miles of open water, converging with the unhurried confidence of ships that have never once had to hurry.

The Calloway fleet. All of it. Or enough of it that the difference wouldn't matter.

"They knew," Finch said quietly. "They've known since the second waypoint. Maybe since the harbor."

Since Voss. Since the harbormaster's greasy palm. Since the moment I'd refused Voss's offer at anchor and watched him smile like a man filing a receipt. I felt the old familiar arithmetic start up in my head — debt, interest, the price of everything — and then I made myself stop, because I'd spent six tides learning that some things don't fit in ledgers.

Below me, in the flooded hold, the strongroom waited with its gold and its paper. The gold that belonged to Mara Wren. The papers that proved Calloway had murdered forty-one sailors for an insurance payout and called it weather.

And somewhere out on those grey waters, tied up in a cabin on the flagship, was Bram.

"Odile," I called down the hatch. "How long can we sit here?"

Her voice floated up, calm as tide tables. "Until the water drops another four feet, and then nobody leaves or enters until the next high. Nine hours. Maybe ten."

Nine hours. The fleet would be here in two.

* * *

**Bram**

They had given up asking questions sometime during the second day, which suited me fine. Questions are just hooks, and I've spent my whole life being too big to land.

Instead they gave me a chair in the flagship's wardroom and a man named Pell who sat across from me doing nothing, which is harder work than it looks. Pell did it very well. He drank coffee. He read a paperback with a torn cover. Every hour or so he would look up and say something like, "Voss should be here within the day," and watch my face the way you watch a pot you're not sure is boiling.

My face wasn't boiling. My face was thinking about Odile's coffee, which is terrible, and about how Finch hums when she works on engines, badly, off-key, like the engine might improve by association. I thought about Ines standing in the rain outside Wren's door the night we buried him, not crying, just standing, like a fence post deciding whether to become a tree.

"You're a big man," Pell said, conversationally, around hour nine. "Big men are useful. Calloway pays well. Better than whatever Marchetti's paying you, which I'm guessing is nothing, because Marchetti has nothing."

"I sail with him because he asks nicely," I said.

Pell smiled. "And what happens when he asks you to do something stupid?"

"He hasn't yet."

"He will. Stupid men always do, eventually, and Marchetti is the stupidest kind — the kind that thinks he's clever." Pell turned a page. "When he does, you'll want to be on our side of the rail. Think about it."

I thought about it. I thought about it the way you think about a stone you've decided not to pick up.

What I didn't tell Pell — what I hadn't told anyone, because it seemed like showing off — was that I had spent two days learning this ship the way I learn everything: slowly, patiently, with my hands in my pockets and my eyes open. I knew the engine room ran two watches and the night watch was one man who slept at his post. I knew the aft cargo hatch lock was rusted and the forward one wasn't. I knew the ship's boats were davit-launched and the davits needed two men and four minutes. I knew where the flare locker was, and that it was unlocked, because Pell himself had fetched a cigarette lighter from near it and hadn't bothered to turn the key.

I was not planning anything. Planning implies urgency. I was simply keeping accounts. Someday, someone might ask me what I knew about the flagship *Cormorant*, and I intended to answer at length.

Near dawn — I could tell by the porthole going from black to bruise-colored — the engines changed their note. Slowing. And through the porthole I saw a shape rise out of the sea haze ahead: a headland I recognized from charts I'd studied over Odile's shoulder, and beyond it, low and dark, the mouth of a cave.

Pell stood up. For the first time in two days, he looked interested.

"Well," he said. "Right on schedule."

* * *

**Odile**

I have piloted ships through the Serrated Reach, through fog so thick you navigate by the taste of the air, through a war once, briefly, by accident. I have never seen anything quite as ugly as six sets of running lights closing on a cave mouth in formation.

"There's the *Cormorant*," Finch said beside me at the glass. She'd named each ship as it resolved, reading flags through the binoculars with the fierce competence of a girl who had taught herself everything she knew and resented every teacher she'd never had. "*Petrel*. Two tenders. That's the salvage barge — they brought a barge, Ines. They're not guessing. They came to strip her to the rivets."

"They came to take everything," I said. "That's different from stripping."

Ines stood between us at the rail of our little boat, tucked in the shadow of the cave's inner wall where the falling tide had left us perched on wet stone like gulls on a ledge. He hadn't spoken in a while. I've known him long enough now — eleven days, which on a small boat is roughly a decade — to know his silences. This one was the bad kind. This was the silence of a man holding two things that don't fit in the same hand.

Below us, in the green throat of the cave, the Meridian waited with her gold and her ghosts. Above us, on the water, the fleet arranged itself with the patience of a noose.

"Voss is signaling," Finch said. "Lamp. It's — hang on." Her lips moved as she read. Then she stopped moving them, and looked up at Ines with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the face of someone about to deliver news they'd rather set on fire.

"What does it say?" Ines asked.

"'Trade the strongroom for your big friend,'" Finch read aloud, flat. "'You have until the tide turns. After that, we come in and take both.' Signed with a little flourish. He drew a smiley face, Ines. The man drew a smiley face."

Of course he did. Voss was the sort of cheerful that should be classified as a weapon.

Ines looked down into the cave. I watched him do the thing he does — the thing I'd first seen him do on the cliffs, reading a squall line the way priests read scripture — except this time there was no sky to read. Just numbers. Gold on one side, a man on the other, and a promise in the middle that had never specified which currency it was drawn in.

"We can't fight six ships," I said, because someone had to say it and it's my job on this boat to say true things in a level voice. "We can't outrun them. We can't hide. The tide closes this place in five hours and opens it again tomorrow, and they'll still be here tomorrow."

"I know."

"And Bram—" I stopped. Started again. "Bram would tell you to take the gold to the girl. You know he would. He'd say it slowly, and kindly, and he'd mean every word."

"I know that too." Ines's jaw worked. "That's what makes it filthy. They picked him because they know exactly what he's worth to us. Voss isn't buying gold. He's pricing friendship."

Finch said, very quietly, "Then don't sell it."

We all looked at her. Nineteen years old, grease to the elbows, granddaughter of a dead man none of us had known alive, and she looked back at us with Wren's own stubborn chin.

"The promise wasn't 'deliver the treasure,'" she said. "I've read the letter a hundred times. It says *see that my daughter gets what's hers.* What's hers isn't the gold. The gold is just — it's the bait. What's hers is the truth about what Calloway did. Granddad told me once, before he left, before I understood — he said the Meridian wasn't lost, she was *spent*. Like a coin. And nobody spends a ship full of men unless somebody profits." She jabbed a finger toward the fleet. "Those papers are worth more than the strongroom. Those papers are worth *all of it* — the fleet, the company, Voss, everything. So give them the gold. Let them choke on it. Keep the truth."

The cave dripped. Somewhere below, the wreck settled a fraction deeper into her century of sleep.

Ines closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was smiling, and it was not a nice smile, and I found I liked him better for it.

"Finch," he said. "How well do you know the Meridian's boiler room?"

* * *

**Ines**

Here is what nobody tells you about promises: they are easy to make and impossible to interpret, like weather, like women, like loan agreements. Aldous Wren had made me swear an oath in a fever, blood on the floorboards, and I had sworn it the way you swear things at three in the morning — completely, instantly, and without reading the terms.

*The salvage goes to Mara. Not to any fleet. Not to any creditor.*

Standing at the cave mouth with six ships coming to collect, I finally understood that the oath had never been about gold at all. Wren had spent thirty years knowing what Calloway did and dying with it locked in his chest. The gold was his daughter's inheritance, yes. But the truth was his daughter's *name* — because as long as the Meridian was just a wreck, Mara Wren was just the daughter of a prospector with debts, one more creditor circling one more estate. With the truth, she was something else entirely.

Voss wanted the strongroom. Fine. Voss could have the strongroom, every bar of it, and I hoped it gave him indigestion for the rest of his short, cheerful life.

I took out Wren's letter — the real one, the one I'd carried against my ribs since the dawn he died — and read it once more by lamplight. *See that my daughter gets what's hers. All of it. Especially the part they'll kill to keep buried.*

Especially the part they'll kill to keep buried.

"All right," I said. "Here's how this goes. We give Voss his trade. Bram walks free. And while his people are loading gold bars and congratulating themselves—"

"—the boilers blow the secondary channel," Finch finished, eyes alight. "The one on the north wall. The one the fleet doesn't know about, because it's been sealed under rubble for sixty years, and because I've spent two days down there with a wrench and a prayer."

"And the tide keeps dropping," Odile said, catching on, her voice going soft and dangerous the way it did. "Which means when that channel opens, the big ships can't follow us through it. Only us."

"Only us," I agreed.

It was a mad plan. It was the kind of plan that gets everyone killed, and I loved it the way you love a storm on the horizon — from a distance that fools you into thinking it's beautiful.

Through the cave mouth, the lead tender began working its way toward the rocks, a lantern swinging at its bow, and I could imagine Voss aboard it, smiling his smiley-face smile, certain he had already won.

Let him be certain. Certainty is heavy. Men carrying it can't run.

"Finch," I said. "Get below. You have four hours."

She was already gone, hammer in hand, humming.
