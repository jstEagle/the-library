## 18. The Devil's Bargain

There is an old saying among storm chasers: the sky always gives you one last look before it takes everything. I'd spent fifteen years believing it was about weather. Standing at the mouth of the drowned cathedral with Voss's tender grinding against the rocks and the tide already falling past the third marker on Odile's slate, I finally understood it was about men.

Voss came up the cave's shingle beach like a man arriving at his own coronation. He'd dressed for it, too — a gray coat too fine for salt spray, boots that would never forgive him, and that smile he wore the way other men wore knives, openly and with pride.

"Marchetti." He spread his arms as if the cave were a gift he'd wrapped himself. "You found it. I knew you would. That's why I've always liked you — you're a dog with a bone, and dogs with bones are honest."

Behind him, two Calloway deckhands dragged Bram down the tender's gangway. They'd bound his wrists in front of him, which told me they'd been careless rather than clever; Bram could have folded either of them into a ballast locker without uncrossing his expression. But he let them steer him, docile as a churchgoer, and when his eyes found mine across the shingle there was no fear in them at all. There was something worse. There was apology.

"Let him go," I said.

"All in good time." Voss strolled forward, boots crunching, and paused to admire the arch of the cave ceiling, the black ribs of the Meridian showing through the green water where the tide had dropped enough to bare her forecastle rail. "Remarkable. Forty years she's sat here, and Calloway's own charts had her three miles north. You know what that tells me, Marchetti? It tells me your dead prospector was better at this than our entire survey office. There's a lesson in that about paying attention to old men."

"You have thirty seconds of my patience," I said. "The tide's going out. When it does, that entrance closes."

"Yes, so I'm told." He checked a pocket watch with theatrical leisure. "Which is precisely why we're having this conversation now, while you're motivated. Here's my offer, and I want you to appreciate its elegance. The gold in that strongroom — all of it — comes out on my tenders. In exchange, Bram walks free, your debt vanishes from my ledger, and I put in a word with the harbormaster about your impoundment. You sail away clean. No debt, no dead friend, no more looking over your shoulder. Freedom, Marchetti. On paper, notarized, genuine."

I looked at Bram. Bram looked back, and very slowly, deliberately, shook his head once. The big man had been hostage for less than a day and he was already trying to manage my negotiations.

"And if I refuse?"

Voss sighed, genuinely regretful, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Then the tide goes out, the entrance narrows to nothing, and we all sit here together until it comes back. Except Bram won't be sitting with us. He'll be on the flagship, and the flagship will be somewhere else entirely by then. You'll have traded your friend for a pile of metal that won't be worth anything until spring, assuming Calloway's lawyers don't drown Mara Wren's claims in filings before then. Which they will. They're very good at drowning things." He smiled. "Ask the *Meridian*."

That landed harder than he could have known. Or maybe he knew exactly. Men like Voss collect information the way I collected photographs of storms — obsessively, and for reasons they don't fully examine.

I thought about Aldous Wren dying on my floor, his hand light as gull-bone on my wrist, making me swear. I thought about how easily I'd sworn. I thought about the documents in the oilskin packet inside my coat, pressed flat against my chest like a second heartbeat — forty-year-old manifests, a signed order of scuttling, the confession of a bosun who'd been paid to open the sea cocks and had spent the rest of his short life drinking himself toward forgiveness. Proof that Calloway hadn't lost the *Meridian*. Calloway had murdered her, for the insurance, and let eleven sailors become a rumor.

The gold was treasure. The papers were justice. And the promise — I was only now understanding what Aldous Wren had actually asked of me — was both.

"Deal," I said.

Voss's smile widened. "There's the pragmatist I loaned money to."

"But the gold comes out under my supervision. My crew handles the lifting gear. Your men stay on the water."

"Paranoid."

"Cautious. There's a difference, though I understand how you'd confuse them."

* * *

Finch worked the Meridian's old davit winch like she'd been born to it, which in a way she had — born to machines nobody else wanted, born to coax life out of rust. She'd greased the cable with tallow from our stores and muttered endearments to the gears the whole time, and the winch groaned but obeyed. Odile timed the lifts against the swell, calling the rhythm, and I ran the strongroom sling while the Calloway tenders idled at the entrance like wolves pretending to be patient.

We brought up four chests. Each one hit the shingle with a sound like a struck bell, and each time, something in my chest rang along with it. This was the weight of a promise made physical. Gold is just metal until someone dies for it; then it becomes something else entirely.

Bram stood apart under guard, watching us work. At one point Odile passed close to him and I saw her lips move — three words, quick, almost invisible. I didn't catch them. Later I understood: she'd been telling him where the secondary channel was, and how narrow, and how the flagship drawing twenty feet couldn't follow them through it even if it wanted to. Odile the disgraced pilot, quietly doing the one thing nobody could take from her: knowing the water better than anyone alive.

By the third lift, the tide had fallen past the fifth marker. The entrance that had admitted the *Reckoning* at high water now showed black rock teeth on either side, and the gap between them had shrunk to perhaps forty feet. Enough for the tenders. Not enough — I watched Odile measure it with a pilot's eye and go carefully blank — for the *Providence*, the Calloway flagship, whose superstructure I could see standing off beyond the rocks like a hotel that had lost its way.

Voss counted the fourth chest's contents personally, biting nothing because gold doesn't need biting, weighing it instead in that small brass scale his clerk produced from nowhere. Satisfied, he gestured, and the deckhands cut Bram's bindings.

Bram rubbed his wrists, walked to me across the shingle, and stopped close enough that his voice reached only me.

"They asked me questions for six hours," he rumbled. "I told them stories. Long ones. About my childhood. Very boring." A pause. "One of their officers kept leaving the room when I mentioned the scuttling order. He came back gray."

"Good," I said.

"Ines." His hand settled on my shoulder, gentle as ever, heavy as ever. "Whatever you're planning — and you have the face of a man planning — Finch has been down in the engine spaces since dawn. I heard her tapping. She wasn't fixing anything."

"I know," I said.

He nodded slowly, and something that was almost peace crossed his battered face. "Then I'm glad I'm standing next to you and not him."

* * *

Voss loaded his gold with the efficiency of a man who had done many ugly things beautifully. The last chest went over the tender's gunwale as the tide passed the sixth marker, and the entrance was down to thirty feet, and the light coming through it had gone from white to the bruised amber of late afternoon.

"A pleasure doing business, Marchetti." Voss settled onto the tender's stern bench, gold stacked around him like a throne being built in real time. "You know, I meant what I said about the debt. Consider it closed. Consider this whole wretched episode closed. You got your friend, I got my salvage, everyone walks away richer—"

"The *Wren* family walks away poorer," I said. "But then, they've had practice."

His smile flickered — the first honest thing I'd seen on him all day — and then the tender backed water and turned for the entrance, and I turned away before I could watch greed sail safely out of reach.

Except it didn't.

I heard it before I understood it: a change in the water's voice at the cave mouth, the slap of swell becoming the grind of swell, and then Odile's voice cracking across the cavern like a whip.

"*Ines!* The tide — look at the tide!"

I looked. The sixth marker stone was underwater. The seventh was disappearing. And the entrance — the entrance was closing faster than any tide table predicted, because the trailing edge of the storm we'd outrun two days ago was pushing a long groundswell through the outer passage, and every swell that rolled in now piled the shingle higher and dragged the sandbar at the throat of the channel shallower. Nature, it turns out, keeps its own ledgers.

And the *Providence*, seeing her tenders through, did what flagships do. She moved to follow.

"She can't make it!" Odile was shouting, scrambling up onto the Meridian's exposed foredeck, pointing. "Twenty feet drawn! The bar's at sixteen and falling! Tell them — somebody tell them—"

Nobody told them. Or somebody tried, and the flagship's master was the kind of man who interpreted warnings as challenges. I watched the *Providence*'s lights swing toward the gap, watched her bow wave flatten as she entered the throat of the channel, watched her lift on the first swell and strike — a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, a sound like a cathedral bell falling downstairs — and settle, hard, onto the bar, wedged between the rock teeth as the water continued to fall around her hull.

Silence, of a kind. Then shouting, faint and furious, carrying across the water. Then Voss's voice, clear as a chapel bell, rising above all of it in tones of pure, incandescent disbelief.

The largest ship in the Calloway fleet was aground in the mouth of the cave, blocking the exit, trapping us all inside together — the debt collector and the debtor, the murderer and the witness, the gold and the proof of how it was gotten.

Finch appeared beside me on the foredeck, grease to her elbows, eyes shining with a light I recognized from photographs of storms.

"So," she said. "About those boilers."

* * *

The *Meridian* had gone down with her fires banked, and forty years of cold seawater had done what cold seawater does — eaten thin what was thin, left thick what was thick. Her boilers were iron monsters built in an era when they made everything to survive God's temper. Finch had spent two days among them, tapping, listening, mapping, and what she'd found was this: the aft boiler's shell was sound, but its firebox was dry, sealed, and packed with forty years of silt that would compress rather than vent. Charge it right, and it wouldn't explode so much as *insist* — a single, focused argument delivered through the weakest wall of the cave, which happened to be the shale seam behind the secondary channel, the one Odile had been staring at since we arrived with the expression of a woman reading a door she'd known about since girlhood.

"It'll work," Finch said, for the fourth time, spreading her chalk diagram on the deck planks where the lantern light could find it. "Probably. Sixty-forty."

"Those odds again," Odile said. "Your sixty-forty is other people's coin flip."

"My sixty-forty is *informed* coin flip." Finch jabbed the chalk at the diagram. "The charge goes here, in the firebox. We flood the primary channel side to direct the blast — water carries the push where we want it. The shale behind the secondary channel is already fractured; I can feel it humming when the swell hits. We're not breaking through solid rock, we're asking a cracked window to finish the job."

"And if the window declines?" Bram asked mildly.

"Then we're all trapped in a cave with a grounded enemy flagship until the next spring tide, which is nine days, and we've got food for four." Finch shrugged. "So. Sixty-forty."

I looked at the diagram. I looked at the black water where the *Meridian* lay cradled like a saint's bones. Somewhere below us, Aldous Wren's ship was about to be asked to do one last thing, and I found — this is the truest sentence I will ever write — that I was not afraid. I was angry. Anger is fear with somewhere to go, and I finally had somewhere to go.

"How long to rig it?"

"Two hours. Maybe three. I need the copper line off the Reckoning's still, and I need everybody's lamp oil." She hesitated, and for once the runaway mechanic looked her age — seventeen, scared, certain anyway. "Ines. If I'm wrong about the direction of the push..."

"Then you'll be wrong quickly," I said, "and none of us will have time to be disappointed."

She laughed — a short, startled bark — and went to work.

* * *

We rigged through the night watches while the *Providence* groaned on the bar outside and her boats pulled back and forth carrying orders nobody could execute. Twice, Calloway boarding parties tried the secondary channel in skiffs and Odile's knowledge of the currents turned them back — twice they came around the bend into water that looked passable and behaved otherwise, and twice we heard their curses echo off the shale. By midnight the whole fleet knew we were armed with something worse than guns: geography.

At the second hour past midnight, Finch called us down into the engine spaces one final time. The boiler loomed in the lamplight, patched and packed and wired, absurd and magnificent, a dead ship's heart taught to beat once more.

"One firing," Finch said. Her hands were steady. Her voice mostly was. "When I pull this, we run for the secondary channel mouth and we pray the water does the aiming. Everyone clear on where they run?"

We were clear. We took our places. And as I crouched at the tunnel's edge with the oilskin packet of documents flat against my chest, I thought of Aldous Wren, and of the oath I'd sworn too easily, and of how a promise is a small cheap thing right up until the moment you have to pay for it — and then it is the only currency that matters.

"Light her up," I said.

Finch pulled.

The *Meridian*'s boiler spoke — one syllable, vast, final — and the world became noise and water and the taste of shale dust, and somewhere ahead of us, in the dark, a forty-year-old wound in the rock began, at last, to open.

Through the roar, I heard Odile screaming the heading, and I ran toward the sound, and the chapter of my life that began with a stranger dying at my door came down to this: a crack of gray light ahead, widening, widening —

— and beyond it, the storm's trailing edge coming in like a debt called due.
