## 19. Six Tides Spent

**Ines**

I have chased storms for eleven years, and I will tell you the thing no documentary ever says: the trailing edge of a storm is worse than the leading edge. The front is drama. The back is a hangover — ragged wind, rain coming sideways out of a sky that can't decide what it's finished doing to you, and every wave convinced it has one last insult left in it.

Finch's boilers went off at 4:17 in the morning, and I know the time because Bram's watch was in my pocket, and I had been holding it like a rosary since we traded him away.

The sound was not an explosion so much as the sea being told a secret it had kept for forty years and objecting violently. The secondary channel — the crack in the cave's eastern wall that Finch had found on Wren's map, half-silted, half-hope — blew outward in a gout of steam, rock, and forty-year-old boiler water that scalded the air white. The *Meridian* shuddered beneath us where she lay in her drowned cathedral, her bones groaning like an old man asked to move furniture one more time.

"Channel's open!" Finch screamed over the roar, hanging off the rail of our boat, soot-blackened and grinning like sin. "Ines! It's open! It actually — I'm a genius, tell everyone I said that!"

"Get us through before the tide argues!" Odile already had the wheel. Of course she did. There is a version of Odile Vasseur that exists only in channels other pilots won't enter, and I have learned not to interrupt her when she surfaces.

We shot through the new gap as the storm's trailing edge arrived, as if the weather had been waiting politely for the fireworks to finish. Wind hit us broadside. Rain came down like the sky settling accounts. And behind us, in the widening dark, the Calloway fleet's largest ship — the *Providence*, Voss aboard, Bram somewhere in her hold — was discovering that the cave mouth was closing around her hull like a fist.

* * *

**Bram**

They put me in the chart room because it had a lockable door and I did not seem worth a cell. This was their first mistake. Their second mistake was leaving me alone with a chart table, a brass lamp, and four hours.

I am not a clever man. I want that understood. I am a large man who is slow to anger and slower to forget, and there is a difference between those things that smart people learn too late. What I did in that chart room was not cleverness. It was patience. I counted footsteps. I noted that the escort officers — six of them, young, tired, increasingly unhappy — took their orders from Voss's man Corrigan, but took their meals together, apart from the salvage crew, and spoke about the flagship's captain the way sailors speak about a captain they do not trust: carefully, and only after checking the door.

And I noticed, because a man who has been blacklisted from every crew in port learns to notice who else is unwanted, that the escort officers were not Calloway men. They were hired. Contracted. Men with families and pensions and a paper that said if the *Providence* sank in a cave chasing illegal salvage into waters Calloway had no license to work, the paper would not save them.

When the explosion came, the whole ship jumped like a kicked dog. Dishes broke somewhere below. A pipe burst two decks down and men started shouting about the bilge. And through the chart room door I heard Corrigan say, calm as a banker: "Whatever happens, the cargo comes first."

Cargo first. I have heard those three words before, once, on a night I do not talk about, when a company decided forty tons of ore were worth more than the eleven people loading it. I put my hand flat against the door and I made myself a promise in the same words Aldous Wren must have used, wherever he was: *not this time.*

Then the ship began to move backward, which ships are not supposed to do, and the shouting got much worse.

* * *

**Mara**

I had been sailing for nine hours on a skiff I chartered from a man who lied about its engine twice before admitting the truth, and I had spent most of those hours composing speeches I would never deliver. That is what lawyers do with fear. We file it.

The storm's trailing edge found me an hour past dawn, and I nearly turned back. Nearly. But I had spent three years losing courtrooms to Calloway Maritime, and I knew exactly what it felt like to turn back, and I could not afford another taste of it. So I ran the edge with both hands on the wheel and my father's letter open under a weight of brass on the chart table, reading it again at every lull, the way you touch a bruise to check it still hurts.

*"To my daughter Mara, who will be angry, and should be."*

He was right. I was furious with him — furious that he'd waited until he was dying, furious that he'd trusted strangers with what he never trusted me, furious most of all that the strangers had apparently kept his promise better than I'd kept faith with him. I had written him off years ago. He had known that, and loved me anyway, and spent his last coin on a map instead of medicine because he believed I would rather have justice than grief.

He was right about that too, and I hated him for it, and I sailed faster.

I saw the smoke first — a gray pillar rising off the cape where no smoke should be — and then the boom of it reached me across the water, late and flat. Then, through the rain, the impossible sight: a channel in the cliffs where charts said there was none, and a black ship wedged in it like a cork in a bottle, and smaller boats scattering from her sides like startled birds.

And cutting toward me out of the murk, riding seas no sane boat should ride, a patched-up chaser flying no colors at all, with a soot-stained girl at the rail waving both arms like a semaphore gone mad.

"That's Finch," I said to nobody. My father's granddaughter. I had done the arithmetic two nights ago and not slept since. "That's — oh, you absolute old fool."

I turned the skiff toward them.

* * *

**Finch**

Okay. So. The plan — and by "the plan" I mean the thing Ines said quietly while we were rigging the boilers, in the voice he uses when he's decided something and doesn't want a vote — was this: give Voss the gold, take Bram, keep the documents, let the tide and the cave do the rest. The documents were already stowed under my engine housing, wrapped in oilcloth, because Ines trusted me with the only thing on this ocean worth more than gold and never once made a speech about it. That's how you know you're family. Nobody makes speeches.

But plans are just prayers with logistics, and here's what actually happened.

We came out of the new channel into the teeth of the storm's backside, and the *Providence* was stuck fast behind us, wedged as the tide fell, and Voss's tender came out through the chaos anyway — because of course it did; that man would negotiate with a hurricane — and there was Bram on her deck, alive, soaked, enormous, looking like a lighthouse that had decided to go for a walk.

And then Mara's skiff came out of the rain.

I saw her before anyone. I'd memorized the face from the photograph in Wren's kit — younger, angrier, same jaw. She came alongside shouting over the wind, and she held up a leather case like it was a torch, and she screamed five words that changed the shape of the morning:

"I have the fleet's contracts!"

And here is the part I'll tell my grandchildren, if I ever get any and ever become the kind of person who deserves them: the escort officers heard her. All six boats' worth. Because Mara Wren didn't shout threats. She shouted *facts* — that the salvage was unlicensed, that the wreck site was inside protected waters, that every officer assisting was personally liable, that she had affidavits, that the courts were already moving, and that the men who surrendered the flagship now would be witnesses instead of defendants.

I watched a career happen in real time. One by one, the escort boats turned. Not toward us. Toward the *Providence*. Toward their own flagship, guns out, in the rain, because a lawyer on a rented skiff had reminded them whose side of the ledger they were really on.

Voss stood on his tender's deck between two armies of his own making, and I have never seen a man look so much like a man doing arithmetic that keeps coming out wrong.

* * *

**Ines**

Corrigan surrendered the *Providence* eleven minutes later. I know because Odile timed it, because Odile times everything, and because she wanted it entered into the record — her phrase — that mutiny is faster than salvage.

They brought Bram out through the falling tide and the thinning rain, and when our boats came alongside, he stepped across the gap like a man crossing a street, and then he picked me up. Both feet off the deck. I am not a small man and I left the water entirely.

"You came back," he said.

"You blew up a cathedral," I said. "We're even."

Mara Wren came aboard last. She looked at the oilcloth packet I handed her without a word — the documents, the proof that Calloway had scuttled the *Meridian* with her own crew aboard forty years ago, insured her twice, and called it weather. She held it the way you hold something hot and precious at once. Then she looked at me with her father's exact eyes and said:

"The letter said you'd swear an oath."

"I did."

"To a dying stranger. For nothing. For a debt that wasn't yours." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, just enough. "Why?"

And I thought about lying, because I'm good at it, but the whole crew was standing there listening — Odile pretending to study the horizon, Finch pretending not to, Bram not pretending at all — and lies die quickly in front of family.

"Because he asked," I said. "Nobody had asked me for anything honest in years. Everything else was interest rates. It was the first clean thing anyone had handed me since I came to this coast, and I wasn't going to drop it."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Behind her, the *Providence* sat trapped in stone, her escorts anchored around her like a verdict, and Voss's tender drifted empty at her rail because Voss, they said later, had gone over the side in a survival suit sometime during the mutiny and been fished out by his own mutinous officers, which is the sea's idea of a punchline.

"Well," Mara said finally, tucking the packet inside her coat. "The courts will want statements. All of you. Starting with the girl who blew up a national landmark."

"It wasn't national yet," Finch said. "It was just a cliff."

Behind us, the sun came through the storm's torn edge and lit the channel — Finch's channel, Wren's channel, the way through that hadn't existed yesterday — and the tide was turning, and for the first time in six tides, it was turning our way.

Odile cleared her throat. "Captain," she said. "Shall I set a course for home?"

I looked at the crew. My crew. Nobody's crew. Ours.

"Yes," I said. "Home."
