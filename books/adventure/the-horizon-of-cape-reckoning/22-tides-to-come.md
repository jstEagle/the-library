## 22. Tides to Come

**Ines**

The boat has a new name now.

I painted it myself, which was a mistake, because I am a man who can read a squall line from forty miles out but cannot paint a straight letter to save his life. The *A* leans like a drunk against the lamppost of the *l*. The *W* looks like it's trying to escape. Odile says it gives her character. Finch says it gives her something to laugh at when the engine dies, which is apparently often. Bram said nothing, because Bram never says anything about my flaws unless they're the kind that get people killed, and crooked letters don't qualify.

*Aldous Wren*, in white over the old name, which I sanded off but couldn't entirely kill. If you know where to look — and I know where to look — you can still see the ghost of *Voss's Folly* underneath. That had been the boat's name when Voss held her paper, back when she was less a boat than a collateralized asset with an engine problem. I like that the old name is still down there somewhere, drowned under the new one. It feels honest. Nothing on this coast is ever entirely erased. The sea keeps everything; we just stack new meanings on top and call it ours.

It's been four months since Cape Reckoning spat us back out, half-drowned and richer than we deserved, and the town still hasn't decided what to make of us. The harbormaster resigned before the scandal could reach him, which everyone agrees was the smartest navigational decision made all year. Calloway's offices stand dark behind papered windows. There's talk of a trial in the spring, though Mara says trials are weather too — you chart them, you provision for them, and then they do whatever they want anyway.

Me, I'm standing at the rail on a Tuesday morning with coffee going cold in my hand, watching Odile take us out past the light for no reason at all except that we can.

That's the part I'm still learning. For years, every mile of water had a price tag on it. Fuel cost money I didn't have. Time cost interest. Even looking at the horizon felt like spending something I'd have to pay back later. Now Odile puts the helm over and says "let's see what's past the Serrated Reach today," and nobody asks what it pays. We just go. It turns out that's what freedom is — not the absence of debt, exactly, but the absence of arithmetic.

Finch appears at my elbow, seventeen going on forty-five, hands stained the permanent color of engine grease that no soap on this coast will ever fully lift.

"Number three cylinder's running hot again," she reports. "I've got it. Mostly. Don't ask what 'mostly' means."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking it loudly."

She's right. I was. Four months ago I accused this kid of selling us to the fleet, and she stood there in the rain and told me the truth — that Aldous Wren was her grandfather by a daughter he'd lost track of, that she'd recognized his handwriting on that map because she'd grown up copying it in the margins of her schoolbooks. She forgave me faster than I forgave myself. Kids do that. They haven't learned yet that forgiveness is supposed to be expensive.

"Hey," she says, quieter now, watching the water instead of me. "You ever hear from the lawyers? About the gold?"

"Last check cleared last week." I keep my voice light. "Debts are dead. Every one."

"And you're just — okay with that? You gave up a fortune. You gave up basically all of it."

I think about how to answer that, and decide the truth is the only thing worth giving her. "I did the math once," I say. "Back when I owed Voss. I figured out exactly how much money would make me safe. And then I got the money, or enough of it, and I discovered the number was wrong. Safety isn't a figure, Finch. It's a crew."

She looks at me like I've said something embarrassing, which is fair, and then she punches my arm hard enough to leave a mark, which is also fair, and goes below to argue with her cylinder.

Bram is at the bow rail where Bram always is, a mountain in a fisherman's sweater, watching the cape slide by. He spent eleven days on the Calloway flagship as a hostage and came off it having memorized, as far as anyone can tell, the location of every valve, seam, and structural weakness aboard her. When the escort officers turned on their own captain in that collapsing cave — turned because Mara walked into the storm with documents proving Calloway had murdered a crew of thirty-eight men decades ago and called it weather — it was Bram who kept Voss's people from doing anything stupid. Not with force. With presence. Some men are arguments. Bram is a verdict.

He catches me looking and lifts two fingers off the rail. That's Bram for *good morning, the world persists*. I lift mine back.

Behind us, the fog is burning off the cliffs of Cape Reckoning, and ahead of us there's nothing but open water and a sky the color of a promise kept. Somewhere under that water lies the Meridian, or what's left of her, resting in her drowned cathedral with her strongroom empty and her secrets delivered. Sometimes I dream about her. In the dreams she's not a wreck. She's a door, and I'm always walking through it into a room full of light, and Aldous Wren is sitting there waiting to hear whether I managed it.

I always tell him: barely. But yes.

* * *

**Odile**

People ask me — well, one person asked me, a journalist from the coastal paper who came sniffing around after the trial — whether piloting the *Aldous Wren* feels different from piloting other boats. As if a hull is a hull and water is water and the name on the stern changes the physics.

I told her: every boat I ever steered before this one, I was steering away from something. My license, my reputation, my own stupid pride — I ran the Serrated Reach drunk on confidence at twenty-nine and put a ferry on the rocks, and after that every helm I touched was an apology. You can sail an apology for years. I did. It handles poorly.

This boat doesn't apologize. This boat goes where we point her because we want to see what's there.

Take this morning. Ines wanted the outside passage around the Reach, the deep-water route, no reason except curiosity and good light. Ten years ago I'd have refused — outside passage in autumn, with the barometer doing that slow nervous dance it does before the season turns? Reckless. But I've learned something about recklessness since I met these people: there's the kind that comes from not caring, and the kind that comes from caring so much you'll spend anything to see a thing through. Ines is the second kind. I checked, during the storm, during the cave, during all of it. I checked the way you check a mooring knot — hard, twice, expecting failure.

The knot holds.

So I took us out the outside passage, and the Reach opened up around us like it always does, those black teeth of rock rising out of green water, and I threaded them from memory the way my father taught me before he taught me anything about shame. Finch was below singing to the engine — she sings to it, actual songs, terrible ones, and the wretched machine runs better for it, which I refuse to examine scientifically. Bram was forward. Ines was at the rail reading the sky.

And I stood at the wheel of a boat with my name nowhere on her papers except as pilot, and I thought: this is the first vessel I've ever loved.

The journalist wouldn't have understood that. She wanted drama — betrayal, treasure, the explosion, all of it. She wrote her article and got most of it right and called us "the crew nobody wanted," which Ines found hilarious and had framed. But she didn't write the true ending, because the true ending isn't an event. The true ending is a Tuesday morning in October, and a woman at a wheel who used to be afraid of her own hands, steering toward the horizon because it's there, and the horizon, for once, asking nothing back.

We cleared the cape at noon. The wind freshened from the southwest, clean and cold, the first breath of the season's change. I trimmed our course and felt the boat answer, quick and willing, and thought: Aldous Wren, whoever you were, wherever you are — your granddaughter sings to your engine, your name rides straighter than any letter Ines could paint, and your promise is four months old and holding.

Not bad for a dying man's last bet.

* * *

**Mara**

I grew up believing my father abandoned us. That was the story my mother told, and grief makes its stories load-bearing — pull one out and the whole house comes down, so you leave it standing even after you stop believing it.

Then a stranger's crew crossed a hundred miles of hostile water to hand me a letter written in a fever, and the house came down anyway, and what I found underneath was different. Not better. Just different. He hadn't left. He'd been digging himself out of a hole his whole life, trying to come home with something worth the coming home, and the hole swallowed him two hundred yards short. The salvage was his way back. The promise was his apology. And a debt-chaser named Ines Marchetti — a man with every reason to sell that promise and none to keep it — carried it the last two hundred yards for him.

I'm a lawyer. I don't believe in things easily; belief is a liability in my profession. But I believe in that.

Which is why I'm aboard.

The firm thinks I'm on holiday. The firm can think what it likes; half my caseload dissolved when the Calloway documents went public, and the other half is the class action, and the class action is being handled competently by people I trained, and if I sit in that office one more week I will start throwing furniture. So I chartered nothing this time. I bought a berth. There's a difference — chartering is a transaction, and buying a berth is a decision, and I have decided that the sea and I are going to be seeing more of each other.

Ines met the news with the expression of a man who has learned not to argue with lawyers on their own deck. "Partner," he said carefully, "implies shares."

"Shares imply profits. I've seen your books. I'm investing in the principle of the thing."

"The principle doesn't pay dividends."

"Neither did my father, and I waited thirty years on him." I let that sit. "Besides, someone aboard should be able to read a contract. Your mechanic signed on with a handshake. Your pilot's employment terms consist of the word 'obviously.' You need counsel, Ines. You especially need counsel, because I've seen what happens when you find things."

He laughed at that — really laughed, head back, the laugh of a man who nearly drowned and decided to spend the surplus. Then he showed me the books, such as they were, and I rolled up my sleeves, and that's how I spent the afternoon: converting a storm chaser's chaos into columns. Somewhere between the fuel ledger and the dive-gear depreciation, the sun went low and gold across the water, and Finch came up to announce dinner ("it's stew, it's always stew, Bram makes it, don't ask what's in it"), and I sat down at a scarred table with four people who were strangers to me in spring, and I was — 

Happy. There. I said it. Nobody witnessed it but the stew.

My mother used to say hope was a tide — it goes out, but it always comes back, and the trick is building your house above the high-water line. I built mine low, deliberately, close to the water, because I wanted to be able to reach it. Tonight the tide is in. The windows are wet. I'm not moving the house.

**Bram**

Good stew tonight.

* * *

**Ines**

Bram's contribution to this account is accurate and complete, as usual. The stew was good. It is always good. Bram cooks the way he does everything else — patiently, silently, with results that require no explanation.

But I started this account, so I'll finish it, and I'll finish it with the letter, because the letter arrived three days ago and I've been carrying it around like a stone in my pocket, waiting for the right moment, which I now understand is a coward's phrase. There is no right moment. There is only the moment you're in.

It came to the harbor office, forwarded twice, addressed in a spidery hand to "the master of the vessel formerly known as Voss's Folly." Inside, a single sheet, and inside that, a photograph gone soft with age.

The letter reads:

*Master Marchetti —*

*I write to you because you are the only man alive who knows what it is to carry another man's promise across open water, and because the men I would trust with this are all dead or worse.*

*In 1961 I surveyed a wreck off the Skerries, north of here, that I could not identify and could not afford to work. She sits in sixty feet under a kelp shelf, intact, and her lines are wrong for any merchantman I know. Last month a diver brought up from near her a sealed case bearing the crest of a shipping house that officially lost no vessel in these waters in that decade. Officially.*

*I am eighty-one years old. I cannot dive. I cannot sail. I can pay for the fuel and the air and the lawyer — I am told you know a good one.*

*I am not asking you to bring me what she carries. I am asking what you asked for once, from a dying stranger on your doorstep: deliver it to the ones it belongs to. Her people's families, if any live. The truth, if truth wants it.*

*Come look. Bring your crew. Bring your courage. The window opens with the spring tides.*

*Yours in the trade of promises,*
*E. Vasquez, harbormaster, retired, Port Ellery*

The photograph shows a sonar sweep: a long dark shape under a pale shelf of kelp, lying straight and patient as a sleeper who hasn't decided to wake.

I've read it maybe forty times. I've shown it to no one, which Odile noticed within a day, because Odile notices everything, and this morning she simply said, "Whenever you're ready," and went back to her wheel, and that was the whole conversation.

Here's what I know. Four months ago a dying man fell against my door and I made a promise before I understood its weight, and it cost me nearly everything — my boat, my blood, my faith in every harbor I'd ever trusted — and it gave me back more than it took, and I would swear the same oath again tomorrow without hesitating, and that fact frightens me more than any storm I've ever chased.

Here's what else I know. The spring tides begin in nine days. Port Ellery is two days' sail north. The barometer is falling the way it does when the season turns, and Finch will sing to the engine, and Odile will thread whatever rocks stand in our way, and Bram will cook, and Mara will read the contracts, and I will stand at the rail and read the sky.

I fold the letter and put it back in my pocket, next to my heart, where promises live.

"Odile," I call forward. "How do you feel about the Skerries?"

She doesn't turn around. "Obviously," she says.

And the *Aldous Wren* — crooked letters, hot third cylinder, ghost of an old name still drowning beneath the new — swings her bow north, into the wind, toward the next wreck and the next promise, and the horizon opens like a door.
