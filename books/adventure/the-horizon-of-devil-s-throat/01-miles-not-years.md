## 1. Miles, Not Years

The thief had made three mistakes by noon, and Ansel Okafor had counted every one of them the way other men counted money.

The first mistake was stealing from the dry goods store in Perdition Flats at dawn, when the whole town was awake and watching from behind curtains. The second was taking a pair of boots two sizes too large, which meant he clowned along like a heron on ice, and a man who clowns leaves a signature in the sand as legible as a signature in ink. The third mistake — and this was the one that made Ansel almost fond of him — was running west across the flats instead of north into the breaks, where a boy with new stolen boots might actually have disappeared.

West was open country. West was where Ansel lived.

He sat his mule, Bess, at the top of a low rise and let the animal breathe while he read the ground below. The flats spread out pale and shimmering to the horizon, cracked into a mosaic of clay plates that clicked softly against each other when the wind moved, like a thousand small conversations he was never invited into. Twenty years he'd been reading this language. Twenty years, and he could tell you the age of a track to the hour, the weight of the man who made it to the pound, and whether he'd been carrying worry or only water.

The boy's tracks were fresh, maybe three hours old, and they told a whole sad story. Long panicked strides at first, then shorter ones, then a stretch where he'd stopped and turned in a circle — Ansel could see the pivot scuffs — trying to decide whether to go back. He hadn't gone back. He'd kept going, and the tracks got shorter still, and somewhere around the saltbush line they'd developed the drag of a boy who had realized, with the particular horror of the young, that the desert does not care what you stole or why.

Ansel touched his heels to Bess and started down.

He had learned long ago not to think of the distance ahead as distance. That was the trick of it, the only trick worth knowing in this country. A man who thought *forty miles* would lie awake at night doing arithmetic against his own courage. But a man who thought *that ridge, and then that dry wash, and then the place where the two buttes stand like a married couple who've stopped speaking* — that man could cross the whole territory one small landmark at a time and never once feel the weight of the whole.

Ansel had gotten so good at this that somewhere along the way he'd stopped measuring anything else. He didn't think of his life in years anymore. Years were slippery, and they piled up on a man without his permission. But miles — miles were honest. He knew he was forty-one years old the way you know a thing you've been told, but he knew he was eleven thousand miles out of Perdition Flats and four hundred miles past his last real conversation, and those numbers felt truer. When people asked how long he'd been a ranger, he said *long enough to have worn out four hats*, which was both true and a measurement of distance, if you counted the miles those hats had seen, which he did.

The sun climbed. Bess plodded with the patient indignation of her kind, ears swiveling back every so often to inform him that she was aware the pace was insulting and she wished it noted in the record. The flats gave way to hardpan, the hardpan to a stretch of greasewood, and the boy's tracks led on, getting more honest by the mile. By early afternoon they were just walking tracks. A tired boy, walking.

Ansel found him at a dry waterhole in the shadow of a rock overhang, sitting with his back against the stone and the enormous boots beside him, his own feet wrapped in rags. He was maybe sixteen, all elbows and dust, and he was crying in the way boys cry — silently, furiously, as though the tears were happening to someone else and he resented them for it.

"Waterhole's dry," Ansel said.

The boy came up off the ground like a startled bird, one hand scrabbling for a rock. He found one. He held it up in a grip that would have been more convincing if his arm hadn't been shaking.

"Easy," Ansel said. He swung down off Bess and stood there, hands loose at his sides, letting the boy look at him. He knew what the boy saw: a broad man in a sun-bleached coat, a badge worn dull with age, a face weathered into something between forty and geology. Not frightening, exactly. Just inevitable. He'd been told once by a woman in Cinnabar that he had the look of a man the desert sends when it wants something back. He'd thought about that for a long time afterward.

"I didn't steal nothing," the boy said.

"You stole a pair of boots."

"I *borrowed* — " The boy stopped. Even he could hear how it sounded. His shoulders came down. "They were for my brother. He's got feet like dinner plates and his old ones split clean through, and Mr. Harrow at the store wouldn't give credit till harvest, and harvest's — " He stopped again, jaw working. "You going to take me in?"

Ansel looked at him for a long moment. The rock, the rags, the boots sitting there between them like evidence in a trial nobody wanted to hold. Behind the boy's eyes there was a calculation Ansel recognized, because he'd seen it a hundred times and worn it himself once: the arithmetic of a person deciding how much trouble they could survive.

"What's your name?"

"Tomas."

"Tomas. You know what's out here, past this waterhole?"

The boy glanced west. "Nothing."

"That's right. Nothing. Fifty miles of nothing, and then the Throat, and past the Throat there's country that eats people who know what they're doing. You were headed into it in boots two sizes too big." Ansel crouched, slow, and picked up one of the stolen boots. It was good leather. Harrow stocked good leather. "Your brother's feet are bigger than mine," he said, and something in his own voice surprised him — a dry note of humor he'd half forgotten he owned.

"He's a big fellow," Tomas admitted.

Ansel sat down on a rock, which was not what rangers did when apprehending thieves, and the boy's confusion was almost comical. "Tell me about the harvest."

So the boy told him. The story came out in pieces, the way these stories always did — the wheat blight two seasons back, the father's lungs gone bad from the dust, the mother selling eggs to a town that had stopped having money to buy them. None of it was remarkable. That was the thing Ansel had learned in twenty years of riding the flats: every thief had a story and every story was ordinary, and the desert was full of ordinary people pushed one season past what ordinary could hold.

He should take the boy in. That was the job. The badge on his chest said so, in the language of a government three hundred miles east that had never once, in Ansel's experience, been within fifty miles of a problem it was solving. Harrow would press charges because Harrow was owed, and the magistrate at Perdition Flats would fine the family money they didn't have, and the boy would work it off, and the whole machinery would grind along, fair as a millstone and about as gentle.

Ansel looked at the badge. It was a small thing, tin and enamel, and he'd polished it so many times over the years that the face of it had gone soft and vague, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. He sometimes thought it had worn down to the shape of what it actually meant, which was less than anyone claimed.

"Here's what's going to happen," he said. "You're going to walk back to Perdition Flats. It's twenty-two miles. You'll make it by midnight if you don't dawdle, and you'll sleep in the shade tomorrow because you'll be too tired to be useful anyway. Tomorrow evening you'll go to Harrow and you'll tell him you've got work lined up with the fence crew at the Bar T, and you'll ask him to hold the boots against your first wages. Harrow's a hard man but he's not a fool, and a boy who walks into his store and offers terms is a boy he'll deal with."

Tomas stared at him. "And the — you're not arresting me?"

"I'm going to sit here until you're out of sight," Ansel said, "because if I watch you walk away I'll have to decide whether I'm a ranger or a liar, and I'd rather not decide anything today. Go on."

The boy gathered his rags and his boots and stood there a moment longer, working his mouth around something. What he finally said was, "Thank you, sir," in a voice that cracked down the middle, and then he was gone, quick across the hardpan, and Ansel sat on his rock and watched the distance open between them and told himself it was twenty-two miles, just twenty-two miles, one landmark at a time, and refused to examine what else it was.

He'd let them go before. Not often — maybe once every few years, when the story was right and the theft was hunger and the law felt like a boot on the wrong neck. But each time, it had gotten a little harder to put the badge back on in the morning. Each time, the arithmetic of the job came out a little worse. Twenty years of catching people and handing them to a system, and Ansel could no longer say with a straight face that the system caught the right people. It caught the slow ones. The desperate ones. The ones with feet like dinner plates and no credit at the store.

The ones it never caught wore better boots.

He watered Bess from his canteen, sharing it the way you do with an animal who has opinions about you, and ate a little bread, and watched the sun lean west. The flats went gold, then rose, then gray. Out there past the horizon was the Devil's Throat, and he thought about it the way you think about a tooth that aches only sometimes — with a small, superstitious wish not to think about it at all. Bad country. Country that ate people who knew what they were doing.

He was two days from Perdition Flats, and Perdition Flats was where the wire office was, and the wire office was where the world found him when the world needed something found. He'd told himself for years that he'd quit when the arithmetic stopped coming out even. He was starting to suspect it had stopped years ago and he simply hadn't checked.

Bess sighed. Ansel swung up into the saddle, and they turned east, toward the lights of a town that owed him nothing and paid him anyway.

They were an hour out of the waterhole, riding through the first cool of evening, when he saw the rider coming — a government man by the cut of his coat, raising dust at a pace that said the news was either very good or very bad. Ansel reined in and waited, and the man drew up, breathing hard, and looked at him with the particular expression of someone carrying a problem too heavy to set down.

"Ranger Okafor? Thank God. A survey team's gone missing out past the Throat. Six people." He swallowed. "The government would like to hire you to find them."

Ansel looked past the man's shoulder, west, at the darkening flats and the country beyond, and felt the ache in that tooth again.

* * *

The wire office in Perdition Flats smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and the liaison — a neat, damp man named Voss who kept wiping his palms on his trousers as though the desert itself were a form of misconduct — spread a map across the counter with hands that would not quite stay still.

"Last transmission was eleven days ago," Voss said. "Routine position report. Then nothing."

"Eleven days." Ansel studied the map, the little penciled X out past the Throat where the survey camp was marked. "You waited eleven days?"

"The department required — there are procedures — "

"There are," Ansel said, "and out there, procedures are what's left after the buzzards finish." He tapped the X once. "I'll need their supply manifest, their route plan, and the names of every man on that team. And I'll need it tonight."

Voss blinked. "You'll take the contract, then?"

Ansel looked at the map a moment longer. Eleven days, into that country, with a government that moved like a glacier and worried like a guilty man. He thought about the boy walking home in the dark, twenty-two miles, one landmark at a time. He thought about the arithmetic.

"I'll take it," he said. "But I ride at first light."
