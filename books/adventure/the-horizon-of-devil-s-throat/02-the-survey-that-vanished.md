## 2. The Survey That Vanished

Ansel Okafor had a rule about government paperwork: the thicker the file, the thinner the truth. The dossier Voss delivered at midnight ran to forty pages, most of which were forms confirming that other forms existed.

He read it by lamplight anyway, all of it, twice, because out there the paper was all he'd have.

Five men and one woman. A geological survey team, contracted by the Ministry of Interior Resources to chart the eastern approaches of the Devil's Throat badlands — elevation profiles, water tables, mineral strata. Standard work, or so the file insisted. They'd gone in on the ninth day of the month with two mules, a theodolite, and eleven days' provisions. Their last scheduled signal back to the relay station had come and gone eight days ago. No distress call. No wreckage. Nothing.

The liaison's cover letter used the phrase *unexplained delay* three times. Ansel had been a ranger for twenty years, and he'd learned that governments said *delay* the way guilty men said *I was home all evening*.

* * *

The country changed character forty miles past the last water hole. The flats gave way to broken ground — shale ridges like the backs of sleeping animals, dry washes braiding together and coming apart again, everything the color of old rust and old bone. Ansel rode slowly through it, not because his mule needed the pace but because this kind of country rewarded patience the way a river rewarded a fisherman: you got out exactly what you put in.

On the third day he found their trail.

It wasn't hard. Survey teams traveled like parades. He picked up the mule tracks in a wash and followed them northeast for half a day, reading them the way other men read letters — here they'd stopped to take a bearing, there they'd camped one night, the fire ring built neat and small, the way careful people build fires. One of the mules had thrown a shoe on the right hind; he noted it without thinking about it, the way he noted everything, filing it away in the great ledger he kept behind his eyes.

Toward late afternoon, the trail bent toward a low mesa with a sheltered flat at its base, and Ansel knew before he crested the last rise that he'd found them.

That was what bothered him. He shouldn't have known. There should have been buzzards, or smoke, or scattered gear, or some sign a man could point to and say *there*. Instead there was just a certainty settling over him like dusk, and when he came down onto the flat, he understood why.

Their camp stood intact.

Not wrecked. Not abandoned in haste. *Intact*, the way a room is intact when its owner has stepped out to fetch water and will be back any minute.

Two tents pitched square, guy lines taut, stakes driven properly. The mules stood hobbled near a seep, both alive, both placid, both shod — including the right hind that had thrown a shoe, meaning someone had reset it, meaning somebody had been here doing farrier work after the shoe was lost, and had done it well. Cooking gear stacked and cleaned. Instruments packed in their cases and lashed under a canvas fly against the sun. The theodolite sat on its tripod, covered, aimed at nothing in particular.

Ansel sat his mule at the edge of camp for a long moment and let his eyes do the walking.

Six bedrolls. Five accounted for by the file's roster, plus the junior cartographer, Reyes, whose name appeared only in an appendix — a woman, twenty-six, hired three weeks before departure. Six bedrolls, six mess kits, six sets of personal effects arranged with a tidiness that made his neck prickle. Boots lined up outside one tent. A jacket folded over a rock. Someone's pipe resting on a mess tin, bowl up, waiting to be filled.

Nobody walked away from a pipe mid-smoke. Nobody walked away from a camp this orderly unless they meant to come back in five minutes, and nobody stayed away five minutes from a camp with their boots still in it.

He dismounted and walked the site the way he'd once walked crime scenes in another life, heel to toe, hands loose, mind open. He gave himself an hour and found, in that hour, exactly nothing — no blood, no shell casings, no drag marks, no scuff of struggle. Even the fire pit held ash that had been raked smooth.

That was the thing about nothing. Out here, nothing didn't happen. Nothing had to be made.

* * *

He ate cold beans sitting on a supply crate, watching the light go amber and then copper across the mesa, and let the camp talk to him.

Governments thought in straight lines. If you wanted a team to disappear, the lazy way was violence: burn it, bury it, scatter it. But burning left ash that told stories, and burial left ground that didn't sit right, and scattering meant pieces might blow into the path of some passing prospector with a good eye. The tidy way was different. The tidy way was to make the disappearance look like an accident of distance — as though the desert itself had swallowed them, as though they'd simply wandered off into the badlands and been taken by thirst or heat or the Throat's old hunger. A tragedy. Regrettable. Unknowable.

But whoever had done the tidying had been interrupted, or careless, or both.

Because the camp was too perfect, and the desert was not perfect. Wind should have drifted sand against the windward tent walls. It hadn't. Dust should have dulled the instrument cases. It hadn't, not much. The whole scene had the freshness of something staged recently — within days, maybe less — and yet the last known position of the team put them here nearly three weeks ago.

Somebody had come back. Somebody had spent hours making sure this place looked exactly like a mystery instead of a message.

Ansel turned his cup in his fingers. Twenty years he'd tracked smugglers and claim-jumpers and men running from debts and wives alike, and every one of them had left a story in the ground, because every man does. You couldn't move through the world without telling it where you'd been. This camp told a story too. It was just that the story was a lie, and lies, unlike tracks, don't wear down with weather. Lies stay crisp forever until somebody reads them closely enough to see the ink.

He banked a small fire after dark and went over the camp again with a lantern, slow, methodical, on his knees in places, and it paid off the way patience always pays off, small and then suddenly large.

Under the lip of the supply crate he'd eaten from, wedged where a casual searcher wouldn't look but a nervous one would, he found a scrap of survey film — a strip of photographic negative, maybe four inches long, cut raggedly with a knife. Someone had hidden it in a hurry and hidden it well, tucked into a gap in the crate's joinery that even he had missed on his first pass.

He held it up to the lantern light.

Most of the frame showed what you'd expect: canyon walls, a datum marker, the pale ribbon of a dry riverbed. Survey work. But along the lower edge, where the frame caught a stretch of cliff face, there was a dark shape — angular, huge, half-swallowed by shadow and rubble, with a curve to it that no geology makes. A curve like a hull.

And burned into the emulsion's margin in tiny, precise lettering, in a surveyor's hand: coordinates. Not coordinates of anything on the Ministry's filed route plan. Coordinates deep inside the Throat proper — the section every map in the territory marked with the same two words: *impassable* *seasonally.*

Ansel lowered the strip and looked out at the darkness where the badlands went down toward the Throat, and felt the unease in his gut finish its slow curdle into dread.

A survey team doesn't hide film under a supply crate. Survey teams photograph rocks. Whatever that shape in the negative was, someone on this team had seen it, recorded it, and understood — clearly enough to cut the evidence free and cache it — that recording it was the reason none of them would be going home.

He thought about Voss's cover letter. *Unexplained delay.* He thought about how fast the Ministry had hired him, faster than any glacier he'd ever met, and how worried Voss had seemed underneath the procedure-speak, and how the worry had looked less like grief and more like accounting — a man checking whether a ledger balanced.

They hadn't sent him to find these people. He was almost sure of it now. They'd sent him to find out how much was knowable.

Ansel fed the scrap of film into an oilcloth pouch and then into the inside pocket of his coat, next to his heart, where he kept the things he wasn't ready to share with his employers. Then he doused the fire, checked his mule, and stood a while in the cold starlight looking south.

Somewhere out there were six people who had vanished mid-stride, leaving their boots behind. Somewhere out there was whatever waited at those coordinates, wearing a curve like a hull. And somewhere between here and there was whoever had combed this camp clean enough to fool a government, though not, thank God, fool a ranger.

In the morning he cast wide around the camp's perimeter, working outward in ever-larger circles, and it was on the third circle, sixty yards out among the shale ribs, that he found it.

One boot print. A man's, size eleven, pressed deep — someone moving fast, or heavy, or both — heading south toward the Throat. And then, ten paces later, in the middle of a full stride, it simply ended. Not fading. Not veering. Ended, mid-step, as if the man who made it had been lifted off the earth, or as if someone walking behind him had carefully swept the sand with a branch and missed one print, and gotten lazy.

Ansel crouched over it for a long time.

Engineered, he thought. All of it engineered. And somewhere out ahead of him, at the end of a trail that had been erased by professionals, was either a grave or a witness.

He rose, turned his mule south, and rode toward the Throat.
