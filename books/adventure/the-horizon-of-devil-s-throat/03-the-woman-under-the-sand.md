## 3. The Woman Under the Sand

The first thing Ilsa Reyes knew was weight.

Not pain — pain came later, arriving politely like a bill collector who had waited a decent interval. First there was only weight, the whole desert lying on top of her like a patient lover who had decided never to leave. Sand in her mouth. Sand behind her eyes. Sand packed into her ears so that her own heartbeat sounded like someone knocking on a door very far away.

*Knock,* said her heart. *Knock. Knock.*

She thought, with the strange clarity of the nearly dead: *I am under the ground and nobody is home.*

Then she remembered why, and wished she were still nobody.

* * *

She had been running. That much surfaced first, the way wreckage surfaces — sideways, reluctant, trailing things she didn't want to look at. Running south along the rim of Devil's Throat with the survey film canisters strapped to her chest like a bandolier of eggs, except these eggs held the truth about a minister's signature and a water contract that would drain three settlements dry, and men had been shooting at her own team to keep those eggs from hatching.

Men with government papers. She kept coming back to that detail, turning it over in the dark of her memory like a stone too hot to hold but impossible to drop. Not bandits. Bandits would have taken the instruments and been done with it. These men had worn contractor gray and carried authorization folders in oilcloth sleeves, and when Dr. Voss had asked to see their credentials — because Voss asked everyone for everything, bless his pedantic soul — they had shot him mid-sentence.

Mid-*sentence*. He'd gotten as far as "pursuant to section" before the bullet took him, and Ilsa had thought, absurdly, *he'll be furious he never finished the citation.*

Then she'd run, and the ground had opened, or she had fallen, or the sand had simply decided — the desert was like that, capricious as a cat — and the world had gone soft and dark and heavy.

*Knock. Knock.*

*Somebody's at the door,* she thought. *Don't answer it. The last people who answered doors out here got shot.*

But her hands were already moving. That was the terrible thing about being alive: the body voted before the mind finished counting ballots. Her fingers found sand above her face and pushed, and pushed, and the weight shifted by an ounce, then two, and somewhere above her a voice said words she couldn't hear through the packing in her ears, and then light came down through the hole she'd made like water finding a crack in a dam.

A face appeared in it. Brown, weathered, bearded, framed against a sky so blue it looked painted on.

"Well," the face said. "You're either very lucky or very stubborn."

Ilsa spat sand at him. It was all she had, and she spent it freely.

* * *

He dug her out the way he seemed to do everything else — without hurry, without fuss, talking the whole time in a low, even voice about nothing. About the wind direction. About how the dune had slumped after last week's blow and how she'd been lucky to leave herself an air pocket with her elbow, which apparently she had, though Ilsa had no memory of engineering anything; her body had simply known what her mind hadn't, which was more than could be said for most people she'd worked with.

When enough of her was free that she could sit up, she discovered she was shaking. Not from cold. The sun stood high and merciless and the air tasted like a forge. She shook anyway, a fine continuous tremor, and hated it, and shook.

"Water." Her voice came out as gravel. "Please."

He gave her a canteen and watched her drink with the professional attention of a man assessing how badly broken something was. He looked like a ranger — the badge on his chest said so, though the badge looked tired, the way badges do on men who've worn them past the point where the wearing means anything simple.

"Ilsa Reyes," she managed. "Junior cartographer. Meridian Survey Team, government charter 44-C."

"Ansel Okafor." He didn't offer a hand, which she appreciated; she wasn't sure hers would hold up to the courtesy. "Looking for your team. Found their camp instead."

The word *camp* went through her like a splinter. "Found them?"

"No." He said it plainly, no cushioning, and some distant part of her registered that this was a kindness — that he understood she'd already done the arithmetic herself, out here alone under the sand, and didn't need it performed for her again. "Camp was intact. Gear stacked neat. No tracks out. One boot print that just... stops."

"That's how they do it," Ilsa said. The shaking was getting worse. She gripped the canteen to give her hands a job. "That's how they did it. They come in quiet, they take you one at a time, and the desert does the paperwork. No bodies, no witnesses, no report. Just a camp that looks abandoned and a file that says *lost to conditions.*"

Okafor was quiet for a moment. His eyes — dark, unhurried, missing nothing — moved over her face like he was reading contour lines.

"You saw them."

"I saw them shoot my team." It came out flat and hard and surprised her with its steadiness. Good. Somewhere under the terror there was a spine; it was good to know it had survived the burial. "Six of them. Contractor gray, government papers, rifles with the serial numbers filed but the scopes new. They shot Dr. Voss while he was asking for their authorization. They shot Tomas before he cleared his holster — he was our geologist, he carried a sidearm like a man carries a pen, for show. They shot Priya in the back when she ran for the mules." Her voice cracked on Priya and she let it, deliberately, because these were cracks that deserved to show. "I ran because I had the film. Because Voss gave me the film and told me to run, and it's the only order of his I ever followed correctly, and I have hated myself for following it since."

"And the wreck," Okafor said. "You mentioned a wreck."

Had she? She must have, in the delirium, in whatever half-conscious muttering had led him to dig in this particular stretch of nowhere. She laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, sand still gritting between her teeth.

"You want to hear about the wreck."

"I want to hear everything," Okafor said. "But start with the wreck."

* * *

So she told him, sitting in the shadow of the dune that had tried to swallow her, drinking his water in small sips, watching the horizon over his shoulder the whole time because part of her brain refused to stop doing sentry duty even now.

"We found it on day nine of the survey. A transport airship, big one, military frame — down in the Throat itself, wedged in the narrows where the canyon bends around the old pillar rock. Twenty years in the sand, maybe more. Everyone assumed anything that fell in the Throat was stripped clean decades ago." She paused. "It wasn't."

"The manifest," Okafor said.

Her head came up sharply. "How do you—"

"Lucky guess," he said, in a tone that suggested it was not. "Sealed manifest. Official cargo. Go on."

"It's a water manifest." The words came faster now, twenty years of cartographer's precision finally finding their purpose. "Bulk water rights, transfer contracts, allocation schedules — signed off by the Ministry of Interior Resources, countersigned by Deputy Minister Corvane himself. Except the allocations don't match anything public. This is the real ledger. Three aquifer basins signed away to private holding companies for fractions of assessed value, and the holding companies trace back — I traced them, I'm a *cartographer*, tracing is what I *do* — they trace back to Corvane's brother-in-law and two men who used to be his business partners before he entered public life."

Okafor said nothing. He had gone very still, the way the desert goes still before wind.

"They weren't shooting at us because we saw a crashed ship," Ilsa said. "Ships crash. Ships are sad and expensive and nobody kills a survey team over scrap metal. They shot us because Voss opened the strongbox and read the manifest aloud, because Voss believed — God help him — that government paper meant government protection. He said, *we're chartered personnel, they wouldn't dare.*" She heard her own voice rise and made herself bring it down. "They dared. And then they came after me across four miles of open flats, shooting, until the ground took me. And now they think I'm dead, and the film is—" 

She stopped. Her hand went to her chest, to the place where the bandolier strap should have been, and found only the torn remains of her shirt and bare skin crusted with salt and blood.

The world tilted.

"No," she said. "No, no, no—"

"Easy." Okafor's hand closed over her wrist, firm, grounding. "Is this what you're looking for?"

He held up a small tin film canister, dented, sand-scoured, unmistakably one of hers. Then a second. Then, from inside his coat, a third.

"I found your trail," he said. "Or rather, your trail found me. You shed these like a bird molting, every hundred yards or so, right up until the dune got you. I figured they mattered. You don't bury yourself alive carrying junk."

Ilsa stared at the canisters. Something in her chest unclenched for the first time since Voss had pressed them into her hands and said *run* — and then, immediately, clenched tighter, because relief was just terror changing clothes.

"There were seven," she whispered.

"There were seven," Okafor agreed. "Four are still out there, scattered along your line of flight. Which means anyone who finds your trail finds them. And someone will, because whoever shot your team knows exactly what they lost, and they'll sweep this ground before the week's out." He tucked the canisters away and looked at her with those unhurried dark eyes. "So here's my question, Miss Reyes. And think carefully, because I only ask it once."

The wind moved across the flats, lifting a thin veil of sand off the dune's shoulder, and Ilsa Reyes — junior cartographer, sole survivor, woman who had woken up knowing too much to be allowed to live — sat in the shadow of her own grave and felt the fear in her gut transmute, slowly, into something harder and more useful.

"Ask," she said.

"What's in the Throat worth killing six people and hunting a seventh?" He paused. "Besides the manifest."

And Ilsa smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and she was proud of it.

"The rest of the crew," she said. "Voss wasn't the only one who read it. Our senior hydrologist, Marta Okonkwo — she pulled a sample core from the wreck's ballast tanks before the shooters arrived. Sealed it, logged it, and split off north with the field kit while Voss and I played decoy." She watched the name land on him. "Marta's alive, Ranger. Or she was forty hours ago. And if she's alive, she's got physical evidence that doesn't burn, doesn't bury, and doesn't forgive. Corvane's men aren't sweeping these flats for film canisters."

She pushed herself upright on shaking legs, defiant, dizzy, alive.

"They're looking for her. And we're going to find her first."
