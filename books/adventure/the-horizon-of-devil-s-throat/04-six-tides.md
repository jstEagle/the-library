## 4. Six Tides

Ilsa Reyes slept for eleven hours, and Ansel spent every one of them watching the desert do what it always did, which was nothing, magnificently.

He'd carried her back to his camp at the edge of the flats — a lean-to of canvas and creosote poles he'd built the year he stopped pretending he might build a house. She'd drunk two canteens of water, eaten half a tin of beans without tasting them, and gone down like a dropped rope. Now she lay in the shade with her boots still on, one hand curled around the scrap of survey film as if it were a child's finger, and the desert went about its business: heat shimmering off the salt, a lizard doing push-ups on a rock for reasons known only to lizards, the wind moving sand from one place to another in a transaction that had been running since before there was anyone around to bill for it.

Ansel sat on an ammunition crate and did sums.

It was what he did instead of praying. Twenty years of ranger work had taught him that most trouble could be solved by arithmetic if you were honest enough with your numbers. A man's tracks told you his weight. His water told you his range. His fear told you where he'd run. The desert was just a ledger, and every living thing wrote its account in it whether it meant to or not.

The problem this morning was that the numbers wouldn't sit still.

A survey team of six, vanished. A camp packed up neat as a church social. One boot print ending mid-stride, which meant either a man lifted into the air by God — unlikely; God hadn't invested in this territory in years — or a man carried, which meant other men, which meant organization, which meant money. And now a woman dug out of a sand drift who talked about government papers and men shooting at cartographers.

Cartographers. He turned the word over. Men whose weapons were rulers and pencils, murdered for drawing lines. Somebody had looked at a map being made and decided the map was more dangerous than the men.

That offended him. Not morally — he'd made his peace long ago with the fact that morality was a luxury item out here, sold by the ounce like everything else. What offended him was the arithmetic. A lie that big required maintenance. It required shooters and diggers and liaisons with clean fingernails, and all of it balanced against six lives written down as cost. Somewhere in some office a man had done that division and gotten a number he could live with.

Ansel had never been able to live with any number that came out even.

Behind him, Ilsa stirred. He heard her wake the way he heard everything now — first the breath changing, then the small sounds of a body taking inventory of itself, then silence while she remembered where she was and what had happened to her. He gave her the courtesy of not turning around until she spoke.

"You stayed," she said. Her voice was gravel and rust but it held.

"Camp's mine. Where would I go?"

"People don't stay." She sat up slowly, wincing at whatever the sand drift had done to her ribs. "People hear 'government' and develop urgent business elsewhere."

"I'm slow at developing things."

She looked at him properly for the first time — took in the badge, the rifle, the face underneath the hat brim — and he watched her decide something. He'd seen that look before, usually on people about to ask him for something they were ashamed of asking.

"You're the ranger," she said. "Okafor."

"That's what the badge says when it's feeling talkative."

"They sent *you* to find us?" Something moved through her face that wasn't quite laughter. "They sent the man who catches thieves."

"And?"

"And nothing." She reached for the water tin. Her hands shook; she steadied them against her knee and drank anyway, because out here you learned to drink through the shaking. "Nothing. Forget it."

Ansel let the silence work. It was the only tool he owned that never needed sharpening.

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way people tell things that have broken them — not chronologically but by weight, heaviest first.

"The wreck is in the Throat," she said. "The Devil's Throat proper, past where the maps stop pretending. An airship. Big one — three hundred feet if she's an inch, hull torn open along the port side like somebody unzipped her. She's been down a long time. Decades."

"Nobody's found her in decades?"

"Nobody was looking. She went down in the narrows during a flood year, and the Throat eats its dead. But the dry season's been pulling the water table down for three years straight, and the riverbed's exposed further every summer. Our survey was mapping the new channel." She laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "We thought we were mapping a riverbed. We were mapping a graveyard."

"What's in her?"

"Cargo. Sealed manifest, official stamps, the whole liturgy." Ilsa set down the water and looked at him, and her eyes were the clearest thing about her. "Water rights, Ranger. Concessions. Contracts signed by a minister's own office selling the aquifer under four counties to a holding company that doesn't exist — except on paper, where it exists very well, and the paper was in that ship's strongroom when she went down. Originals. Signed. Dated."

Ansel felt something cold settle behind his sternum, the way it did when a trail suddenly ran straight after days of switchbacks. "And the survey team found it."

"Damaris pulled the manifest himself. Read it by lantern light in the wreck's chart room. He read it twice." Her voice caught, steadied. "He said — he actually said, 'Well, girls, we've found the bottom of everything.' Then we walked out, and three days later the men with government papers came to our camp at dawn, and they weren't there to serve tea."

"The liaison who hired me," Ansel said slowly. "Vasquez."

"He'll be very sorry you haven't found us yet," Ilsa said. "Sorry enough to send better men than Vasquez. That's how it works. First they send someone to find you. When finding becomes a problem, they send someone to finish you."

"And you ran instead of dying politely."

"I ran." She said it without shame, chin up, daring him to find it funny. "Four days in open desert with half a canteen. I buried myself in a drift when I saw their dust, because a dead woman can't testify and a buried one can't be found. It wasn't brave. It was arithmetic. I'm the last copy of that manifest that walks and talks, Ranger. They don't need me alive. So I stayed underground and breathed through a reed and listened to my heart try to claw its way out of my chest, and I lived, because living is the only revenge I'm currently equipped for."

Ansel looked at her a long moment. There were women in the settlements who'd have said all that with tears. Ilsa Reyes said it like a supply list, and somehow that was worse, and better.

"How many men are coming?" he asked.

"A salvage fleet. That's what the papers called them — salvage contractors, licensed recovery, all very legal-sounding. Three ships, maybe forty men, heavy equipment. They're not going to recover anything. They're going to reduce that wreck to slag and the manifest to ash, and anyone standing near it to the same." She paused. "They left the capital eight days ago."

Eight days. Ansel did the math without wanting to. Salt pans, then the hard country, then the Throat approach — a fleet that size moved slow but didn't stop. Call it two days ahead of anything he and a half-dead cartographer could manage on foot and one tired horse.

Which brought him to the other number.

"When does the season close?" he asked, though he already knew. Every soul in the territory knew. You didn't survive out here by forgetting the calendar the sky kept.

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Six tides."

* * *

Here was the thing about the Devil's Throat that no map ever got right: it flooded twice a day.

Not rain — there was no rain to speak of, hadn't been real rain in years. The tides came from the old sea channels far to the south, pressure waves rolling up through the canyon system on the moon's schedule, filling the lower narrows forty feet deep in under an hour and draining again just as fast. The old-timers called it the canyon breathing. The smart ones called it a clock. The dead ones had called it nothing, because they'd miscounted.

And in six tides, the dry season would end the breathing altogether. The springs feeding the deep channels would fail, the silt would set like poured concrete, and the Throat would seal itself for another year — impassable, unmappable, and perfectly capable of hiding a wreck, a manifest, and forty armed men until spring.

Six tides. Six chances to get in, get the truth, and get out before the desert closed its mouth.

Ansel stood and walked to the edge of the shade and looked east, toward the badlands, where the horizon did its usual trick of promising nothing. Somewhere out there was a fleet. Somewhere beyond it was a wreck full of paper worth more than everyone in four counties put together. And somewhere behind him was a government office where a man named Vasquez was waiting for a report, and every hour Ansel delayed was an hour the report got stranger.

He could walk away. That was the honest arithmetic too. Ride north, turn in a report full of careful nothing, collect his fee, and let the fleet and the flood and the minister sort it out among themselves. The desert didn't need heroes. The desert needed people who knew when to be elsewhere, and Ansel Okafor had survived twenty years by being elsewhere with great precision.

But.

He thought about the boot print ending mid-stride. About six sets of gear stacked neat as a church social. About a junior cartographer breathing through a reed under four inches of sand while men with licenses looked for her. About a lie so large it required a fleet to maintain it, and the particular kind of man who signs off on fleets.

The math of it offended him. That was all. Not justice — justice was a word city people used for whatever they'd decided to want. Just arithmetic. A lie that size, balanced against that many lives, and somewhere a man had checked the sum and found it acceptable. Ansel had spent twenty years catching boys who stole water and horses, small crimes with small sums, and here was the big ledger, the master book, sitting in a dead ship in a dying canyon, and nobody was auditing it.

Except, apparently, him.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsa said behind him. "Where you stare at the horizon like it owes you money."

"It usually does."

"Are you going to help me or not? Because I need to know whether to start walking on my own, and my current walking capacity is best described as theoretical."

Ansel turned around. She was sitting up straight now, film scrap in her fist, looking at him with the particular ferocity of someone who has already died once today and found it inconvenient.

He should say no. Every prudent cell in his body voted no. Prudence had kept him alive through droughts and feuds and one memorable winter with a cannibalistic mule, and prudence said: one ranger, one witness, six tides, forty guns. Those numbers didn't balance. Those numbers were a joke.

But the lie didn't balance either, and it was winning.

"I'll take you to the Throat," he said. "That's the offer. I'll get you there ahead of the fleet if the desert allows it, and what happens after that, we invent as needed. I'm not promising you justice. I'm not even promising you survival. I'm promising you miles, and I've got a professional relationship with miles."

Ilsa studied him. "You know they'll kill us."

"Probably."

"You know nobody will ever know what we did."

"The desert will know. The desert keeps books." He picked up his hat and settled it, and felt something in his chest shift loose — not excitement, nothing so young as excitement. More like an old lock finally agreeing to turn. "One condition. We move within the hour, and you tell me everything Damaris read out of that manifest. Every name, every date, every signature. If I'm going to die for a piece of paper, I intend to know its reading level."

For the first time since he'd dug her out of the sand, Ilsa Reyes smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman doing her own arithmetic and liking her totals better than his.

"Get your horse, Ranger," she said. "The tide's already going out."

Far to the east, thin as a scratch on glass, a plume of dust stood up against the horizon — and began, patiently, to move.
