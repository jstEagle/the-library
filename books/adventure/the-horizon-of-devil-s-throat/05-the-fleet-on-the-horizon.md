## 5. The Fleet on the Horizon

The salt pans began at dawn, and by midmorning Ansel had stopped believing in anything but white.

It stretched ahead of them like a frozen sea, cracked into hexagons the size of dinner tables, each one glittering under a sun that had no mercy in it at all. The heat came up off the crust in waves, so that the horizon wobbled and swam, and every half hour Ilsa swore she saw water out there, and every half hour Ansel told her she didn't.

"You could have mentioned," she said, riding beside him with her canteen held against her chest like a child. "When you agreed to this. You could have said, *Ilsa, we'll be crossing the largest salt flat in the territory, and it will be like riding across the inside of an oven.*"

"I did say that."

"You said, 'it's a hard crossing.' That is not the same thing. A man says 'it's a hard crossing' when he means there'll be some hills."

Ansel allowed himself a small smile behind his scarf. Two days out from the Throat's edge, and she was already talking again — really talking, not the fevered raving of a woman he'd dug out of the sand, but the dry, needling talk of someone determined to be a person and not a patient. He found he liked it more than was strictly convenient.

"The trick," he said, "is to stop fighting the heat. Let it be part of you."

"That is the worst advice I have ever heard from a professional."

"Most of my advice is. People keep paying for it anyway."

She laughed — a short, cracked laugh, surprised out of her — and then pressed her lips together like she'd been caught doing something improper. Grief did that, he'd noticed. It made joy feel like trespassing.

They rode on. The mule plodded. The sun climbed.

It was a little past noon, when the light was flattest and cruelest, that Ilsa stood in her stirrups and went very still.

"Ansel."

He followed her arm. Off to the northwest, where the salt gave way to a smudge of broken country, there was a line on the sky. Not cloud — clouds over the pans came soft and gray. This was brown, straight-edged, organized. Dust raised by engines, lots of them, moving in formation.

"A dust plume," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when a person is holding themselves together with both hands. "That's them, isn't it. That's the fleet."

Ansel took off his hat and used the brim to shade his eyes, though it changed nothing. He already knew. Twenty years of reading country had taught him what different kinds of movement looked like from a long way off: a herd, a caravan, a storm. This was none of those. This was machinery, and it was heavy, and it was not hurrying because it did not think anyone alive could possibly be racing it.

"They're not running," he said slowly. "Look at the spread. They're driving like men who own the road."

"Why wouldn't they? As far as they know, everyone who knew about the wreck is dead." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I'm supposed to be dead too, Ansel. They shot me and left me for the sand to finish."

"Then let's stay dead a while longer," Ansel said. "Dead people don't cross open salt in daylight."

* * *

They cut south along the pan's ragged margin, keeping to a spine of dark rock that broke the white like the keel of a buried ship, and made their camp that night in the lee of it, cold-camped, no fire. From there Ansel did what he had done for twenty years: he sat still and watched the world tell him things.

The fleet's plume crawled north all afternoon. By dusk it had reached the notch in the far mesa line where the old survey maps showed a fueling depot — one of the government relay stations strung across the badlands like beads on a wire, each one stocked, staffed, and officially nonexistent. Ansel knew them the way a fisherman knows holes in a river.

"They'll stop there tonight," he said. "Fuel and water. Maybe swap drivers. Then they push for the Throat tomorrow at first light."

Ilsa sat wrapped in a blanket, working sums in the dirt with a stick. "The wreck's two days' ride past the depot if they take the freight road. We're cutting the slot canyons — a day and a half, if the slots are passable." She looked up. "We're still behind, aren't we."

"Behind by about a day." He turned his hat slowly in his hands. "Which means we don't beat them on the road. We beat them by knowing something they don't. And right now the only thing we know that they don't is how to move through country that eats machines."

"And the depot," Ilsa said quietly.

He looked at her.

"We're out of fuel for the pump-lamp and the radio's dead — my radio died with the survey camp, and yours won't hold a charge. If we're going to be in those canyons when the tides turn, we need to hear the weather. And if we come out of this alive—" she stopped, jaw tightening, "—someone has to be able to call for help that isn't *theirs*. Every lawman, every garrison, every relief column in this territory answers to the same ministry. We need a voice that goes somewhere else."

Ansel looked north, toward the depot's faint glow against the stars. A night raid. Fuel, a radio, maybe maps of the fleet's own route lying on a desk somewhere. Everything they needed, sitting in a station manned by contractors who had shot down a survey team and buried a cartographer alive.

It should have felt harder than it did. That was the thing he noticed later, turning it over in the dark — how reasonable it sounded, how clean. He was a ranger. He had a badge in his pocket that said the law was on his side, except the law was the thing chasing them. So the arithmetic came out simple: take from them what they stole first.

Simple arithmetic is where most sins start. But he didn't know that yet.

"All right," he said. "But we do it quiet. Nobody dies tonight that doesn't make us."

* * *

The depot sat in a bowl of shattered basalt, a cluster of prefab sheds and a fuel bladder under netting, lit by two work lamps and guarded — Ansel counted through his field glasses — by four men. One on the perimeter walking a slow circuit. One smoking on the loading dock. Two inside the comms shed, visible through the window, playing cards.

Contractors, not soldiers. He could tell by the way they stood: comfortable, bored, men being paid by the day rather than committed by conviction. Men who had signed on to guard fuel depots and found themselves guarding secrets instead.

He left Ilsa with the mule on the rim, the rifle across her knees. "You see anyone come out that isn't one of these four, you put a round over our heads and run."

"And leave you?"

"Leave me. That's not a discussion."

"It's absolutely a discussion—"

"Ilsa." He kept his voice gentle. "If it goes wrong, the truth walks out of here on your legs or not at all. I'm replaceable. You're the only testimony in the world."

Something moved behind her eyes — anger, and fear, and underneath both of them a stubbornness he was beginning to suspect could outlast the desert itself. She didn't answer. She just checked the rifle's action with a click that meant *we will discuss this later.*

He went down the slope like smoke.

Twenty years of tracking thieves had taught him more than how to find people. It had taught him how to be unseen by them — how to read the rhythm of a sentry's circuit, how to move when wind moved, how to let shadow do the walking. The perimeter man passed within six feet of him without ever knowing the night had grown one extra shape.

The comms shed's back door wasn't locked. Sloppy. Rich men's employees are always sloppy, because rich men never imagine the desert talks back.

Inside, the card players had their backs to him. On the desk: a radio set, military band, newer than anything Ansel had ever been issued. Beside it, a clipboard with the fleet's schedule in a clerk's neat hand. He photographed nothing — there was no camera — so he read it twice and put it in his shirt. Fuel top-off complete by 0500. Departure 0530. Freight road to the Throat, arrival estimated tomorrow evening. And beneath that, in a different hand, a line that made the room go cold around him:

*Minister's directive: site demolition authorized upon recovery of documents. No survivors provisioned.*

No survivors provisioned. Not even their own.

He was folding the schedule away when the door swung open.

One of the card players, stepping out for whatever men step out for. A big man, gray at the temples, a pistol holstered on his hip and a coffee cup in his hand. He saw Ansel. Ansel saw him. For one full second neither of them moved, two men caught in the strange suspended time that exists between a life and its ending.

Then the man went for the holster.

Later, Ansel would try to reconstruct the moment honestly, because honesty was the only currency he had left after that night. He had not planned it. His hand had simply gone to his own sidearm the way a hand goes to a falling glass, and the shot went off in the closed shed like the world cracking in half, and the coffee cup hit the floor and rolled, and the man sat down against the wall with a look of genuine surprise, as if the universe had made a clerical error.

The other card player came through the front door shouting, and Ilsa's rifle spoke once from the rim — a warning round, high — and the man froze, hands up, and everything after that was noise and motion: fuel cans dragged to the mule, the radio slung across Ansel's shoulder, the second contractor kneeling with his fingers laced behind his head, saying *don't, don't, don't* in a small voice that belonged to somebody's son.

They were back over the rim and gone before the depot finished waking up.

* * *

They rode hard until the salt was hours behind them and the broken country swallowed their trail, then made cold camp again in a slot so narrow the stars were a river overhead. Ilsa built no fire either. She just sat close, shoulder to shoulder, sharing warmth the way the honest poor share everything.

"You killed him," she said at last. Not accusing. Just naming it, the way you name weather.

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

Ansel considered the question with the care it deserved. He turned it over the way he'd turned his hat earlier, looking for the crack in it, the place where the truth leaked through.

What frightened him was not the shooting. He'd drawn on men before; he'd been lucky, mostly, and good enough not to need luck often. What frightened him was the space before the shot — that single suspended second — and how in it he had felt not panic but *clarity*. Clean, cold, total clarity, the kind he got reading sign or finding water. His body had known exactly what to do and had done it without consulting him, and afterward his hands had been steady. Steady as a surgeon's. Steady as a man doing a job.

Power over life and death, sitting easy in his hand. Sitting *comfortable*.

"No," he said finally. "But I will be. Or I won't, and either way the answer's mine to carry."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "My team leader used to say the desert doesn't make you cruel. It just shows you what you brought with you." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Get some sleep, ranger. Tomorrow we find out if your slot canyons are as good as your reputation."

He slept in fragments, and each time he woke, the stars had shifted and the man with the coffee cup was sitting against the wall in his memory, surprised, ordinary, dead. *No survivors provisioned,* the schedule said. The minister hadn't even bothered to pretend the contractors would live through it. Men hired to bury a secret, scheduled to become part of it.

Near dawn, Ilsa shook him awake, and there was something in her face that made the cold in his chest go colder.

"Ansel. Listen."

He listened. Far off to the north, thin as a thread, came the sound of engines — many engines — but wrong, somehow. Not the steady grind of the freight road. Scattered. Searching.

"They've left the depot early," she whispered. "And they're fanning out. They know someone's ahead of them."

Ansel rolled to his knees, every ache in his body suddenly irrelevant. Ahead of them lay the mouth of the slot canyons, the labyrinth, the last ground on earth where a machine couldn't follow. Behind them, the fleet was spreading across the broken country like spilled ink.

And somewhere between the two, moving fast and light, were scouts — riders sent ahead to find the gap before the flood of steel arrived.

He tightened the girth on the mule and thought, absurdly, of a boy he'd once let go on the flats outside Perdition, a petty thief with quick eyes and quicker feet, who knew these canyons better than any map.

"Hold on to that radio," he said. "We're going to need guides."
