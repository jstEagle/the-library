## 6. What the Thief Knew

Ilsa had decided, somewhere around the third day of riding behind Ansel Okafor, that she hated the desert on principle and would be filing a formal complaint with whatever authority governed such things.

The complaint would read: *I was promised a career in cartography. Cartography involves paper. Paper does not involve sand in places sand has no business being.*

She shifted in the saddle and winced. The bruises from her burial had gone from purple to a sickly yellow-green, and every jolt of the horse reminded her body that it had recently been exhumed. Ahead of them, Ansel rode with the easy, economical stillness of a man who had made peace with his spine long ago. He hadn't spoken in an hour. She'd learned that this wasn't rudeness; it was how he listened to the land, the way other men listened to radio.

The badlands had changed since they left the salt pans. The open country had folded in on itself, walls of red stone rising on either side of a dry wash, narrowing until the sky became a ribbon of blue overhead. It was beautiful, in the way a knife is beautiful.

"Devil's Throat," Ansel said, without turning. "We're in it now."

"I noticed," Ilsa said. "It's very atmospheric. If we die here, at least the setting will be appropriate."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him — one breath through the nose, quickly suppressed, as though humor were a ration he was saving for emergencies. She was beginning to collect those almost-laughs like a miser collects coins.

Then Ansel's hand came up, flat, and she reined in so fast her horse bumped his tail.

Ahead, sitting on a rock in the middle of the wash as if he'd been placed there by a stagehand, was a boy.

He looked sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and sunburn, wearing a coat three sizes too large and an expression of tremendous self-satisfaction. A battered rifle lay across his knees with the casualness of someone who had never actually pointed it at anything.

"Morning, Ranger," the boy said.

Ansel sighed the sigh of a man greeting an old mistake. "Tomas."

"You remember me." The boy beamed.

"I remember letting you go. Which was apparently a mistake, because here you are again, sitting in a slot canyon armed with a weapon you clearly don't know how to hold."

"It's not loaded," Tomas said cheerfully. "It's for atmosphere." He swung off the rock and approached, and only then did Ilsa see him really look at her — a quick, sharp assessment, cataloguing her bruises, her sun-scoured face, the way she sat her saddle like a woman who'd been dug out of a grave two weeks ago. Whatever he concluded, he kept it behind his teeth.

"This is Tomas," Ansel said to her. "A thief."

"Reformed," said Tomas.

"A thief I caught stealing water rations off a government convoy last month. I let him go."

"Why?" Ilsa asked.

Ansel was quiet a moment. "Because he was carrying the water to his sister's settlement. Because the convoy was overstocked. And because the paperwork for arresting him properly would have taken me four days to fill out." He paused. "Two of those reasons were good ones."

Tomas grinned like this was the finest story ever told about him. "And now the ranger needs me. Because you want to go into the deep Throat, and there is exactly one person alive who knows the ways through, and that person is me."

"The smugglers' routes," Ansel said.

"The smugglers' routes," Tomas agreed, spreading his arms as if unveiling a cathedral. "Twenty years my family has run this canyon. Water, medicine, things the government says don't exist. My uncle knew every crack in these walls before your survey teams even had maps of it. And then—" the grin dropped away, and something older and harder moved behind his eyes "—then your survey team walked in here six weeks ago, and everything got complicated."

Ilsa felt it before she understood it: a cold hand closing around her sternum. "You knew about the survey team?"

"Everyone in the canyon settlements knew. Eleven people, government papers, fancy instruments. They hired guides from the eastern settlements, polite about it, paid well." Tomas looked at her directly. "They went up the narrows toward the old wreck site. And the fleet men were already waiting."

* * *

They made camp that night in a side chamber Tomas called the Chapel — a hollow in the canyon wall where the rock had eroded into fluted columns, and where a seep of water dripped into a basin some patient geology had carved. Tomas built a small fire, shielded by stones, and cooked jackrabbit with a competence that contradicted his general air of being a decorative teenager.

Ilsa sat close to the flames and tried to make the arithmetic work.

Eleven people. She could name them. Dr. Amara Chen, who hummed when she drew contour lines and once gave Ilsa her own boots when Ilsa's fell apart in the field. Okonkwo, the team lead, who drank his coffee black and called everyone "colleague" including the mules. The Vasquez twins, who finished each other's sentences and each other's surveys. Little Ferro, who was twenty-three and wrote letters home every night by lantern light, and Ilsa had teased him about it, and he'd said, "My mother worries," and Ilsa had said, "Mine doesn't, she knows I'm indestructible," and they had both laughed—

She realized Ansel was watching her.

"How much do you know?" she asked Tomas. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "About what happened to them."

Tomas turned the rabbit over the fire. When he spoke, he didn't perform anymore. "The fleet came into the Throat three days before your team reached the narrows. Six boats, government plates painted over. They set up at the junction where the canyon splits. Anyone coming up from the south has to pass it. There's no other way — except ours." He tapped his own chest. "My family's way."

"They weren't after the survey team," Ansel said slowly. "They were after the wreck. The survey team just—" 

"Just walked into the middle of it," Tomas finished. "Your colleagues found the Meridian Star first. That's the thing nobody outside figured out. They weren't lost. They were ahead. Three days ahead of the fleet, up the narrows, mapping the approach to the wreck. And the fleet couldn't let anyone map an approach they wanted to keep secret, so—" He made a small, terrible gesture, a hand opening.

Firelight. The drip of water into the basin. Ilsa discovered she was gripping her own knee hard enough to leave marks.

"We were collateral," she said.

"You were witnesses," Tomas corrected gently. "Collateral is what happens by accident. This was on purpose. They shot first and asked questions of nobody. You're alive because you fell somewhere they didn't check, or because somebody got sloppy, or because the desert decided you had more to say. Pick whichever story helps you sleep."

None of them helped her sleep. But one of them made her angry, and anger was fuel, and she had learned in the past two weeks that fuel was the only thing worth carrying in this country.

"Okonkwo sent us up the narrows in pairs," she heard herself say. "Standard protocol after the second supply drop went missing. He thought we were being watched. He was right, and he still sent us forward, because the survey mattered more than the risk." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He called it diligence. We were walking his people into a firing line and calling it diligence."

"That's not on you," Ansel said quietly.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't give me absolution, Ranger, I haven't asked for any. I was the junior cartographer. I drew the maps that told us the narrows were passable. My lines, my confidence, my 'yes, colleague, we can make good time.'" She stared into the fire until her eyes burned for a reason that had nothing to do with smoke. "So no. I'm not going to sit in this pretty chapel and grieve beautifully. Grief is what they want from us. Grief stays home and writes letters. I intend to finish the survey."

Tomas looked at her with something new in his face — respect, maybe, or recognition. "Finish it?"

"Every contour. Every approach route. The whole thing, exactly as Okonkwo planned it, delivered to people who'll use it." She turned to Ansel. "You want the manifest to topple a minister. Fine. But the manifest is a piece of paper. Maps are how the truth travels afterward. The water rights, the wreck site, the routes the fleet used to sneak in here — all of it, drawn clean, copied wide. You can't burn a map out of ten thousand hands."

Ansel regarded her for a long moment. In the firelight she saw something shift in him — the professional unease she'd come to know giving way to something else, something that looked almost like hope wearing a workman's clothes.

"Tomas," he said. "How many of the smugglers' routes have never been on a government map?"

"All of them." The boy smiled, slow and wide. "Every single one. Why do you think the fleet's been stuck at the junction for six weeks? They've got boats and guns and absolutely no idea where the canyon goes." He stood, brushing off his enormous coat, and struck a pose against the firelight that suggested he had rehearsed this. "Which means, friends, that for the first time in the history of Devil's Throat, the smugglers hold the high ground, the low ground, and every ground in between. And I am the only guide willing to take you where you need to go."

"What's your price?" Ansel asked warily.

"Oh, we'll get to my price." Tomas's grin turned positively piratical. "But first you should hear what my uncle's people saw at the wreck site last week. Because the fleet isn't just guarding the Meridian Star anymore, Ranger. They've started building something around it. Big. Metal. And there's a barge coming up the dry wash tomorrow with equipment none of us can name — but my cousin swears she heard the crew call it by a word that made her blood run cold."

Ilsa leaned forward. "What word?"

Tomas let the silence stretch, savoring it, and then the grin fell off his face entirely.

"*Demolition.*"
