## 7. Tide Against Tide

The narrows of Devil's Throat did not look like a place where water had ever existed. The walls rose sheer and rust-red on either side, close enough in places that Ansel could have touched both with his outstretched hands, and the sky above was a ribbon of blue no wider than a hat brim. The sand underfoot was dry as gunpowder and sang faintly when they walked on it.

"Beautiful," Ilsa said, in the tone of someone cataloguing evidence for a murder trial.

"It's honest country," Ansel said. "It never pretends to be anything but what it is."

"What it is, is a funnel."

"That too."

He had been explaining the tides to her since dawn — not ocean tides, but the desert's own version, the way the canyon breathed water twice a day when rain fell in the high country fifty miles north. The locals called them tides because that's what they behaved like: predictable, patient, and utterly indifferent to what stood in their way. A flood could come down these narrows on a cloudless afternoon, a wall of brown water six feet high moving at the speed of a trotting horse, carrying boulders the size of sleeping cattle.

"So we're walking down the throat of something that swallows people," Ilsa said, "on a schedule."

"You wanted the shortcut."

"I wanted *a* shortcut. You volunteered this one with suspicious enthusiasm."

Ansel almost smiled. Three days out from the salt pans, three days since the outpost and the man he'd killed, and she still found things to needle him about. It was, he was beginning to understand, how she kept herself stitched together. He'd stopped thinking of her as the witness somewhere around the second morning, when she'd taken over the navigation without asking and turned out to be better at it than he was. Employer and witness had quietly become something else — two people walking in the same direction for reasons neither of them fully said aloud.

The boy — Teo, the thief he'd let go, who had materialized out of a slot canyon two days back like a debt coming due — ranged ahead of them, reading the stone the way Ansel read sand. He'd been raised in the Throat's smuggler routes, he said, running messages and contraband since he was nine. He knew which side channels flooded first and which led nowhere.

Which is why, when Teo came sprinting back around the bend with his face drained white, Ansel believed him instantly.

"Water," Teo gasped. "High country. Big storm — you can hear it."

And then Ansel could hear it too. Not the water itself, not yet, but a low grinding roar from upstream, like the canyon clearing its throat.

"Side channel," Ansel said, already moving. "Now."

* * *

They made it forty yards up the side slot before the main narrows filled.

The sound was worse than the sight — a freight-train bellow that bounced off the walls and came at them from every direction at once. Then the water arrived, brown and muscular, slamming into the junction below them and climbing the walls in a churning fist. Spray reached them even forty feet up the side channel, cold and smelling of wet stone and faraway thunder.

Ilsa pressed her back to the rock beside him. Her hand found his in the dark of the spray and held on, and neither of them remarked on it.

"How long?" she shouted over the roar.

"Tide runs an hour, maybe two. We wait it out." He looked up the side slot. It climbed steeply, narrowing to a crack. "We're not going anywhere anyway."

It was Teo who spotted them. "Rangers," he said, pointing down through the spray.

Two figures clung to a ledge above the drowned narrows, maybe sixty yards downstream and twenty feet lower. Fleet scouts — Ansel recognized the grey slickers, the government-issue packs. One of them was waving. The other wasn't moving much.

"They'll drown," Ilsa said.

"They'll drown," Ansel agreed.

The tide was still rising. He could see it in the way the foam crept up the walls below the ledge, eating the scouts' footing inch by inch. The one who was waving had stopped waving and started doing arithmetic — Ansel could see him doing it, the same arithmetic any desert man would do: *how long can I hold, how long will this last, is anyone coming.*

Ansel did the arithmetic too. The side slot above them narrowed to a crack too tight for a grown man. Below, the junction was a boiling cauldron. Between here and there, a sloping shelf of rock ran along the wall, half-submerged already, slick as oiled glass.

"There's a route," he said. "It's bad."

"Define bad," Ilsa said.

"Wet."

She looked at him for a long moment, spray streaming off her chin. "You're going down there."

"Yeah."

"And I'm supposed to do what, watch?"

"You're supposed to hold my rope."

* * *

The rope was Teo's idea, actually — the boy carried fifty feet of braided hide line the way other children carried marbles, and he rigged an anchor around a stone knob with the brisk competence of someone who'd hauled smugglers' cargo up these walls since before his voice broke. Ansel went down the shelf on the line, boots skidding on wet rock, the flood roaring past close enough to soak him to the skin in the first ten feet.

The first scout was dead by the time he reached him. The water had taken him off the ledge while Ansel was still crossing; Ansel saw the body turn over twice in the brown torrent and vanish downstream, arms open, almost peaceful. He filed the sight away in the place where he kept such things, next to the man from the outpost, and kept moving.

The second scout had wedged himself into a crack above the foam line, both arms hooked into the stone, screaming something Ansel couldn't hear over the water. When Ansel got a hand on his collar, the man fought him — pure animal panic, elbow catching Ansel across the jaw — until Ansel put his mouth against the man's ear and roared, "You want to live or win? Pick one!"

That got through. The scout went limp enough to be managed, and Ansel hauled him up the shelf hand over hand, the rope singing, Ilsa and Teo pulling from above, until they all collapsed on the dry stone of the side slot, four people breathing like landed fish.

* * *

The scout was young. That was the thing that got Ansel, sitting there wringing out his shirt while the tide slowly began its long retreat below them. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, with a sunburned nose and hands that hadn't finished callousing. His name, once he'd stopped shaking enough to talk, was Perrin Voss, and he was a hydrographic surveyor's assistant, third class, which apparently meant he drew maps of places the fleet intended to take apart.

His partner had been named Dov. He said it once, quietly, looking down at the emptying narrows, and didn't say it again.

"You're Okafor," Voss said suddenly, squinting at him. "The ranger. They briefed us on you. Said you were—" He stopped, recalculating, the way young men do when they realize the person they're talking to has just pulled them out of a flood.

"Said I was what?"

"Trouble." Voss shrugged, water dripping off him. "They weren't wrong."

Ilsa sat down across from him, deliberately, the way she sat down across from witnesses. "Tell us about the timetable, Mr. Voss."

"I'm not—"

"You watched your partner die in a canyon your employers sent you into during flood season. You're going to sit there and tell me you owe them loyalty?" She leaned forward. "I was on that survey team, son. I buried my colleagues under a cairn with my own hands. Talk."

Voss talked.

The fleet was two days behind them now — the flood had cost them the low route, and they'd have to swing wide through the eastern slots, adding a day. They'd reach the Meridian Star on the fifth day. But they weren't coming to salvage, not really. Voss had seen the manifests, had helped load the crates at the last depot: shaped charges, incendiary compound, enough demolition gear to bring the whole wreck down into the deepest sink of the canyon.

"The minister's orders," he said. "Nothing leaves the Throat. Not the manifest, not the wreck, not—" He hesitated.

"Not the survey team," Ilsa said flatly. "Not me."

Voss looked at her and didn't lie. "There was a list."

Ansel felt something go very quiet and very cold in his chest. "A list of names."

"People who'd seen the original manifest. Anyone who could testify." Voss swallowed. "Your name was on it too, Ranger. Both of you. The minister wants the canyon sealed with everything inside it. The dry season does most of the work — after the last tide, the lower routes silt up for eight months. By the time anyone gets back in, there'll be nothing but a rockfall and a story about an accident."

Teo, who had been uncharacteristically silent through all of this, spoke up from the shadows of the slot. "My uncle ran cargo through the Throat for eleven years. Nobody ever sealed it. Not the government, not the floods, nobody. There are ways in that aren't on any map."

"There are ways in that aren't on *my* map," Voss said, and something in his voice made them all look at him. He was staring at his own hands. "Dov and me, we signed on for survey wages. Nobody told us we were the cleanup crew." He looked up. "I'll draw you the fleet's route. All of it. Every camp, every checkpoint, the timetable."

"Why?" Ilsa asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"Because Dov's name isn't going to be on anybody's list," Voss said. "Because somebody should come out of this canyon knowing what happened down here." He wiped his face. "And because you pulled me out."

* * *

The tide died away in the late afternoon, the narrows draining with a long gurgle like a giant bath emptying, leaving the stone walls darkened and dripping and the air washed clean and cool. They moved downstream while the light lasted, Voss walking between them not as a prisoner but as something none of them had quite named — a defector, maybe, or just a scared kid who'd chosen a side.

At dusk they made camp in a smuggler's cache Teo knew, a hollow behind a fallen slab with an old iron stove and a generation of initials carved into the ceiling. Ilsa cooked; Voss drew maps on the back of a fuel ration card, his pencil quick and precise. Ansel sat at the entrance watching the last light drain out of the western sky.

Ilsa came and sat beside him. For a while neither of them said anything. Down in the canyon, the first stars came out over the black line of the walls.

"He gave us a day," she said finally. "Maybe less, if the fleet pushes hard."

"We'll need it."

"And if we don't get there in time — if they seal that canyon with everyone in it—" She stopped. He heard her breathe, deliberate, the way she did when she was choosing not to break. "Ansel. My name was on a list. Written down in some office, between supply requisitions. Like freight."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I keep thinking about Dov, that poor boy's partner, floating down that canyon, and I think — that's what the list looks like when it comes due." She turned to face him, and in the dim light her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "I'm done being evidence. I'm done being a witness. Whatever happens at that wreck, I'm not standing behind anyone. I'm standing next to you."

Ansel looked at her. Twenty years of measuring distances, and he couldn't find the distance between them anymore, and for the first time in his life that didn't frighten him.

"Next to me," he said. "All right."

From inside the cache, Voss called out, his voice cracking with something between exhaustion and urgency: "Okafor! You need to see this — there's a mark on the fleet's route map. A camp that shouldn't exist. And the timetable says they're sending someone ahead of the main force. Someone's already at the wreck."

Ansel was on his feet before the words finished landing.

"Who?"

Voss turned the fuel card toward the firelight. Pencil marks, checkpoints, dates — and one notation in red, circled twice, in a hand that wasn't Voss's.

"*Meridian Star — advance party — Minister's personal envoy.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the high country, thunder grumbled over the next tide.

"Six days early," Ilsa whispered. "Whoever that envoy is — they're already there."
