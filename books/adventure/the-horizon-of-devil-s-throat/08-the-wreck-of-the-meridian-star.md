## 8. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

They came over the last ridge an hour before dawn, and Ansel Okafor stopped his horse without meaning to.

Below them, wedged into a shelf of the canyon like a swallowed thing, lay the *Meridian Star*.

He had seen wrecks before. The desert was generous with them — prospectors' balloons deflated in the flats, mail skimmers folded into mesas, once an entire passenger liner beached on a dry lakebed like a whale that had believed the water would come back. But those were bones. This was different. The *Meridian Star* had gone down whole. Her hull was split along one seam, her gondola canted at an angle that suggested she'd been set down rather than crashed, and her skin — pale grey, government-issue, the kind of paint that cost more than a rancher made in a year — still caught the starlight and threw it back faintly, like something sleeping under dust that refused to look dead.

"She's big," Ilsa said quietly. She hadn't slept much since the narrows; neither of them had. "Bigger than the reports said."

"Reports lie," Ansel said. "That's sort of our problem."

Beside him, the boy — Dez, he'd finally learned the name, though the boy still answered to nothing so reliably as to the promise of food — shifted in his saddle and spat thoughtfully over the edge.

"That's been there three years," Dez said. "Maybe four. Smugglers call it the Old Lady. Nobody goes near it."

"Why not?" Ilsa asked.

Dez shrugged with the elaborate indifference of someone who knew exactly why not and found the question beneath him. "Because men who go near it stop coming back. And because it's haunted." He paused. "Also the wind screams through her ribs at night, which is honestly reason enough for me."

Ansel looked at the wreck a long moment. Three years. The survey team had found her weeks ago, not years. Which meant the smugglers had known about the *Meridian Star* all along and left her alone — either out of superstition or out of something smarter. He suspected smarter. Men who moved contraband through Devil's Throat didn't survive by poking at things the government might come looking for.

"All right," he said. "We go down slow. Horses stay here. Ilsa—"

"I'm coming."

"You're coming," he agreed, because arguing with her cost more than it returned, "and you stay behind me until we know what's in there."

"And if what's in there is ghosts?"

"Then you stay behind me anyway. Ghosts get my face first."

The descent took the better part of two hours, down switchbacks the canyon had cracked into itself, through shadow that held the night's cold like a cupped hand. By the time they reached the canyon floor the sky above had gone from black to the deep bruised purple that came just before first light, and the *Meridian Star* rose over them entire — a hundred and forty feet of hull, its nose buried in a fan of rockfall, its tail jutting out over the wash like a finger pointing home to some country none of them would ever see.

Ansel circled it once, reading the ground the way other men read letters. No tracks recent enough to matter. Old fire ring, cold for months. A rope ladder at the gash in the hull, new rope, which meant somebody climbed up and down it often enough to wear it but careful enough to keep it maintained.

Somebody lived here.

"Somebody lives here," he said aloud.

"The survey leader," Ilsa breathed. "Vasquez. Captain Emilio Vasquez. If anyone got out—" She was already moving toward the ladder, and this time Ansel didn't stop her, because there was no version of stopping her that ended well.

He went up first anyway. Some arguments you won by simply being faster.

* * *

Inside, the wreck smelled of lamp oil and old coffee and, underneath both, the faint sweet rot of sealed machinery. Ansel came through the gash into what had been the cargo bay — a cathedral of a room, ribbed with steel, dark as a throat — and put his back to the wall out of twenty years of habit, and it was that habit that saved the conversation, if not his dignity.

"Stop there."

A lantern flared. Behind it stood a man.

He was gaunt in the way of men who had eaten carefully for a long time, bearded past the point of grooming into the territory of philosophy, wearing a survey officer's coat that had been patched with sailcloth and wire. He held a pistol in both hands, and his grip was steady, and his eyes went from Ansel's face to Ilsa's and changed entirely.

"Reyes?" he said. "Ilsa Reyes?"

"Captain Vasquez." Her voice cracked on it. "God. God, we thought — we looked for bodies, we—"

"There are no bodies." Vasquez lowered the pistol slowly, and then, as if remembering how, extended his free hand and pumped Ansel's arm twice, hard. "You're the ranger. Okafor. They sent a ranger after us? After three years?"

"They didn't send me after you," Ansel said. "They sent me after a missing survey team from six weeks ago. Your team."

Something flickered across Vasquez's face — quick, controlled, gone. "My team scattered. Protocol. When the contractors hit our camp, protocol was dispersal. I came here because I already knew about the ship. We mapped her three years ago on a supply run. I've been living aboard ever since, off and on. Watching."

"Watching what?" Ansel asked, though he already knew. You could feel the answer in the room. It sat in the cargo hold with them like ballast.

Vasquez turned and led them deeper into the ship, past crates stenciled with numbers that meant nothing and everything, up a companionway into what had been the officers' mess. A table. Two chairs. On the table, in a document case chained to the table leg, gray and official and utterly ordinary-looking:

The manifest.

Ilsa made a sound low in her throat, half relief, half grief, and started forward, and Vasquez put his hand flat on the case.

"Not yet," he said gently. "Sit down. Both of you. There are things you need to understand."

Ansel did not sit down. He leaned against the doorframe instead, arms crossed, and watched the man who had been dead for six weeks being very much alive, and let the quiet do its work. Quiet usually did. People filled it with the truth eventually, the way water fills a hole.

Vasquez lasted almost a full minute.

"The manifest implicates Minister Corvane," he said. "Directly. Signatures, dates, transfer authorizations. Water rights sold out from under eleven settlements, routed through shell companies, the proceeds moved through the Reclamation Bureau itself. My team didn't find this ship by accident, Ranger. We were tipped. And when we confirmed what she carried, certain people decided the confirmation should die with us." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, thin and proud and cracked. "Six of my people are dead. I intend for it to have bought something."

"It will," Ilsa said fiercely. "Emilio, we can get it out. There are people — the canyon settlements, the press in Solano, if we move tonight, before the fleet—"

"The fleet." Vasquez nodded slowly. "Yes. I've seen their dust. Two days out, maybe less now." He looked at the case, and his hand rested on it the way a man rests his hand on a child's head. "Which brings me to the part you won't like."

Ansel felt it coming the way you feel weather. "Say it anyway."

"I've spent three years keeping this safe. Three years alone in a dead ship, eating scorpions and drinking condensation, watching every approach to this canyon, waiting for the right moment. And the right moment, Ranger, is not a mob of miners with a mimeographed broadsheet. The right moment is leverage." Vasquez straightened. "Corvane's people have already contacted me. Through intermediaries. They know where I am — they've always known, that's the joke of it, they could never come take it because taking it proves nothing and kills their deniability. So they offered terms. Full amnesty. Restoration of rank. Pensions for the families of my dead. And in exchange, the manifest comes back to the Bureau, quiet, sealed, and everyone walks away whole."

The silence afterward had weight, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

"You'd give it back to them," Ilsa said. Her voice was very small and very level, which Ansel had learned, in the short brutal education of the last week, was the most dangerous register she owned. "Six people dead. Marta. Okonkwo. Little Devi, who was twenty-three, Emilio, she was *twenty-three*. You'd hand it back and call it whole."

"I'd call it *survived*," Vasquez snapped, and for a moment the officer showed through the hermit, rigid and furious. "You were under the sand, Reyes. You don't know what three years of watching the horizon does to a man. Every dawn I thought: today they come. Today the shot comes out of the sun. I have earned the right to end it."

"Earned," Ilsa repeated softly. "And them? Did they earn it?"

"That's not—"

"That's exactly what it is."

Ansel let them go at each other for a moment. Then he said, quietly, "Captain. Who contacted you?"

Vasquez blinked. "What?"

"The intermediary. The one carrying Corvane's offer. Name. Description. How did they reach a man who's been a ghost for three years?"

For the first time, Vasquez hesitated. And Ansel Okafor, who had measured his life in miles, recognized the particular quality of that hesitation: the pause of a man standing at the edge of a distance he had not yet admitted to himself.

"A lawyer," Vasquez said finally. "Out of Solano. Silver-haired fellow, very reasonable, very sympathetic. Said he represented parties within the Bureau who wanted this handled humanely."

"What was his name?"

"Halloran."

Ansel nodded slowly. Halloran. The same name that had been signed, in neat bureaucratic script, to the liaison's letter that had hired him in the first place. The same name on the salvage fleet's charter, buried in the paperwork Ilsa had memorized from the camp records. The same name, Ansel would have bet his badge, on whatever paper Vasquez had been promised.

"Captain," he said, "the man who offered you amnesty is the man who ordered your team shot."

Vasquez stared at him. "That's not possible. Why would he—"

"Because a sealed manifest proves nothing. A dead cartographer who saw it proves everything. You're not the buyer of your own truth, Captain. You're the last item on the inventory." Ansel pushed off the doorframe. "They don't want the manifest back. They want the only man alive who knows what's in it, walking voluntarily into Solano, where he can have an accident that looks like grief."

Outside, far off across the salt pans, the eastern sky was going the color of old tin. And beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, a smudge of dust that had not been there yesterday.

The fleet. Less than a day out.

Vasquez looked from Ansel to Ilsa to the case on the table, and Ansel watched the arithmetic run behind the man's eyes — three years of survival grinding against six graves — and understood that no argument he could make would be worth as much as what happened next in that skull.

"Captain," Ilsa said, and her voice gentled into something that wasn't gentle at all. "Marta carried you on her back for two miles after the ambush. She died doing it. Don't you dare make her death a signature on Halloran's amnesty."

Vasquez closed his eyes.

And that was when Dez's voice came echoing down from the crow's nest he'd claimed without asking, cracking with a fear that made Ansel's hand go to his rifle before the words even landed:

"Ranger! Ranger, there's riders on the rim — a lot of riders — and they ain't flying any colors I know!"

Ansel crossed to the gash in the hull and looked out.

Along the eastern rim of Devil's Throat, black against the tin-colored dawn, came the advance scouts of the salvage fleet. Hours early. Riding hard.

And leading them, unmistakable even at this distance by the silver catch of his hair, rode a man in a lawyer's coat.

Halloran had come to collect in person.
