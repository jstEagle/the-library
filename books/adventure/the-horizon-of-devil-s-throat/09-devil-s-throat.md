## 9. Devil's Throat

The Meridian Star groaned when the wind came up the canyon, a sound like an old man turning over in his sleep, and Ilsa Reyes stood beneath her ribs and felt the whole wreck shiver through her boots.

They had one tide left. Maybe less. The air in Devil's Throat had gone thin and dry as paper, and the walls of the canyon — red sandstone stacked a thousand feet high, veined with salt — seemed to lean inward like conspirators sharing a secret. Somewhere above them, the last of the wet season's water sat pooled behind a sill of gravel and debris, waiting for permission to come down.

Ansel crouched at the mouth of the wreck, checking the loads on his rifle with the unhurried patience of a man who had done it ten thousand times. "Fleet's two hours out," he said. "Maybe three if their heavy crawler breaks down again. It's been limping since the salt pans."

"You sound almost fond of that crawler."

"I respect anything that refuses to die." He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face she'd been learning to read these past weeks — not softness exactly, but the absence of armor. "You should be on the high shelf with the duplicates."

"I should be here," Ilsa said. "I've been running from this canyon since they buried me in it. I'm done running."

He didn't argue. That was one of the thousand things she loved about him, though she hadn't said it yet, and wasn't sure she would get the chance.

* * *

The survey leader's name was Emory Vance, and he had been alive inside the Meridian Star for eleven months.

Ilsa still couldn't quite believe it. The man who had signed off on every leg of their survey route, who had shaken her hand at the trailhead and told her the badlands were perfectly safe, had crawled into the wreck after the ambush, sealed himself in with the manifest, and lived — eating salvage rations, catching condensation on tarps, watching through a cracked porthole while the government he served sent killers to erase him.

And now, with the fleet's dust plume rising over the southern rim like a second sunrise, he wanted to trade.

"Amnesty," Vance said. He held the manifest case against his chest the way a man holds a child. "Full pardon, reinstatement, back pay. The minister's people offered. All I have to do is hand it over and testify that the crash was pilot error."

"The minister's people shot my team," Ilsa said. Her voice came out very quiet, which surprised her; she had expected shouting. "Dara. Okonkwo. Little Mei, who was twenty-three years old and drew maps so beautiful people framed them. You watched it happen from inside this ship and you didn't fire a shot."

"They had automatic weapons. I had a sidearm and no chance—"

"You had a chance to warn us." She stepped closer. Behind Vance, through the torn hull, Ansel moved without seeming to move, drifting toward the far bulkhead where the light came through in dusty blades. "You let them walk into it because you thought if anyone died, it wouldn't be you. And now you want to sell the only thing those people bought with their lives."

"It's survival," Vance said, and his voice cracked on the word, and for one moment Ilsa saw him clearly: not a villain, just a small man in a large catastrophe, holding the truth like a hot coal he couldn't put down and couldn't carry any farther.

"That's what they're counting on," she said. "Everyone surviving alone."

* * *

The fleet came down the throat of the canyon at midday, and the sound arrived before they did — engines grinding against stone, the crackle of radios, men shouting orders that echoed off the walls and multiplied until it sounded like an army instead of thirty contractors in armored crawlers.

Ilsa lay flat on the high shelf with Ansel beside her, the manifest case between them, six duplicate film canisters wrapped in oilcloth along the ledge. Below, the first crawler nosed into the open ground before the wreck, and men spilled out of it with rifles, fanning wide.

"There," Ansel murmured. He pointed upstream, to where the canyon narrowed to a slot barely wider than a wagon. "That's where the flood comes through. When it comes."

"If it comes."

"It'll come." He said it the way other men said *the sun will rise*. "Dry season's last tide. The boy told you — the old settlements used to ring a bell when the sill broke. Whole canyon fills to the third ledge in twenty minutes. Then it drains, and the silt stays." His jaw tightened. "Their heavies are sitting right in the drain path."

"So we wait?"

"We wait for the water. We don't wait for them to find the shelf." He rolled onto his knees, and Ilsa saw the change come over him — the ranger surfacing, the badge-man, the tracker who had spent twenty years measuring distances. "When they reach the wreck, we go down the far chute. Fast and quiet. You carry the case. I carry everything else."

"And the duplicates?"

"Scattered already. Three settlements, four trusted hands. If we don't walk out, the truth does." He looked at her then, really looked, and the noise of the fleet seemed to fall away. "Ilsa. Whatever happens down there—"

"Don't." Her throat was tight. "Don't say it like a goodbye. Say it like something else or don't say it at all."

For a moment he almost smiled. "Then I'll say it later. Loudly. In front of witnesses."

"Ansel Okafor, are you proposing to make a scene?"

"I've been informed it's the only way to do anything worth doing."

Gunfire started below, and whatever else either of them might have said went unsaid, saved, banked like a fire against the night.

* * *

They went down the chute while the contractors swarmed the wreck, and it nearly worked.

It nearly worked until a sentry on the eastern rocks spotted movement and opened up, and the world became noise and stone-dust and Ansel's arm around her, hauling her behind a fallen slab as rounds sparked off rock an inch from her ear. She heard herself screaming something — his name, maybe, or just rage — and then Ansel rose up over the slab and fired three times, deliberate as a clock, and the sentry stopped shooting.

"He's down, not dead," Ansel said, pulling her onward. "Keep moving."

But more of them were coming, cutting across the canyon floor, and Vance — God help her, Vance — chose that moment to walk out of the wreck with his hands raised, manifest case held high, calling out to the fleet commander like a man hailing a coach he'd paid for.

The commander shot him.

Not immediately — there was a terrible pause, a conversation Ilsa couldn't hear, Vance gesturing with the case, offering it — and then a single rifle crack, and the survey leader folded onto the sand with the truth spilling from its broken case around him like shed feathers.

"No!" Ilsa was moving before she decided to, scrambling down the talus, because the manifest — the original, the only official copy, the thing Dara and Okonkwo and Mei had died carrying — was lying in the open between two armies.

She got halfway there when the canyon spoke.

It began as a sound under the sound, a deep subterranean groan, and then the bell — the old settlement bell wired to the sill upstream, which the boy had sworn still hung there — rang once, wild and cracked, and every soul in Devil's Throat looked up at the same instant.

The water came down the slot canyon like the end of the world.

Ilsa had imagined floods. She had studied flood plains, drawn their contours, taught junior cartographers to mark them in blue. Nothing had prepared her for the wall of it — brown and white-capped and roaring, carrying boulders the size of houses, filling the narrows in seconds, swallowing the crawlers' heavy transport where it sat in the drain path like toys left in a gutter. Men ran. Some made the ledges. Some didn't. The commander's crawler turned ponderously for high ground and the water took it broadside and rolled it, once, twice, and pinned it in new-laid silt up to its turret.

Ilsa reached the case. Water swirled around her knees, then her waist, cold and shocking, dragging at her. A hand closed on her arm — Ansel, chest-deep beside her, one arm locked through a loop of cable anchored to the wreck's landing strut.

"Hold on to me!" he roared over the roar.

"I have the manifest!"

"Hold on to *me*, Ilsa!"

And she did — she dropped the case into the crook of her elbow and wrapped her free arm around his neck and the flood lifted them both and the cable sang taut and held, and for a long minute the world was nothing but water and the two of them and the stubborn steel wire some dead engineer had strung eleven months ago.

When the water drained, the canyon floor had been remade. Silt lay smooth and gray as poured concrete, hip-deep in places, and the fleet's heavy equipment slept beneath it, entombed for the season, maybe forever. The survivors of the salvage crews clung to the ledges, weaponless in every way that mattered, watching the two of them wade ashore through the settling mud.

Nobody fired. There didn't seem to be any point anymore.

* * *

They burned the official copy that evening on the third ledge, where the wind funneled clean through the canyon mouth.

Ansel fed the documents into the flame page by page — the manifest, the shipping codes, the minister's signatures, the whole rotten ledger of sold water rights — and Ilsa watched the truth turn to light and go up. It should have felt like loss. It felt like opening a window.

"The duplicates are already out there," Ansel said. "Old Marta at Salt Well has hers. The ferryman at Coyote Landing. The schoolteacher in Dry Fork, who I happen to know reads everything twice and tells everyone about it." Almost a smile. "By the time the government figures out what happened here, there'll be more copies than they can burn and more witnesses than they can buy."

"And the investigation stalls. No evidence. Just rumors spreading town to town like rain."

"Rumors with signatures attached." He watched the last page catch. "The desert keeps what the cities lose. Always has."

Below them, the salvage survivors were picking through their buried convoy like mourners at a graveside. Somewhere among the ledges, the boy — the thief Ansel had freed a lifetime ago, a hundred miles ago — was already climbing away south with a canister strapped to his back, grinning like the scamp he was, carrying the truth to settlements even Ilsa couldn't name.

Ilsa leaned into Ansel's shoulder. The canyon walls glowed rust-red in the dying light, and the Meridian Star stood silent above the silt line, her long watch finally over.

"You said earlier," Ilsa said. "Something you'd say later. In front of witnesses."

Ansel looked around at the empty ledge, the settling dust, the vast indifferent sky. "Witnesses appear to be scarce."

"I witnessed everything."

"Well then." He turned to her, mud-streaked and smoke-smelling and calmer than any man had a right to be at the end of the world, and took her hand. "Ilsa Reyes. I have measured my whole life in miles. I'd like to start measuring it in mornings. Yours, specifically, if you'll allow it."

She laughed — actually laughed, here at the bottom of Devil's Throat with a government fleet entombed in silt below and a burned conspiracy rising as smoke above — and pulled him close, and kissed him while the wind carried the ashes east toward all those waiting towns.

Above the canyon rim, unseen by either of them, a single rider on a fast horse turned north toward the capital, carrying word that the manifest was destroyed — and that the woman who could testify to everything in it was still alive.
