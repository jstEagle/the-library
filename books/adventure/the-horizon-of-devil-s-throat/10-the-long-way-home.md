## 10. The Long Way Home

The truth traveled the way water did in the desert: slowly, invisibly, and with more power than anyone gave it credit for.

Ansel heard it first in Perdido Wells, three weeks after the tide took the fleet's machines and the dry season sealed Devil's Throat like a book snapping shut. He was drinking coffee that tasted like it had been strained through a boot when a drover at the next table read aloud from a hand-copied broadsheet — no printer's mark, no date, just the manifest's numbers laid out plain, with the minister's name attached to them like a brand on a steer.

"Water rights," the drover said, shaking his head. "Sold out from under half the territory. And they killed a survey team to bury it."

"Allegedly," said the café owner, in the tone of a man who wanted very much to keep his roof.

"Sure," the drover agreed, and read the numbers again, louder.

By the time Ansel paid his tab, the drover had copied the figures onto a scrap of his own, and Ansel understood he was watching the thing he and Ilsa had set loose in the canyon doing exactly what they'd hoped. It wasn't in the papers. The territorial gazette ran a stern editorial about *unsubstantiated rumor and seditious pamphleteering*, which, in Ansel's experience, was what the truth looked like when it was winning.

The minister's investigation, such as it was, stalled in full public view. A commission was formed. The commission announced it could not proceed without the original manifest, which had, regrettably, been destroyed in a canyon fire of unknown origin. Witnesses were summoned. The witnesses were drovers, well-diggers, ferry operators, a slot-canyon guide of dubious legal standing who gave his testimony while eating a meat pie and enjoying himself enormously. Every settlement from Perdido Wells to the Salt Coast had its own copy of the numbers now, and every copy had been copied, and the copies had been copied, until the manifest existed not as a document but as a fact — and facts, unlike documents, could not be burned.

"They can't arrest a whole desert," Ilsa had said, the night they scattered the last duplicates. She'd been sitting on the porch of a way station, ink-stained to the elbow, redrawing the Throat's channels from memory because the official maps were lies too. "They spent twenty years making sure nobody counted the water. Now everybody's counting."

* * *

Ansel turned in his badge on a Tuesday, because it seemed like the kind of thing that should be done on a day with a name.

The district office in Cielo Rojo was a converted rail depot that smelled of lamp oil and old paperwork, and the clerk behind the desk — a young man named Prewitt who had never been farther from the depot than the water tower — looked at the star Ansel set on the counter the way a man looks at a live scorpion.

"You're resigning?"

"I'm returning it," Ansel said. "It was never really mine. It was a loan against my better judgment."

Prewitt consulted a form. "Reason for separation?"

Ansel considered the question. Twenty years of reasons queued up: the survey team, the boy he'd let go, the man he'd killed at the outpost, the manifest burning orange against the canyon walls, Ilsa's face in the gunfire, lit like a lantern. He could have filled out the form in a ledger's worth of ink.

"Retirement," he said.

"You're not old enough to retire."

"I'm old enough to be tired."

Prewitt stamped the form anyway, because clerks everywhere eventually surrender to the inevitable, and slid a receipt across the counter. Ansel looked at it a long moment. Twenty years, reduced to a line item. He'd measured all of it in miles — so many miles to the thief, so many to the wreck, so many back. He'd known the distance between every water hole in the territory better than he'd known his own face. And now the badge was gone and the miles were just miles, and he found he had no idea how far it was to tomorrow, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like warmth.

Outside, Ilsa was leaning against the fence with two horses and an expression he'd learned to read the way he'd once read cloud banks.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Did you measure the office?"

"Twice," he said. "Forty-one feet by thirty. The clerk's desk is crooked."

"And?"

"And I'm never doing it again."

She smiled, and handed him his reins, and they rode east out of Cielo Rojo while the sun was still deciding whether to commit to the morning.

* * *

They took the long way, which was to say they took every way.

East of Cielo Rojo the country softened — the flats gave way to grass, the grass to scrub oak, the scrub oak to country with actual rivers in it, running above ground where a person could see them, which still struck Ansel as faintly indecent. They camped where the water was good and moved when they felt like it, which took some practice. Ansel had spent two decades moving on schedule, covering ground because the ground was there to be covered, and the first week Ilsa had to physically restrain him from breaking camp before dawn.

"The trail doesn't care if we're early," she said, pulling him back down onto the bedroll by the collar.

"I'm not used to—" He gestured at the entire unhurried morning.

"Neither am I. That's the point."

So they learned it together, the way you learn a new language: badly at first, then less badly. They learned to sit through a whole sunset without calculating how many miles of light were left. They learned that a town was worth stopping in even if it had nothing they needed, because the baker in San Rafael made rolls with figs in them and the ferryman at the Río Claro told stories that were forty percent true, which was a good percentage for a ferryman.

In San Rafael, the broadsheet caught up with them again — the numbers, copied and recopied, now with a verse appended that somebody had set to a work song, sung by children skipping rope in the plaza. *Count the water, count the lie, count the minister's alibi.* Ilsa listened with her hand over her mouth, and when the verse ended she said, quietly, "Marta would have liked that. She used to put our survey figures to song when she thought we were falling asleep on the numbers."

Ansel didn't say anything. He'd learned, in the canyon, that grief didn't need company so much as it needed room, and he gave her the room, and after a while she took his hand, and they walked on, and the children sang it again behind them.

* * *

The boy found them in the hill country above the Río Claro, which surprised Ansel not at all.

He came over the rise at dusk, leading a mule loaded with what appeared to be an entire general store, and announced, "I knew I'd catch you. You ride slow now."

"We ride *enjoyably*," Ilsa corrected.

"Sure." The boy — Tomás, though he'd given Ansel four other names over the years, all of them stolen — swung down and helped himself to their coffee without asking, which was traditional. "Everyone in the Throat settlements is talking. The ferry people, the salt-gatherers. They say a ranger burned the manifest." He looked at Ansel with open, insolent delight. "They say he burned it and gave the truth away for free."

"They say right," Ansel said.

"They also say he let a thief go, once, and it turned out to be the smartest lawman move in twenty years." Tomás grinned. "I've been telling that story. I've improved it. In my version you chased me forty miles."

"It was twelve."

"Forty," Tomás said serenely, "is a better story."

He stayed three days, regaling them with news from the canyon country — the fleet's survivors testifying, the commission leaking like a sieve, the minister's allies resigning one by one with statements about *spending time with family* that fooled nobody. On the third morning he repacked his mule, mounted up, and looked down at them from the saddle with a sudden, unguarded seriousness.

"You know what you did, right? You didn't just burn a paper. You made it so the desert knows its own worth." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sincerity, and recovered instantly. "Anyway. If anyone asks, I was never here. I'm a ghost. Ghosts don't share coffee."

He was gone over the rise before Ilsa finished laughing.

* * *

On the last night of autumn, they made camp on a ridge with the whole eastern country laid out below them, going purple at the edges. Ilsa was sketching — she sketched everything now, the way she used to map everything, except these were not maps of anywhere, just shapes and light and the particular way a river held the sky. Ansel sat beside her and did the thing he was still, after months, practicing: nothing.

"You're counting," she said, without looking up.

"I'm not."

"Your lips move when you count."

"I'm estimating the distance to the next town."

"Ansel."

"Eleven miles," he admitted. "Give or take."

She set down the sketch and looked at him, and her eyes in the last light were the exact brown of the canyon walls where he'd found her half-buried and furious and alive. "What's eleven miles?"

"Nothing," he said, and found that it was true. "It's nothing at all. It's just — where the road goes."

"And what's here?"

He considered the camp, the small fire, the coffee, the woman, the ridge, the dark coming on gentle instead of hostile. He considered that he had spent his whole adult life knowing the distance between every two points in the territory and had never once, until this season, known what any of those points were *for*.

"Here is here," he said.

Ilsa laughed — the real one, the one he'd worked months to earn. "That's the worst sentence you've ever said."

"It's the best one I know."

She leaned into him, and the fire cracked, and somewhere below a coyote started up its long complaint to the moon, and Ansel Okafor, who had measured his life in miles, sat on a ridge with a woman who drew the world instead of mapping it, and counted nothing at all.

Tomorrow there would be eleven miles. The day after, who knew. The road went east, and east was not a distance anymore.

It was a direction.

And for the first time in twenty years, he wasn't riding toward something he'd lost.

He was riding toward something he'd found.
