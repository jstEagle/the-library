## 1. The Weight of Silence

The mule went lame three kilometers out from Kessler Station, and Yara Zhou fixed it before the client even noticed.

They'd heard the hitch in the animal's gait—*tok, tok, drag*—and had the pack off and the hoof up before Halvorsen finished his sentence about the weather. A stone bruise, nothing serious. Yara pried it loose with a hoof pick, packed the frog with pine tar and a folded square of felt, wrapped it, and had the load redistributed across the other two animals while the client was still consulting his watch.

"Something wrong?" Halvorsen asked.

"Routine," Yara said. "We're fine."

That word again. *Fine.* It was the load-bearing wall of Yara's whole vocabulary. The trail was fine, the weather was fine, the mule was fine, the water crossing ahead would be fine. Say it enough times in a flat voice and people believed you, because most clients didn't want adventure—they wanted the *feeling* of having survived something, photographed and certified by a guide who never looked rattled.

Yara had spent ten years becoming the person who never looked rattled.

They walked on. The Kessler foothills rose around them in long green shoulders, terraced at the bottom where the valley farmers still grew millet the old way, then forest, then the grey scree that marked the tree line like a receding hairline. Above all of it, half-hidden in cloud as always, Mount Kessler itself—the massif the whole range was named for, though nobody alive had stood on its true summit and come back to say what they'd found there. Yara didn't look at it. That was a habit too, one of many.

"So," Halvorsen said, catching up. He was a mining geologist, mid-fifties, sunburned at the ears, the kind of man who filled silence the way other men filled a pipe. "Your name's Zhou."

"It is."

"Zhou." He let it hang there, testing the weight of it. "Like *Dr.* Zhou."

Yara adjusted a strap that didn't need adjusting. "Common name, back home."

"I don't mean anything by it. I just—" He laughed, a little embarrassed, a little delighted. "My wife has her book. *The Buried Rivers of Asia*. Read it twice. She'd lose her mind if she knew I was trekking with—"

"Is that the fork?" Yara said, pointing ahead. "I thought it was two ridges further. Let me check the map."

There wasn't any ambiguity about the fork. Yara could walk that trail blindfolded, could probably walk it asleep, had in fact once done both after a bad night. But maps gave hands something to do, and hands with something to do kept mouths quiet, and so Yara unfolded the survey sheet against a boulder and studied it with great seriousness while Halvorsen peered over their shoulder and mercifully shut up about Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou, celebrated archaeologist, winner of the Aldring Prize, author of four books, subject of a documentary narrated by someone with a very calm voice, discoverer—or rather, *announcer*—of discoveries that had made a lot of other people very rich.

The consortium's logo had been on the flyleaf of every one of those books. Yara had stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

"This way," Yara said, rolling the map closed. "Two days to the supply cache at Redstone Shelf. Three if the river's up."

Halvorsen nodded and, blessedly, started talking about gravel.

Gravel was safe. Gravel was Yara's favorite topic in the world. They talked about glacial till and sediment load and the way the Kessler watershed carried iron down from the high country until the rivers ran the color of an old coin, and Yara answered in short, competent sentences and thought about nothing at all, which was its own kind of luxury.

This was the trick of the life Yara had built: it fit exactly. Not a hand too large or small. Guiding treks through country where the clients cared about altitude and not lineage. Living eight months of the year above the cloud line in a canvas tent that smelled like woodsmoke and mule sweat, and the other four months in a rented room in Harrow Bend over a boat repair shop, where the landlord called them "skipper" because he'd misheard "Zhou" once and Yara had never corrected him. No surname. No history. A person assembled entirely out of competence and forward motion.

It had taken ten years to build and it weighed almost nothing. That was the point. That was the entire point.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a stand of blue pine above the river, and Yara did everything in the same order they always did it: tent lines taut, kitchen downwind, fire ring rebuilt from stones because the last party through had left a lazy scatter. Clients always remarked on it. *You're so organized*, they said, as if order were a personality instead of a fortress.

Halvorsen sat by the fire eating freeze-dried noodles and talking about his daughter, who was applying to universities and wanted to study "something real, you know? Fieldwork. Like archaeology, maybe." And then, inevitably: "Which reminds me. Your mother—"

"She's not here," Yara said.

Halvorsen blinked. "No. I know. I just meant—"

"You were asking how she is. She's well, as far as I know. We don't correspond much." Yara fed a branch into the fire. "More tea?"

It wasn't quite a lie. Correspondence implied exchange. Twice a year Mei-Lin Zhou sent a card—a postcard, always, never a letter—written in her small precise hand. *Thinking of you. The work continues. Be well.* Signed M.L.Z., as though she were signing off a field report. Yara kept them in a tin under the floorboards of the Harrow Bend room, unopened for months at a time, and told no one they existed.

The truth was more complicated than *we don't correspond much*, but the truth required a fire bigger than this one, and a night longer, and a client who hadn't paid in advance.

"Right," Halvorsen said. "Sorry. None of my business."

"It isn't," Yara agreed, and handed him the tea, and smiled—not warmly, but enough—and Halvorsen smiled back with relief, because clients always preferred a guide who was slightly cold over a guide who was complicated. Cold meant professional. Complicated meant the mountain might do something unexpected.

The fire settled. Somewhere upslope a night bird made its single dry note, over and over, like a question nobody was going to answer. Yara washed the cook pot in the dark with practiced hands and listened to Halvorsen zip himself into his tent, and then sat alone for a while, the way they always did, keeping the last hour of every day for themselves.

Ten years ago tonight—almost to the week—Yara had been seventeen years old, sitting in a white field tent at a site called Nine, holding a pen over a piece of paper that a kind-faced lawyer said was *just a formality, standard procedure, nothing to worry about*. On the table beside the paper had been a photograph of bones. Yara remembered thinking, with the strange clarity teenagers have, that the pen was heavier than it looked.

They had signed it. They had gone home. They had grown up into this: a life measured out in rope lengths and ration weights, in clients who asked questions and trails that didn't, in the daily, disciplined practice of being nobody in particular.

Some nights it worked.

Yara banked the fire, checked the mules' pickets twice, and lay in the tent doorway watching the clouds tear off the summit of Kessler, silvered by a moon that wouldn't rise properly for another hour. The mountain didn't care who their mother was. That was the only honest thing left in Yara's life, and they clung to it the way climbers cling to rock—without sentiment, without gratitude, just grip.

Tomorrow they'd cross the river and make the lower switchbacks. In three days, Redstone Shelf. Then back down, another client, another season. Fine.

* * *

It was on the second morning, at the river crossing, that Yara saw the thing they would think about later, though at the time they almost missed it.

The ford was running high with early melt, brown-green and loud, and while Yara was rigging the rope line for the mules, Halvorsen pointed downstream and shouted something over the water noise. Yara followed his finger.

On the far bank, maybe two hundred meters down, a man was standing at the edge of the willows looking at them. Even at that distance there was something wrong with how he stood—too still, planted, like something set down rather than something standing. He wore a heavy coat despite the season. As Yara watched, he raised one arm—not waving, Yara realized. Checking something. A compass, maybe, or a GPS unit held out flat on his palm.

Then he turned and walked back into the trees, unhurried, and the willows closed behind him.

"Prospector?" Halvorsen shouted.

"Probably." Yara turned back to the rope line. "Country's full of them this time of year."

It was a reasonable answer. It was even mostly true. There *were* prospectors all through the Kessler foothills, lean men and women with pans and claims and stories, and a stranger on a bank was nothing, less than nothing, a footnote the landscape produced hourly.

But Yara's hands, tying off the anchor knot, were not quite steady, and Yara noticed that too, and filed it away in the place where they kept everything they didn't say—the tin under the floorboards, the postcards, the pen heavier than it looked.

The mules crossed fine. The day stayed fine. By evening they had gained the lower switchbacks and the whole green spine of the foothills lay below them in the last light, and Yara cooked rice and answered Halvorsen's questions about gravel until he slept.

And in the willow country behind them, far down by the river, something was moving north along the water—unhurried, methodical, checking something against its palm every few hundred meters—as though following a thread that led, eventually, up.
