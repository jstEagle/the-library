## 2. A Stranger at the Fire

The storm came down the Kessler foothills like something with intent.

Yara had been watching it build all afternoon—a bruise of cloud stacking itself against the high peaks, swallowing the ridgeline above the tree limit—and by the time they'd pitched the client tents at the bend of the Serak River, the light had gone the color of old tin. Four clients on a ten-day supply trek to the Meridian survey station. Easy money. The kind of trek Yara had run forty times without incident, and would run forty more, if the weather cooperated and nobody asked questions.

Nobody had asked questions today. Small mercy.

"Storm's going to sit on us," Yara said, driving the last stake with the heel of their palm. "We're here for the night. Maybe two if it stalls."

The clients—two geologists, a botanist, and a freelance photographer named Okafor-no-relation who'd already made two jokes about the name—grumbled in the way clients grumble when the schedule bends. Yara let them. Complaints were fine. Complaints were surface weather. It was the other kind of question, the quiet kind, the *so what's it like being Dr. Zhou's kid* kind, that Yara had spent a decade learning to route around, the way water routes around stone, patient and total, until the stone is smooth and the water has moved on.

The botanist, a young woman named Priya with a laugh like a dropped pan, had almost gotten there at lunch. *My supervisor did a field season with your mother back in—* and Yara had been already standing, already saying *more tea, anyone*, already moving, and the sentence had died in the air the way those sentences always died. Priya hadn't pushed. They never pushed. People could smell the wall even when they couldn't see it, and most of them were polite enough not to press their faces against it.

Yara checked the food barrels, double-lashed the tarp over the gear cache, and set the cook tent's stove going against the coming cold. The river ran high and gray with glacier milk, loud enough to talk over, which Yara liked. Loud rivers made silence a choice instead of a condition.

The first drops hit at dusk. Then the sky opened.

* * *

It was Priya who heard him first, near midnight, over the hammer of rain on canvas.

"Yara. There's someone out there."

Yara unzipped the cook tent flap and the wind shoved in, wet and cold, and for a moment there was nothing but the storm and the black shapes of the pines bending. Then a light. Not a headlamp—too unsteady, swinging in arcs, the beam jumping off the rain like a struck match. And a shape behind it, moving wrong, lurching from tree to tree the way a man moves when his legs have stopped taking instructions.

"Stay in your tents," Yara said, and stepped out into it.

The rain was a physical thing, a weight on the shoulders, cold enough to burn. Yara clicked on their own lamp and held it high, and the shape stopped, swayed, and shouted something that the wind took most of. Yara caught fragments. *—valley—* and *—green, it's green up there—* and then, closer, as the man staggered into the light: *—they followed me, they'll come, you have to—*

He went down at the edge of the cook fire's glow like a puppet with cut strings.

Yara stood over him for a long moment, rain streaming off their hood, and made the calculation they'd been trained to make and had made a hundred times on a hundred ridges. Stranger. Storm. Alone. No pack that Yara could see, or a pack that had been abandoned somewhere behind him. Altitude sickness, maybe, or exposure, or something worse. A man like this could die in the night ten meters from a warm fire, and that would be his story, not Yara's.

But he was breathing. Yara could see the shallow hitch of it, the chest rising wrong, and there was blood on his temple, black in the lamplight, and his hands—Yara crouched, against every instinct, and turned one of those hands over. The fingers were split and frost-nipped at the tips. He'd been high. He'd been very high, above the ice line, and come down fast and hard, and his body was presenting the bill.

"Damn it," Yara said quietly, to no one, to themselves, to the decade of careful nothing they'd built. Then they got their shoulders under the man's arms and hauled.

He was lighter than he looked. That was the worst part. A big-framed man, sixty maybe, worn down to wire and sinew, and he weighed what a man weighs after the mountain has been eating him for days.

* * *

Yara got him into the cook tent and onto the sleeping pad nearest the stove, and by then the clients had crept out despite instructions, drawn the way people are always drawn to a crisis that isn't theirs.

"Is he—" Priya started.

"He's alive. Give me room."

They gave room. Yara worked. Cut away the ruined shell jacket—it was a good jacket, expedition grade, but torn along one sleeve as if by wire, and stiff with old and new blood—and got a thermal blanket around him. Checked pupils: uneven, sluggish, but responsive. Pulse fast and thin. The head wound was superficial, already clotted, though the scalp always bled like a lie. The real problem was the hands and the confusion and the simple, brutal arithmetic of a body that had burned through its reserves.

"Okafor-no-relation," Yara said. "Boil water. Priya, there's a white kit barrel by the gear cache, red strap, bring it. The rest of you—tents. Now."

This time they went.

The man surfaced while Yara was taping a glucose-and-saline line into a vein that kept trying to slide away. His eyes opened, gray and unfocused, and fixed on Yara's face with an intensity that had nothing to do with recognition.

"Kessler," he rasped. "You know the Kessler."

"Everyone here knows the Kessler."

"No." His hand came up, grabbed Yara's wrist with a grip that had no business existing in that body. "The green valley. Above the ice. Terraces cut into the rock, standing stones as tall as two men. It's real. I saw it. I *stood in it*." His voice cracked and climbed. "They'll say I didn't. They'll say I'm a drunk and a liar and a man who lost his partner in a crevasse because he was careless. But I stood there, and the city is there, and they want it buried—"

"Slow," Yara said. "You've been in the cold. Your brain's lying to you. It does that."

"It's not lying." His grip tightened. "Elias Vann. Prospector, thirty-one years in these ranges. I don't rave, guide. I don't rave."

*Vann.* The name snagged somewhere in the back of Yara's mind, an old notice board in a Harrow Bend outfitter's, a claim dispute in a regional paper years ago. A name from the margins of the range, the kind of man who lived up where Yara worked and never came down except to resupply and argue with bankers.

"Elias," Yara said, "you're hypothermic, dehydrated, and concussed. Whatever you saw up there, your body is charging you interest on it right now. Drink this."

He drank. Half of it went down his chin. But he drank, and Yara held the cup steady and watched the storm hammer the canvas and felt the old, unwelcome warmth move through their chest, the one that had gotten them into this line of work in the first place, back when they'd still believed the work could be simple. You see a man dying. You pull him back. It's not philosophy. It's not a door opening onto anything. It's just what hands do.

Except Yara had spent ten years teaching their hands to do less. To guide, to fix, to feed, to carry—and then to stop. To not ask. To not be asked. Every rescue, every kindness, was a thread, and threads got pulled, and pulled threads unraveled the whole careful garment of a life that nobody looked at twice.

"Your partner," Yara said, because the man had said *lost his partner in a crevasse*, and that was the kind of detail that didn't come from hypoxia. "There's someone else up there?"

Vann's face did something complicated. Grief and fury fighting for the same ground.

"Dez. Dezimbe. Forty years we worked claims together." He stared at the stove. "He didn't come down."

"When?"

"Six days. Maybe seven. I stopped counting." His voice went flat, which was worse than the cracking. "I carried what I could carry and I came down to find someone who'd listen, and every outfitter in Harrow Bend has consortium money in the till, so I walked. Three days down the east side. In the rain."

Yara's hands went still on the tape roll.

The consortium. Meridian Extraction, and its parent, and its parent's parent—the name that lived in the endowment of a dozen university chairs, in the funding line of the greatest highland survey of the century, in the acknowledgments of a famous book Yara had never finished reading. The name Yara had spent a decade building a life that didn't touch.

"Consortium men don't chase prospectors," Yara said, keeping the voice level. "They buy claims. They send lawyers."

"They sent three men with a helicopter and a contract Dez wouldn't sign." Vann's eyes came up and found Yara's, and they were clear now, horribly clear, the fever burning off whatever fog had been between. "They followed me to the pass. I lost them in the scree. But they'll come down the mountain asking questions, guide, and when they ask, you're going to have to decide what kind of person you are. Everyone does, eventually. I decided thirty years too late."

The stove ticked. The rain kept its rhythm on the canvas. Somewhere outside, the river said the only thing it ever said.

"You need sleep," Yara said. "Real sleep. I'll check your hands again in two hours and we'll talk when you can stand up."

Vann almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile, because it was the smile of a man who knew exactly how much time he was negotiating for.

"You're her kid," he said. "The Zhou kid. I saw the resemblance when you turned me over." His eyes were already closing. "You have her hands. Steady. She used to draw with those hands, at Site Nine, before—"

"Sleep," Yara said, harder than they meant to.

But Vann was already going under, sliding down into the black with the words still on his lips, and Yara sat alone in the cook tent with a stranger's blood on their gloves and a name in the air that they had crossed a mountain range and an ocean and ten silent years to stop hearing.

*Site Nine.*

The stove ticked. Yara looked at their hands, at the steady, traitorous hands, and outside the storm began, at last, to ease.

Above the ice line, Vann had said. Terraces. Standing stones. A green valley where nothing green should be.

Yara told themself they'd hand him to the station medic at Meridian in the morning and wash their hands of the whole business.

They told themself that for a long time, sitting by the fire, listening to the man breathe.
