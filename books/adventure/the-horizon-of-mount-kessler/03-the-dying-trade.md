## 3. The Dying Trade

Elias Vann had spent sixty-one years learning exactly how his body would fail him, and it turned out to be the lungs first. Not the heart, which he'd always figured for the dramatic exit—not the knees that had carried him up eleven ranges on three continents—but the lungs, filling slow and quiet like a cup held under a tap, until every breath came in through a straw.

He lay in the corner of Yara Zhou's mess tent and listened to himself drown by inches.

The rain ticked against the canvas. Somewhere outside, a client snored with the deep, untroubled rhythm of a man who had never found anything worth killing for. Vann almost laughed at that, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough went on so long he saw white at the edges of things.

When it finally released him, someone was crouched beside him with a tin cup.

"Small sips," Yara said. "Just wet your mouth."

Vann took the cup. The guide's hands were steady, cracked at the knuckles, nails cut short—the hands of someone who did everything themselves because trusting other people cost more than labor. He'd watched those hands all evening: pitching his shelter out of the wind, boiling water, checking his pupils with a penlight, doing the arithmetic of altitude sickness versus pneumonia versus the thing in his chest that was neither and both. Competent. Careful. And behind all of it, a wariness Vann recognized because he'd worn the same expression for forty years, ever since a man in a consortium jacket had explained to him, very politely, what happened to prospectors who filed claims without partners.

"You should have left me on the trail," Vann rasped.

"I considered it."

"Honest." He sipped. The water tasted of smoke and iodine. "You're Mei-Lin Zhou's kid."

He felt it land. A stillness in the tent, small but total, like the pause between lightning and thunder. Yara set the kettle down with exaggerated care.

"The clients talk," Vann said. "Down at the trailhead, before we started. Man from Singapore, loud as a foghorn. 'Do you know who guides this trek? The daughter of Dr. Zhou.' He thought it was a selling point." Vann managed something like a smile. "You didn't correct him. Just said the route was steep and asked if his boots were broken in."

"He hadn't broken them in."

"No. He hadn't." Vann's breath caught, held, let go. "That's how I knew you were real. People who are hiding something check whether you've noticed. People who are just tired don't bother."

Yara said nothing. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming now, the monsoon's advance scouts testing the passes two months early or maybe right on time—Vann had lost track of seasons somewhere around the third week above the ice line.

"I'm going to die tonight," he said.

* * *

Yara didn't flinch. That was the first thing. Most people flinched—they looked away, or they rushed to argue, *don't say that, you'll be fine, we'll get you down*, all the comfortable lies that dying men had to spend their last hours pretending to believe. Yara just looked at him, and Vann watched them do the same arithmetic they'd been doing all evening, except now the numbers included him.

"There's a clinic at Kessler Station," they said. "Two days down. If we carry you—"

"If you carry me, you lose the clients' window over the pass, and they paid for the pass, not for an old man's corpse." He waved the cup. "And I'd be dead by the glacier anyway. I know the sound my chest makes. I've heard it before, in a man named Okonkwo, at six thousand meters in the Talus Range. We got him down. It didn't matter."

"You could try."

"I could." Vann let his head rest back against the pack they'd wedged under it. "But here's the thing about trying, guide. Trying takes all your strength, and I've got one night's worth left, and I intend to spend it on something better than comfort."

He'd rehearsed this speech for three days, actually—since the fever came, since he'd understood the blood in his spit wasn't from the dry air. Rehearsed it lying in the rocks above the river where his partner had died, rehearsed it stumbling down the goat track with his vision going gray at the edges, rehearsed it through the long afternoon while Yara Zhou worked over him with the impersonal tenderness of someone who patched strangers because it was easier than being known by them. And now, with the hour finally on him, the speech fell apart in his mouth like wet paper.

So he told it plain instead.

"Forty years ago I was a surveyor's assistant. Good job. Clean work. The Meridian Consortium had contracts all through these ranges—minerals, mostly, but they liked having archaeologists on retainer because heritage surveys made the permits go smoother. Dr. Zhou ran the heritage side. Brilliant woman. Everyone said so."

"I know what everyone said."

Vann nodded slowly. "Then you know she was good. Genuinely good. That's what nobody understands about how these things work—it's never the frauds who get used. Frauds get caught. It's the honest ones. You put an honest person's name on a report and the report becomes unimpeachable, and then the company owns the name, and the name owns the truth, and everybody gets to keep their integrity because the integrity is doing the damage for them."

"You're talking about my mother."

"I'm talking about Site Nine," Vann said, and watched the name hit like a dropped stone.

* * *

The tent flap moved once in the wind. Yara had gone very still, the way people go still when a sound from childhood comes back to them—a voice calling from another room, a door closing in a house they no longer lived in.

"You were there," Yara said. Not a question.

"I was nineteen. Junior man on the ground team, carrying stakes and flagging tape." Vann closed his eyes and the mountain came back to him whole: the wind combing the grass, the smell of thawing earth, the morning light coming up the valley like something poured. "We were cutting a survey line along the eastern terrace when Chandra—my crew chief—stopped walking. There was a depression in the ground, old stonework under the sod. We flagged it. Standard procedure. Two days later the heritage team arrived, and two days after that, the site was sealed, and two weeks after that, the permits came through and the bulldozers started, and the sealed site was graded into a haul road before anyone outside the company ever saw a photograph."

"They buried it."

"They paved it," Vann said. "There's a difference. Burial is what you do to hide a thing. Paving is what you do to make sure nobody ever thinks to look. And the reports—" He coughed, rode it out, kept going because he had to, because the night was short and the story was long. "The reports were clean. Signed, peer-reviewed, archived. Dr. Zhou's signature on the cultural assessment. No significant deposits. No human remains. I stood twenty meters from her when she signed it. She wasn't looking at the paper. She was looking at the man from the consortium who was looking at her, and I remember thinking, even at nineteen, even knowing nothing—that's not a woman signing a document. That's a woman being shown the price of not signing it."

"Stop." Yara's voice came out flat and hard. "Whatever you think you—"

"I know what I saw." Vann opened his eyes. "I've had forty years to doubt it and I can't. Can you?"

Silence. Rain. The stove ticking as it cooled.

"I was a child," Yara said finally.

"Yes. And they made you sign something anyway. Didn't they."

The stillness again—but different now, cracked open, and Vann felt a terrible pity mixed with a terrible relief, because he had carried this alone for four decades and here, of all the tents on all the mountains, was the one person alive who already knew the shape of it.

"I'm not here to judge your mother," he said. "She might be guilty. She might be a hostage. I genuinely don't know, and I've stopped pretending the difference matters to what happens next. What matters is this."

He reached under the blanket, fingers finding the oilskin packet he'd slept with against his ribs since the river, since Chandra's grandson had pressed it into his hands in Harrow Bend with tears running down his face. His hands shook getting it free. Everything shook now. But the packet came out whole, tied with waxed cord, dense as a brick.

"What matters is that the paving didn't work."

* * *

He untied the cord with clumsy fingers and let Yara see what was inside without surrendering it yet. Photographs, film negatives in glassine sleeves, a survey notebook swollen with damp, and folded at the center, a map drawn in his own hand on drafting film—forty years of a secret rendered in ink and contour lines.

"After the grading, I stole what I could. Original contact prints from the site office. Chandra's field notes—he kept a second set, off the books, because he was smarter than me and knew someday it would matter. Then the company transferred me to the far side of the world and I spent thirty years telling myself I was done, I was safe, I'd done enough by surviving." His laugh was a ruin. "You know what survival is, guide? Survival is complicity with a slower heartbeat."

"And then?" Yara's voice had changed. Quieter. The wariness still there, but underneath it something awake.

"And then three years ago a dam project up-valley required a new cut, and the cut exposed the eastern terrace again, deeper this time. Terracing walls. Standing stones. Charcoal layers with bone in them, and not old bone—colonial-era bone, musket balls among it. A massacre, written in the ground, at the exact coordinates my map says the consortium paved over. And the consortium's own response was to buy the survey, seal the cut, and start asking around for anyone from the original crew who might still be talking." Vann met Yara's eyes. "They found Chandra first. In March. River accident, they said. He couldn't swim, they said, though he grew up on the water. Last month they found his grandson's shop burned, and the boy handed me this packet at his father's grave and told me to run."

"So run."

"I am." Vann gestured at his own chest, at the whole ruined apparatus of him. "This is running. This is as far as I get." He pulled a breath that cost him visibly. "There's a fixer. Corrigan. You may meet him; he's thorough. He doesn't want the photographs destroyed, understand—that would be admission. He wants them acquired. Filed. Made property. Because a truth the company owns is a truth that never happened, and a truth in a court archive is a truth that takes twenty years and three appeals to happen, and by then everyone who remembers the valley will be dead of natural causes like me."

The rain hammered. Vann held out the packet, and his arm trembled with the weight of it, which was ridiculous, because it weighed less than a kilo and more than anything he'd ever lifted.

"I can't take it up myself. So I'm trading. That's the only kind of deal I trust—one where both sides give something they can't afford to lose." He swallowed. "Here's my side. The map, the negatives, the notes, everything I have. Forty years of it. And here's what I want in return, and it isn't money, so don't insult us both by offering."

"Tell me."

"Take it up. Not down—up. Above the ice line, into the green valley at the head of the range, the place on the map marked with the old pre-colonial name. There's a chamber in the great hall, behind the third standing stone, where the wall paintings run floor to ceiling. The whole history is on that wall—who built the terraces, who came with muskets, what was done in the winter of the occupation. My photographs prove the company knew. The wall proves what they knew. Together they're not a claim, they're a verdict." His voice frayed. "Put the evidence where it belongs, with the dead, documented properly, witnessed—and then get it out by any channel Corrigan can't buy, before the monsoon seals the passes and buys him the time he needs. Don't sell it. Don't trade it. Don't hand it to any man in a consortium jacket, no matter what he offers, no matter whose name he drops." A breath. "Especially then."

* * *

Yara took the packet.

Not immediately—first they sat with it, and Vann watched them weigh it the way he'd watched climbers weigh a traverse, reading the exposure, counting the holds, deciding whether the fall was survivable. He respected that. He'd have distrusted a yes that came fast.

"If I take this," Yara said, "I stop being invisible. You understand that. Whatever life I've built—"

"It's a good life," Vann said. "I've watched you build it for two days. Quiet routes, quiet clients, competence as camouflage. I did the same thing for thirty years. And I'm here to tell you how it ends: it ends with a burned shop in Harrow Bend and a boy crying at his father's grave, because the people we hid from never stopped looking, they were just waiting for the debt to come due." He pushed the packet the last inch. "Invisibility isn't safety, guide. It's just a longer fuse."

Yara's jaw worked. Outside, a client stirred in his sleep and muttered, and the rain filled the silence like a held note.

"Say it as an oath," Vann said. "Not to me. To the valley. I won't be able to check up on you, and frankly I'll be past caring, but oaths aren't for the oath-keeper's benefit either. They're for the 3 a.m. hours on some glacier when quitting would be easier. You'll want something harder than my memory to hold onto."

Yara looked at the packet a long moment. Then they placed their hand flat on the oilskin, the way you'd steady a wounded bird, and spoke to the tent, to the rain, to the dark shoulder of the mountain beyond it.

"I'll take it up. I won't sell it. I won't give it to Corrigan or any man wearing the consortium's interests. I'll put it where it belongs and bring it out before the passes close." A pause. "I can't promise I'll survive it."

"Nobody can promise that," Vann said. "That's what makes it an oath instead of a contract."

His breath was failing faster now; he could feel the edges graying in. He made himself stay in the room for the last part, because the last part mattered.

"Chandra's grandson is named Devi. He's in Harrow Bend, working the docks. When this is done—if it's done—someone should tell him it meant something. Not the newspapers. Him." Vann's eyes were closing whether he wished it or not. "And guide. One more thing. The map has marks on it you'll recognize. Survey marks. From the old heritage grids. When you find them, don't look away, and don't forgive too fast, and don't damn too fast either. Just follow them up. They go exactly where I told you they go."

He felt the packet leave his hand and heard it settle into Yara's pack, zipped deep, and that sound—the small zip of a tent pocket closing over forty years of fear—was the finest sound of his life, finer than any summit, and he let go of the day holding onto it.

The rain went on. The stove ticked. Elias Vann breathed through a straw toward morning, and did not quite reach it, and did not need to.

* * *

An hour before dawn, half-dreaming, he heard Yara moving quietly in the main tent: the scratch of a pencil, the rustle of drafting film, a low voice reading coordinates aloud like a prayer or a curse. Reading the survey marks. Recognizing them.

Good, Vann thought, from very far away. Now it's yours. Carry it higher than I could.

Outside, far below in the black valley, unseen, a single lamp wound up the switchback trail toward camp, patient as interest accruing, and the rain began to turn to snow.
