## 4. What the Map Shows

Yara buried Elias Vann at dawn, in the thin soil above the tree line where the ground was soft enough to dig and hard enough to argue about.

There wasn't much ceremony available. Nadia hadn't arrived yet—wouldn't arrive for days, if the telegram even reached Harrow Bend before she did—and Bektour had gone down-valley with the supply trek, so it was just Yara and a shovel and a body that weighed less than it should have, because Vann had been starving for weeks before the mountain finished him.

Yara said the words anyway. Not prayers; Yara didn't have any. Just the facts, spoken aloud into the cold morning air, because someone should hear them even if no one did.

"Elias Vann. Prospector. He found something worth killing for, and he trusted the wrong person to carry it."

The wind took the words east, toward Kessler's white shoulder. Yara filled the grave, stacked stones over it because there were no trees this high for a marker, and went back inside to look at the map.

* * *

The tent was warm enough once the stove caught. Yara boiled water they didn't drink and spread Vann's map across the folding table, weighting the corners with a tin cup, a carabiner, a stone from the grave.

It was beautiful work. That was the first thing. People assumed prospectors were illiterate vandals with rock hammers, but Vann's hand was fine and sure—a surveyor's hand, or close to it. The map covered the entire western face of the Kessler massif on oilskin, drawn in ink that hadn't faded, annotated in three colors of pencil. Elevations along every ridge. Water sources marked with small careful symbols. Notes in a cramped, slanted script: *ice bridge unsafe after June*, *scree—go wide left*, *here I turned back, 1987.*

Thirty-seven years of returning. Yara felt that in the chest, somewhere under the ribs. A man who kept coming back to a mountain for nearly four decades wasn't looking for gold.

The route itself started where all routes started—at the lower moraine, past the old waystation, up through the pass the local porters called the Pass of Teeth for reasons the map made graphically clear. But beyond the pass, Vann's line departed from anything Yara knew. It cut west along an exposed traverse beneath a hanging glacier, dropped through a notch Yara had never seen marked on any map, and descended—descended, which was wrong, everything about the Kessler Range said you climbed until you couldn't and then you came home—into a valley that Vann had shaded green.

Not colored. Shaded, in fine crosshatched strokes, like he was trying to render something he'd only seen once and couldn't stop seeing.

Above the valley, in letters smaller than the rest of the map's labels, as if he'd been embarrassed by them: **THE CITY**.

Yara sat back. The stove ticked. Outside, a raven said something ugly to another raven and got an answer.

"Okay," Yara said quietly, to the map, to Vann, to nobody. "Show me."

And the map did. Because that was the second thing—the thing that turned Yara's hands cold on a warm morning, the thing that made them get up twice to check the tent flap was buttoned against nothing.

Vann hadn't just drawn the route. He'd drawn the *survey marks*. Little triangles with numbers inside them, placed along his route at intervals, the way a formal expedition would place them. Triangulation stations. Benchmarks. And beside each one, a code.

Yara knew those codes.

They'd grown up with those codes, in a house where dinner conversation was stratigraphy and grant deadlines, where their mother's field notes lived in acid-free boxes in the garage and a teenage Yara had read them the way other kids read comic books—obsessively, cover to cover, hiding them under the bed. Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou's early surveys of the Kessler foothills. Before Site Nine. Before everything. Her station codes had followed a convention set by the Halvern Consortium's mapping division, because the consortium funded her first three seasons and insisted on standardization: a letter for the survey block, a number for the station, a dash-code for elevation datum.

K-14-2200. K-14-2380. K-15-1910.

There they were on Vann's map. In the same hand as everything else, but older—the pencil grayer, the pressure lighter. Stations Vann had found already standing when he first came through in the eighties. Survey monuments, planted decades before him, on a route leading to a pre-colonial city that officially did not exist.

Yara pulled the camp ledger over and, on the back page, wrote out the codes from memory. Their mother's codes. They could see her handwriting as they wrote—she'd numbered everything twice, once in pencil and once over the pencil in pen, because she trusted nothing to last. Yara had inherited exactly none of that certainty. They'd built a whole life on the assumption that things don't last, that erasure is not only possible but easy, that you can walk far enough into the mountains and become no one.

The codes matched. All eleven of them.

Which meant one of two things. Either Vann had somehow gotten hold of Mei-Lin Zhou's private survey data—which meant he'd known who Yara was before he ever staggered into their camp—or the consortium had surveyed the western face themselves, forty years ago, found what Vann spent his life finding, and buried the maps.

Yara thought about their mother's third season. The season that ended early. The season she never talked about, the one where the funding moved from archaeology to something called "resource assessment," and the field notes from those months were missing from the boxes—not lost, missing, gaps in the numbering that Yara had noticed at sixteen and been told, in a voice they'd never heard their mother use before or since, *some things are not yours to find.*

They'd thought she was protecting them.

Sitting in a freezing tent with a dead man's map, Yara understood, with the slow horrible clarity of a rope coming tight, that she might have been protecting herself. Or being protected. That the silence agreement Yara had signed at seventeen—Site Nine, the bones, the men in clean boots who watched while a lawyer explained what signing meant—that agreement hadn't been an anomaly. It had been policy. The consortium hadn't stumbled onto inconvenient history. They'd been managing it for two generations, the way you manage water seepage behind a dam wall, patiently, patch by patch.

And now the dam wall was a dead prospector's oilskin map, sitting on a folding table in a guide's tent, and Yara Zhou—who had spent ten years becoming unfindable—was holding it.

They laughed. It came out wrong, more breath than sound, and they put their head in their hands and let the stove tick and the ravens argue and did not look at the map for a long time.

Then they looked at it again, because that was the job now. Vann had bought their promise with his last hours, and whatever else Yara was—coward, ghost, complicit daughter—they did not break oaths to dead men. They'd learned what that cost from watching other people pay it.

* * *

The fixer arrived at four in the afternoon, which told Yara two things before he said a word.

First: he'd come up from Harrow Bend today, which meant he'd left this morning at the latest, which meant word of Vann's death had reached the lowlands within hours of it happening. Someone had been watching the trail.

Second: he'd come alone, on foot, dressed like a day hiker in gear that cost more than Yara's entire outfitting operation. Fixers dressed down when they wanted to seem harmless. This one hadn't bothered to dress down well. His boots were too clean. Nobody's boots are too clean after the climb from Harrow Bend unless they changed them at the waystation, and the waystation was a ruin.

Yara saw him coming from two hundred meters and had time to decide who to be. That was the strange gift of the mountains—you always got a few minutes to choose your self. Ten years ago, Yara Zhou the invisible guide would have doused the stove, taken the clients' valuables, and melted into the scree fields until the stranger passed. There were a dozen hollows within running distance. Yara knew every one.

Instead they stayed at the stove, laid the map back in its oilskin sleeve, slid the sleeve under their sleeping pad, and put the kettle on for two.

He came up the last stretch without hurrying, which took a certain kind of confidence at this altitude. He was maybe fifty-five, gray at the temples, with the compact build of a man who'd been athletic on purpose rather than by trade. His face was pleasant the way a hotel lobby is pleasant. When he smiled, the smile arrived slightly before the rest of his expression, like a scout sent ahead.

"Ms.—?" He paused delicately on the title, offering Yara the chance to fill in whatever name they preferred.

"Okafor," Yara said. Wren Okafor, licensed trekking guide, registered in the district office in Harrow Bend, no relations of note. It was a good name. It had held for ten years. They watched it leave their mouth and wondered if it would come back.

"Wren." He tried it out, tasting it, and shook his head slightly like a man declining a dish. "That's a lovely name. Very convincing." He accepted the tea with both hands around the tin cup, a gesture of practiced humility. "My name is Corrigan. I'm looking for a man named Elias Vann. Older gentleman. American. He'd have been—well, frankly, he'd have looked half-dead."

"He was half-dead," Yara said. "You're a week late for most of it."

Corrigan's eyes did a small, efficient inventory of the camp while his face stayed warm and sympathetic. The extra mug drying on a stone. The fresh-turned earth visible past the tent wall, if you knew to look—he knew to look; his gaze touched the cairn for half a second and moved on. The single sleeping bag. The ledger on the table, back page down.

"I'm sorry to hear that," he said, and sounded like he meant it, and probably did, the way a man means it when a piece of machinery he'd budgeted for finally fails. "Heart?"

"Exposure. Age. A life lived outdoors by himself." Yara sipped their own tea. "I found him on the trail below the pass. He lasted two days. I buried him yesterday morning. You're welcome to see the grave. I can show you where."

"That won't be necessary." Corrigan drank his tea like a man humoring a ritual. "Did he say anything? At the end? Men like Vann usually want to talk, near the end. Unburden themselves." Another smile, the scout arriving first. "It would be a kindness, letting the family know his last words. He has family, you know. A granddaughter. Estranged, but blood is blood. She'll want to know he didn't die alone with his secrets."

*With his secrets.* The phrase landed precisely where it was aimed.

"He talked about the mountain," Yara said. "Green valleys. Gold. Delirium stuff. I wrote down what I could make sense of, for the coroner's office in Harrow Bend. You can read my report there, if you file a request. That's the process."

"The process." Corrigan nodded slowly, appreciating it. "Yes. I've heard there is one." He set the cup down on the flat stone Yara had offered, carefully, aligning the handle. "Ms. Okafor—Wren—I'm going to be direct with you, because I think we'll respect each other better that way, and because you strike me as a person who dislikes being managed. Which I understand. Managed people make poor allies and worse enemies."

"Is that what we're deciding to be?"

"I haven't decided anything yet. I'm a very slow decision-maker." He looked out at the mountain then, genuinely, and for a moment the lobby-smile fell away and something older and tireder looked out instead. "Forty years ago I carried packs on this range for the same people I work for now. I know what it does to a person, loving a place that's already spoken for. So here's my direct question, and please consider that lying to me costs you nothing today and a great deal later, and I will know, and I don't enjoy knowing."

He turned back.

"Did Elias Vann give you anything? A document. A map. Anything in his possession besides clothes and regret."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above them, snow let go of a cornice with a sound like a held breath released, and neither of them looked up, both of them listening to it fall.

"No," Yara said.

Corrigan studied them for a long moment. Whatever he was reading, he read it thoroughly—face, hands, the set of the shoulders, the sleeping pad that was lumpy in a way sleeping pads shouldn't be. Then he sighed, stood, brushed off his immaculate trousers, and produced a card from his breast pocket, laying it on the table between them with two fingers, like a dealer showing one card of many.

"When you reconsider—and I say *when*, not *if*, because men like Vann don't carry things up mountains for forty years and give them away for a burial—you can reach me at the number on that card, any hour. I'll be in Harrow Bend through the end of the month. After that, I'm afraid, the weather closes everything, and matters tend to resolve themselves." He walked three steps toward the trail, then stopped, and spoke without turning around, his voice carrying easily in the thin air.

"Oh—one more thing. Small world, these mountains. You remind me of someone. A woman I met once, years ago, at a site in the eastern foothills. Brilliant woman. Zhou, her name was. Mei-Lin Zhou." Now he did turn, and the smile was fully present, and it was not kind. "She had your exact way of standing. Like the ground owed her an apology. Funny, the faces you keep."

He went down the trail without hurrying, taking his time on the switchbacks, a man entirely certain that no one was going to push him.

Yara stood in the doorway of the tent until he was a speck, then a memory, then gone. Then they dug the map out from under the sleeping pad, and sat down, and looked at the green-shaded valley for a long time.

Invisible for ten years, and undone in a single morning by a man who'd recognized their posture. Their mother's posture. Yara almost laughed again. All those years of the borrowed name, the scrubbed records, the careful ordinariness—and it was the body that betrayed them, the body they'd inherited, the one thing they couldn't burn.

They packed that night. Not the full kit—the fast kit, the one that said *moving soon and light*. If Corrigan believed them, they had until the end of the month. If he didn't believe them—and he didn't; he'd looked at the sleeping pad, he'd looked right at it—they had less. Maybe less than the telegram took to reach Harrow Bend. Maybe less than it took for a granddaughter to read her grandfather's name in a coroner's log and start asking who found him.

Yara blew out the stove and lay in the dark doing arithmetic: days to landfall, days to Harrow Bend, days until the consortium decided the polite approach had failed and sent up men whose boots were dirty because they'd actually walked in them.

One number refused to stay solved: *Nadia Vann.* Blood is blood, Corrigan had said, wearing it like a threat.

By midnight Yara had written the telegram three times. By dawn they'd burned two drafts in the stove and kept the shortest one, and when the mail runner came through two days later, bound down-valley with the season's last letters before the rains, Yara handed it over and watched it go and understood, standing there in the wind with empty hands, that the quiet life was over—that it had probably been over since a dying man grabbed their wrist and made them swear—and that everything from here forward would be done in front of witnesses.

The telegram read:

**VANN DIED IN MY CAMP. HE LEFT SOMETHING THAT BELONGS TO YOU. COME TO HARROW BEND, THE ANCHOR BAR, TEN DAYS. COME ALONE. —W**

Yara broke camp the same morning and headed down, taking the long way, watching their backtrail the whole descent.

Behind them, high on the white shoulder of Kessler, the hanging glacier calved once, softly, like something settling in its sleep.
