## 5. The Granddaughter

The telegram came to the Harrow Bend post office on a Tuesday, and Nadia Vann didn't open it until Thursday.

That was the thing about grief she'd learned early: it kept. Like salted fish, like grain in a sealed jar, it would wait for you. You could stack other days on top of it—shifts at the cannery, the landlord's notes slid under the door, the small administrative labor of being alive—and it would sit there underneath, patient as sediment, until you were ready to dig.

She was not ready to dig. She opened it anyway, standing in the queue with the paper knife in her hand, because the envelope had the consortium's letterhead on it and anything with that letterhead in Harrow Bend was either money or trouble, and her family had never once received money.

*REGRET TO INFORM ELIAS VANN DECEASED STOP REMAINS INTERRED KESSLER FOOTHILLS STATION FOUR STOP EFFECTS HELD PENDING CLAIM STOP CONTACT THIS OFFICE.*

She read it three times. The words didn't change. That was the cruelty of telegrams—they gave you no room to negotiate. Her grandfather was dead. His remains were "interred," which meant someone had dug a hole fast and shallow and put him in it before the ground thought better of the arrangement. And his effects—the map, she thought immediately, *the map*, because she'd grown up on stories of that map the way other children grew up on lullabies—were being held by men who filed things.

Nadia folded the telegram into quarters, then eighths, then small enough to disappear into her fist. She walked out of the post office into the flat grey light off the river, and she did not cry. Crying was for later, or never; her family tradition leaned toward never.

Elias Vann had been dead to her for six years already, so this was, technically, a redundancy. He'd made his choice back then—his maps, his mountain, his long absences punctuated by letters full of altitude figures and almost-apologies—and she'd made hers: she stopped writing back. Six years of silence, conducted with the discipline of two people who were related and knew it. And now one of them was beyond apology, which meant the silence could finally be resolved the only way silences like that ever got resolved: badly.

She went to the consortium office first, because anger is efficient and she wanted to spend hers somewhere useful.

The office occupied a converted warehouse near the docks, all plate glass and new paint, the company name stenciled in letters so large they seemed embarrassed by the building around them. Inside, a clerk with a face like a closed ledger looked up from behind a counter of varnished pine.

"I'm here about Elias Vann's effects."

"Name?"

"Nadia Vann. I'm his granddaughter."

The clerk consulted something beneath the counter. When he looked up again there was a new quality in his expression—not sympathy exactly, but the careful blankness of a man who has been instructed to give away nothing and is proud of how well he's doing it.

"Next of kin has been determined to be—" he checked again, though he clearly knew it by heart, "—the estate is in probate review. Effects cannot be released at this time."

"What effects are we talking about? Specifically."

"Personal items. Documents." A pause, calibrated. "Survey materials."

"Survey materials," Nadia repeated. "His maps."

"I couldn't speak to specifics."

"You just did."

He smiled without warmth and told her, with great courtesy, that she was welcome to submit a claim through proper channels. Proper channels, she understood, was the consortium's word for a drawer they closed slowly enough for you to watch.

She walked out onto the dock road and stood looking at the river, brown and patient, carrying its freight of silt and secrets down toward the delta. Behind her, men were loading barges with crates stamped with the same stencil she'd just walked out from under. The whole town ran on those crates. Half of Harrow Bend ate because of them.

Her grandfather had spent forty years telling anyone who'd listen that the consortium was strip-mining the highlands above the ice line, and forty years being laughed at for it, and now they had his maps and he was in a shallow hole at Station Four, and the laughter was over because there was nothing left to laugh at. They'd won. They always won. It was practically their business model.

Except.

Except the telegram had said *effects held pending claim*. Held, not destroyed. Which meant whatever Elias Vann had carried out of those hills was still intact somewhere in a filing cabinet, waiting for a woman with paperwork to come collect it.

Or—

Or it wasn't. Because the telegram was four days old, and Station Four was nine days' walk from anywhere, and in between those two facts there was a gap wide enough for any number of things to happen. Things like prospectors stumbling half-dead into trekking camps. Things like strangers.

She'd heard the story by evening, the way you hear everything in Harrow Bend, which is to say from the dockworkers over cheap beer at the Anchorage, the bar nearest the water where the barge crews drank and nobody asked what was in the crates. A guide named Zhou—Yara Zhou, ran treks up the Kessler foothills, good reputation, kept to themselves—had brought a dying man down from the high country last week. Old prospector. Bad leg, bad lung, bad everything. Died at the medical station two days ago.

"And here's the part," said Denny Okafor, a deckhand who fancied himself the town's newspaper, leaning across the sticky table with the confidence of a man who had never once been wrong about gossip. "Zhou's got the old man's kit. Whole pack. Map books, notebooks, the lot. Carried it down himself rather than let the station boys go through it."

Nadia set down her glass very carefully, because if she didn't set it down carefully she was going to throw it.

"They're at the Anchorage?" she asked. "This Zhou?"

"Room upstairs, far as I know. Been quiet since they got in. Sending telegrams, buying supplies." He grinned. "Why? You know them?"

"No," Nadia said, standing. "But I'm going to."

* * *

The bar smelled of engine oil and spilled beer and decades of both. It was mid-morning, which meant the Anchorage belonged to the dockworkers coming off night shift and the few drinkers too stubborn to call it anything else. Nadia took the stairs at the back, boots loud on boards gone soft with rot, and found the door at the end of the hall marked 4.

She knocked once, hard, and didn't wait.

Yara Zhou was sitting on the bed with a field notebook open on their knees, and they looked up when the door swung open with an expression that suggested this was neither the first nor the worst interruption of their week. They were younger than Nadia expected—or maybe just younger-looking, all sharp angles and deliberate calm, dark hair cut short, hands stained with ink and rope tar. A stranger. Exactly as advertised.

"You're Zhou," Nadia said.

"Yes."

"Nadia Vann. Elias Vann was my grandfather."

Something moved behind Zhou's eyes—recognition, calculation, maybe regret—but their voice stayed level. "I'm sorry for your loss. I was with him at the end. He didn't suffer long."

"He suffered six years of me not writing, so don't apologize for the last two days." She stepped fully inside and shut the door behind her, because what she had to say wasn't for hallways. "You have his pack. His maps. I want them."

Zhou set the notebook aside, unhurried, which infuriated her more than any argument would have. "Sit down, Ms. Vann."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." They studied her for a moment with a gaze that felt like being read aloud from. "Your grandfather asked me to do something with that map. Not to hand it to whoever walked through the door asking."

"I'm not whoever. I'm his blood."

"That's what makes it complicated," Zhou said quietly. "Not less."

The words landed harder than they should have. Nadia crossed her arms and made herself breathe. Outside, a barge horn sounded on the river, low and mournful, like the town clearing its throat.

"Tell me how he died," she said. Because that was the real question, buried under all the others, the one she'd carried since the post office. "Actually tell me. Not the polite version."

Zhou considered her for a long moment. Then they gestured at the chair, and this time she sat.

"He came into my camp above the tree line," they said. "Half-dead. Leg wound gone septic, lungs full of fluid. He'd been walking on it for days—I don't know how many. He talked about a green valley above the ice line. Terraces. Standing stones. A city nobody's surveyed." Their eyes didn't leave hers. "He traded me the map for a promise. Take it up the mountain. Don't sell it. Don't give it to the consortium men who killed his partner."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, glasses clinked; a woman laughed; the ordinary world went on with its noise, indifferent to the fact that Nadia Vann's entire understanding of the last six years had just tilted sideways.

"Killed," she repeated. "His partner."

"Tomas Reyes might know more—he was a surveyor who worked with your grandfather once. But yes. Killed. That's what Elias believed, and Elias didn't believe things lightly."

Nadia looked down at her hands. They were shaking. She clenched them into fists and made them stop, because Vanns didn't shake, Vanns got angry, and anger was a tool and tools didn't tremble.

"When I was twelve," she said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "he tried to show me that valley on a napkin. Drew it right there at the kitchen table while my mother washed dishes and pretended not to listen. Terraces, stones, the whole thing. I laughed at him. Told him he was chasing ghost stories." She looked up. "Six years of silence because I picked my mother's side in an argument I was too young to understand, and now he's dead in a hole at Station Four and some consortium clerk tells me his maps are 'survey materials.'"

"So you want them back."

"I want what's mine."

"It's not yours," Zhou said—not cruelly, but without flinching either. "It's his. And he gave it away. To me. Under oath." They leaned forward slightly. "Here's what I can offer instead. I'm going up that mountain before the monsoon seals the passes. Eleven days, maybe less. I need people who have reasons of their own to be there. If your reason is strong enough, you come with me. You carry the map yourself, you see the valley with your own eyes, and when we get back, you decide what happens to it. Him and me, we made a deal. But you're his granddaughter, and I think—" they paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—I think he'd have wanted you there."

It was, Nadia would think later, a masterful piece of manipulation disguised as honesty, and she resented how well it worked.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: the map was the last conversation she'd ever have with Elias Vann, and it was currently happening without her. Every day it sat in this stranger's room was a day of her grandfather speaking and her not listening, and she had done six years of not listening already, and she was done. Done with silence, done with propriety, done with being the reasonable granddaughter who waited for proper channels.

Proper channels had buried him in a shallow grave.

"I have conditions," she said.

"Of course you do."

"One: I see the map. Tonight. All of it. Two: nobody touches it but me from here to the valley—your oath, not mine, since you're so fond of oaths. Three:" she stood, and the chair scraped back, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "when we find whatever killed his partner, I'm the one who decides what happens next. Not the consortium. Not you. Me."

Zhou regarded her for a moment, and then—there was no other word for it—smiled. Not warmly. With respect, maybe, or recognition. The smile of someone meeting a mirror angled differently.

"Deal," they said.

Nadia nodded once, turned for the door, and had her hand on the handle when Zhou spoke again, quieter.

"For what it's worth—your grandfather talked about you. At the end. Said you argued with him about the napkin drawing." A pause. "Said you were the only Vann who ever checked his math."

She didn't turn around. She stood there with her hand on the cold handle and her throat tight and her eyes burning, and she said, "Then he died knowing I was right about something," and walked out before her voice could crack on the last word.

Downstairs, the bar noise swallowed her. She pushed through the dock road crowd toward the consortium office, toward telegrams and supplies and the eleven-day clock already running, and behind her, upstairs in Room 4 of the Anchorage, Yara Zhou watched the door close and began, methodically, to plan an expedition.
