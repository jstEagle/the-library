## 6. Terms of Ascent

Nadia Vann didn't swing at me, which I counted as progress. She'd been winding up for it since the dockside bar—shoulders coiled, jaw set like her grandfather's survey stakes—and when I said *come with me instead*, the punch died somewhere in her forearms and turned into something worse: a stare.

"Come with you," she repeated. "Up a mountain. With the man who's been sitting on my grandfather's map for three days."

"I've been sitting on his body for three days. The map came later."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No," I said. "It's supposed to be true."

We were in the back room of the Harrow Bend outfitter where I kept my gear between seasons—a room that smelled of rope wax and kerosene and the particular mildew of things stored too long near a river. Nadia stood by the door the way people stand when they haven't decided whether they're staying or leaving, one hand on the frame, weight on her back foot. She had her grandfather's coloring and none of his softness. Elias Vann, half-dead in my camp, had still managed to look apologetic about taking up space. His granddaughter looked like she'd never apologized for anything in her life.

"You buried him," she said.

"There wasn't time to bring him down before the thaw rotted the trail. I marked the cairn. You can have the coordinates."

She flinched. Good, some part of me noted coldly. Bad, another part answered. She was twenty-six, maybe, and she'd just learned her last living relative was a cairn on a ridge, and I was handing her coordinates like a receipt.

"He wrote to me," she said suddenly. "Every year. Birthday, New Year. Same envelope, no return address. I never wrote back." Her hand came off the doorframe. "The last one had a drawing in it. A valley. Green. He'd drawn it like a kid draws—crayon green, impossible green. And underneath he wrote, *I found it, Nadi. I finally found it.*"

"And then he walked up there alone at sixty-eight years old," I said.

"He walked up there because nobody would listen to him. Forty years of being called a crank. My mother wouldn't take his calls. The consortium blacklisted him from every claim office in three provinces." Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "So don't stand there and tell me what he did or didn't do. Tell me what you're going to do."

I told her. All of it—the map, the survey marks that matched field notes I had no business remembering, the fixer named Corrigan who'd already come asking questions with a smile like a folded knife. I told her the monsoon was coming early and the passes would seal, and that if anyone was going to carry Elias Vann's green valley out of the dark, it had to happen in the next three weeks or not at all.

When I finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"You need me," she said. "That's why you're offering this. Not because of grief. Because you need a reason to go that isn't just your own conscience."

I could have lied. I was good at lying; I'd built a whole life out of it. But something about her grandfather's crayon-green valley made lying feel like a debt I couldn't afford.

"Yes," I said. "Both things can be true."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she stuck out her hand—not to shake, but palm-up, flat, an old river-town gesture I hadn't seen since my father's crew. *Terms on the table.*

"The map stays in my sight or mine to carry," she said. "You don't make decisions about my grandfather's mountain without me in the room. And when we find whatever killed him—" her voice steadied, deliberately, "—whoever killed him—I'm there. Front of the line."

"Done," I said, and put my palm against hers.

It was the first contract I'd ever signed that scared me more than the mountain did.

* * *

Tomas Reyes was drinking his reputation away at the Anchorline Hotel, which in Harrow Bend meant he was drinking it away in public, methodically, at eleven in the morning, wearing a shirt that had been ironed sometime during a better decade.

He'd been the best surveyor on the range until the Kessler Mining Consortium needed a contour map of a slope that was going to fail onto a village, and Reyes put the failure line exactly where it was, in writing, with stamps and signatures, and got fired within the month when the report proved inconvenient. The slope failed. Eleven people died. His report surfaced afterward like a body in spring meltwater, and instead of making him a hero it made him a problem—because if Reyes was right, then everyone who'd buried the report was liable, and liability is the one thing money can't dissolve fast enough.

He looked up when I sat down across from him. He didn't ask who I was. Men in his position stop asking; they assume everyone at their table wants something.

"You're Zhou," he said. "The guide. The daughter."

"The guide," I said. "The daughter part is negotiable."

"It's never negotiable. That's the whole trick of it." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the wet wood, precise as everything else about him. "What do you want?"

"A route up the Kessler massif through the eastern passes before landfall. Off every registered trail. You'll need to read terrain nobody's surveyed since the fifties."

"I don't do terrain anymore."

"You do drinking. I watched you do it very competently for four minutes."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition. There's a specific fellowship among people whose competence has been taken from them, and we both knew the handshake.

"They'll have people on the mountain," he said. "If it's what I think it is. The consortium doesn't send one man for nothing."

"One man so far. Corrigan."

Reyes went very still. Then he laughed, short and awful. "Corrigan. God. He was junior counsel on the slope case. He shook my hand at the hearing." He set the glass down and pushed it away, an inch, two inches, like moving a chess piece off a board. "How many days?"

"Eleven to the valley, if the weather holds. Six back, hard."

"And when we get there? What are we carrying out?"

"Evidence," I said. "Of something older than the consortium and worse than the slope. Vann found a city, Tomas. Pre-colonial. Intact. And under it, proof of what happened to the people who built it—and proof the company has known since before I was born."

He stared at me. Then he did something I didn't expect: he took out a small notebook, worn soft at the corners, and clicked a pencil, and the surveyor came back into his face like a lamp coming on in a dead house.

"Eastern passes," he said. "Show me the map."

* * *

Bektour found us, which is how Bektour did everything. We were loading at the trailhead at dawn when he simply appeared at the edge of the light with a framed pack taller than himself and a face that gave away nothing except patience.

He was Kyrgyz by way of three countries and two deportations, thirty years old, and owed more money than his porter's wages would clear in a decade—most of it to the family of a man injured on a consortium haul he'd led through a whiteout because the dispatcher told him to go. The injury wasn't his fault. The debt was assigned to him anyway, the way debts flow downhill to whoever can least afford them. Every guide in Harrow Bend had used him and praised him and paid him late, myself included, and he carried all of it—wages owed, blame assigned, a brother's medical bills—with the same flat endurance he carried loads.

"You want Kessler east side," he said. Not a question. Word travels in porters' circles faster than radio.

"Before landfall. Fast and heavy."

"How heavy?"

"Rope, hardware, camera kit, six weeks food for five. Plus whatever we find that needs carrying out."

His eyes flicked to Nadia, to Reyes, to me, doing the arithmetic porters do without ever writing it down. Five clients' worth of gear, three of whom had clearly never carried anything heavier than a grudge.

"Double rate," he said. "Half now. And when we come down, you write me a letter. On paper. Saying the whiteout run wasn't my fault. I give it to the family. Maybe they stop looking at me like that."

The request landed harder than any price would have. He didn't want money. He wanted to stop being guilty for something he hadn't done—which was, I was beginning to understand, the entire economy of this mountain.

"Half now, letter guaranteed," I said. "And Bektour—if I die up there, the letter's in my jacket, left pocket. Someone will bring it down."

He considered this with the gravity other men reserve for scripture. Then he nodded once, shouldered the pack, and started walking. We followed. It seemed like the correct response to a man like that.

* * *

We made eight miles the first day, which was slow, and I knew why. Nobody trusted anybody yet. Nadia walked ten meters ahead of the group as if distance were a form of vigilance. Reyes navigated out loud, narrating every bearing, partly because he loved it and partly because he needed us to hear that he was still good at something. Bektour carried forty kilos and said nothing, which somehow said the most.

At the fire that night I watched them arrange themselves around the stove—Nadia closest to the flames but facing outward, Reyes cleaning glasses that were already clean, Bektour mending a strap with hands that never stopped working—and I thought: this is not a team. This is four strangers bound by a dead man's crayon drawing and their own private debts, walking into weather that wants to kill them, pursued by a company that definitely does.

But then Nadia asked Reyes, grudgingly, how he'd known the eastern pass existed. And Reyes, surprised to be asked, drew contours in the dirt with a stick, and explained, and forgot to be bitter for almost a full minute. And Bektour looked up from his strap and corrected one of the contours—he'd crossed that shoulder twice in whiteouts—and Reyes actually listened, actually adjusted the line, and nodded at him across the fire like one professional acknowledging another.

Nobody smiled. Nobody declared anything. But the circle around the stove had closed by half a meter, and in the mountains that's how families start—not with trust, but with the discovery that you need each other's hands more than you fear each other's motives.

I banked the fire and took first watch and told myself the tightness in my chest was just the altitude.

It was two days later, at the mouth of the eastern pass, that we saw the smoke of another camp on our backtrail—too low to be locals, too organized to be lost—and Bektour, squinting at it, said quietly:

"Corrigan is coming."
