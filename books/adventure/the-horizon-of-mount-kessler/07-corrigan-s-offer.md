## 7. Corrigan's Offer

Corrigan had been in Harrow Bend for six hours before he saw Yara Zhou, and in that time he'd learned three things: the town smelled of diesel and river rot, the dockside bar was called the Anvil and it closed when the owner got tired, and Elias Vann's granddaughter had arrived on the morning ferry looking like a woman who intended to burn something down.

He sat in the corner of the Anvil with his back to the wall, a glass of beer he wasn't drinking, and watched the guide.

Yara Zhou moved through a crowd like water through rocks—present everywhere, noticed nowhere. Corrigan had read the file twice on the flight up. It was thin, which was itself information. Thirty-four years old. Ten years of guiding treks through the Kessler foothills under a permit registered to a company that consisted, as far as anyone could tell, of Yara Zhou and a filing cabinet. No social media. No press. One photograph from fifteen years ago, taken at a university gala, standing half a step behind Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou in a dress shirt that didn't fit, wearing the expression of someone posing for a passport photo at their own funeral.

The file's last page was a single typed line from an analyst whose name Corrigan had never learned: *Subject has spent a decade ensuring there is nothing more to know.*

Corrigan respected that. He'd spent twenty years making sure there was nothing more to know about himself, and he knew what it cost.

* * *

He waited until the bar thinned out. He waited until Nadia Vann left—she'd confronted Zhou at the counter, loud enough that even the drunk by the jukebox had turned around, and Zhou had said something quiet that made her sit down instead of swing, which told Corrigan the guide was better than the file suggested. He waited until the owner started stacking chairs.

Then he crossed the room and sat down across from Yara Zhou without being invited.

"Mr. Zhou," he said. "Or Ms. I genuinely couldn't tell from the paperwork."

"You could have just asked."

"I could have." Corrigan set his glass down. "But then you'd have had time to decide how to answer, and I find first answers are the honest ones."

Yara looked at him the way people look at weather coming over a ridge—not afraid yet, but recalibrating. Up close the resemblance to Mei-Lin Zhou was fainter than the photograph implied. The mother had a face built for conference stages; the child had a face built for wind. Same jaw, though. Same watchfulness behind the eyes, like a person permanently listening for footsteps.

"You're consortium," Yara said.

"Meridian Consolidated, Resources Division. My name is Corrigan." He let the silence do its work. "I'm told you had a guest in your camp last week. A prospector. Older gentleman, bad lungs, worse judgment about altitude."

"I get a lot of guests."

"Elias Vann." He said the name flatly, no weight on it, and watched for the flinch. There wasn't one. Good. Or good training. "He died, I understand. I'd like to express the company's condolences."

"The company killed his partner. You can pass those condolences along personally."

There it was. Not a flinch—a door opening an inch, deliberately, to see what he'd do with it. Corrigan smiled the way he'd been taught to smile in rooms where smiling was currency.

"Mr. Vann was a troubled man," he said. "He'd been in these mountains a long time, and long-time men start seeing things. Claims. Patterns in rock. Cities where there are only scree fields." He reached into his jacket—not fast, letting them watch—and placed an envelope on the table between them. "The company would like to recover his effects. Maps, notes, personal items. Things that belong to his estate, frankly, and to whatever claims he may have filed that were never properly adjudicated. We're prepared to make this simple."

"How simple?"

"Fifty thousand," Corrigan said. "For the map and anything else he gave you. Cash, tonight, no receipts. And a further arrangement if you're interested in ongoing work—the company always needs guides who know the high country and don't ask questions. You'd be surprised how well that particular skill set pays."

Yara didn't look at the envelope. That was the first genuinely interesting thing they'd done. Most people looked. Most people looked and then hated themselves for looking, and that self-hatred was a lever you could pull all the way to yes.

"And if I say the prospector gave me nothing?"

"Then I'll believe you," Corrigan said pleasantly, "and I'll stay in town anyway, because my expense account is generous and the Anvil's beer is terrible but plentiful, and eventually I'll learn everything about you that there is to learn. Which, Mr. Zhou—" he paused, savoring it, the way you savor the last step before a drop "—is not very much. That's the remarkable thing. Ten years in this valley and there's almost nothing written about you anywhere. Do you know how difficult that is? To be invisible? I've tried. It takes work every single day. One loose thread and the whole weave comes apart."

"Is there a point approaching?"

"The point," Corrigan said, "is that I don't need the map to find you. I already found you. The map just tells me whether this conversation ends with money or with something less pleasant. And I'd very much like it to end with money, because I'm paid either way and only one of those outcomes requires paperwork."

* * *

Outside, the river ran brown with early melt, loud enough to cover a conversation if you stood close to it. Yara walked, and Corrigan walked alongside, hands in pockets, matching pace without appearing to. This was the part of the job he was actually good at—not the threats, not the envelopes, but the walking. The long patient adjacency. People told you things on walks they'd never tell you sitting down.

"Your mother," Corrigan said, "was the finest field archaeologist of her generation."

Yara stopped walking.

It was small—a hitch in the stride, half a second, corrected immediately—but after twenty years Corrigan could read people the way surveyors read contour lines, and that half-second was a cliff edge drawn on a map.

"I'm sorry," Yara said. "Do I know you?"

"No. But I know Site Nine."

The river filled the silence. Somewhere upstream a truck changed gears on the switchback road.

"You should be careful," Yara said quietly, "throwing names like that around."

"I've been careful for twenty years. It's exhausting." Corrigan looked out at the water. "In 1998, Meridian funded a survey in the upper Kessler. Cultural resources assessment, standard pre-clearance work, the kind of thing you do so the permits come clean. Dr. Zhou led it. She was brilliant. She found things in that valley that rewrote three textbooks." He turned back. "She also had a teenage assistant that summer. Family, I think. Quiet kid, good with a trowel. And that kid found something at Site Nine that didn't fit the assessment, and the assessment needed to fit, because downstream of Site Nine there was four hundred million dollars of infrastructure waiting on a signature."

"You were there."

"I was twenty-six years old and I carried boxes," Corrigan said. "That's all I did. I carried boxes from the site office to a truck, and the truck went down the mountain, and I told myself for a very long time that carrying boxes isn't the same as knowing what's in them." He shrugged. "It is, by the way. In case you were wondering. It took me fifteen years and a divorce to admit that."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because Vann's map goes to the same valley, and the company has the same problem it had in 1998, and I am once again the man who carries boxes." He held up a hand before Yara could speak. "I'm not confessing. I'm negotiating. Here's my actual offer, the real one, the one I flew up here to make."

They stood facing each other in the dark by the brown water, and Corrigan laid it out plainly, because plainness was disarming and he needed this disarmed.

"Take the money. Take two hundred thousand, not fifty. Leave tomorrow on any trek you like, any direction, and I will personally guarantee that no one follows you, no one audits your permits, and no one ever connects the name Zhou to the name Vann in any document, anywhere, ever. Your mother retires next year with her reputation intact. Her institutes keep their funding. Her students keep their careers. And you go back to being nobody, which I know—" he raised a finger "—I *know* sounds like an insult, but I mean it as the highest offer I can make. Nobody is safe. Nobody gets to keep their hands clean. I've seen what happens to somebodies in this business, and it's always the same: they become a paragraph in someone else's report."

"And the valley?"

"There is no valley," Corrigan said gently. "There's a scree field above the ice line and a dead prospector's fever dream. That's what the record will show, and records are the only thing that survive us."

* * *

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Corrigan allowed himself the first flicker of confidence. He'd done the math on the walk over: ten years of hiding bought at the price of one signature in 1998. People who build lives on top of a buried thing will pay almost anything to keep it buried, because digging it up means digging up themselves. He'd used that arithmetic a dozen times. It had never failed.

"You said your mother's reputation," Yara said finally. Not a question. A correction. "You mean her career. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it, because you've spent twenty years watching yours and hers diverge."

Corrigan said nothing.

"She signed the agreement too," Yara went on, voice level, eyes on the river. "Did you know that? I signed mine when I was sixteen. They put it in front of me at a table in a site office and my mother stood behind the chair and she didn't say stop and she didn't say sign, she just—stood there. Like furniture. Like the table." A breath. "So here's what I know about offers like yours, Mr. Corrigan. I've heard one before. I was younger, and the number was smaller, and I took it, and I've spent ten years learning what it cost. So you can keep your two hundred thousand, and you can keep your guarantee, because I already know exactly how much a Meridian guarantee weighs. I own one. It's the heaviest thing I've ever carried."

Corrigan felt something shift in his chest that he refused to call admiration.

"That's a noble speech," he said. "Nobility photographs badly at funerals."

"It's not nobility. It's arithmetic." Yara turned to face him fully, and for the first time all night Corrigan saw the family resemblance complete itself—not the jaw, not the eyes, but the look. The look of a person who has already counted the cost and decided the bill was worth it. "You offered me invisibility. I've lived there. The rent is unbearable."

"And if I told you the company won't stop? That I'm the polite version? That the men who visited Vann's partner weren't carrying envelopes?" He let it hang. "I came alone, Mr. Zhou. That was my choice, and I'd take it as a courtesy if you remembered it. But I don't choose who comes next."

"I know." Yara picked up the envelope from where it still sat—he'd transferred it without noticing, or they had, one of them had—and pressed it back against his chest. "Keep your money. Buy something for Vann's granddaughter. She's in town, and she's angrier than I am, and unlike me she has nothing left to lose."

Then Yara walked away up the dock road, unhurried, a figure dissolving into the dark between the warehouses, and Corrigan stood alone by the river holding an envelope full of cash that had suddenly become evidence.

* * *

Back in his room above the chandlery, he called it in. The voice on the other end listened without interrupting, which was never a good sign.

"They declined," Corrigan said. "Both figures. And they know about Site Nine—they were there. It's the assistant from '98. It was always going to be the assistant."

A pause. Paper shuffling somewhere in a city he'd never been allowed to visit.

"Then we proceed to stage two," the voice said. "How many days to landfall?"

"Monsoon's running early. Call it three weeks to the passes closing, less if the forecast holds."

"And Vann's map puts the approach at how many days?"

"Nine. Ten with weather." Corrigan closed his eyes. He already knew what was coming, had known since the envelope came back against his chest, and some tired honest part of him had known since the truck in 1998 backed down the mountain.

"Then you have nine days, Mr. Corrigan. Whatever it takes. The valley doesn't exist."

The line went dead. Corrigan sat on the edge of the bed in the smell of diesel and river rot and thought about a sixteen-year-old at a folding table, signing their name while their mother stood behind the chair like furniture.

He'd carried the boxes. He'd told himself it wasn't the same as knowing.

Downstairs, through the floorboards, he could hear the Anvil filling up—laughter, a bottle breaking, Nadia Vann's voice rising over it like smoke. And beneath all of it, faint but relentless, the river, running brown and fast toward the lowlands, carrying the melt off a mountain that had kept its secret for five hundred years and was clearly done keeping it now.

Corrigan opened the envelope, counted the bills out of habit, and began, methodically, to plan a climb.
