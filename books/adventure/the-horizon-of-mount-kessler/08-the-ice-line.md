## 8. The Ice Line

The glacier announced itself before we saw it—a sound like a door closing somewhere far below the world, a groan that traveled up through our boots and settled in the teeth. We'd been climbing since first light, Bektour setting a pace that made Tomas grumble about his knees and Nadia say nothing at all, which for Nadia had become its own kind of agreement.

We crested the moraine ridge at midmorning, and there it was: the ice line. A river of gray-green glacier pouring down from the shoulder of Kessler's massif, cracked and creased like old tin, running three kilometers to where it vanished into cloud.

"Beautiful," Tomas said, in the tone of a man identifying a disease.

"It's the only way through," I said. "Vann's route crosses here, climbs the lateral moraine on the far side, then cuts up through the col. Two days on ice if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we turn around and explain to Corrigan's people why we gave up."

Nobody laughed. Behind us, far down the valley, we'd spotted them again at dawn—four dots moving with the unhurried confidence of men on salary. Corrigan wasn't chasing us. He didn't need to. He was herding us, letting the mountain do the work of persuasion while he saved his energy for the offer he clearly believed we couldn't refuse.

I checked the ropes myself, twice, though Bektour checked them a third time without being asked. He'd gone quiet since Harrow Bend—not sullen, just concentrated, the way a man gets when he's carrying something heavier than his load. I knew that silence. I'd lived inside it for ten years.

"Rope teams," I said. "Nadia with me on point. Tomas and Bektour behind. Twenty meters between us, no slack, and nobody moves until the person ahead calls their footing. The glacier is hollow in places. It will lie to you."

"The mountain lies," Bektour said mildly, "but it doesn't cheat."

Tomas snorted. "Is that a proverb?"

"My grandmother's. She also said never trust a surveyor who smiles." He glanced back at Tomas. "You smile too much, Reyes."

"I'm a delight," Tomas said, "and my license says so."

It was the most any of us had talked in two days. I let it run. Fear does that—loosens people, or seals them shut, and you don't get to choose which. All you can do is keep talking until you find out.

* * *

The first hour was almost kind. The glacier's surface near the toe was firm, wind-scoured, blue ice showing through in patches like bone under skin. We moved well. Nadia read the surface the way Vann must have taught her without either of them admitting it—reading the sag lines, the dirty bands where meltwater had carried rock dust down into the ice, the places where the snow bridges stretched too smooth, too white, too eager to be trusted.

"Bridge," she called, forty minutes in, pointing with her axe at a depression no wider than a dinner table. "Go wide."

I went wide. So did Tomas. Bektour, hauling his hundred pounds of gear, cut it closer than he needed to—and the bridge held, but it sighed under him, a soft exhalation of compressed snow that none of us pretended not to hear.

"Sorry," Bektour said.

"Don't apologize to me," Nadia said. "Apologize to the glacier."

He actually did—murmured something in Kyrgyz, touched the ice with two fingers as he passed. Tomas looked at me. I shrugged. There are things you learn on mountains that have nothing to do with mountains.

By noon the crevasses stopped being features and became the landscape itself. We were crossing a braided maze of them, slots of green-black dark running every direction, some narrow enough to step, some wide enough that we roped around and detoured twenty minutes to cross a snow bridge Vann had marked on his map with a small, confident X. The map was good. That was the thing that kept surprising me. Every time I braced for Vann's route to fail us, it held—his marks accurate to within meters, his warnings precise. A man had walked this alone, years ago, and written down the truth of it carefully enough to save our lives after his own was spent.

"You knew him," Nadia said as we waited at the bridge. Her voice was flat, working hard to be flat. "At the end."

"He stumbled into my camp half-dead. He had maybe a day left in him."

"What did he talk about?"

"The valley. Mostly the valley. And about you." I hadn't planned to say it. It came out anyway, because the glacier was listening and glaciers don't let you keep things. "He said he'd written letters for eleven years and mailed none of them. Said he kept starting them with *I'm sorry* and they always sounded like excuses, so he'd tell you in person instead. Kept waiting to come back rich enough that sorry would count for something."

Nadia looked at the ice for a long moment. "Rich," she said finally. "God, Grandpa."

Then she stepped onto the bridge, tested it with one stamp, and crossed clean, and the conversation was over, and I understood that I'd given her something she'd carry the rest of the climb, and that she hated me a little for it, and needed it anyway.

* * *

It happened an hour past the bridge, on a long diagonal traverse below the serac field, where the glacier bent around the massif's knee and the surface tilted toward a drop none of us wanted to measure.

Bektour was second rope behind me. I heard him call his footing—"solid, moving"—and heard the word break in the middle, and turned in time to see the snow open beneath him like a mouth.

No drama. No scream. Just a man-shaped absence where a man had been, and the rope snapping taut, and the sickening arithmetic of a fall into a crevasse whose bottom we could not see.

I threw myself flat, axe buried, and the rope burned across my shoulder and hauled me forward a hand's width before it caught. My boots skated on the tilt. Below me, faintly, impossibly calmly, Bektour's voice rose out of the dark: "I am fine. The ledge is fine. Do not everyone look down at once."

But the rope was singing with his weight, and my anchor was my body and a prayer, and I could feel the lip of the crevasse beginning to crumble under the rope's bite, snow hissing down past me into the slot.

"Nadia!" I shouted. "Anchor!"

She was already moving. I'll see it the rest of my life: she didn't hesitate, didn't shout, didn't do anything cinematic. She dropped her pack, drove both axes in six meters back from the lip where the snow was still trustworthy, clipped the spare rope through them, and crawled forward on her belly to set a second picket above me—all in the time it took me to stop sliding. Then her hands were on the main rope, taking it around her back, leaning into it with her whole slight weight, and the load came off my shoulders like a hand lifting.

"Tomas—pulley," she said. "Three-to-one. Yara, edge protection, pad the lip."

And we worked. God help me, we worked like a crew. Tomas rigged the haul system with shaking hands and perfect technique, muttering the physics aloud like a rosary. I padded the lip with a folded foam mat so the rope wouldn't saw itself apart. Nadia held the whole falling weight of a grown man on her harness and her spine and never once asked how he was doing, because asking would have meant acknowledging what would happen if she stopped.

"On three," Tomas said. "One—"

"I can climb," Bektour's voice floated up. "Give me a foot of slack and a wall, and I will insult my way up this hole."

"Two—"

"He means it," Nadia said. "Slack him five feet. He's stronger than all of us combined and twice as stubborn."

We slacked him. We watched the rope go still, then twitch, then rise. It took him nine minutes to climb nine meters out of a crack in the world, and when his head came over the lip, frost-rimed and furious and grinning, Tomas grabbed his collar and Nadia let go of the rope and sat down hard in the snow, and I discovered my legs had opinions about standing.

Bektour lay on his back, breathing at the sky. "The ledge," he reported, "was excellent. Very comfortable. Five stars."

"Shut up," Nadia said, and put her forehead against his shoulder, and stayed there four seconds exactly, and then got up and started coiling rope because feelings are for base camp.

* * *

We made camp that night in a wind scoop below the lateral moraine, walls built from cut snow blocks, stove hissing in the vestibule. Nobody suggested pushing on. The glacier had voted.

We melted snow and ate noodles with butter and chili oil, and for a while the only sound was the wind testing our walls and the small domestic roar of the flame. Then Bektour said, quietly, "When I was in the water, in the dark—I was not afraid of dying."

Tomas looked up from his pot.

"I was afraid of dying stupidly. Alone, in a hole, over nothing." He stirred his noodles. "My village is under a reservoir now. You know this. When the dam filled, we stood on the hillside and watched the water come up the streets. Everyone was crying except my uncle, who said, *at least we all go together.* That is a terrible sentence. But I understood him today, hanging on the rope. Better the hole with company than the summit alone."

"That's bleak," Tomas said.

"That's family," Bektour said.

Nadia snorted into her cup. "We met nine days ago."

"So did brothers at some point," Bektour said. "Ask anyone's mother."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then, because the stove light makes honest men of us all, he said: "I lost my license over a bridge. Did I tell you? No. Well. I signed a survey I didn't believe. The numbers were wrong and I wrote them down right anyway, because the client was big and the winter was coming and I told myself someone else would catch it. Someone else didn't. Two workers went into the river." He turned his cup slowly. "So when your grandfather's map showed consortium survey marks, Zhou—when I realized what kind of people we're stealing from—I thought, good. Finally, a theft I can believe in."

"We're not stealing," I said.

"No," Tomas agreed. "That's the joke. For once in my life I'm the honest one, and it's illegal."

Nadia laughed—short, surprised, real. And then, looking into her cup: "Grandpa used to say the mountains keep better records than governments. I thought it was just something old men said." She looked up at the wall of our shelter, at the darkness beyond it where the glacier creaked and settled. "He walked this exact ice alone. Carrying the same truth we're carrying. And nobody came for him, so he came back for it himself, at seventy-something, with bad lungs." Her jaw tightened. "We're not losing this thing. Whatever Corrigan offers, whatever the weather does. We're not losing it."

"No," I said. "We're not."

And the strange thing—the thing I kept turning over after the others zipped into their bags—was that I believed it. Not because of the map, or the evidence, or even the dead. Because of the rope. Because for one hour on the ice, four strangers had become a single organism with one purpose, and every one of us had felt it, and none of us would say it aloud, and it was more binding than any contract Corrigan could put on a table.

Fragile. New. Real.

I took first watch. Around midnight the clouds tore open briefly and I saw the upper mountain, black against stars, and below us, tiny and patient, two lights moving up the valley we'd crossed that morning.

Corrigan's team. On the glacier. At night.

They weren't herding anymore.

I woke Nadia without a word, and pointed, and watched her face do the math alongside mine: they'd gained a full day on us while we pulled a man out of a hole.

"How long to the col?" she whispered.

"Eight hours, if the weather holds."

"Then we don't sleep tomorrow either." She was already reaching for her boots. "Wake Tomas. We move at first light—and Yara?" She nodded down-valley at the approaching lights. "Next time somebody falls in a hole, we'd better hope it's one of theirs."
