## 9. Storm Warning

The radio woke me before the alarm did.

I'd rigged it to charge off the solar panel during the day and kept it in my sleeping bag at night—not because I was afraid someone would steal it, but because cold kills batteries and I'd learned that lesson on a survey job in the Cordillera Blanca, back when I still had a license worth killing. So when it crackled against my ribs at four in the morning, I came up out of sleep already reaching for it, and I heard the tail end of the weather bulletin through a fog of static.

"—monsoon onset now forecast for the twentieth, roughly two weeks ahead of seasonal norms. Anomalous sea-surface temperatures in the basin. All high-route parties advised to—"

Static. Then the automated time signal, and then nothing but the hiss of the ionosphere doing whatever it does at that hour.

I lay there doing arithmetic instead of sleeping.

*Two weeks early.* The forecast models I'd pulled in Harrow Bend had put landfall at the first week of July. Every plan we'd made—the route, the food caches, the turnaround times—was built on that date. And forecasts were wrong all the time, but they were usually wrong by days, not weeks. When they were wrong by weeks, it meant something had shifted underneath everything, some big slow machine changing its mind, and no amount of wishing moved it back.

Eleven days. That's what it came to. Eleven days to reach the valley, do whatever Yara intended to do there—I'd stopped asking for details around day four; you don't ask a man what he's carrying if you've agreed to help him carry it—and get back over the Pass of Teeth before the weather closed like a door.

Eleven days, with Bektour's ribs cracked, with Nadia running on coffee and grief, with Corrigan's team a full day behind us and closing.

I unzipped the bag and got out the notebook.

* * *

People assume surveyors love maps. We do, but what we actually love is error bars. A map is a story; an error bar is how much of the story you're allowed to believe. My whole career, before it ended, was built on knowing exactly how much to trust a line drawn on paper—and exactly how much money changes hands when somebody wants you to trust it more than you should.

So I sat in the pre-dawn dark with my headlamp hooded red and worked the problem the way I used to work it for clients who paid on time.

Distance to the col: eight hours at our current pace, which was a polite fiction. Our current pace included Bektour moving at sixty percent, Nadia refusing to leave him, and Yara quietly redistributing weight whenever anyone wasn't looking. Call it ten hours honest. From the col, Vann's map showed a descent into the valley system—two days, maybe three, depending on what "the green stair" turned out to mean when we were standing on it. Time in the valley: unknown. Yara had said one full day for documentation. I'd watched them say it, and I'd watched their eyes, and I'd added a day.

Then out. The way we came, or the eastern route Vann had marked as a dotted line and labeled, in handwriting gone brown with age: *long way down, but it holds.*

Long ways down take longer. Call it three days minimum, four with a wounded man.

I wrote the numbers down and looked at them, and they didn't get better from looking.

Total: eleven days, best case, if nothing went wrong. And things had been going wrong since Harrow Bend, at a rate of roughly one catastrophe per forty-eight hours. The monsoon arriving on the twentieth gave us eleven days plus whatever grace the first storms bought us—which, in the Kessler Range, is none. The first storms here aren't rain. They're the glaciers waking up, meltwater finding new channels under your camp, rockfall where rockfall has never been. The mountain doesn't get wet in a monsoon. It gets *unmade*, slowly, and then all at once.

I did the thing I always do when the numbers are bad, which is check them twice, then check them a third time hoping I made an error, then accept them.

Then I went and woke Yara.

* * *

Yara took the news standing, which didn't surprise me. They'd slept in their boots again—I'd stopped commenting on it. There's a kind of person who sleeps ready to move, and every expedition I've ever been on lived or died by whether that person was also the one making decisions.

"Say the number again," Yara said.

"Eleven days. Landfall on the twentieth, give or take. If we're not below the ice line by the seventeenth, we shouldn't be above it at all."

"And the pass?"

"The pass closes when the snowpack goes unstable. Historically that tracks about three days behind first landfall at this elevation. So the seventeenth is hard, the nineteenth is suicide." I handed over the notebook. "I've marked the decision points. If we hit the col tomorrow night, we're on schedule. Anything after that eats margin."

Yara read the page longer than the page deserved. I let them. I've worked for people who skim numbers and people who sit inside them, and the second kind are worth following even when they're wrong.

"We tell them at breakfast," Yara said finally. "All of it. Nadia will want to push anyway."

"Nadia will want to push regardless of what the numbers say. That's not a criticism. It's just her operating temperature."

Something moved across Yara's face—not quite a smile. "You've known her five days."

"I've known clients for twenty years. She's easy by comparison." I zipped the notebook away. "There's a second problem."

Yara waited.

"Corrigan gained on us while we were pulling Bektour out of the ice. He knows it. Which means he knows our pace is set by our weakest member, and he knows exactly how long he has to run us down before the weather makes the whole question academic—for both of us." I nodded toward the glacier, invisible in the dark but present the way a debt is present. "He doesn't need to catch us. He needs to arrive after us and before we leave. Everything he does from here is timing, not pursuit."

"You sound like you admire it."

"I sound like I used to be paid by people like him." I said it flat, and I watched Yara decide not to ask. Good. That conversation could wait for a lower altitude, or never. "One more thing. The radio picked up a fragment before the bulletin—a voice, encrypted burst traffic, the kind consortium field teams use. Two transmissions in the last hour. Short ones."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning they're coordinating with somebody who isn't on the glacier. Somebody downstream, probably at Harrow Bend or the mine office. Corrigan isn't just chasing us anymore, Yara. He's reporting." I let that sit. "You don't report up a chain unless the chain has given you terms. And you don't give a man terms unless you expect him to need them."

Yara looked east, where the sky was going the color of dirty tin. "Terms for what?"

"For whatever happens when he catches us. Or—" and I heard myself say it before I decided to, the old surveyor's habit of stating the worst case out loud so nobody could pretend they hadn't heard it, "—for whatever he's authorized to do if he can't stop us any other way."

* * *

Breakfast was oatmeal and bad news, in that order.

I laid it out the way I'd lay it out for a client: numbers first, assumptions flagged, worst case stated without drama. Nadia listened with her arms crossed and her jaw set. Bektour sat propped against a pack with his ribs strapped, drinking tea, saying nothing—he was the only one of us who'd grown up with mountains that made promises like these, and I suspected he was reserving judgment until the sky weighed in.

"So we go faster," Nadia said, when I finished.

"We can't go faster. We're already moving at the speed of Bektour's rib cartilage, and everyone here knows it, including him."

"It's true," Bektour said mildly. "I am the schedule now."

"That's not what I—"

"It's fine." He said it the way he says most things, like a man setting down something heavy and deciding not to pick it back up. "In my village we had a word. The river decides, and the boat argues. I would rather be useful slowly than fast and dead. But I will not be the reason you turn around." He looked at Yara. "If the schedule says leave me at the col with half the food and the radio, the schedule is right."

Nobody said anything for a second. Nadia opened her mouth; Yara shook their head, barely.

"We're not leaving anybody at the col," Yara said. "New math. Tomas—what do we cut?"

That was the question I'd been waiting for, and I'll admit I respected it. Most people ask *how do we keep everything*. Leaders ask *what goes*.

"Weight first. We cache the climbing hardware we won't need past the col—half the rack, the spare rope. That buys us half a day. Then rest structure: we stop moving in daylight only, no camps, just rotation stops. Brutal, but it works for six days if nobody gets stupid." I flipped the notebook around. "And we change the exit. Forget coming back the way we came. Vann's eastern route drops elevation faster and stays off the glacier. If the monsoon hits while we're on it, we're in forest, not on ice. People survive monsoons in forests."

"You've never seen the eastern route," Nadia said.

"No. Neither have you. Neither has anyone alive except a dead prospector, which is why it's marked in pencil and why I'm flagging it as the highest-risk item on the board." I met her eyes. "But risk isn't a thing you avoid out here. It's a thing you spend. I'm telling you where I think we get the best exchange rate."

Bektour made a low sound that might have been approval. Nadia uncrossed her arms slowly, which from her was a treaty signing.

"Then we move now," she said. "Not at first light. Now."

* * *

We broke camp in the dark, and I want to record, for whoever ends up reading this someday, that breaking camp in the dark on a glacier is where expeditions go to die. You lose gear. You miss crevasse bridges. You make small errors that compound like interest. I said as much, and Yara overruled me, and I complied—because I'm the one who signed on knowing whose expedition this was—but I checked every anchor twice and I made Nadia check Bektour's harness a third time, and if she thought I was being fussy about it, she was right, and I'd do it again.

The light came up gray and flat, the whole sky one low ceiling of cloud pressing down like a lid. Bad light for reading terrain. Worse light for spotting people.

It was Nadia who saw them.

She'd taken point—we rotated the lead every hour to spread the navigation load, another of my rules Yara had adopted without discussion—and somewhere around the second hour she stopped, dropped to one knee, and held up a fist. We clustered in behind a serac like it was a wall, which against optics it wasn't, and she pointed back along our own track.

Three figures. Maybe four. Small with distance, blue against white, moving in the compact economical way of people who are paid to move that way. They weren't following our trail anymore—they were cutting straight across the glacier's grain, taking a line that would intercept us at the base of the moraine below the col.

"They're not herding," I said, putting the binoculars up. "They're converging."

"How long?" Yara asked.

"At their pace? They reach the moraine mid-afternoon. We reach it late afternoon, if we hold this speed." I lowered the glasses. "Which means we walk into them, or we detour, and the detour costs us the col today, and the col today is the whole schedule."

The wind came down the glacier and pushed at us, smelling of snow from somewhere far above, snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it. Bektour looked at the sky the way a farmer looks at a field.

"There's a third option," Nadia said quietly.

Everyone looked at her.

"We split the difference. Yara, Bektour, and I take the direct line and hold Corrigan's attention—slow, visible, exactly what they expect. Tomas takes the radio and the map and cuts north along the lateral moraine, high, fast, alone. You come down into the valley from the other side and meet us there." Her jaw was tight. "If they stop us, they stop the people they think matter. The evidence walks in the back door."

I looked at her and felt something I hadn't felt on a paid job in years, which was the specific chill of watching a client propose a plan that might actually work.

"It could work," I said. "It could also get the three of you caught alone on a glacier with men who cut ropes. I've seen what Corrigan's people consider acceptable losses. Have you?"

"Yes," Nadia said. "My grandfather. Twice."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with.

Yara stood very still, looking north along the high moraine line where I'd be walking, then west at the three blue figures, then at each of us in turn, doing whatever it is Yara does behind those careful eyes—weighing people, not just routes. I've watched a lot of leaders make decisions. Most of them look at maps. The good ones look at faces.

"Tomás," Yara said. "If you take the map and they take us, what do you do?"

"What you told Vann you'd do. Finish it. Get it home."

"And if we take the map and they take *you*?"

"Then you finish it too, and you don't trade anything to get me back, and you don't feel guilty about it either." I said it lightly, mostly successfully. "I'm a disgraced surveyor, Yara. I'm the cheapest thing on this expedition."

"You're the only one who can read the terrain," Yara said. "You're the most expensive thing on it." They turned away, toward the col, toward the thin gray seam of the pass above it, and I watched them arrive at the decision the way you watch weather arrive—you see it coming long before it lands. "No split. Not yet. We stay together until the col. After the col, the terrain gives us options, and we choose with better information. Tomas—call it. How much lead do we keep if we push hard from here?"

I raised the binoculars one last time. The blue figures had stopped too, tiny against the white, and even at this distance I could see the shape of what they were doing: one man apart from the others, face angled toward us, holding something up to his eye.

Glass meeting glass, across a mile of ice.

"He sees us," I said. "And he's not hurrying anymore. That's the part I don't like. Men hurry when they're chasing. They stroll when they already know where you're going." I lowered the binoculars and found Yara watching me. "Eleven days, one col, and a man with a radio who thinks this is already over. We should move. Now. While strolling is still all he's doing."

Above us, at the head of the glacier, the cloud ceiling dropped a hundred feet, quiet as a hand coming down.

The mountain had started counting too.
