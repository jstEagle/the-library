## 10. The Pass of Teeth

The pass announced itself the way bad news does—quietly, then all at once.

Yara heard it first as a sound like distant thunder, though the sky was a hard, cloudless blue. Then Bektour, two switchbacks ahead with the heavy load, stopped dead and raised one fist. Everyone froze. The mountain held its breath. And then the rockfall came down on the far side of the ridge in a long white roar, dust boiling up over the col like smoke from a fire no one could see.

When it settled, the high pass was gone.

They reached the saddle an hour later, moving fast and quiet because that was what Yara had trained into them, and stood at the lip of it looking down into the throat where the trail used to be. Where the trail used to be was the operative phrase. Half the mountainside had let go. A fan of shattered granite two hundred meters wide lay across the route, slabs the size of buses stacked at angles that made Yara's knees ache just to look at them, and above the whole mess hung the source of it—a fractured headwall with fresh scars running through it like veins. It wasn't finished falling. That was the thing about rockfall. You weren't looking at an event. You were looking at a process.

"No," Tomas said flatly.

Yara didn't answer. They were already doing the math, and the math kept coming out wrong. Eleven days. Tomas's number, from the radio report two nights ago—the monsoon coming two weeks early, landfall now a countdown instead of a season. Six days up to the valley if the high pass held. Four days out by the river route. One day of margin, maybe two if they pushed hard, and margin on a mountain was the first thing to die.

"Through that?" Nadia gestured at the debris field. "You can't be serious."

"I'm not," Yara said. "Not through it. Over it." They turned and looked east along the ridgeline, to where Vann's map said the truth lived. "There's another way."

* * *

They made camp early, below the ridge in the lee of a granite fin, because Yara wanted light for what came next. They spread the map on Tomas's groundsheet, weighting the corners with rocks, and the four of them crouched around it like conspirators.

Vann had drawn the traverse himself, in his cramped prospector's hand: a dotted line skirting beneath the eastern headwall, three hundred meters of slope angled at forty-five degrees or worse, exposed to the drop into the northern gorge the whole way. No rope fixed. No ledges marked. Just the line, and beside it, in ink gone brown with age, six words:

*I would not do this twice.*

"He wrote warnings like a man writing his own obituary," Nadia said quietly.

"He survived it once," Tomas said. "That's one more than I'd like."

Bektour said nothing for a long moment. He was studying the map upside down, which he always did, turning problems around until they surrendered. Then he looked up at Yara. "The gorge below. How deep?"

"Deep enough that you won't hear the bottom."

He nodded slowly, the way he did when a thing confirmed something he'd already decided. "Then we go early. Cold rock holds. Warm rock lets go. My grandfather taught me this when I was small—we were quarry people, before the dam." His jaw tightened on the last word, the way it always did, and moved on. "First light. We cross while the mountain is still asleep."

It was as good a plan as any, which is to say it was a plan for gambling rather than dying, and Yara took it. Rope spacing, pack order, who carried what so nobody's balance was compromised. Tomas wanted the front—he'd spent years surveying exactly this kind of terrain before his license went up in the scandal that still followed him—and Yara gave it to him without ceremony. Trust given plainly was worth more than trust argued about.

They ate, and checked gear twice, and turned in early. But Yara sat up past last light, map on their knees, reading Vann's marginal notes again by headlamp. The man had annotated everything. Water sources. Campsites. The mood of the weather. And along the traverse itself, a single longer note, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*If you are reading this, you have already decided. Good. Deciding at the edge kills more people than the edge does.*

Yara clicked off the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the mountain tick and settle, thinking about all the decisions they'd already made without admitting it.

* * *

First light was a rumor, not a fact—grey air, black rock, breath hanging—and they were roped and moving before the sun cleared the eastern peaks.

Tomas led. He moved beautifully, actually; whatever else the man had lost, his craft hadn't gone anywhere. He read the slope like text, choosing each placement, testing each hold with a patience that looked slow but wasn't. Behind him Nadia, then Bektour, then Yara on anchor duty, paying out rope, watching everyone's feet the way a doctor watches a pulse.

For the first hour it was merely terrible. Steep, loose, vertiginous—but coherent. There were holds. There was a logic to the rock. Yara let themselves believe, in the way climbers do, that belief itself was ballast.

Then the slope changed character.

A band of decomposing schist ran across their line like a rotten seam in good timber, and where it crossed, there were no holds—only a tilted ramp of gravel over slick stone, angling down toward the gorge, wide enough for one boot, narrow enough that the drop filled your entire lower field of vision. Below it, the gorge went down and down until detail failed and it became simply depth, an absence with wind in it.

Tomas got across it and set an anchor. Nadia started across.

She was halfway—weight committed, nothing left but the next step—when her downhill foot sheared the gravel loose. A sheet of scree the size of a door peeled away under her and went sliding toward the void, taking her balance with it.

Yara felt it happen through the rope before they saw it: a lurch, a sickening transfer of weight. Nadia dropped onto her uphill hip, arms pinwheeling, one hand slapping stone and finding nothing, sliding—slow, horrible, controlled only by friction and will—toward the edge where the world ended.

"Weight down!" Yara shouted, already locking off. "Don't reach! Stay low!"

Nadia did the right thing, which is the hardest thing—she stopped reaching for salvation and pressed herself flat against the slope, cheek to the rock, breathing in short animal gasps, her boots wedged against a rib of stone no wider than a hand. The scree she'd shed rattled away below them for five full seconds before the gorge swallowed the sound.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved except Bektour, who had traversed back along his own line with terrifying calm and was now directly above her, anchored, feeding slack out of a coil with both hands. "Nadia." His voice was level as a spirit bubble. "Your left boot. The rib. Kick it deeper. Slowly. Only that."

She kicked it deeper. Only that.

"Now your hands. Above your head, the grey ledge. Small steps. I have the rope. The rope has you."

She climbed. It took ninety seconds and cost her something permanent—Yara could see that in her face when she pulled over the lip onto solid ground, could see that some part of her confidence had been left on that slope and wasn't coming back. She sat down hard against a boulder and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped.

"I'm fine," she said, to no one, before anyone asked.

No one argued. That was its own kindness.

They finished the traverse by mid-morning, cold rock giving way to sun-warmed rock, every meter bought with attention. When they finally stepped off the slope onto the broad shoulder beyond, where the terrain relaxed into scree and sky, Tomas sat down where he stood and laughed—one sharp, disbelieving bark. Bektour uncoiled the ropes with the care of a man folding a flag. And Yara stood looking back at the line they'd crossed, at the seam of schist glinting in the sun, and felt the strange lightness that comes after mortal fear passes through a group and leaves everyone holding pieces of each other.

That was when Nadia said it.

"My grandfather wrote to me. Every year. For nine years."

* * *

They were sitting in the sun with the stove hissing water for tea, and none of them had suggested moving on yet, because nobody had the appetite to pretend the morning hadn't happened. Nadia had her back against the boulder and her boots stretched out and she was looking at the far horizon, not at any of them, when she said it.

Yara turned. "What?"

"Elias. Letters. Every year on my birthday, and every year after the anniversary of—" she waved a hand vaguely, the family catastrophe neither of them needed explained, "—the falling-out. Nine letters I never answered. My mother burned the first three. I read the rest." She finally looked at Yara, and her eyes were dry and fierce. "I knew what he was looking for. Before I ever met you in that bar. Before the map."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, a bird rode the thermals off the headwall.

"He wrote about a green valley," she went on. "Above the ice line. Terraces. Standing stones. He wrote about it like a man describing a cathedral he'd found God in. And he wrote about the consortium men following him, and about his partner, Devi—did he tell you about Devi?—who fell into a river that didn't have a name on any chart. And in the last letter, the one that came eight months ago, he wrote: *If anything happens to me, find the guide named Zhou. Not because they're the best. Because they're the only one who'll hate the consortium as much as I do.*"

The words landed in the circle of them like a dropped tool clattering on rock.

"You knew," Yara said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—an inventory. All those weeks of watching Nadia grieve and rage and demand her grandfather's map back, and underneath it she had known the destination the whole time. "You let us think you came for him. For closure."

"I came for him," Nadia said sharply. "And for the truth, and yes, for the map, because it was *his*, and I thought—" She stopped. Breathed. Started over, quieter. "I thought if I told you I knew what he'd found, you'd treat me like a journalist. Or worse, like someone who might sell it. So I played the angry granddaughter, because angry granddaughters get taken up mountains and journalists get left in bars." She looked around the circle—at Tomas, who was very carefully not looking up from the stove; at Bektour, whose expression gave nothing away. "I'm not proud of it. But I'm done pretending, because that slope down there taught me something this morning. Up here, secrets are ballast you dump or drown with."

Silence. The wind moved through the scree with a sound like slow applause.

Yara found, to their surprise, that they weren't angry. They were tired in a deep old way, and underneath the tiredness something was loosening—because if Nadia had been carrying her secret up the mountain, then Yara had been carrying theirs, and the arithmetic of silence between them suddenly balanced at zero.

"Devi," Yara said. "He never told me her name. He called her 'my partner' right up to the end. Like saying her name out loud would finish killing her." They pulled the map from their jacket and smoothed it on the groundsheet between them, and turned it so Nadia could see the survey marks clustered around the valley—those precise, institutional crosses that Yara had recognized weeks ago, the ones that matched notes from a filing cabinet in a childhood house. "Look at these. These aren't prospector marks. These are consortium survey crosses, pre-dating everything. Your grandfather didn't discover this valley. He *re-found* something they'd already catalogued and buried."

Nadia stared at the crosses. Her jaw worked. "They knew."

"They've known for decades." Yara heard their own voice go flat and careful, the voice they used for dangerous facts. "Which means Site Nine isn't an anomaly. It's a pattern."

"What's Site Nine?" Tomas asked quietly. He'd earned the question; he'd held the front of the rope all morning.

Yara looked at him, then at Bektour, then last and longest at Nadia, and understood that the moment had arrived whether they wanted it or not—that the mountain had stripped them all down to the load-bearing truths, and the next one was theirs to give or withhold. Tonight they'd camp at the ruined waystation Vann had marked. Tomorrow, the descent into the green valley. After that, no telling what Corrigan's team was doing on their trail.

"Not here," Yara said finally. "Tonight. At the waystation. I'll tell you all of it—what I found when I was sixteen, what my mother's funders made me sign, why I've spent ten years up here where nobody asks questions." They folded the map with deliberate care. "If we're going to carry this thing down off the mountain, we should all know exactly how heavy it is."

Nadia nodded slowly. Then she reached across and touched the folded map, just briefly, fingertips on oilcloth. "He wrote in the last letter that the valley would change everything." Her voice cracked, just once, and she steadied it. "He didn't say it would change us."

"It's still deciding," Bektour said, and passed the tea around, and for a while nobody said anything at all, which was its own kind of honesty.

They broke camp an hour later and moved up-valley toward the ruins, four people roped by more than cord now, and behind them the Pass of Teeth glittered in the sun like something that had tried to bite and missed.

But that night, as they picked their way down toward the dark shape of the waystation, Yara spotted it first: a pinpoint of orange light on the ridgeline they'd crossed that morning, where no fire should be.

Someone was following.

Someone was close.
