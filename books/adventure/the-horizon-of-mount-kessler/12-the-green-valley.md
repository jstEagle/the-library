## 12. The Green Valley

Bektour smelled the valley before he saw it.

Green. That was the smell—wet earth, cut grass, something flowering that had no business flowering at four thousand meters. For three days they had come down off the Pass of Teeth with their ropes singing in the wind and their water bottles frozen solid at dawn, and everything above the ice line had smelled like nothing at all, like stone and cold and the inside of your own mouth. Then the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, dropped through a notch Vann's map called the Needle's Eye, and the air changed.

He stopped walking.

"You smell that?" he said, and his voice came out strange, half-strangled.

Nadia Vann turned back to him. Windburned, rope-burned, three weeks of hard miles written on her face—and still she managed to look like she was about to argue with him. She'd been arguing with him pleasantly since the glacier, the way you argue with someone you've decided to keep alive. "Smell what?"

"Grass." He pulled his glove off and held his bare hand up like he was testing for rain. "Growing grass."

Tomas Reyes, behind him with the survey frame strapped across his back like a coffin lid, laughed once. "Altitude sickness."

"I don't get altitude sick," Bektour said. "I *am* altitude."

It was Yara who came forward. They always came forward when it mattered, moving quiet on the broken ground the way they moved everywhere, a person built out of careful steps. They stood beside Bektour at the notch and breathed in, and Bektour watched their face do something complicated—the same thing it did whenever the mountain gave them something they hadn't planned for. Not joy. Recognition, maybe. Like meeting someone you'd only heard described.

"Vann wasn't raving," Yara said quietly.

"He was half-dead," Nadia said. "Half-dead men say true things by accident."

"Then let's go be accidental," Tomas said, and shouldered past them all into the notch.

* * *

The trail down was old.

Bektour knew trails. He had carried loads on trails since he was eleven years old—tourist trails, smuggling trails, herding trails, the ghost-trails of roads that had existed before anyone drew borders here. A trail tells you its age the way a rope does: by how it's worn, where it holds, where it's been repaired. This one had steps cut into the living rock, wide ones, spaced for people carrying heavy things downhill. Not pack animals. People. The steps were worn concave, each one dished like a spoon by centuries of feet, and no government had ever built anything that lasted long enough to wear stone like that.

"Look at these," he said, crouching. His breath fogged. He put his palm flat on a step and felt the smoothness of it, the patience in it. "Somebody cut this so their grandmother wouldn't fall. You don't do that for a camp. You do that for a home."

Nobody answered him. Above him, Yara had gone very still, the way they went still when they were reading the mountain instead of walking it.

They descended through cloud for an hour, the steps switchbacking down a cliff face with a drop on the left that would have killed them all if any one of them slipped, and nobody talked, because talking on exposed ground is how you die. Bektour counted his steps because counting kept the fear in a small box. At step six hundred the cloud tore open below them like a curtain being yanked aside.

And there it was.

A valley. A real green valley, cradled between walls of granite and ice, maybe three kilometers end to end, floored with grass gone gold-green in the late season, threaded with a river the color of sky. Terraces climbed the south-facing slopes in long stacked curves—hundreds of them, thousands, stone-walled fields stepping up the mountainside in geometry so patient it looked grown rather than built. And rising from the terraces, standing stones. Dozens of them. Tall dark monoliths planted upright in rows and rings, some leaning, some fallen, each carved—his eyes couldn't make sense of it at this distance—with patterns that caught the light differently than weathering should.

At the valley's center, on a low rise above the river, stood walls. Walls of fitted stone without mortar, forming rooms, courts, a hall whose roofline had fallen but whose bones still stood two stories high. Beyond it, smaller structures scattered along the terraces like seeds spilled from a bag.

No smoke. No paths but the old ones. No sound but the river.

"Holy mother," Tomas whispered behind him. Then, because Tomas was Tomas and could not help himself even at the edge of the greatest discovery of his ruined career: "Do you understand what this does to every chronology in the field? Every single one?"

"It's beautiful," Nadia said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she didn't fix it.

Bektour didn't say anything. He was looking at the terraces.

He was looking at the terraces and he was seeing water.

* * *

They made camp on a terrace below the great hall, because Yara said never camp inside ruins until you know why they were abandoned, and Bektour thought that was the wisest sentence anyone had spoken in weeks. They pitched tents among the standing stones' shadows, and while Tomas set up his instruments with hands that shook from excitement rather than cold, and Nadia walked the near terraces touching every wall like she was greeting relatives, Bektour filled the stove and made tea, because that was his job and jobs hold a man together.

Yara sat down across the fire from him. That was unusual. Yara guided, led, decided, and kept their own counsel; they did not sit across fires looking at you like they wanted to hear what was in your head.

"You've been quiet since the notch," Yara said.

"The tea needs four minutes."

"Bektour."

He poured anyway, because the ritual helped, and handed Yara a cup, and looked out over the terraces going blue in the dusk. Somewhere in the stones the wind made a low note, like a bottle blown across.

"My village is under sixty meters of reservoir," he said.

Yara said nothing. They were good at saying nothing. It was one of the first things Bektour had learned about them, back on the glacier, back when he was just a porter with debts and a name nobody asked about.

"The Kessler Power project. You know this—the consortium built it, the same consortium that paid for your mother's digs, the same one whose man has been following us up the mountain like a bad smell." He turned the cup in his hands. "The engineers came in 2009. Very polite men with very good maps. They measured our houses and our mosque and the graves of my grandfathers, and they wrote numbers next to all of it, and then the water came. They call it displacement compensation. I call it the price of a photograph. They photographed everything, gave us copies, and drowned it. My mother's kitchen is under the water. When the lake is calm, sometimes the light goes down deep enough that you can almost see the minaret. Almost."

The wind note rose and fell in the stones.

"So when I look at those terraces," Bektour said, "I am not thinking, *how wonderful, how ancient.* I am thinking: this is what a place looks like before the men with good maps arrive. This is what my village looked like in 2008. Full of steps worn for grandmothers. Full of walls somebody loved." He looked at Yara straight on. "You found bones when you were young. I understood then why you are doing this. Now you understand me. We are not climbing for wonder, any of us. Wonder is what they use to buy you."

Yara held his gaze across the fire. In the firelight their face was older than their years, or maybe just finally honest about it.

"They're not going to flood this one," Yara said.

"No," Bektour agreed. "They're going to do worse. Floods leave graves. This—" he nodded at the dark mass of the great hall against the last light, "—this they will make disappear the way they made Site Nine disappear. Paper. Permits. A quarry license. One morning there will be a road and a fence and a sign, and in ten years tourists will stand where we're sitting and be told the stones were always a mine's tailings pile. I have watched them erase a living village. This one is easier. Nobody alive remembers it."

"That's why we document," Yara said. "Everything. Before landfall seals us in, and after, whatever it takes."

"And then what? Paper burns. Files get lost. I have seen files get lost." Bektour drank his tea, bitter and too hot, and let it scald him awake. "But you know this already. You signed their paper when you were fifteen years old. So tell me, guide. Why did you really bring us here? To see it? Or to make sure the world can't un-see it?"

For a long moment Yara looked at the standing stones, black against the deepening blue, and Bektour saw them decide something—not what to answer, but whether to answer at all.

"To make sure," Yara said.

"Good," Bektour said. "Then tomorrow we work. And Yara—" he tapped the ground with one finger, the packed ancient earth of the terrace, "—we sleep outside the walls. Whatever happened here, the dead built those steps for their grandmothers. We will not walk on their bones for comfort."

Yara nodded slowly. "I think they'd have liked you."

"Everyone likes me," Bektour said. "It's the debts that don't."

And for the first time since Elias Vann died in their camp, Yara Zhou laughed—a short, rough, real laugh—and Bektour counted it, the way he counted steps on exposed ground, as one more thing that would keep him alive.

* * *

He woke before dawn out of habit and lay in his bag watching the stars wheel over the valley rim, and that was when he heard it: footsteps on the terrace above, soft and quick. Nadia, wrapped in her sleeping bag like a shawl, standing among the standing stones facing east.

He crawled out and joined her without asking. Some vigils you share.

The sky over the eastern wall of the valley was turning the gray that comes before gold, and the first light came down the glacier wall opposite in a slow flood, catching the tops of the monoliths one by one, and Bektour saw what the carvings were. Not decoration. Figures. Rows upon rows of figures carved into the stones—people, hundreds of them, arms linked, standing in lines that marched around each stone and spiraled up it, and at the top of each spiral a single figure with raised arms, facing the sunrise.

"It's a census," Nadia whispered. "Or a memorial. Look—they're holding hands. Every one of them."

"Both," Bektour said. He heard his own voice go flat and certain. "My grandmother used to say: a people who count themselves are preparing to survive. A people who count their dead are making sure no one can pretend they never lived." He pointed at the spirals of linked figures. "That is a list of names, Nadia Vann. Carved in stone so that no company, no empire, no flood could ever take the count away."

The sun cleared the rim and the whole valley lit up at once—terraces and river and the great hall's standing walls—and below them, at the base of the nearest monolith, half-buried in turf, Tomas was already digging, already shouting for Yara, already waving a trowel like a torch.

And down the valley, far off at the mouth of the Needle's Eye, something glinted. Once. Twice. Like sunlight off glass, or off binoculars.

Bektour's anger, which had slept all night, opened one eye.

"They found us," he said.

Nadia followed his gaze and went very still. "How?"

"There is always a map," Bektour said. "There is always a man with a map."
