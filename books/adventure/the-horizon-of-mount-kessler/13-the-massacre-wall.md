## 13. The Massacre Wall

The great hall announced itself before we saw it—a change in the air, a hush, the way sound behaves differently inside old stone. Bektour stopped at the threshold and wouldn't come in. Not fear, I understood later. Reverence. He said afterward that some rooms you enter barefoot or not at all.

Nadia and Tomas went ahead with the lamps.

I've stood in cathedrals. I've stood in my mother's excavation trenches at dawn, when the light comes down the walls and every broken potsherd looks like it's waiting to speak. None of it prepared me for this hall. It was forty meters long, built of fitted stone so precise you couldn't slide a knife blade between the blocks, and the roof had fallen in one long slant that let the morning fall through like a stage light. Dust hung in the beam. Nobody breathed loud.

The murals started on the left wall, near the door, painted in ochre and iron red and a blue that shouldn't have survived five centuries but had—the mineral kind of blue that comes from grinding something precious, lapis maybe, traded up from a country none of us could name.

"They're a chronicle," Tomas said. He'd gone quiet in the way he did when his surveyor's brain was working. "Look. It reads left to right. Planting. Harvest. The terraces we climbed past." His lamp moved across figures—farmers bent over rows, dancers with raised arms, a line of children following a drum. "This is a city showing itself how it lived."

"Then what's that?" Nadia asked.

Her lamp had found the seam where the living ended.

It began almost gently: traders arriving with pack animals, the same friendly proportions as everything before. Then the painter's hand changed—or the world did. The animals became soldiers. The figures of the city's people shrank in scale, the way grief shrinks people, and the red pigment thickened until it wasn't paint anymore, it was just blood rendered in the only chemistry available.

A line of people driven downhill, hands bound. A building burning—rendered with such care you could see the individual flames. And then the wall that made Bektour finally step inside despite himself, made all of us go silent for a long time:

Rows of figures. Seated. Kneeling. Laid flat.

Dozens of them. Hundreds, if the painter meant each mark to be one person, and he clearly did, because he gave them faces. Not stylized faces—individual ones. Eyes open in some. Closed in others. One figure near the end had its hands raised, and the painter had spent real effort on those hands, on making you understand they were empty.

"How many?" Nadia asked, though she'd already counted. I watched her lips move.

Tomas answered anyway, because someone had to say it out loud. "If the register continues—" his lamp traveled along the wall, row after row, to the corner, around the corner, onto the next wall, where it stopped abruptly at a patch of raw plaster, unfinished, "—four hundred. Maybe more. The artist ran out of wall. Or out of names he knew."

"The colonial records," I said. My voice sounded wrong in the hall, too modern. "There was a punitive expedition into the highlands in the 1600s. The garrison reports called it an 'action against hostiles.' Two paragraphs in a provincial archive. I read them years ago and thought—nothing. I thought nothing about it at all."

"You were nineteen," Nadia said. Not unkindly.

"I was trained not to think about it," I corrected. "There's a difference."

Bektour touched the wall below the lowest row of figures—not the painting, just the stone beneath it—and said something in Kyrgyz too soft for me to catch. Then, in English: "My grandmother's village is under the reservoir now. When we visit it, we take a boat. There is a marker on the shore. It says the dam brought progress." He looked at me. "Who wrote your two paragraphs?"

* * *

We worked the rest of the day, because work was the only answer we had that didn't curdle.

Tomas set up his instruments and shot the hall properly—every measurement he'd been trained to take and had lost his license taking for the wrong people. He photographed the murals in overlapping frames with the good camera, then again with the backup, then a third time with Nadia holding reflectors to kill the harsh shadows. We were documenting for courts that might never convene, for archives that might refuse us, for descendants who didn't yet know their ancestors' faces had been saved by a dead man's obsession and a painter who refused to let four hundred people be two paragraphs.

Vann's cache was where his map said it would be. Of course it was. That man had been dying for months and still sealed his oilcloth bundles with soldier's precision.

They were under the floor of a side chamber—flagstones pried up and reset, invisible unless you knew which stone sat a finger's width proud. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas: a steel box, and inside the box, negatives. Glass plate negatives, heavy as communion tablets, packed in wool.

I held one up to the lamplight and the room tilted.

A survey photograph. Stamped on the margin in faded ink: **KESSLER CONSOLIDATED MINERALS — PRELIMINARY SURVEY, PLATE 41**. Dated sixty-one years ago. It showed this valley from the rim—the green bowl, the terraces, the standing stones—and in the foreground, sharp enough to count, the great hall with its roof still partially intact.

And written across the plate in surveyor's pencil, in a hand I knew better than I wanted to: *Structure of significant archaeological interest. Recommend full documentation prior to Phase II.*

Phase II. I knew what Phase II was, because I'd grown up hearing about it at dinner tables. Phase II was the access road, the exploration licenses, the core samples. Phase II was the reason my mother had funding for twenty years and Site Nine got paved.

"There are forty of them," Tomas said, turning plates with cotton gloves he'd produced from nowhere. "Different angles. Different seasons. They flew the whole valley." He stopped at one and went very still. "Yara. This one's annotated twice. Second annotation's newer."

I took it. The first note was the recommendation. The second, in different ink, different decade, was three words in a corporate secretary's tidy script:

*Interest noted. Proceed.*

"They knew," Nadia said. She wasn't looking at the negative. She was looking at the mural through the doorway, at the rows of faces. "Sixty years ago they stood on the rim with cameras and they looked at exactly what we're looking at, and they wrote 'proceed.' They built the haul road over—" her voice caught and she mastered it the way her grandfather must have taught her without ever meaning to, "—over them. Over the evidence. And then they hired archaeologists to make sure no one dug in the wrong places."

"Not my mother," I said. Too fast. Too defensive. The words tasted like the silence agreement I'd signed at nineteen, the one Corrigan's people kept in a file somewhere with my signature on it.

"No," Nadia agreed quietly. "Not only her. That's worse, Yara. You know that's worse."

I did know. That was the whole weight of it. Not one villain—one system, one tidy chain of interest-noted-proceed, running from a colonial garrison to a mining boardroom to field notes to my own teenage hands pressing a pen to paper while my mother stood in the tent doorway not looking at me.

"What proof is for"—that was the question Nadia asked that night, and I'll get to that. First there was the matter of getting the proof off this mountain.

* * *

We argued by stove-light in the side chamber, voices low even though the nearest human being besides us was a two-day march away. Or should have been.

"Three sets," Tomas said, sketching on the back of a supply manifest. "Originals stay here, sealed, hidden better than Vann hid them—we move them every night. One duplicate set goes out with whoever moves fastest toward the river. One set stays on our bodies, distributed, so no single loss kills the record."

"And the river route is open for how long?" Bektour asked.

I'd checked the gauge readings that morning. "Nine days. Maybe eight. After that the melt turns the gorge into something you don't run."

"Then someone leaves in six days with the duplicates," Nadia said, "and the rest of us keep working until the weather closes the valley, because if we leave early, Corrigan burns this place the week after we're gone and there's nothing left to prove anything."

She said Corrigan's name easily now. We all did. Somewhere behind us on the glacier he was climbing with his crew and his permits and his white flags folded somewhere in his kit, and pretending he wasn't coming was a luxury we'd stopped affording ourselves around the Pass of Teeth.

"Duplicate negatives take time," Tomas warned. "Copying glass plate in field conditions, by lantern—two days of work, minimum, and any vibration ruins a plate."

"Then we start tonight," Nadia said.

"We start tonight," I agreed, and it occurred to me, watching them divide the labor without being asked—Tomas rigging a copy stand from tent poles and a lens board, Nadia sorting plates into travel order, Bektour re-hiding the original cache in a new location only he would know—that Elias Vann had bought this exact moment with his last breath. A stranger's promise, kept by strangers. He'd known none of our names.

Later, when the others slept in shifts and the copy stand's lantern threw Tomas's shadow huge against the chamber wall, I went back into the great hall alone.

That's a thing guides do. We walk the ground at night to know it honestly, without the group's noise. But the truth is I went to be alone with the wall, the way you visit a grave.

The beam of moonlight had shifted since morning. It lay now across the section of mural where the painter had recorded the aftermath—and this part I hadn't understood in daylight, hadn't wanted to. Because the final panels weren't only death. Survivors walking away uphill, carrying children, carrying seeds, carrying fire. And above them, painted higher than any figure in the entire chronicle, larger than life: a mountain. Mount Kessler, unmistakable, its summit wrapped in the same cloud-band the painter had used elsewhere to mean *the sacred*, *the unseen*, *the place beyond reaching*.

The survivors walked toward the mountain, and the mountain held the horizon, and the painter had put the horizon in the picture deliberately, because the picture's whole argument was: *we were destroyed, and we endured, and look—there is still a sky above us.*

Four hundred faces. Interest noted. Proceed.

I pressed my palm flat against the stone, cold as river water, and made the same promise I'd made to a dying man, extended now to everyone this wall remembered. Not just *take it home*. Take *them* home. Out of two paragraphs. Into the light.

Behind me, gravel clicked.

I turned, lamp up, heart slamming—and it was only Bektour, standing barefoot at the threshold where he'd stopped that first morning, holding two cups of tea.

"I thought you don't enter barefoot," I said.

"For the ancestors, no." He handed me a cup and nodded at the wall. "For the ones who killed them, also no. But for you—for you I make exception." He drank, and looked a long time at the rows of faces. "Tomorrow the copying starts in earnest. Six days. And Yara—" he paused, and the pause had weight, the way his pauses always did, "—this morning I climbed to the eastern rim to check the descent route Vann marked. The one we may need."

"Yes?"

He looked at me, and the firelight from his cup's reflection moved in his eyes, and I understood before he spoke that the news was bad, because Bektour delivered good news immediately and saved his breath for the rest.

"There are fires on the southern ridge," he said. "Three of them, banked low. Campfires, disciplined, spaced like men who've done this before." He sipped his tea. "Corrigan is two days behind us. Maybe less."

Above us, the moon slid off the mural, and the faces went dark, and somewhere down-valley, faint as a memory, came the first far-off mutter of a storm season starting early.
