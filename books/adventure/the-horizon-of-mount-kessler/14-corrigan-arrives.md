## 14. Corrigan Arrives

The valley did not want them.

Corrigan felt it in the way the mist closed behind his column, in the silence of the birds, in the look Bektour gave him from across the terraces—no hatred, which would have been easier, just an accounting, like a man tallying a debt he already knew he'd never collect.

They came down the eastern shelf at first light, six men walking slow with their hands visible and a white bedsheet rigged between two trekking poles like something out of a very old war. Corrigan had argued against the sheet. Flags invited gunfire as often as they prevented it, and these were not soldiers up there. But the consortium's lawyers had been explicit: no violence on record, nothing that could be subpoenaed, photographed, or remembered by anyone with a phone. So they walked in under white cloth, carrying money instead of rifles, because money was cleaner and it worked.

He had done this before. Not here—never here—but other places with other names. A river valley in one country, a ridge line in another, always the same arithmetic: what does it cost to make people leave, versus what does it cost if they stay? Usually it was cheaper to pay. Usually people wanted to be paid.

He looked up at the terraces rising out of the green haze and revised his estimate. This place was older than the arithmetic.

* * *

They had watched the expedition for nine days.

Corrigan's spotters had picked up their trail at the Pass of Teeth, lost them for two days in cloud, found them again through the thermal scope at night—a scatter of warm dots moving down into a bowl that didn't appear correctly on any satellite image the company had licensed. That was its own kind of answer. The consortium's mapping contractor had "corrected" this quadrangle twenty-two years ago, and Corrigan had signed off on the correction himself, back when he was a field man doing corrections without asking what they were for. He'd told himself then that everyone signs things. It had taken him two decades to understand that the signing was the whole job. Everything else—the flights, the permits, the quiet meetings in airport lounges—was decoration around the signature.

Now he stood at the base of the lowest terrace while the mist burned off, and looked at what he'd spent twenty years helping to erase.

Stone walls followed the contour of the slope for what might have been kilometers, stepped and stitched together with a mason's patience. Channels cut for water still held water. There were standing stones up-slope, tall as men, ranked in rows that meant calendar or court or grave—he couldn't tell and didn't try. Above everything, half-swallowed by cloud, the ruins of a great hall opened toward the valley floor like the rib cage of something enormous.

His team stood behind him in a loose line. Dzherenov, ex-military, bored and watchful. The Okonkwo brothers, who were good with ropes and better with radios. Two porters from Harrow Bend who didn't ask questions and got paid not to. And Priya Chandrasekar, the archivist, who was the only person Corrigan had brought whom he actually trusted, because she was the only one whose instructions were honest: assess the site, catalog what can't be moved, report what's already documented. If the evidence exists, we need to know how much of it exists.

That was the real reason for the white flags. Not peace. Inventory.

"They're at the hall," Dzherenov said, glassing the ridge. "Four of them plus the injured porter. They've seen us."

"Of course they've seen us." Corrigan adjusted the strap of his pack. Inside it, wrapped in cloth, were three envelopes. Cash for the immediate inducement, contracts for the long arrangement, and—for one person only—a letter on consortium letterhead with a signature that would make a certain kind of man go pale. "Nobody moves until I say. Nobody touches anything. We are guests, and we behave like it, because the moment we don't, this becomes a story someone else gets to write."

"And if they don't take the offer?"

"Then we've learned something important about them."

He started up the stairs cut into the terrace wall. They were shallow, built for people shorter than him, and each step had been swept clean within living memory—or within some memory. His boots left mud on stone that had waited four hundred years for rain to wear away worse things than him.

At the top of the first terrace, Yara Zhou was waiting.

* * *

Corrigan had met Yara once before, eleven years ago, at a fundraiser in a city where the air conditioning cost more than most of the province earned in a year. Yara had been twenty-four, standing at the edge of a room full of donors, holding a glass of wine they never drank, wearing the expression of someone counting exits. Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou had introduced them. *My child doesn't share my enthusiasm for the past,* she'd said, fond and oblivious, and Yara had shaken his hand with a grip like a closing door.

He hadn't thought about them much since. That was the point of people like Yara—they arranged themselves to be forgettable. But he'd pulled the file after Vann died, and the file was thin in exactly the ways that interested him: no academic appointments, no publications, a guiding business registered under a name slightly different from the name on their passport. Someone had built a life designed to leave no fingerprints. Corrigan recognized the architecture. He'd lived in it himself for years.

"You brought a flag," Yara said now. They stood with their arms crossed, blocking the stair, mud to the knee, a month of mountain written on them. Behind them, at the mouth of the great hall, Nadia Vann watched with a camera hanging forgotten around her neck, and Tomas Reyes stood with his hands in his pockets wearing the particular stillness of a man doing math he doesn't like.

"I brought breakfast, too," Corrigan said. "Rice, lentils, oil, salt. Your porter is hurt. My medic is competent."

"We have a medic."

"Tomas Reyes, suspended surveyor, isn't a medic. He's a mapmaker who fudged flood elevations for a developer who drowned a village. No offense."

"None taken," Tomas said mildly. "You forgot the part where I turned state's evidence and lost everything anyway."

"The part I remember is that you know what documents are worth." Corrigan let that sit. Then he gestured at the terraces, the stones, the hall. "It's extraordinary. All of it. I'm not going to stand here and pretend it isn't. And I'm not going to insult you by pretending I represent anyone but the people I represent. They funded Dr. Zhou's surveys for thirty years. They hold the mineral leases on this entire range. And they would prefer, very strongly, that this valley remain what it has been for four centuries—which is to say, unlisted."

"Unlisted," Nadia repeated. She'd come down the hall steps, camera swinging. "You mean unrecorded. You mean the massacre stays off the books."

"I mean," Corrigan said, "that I'm authorized to make all of you rich enough to disappear somewhere better than this."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, because plainly was his craft. Three envelopes, three tiers.

For the guides and the surveyor: triple the expedition rate, paid today by satellite transfer, plus severance equal to a year's income, plus letters of reference that would restore credentials, clear suspensions, reopen doors. For Tomas specifically: reinstatement of his license, contingent on nothing more than a signed statement that he'd seen no site of significance above the ice line.

For Bektour, who had come down from the hall leaning on a makeshift crutch, his leg bound and his face gray with pain: medical evacuation, fully funded, to any hospital he named. Corrigan had read the file on the dam. Kyzyl-Suu, flooded in '09, four hundred households relocated to a settlement with intermittent power and no work. Bektour had testified; the testimony had gone nowhere. Corrigan knew exactly what the man's debts were, because his employer had created them.

And for Nadia Vann—the hardest case, so he saved her for last—an envelope containing not money but paper: her grandfather's seized assets, released. Elias Vann's claims, his equipment, his cabin on the Tulameen, everything the consortium's lawyers had frozen when he'd stopped selling and started digging. "Your grandfather spent his last decade fighting us through channels that don't work," Corrigan said. "I can hand you back every piece of paper he ever filed. You could win. Legally, properly, win. On one condition: the photographs and negatives from this valley come to me, not to a newsroom. What happened here gets memorialized—with funding, with a proper investigation, with names—once our lawyers control what the record says."

"That's not winning," Nadia said. "That's you writing my grandfather's obituary."

"It's the only version of his obituary that will ever be printed."

* * *

The wind came up the valley in the late morning and carried the smell of the glacier, cold and mineral. Somewhere below, meltwater ran loud in the old channels, finding paths the dead city's builders had cut and the mountain had kept.

Tomas wavered.

Corrigan watched it happen and did not rush it. Tomas Reyes was not corrupt; that was the tragedy of him. He'd falsified one report under pressure and had spent nine years paying for it, and a man like that doesn't dream of wealth—he dreams of being allowed back inside. reinstatement. A stamp on a card. A desk with his name on it. Corrigan had watched stronger men fold for less.

"Reinstatement," Tomas said quietly. "You can actually do that."

"I can do that today."

"And all I sign is that there's nothing up here."

"All you sign is that you surveyed the upper Kessler drainage in poor conditions and found nothing requiring further study. Which, given that your instruments were destroyed in the storm—" Corrigan shrugged, gently, professionally. "It's even true, from a certain angle. You found nothing. You found what someone else found. Your hands are clean."

Tomas looked at the hall. At the murals inside it that Corrigan had not yet seen but knew were there, because Vann's letters had described them in a hand that shook. At Yara, who said nothing, because this wasn't theirs to argue—it was Tomas's weight to lift or set down.

"I need to think about it," Tomas said.

"Take the afternoon," Corrigan said. "Take the night. Sincerity takes time."

Bektour refused flatly.

He did it standing up, which Corrigan understood cost him something, the ruined leg braced against the terrace wall, sweat standing on his forehead. He didn't raise his voice. He spoke in accented English, slowly, so that everyone including Corrigan's porters could follow.

"My village is under ninety meters of water. You know this. Your company built the dam, and when I talked, they took my name off the compensation list, and my family waited eight years for a payment that will come when the lake dries." He paused. "Now you offer to carry me out on a helicopter and fix my leg, if I walk past this valley and say it is empty. You think I do not know what empty means? Empty is what they made my home. I will not help make it again."

"Bektour," Corrigan said, and used the honest voice, the one he kept folded flat for occasions like this, "your leg is bad. Infection in the joint, maybe. Without surgery you may lose more than the leg. I am offering you the only helicopter that will ever land in this valley."

"Then I will limp," Bektour said. "I have been limping since Kyzyl-Suu. One more thing to carry is nothing."

There was a silence after that which Corrigan did not try to fill. He had learned long ago that the refusals were the data. A crew could be bought one at a time; a crew that refused together was a different animal entirely, and he needed to know which kind he'd climbed in here to meet.

Nadia hadn't answered yet either. She stood with her grandfather's envelope unopened in her hands, turning it over, and Corrigan saw that she was crying without any interest in hiding it, and that she was going to refuse him too, and that the refusal was going to cost her more than Bektour's, because Elias Vann's frozen empire was sitting right there in her grip like a door back to a father-figure she'd lost twice.

She put the envelope down on the terrace stones. Didn't throw it. Set it down carefully, the way you return something borrowed.

"He asked you to bring it home," she said to Yara, not to Corrigan. "Not to sell it. Not to trade it." Her jaw tightened. "Tell him no for me. I can't be the one who talks to him anymore."

* * *

Corrigan collected his envelopes and walked back down the terraces alone, leaving his team to make camp at the valley mouth under the white flag, which now seemed less like a truce than a claim staked in cloth.

Priya fell in beside him at the second terrace. "Three refusals, one abstention, one maybe."

"One maybe is all I need. Reyes cracks, the others lose their spine by degrees. It's how it always goes." He said it automatically and heard, as he said it, how little conviction the sentence carried. He stopped at the bottom step and looked back up at the hall, at the small figures of people who had chosen the hard thing in front of witnesses, and something in his chest moved in a way he associated—uncomfortably—with altitude sickness.

"Corrigan," Priya said quietly. "The archive assessment. If they release what's in that hall before we contain it—"

"I know what happens."

"Do you?" She was one of perhaps three people alive who could say that to him. "Because you keep looking at Zhou like you're not here on assignment."

He didn't answer. Below them the meltwater roared through its ancient channels, patient as bookkeeping. Above, clouds were building along the western rim earlier than they should, stacking up dark and organized, and Corrigan—who had spent thirty years reading weather reports and political ones, who had landed the day before landfall more times than he could count—felt the old professional dread settle over him like a coat.

The monsoon was early. Everyone's timetable was about to become fiction.

Behind him, up in the hall, he heard Tomas Reyes laugh—short, unhappy, the laugh of a man who has just made a decision and hates it—and Corrigan stood very still on the bottom stair and understood, with the clarity that arrives too late or too early, that the maybe had just broken the wrong way.

He turned to Dzherenov. "Get on the radio. Quietly."

"To base?"

"No." Corrigan watched the clouds eat the rim of the valley he'd been sent to bury. "To the pilot. Tell him I need options on extraction that don't exist yet."
