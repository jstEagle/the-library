## 15. Sabotage

Nadia woke to the sound of the radio dying.

Not a dramatic sound—no explosion, no shout. Just a wrongness in the pre-dawn quiet, a gap where the generator's hum should have been. She lay in her bag for three full seconds before her body understood what her mind already knew: something had changed in the night.

She crawled out of the tent into blue half-light. The valley was beautiful at this hour, she'd give it that. Mist pooled in the terraces like milk poured over stone steps, and the standing stones on the eastern ridge caught the first gray light and held it. Somewhere below, meltwater ran in the channels the old city's builders had cut a thousand years ago, still doing their job.

The radio tent was torn open along one seam.

Tomas was already there, kneeling in the wreckage with his hands moving over the equipment the way a doctor moves over a body—checking for signs of life, finding none. The transceiver was a crushed thing, its casing stove in by something heavy and deliberate. The antenna cable had been cut in four places. Not slashed in anger. Cut cleanly, section by section, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing.

"Corrigan?" Nadia said.

"Who else." Tomas didn't look up. "Took him all night, too. Look at this." He lifted a length of cable. "He stripped the insulation off the spares first. Every spare. Even the wire in the solar regulator."

"He wanted us deaf," Nadia said. "Not just until we fixed it. Permanently."

"He wanted us deaf, dumb, and—" Tomas stopped. His head came up slowly. "Nadia. Where are the anchors?"

She felt it before she understood it. A cold that started in her stomach and climbed.

The exit traverse.

* * *

They reached the base of the traverse as the sun cleared the rim and lit the whole face of it orange and gold, which would have been beautiful if Nadia hadn't been looking at the ropes.

There had been six lines strung across the exposed shelf—the route Vann's map called *the Ladder*, two hundred meters of broken rock above a drop into the gorge, the only way out of the valley that didn't involve climbing back over the pass in monsoon conditions. Yara's team had anchored them over three days of careful work, drilling and hammering, testing each placement twice because Tomas Reyes did nothing once.

Five of the six anchor stations were gone.

Not just cut—removed. The bolts had been backed out and taken. The slings around the horn of rock they called the Priest were gone; someone had climbed up there in the dark and untied Nadia's own knots, the ones she'd dressed and set herself, and carried the cordalette away. On the last station, the one guarding the crux move, they'd left a message: the rope itself, severed and coiled neatly on the ledge, tied off with a bowline no climber would use, a bowline a man learns in an office.

"They took the hardware," Bektour said behind her. He'd come up fast, breathing hard, and now he stood staring at the empty wall with an expression Nadia had never seen on him—not fear, not anger. Recognition. "In my country, when soldiers want a road closed, they do not blow the bridge. They take the planks. So people know: you may rebuild, but we will come again."

"How long to re-rig?" Nadia asked.

Tomas crouched at the first station, running his thumb over the bolt hole. "If I had my kit? Two days, maybe three, working safe. But look." He turned the drill bit he'd pulled from his pack toward her. The tungsten tip was gone, snapped clean. In its place, someone had hammered in a soft steel blank—the kind of sabotage that looked like wear and tear unless you knew tools. "They got into our packs. While we slept. All of them."

Nadia thought about the camp layout. Four tents. Six people. And—

"The porters," she said. "Where's Deme?"

Bektour went very still.

Deme was one of the two local men Corrigan's advance team had hired before Yara outbid them for the crossing—one of the two who'd stayed when his partner walked off the mountain rather than go near the valley. Twenty-two years old, quick smile, hands always moving. He'd been sleeping ten feet from Nadia's tent.

"Deme!" Bektour's voice cracked across the valley and came back off the walls. Nothing answered but water.

They found his tent empty, bedroll folded. Folded—a soldier's habit or a guilty man's tidiness, Nadia couldn't decide which was worse. On top of the roll sat an envelope weighted with a stone. Inside, a single line in careful English:

*I am sorry. They have my brother's debt. Do not follow me down.*

"Bektour," Nadia started, but he was already reading it, and she watched the words land on him like blows. She knew what debt meant in the lowland villages. She'd grown up comfortable in Harrow Bend and even she knew. A brother's debt could be a lifetime. Could be a generation.

"He told me," Bektour said quietly. "Three nights ago, by the fire. He asked me what I would do, if someone held my family's paper. I told him I would burn the paper." His jaw worked. "I did not ask him whose name was on his."

"That's not your fault."

"No." He folded the note with great care and put it inside his jacket, against his chest. "It is Corrigan's fault. That is different from no one's fault."

* * *

Yara arrived last, and Nadia watched them take it in—the way their eyes moved from the severed rope to the empty anchors to the three of them standing at the base of the wall, and how their face did the thing it always did, closing down into competence, the grief and fury packed away somewhere load-bearing.

"Talk to me," Yara said.

"One porter gone, took his pay and our trust. Radio destroyed beyond field repair. Spares sabotaged. Five of six anchor stations stripped, and our drilling kit's dead." Nadia heard her own voice come out flat and hard, her grandfather's voice, the prospector's voice, inventorying a claim that had gone bad. "We can't call out. We can't climb out fast. And the monsoon is—" she checked the sky reflexively, uselessly, "—close."

"How close?"

"Tomas says five days to landfall, maybe less. The pressure's been falling since yesterday."

Yara stood looking at the traverse for a long moment. Above them the orange light had gone white and hard. Somewhere on the rim, clouds were building that hadn't been there yesterday, stacked like masonry.

"Then we assume he's watching," Yara said finally. "Everything we do next, we do assuming eyes on us. Which means we don't panic, and we don't scatter, and we don't send anyone down that wall on half-rigged anchors to prove a point." They turned. "Nobody dies proving a point. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Nadia said. But her chest was tight, because she'd already done the arithmetic Yara was refusing to do out loud, and the numbers were ugly.

* * *

They held a council in the great hall, because it was the only place with walls thick enough to feel like shelter and because, Nadia suspected, Yara wanted to make the decision under the murals—in front of the dead, so to speak. The painted figures looked down from the plaster: the procession, the harvest, and then the panel nobody liked to stand beneath, the one with the fire and the falling figures and the soldiers whose faces the old artists had left deliberately blank, as if even eight hundred years ago some things were better done by men without faces.

"We have two problems," Yara said. "Getting out, and getting the evidence out. They're not the same problem anymore."

"Explain," Bektour said.

"If we all wait for a full re-rig, we might lose the weather window. If we split and send a fast team down on minimal anchors, we save days—but every meter of that traverse gets more lethal with fewer lines." Yara laid the map out on the floor between them, Vann's map, the grease-pencil marks fading but legible. "And there's a third problem. Corrigan didn't destroy the radio to strand us. He destroyed it so nobody can report what happens next. Whatever he does to us—or whatever happens to us—he wants it unwitnessed."

"So we become witnesses," Nadia said.

Everyone looked at her.

"My grandfather spent thirty years walking away from this valley," she said. "Every letter, every drunk night in Harrow Bend, he was circling it. Coming back. He died forty miles from here with a map in his coat, and the last thing he asked was that it be brought home." She tapped the oilskin packet where it sat in its case beside the duplicate negatives. "Corrigan thinks cutting a rope ends this. Fine. Then we write it down. Everything. Today. Every mural, every photo, every measurement, in triplicate, in three separate packs on three separate bodies. If one of us makes it down, it survives. If none of us make it down—" she shrugged, and hated how casual she had to make it sound, "—then it was never going to survive anyway."

"It will not come to that," Bektour said.

"You don't know that."

"I know that man." Bektour nodded toward the entrance, toward the valley mouth and everything beyond it. "A man who takes bolts in the night is a man who is afraid of daylight. He had us outnumbered and he still crept. That is not strength, Nadia Vann. That is a man on a schedule he does not control, spending money he was not given, hoping we break before his masters ask questions." He smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile. "So we do not break. We make him spend."

Tomas laughed—a short, surprised bark—and then covered it with his hand, embarrassed. "Sorry. It's just—I spent four years after the license hearing thinking I was finished. Sitting in Harrow Bend redrawing other people's surveys for beer money. And now I'm sitting in an undiscovered city planning counter-sabotage against a mining consortium, and honestly? This is the best I've felt in a decade." He shook his head. "That's probably a character flaw."

"It's a survival trait," Nadia said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in shifts, and Nadia took the murals.

She photographed until her memory card filled, then swapped cards and kept going, then started drawing by hand when the batteries ran low—her grandfather's trick, learned from his letters, the old surveyor's discipline of ink and grid paper that no battery failure could touch. Panel by panel she rendered the procession of kings, the star calendar, the granary records, and then the massacre wall itself, working faster as she went because the light was dying and because something in the painted figures' postures made her want to finish and step back.

It was while she worked that she noticed the detail.

At the edge of the massacre panel, half-hidden by a crack in the plaster, there was a smaller figure set apart from the others—a woman, standing upright amid the falling bodies, holding something above her head. Not a weapon. A bundle. Records, maybe. Or a child. Below her feet, in the old script Tomas had begun piecing together from the granary panels, a single line of text.

"Tomas," she called. "What does this say?"

He came over, squinted, and went pale in the failing light. "It says: *she carried the names out.*"

Nadia stared at the small painted figure. Eight hundred years dead, and someone had painted her carrying the truth out of a burning city, because someone had understood, even then, that the buildings could be burned but the record had to run.

"Okay," she said softly. "Okay. That's the job."

* * *

Full dark. The stove hissed in the lee of the hall's doorway, and they ate in silence, four people listening to a valley that had gone quiet in the wrong way. No wind. No animal sounds. The meltwater channels ran on, indifferent.

It was Bektour who heard it first—a scrape of boot on stone, far off, toward the terrace steps. He put one finger to his lips and pointed.

They killed the stove and waited.

For a long time, nothing. Then, faintly, from the direction of the lower terraces, a sound that might have been a voice and might have been wind, and then—deliberately, unmistakably—a whistle. Three notes, descending. The signal Corrigan's crew used on the trail, the one Nadia had catalogued days ago without knowing why it mattered.

"He's counting us," Tomas breathed. "Listening for how many answer."

Nobody whistled back. The silence stretched until Nadia's ears rang, and then the sound faded downslope, unhurried, a man taking his evening walk.

When it was fully gone, Yara spoke into the dark, very quietly.

"Tomorrow we rig the traverse. Two lines minimum, fast and dirty, and we decide who goes." A pause. "And tomorrow night, we sleep in shifts, armed, because he's done being subtle."

Nadia lay back on cold stone and stared up at the ceiling the old builders had painted with stars, and thought about Deme's folded bedroll, and the woman in the mural with the bundle raised over her head, and the five empty anchor points gleaming faintly in the starlight like pulled teeth.

Someone was going down that wall in the next few days on anchors driven in haste by exhausted people while a man with a budget and no conscience watched from the rocks. The math said so. The math had been saying it since the radio died.

She just intended to make damn sure that when the math collected, it collected the evidence first—and Corrigan's career second—and that whoever paid the rest of the price, it would not be silent, and it would not be forgotten.

Above the rim, the first fat drops of the early monsoon began to fall, hissing on hot stone, and somewhere down-valley a whistle sounded again—two notes this time, urgent, calling home.
