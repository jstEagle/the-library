## 16. The Descent Decision

I woke before the others and climbed to the rim of the valley to look at the sky.

It was doing something wrong. The clouds that had been sitting on the eastern peaks all week had thickened overnight into a single unbroken lid, grey-green at the edges, the color of water seen through ice. Below the lid the air was warm and dead still, no wind at all, which was worse than wind. Every old trekker I knew had a name for that stillness. Bektour's people called it *the mountain holding its breath*. My mother used to call it the pre-monsoon hush, and she'd say it with a kind of reverence that always struck me as obscene—reverence for a thing that killed porters every year.

Six days. Maybe five, if the radio's forecast from two nights ago was already stale, and it probably was, because the last transmission we'd picked up had been half static and Tomas had spent twenty minutes coaxing a barometric reading out of the altimeter like he was negotiating with a hostage-taker.

Below me, the valley lay in its bowl of green. Terraces stepping down toward the river. Standing stones catching the first flat light. The great hall with its roofless walls open to the sky, the murals inside still holding their reds and ochres after however many centuries, as if they'd been painted yesterday by someone who knew exactly what they wanted remembered.

That was the whole point of this place, I thought. Memory, made permanent. And we were going to be the ones who carried it out—or died trying.

I went down to camp.

* * *

Tomas had the map spread on a groundsheet between the stoves, weighted at the corners with stones, and he'd been up longer than I had. He'd drawn on it. That was the first thing I saw—the careful pencil work over Vann's faded ink, contour lines re-established, distances annotated in his small precise hand. He looked like himself again, or close to it: sleeves rolled, glasses pushed up into his hair, the particular intensity of a man doing the only thing he'd ever been good at and knowing it.

"Sit," he said, without looking up. "You'll want to be sitting."

I sat. Nadia came out of her tent pulling her jacket on, hair still damp from the stream. Bektour followed, moving carefully—his ribs were taped where the rockfall at the waystation had caught him, and every day the tape came off a little later and went back on a little tighter. He lowered himself onto a crate and said nothing, which from Bektour meant he'd been awake even earlier than the rest of us and had already thought through whatever Tomas was about to say.

"The eastern route," Tomas began, and tapped the map. "Vann's marked line, down the far side, past the tarns, joining the river gorge below the icefall. It's good. It's genuinely good—it avoids the exit traverse entirely, which Corrigan's men cut, and it stays under the treeline for most of its length." He paused. "But it's four days. Four days at expedition pace with full loads, maybe three and a half if we push hard and nothing goes wrong. And nothing is going to go right."

"So we leave today," Nadia said.

"We can't leave today." He finally looked up. "Because of what we're carrying."

He turned the map over, and underneath it were the other things: the oilskin packets, sealed and numbered in Yara's own hand. The original survey photographs Vann had cached above the ice line forty years ago. The negatives. The rubbings and tracings from the massacre wall. Pages of field notes in four different hands. Everything the consortium would burn the mountain down to destroy.

"It's eleven kilos," Tomas said. "Eleven kilos of paper and film, wrapped and waterproofed. And here's the arithmetic nobody wants to hear. If all five of us go together, we move at the pace of our slowest member and our heaviest load, and we arrive at the gorge with maybe a day of margin—and then we run white water on a raft built from tent poles, with everyone exhausted, in weather that will already be turning. If the river's high, we lose the lot. All of it, in one basket, going down one river."

Nobody spoke. Above us, somewhere on the rim, loose stone ticked downhill—a small sound, but in that pre-storm hush it carried like a shot.

"And if we split?" I said.

Tomas had been waiting for it. "If we split," he said, "one team takes the evidence and travels light. Two climbers, minimal kit, moving fast—two days to the gorge, maybe less. They get the packet downriver to Harrow Bend with days to spare, whatever happens after that." His finger moved across the map to the valley. "The rest hold here. Keep documenting. Photograph everything we haven't photographed yet. Survey the terraces properly. And when Corrigan comes—and he'll come, because his men are still up there at the col and he knows we haven't left—there are enough of us left to stand between him and this place."

"That's not standing," Bektour said quietly. "That's being bait."

"It's both," I said.

They all looked at me. I hadn't meant to say it out loud, but there it was, hanging in the cold air, and once it was said there was no taking it back.

* * *

Here is what I had learned about leadership in fifteen years of guiding, and none of it had come easily: you don't decide for people. You put the truth in front of them, all of it, ugly side up, and then you let them choose what they're willing to carry. The clients who paid for my treks never understood that—they wanted a leader who decided, who absorbed risk on their behalf so they could feel safe. But safety was a story we told ourselves around stoves. What I could actually offer was honesty, and a route, and the willingness to walk point.

So I laid it out.

"If the fast team makes the river, the evidence gets out no matter what happens here. Corrigan burns the murals, drives us off, does whatever he does—we've lost the site, but not the proof. The archive copy exists. Nadia's contacts exist. It becomes a matter of public record within the week." I looked at each of them in turn. "If the fast team doesn't make the river—if the gorge takes them, or Corrigan's men catch them on the descent—then everything depends on whoever's left. Which means whoever stays has to keep the originals intact long enough for a second attempt after landfall, when the passes reopen. Months. Possibly a winter in this valley. Possibly worse."

"And the slow team," Bektour said, "is the one that dies if the fast team fails."

"No," I said. "The slow team is the one that's *here*, with the food, the shelter, the numbers, and the high ground. Fast team carries the risk of the river. Slow team carries the risk of staying. Nobody's bait unless they volunteer to be."

Nadia was staring at the map. She'd gone very still, the way she did when she was working through something she didn't want to say yet. I'd learned that stillness too, these weeks—her grandfather's stillness, though she'd hate hearing that.

"How many on the fast team?" she asked.

"Two," Tomas said. "Lightest climbers. One who knows the river country." He glanced at Bektour. "Which should be you, but your ribs say otherwise, and I'm not arguing with your ribs."

"My ribs are fine."

"Your ribs are lying," Nadia said. "Like grandfather used to."

Something crossed Bektour's face—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. He'd started letting us talk about Elias like a person instead of a corpse, somewhere around the time we found the cache. Grief, I'd come to think, was mostly a logistics problem: what to do with all the love that has nowhere left to go.

"So it's me," Tomas said, "and one more. Someone strong enough to paddle twelve hours a day and smart enough not to panic in the gorge." He hesitated, and I saw him arrive at the same answer I'd already reached, hours ago, alone on the rim. "Yara. You know the lower watershed better than any of us. And—" he stopped himself, but the rest was obvious: *and you're the one they want. If Corrigan's men intercept anyone, they intercept you, and the map dies with the messenger.*

Except that wasn't how I read it. If they intercepted me, they'd have me, and I'd spent ten years learning how to disappear into mountains. The evidence was the thing that couldn't fight back. The evidence needed someone stubborn.

"Nadia," I said.

Her head came up.

"You grew up on that river. Your grandfather took you down the gorge when you were fourteen—I've read the letters, you told me yourself. You're the strongest paddler we have besides Bektour. You're light. You're fast." I made myself keep going, because the easy part was over. "And the evidence is your grandfather's. He asked me to carry it home, and I'm telling you the best way to do that is to put it in your hands."

For a moment she didn't move. Then: "You're giving it away."

"I'm splitting it. We make duplicates tonight—Tomas can shoot copies of the photos, we trace the map, we divide the notes. Originals go with the fast team. Duplicates stay here with the slow team, buried in separate caches. If either group reaches the outside, the story gets out."

"That's not what I mean." Her voice had an edge now, grief sharpened on a whetstone for years and finally finding something worth cutting. "You've carried it since he died in your tent. You promised *him*. And now you're handing it off because—" she stopped, jaw tight.

"Because the promise was to bring it home," I said. "Not to be the one holding it. Those are different things, and I've had six days to understand the difference."

She held my eyes for a long time. Around us the camp breathed—stove hiss, rope creak, the small sounds of gear being checked by people pretending not to listen. Bektour studied his hands. Tomas adjusted his glasses twice.

"All right," Nadia said finally. "Fast team. Me and you."

"I'll go," I said. "Tomas holds the valley. Bektour holds the valley. We leave at first light tomorrow, travel the east ridge while it's still frozen, drop into the trees by noon. We should hit the gorge in two days, raft it on day three, and be at the Bend a full day before landfall—if the forecast holds. Which it won't. So we build margin everywhere we can."

"And us," Bektour said. It wasn't quite a question, but I answered it anyway.

"You document everything. Every terrace, every stone, every wall—with the duplicates, so the copies leave even if the originals somehow don't. You keep the stove smoke low and the watch rotation tight. Corrigan's got four men at the col and a grudge. Don't give him anything to aim at." I looked at Tomas. "And if we don't signal from the gorge by noon on day three, you assume the worst and start planning the winter option. No heroics down the mountain. Promise me."

Tomas smiled thinly. "Surveyors don't do heroics. We do redundancy."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon.

Tomas photographed the originals against a sheet of white canvas, frame by frame, checking exposure with a hand lens like a man defusing ordnance. I traced Vann's map onto fresh paper, my hand copying his dying hand, every survey mark, every annotation, including the warning about the exposed traverse written in a script that had deteriorated as his body did. Nadia packed the oilskin packets, resealed them, weighed them, repacked them. Twice she stopped mid-motion, holding one of the old survey photographs—the consortium's own men, young and squinting in 1960s sun, standing on bones—and I watched her memorize faces. She was building a list. I recognized the exercise. I'd done the same thing with Site Nine, at sixteen, memorizing the backs of men whose names I was made to sign away.

Bektour cooked the big meal, rice and lentils and the last of the dried meat, refusing help, and made everyone eat until it was almost funny. "No one runs a gorge hungry," he said. "No one holds a valley hungry either. Eat."

At dusk I walked out onto the lowest terrace with the duplicate set and buried it, per plan, beneath a cairn of stones that looked like a hundred other cairns in the valley—except this one, if we failed, would someday matter more than everything else in sight. I pressed the stones down with my palms and stayed kneeling a moment.

Ten years ago I'd have said this was the farthest I'd ever be from my mother's world: a buried box of photographs, a war over memory, a valley no map admitted existed. But kneeling there I felt something I hadn't expected, which was that she would understand this. Not approve—understand. Whatever else Dr. Mei-Lin Zhou had signed, whatever silence she'd bought into, she'd taught me to read a landscape the way other people read letters. This valley had been writing to us all week. We were just the first in a long time to answer.

Behind me, footsteps on the terrace stones. Nadia, arms crossed against the cold, looking down at the cairn.

"You could've kept it," she said.

"It was never mine to keep."

"Grandfather would've liked you." She said it flatly, like a fact she resented. "He liked stubborn people who pretended they weren't."

I stood, brushed the dirt from my knees. "Get some sleep. First light."

She lingered. "Yara. If the gorge takes us—"

"It won't."

"If it does." Her voice was steady. "Tell them what he was. Not the prospector. Tell them he found it first, and he kept it safe, and he sent it home."

Above the eastern peaks, the cloud lid had begun, very slowly, to sink.
