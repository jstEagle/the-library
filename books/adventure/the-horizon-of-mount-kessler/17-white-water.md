## 17. White Water

The river told us everything we needed to know before we ever touched it. It was the color of milk glass and it moved like something with a spine—a single flexing muscle of meltwater running down the throat of the gorge, swallowing boulders whole and spitting back spray that hung in the air like breath.

"Tell me again why this is a good idea," I said.

Yara crouched at the water's edge, testing the current with a dead branch they'd stripped of bark. The branch vanished in three seconds. They watched the spot where it went under for a long moment, then stood and brushed off their knees.

"It's not," they said. "It's just faster than walking."

Tomas laughed from behind us, high and thin, the laugh of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. He'd spent the morning doing exactly that—elevation loss to the lowland confluence, river speed, daylight remaining, monsoon days remaining—and every time he arrived at the same number his face went grayer. Eleven hours by water. Four days by trail, if the trail still existed after last night's rain, which Tomas doubted out loud twice an hour.

So: the raft.

We built it where Vann's map marked a gravel bar shaped, according to his cramped annotation, *like a sleeping dog*. Elias Vann had run this gorge thirty years ago with a partner who never came home, and he'd left instructions in the margins as if he'd always known someone would need them. *Lash poles in threes. Thigh-deep entry only. Pray past the second bend.* His handwriting got shakier toward the bottom of the page, but the words stayed precise, the way handwriting does when the person writing it is more afraid of being misunderstood than of dying.

We had six tent poles, two paddles cut from pack-frame boards, forty meters of rope, four dry bags, one oilskin packet, and no prayer any of us was willing to say out loud.

* * *

The oilskin packet sat against my chest inside my jacket, strapped there with a bandage wrap because we had nothing else. It weighed almost nothing. That was the thing nobody warned you about evidence—it was lighter than a stone, lighter than a rope, lighter than the promise Yara had made to a dying man in a freezing tent, and it was heavier than all of us put together.

The originals. Every negative Vann cached in the valley, every photograph Yara and Bektour shot of the massacre wall, every page of field notes in my grandfather's hand describing what the consortium's survey teams found in 1971 and what their superiors ordered them to do about it. Sealed in wax and oilcloth the way Bektour insisted, because Bektour knew about water. His village was under a reservoir now. He treated rivers like former acquaintances who'd turned criminal—respectful, watchful, never surprised.

The duplicates were Tomas's idea. A second set, shot on the spare film, packed loose in a dry bag lashed to the raft's center pole. Redundancy. Insurance. If the raft went down, the chest packet might survive; if the chest packet failed, maybe the dry bag would wash ashore somewhere findable. It was good logic. I agreed with it at the time.

I want that on the record. I agreed with it at the time.

* * *

We launched at first light, or what passed for first light under cloud cover so low it felt like a ceiling pressing down on our hats. The fast team was me, Yara, and Tomas. Bektour stayed above with the documentation crew and his cracked ribs—he'd argued hardest to come, which was how I knew how much it cost him to stay. He gripped my forearm at the gravel bar, hard enough to leave marks, and said something in his own language, then translated: "The river owes me. Collect."

Yara took the stern paddle. I took the bow. Tomas rode the middle with the dry bag between his knees and both hands clamped on the lashings, a surveyor reduced to ballast, narrating our position in coordinates nobody asked for because it was the only way he could keep from screaming.

The first kilometer was almost kind. Fast water, clean lines, the raft riding the current like it had been born to it—which, in fairness, it had been born yesterday, from tent poles and desperation, and it flexed and groaned like the living thing it technically wasn't. Spray came over the bow in cold sheets. My hands went numb around the paddle within ten minutes. I didn't care. There was a moment—I'll admit it, there was a moment—when the gorge walls fell away on either side and the river dropped us into open light and I thought: *this is what he felt.* My grandfather, thirty years old, running this same water with the map already drawn and the valley already found. Terror and exhilaration are the same sensation wearing different clothes. You decide afterward which one you were feeling.

Then the second bend came up, and the river stopped being kind.

* * *

Vann's margin note said *pray past the second bend*, and I understood the theology of it immediately. The gorge narrowed to less than half its width and the entire river funneled through a slot between two granite shoulders, dropping—I'd guess—two meters in the space of a boat length. Below the drop, the water didn't flow so much as boil, a churning pit of white that ate the sound of everything else. We could hear it fifty meters before we saw it, a roar like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Eddy!" Yara shouted over the noise, pointing left. "Eddy line, get us left!"

There was no eddy. Or there was, technically—a slack tongue of water behind a mid-channel rock—but reaching it meant crossing current strong enough to fold the raft in half, and Tomas was shouting coordinates, and I was paddling until my shoulders burned, and none of it mattered because the river had already decided.

We went over the drop sideways.

I have run that half-second so many times since that it has worn smooth, like a stone in a pocket. The bow lifting. The world tilting. The sky where the water should be. The specific, obscene slowness of watching your own hands lose the paddle. Then impact—not a crash but a detonation, the whole river arriving at once, and we were inside the boil, inside the white, and the raft was no longer a boat but a bone in a dog's mouth, shaken.

I went under.

Cold is not a temperature down there. It's a fact, total and final, the way gravity is a fact. It closed around me and squeezed the air out in one involuntary grunt and then the river rolled me like laundry. I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *the packet.* My hand was already at my chest, already checking the wrap, before my brain finished forming the thought. Still there. Still sealed. The river could take my paddle, my boots, my mind—fine. Not that.

The surface found me eventually. It always does; that's the dirty secret of whitewater, it spits you back up just often enough to keep hoping. I came up coughing twenty meters below the boil and the raft was there, upside down, Tomas clinging to the center pole with both arms and his eyes shut, and Yara—

Yara was in the water beside him, one fist locked in the rope lashing, being dragged.

"Dry bag!" Tomas was screaming. "The bag—the bag—"

I looked downstream and saw it: the gray dry bag, the duplicates, tumbling end over end through the rapids like something alive and escaping. For one full second I watched it go. One second of pure arithmetic—*it's only copies, it's only copies, let it go*—and then my body ignored me completely, the way bodies do, and I swam.

I don't recommend swimming after a dry bag in Class Five meltwater. I especially don't recommend catching it, which I did, fingertips on the grab loop, a jolt that nearly pulled my arm out of its socket, and then we were both of us—me and the bag—swept into the slack water below the next bend where the gorge widened and the river, having made its point, grew briefly bored with us.

Yara hauled me out by the collar. Then they hauled out the bag. Then they stood over me on the gravel, dripping, breathing like a bellows, and said nothing at all, which from Yara was a sermon.

"I had it," I gasped.

"You had a bag of duplicates."

"I know what I had."

They crouched, forearms on knees, water running off them in ropes. "Nadia. If you'd missed—if the current had taken *you*—" They stopped. Shook water out of their ear. Started over, quieter. "The originals were never in danger. You were the only irreplaceable thing on this river."

"That's not true," I said. "You're on this river too."

Something moved across their face—fast, quickly filed away. They turned and waded to the overturned raft without answering, and together we flipped it and dumped it and counted what the river had left us.

* * *

The inventory, conducted kneeling on gravel with blue lips:

Both paddles: gone. Both paddles: replaced by one, recovered snagged in a willow fifty meters down, because the river giveth in ways it doesn't advertise. Center pole: cracked but holding. Rope: present. Dry bag: present, dented, and—when Tomas opened it with the reverence of a man defusing a bomb—half full of water.

The duplicate negatives floated up out of it like drowned insects. Forty exposures, give or take, curling already, the emulsion beginning to lift. Tomas laid them across a flat rock in the weak light and we watched them die in real time. Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the images weren't already saying—the wall fading, the figures blurring, my grandfather's proof dissolving back into the chemistry it came from.

"We still have the originals," Tomas said finally. His voice was steady. He was looking at the ruined film the way you look at a grave. "This changes nothing operationally."

"It changes everything emotionally," I said.

"Yes," he admitted. "That too."

Yara repacked the oilskin packet against my chest themselves, rewrapping the bandage, pulling each layer tight, checking the wax seals with their thumb. Their hands were shaking—from cold, I decided, though the sun was coming up properly now and the shaking didn't stop when the shivering did.

"One packet," they said. "One body. No heroics. Those are the terms for the rest of the run."

"And if the packet and the body disagree?"

"The packet wins." They met my eyes. "That's the oath, Nadia. I gave it to him. You don't get to renegotiate it in the rapids."

* * *

We ran the rest of the gorge in the long gold light of late afternoon, and it was—God help me—it was magnificent. With one paddle and no choice, we stopped fighting the river and started reading it, the way Vann must have read it, the way Bektour reads all water. Yara called the lines from the stern and I ferried us across the current in long patient arcs and Tomas stopped reciting coordinates and started laughing instead, actual laughter, wild and unguarded, every time we threaded a gap that by rights should have killed us. Once, rounding a bend beneath a cliff streaked white with falcon droppings, a herd of blue sheep watched us pass from a ledge thirty meters up, motionless, unimpressed, eternal. I yelled at them. They declined to respond.

The gorge opened. The water spread, slowed, turned from violence into mere momentum. In the last hour the current carried us and we barely worked at all, three soaked people on a raft of tent poles drifting down through country no road had ever touched, and I understood—for the first time, really understood—why my grandfather kept coming back to these mountains after everyone stopped believing him. Not for the city. Not for the proof. For this: the feeling of being small in a place that doesn't know your name and doesn't need to.

The confluence appeared at dusk, the tributary swelling the main river, and on the far bank, exactly where the plan said, a thread of smoke rose from a cook fire. Bektour's team had made the descent route. Figures stood up against the sky, waving.

Tomas checked his watch, then the western horizon, where the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent.

"Nine hours by water," he said. "We're ahead of schedule."

Ahead of schedule. On this mountain, under this sky, with Corrigan somewhere behind us and the monsoon somewhere ahead. I put my hand flat against my chest, against the packet, and felt it there—light as paper, heavy as the dead.

Yara steered us for the smoke.

* * *

We hit the shallows running and the team waded out to meet us, and it was Bektour who reached the raft first, cracked ribs and all, and it was Bektour who read our faces in one glance and asked the only question that mattered:

"The packet?"

I tapped my chest. He exhaled, long and slow, and pressed his forehead briefly against mine—an old-country gesture, I'd learn later, reserved for family and survivors.

Then the youngest porter, Dawa, came down from the ridge at a dead sprint, waving something over his head. A scrap of foil wrapper. A cigarette butt. The trampled mud of a campsite on the overlook above the confluence, abandoned within the day.

"They were here," he panted. "Six men, maybe seven. Heading downriver. Ahead of us."

The smoke from our cook fire bent suddenly east, and far to the west, under the last light, the clouds over Mount Kessler had begun, very quietly, to fall.
