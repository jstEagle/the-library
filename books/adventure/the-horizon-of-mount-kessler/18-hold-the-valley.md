## 18. Hold the Valley

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years telling himself he was a careful man. Careful men checked their instruments twice. Careful men didn't take the shortcut across the Talvera gorge, didn't sign off on the survey that flattened the ridge road, didn't drink alone in a hotel bar in Harrow Bend while their license sat revoked in a drawer three hundred kilometers away. Careful men kept their heads down.

He had been very careful for eleven years, and it had gotten him exactly nowhere.

So when Yara Zhou stood in the green valley's great hall and said, *we document everything*, Tomas had felt something in his chest unclench — not joy, nothing so clean as joy, but the specific relief of a man who has been holding his breath since he was thirty-four years old.

Now, on the fourth morning in the valley, with the monsoon two days out and Corrigan's crew camped at the valley mouth like a tumor, Tomas crouched at the base of the eastern terrace with his theodolite balanced on a cairn of river stones, and he was not careful at all. He was thorough. There was a difference, and it had taken him half a lifetime to learn it.

"Grid seven," he said into the recorder. "Terrace wall, east face. Carved channeling, agricultural function, consistent with irrigation off the snowmelt stream. Depth of cut approximately—" he leaned in, squinted, "—forty centimeters. Tool marks visible. This is not seasonal work. This is infrastructure."

The recorder caught his voice and the wind and, beneath both, the low constant rush of the melt-swollen river. Somewhere above him, Nadia was photographing the standing stones on the ridge, her red jacket a bright stitch against grey rock. Bektour was hauling water up from the stream, two drums on a shoulder yoke he'd built himself from a broken trekking pole and a length of rope, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had carried loads since before he could read. And Yara —

Yara was in the great hall, and had been since dawn, lying on their back on the cold flagstones with a raking light rig made from a headlamp and a strip of tin foil, reading the murals line by line.

*The Massacre Wall*, Nadia had named it, and the name stuck because there was no better one. Forty meters of painted stone, older than any archive in the capital, and running along its lower register — unmistakable, deliberate, painted by people who wanted it remembered — a procession of figures falling. Spears. Fire. Children carried up the terraces by women who did not look back. And at the far end, rendered in a pigment that had not faded, a row of tall pale figures with banners, standing over the dead.

And beneath that, in a later hand, cruder, angrier: a second layer of paint. Colonial-era glyphs. Survey marks.

Vann's cached photographs, sealed in the oilskin drum under the altar stone, showed the same wall in 1971 — pristine, unexcavated, already photographed by the consortium's original survey team. Already known. Already buried in a filing cabinet while the company built its access roads over the valley's lower approaches.

"They knew," Yara had said the first night, voice flat as slate. "They've always known."

Tomas adjusted the theodolite, logged the reading, and let himself think about what came after. If they got out. If the evidence got out. Hearings, probably. Inquiries. His name in reports — *surveyor, disgraced, reinstated for purposes of testimony*. He found, to his surprise, that he no longer flinched from the idea of his name in print. Eleven years ago it would have destroyed him. Now it felt like weather.

"Tomas." Bektour's voice, from down by the stream. Not loud. Bektour never spoke loudly; he'd told them once that in his village, loud voices meant either weddings or funerals, and you learned to listen for which. "Someone is coming up the trail."

Tomas turned. The valley mouth was a kilometer away, a notch in the cliffs where the river exited through white water, and the consortium camp sat just inside it — six tents, a generator's drone, and Corrigan's men moving through the days with the patient menace of men being paid by the hour to wait. All morning they'd been quiet. Too quiet. Tomas had noted it in his log without letting himself note what it might mean.

Now four of them were on the switchback trail, moving fast, carrying things.

Not rifles. Worse. Jerrycans.

"Bektour." Tomas was already moving, theodolite abandoned, boots skidding on wet grass. "Get Yara. Get everyone. They've got fuel."

* * *

The great hall opened onto the valley floor through a colonnade of fallen pillars, and Tomas came through it at a run, shouting Yara's name before he was through the trees.

Yara was already up, headlamp still strapped to their forehead, foil light rig dangling from one hand. They'd heard it too — or seen it, or simply read the shape of Tomas's panic the way they read everything, fast and total. Behind them the murals climbed the wall into shadow: the falling figures, the fire, the children on the terraces.

"How many?" Yara asked.

"Four. Jerrycans. They're maybe twenty minutes out."

"Nadia's on the ridge." Yara was already calculating; Tomas could see it happening behind their eyes, the same cold triage they'd done at the crevasse, at the pass, at every point where the mountain tried to kill them. "She won't see them until—"

"I'll go." Bektour set down his water drums. He'd come up behind them silently, as he did everything, and now he was pulling on his pack with the straps already adjusted, because Bektour adjusted everything before it needed adjusting. "I know the ground below the hall. The scree field. If they come up the switchbacks, they have to cross it."

"You're not meeting four men alone," Yara said.

"I'm not meeting them." Bektour shouldered the pack. "I'm slowing them. There is a place where the trail narrows — the rockfall from last spring, you remember, we crossed it single file. I can make it impassable again. Ten minutes of work buys us an hour."

"And if they don't stop?"

Bektour looked at Yara with the level patience he brought to everything, even this. Even men with jerrycans coming to burn forty meters of irreplaceable history. "Then I will be between them and the wall for as long as between lasts."

"That's not—"

"Yara." Bektour said their name the way he said few words: carefully, completely. "My village is under sixty meters of reservoir water. I have stood on a boat above my own house and watched the water take the minaret. You think I do not know what it means when men come to erase a place?" He picked up his ice axe. "Go wake the others. Start moving the drums, the negatives, the laptops, up to the cave. I will be back before the rain."

He was gone through the colonnade before Yara finished drawing breath.

* * *

What followed was ninety minutes of the worst kind of work — urgent, physical, and utterly without glory. They hauled everything irreplaceable up the goat track to the shallow cave above the hall: Vann's map, the oilskin drum of originals, Tomas's notebooks, the satellite messenger, the food they couldn't afford to lose. Nadia came down off the ridge at a pace that should have broken an ankle and didn't, and when she understood what was happening she went white and then hard, and started carrying double loads without a word.

Tomas stayed at the hall's edge, watching the trail through binoculars, radio in hand. The static had gotten worse all day — the monsoon's leading edge, chewing the signal — but he could still hear Bektour's check-ins, terse and timed.

"Trail narrow point reached. Beginning work."

Ten minutes of silence.

"First section blocked. Moving to second."

Then, at 14:40 by Tomas's watch, the radio crackled and Bektour's voice came through wrong. Tight. Fast.

"They are not stopping. They are climbing past me. I am at the narrows, they are—" Static ate the rest.

"Bektour, say again." Nothing. "Say again, damn it."

Nadia appeared at his shoulder. "What did he say?"

"He's at the narrows. Four men coming up. He's going to try to hold the choke point." Tomas keyed the radio again. "Bektour, fall back. Do you copy? Fall back to the hall, we hold them here together—"

Static. Then, faintly, sounds that were not words. Shouting. Rock clattering. A sound Tomas would spend the next several nights hearing in his sleep: the flat, heavy knock of a body hitting stone.

Then nothing at all.

* * *

They went down the trail the way water goes downhill — total, committed, finding every line. Yara in front with the ice axe reversed, Nadia behind, Tomas last and hating it, because Tomas had spent eleven years learning that the rear position was where cowards put themselves and he refused, today, to be a coward.

They found the first consortium man fifty meters below the narrows, sitting against a boulder with his hands pressed to a gash on his scalp, jerrycan tipped over beside him, fuel pooling harmlessly into the dirt. He saw Yara's axe and raised both palms.

"He threw rocks at us," the man said, half laughing, half crying. "From above. Just — rocks. We couldn't get past. Petrov went up the side and he—" He gestured vaguely upward. "There was a slide. Loose stuff. He grabbed the guy — the porter — and they both went."

Tomas's stomach dropped through the mountain.

They found the second man ten minutes later, limping down toward the valley mouth, and Yara let him go with a look that promised consequences no court would ever enforce. And then they found the narrows, and the slide scar down the rock face, fresh and raw, and at the bottom of it, wedged against a slab, one leg bent in a way legs do not bend—

—and moving. Groaning. Alive.

Bektour's eyes found them before they reached him. He was grey-lipped, breathing in short controlled pulls, one arm pinned awkwardly beneath him, and the first thing he said — the first thing, before pain, before anything — was:

"The trail is blocked. They cannot come up. Check it."

"We'll check it," Yara said, kneeling. "We're checking you first."

"My leg is not the mission." But his voice cracked on *mission*, and Nadia made a sound low in her throat, and Tomas knelt on the other side and put his hands where his wilderness medicine course, eleven years and one revoked license ago, told him to put them.

Femur, almost certainly. Possible pelvic involvement. They splinted him with trekking poles and the yoke from his water drums, dosed him from the team med kit, and it took four of them on a rope litter system improvised from the haul lines to move him up the switchbacks — slow, brutal hours, the clouds lowering the whole time, the air going thick and mineral-smelling, the first fat drops starting to spot the rock at dusk.

At the top of the trail, before the colonnade, Bektour caught Yara's sleeve with his good hand.

"The wall," he said. "Did they reach the wall?"

"No," Yara said. "Nobody reached the wall."

Bektour nodded once, satisfied, and let the morphine take him.

* * *

They laid him in the great hall, on bedrolls dragged close to the mural wall, because it was the only shelter left worth the name and because — though nobody said it — it seemed right that he should wake beneath the thing he'd bled for. Tomas sat with him through the worst of the evening, monitoring pulses, keeping him warm, while outside the storm arrived in earnest.

It did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

By full dark the wind was screaming through the colonnade, and the temperature had fallen ten degrees, and the clouds — which had been a ceiling all day — became a wall, rolling over the rim of the valley and down its sides like something poured. The last light died greenish and strange. Rain came sideways, then harder than rain, a driven horizontal flood that turned the stream to a brown torrent in minutes and made the river at the valley mouth sound like a freight train.

Tomas stepped out under the pillar overhang once, just once, and looked up.

No stars. No rim. No horizon. The valley that had taken them two weeks to enter had closed around them like a fist, and somewhere above all that churning black, the monsoon proper was making landfall over the whole range, sealing the high passes under meters of falling water and snow.

Eleven days, Tomas had calculated in the storm-warning chapter of this expedition. They'd used nine getting here. The math had been tight and honest and they'd beaten it by a day, and it hadn't mattered, because the mountain had never been the thing trying to keep them here.

Corrigan was.

That was the thought Tomas carried back inside, wringing out his jacket, and it stopped him two steps into the hall. Because Corrigan's camp was at the valley mouth. Downstream. On the wrong side of a river in full spate, under the same drowning sky, with a man concussed and another limping and no route out that Tomas knew of until the eastern passes dried — weeks, possibly.

Corrigan wasn't leaving either.

Which meant that whatever happened next wouldn't happen on a mountainside, or a trail, or across the distance of a rifle shot. It would happen here, in this hall, face to face, with nowhere for anyone to go and nothing left to do but talk — or worse.

Yara was standing at the mouth of the colonnade, watching the dark where the valley mouth should be. Tomas came up beside them and followed their gaze and saw it: far off, small, flickering through the rain-haze, lights. Headlamps. A column of them, moving up from the flooded camp toward the hall, slow and deliberate, six points of light advancing through the storm like something rising from deep water.

"They're coming," Tomas said.

"I know," Yara said.

Behind them, the murals ran forty meters up the wall — the spears, the fire, the women on the terraces, the tall pale figures with banners standing over the dead — and the headlamps' glow reached the edge of the painted stone and began, slowly, to climb.
