## 19. Landfall

The storm did not arrive so much as descend.

One hour the rim of the valley was a clean line of granite against a bruised sky; the next it was gone, swallowed whole, and the rain came down in ropes so thick they made a sound like applause—ten thousand hands clapping on stone, on canvas, on skin. The temperature dropped ten degrees between one breath and the next. Bektour's litter, rigged under an overhang at the base of the great hall, was soaked through before we got the tarp doubled over him.

"Inside," I shouted to Tomas over the noise. "Everyone inside."

The hall had no door anymore—that had rotted or been pulled down centuries ago—but the entrance was a low throat of dressed stone, barely shoulder-width, and once you were through it the roar of the storm fell away like someone had shut a window. We stood dripping in the dark while our headlamps found the walls: the murals, the procession of figures, the long terrible frieze of the massacre that Nadia had photographed twice over.

Corrigan came through last. He'd sent his men back under the overhang with the porters who hadn't defected—the two who remained looked like they wanted to be anywhere else—and he ducked into the hall alone, water sheeting off his jacket, and stood there breathing hard like a man who'd just run a race he'd already lost.

"Well," he said. "This is cozy."

Nobody answered him. Nadia checked the seals on the evidence case for the fourth time that hour. Tomas spread the radio's corpse on a flat stone and poked at it without hope. Bektour lay where we'd carried him, leg splinted, feverish but awake, watching Corrigan with the flat patience of a man who has decided not to hate anyone until there's time for it later.

I built the stove up in the center of the hall, under the smoke hole, because if we were going to be trapped together we were going to be warm doing it. Corrigan watched me light it. He didn't ask permission. That was something I'd learned about him over the past weeks: he never asked permission, but he also never took what wasn't offered. He waited. It was more unnerving than violence would have been.

"You could have burned this place," I said, nodding at the walls. "You tried. Why stop?"

"Because your porter put himself between my man and a wall of dry old plaster, and because the rain came, and because—" he paused, choosing, "—because burning it wouldn't have worked anyway. You'd already shipped half of it downriver. I've known that since the gorge. Burning it now is just arson with bad publicity."

"So why are you still here?"

He looked around the hall, at the murals lit orange by the stove flame, at the figures marching across five hundred years of somebody's memory. For a moment he seemed genuinely to consider the question.

"Shelter," he said finally. "Same as you."

* * *

The storm settled in for the night the way storms do in these mountains—not passing through but moving in, unpacking, making itself at home. Water found paths down the walls that hadn't run in four hundred years and ran them again, silver threads in the lamplight. Somewhere above us a slab of the roof shifted and ground against its neighbors, and everyone flinched, and nothing collapsed.

We ate. Corrigan produced food from his own pack—decent food, freeze-dried meals from a brand none of us could afford—and set three of them near the stove without comment. Nadia stared at them like they might be poisoned. Bektour took one and said thank you, which broke whatever spell the rest of us were maintaining.

It was past midnight when Corrigan said my name.

Not Zhou. Not *the guide*. My name, first name, the one nobody on this mountain used except my mother, twenty years ago, in a tent at Site Nine.

I looked up. He was sitting across the fire with his boots off, which somehow made him look younger and worse at the same time.

"You don't remember me," he said. "That's fine. I didn't expect you to. I was nobody then. Twenty-three years old, straight out of a surveying program I'd paid for with loans, and the consortium gave me a job title and a truck and told me I was part of history." He turned his hands toward the fire. "I drove the supply runs to Site Nine. Summer of the bones."

The word went through me like cold water. Beside me, Nadia went very still.

"You were there," I said.

"I was there for all of it. The excavation, the... pause. The men who came in trucks with no markings. The day they fenced off the north trench and told Dr. Zhou's team the site was being reclassified as geologically unstable." His voice was level, almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "And I remember a kid. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Your mother brought you out that summer because she thought—God knows why she thought it—a dig would be good for you. And you climbed down into the north trench before the fence went up, and you came out holding a femur, and you asked her what animal it was from."

"I know what I asked," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "She said human. She said sit down."

"And then the men came, and there were papers, and your mother sat with you for two hours in the supply tent explaining that some things couldn't be undone by shouting, that the science would come out eventually, that the important thing was that *you* weren't hurt." He shook his head slowly. "I carried the tea in. I stood at the flap of that tent and listened to a brilliant woman tell her child a lie she probably believed herself, because believing it was the only way she could keep working. And I signed the same agreement you signed, except mine had a salary attached."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof in waves, like the mountain itself was trying to say something.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because you keep looking at me like I'm the monster in this story, and I need you to understand that there isn't one. There's just a line of people, each one holding a small piece of something awful, each one telling themselves their piece is too small to matter." He gestured at the murals. "That's how it happened here too, isn't it? Look at it. It wasn't one massacre. It was a garrison commander following orders, and clerks recording rations, and masons building the wall over the bodies afterward because the bodies needed to go somewhere and the order came from someone whose face they never saw. Nobody in this picture thought they were the villain. They were all just standing where they stood, doing the next thing."

"That's not an excuse," Nadia said sharply. "That's a defense brief."

"It's both," Corrigan said. "That's the problem. It's always both."

* * *

Bektour slept. Tomas slept, or pretended to, curled against the far wall with his back to all of us—an eloquent statement in itself. Nadia stayed awake, arms around her knees, listening the way she'd been listening since Harrow Bend: like every conversation might contain a piece of her grandfather she hadn't gotten yet.

Around two in the morning, Corrigan said, "Your grandfather was better than any of us."

Nadia's head came up.

"Elias Vann. I tracked him for eleven years, you know. Not to hurt him—at least, not at first. The consortium wanted to know how much he knew, whether he had documentation, whether he could be bought. Standard due diligence for a liability." He laughed, short and joyless. "I met him twice. Once in Harrow Bend, once in a border town whose name I won't say, because I've done enough damage there. Both times he bought me a drink. Both times he talked to me like I was a person. And both times I filed my report and went home and didn't sleep."

"What changed?" Nadia asked. "Between *not hurting him* and your partner dying in his camp?"

Corrigan was quiet for a long time. The rain filled the silence.

"The company changed," he said. "Or I finally saw it clearly—I honestly can't tell you which, and I've spent years trying. There was a shift, maybe eight years ago. The new directorate stopped asking *how much does Vann know* and started asking *what does it cost to make Vann irrelevant*. And the men they hired to answer that question weren't due-diligence men. They were the kind of men who interpret *irrelevant* broadly." He rubbed his face. "My partner Rook was one of them. I want that on the record, wherever your record ends up. I didn't kill Elias Vann's partner. But I trained Rook. I recommended him. When the directorate asked me who could handle a sensitive field matter, I gave them a name, because giving names was my job, and I didn't ask what *sensitive* meant, because not asking was also my job."

"And now?" I said.

"Now I'm sitting in a four-hundred-year-old courtroom," he said, nodding at the murals, "waiting out a storm with the evidence that ends my career, and I find that I'm relieved. Does that make sense? Twelve days ago I'd have traded all of you to the river for a quiet retirement. Tonight I can't muster it. Maybe it's the altitude. Maybe it's watching a man with a broken leg share his dinner with the people who broke it." He looked at Bektour's sleeping shape. "Maybe it's just that I'm tired, and tired people stop carrying things they should have put down years ago."

"You're still going to try to bury it," Nadia said. Not a question.

"No," Corrigan said. "I'm going to try to survive it. Those aren't the same thing anymore, though I understand why you'd think they were." He reached slowly, deliberately, into his inner pocket, and set something on the stone between us: a slim black notebook, its cover swollen with damp. "My reports. Eleven years of them. Dates, names, transfer orders, the payment trail for the north trench 'stabilization.' It's everything you don't have and everything you'll need, because photographs of murals prove what happened here, but paper proves who decided to hide it." He pushed it an inch toward the firelight. "I'm not giving it to you. I'm leaving it there. What happens to it next is your problem, not mine. That way I can tell myself whatever I need to tell myself tomorrow. Same as always."

Nadia picked it up before I could speak. She held it like it might bite, then zipped it into the evidence case beside her grandfather's photographs, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and something else underneath, something almost like grief for a man she'd hated correctly for weeks and now had to hate incorrectly forever.

* * *

Dawn, if you could call it that, was a gray suggestion behind the smoke hole. The rain had thickened rather than broken. Through the entrance throat we could see the valley floor transformed—a brown torrent where the stream had been, terraces vanishing under sheet flow, the standing stones reduced to dark teeth in churning water. No one was going anywhere. Not down, not out, not after anyone. The mountain had closed its hand.

Tomas woke, assessed the radio wreckage again out of pure habit, and said what we were all thinking: "Four days to landfall, minimum, before anything moves. If the eastern route holds."

"If," Bektour said, from his litter, with the first smile I'd seen on him in two days.

Corrigan stood at the entrance, looking out at the drowned valley. I came and stood beside him, because someone had to, and because I'd realized sometime in the night that hating him had become a luxury the storm wouldn't let us afford.

"My mother," I said. "After Site Nine. Did she ever—"

"She spent the rest of her career trying to get back to that trench," Corrigan said quietly. "Every permit application, every funding proposal, every conference paper with a footnote pointing north. I read them all, later, when it became my job to read them. She never said a word about what happened. She just kept building a ladder back to it, rung by rung, for thirty years, hoping someone would climb down before she died." He glanced at me. "You're the ladder, Ms. Zhou. In case nobody told you. Everything you're doing right now—she designed it without knowing it."

I didn't answer. There was nothing in me that could have answered without breaking, and breaking was a thing I'd scheduled for later, maybe for Harrow Bend, maybe for a press conference, maybe for a kitchen table across from a woman who had lied to protect me and imprisoned me with the same sentence.

Behind us, Nadia opened the black notebook and began to read aloud—softly, just for Tomas, just dates and names at first—and the sound of it filled the great hall the way the rain filled the valley, steady and unstoppable, water finding the channels that had been cut for it centuries ago.

Outside, the storm tightened its grip. Inside, the record grew longer by the hour.

And then, near midday, through the roar, Bektour—who had been silent so long we'd stopped checking on him—said, "Listen," and we all heard it at the same time: beneath the rain, from the direction of the western traverse, a sound no storm makes.

Voices. More of them than Corrigan had brought.
