## 20. The Long Way Down

The storm broke on the fourth morning the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and leaving everything it touched changed.

Yara stood at the valley rim while the clouds peeled back in ragged sheets, revealing sky so clean and blue it looked manufactured. Below, the green terraces steamed in the new sun. The great hall's roof had held. Bektour's litter, lashed together from tent poles and pack frames, waited where they'd built it three days ago, because they'd all known—even before the rain stopped—that this was how it would end. Not with helicopters. Not with rescue. With their own legs and their own hands, carrying what they'd found down out of the mountains before anyone could take it from them.

"Eastern route," Tomas said, coming up beside Yara with the map spread flat against his chest to keep the wind off it. He'd been over it eleven times since landfall. Yara knew because they'd counted. "Vann marked it in red. Three days if we're lucky. Five if the weather turns again."

"And if the weather turns again?"

Tomas didn't answer. He didn't have to.

Below them, Nadia was checking the litter's rigging for the sixth time that hour, her fingers moving along each knot with a precision that had nothing to do with climbing and everything to with grief. Bektour lay on his back, his splinted leg propped on a rolled sleeping pad, watching the sky with an expression Yara had learned to read as patience. Not resignation—patience. There was a difference, and Bektour embodied it. A man whose village had drowned under consortium water, who'd taken a twelve-meter fall protecting a wall of painted stone, and who lay now in a homemade stretcher waiting to be carried home like cargo—he had every right to resignation. Instead he had patience, and jokes, most of them terrible.

"The eastern route," Bektour called up, having apparently heard every word. "Is this the one Vann described as 'steep but honest' or 'honest but steep'?"

"Honest," Yara called back. "Mostly."

"Mostly honest." Bektour laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway. "Like everyone I've ever trusted."

Corrigan stood apart, near the mouth of the great hall, with his two remaining men and the particular stillness of a man who has run out of moves. The monsoon had done what Yara never could: stripped him of options. His radio was dead, his exit ropes were cut—his own sabotage, turned against him when the storm sealed the passes—and his white flags were folded now, packed away, meaningless. Last night, in the firelight of the great hall, he'd told them about Site Nine. About being twenty-three years old and following orders and signing his name to a lie that had grown roots inside him for thirty years. He hadn't asked for forgiveness. Yara hadn't offered any. But something had shifted between them in that long wet night—not trust, nothing like trust, but recognition. Two people who had both been made complicit by other people's ambitions, one at nineteen, one at twenty-three, and who had spent the rest of their lives paying interest on it.

Now Corrigan watched them prepare to leave, and Yara watched him watch, and neither of them pretended not to.

"You're taking the eastern route," Corrigan said. It wasn't a question.

"It's the only one open."

"It's also the one nobody knows about." He came closer, hands loose at his sides, an old man's careful walk on scree. "Which means when you come down into Harrow Bend with a litter and a story, there'll be no record of how you got there. No satellite passes. No permits filed. Nothing." He paused. "That's either very smart or very dangerous."

"Both," Nadia said, without looking up from her knots.

Corrigan almost smiled. Almost. Then he looked past them, at the terraces below, at the standing stones catching first light, and something moved across his face that Yara recognized because they'd seen it in mirrors for ten years—the look of a person measuring the distance between what they did and what they should have done, and finding it unbridgeable.

"I have men in Harrow Bend," he said quietly. "Company men. If I radioed them—which I can't—they'd meet you at the trailhead." He met Yara's eyes. "I'm telling you this because I'm not going to radio them. Understand? Whatever happens next, whatever you do with all of it—" he gestured, vaguely, at the valley, the evidence, the whole impossible weight of it, "—that's yours. I'm done carrying other people's secrets down mountains."

Yara searched his face for the trick and found, to their own surprise, that they believed him. Not because he deserved belief. Because he looked, finally, like a man setting down something heavy.

"We leave in an hour," Yara said.

* * *

The eastern route began as kindness and ended as punishment, which was, Yara reflected somewhere on the second day, the most honest thing a mountain route could be.

The first day was gentle—a descending shelf of meadow below the ice line, wildflowers flattened by the storm, meltwater running everywhere in bright threads. They made good time. Bektour, carried in shifts, narrated the landscape like a tour guide with a grudge: *that slope, my cousin would have grazed goats there; that stream, my grandmother would have said the water remembers where it's been.* The others took turns carrying him because that was the arithmetic of a litter—four carriers, one passenger, rotate every forty minutes—and because the work itself had become something none of them named aloud. Sacred wasn't the word. Necessary was.

By the second day the meadows gave way to forest, and the forest gave way to the traverse Vann had marked in red with a single annotation in his cramped hand: *Exposed. Move early. Trust the rock, not your eyes.*

They moved early. They moved through fog that rose off the valley floor in slow gray columns, across ledges no wider than a pack strap, with six hundred meters of empty air breathing cold against their left shoulders. Yara went first, testing each hold, calling back the rhythm: *solid, solid, loose—step wide—solid.* Behind them came Tomas with the front of the litter, then Nadia, then Corrigan's two men, who had quietly become part of the expedition somewhere during the storm without anyone deciding it, then Corrigan himself, then the rear of the litter with Bektour giving cheerful commentary about how this reminded him of a bridge in his home province that had killed four people and been declared a national monument.

"Is that supposed to be comforting?" Nadia gasped, halfway across the worst section.

"No," Bektour said. "It's supposed to be true. Comfort is your own department."

It was Nadia who slipped. Not badly—one boot skidding on wet limestone, a half-second of weightlessness, her hand slapping flat against rock—but enough that the litter lurched, enough that Bektour made a sound low in his throat, enough that for one suspended moment the whole human chain went still above the drop while the fog swallowed their shouts.

Then Tomas locked. Yara felt it through the rope more than saw it—the surveyor bracing against a nub of stone with his whole body, taking the litter's full swinging weight onto his arms, holding it, holding it, saying nothing, just holding, until Nadia found her footing and the load eased and the chain breathed again.

Nobody spoke about it afterward. That was the thing about the mountains, Yara thought, as they came off the traverse into the trees and let themselves shake where no one could see: the mountains kept your worst moments and your best ones in the same silence, and you carried both down with you, and the carrying was the point.

* * *

On the third night, camped in a hollow of pines with the lowlands' first far lights visible through the trees, Bektour asked Yara to sit with him.

The others had gone ahead to check tomorrow's river crossing. Firelight moved on the litter's canvas. Bektour's leg, splinted and bound, stuck out straight as a compass needle.

"In my village," he said, "when someone is carried, there is a rule. The person being carried does not thank the carriers. Do you know why?"

Yara shook their head.

"Because thanks makes it a debt. And family does not carry family to collect debts." He studied the fire. "You have been thanking me all week. For the fall, for the wall, for my stupid jokes. So I am telling you the rule now, before we reach the bottom, where you will want to say something large and embarrassing."

Yara laughed—a rough, unexpected sound. "And what am I supposed to do instead?"

"Nothing. That is the rule." Bektour's eyes crinkled. "But I will tell you a secret. My village flooded nine years ago. The company sent a man with papers, and the papers said the water was progress, and everyone signed except my father, who could not read, and so he signed too, because the man held the paper in front of his face and told him where to make the mark." He was quiet a moment. "For nine years I thought the shame was ours. For signing. For going. Tonight, lying on this thing you built me, looking at these people you collected like stray cats—I think maybe the shame was always theirs. We just agreed to hold it for them."

"That's what Site Nine was," Yara said. The words came easier than they ever had. "A nineteen-year-old kid holding a company's shame. My mother handed it to me herself. She said, *sign this and you can stay in the life.*"

"And did staying in the life feel like living?"

Yara looked at the far lights. Ten years of treks, of clients who never asked questions, of a name shortened and a history buried. "No," they said. "It felt like hiding in plain sight."

Bektour nodded slowly, as if Yara had confirmed something he'd known all along. "Then carry us down," he said. "All of us. The bones too. And when you get to the bottom, put everything down at once."

* * *

They reached the river on the fifth morning.

Nadia's fast team had run this same water eleven days ago on a raft of tent poles, losing the duplicate negatives to the current but keeping the originals sealed in oilskin—originals that were now, Yara knew, sewn into the lining of her jacket, riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The gorge here was lower, wider, calmer than the killing stretch upstream, and they crossed it the way Vann's map indicated: a ford at the gravel bar, waist-deep, linked arm to arm.

The litter went across last, hoisted overhead by all six of them at once, Bektour laughing the entire way—actually laughing, arms out like a man on a carnival ride—until they set him down on the south bank and the laughter caught in his throat and became, briefly, something else. He pressed one palm flat against the ground. Just held it there.

"Solid," he said. "Honest ground."

They stood a moment in the shallows, dripping, the whole strange family of them—two prospectors' heirs, a disgraced surveyor, a fixer's defected crew, a porter with a broken leg, and a guide who had spent a decade being no one. Behind them the Kessler Range stood up into cloud, its high valleys already closing for the season, the green valley invisible from here, undocumented by anything but memory and oilskin and the murals on a wall that Corrigan's employers had tried to burn and failed.

In Harrow Bend, Nadia would call the journalists and the national archive in the same hour. In Harrow Bend, Yara would stand beside their mother and tell the whole story, including Site Nine, including the signature, including the price. None of that had happened yet. It was all downstream, waiting.

Tomas wrung out his shirt and checked the map one last time. "Trailhead's a day's walk. Then the road."

Corrigan's two men had already started walking. Corrigan lingered, looking back up at the mountains with an expression Yara decided not to interpret.

"Zhou," he said, using the name fully for the first time. "When this goes public—and it will go public—there are people who will come for you. Quietly. Politely. With papers." He shouldered his pack. "Don't sign anything."

"I learned that lesson at nineteen."

"Good." He walked a few steps, then stopped. "Your mother's field notes. The ones that matched Vann's survey marks. Did she know what she was annotating?"

Yara had asked themselves that question every night since the glacier. They still didn't have an answer that fit in a sentence. "She knew what she was paid to know," Yara said. "Whether she knew more than that—" they looked up at the range, at everything hidden in it, "—that's a conversation we're going to have. Finally. Face to face."

Corrigan nodded once, as if that, too, was an answer he'd expected, and followed his men up the trail.

They walked. All day and into the dusk, the litter rotating shoulder to shoulder, the forest opening toward the smell of woodsmoke and diesel that meant the world—the ordinary, compromised, waiting world. Somewhere behind them the monsoon clouds were regathering over the passes, sealing the high country until spring, keeping the valley's secret one more season. Somewhere ahead, in a dockside town with one bar and too many telephones, the evidence against an empire sat stitched into a jacket lining, patient as a seed.

Near midnight, under a sky suddenly crowded with stars, Bektour spoke from the litter, drowsy, half-dreaming.

"Yara. When we get down. What will you be?"

Yara walked a while before answering. Not hiding anymore. Not invisible. Not their mother's shadow, not the consortium's silence, not the careful blank space where a person used to be.

"A witness," they said.

Ahead, the first lights of Harrow Bend flickered through the trees—and among them, unmistakable even at this distance, a cluster of vehicles parked outside the dockside bar, headlights burning, engines idling, as if someone had been waiting a long time for exactly this trail, exactly this night, exactly them.
