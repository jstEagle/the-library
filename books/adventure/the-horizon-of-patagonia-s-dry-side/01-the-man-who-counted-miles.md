## 1. The Man Who Counted Miles

The spur line out of Cañadón Mocoso had been dead for nine years, and Orsen Nystrom still walked it every morning before the sun cleared the basalt.

He did not think of it as a ritual. Rituals were for priests and widows, and he was neither. He thought of it as inspection. The fact that no one paid him to inspect a decommissioned spur — that the company had struck it from its books in 1919 and would not care if every sleeper rotted to black lace by Christmas — was beside the point. A man kept his habits the way a mule kept its footing: not because the ground required it, but because the day it stopped, everything else gave way too.

He set out at first light, when the scrubland was the color of pewter and the wind had not yet decided which direction it meant to bully him from. The spur ran four kilometers and three hundred meters east of the station house, out toward the old gravel pit, and along its length lay eleven hundred and forty-two sleepers. He knew this because he had counted them. He counted them most mornings, lips moving slightly, one hand trailing the cold iron of the rail as though greeting an old horse.

*One. Two. Three.*

The sleepers were quebracho, most of them, imported at ruinous expense in the years when the Southern Patagonia Railway still believed in itself, and they had weathered into shapes like ribs — the land's ribs, or the railway's, depending on how sentimental a man was feeling, and Orsen Nystrom permitted himself no sentiment before seven o'clock. Some had split lengthwise. Some had been pulled up by enterprising neighbors for fence posts and firewood, leaving gaps in the count that he noted with a small professional displeasure, as though the thieves had defaced a document rather than salvaged scrap.

*Forty-one. Forty-two.*

He paused at forty-two. He always paused at forty-two, though there was nothing there but a sleeper more sound than its neighbors, sitting square in its bed of ballast like a man who had never done anything wrong. He stood over it for perhaps four seconds, then walked on. Anyone watching from the station house — and nobody watched; nobody ever watched — would have seen only a tall, narrow man in a canvas coat stopping to look at nothing, the way surveyors do, checking some angle invisible to civilians.

Twelve years ago he had stopped sleeping properly. Twelve years ago he had started walking this spur, or spurs like it, wherever the company posted him, and he had learned that a body in motion at dawn could pass for a body at peace. That was the whole trick of it. Discipline was just loneliness wearing a good coat, and he had worn the coat so long now that he sometimes forgot there had ever been another garment.

The sun came up behind him and threw his shadow out ahead along the rail, long and thin and slightly bent at the shoulder, the way his shadow had been since the accident. Not *the* accident. There had been several accidents in twelve years of Patagonian railroading; a man collected them the way his boots collected thorns. But there was one accident he did not name, even in the privacy of his own skull, and when his thoughts drifted toward it — as they did on mornings when the light came up gold and forgiving — he did what he always did. He bent down, took hold of a sleeper's edge, and measured it with his thumb against the knuckle marks he'd trained into his right hand years ago: eight feet six inches, standard gauge timber, give or take the rot.

Measuring was a kind of prayer, if a man needed one. It said: *the world has dimensions, and I know them, and while I know them I am safe inside them.*

*Two hundred and six. Two hundred and seven.*

At the two-hundred-and-seventh sleeper the rail began to sing.

It was a faint thing, a hum transmitted through sixty-year-old iron, and a civilian would have missed it entirely. Orsen felt it come up through his boot soles and stop him mid-stride, and for a moment he simply stood there with his head cocked like an old dog hearing a whistle only it remembered. Trains sang like that. He had spent thirty years listening to iron tell him things — where a wheel was flat, where a fishplate had worked loose, where the roadbed underneath had washed hollow and a locomotive at speed would find out the hard way. Iron was honest. Iron was the only witness in Patagonia that never lied to save itself.

But nothing ran on this spur. Nothing had run on it since the gravel contract lapsed, and the singing was not the song of wheels. It was thinner than that, higher, arrhythmic — the vibration of something striking the rail far off, near the pit, again and again. A man with a hammer. Or a crowbar against the web of the rail, testing it, the way a company man tests whether track is worth pulling up for salvage.

Orsen checked the wind. It was coming off the plateau, out of the west, which meant sound carried east toward him cleanly. He left the rail bed without hurrying — hurrying told a man's enemies things — and cut up through the zampa scrub onto the low rise, where he could see the last eight hundred meters of spur and the ruined loading gantry at the pit beyond.

There was a horse tied at the gantry. A good horse, city-shod, standing with the patient resentment of an animal that had been tied too long. And beneath the gantry, crouched at the rail with a short iron bar, was a man in a town coat raising it and lowering it against the railhead, listening after each strike with his ear nearly touching the metal.

Listening for what? You didn't test salvage rail by ringing it like a bell. Salvage men tested it by sight and pry. No — Orsen narrowed his eyes against the strengthening light — the man wasn't testing the rail. He was using the rail. Using it the way telegraph lines were once used by boys who tapped messages between towns for the pleasure of it, or the way prisoners signaled through walls. Sound travels in iron better than it travels in air. If you wanted to know whether anyone was walking a dead spur at dawn — if you wanted to know it from four kilometers away without being seen doing the asking —

Orsen withdrew a step, slow, letting a clump of coiron grass close between himself and the gantry.

The man straightened, folded his bar away inside his coat, and mounted. He rode west, toward the station, at an easy canter, passing within two hundred meters of Orsen's rise without a glance. Town clothes, town hat, a face Orsen half-knew and couldn't place — one of the provisioning-house crowd, maybe, or one of the board's errand men who passed through twice a year looking at ledgers and leaving them exactly as they found them. The horse kicked up a thread of dust that hung in the still air like a signature.

Orsen stayed on the rise until the rider was gone, then looked back down at the rail, at the place where the man had struck it. Then, because the habit was stronger than the caution, he finished his walk. *Four hundred and ninety. Four hundred and ninety-one.* His lips moved. The sun climbed. The shadow shortened and stopped bending quite so much.

* * *

The telegram was waiting for him at the station house, which was itself a small event, because telegrams did not come to Cañadón Mocoso. Telegrams went to Comodoro Rivadavia, to Puerto Deseado, to places where the wires actually terminated. What came to Cañadón Mocoso was the occasional waybill, the monthly pay chit, and once — memorably — a crate of survey stakes mislabeled from a job in Río Negro that the stationmaster had kept out of spite for two years before burning them for kindling.

The stationmaster was named Elizondo, a round, gray man who had held this post for longer than Orsen had held any post anywhere, and who administered the station's business — such as it was — from a desk buried under timetables for trains that no longer existed. He held the yellow flimsy out at arm's length, the way he held everything, as though it might be damp.

"From Puerto Deseado," Elizondo said. "Came up on the morning motor. The driver says answer's wanted same day." He paused, and his small eyes did the arithmetic they always did when anything unusual crossed his desk. "You expecting anything out of Deseado?"

"No."

"Because the last time someone important remembered you existed—"

"The last time," Orsen said, taking the flimsy, "was a waybill."

Elizondo let it go, which was a courtesy, and turned back to his timetables, which was a mercy. Orsen carried the telegram outside to read it in the open, where the wind could take any expression off his face before it finished forming.

The message was brief. They were all brief; brevity cost money by the word, and the Southern Patagonia Railway counted words the way Orsen counted sleepers.

> NYSTROM ORSEN ENGINEER STOP REPORT PUERTO DESEADO WITHOUT DELAY STOP MATTER CONCERNS SURVEY RECORDS 1901 STOP TRAVEL AUTHORIZED FIRST CLASS STOP BY ORDER BOARD OF DIRECTORS

He read it three times. The wind came off the plateau and worried the paper in his hand, and he folded it away into his breast pocket with the deliberation of a man putting out a small fire.

*Survey records 1901.*

Nineteen-oh-one. The year the company had surveyed the Meseta del Viento and pronounced it waterless, worthless, unworthy of steel — the year before the year everyone in the firm's memory agreed never to discuss, though no one had ever told him directly not to discuss it, which was how the company did its forbidding: by silence, distributed evenly, like ballast. Nineteen-oh-one was a date that lived in the margins of other documents, in the gap between one annual report and the next, in the reason a certain file drawer in the Puerto Deseado office was kept locked and its key kept on a ring that traveled in the general manager's waistcoat pocket.

Orsen knew about that key. He had known about it for eleven years, ever since the season he'd spent reorganizing the Deseado archives — a punishment dressed as an assignment, though he had never confirmed with anyone, including himself, what offense it punished. He had catalogued four thousand documents that spring, and he had found the gap the way he found all gaps: by measuring. Surveys were numbered consecutively, and somewhere between number 214 and number 216 there was a space where 215 should have been, and the register book said *215 — Meseta del Viento reconnaissance — withdrawn,* in ink that matched no other entry in the book, written by a hand that appeared nowhere else in eleven hundred pages.

Withdrawn. Not lost. Not destroyed. Withdrawn, the way a man withdraws money he intends to spend, or a statement he intends never to repeat.

He had closed the register book that afternoon and gone out and walked the Deseado breakwater until dark, and he had told himself what he always told himself: *not your business. You are a man who measures things the company asks him to measure. The company does not ask. Therefore it is not your business.* And he had gone back the next morning and catalogued documents 216 through 4,000 with perfect, blameless attention, and the gap had sat in his mind for eleven years like a sleeper missing from a roadbed — unnoticed by every train, and yet somehow, always, underfoot.

Now the board itself was summoning him. First class travel. *Without delay.* Concerning 1901.

He considered the ways this could be bad, and found the list embarrassingly short, because every item on it ended the same way: they know you saw the gap. Eleven years of careful silence, and either it had held and this was something else entirely, or it had not held and this was the bill coming due. There was no third reading. The company did not send first-class tickets to forgotten men out of nostalgia.

Unless — and here his mind, treacherous instrument that it was, offered the alternative the way a rail offers a junction — unless they wanted him for the opposite reason. Unless somebody up the line had finally decided the past was worth more than its weight in locked drawers. Unless there was money in 1901 now, or politics, or fear, and they wanted the one engineer in the territory who could read an old survey the way a priest reads scripture, and who could be trusted to keep his mouth shut afterward, because his record on that score was — he almost smiled — impeccable.

Either way, the summons was a door. And doors, in Orsen Nystrom's experience, opened in only two directions: further out into the empty country he had made his home, or back toward the thing he had walked twelve years to put behind him.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he always did, because whatever the telegram meant, the instruments still needed cleaning and the quarterly condition report on the Mocoso section still needed writing, and a man who abandoned his routine on the strength of a yellow flimsy was a man who had already admitted the flimsy mattered more than the work. He cleaned the theodolite. He oiled the tripod screws. He wrote *sleeper deterioration within expected parameters* in the report, which was true, and *no traffic anticipated*, which was also true, and both sentences together described a career with the accuracy of an obituary.

In the late afternoon he packed.

He packed the way he did everything: completely, without drama. Instruments in their case, wrapped in wool. The oilskin coat, because the plateau made its own weather regardless of the season. His mother's Bible, which he had not opened in twelve years but which had crossed the Atlantic with him from Bergen and would cross whatever distance remained, because some cargo a man simply hauled. He stood over his kit bag a long moment, then added the small tin box from the bottom of his trunk — the one containing a broken watch, a photograph gone gray, and a letter he had never answered — and then, after a moment, took it out again and put it back in the trunk, and shut the trunk on it.

Some cargo stayed where you buried it. That was the entire art of getting through the years.

Elizondo watched him load the mule in the blue hour, hands in his pockets, radiating the particular satisfaction of a man who has predicted something.

"Deseado," Elizondo said. "First class. Imagine."

"It's a long ride either way."

"Eleven years I've watched you turn down transfers. Comodoro, twice. Gallegos, once — and Gallegos pays double." He rocked on his heels. "A man turns down double wages often enough, I start to think it isn't the wages he's avoiding. It's the offices. Offices have chairs in them, and chairs invite a man to sit, and sitting invites questions." He squinted at Orsen through the dusk. "This one must be different. This one came looking for *you*."

Orsen tightened the last strap. "Keep an eye on the spur," he said. "Section four's ballast is washing. After rain it'll need—"

"I'll note it in the report."

"You'll note it accurately."

"I always do." Elizondo unfolded his arms. "Twelve years, Nystrom. You've walked that dead line every morning God sent, counting ties like a rosary. Don't think I haven't seen. A man doesn't count things that don't matter to him. So whatever's waiting in Deseado—" he shrugged, vast and gray against the last of the light, "—I hope it's worth breaking the count for."

Orsen led the mule out onto the trail before he trusted his face. The wind had died with the sun, and the scrubland lay quiet, and behind him the rails of the dead spur caught the final light and held it, two bright threads running out toward the pit and the horizon and no destination at all.

He did not look back at the station. He had learned that much, at least, in twelve years: you never looked back at a place you might be glad to have left, because gladness was a debt the country always collected later, with interest.

The trail to Puerto Deseado ran south across eighty miles of nothing in particular, and he covered the first miles in the dark by the simple method of having walked them before, in worse conditions, with less reason. The mule plodded. The stars did what Patagonian stars did, which was multiply until the sky seemed less like a ceiling than like a second, finer desert laid over the first. And Orsen Nystrom rode through it counting — not sleepers now, but leagues, then hours, then the rhythm of the hooves, then, when the rhythm failed, the years themselves, backward, the way a man counts change he suspects is short.

Twelve. Eleven. Ten.

Somewhere around year nine he made himself stop, because the counting was going the wrong direction, and a surveyor knows better than anyone that a measurement taken backward never closes the loop. You could not get back to zero by subtraction. Everyone who tried arrived somewhere else instead.

Toward dawn on the second day, with the sea smell reaching inland to meet him and the towers of Puerto Deseado showing above the last fold of the plain like punctuation at the end of a very long sentence, he reined in and took out the telegram and read it a fourth time.

*Matter concerns survey records 1901.*

And beneath the printed words, in the margin, in pencil — pencil, which no telegraph clerk used, pencil, which meant someone had handled this flimsy after transmission and marked it deliberately, knowing it would reach his hands — someone had written five characters:

*AGUA?*

Orsen sat his mule in the cold sea wind and looked at those five characters for a long time. Then he put the flimsy away, turned the mule's head toward the town, and rode down to find out who in Puerto Deseado knew enough to ask the question he had spent eleven years refusing to ask himself.
