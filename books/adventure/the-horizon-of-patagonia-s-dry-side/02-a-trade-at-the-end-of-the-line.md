## 2. A Trade at the End of the Line

The telegram had said *URGENT — COME AT ONCE — SALAS*, and nothing else, and Orsen Nystrom had spent the whole ride down from the spur thinking about how few words a man needed to ruin another man's week. Urgent. Come at once. A name he half-knew from a decade of waybills and freight manifests: Efraín Salas, who had once shipped four crates of ore samples out of Deseado marked *AGRICULTURAL IMPLEMENTS*, and who had once, at a station Christmas dinner Orsen barely remembered attending, told a story about a mule that could smell water through forty feet of limestone. Everyone had laughed. Orsen hadn't. He'd been too busy wondering whether it was true.

Now he stood in the corridor of the Hotel Comercial with his knuckles an inch from the door of room 9, and the smell reached him before anything else did — kerosene, thick and sweetish, the way it got when a lamp burned low all night in a closed room, layered over something else underneath, something medicinal and sour that he recognized without wanting to. His father's room had smelled like that for the last month of his life. Camphor and turpentine and the particular staleness of a body that had stopped bothering to be a body.

He knocked.

"Open," said a voice from inside, cracked but carrying, the voice of a man who had spent years shouting across canyons. "It's open, it's been open since yesterday, come in before the draft does."

The room was small and mean and lit to a dim amber by two lamps burning on the table, which explained the kerosene if not the extravagance. The window was shuttered against the afternoon wind, and the wind hit the shutters anyway, a long steady shove, so that the whole building seemed to breathe. Efraín Salas lay propped in the iron bed like cargo that had shifted in transit, a blanket over legs that were clearly no longer taking instructions, and his face —

Orsen had prepared himself for a dying man. He had not prepared himself for the eyes. They were dark and quick and entirely undimmed, set in a face gone the color and texture of old saddle leather, and they took him in from boots to collar in one pass, cataloguing, weighing, the way Orsen himself read a grade or a cut.

"Nystrom," Salas said. "You came. I owe you a drink you'll never collect."

"You owe me an explanation. Your telegram was eleven words."

"Twelve." A dry rattle that might have been laughter. "I counted them twice. Sit down. No — not the chair, the chair wobbles, sit on the trunk. It holds what the chair can't."

Orsen sat on the trunk. It was a good trunk, brass-cornered, scarred, the kind a man bought young and kept forever because everything he owned fit inside it. There was very little else in the room. A canvas pack with its straps buckled. A tin cup. A bottle of gin standing three-quarters empty beside the lamps, though the smell in the room suggested the kerosene was doing more work than the gin these days. On the table, weighted under the cup, lay a folded square of oilcloth the size of a large book.

"I'll be plain," Salas said, "because I haven't got the hours to be otherwise. You're the engineer. Twelve years on the dry side. You've walked every sleeper between here and the cordillera, they tell me, including the ones nobody pays a man to walk."

"Somebody paid me to walk most of them."

"And the rest you walked for free, because you couldn't help yourself." The dark eyes didn't blink. "That's why it has to be you. Not because you're honest — honesty is cheap, any fool is honest until the price is right. Because you're incurable. You'll want to know what's at the end of a line even if knowing kills you. I need a man whose sickness is stronger than his sense."

Orsen said nothing. Outside, the wind leaned on the shutters again, and somewhere below a horse stamped and a harness buckle chimed against a bit.

Salas's hand came out from under the blanket — a big hand, ruined at the knuckles, nails split to the quick — and rested on the oilcloth without moving it. "Fifty-one years I've been in this country. I came at fifteen with my uncle, chasing the same rumor everyone chases, gold in the basalt, sheep money in the grass. I found neither. What I found is under that cloth, and it isn't mine, and it isn't for sale, and it's worth more than both put together. And I am going to die within the month — don't make the polite face, engineer, I've watched men die of worse than what I have and I know the arithmetic better than any doctor — and I cannot carry it into the ground ungiven, because a thing buried twice is buried forever."

"What is it?"

"A map."

"I assumed that much."

"Not a map of where something *is*." Salas lifted his hand, and now he did move the oilcloth, turning it over slowly, deliberately, the way a man turns a card he's been holding all night. "A map of where something *was made to disappear*."

The lamps hissed. Orsen found that he had stopped breathing through his nose, that the kerosene smell had become the whole world, and he made himself start again.

"There was a survey," Salas said. "1901. Before your time, before the main line, back when the company still thought the plateau might give them something. They sent a crew onto the Meseta del Viento with instruments and mules and a payroll, and they found what they found, and then they came home and wrote in their books that they'd found nothing. Forty-one men went out. The company's records will tell you — go look, I've looked — that no such crew ever existed. But I held the pay book. My father held the pay book. He was the paymaster." Something moved behind the dark eyes, some animal turning over in its den. "And I have spent fifty years buying back the rest of it, piece by piece, from men who were there and from men who burned things and missed a corner."

"Why?" Orsen heard his own voice come out flat, careful. "Why erase a survey?"

"Because of what it showed." Salas smiled, and it was the worst thing in the room, worse than the smell, worse than the ruined legs. "You're an engineer. You know the first commandment of your trade. Water is the whole game on the dry side. Find water and you can build anything; find none and every mile costs double. In 1901 the company believed there was no water on that meseta. That belief is written into their charter, their routes, their land claims, their whole argument for why the port and the coal and the rails belong where they belong. And then a crew of forty-one men stood on that plateau and proved the belief wrong." He tapped the oilcloth once, lightly, with one split nail. "They didn't celebrate. They buried it. And when burying wasn't enough —"

He stopped. The sentence hung there unfinished, and Orsen understood that the ending of it was the reason for the shutters, the two lamps, the locked-or-maybe-not-locked door, the summons to a stranger instead of a friend.

"When burying wasn't enough," Orsen said quietly, "what?"

"That's on the map too." Salas's voice had dropped, gone rough. "Everything's on the map. That's the point of it. Fifty years, engineer. Every cairn I built, every corner of paper I pried loose, every drunk old fool I bought gin for until he talked. It's all drawn there in my own hand, and the hand is failing, which is why tonight is tonight."

He pushed the oilcloth across the table. Orsen didn't take it. Not yet. Some instinct older than courtesy held his hands flat on his knees.

"What do you want from me?"

"To carry it. Not to use it — to *carry* it, out of this room, out of this town, alive, past the men who will ask you questions with smiles on their faces. And one thing more." The dark eyes fixed on him and would not let go. "I have a daughter. Ingrid. She lives in Comodoro Rivadavia. She works the telegraph key there, nights, I'm told. We have not spoken in —" the number seemed to cost him — "nine years. I have written her letters I never sent. They're in the trunk under you; feel free to notice how many."

Orsen shifted on the trunk and felt, absurdly, as though the letters had weight enough to feel through the leather.

"When I'm dead," Salas said, "you will go to her. You will tell her nothing about this. Nothing about the meseta, nothing about the map, nothing about what I've just said. You will tell her only this: that her father kept it. Those are the words. *He kept it.* She'll hate you for the mystery — she hates well enough already, she comes by it honestly — and you'll deserve the hating, and you'll take it, and you won't explain. Can you do that? Swear it plain, because I'll know a lawyer's oath from a real one."

"Why not tell her yourself? Why not send for her now, while there's time to —"

"Because shame is heavier than water, engineer, and I've carried mine fifty years and I will not make her carry the last mile of it while I watch her face change." For the first time the voice cracked clean through, and the quick dark eyes went to the shuttered window as if the answer to something were written on the other side of the boards. Then they came back, and they were hard again. "She gets the truth after I'm ash, and she gets it walking, on her own feet, out where it happened — not in a sickroom smelling of kerosene. Promise me."

Orsen looked at the oilcloth. At the man in the bed who had counted twelve words in a telegram and fifty years in a country and forty-one names in a pay book, and who was now spending his last coin on a promise from a stranger because there was no one left who wasn't either dead or estranged or bought.

"I'm not a good man to promise things," Orsen said. "I keep them. That's different, and sometimes worse."

"It's exactly what I want." 

Orsen took the oilcloth.

It was lighter than he expected, stiff with wax, tied with a length of tarred twine. He did not open it. Some part of him understood that the moment he broke the twine in this room, he stopped being a messenger and became a party, and he wanted — needed — one more hour of being only a man holding a package.

"The route," Salas said, settling back against the headboard as though a great weight had already left him, though his eyes stayed sharp. "It starts at the end of the old spur. Your spur. The one you walk at dawn counting sleepers like a rosary — yes, I know about that too, the whole town knows, they think it's grief, they say poor Nystrom counts his miles because he lost something. Let them think it. Start where the sleepers stop. After that, the map speaks for itself, and it lies a little, here and there, on purpose, in case it's taken from you. I'll tell you which marks are true and which are bait before you go. And Nystrom —" the hand rose again, trembling now, pointing — "the company has men who read hotel registers. You signed yours downstairs. So you'll leave tomorrow, early, before the desk clerk's shift changes, and you'll tell anyone who asks that you came down to inspect culverts. Culverts, engineer. Nobody follows a man to look at culverts."

"How long have they known about you?"

"Since spring, I think. A man came asking about surveys, old ones, very friendly, bought me dinner. I told him stories about gold." The smile again, brief and terrible. "Gold is the best lie in Patagonia. It's the lie everyone wants to hear, so they believe it, and while they're believing it they're not asking about water."

Orsen stood. The trunk felt lighter without him on it, or heavier; he couldn't decide. He tucked the oilcloth inside his coat, against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

"I'll find her," he said. "Ingrid. I'll give her the words."

"*He kept it.*"

"*He kept it.*"

Salas nodded, once, slow, a man closing a ledger. "Then we're done, and I'm the richest bankrupt in the province. Go on. Take the back stair. And Nystrom —" Orsen paused at the door, hand on the latch, and the dying man's voice followed him, quiet and clear as a signal flag — "when you stand on that plateau, and you see what's there, remember that I loved this country longer than the company has owned it. Whatever you decide to do with the truth — decide it for the country, not for the firm. The firm will not outlive the water. The water always wins."

The corridor smelled of dust and cold stove iron. Orsen walked down the back stair into the blue hour before evening, and the wind off the estuary hit him full in the face, and he stood a moment in the street with the oilcloth pressed against his ribs, feeling it there, patient as a seed.

Tomorrow, culverts. Tomorrow night, the night boat or the coast road toward Comodoro Rivadavia, and a telegraph office, and a woman who would slam a door in his face — he knew it already, the way he knew grades by the pull of a mule team, knew it with the certainty of a man who had just agreed to deliver the worst kind of news: news that means well.

Behind him, up in room 9, the two lamps went out one after the other, and the kerosene smell began its long fade into the ordinary air of the world.

* * *

He did not open the map that night. He meant to — lay awake in his own rented room with it on the table under his own single lamp, the tarred twine between his fingers, and did not. Instead he listened to the wind work at the eaves and thought about the strange arithmetic of promises: how a dying man had traded fifty years of silence for one kept word, and how Orsen, who had spent twelve years measuring everything he passed, had just accepted a debt that could not be measured at all.

At dawn he walked to the end of the spur, out of habit, out of something like prayer, and stood where the last sleeper lay half-buried in the scrub, and looked south across the flat toward the invisible line of the meseta.

Somewhere out there was a point marked AGUA, and forty-one graves' worth of a story, and a daughter who hated her father for reasons Orsen was now sworn never to learn from him.

He turned back toward the hotel to settle his bill and invent his culverts, and found himself, for the first time in twelve years, afraid of a journey he hadn't yet begun.
