## 4. Tomás Signs On

The apprentice arrived before the sun did, which Orsen took as a good sign. In twelve years of surveying the dry side he had learned that men who came to work after daylight were men who had slept well, and men who slept well on the eve of field work had not understood the work.

Tomás Ferreyra stood in the doorway of the survey office with his cap in his hands and frost melting off his boots, nineteen years old and built like a fence post someone had planted crooked and then decided to keep anyway. He had a notebook under one arm — an actual bound notebook, not loose papers folded into a pocket like most apprentices carried — and when he saw Orsen looking at it he held it up as though presenting credentials.

"I've been practicing reductions," he said. "Leveling reductions. Every night."

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes, señor."

"Then you can eat again. Sit down."

The boy sat on the edge of the chair the way water sits on a greased plate, and Orsen put the kerosene on to warm and studied him across the desk. He had seen Tomás before, of course — the boy had been running chains through the yards at Comodoro for eight months, and before that he'd haunted the instrument shed at Sarmiento until the old transit man there complained about it in writing. There was always one like him in every crew: the one who showed up early, asked the question nobody wanted answered, and wrote everything down twice.

"Where is your family?" Orsen asked.

"Sarmiento. My mother keeps a boarding house near the plaza." A pause, carefully measured. "My father is dead."

Orsen nodded and said nothing about it, because the boy's face had closed like a ledger, and some accounts you do not open on the first morning.

"I need a second chainman," Orsen said. "Maybe three weeks, maybe five. Rough country. Meseta del Viento and south of it. You'll carry half your weight in instruments and the other half in flour. The pay is field rate plus ten percent because I'm asking you to lie to people about where we're going."

Tomás blinked. "Lie to whom?"

"The stationmaster, probably. Anyone who asks." Orsen leaned back. "I'll be straight with you, Ferreyra, because I'd rather lose you now than at the railhead. This is not routine work. I don't fully know what it is yet myself. If you want a clean season of leveling ditches, say so, and I'll write you a recommendation that will get you onto any crew in the territory."

The boy looked down at his notebook. When he looked up, something in his face had gone very still and very bright at once, the way ice looks over deep water.

"My mother says I ask too many questions for this country," he said. "She says the country answers them eventually and you don't always want it to." He turned the notebook around and pushed it across the desk. "Page forty-one."

Orsen turned to page forty-one. It was a hand-drawn map of the yard at Comodoro, every switch, every water column, every drainage culvert, drawn in pencil so fine it might have been engraved, with elevations noted to the tenth of a foot in margins so small they made Orsen's eyes ache.

"You surveyed the yard yourself? On your own time?"

"On Sundays. Nobody stopped me."

* * *

The stationmaster stopped him, as it happened, within the hour.

Don Anselmo Bravo had run the freight office and the personnel rolls at Comodoro Rivadavia for longer than the wind had run the coast, or so he claimed, and he received Orsen in his office among the smell of ink pads and coal dust with the expression of a man who already knows the request will cost him.

"Meseta del Viento," he repeated. "You want to take my best apprentice into the Meseta del Viento in the shoulder season, with no filed route, no requisition number, and a requisition for water barrels that you've written out to look like track ballast orders." He held up Orsen's paperwork between two fingers as though it smelled. "Nystrom. I have known you twelve years. You are the most honest dishonest man on this line. You file everything. You double-check other men's filings. And now you hand me paper that says four hundred kilos of ballast consigned to nowhere."

"It's a private commission," Orsen said. "Mineral prospecting. The client wants discretion until his claims are staked."

"Client's name?"

"That's the discretion part."

Don Anselmo set the papers down and laced his fingers over his belly, and for a while the only sound was the telegraph next door ticking through somebody else's bad news. Orsen waited. Waiting was most of what he knew how to do; he had made a profession of standing still while the country told him things.

"There were two men here Tuesday," Don Anselmo said at last. "From the board. Buenos Aires suits, riding boots too clean for the dust. Asking after you. Where you'd gone, who you'd hired, whether you'd bought mules." He raised his eyebrows. "I told them I don't keep track of engineers' mules. But Nystrom — when the board asks after a man's mules, the man should ask himself why."

"They offered me a promotion."

"And you refused it."

"I declined it politely."

"Which from you is the same thing." Don Anselmo sighed, and it was the sigh of a man surrendering to something he'd seen coming for years. "Take the boy. Take him and bring him back with all his fingers and both eyes, because his mother feeds me free empanadas every time I come through Sarmiento and if anything happens to him she will know whose signature sent him." He pulled a form toward himself and began to write. "Field rate plus ten percent. And Nystrom—" he didn't look up— "whatever's out there, don't let it eat him whole. Boys that age think they're iron. They're not iron. They're just new."

* * *

They provisioned over four days, and Orsen discovered that assembling a small expedition was a pleasure he had forgotten he knew, the way a man forgets the pleasure of his own hands until he picks up a tool again.

There is a particular arithmetic to outfitting a party for unknown country, and it is arithmetic that forgives nothing. Flour by the hundredweight, salt pork in tins, yerba mate in quantity because the plateau would strip them of comfort faster than food and mate was the last comfort to go. Sugar. Dried peaches, because Ingrid Salas — who had appeared at the provisioning house on the third morning with her father's oilcloth roll under her arm and an expression suggesting she had come to supervise rather than assist — remarked once, only once, that her father had spoken of dried peaches the way other men spoke of saints. Orsen bought eleven pounds without comment and pretended not to see her watching him do it.

The mules he chose himself, walking the corrals at dawn on the fourth day while the animals steamed in the cold. He wanted short-coupled beasts with deep girths and no white socks, because white socks meant pink skin and pink skin meant cracked heels on caliche. He picked six and bargained for them with a dealer named Quintanilla who lied about ages the way other men said good morning, and in the end they settled at prices both men could pretend to regret.

It was the barrels that required care. Water on the dry side was not bought; it was planned, hoarded, defended. Orsen went to the cooperage on the harbor road and ordered eight oak barrels, riveted hoops, paraffin-lined, and paid extra to have them filled, sealed, and delivered to the freight yard after dark. Eighty gallons, which sounded like a river and was barely a week for five people and fourteen animals if the weather turned mean. He told the cooper they were for a track gang laying ballast westward, and the cooper, who had supplied every lie on the coast for thirty years, nodded and wrote *ballast* on his own books without a flicker.

"You're buying a lot of water for ballast," the cooper's son said, leaning in the doorway.

"Ballast gets thirsty," Orsen said, and the old man laughed from inside the shop, and that was the end of it.

Ingrid watched all of this with a scrutiny she made no effort to disguise. She counted barrels. She read the manifests upside down while Orsen filled them out. On the evening of the fourth day, sitting on a coil of rope in the freight yard with the barrels ranked behind her like fat brown soldiers, she said:

"You're provisioning for more than prospecting."

"Prospecting is thirsty work."

"My father prospected for fifty years. He never once bought sealed barrels. He bought a canvas tank and a prayer." She tilted her head at him, and in the lamplight from the yard office her face was all angles and patience. "What did he find out there, Señor Nystrom?"

"He found ground worth looking at," Orsen said. It was true. Every word he had prepared was true, which was the only way to survive a promise — build the wall out of true stones so no one thinks to push on it. "He wanted it walked by someone with instruments. That's all I can tell you until we're past the last sleeper. Your father was specific about that."

Ingrid was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed — a short, unamused sound with grief buried somewhere underneath it like ore under overburden.

"Specific," she said. "Yes. He was that. Fifty years of being specific about everything except the one thing that mattered." She stood and brushed off her skirt. "Fine. Keep your secret, engineer. I've agreed to come and I'll come. But I count everything you load, and I'll count everything you say, and one day the numbers won't match. Men always leave a remainder."

* * *

Tomás spent those four days in a state Orsen recognized from his own youth and had believed long since burned out of him: the state of a young man to whom the world is still mostly doorways.

The boy learned the loads by heart overnight. He re-packed the instrument case twice, then a third time with felt strips cut from an old coat so that the theodolite would ride without chatter. He asked questions in batches, rapid and precise — What datum would they use? How would they handle refraction off hot ground? Did the boss prefer chaining uphill or downhill? — and Orsen answered them all seriously, because he remembered being twenty-two and having a question dismissed by a chief of party, and he remembered exactly which chief, and exactly which year, and the taste of the dust he'd swallowed walking away.

On the third night Orsen found him in the yard after dark, sitting on a barrel with his notebook open, working by the light of the yard lamp.

"You should sleep," Orsen said. "We leave tomorrow."

"One more page." Tomás didn't look up. His pencil moved in strokes small enough to be sewing. "I'm making a master inventory. Everything we're carrying, weights, positions in the packs. So if we have to lighten up fast, we know what we're dropping before we drop it."

Orsen stood in the cold and looked at the boy for a long moment. Fourteen years ago — no, twelve; he kept the count the way other men kept rosaries — he had been handed his first crew and told the same thing everyone is told: *the company provides*. And the company had provided, and had taken away, and he had answered with twelve years of miles, measuring sleepers at dawn because a measured world was a world that couldn't surprise him. Here was a boy inventing redundancy out of pure love of the work, with no disgrace driving him anywhere, just appetite.

"Ferreyra."

"Señor?"

"Why does a nineteen-year-old want to walk into the worst country in the territory?"

Tomás finally looked up. The yard lamp caught the pencil graphite on his fingers and the earnest, undefended face above them.

"Because it's real," he said, as if this were obvious and slightly disappointing to have to say aloud. "The yard here — I know every sleeper in it, I drew them all. There's nothing left to find out. Out there—" he gestured with the pencil at the dark horizon beyond the fences, where the scrubland ran black to the edge of the lamplight and then kept going— "out there the land hasn't decided what it is yet. Somebody has to go decide it. With numbers." He ducked his head, embarrassed. "That sounds stupid."

"No," Orsen said. "That sounds like the reason I started." And he heard, saying it, how long it had been since the reason and the practice had anything to do with each other, and did not say that part aloud.

* * *

They left on a morning of high hard light, the kind of morning that makes even Comodoro look briefly honest, the harbor glittering below the town and the wind coming off the sea with a clean edge on it. Five of them: Orsen, Ingrid riding astride in a man's coat she had clearly won some argument to wear, Tomás perched on the lead mule with the theodolite case strapped across his back like a shell, and the two muleteers — the Vidal brothers, Facundo and Eusebio, taciturn men from the lake country who had signed on for wages Orsen had inflated precisely because he needed their silence along with their hands. Fourteen animals, eighty gallons of water dressed up as ballast, eleven pounds of dried peaches, and one oilcloth map rolled in a lead tube that rode in Orsen's saddlebag against his hip, where he could feel it with every stride like a second heartbeat.

At the edge of town Ingrid reined in once and looked back at the telegraph office, its pole rising thin against the sky. She looked at it the way a person looks at a house where they grew up — cataloguing, refusing to wave. Then she turned forward and did not look back again.

The railhead lay ninety miles southwest, the end of steel where the company's ambition had run out of money and called itself complete. Beyond the last sleeper the trail was only wheel ruts and guanaco paths and the memory of wagons, and the land began doing what it does on the dry side: flattening, widening, pulling the sky down close, until a man on a mule felt less like a traveler than like a punctuation mark in a sentence too long to finish.

They made camp the first night in the lee of a stone corral some sheepman had abandoned, and while the Vidals hobble-staked the mules and Tomás boiled mate with the concentration of a chemist, Ingrid sat on her pack roll and watched Orsen check the barrels by lamplight, pressing each stave, listening to each seal.

"Eighty gallons," she said. "For five people, fourteen animals, and a destination you won't name. My father taught me desert arithmetic before he taught me prayers. That's eleven days, comfortable. Nine, if the wind works against us." She wrapped her blanket around her shoulders. "How far is it, Señor Nystrom? Truly."

Orsen coiled the lamp strap slowly. Across the fire, Tomás had stopped stirring and was watching him too, notebook forgotten in his lap, and even the Vidals had gone quiet in the way of men who pretend not to listen and miss nothing.

"Your father marked the distance in days, not leagues," Orsen said. "Seven days out. Seven back, if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Then we learn what the barrels were for."

Ingrid held his eyes across the fire for a long moment. Whatever she saw there — and she saw more than he liked, that was becoming clear — she apparently decided it was enough, or at least that it was all there was. She lay back on her bedroll and pulled the blanket to her chin.

"Seven days," she said to the stars. "Papá, what did you find?"

No one answered her, and the wind came up off the plateau and moved through the scrub with a sound like pages turning, and Orsen lay awake a long time doing the arithmetic he hadn't said aloud: seven days out, seven back, nine days of water.

And somewhere behind them, he could not stop thinking, two men in Buenos Aires suits were asking after his mules.
