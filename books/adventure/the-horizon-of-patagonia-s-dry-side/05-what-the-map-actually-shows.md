## 5. What the Map Actually Shows

The lamp in Orsen Nystrom's room at the Hotel Comercial had a wick that burned unevenly, and he had long ago stopped asking the boy downstairs to trim it. A crooked flame was a kind of honesty. It threw shadows the way the plateau threw them — nothing straight, everything leaning a little east with the wind — and after twelve years of working by such light, in station houses and section shanties where the kerosene came up from Buenos Aires already half-watered, Orsen had learned to trust what he could see badly more than what he could see well.

He pinned the oilcloth to the wall above his bed with four drawing pins borrowed from Tomás Ferreyra's kit, and for the better part of an hour he simply looked at it.

That was the discipline. Anyone could read a map; the trick was to let it read you first. Efraín Salas had drawn this thing with a steady hand and a dying man's economy — no wasted strokes, no ornament — and it deserved the courtesy of silence before interrogation. So Orsen sat on the edge of the bed with his boots off and his hands flat on his knees, the way he sat through long sermons as a boy in Puerto Madryn, letting the map become furniture, then landscape, then fact.

It was drawn at roughly one inch to four miles, which told him Efraín had worked from something larger and reduced it, or had carried the proportions in his head for so long they'd worn smooth as river stones. The ink was iron-gall, browned at the edges. The route began at the railhead beyond Las Heras — Orsen knew that country the way a man knows the back of his own hand, every arroyo and malacara rise between there and the Colhué Huapi basin — and ran south-southwest across the Meseta del Viento in a series of long, confident legs.

Confident. That was the word that kept snagging him.

A prospector's route wandered. Prospectors followed rumor and mirage, doubled back on their own hopes, sketched the country as a drunk sketches a face. This line did not wander. Every leg ran true to within a degree or two of a bearing, and the turns came at intervals too regular for chance — twelve miles, fifteen, nine, fourteen — like stations on a line that existed only in someone's intention.

Orsen got up, took his notebook from his coat, and began to plot.

He worked as he always worked, in pencil first, light enough to erase shame. He laid out the bearings from the oilcloth against his own surveys of the region — twelve years of them, boxed and indexed in a trunk under the window, the accumulated mileage of a man who had made a religion of distance. And when he transferred the third leg onto his own sheet and saw how it lay against the old contours, he stopped.

He sat back down on the bed. He got up again. He trimmed the lamp wick himself, with a pair of scissors he'd never once used for that purpose, because his hands wanted something small and obedient to do while the rest of him caught up.

The line matched.

Not approximately. Not in the way two routes across open country will inevitably resemble each other, converging on the same passes and waterholes the way all roads converge on the same fords. It matched *exactly* — leg for leg, angle for angle, the whole southern traverse running parallel to and in places directly atop a line Orsen had seen only once, years ago, in a survey folio he had been permitted to look at for exactly one afternoon and never again.

The 1901 reconnaissance.

Every engineer who served the Southern Patagonia Railway learned the company's catechism early: the main line went where it went because the country allowed no alternative. The plateau was impassable, the interior waterless, the gradients impossible — that was the litany, repeated in annual reports and shareholder prospectuses and the patient explanations given to every new man who asked why the railway bent its expensive spine around the Meseta del Viento instead of crossing it. The direct route would have saved two hundred miles. The direct route was a dream. The company had surveyed it in 1901, found it wanting, and built the long way round, and the finding was gospel.

Orsen had been twenty-six when he'd spent that afternoon with the folio. He remembered the room — the records office in Buenos Aires, dust motes hanging in the green light of the window, a clerk named Bianchi who smelled of cigarettes and official indifference. He remembered being struck, even then, by how *good* the 1901 survey was. Instrument work of the highest order. Level notes run twice. A gradient profile across sixty miles of supposed impossibility showing a maximum grade of one in eighty-three — gentle, almost tender, the kind of country a railroad engineer dreams about and rarely gets.

And he remembered the marginal note in the chief engineer's hand, a single line in pencil that the transcribers had left out of every printed summary: *Line sound. Water is the question.*

Then the folio had gone back into its drawer, and Orsen had gone back to his miles, and for eleven years he had not thought of it — or had thought of it only the way a man thinks of a door he noticed once in a house he no longer visits.

Now here was that door again, drawn in a dead prospector's iron-gall ink, forty years later, running across an oilcloth nailed to the wall of a hotel room in Comodoro Rivadavia.

Efraín Salas had not found a route. Efraín Salas had *recovered* one.

* * *

Orsen worked until the lamp burned low and the wind outside changed from west to southwest, which meant rain for the coast and nothing for the plateau, which would take even that away and keep it. He plotted every leg. He checked his own field books against the oilcloth's distances and found agreement everywhere but one place — and the disagreement was itself a message.

At the point where the 1901 line crossed the shallow depression the maps called Bajada de los Huesos — the Slope of Bones, named, Orsen suspected, for something nobody wrote down — Efraín's route broke from the old survey. The prospector's line swung wide, a careful arc of perhaps six miles, avoiding the depression entirely, and then returned to the original bearing as if nothing had happened. As if a man walking a road had stepped off it to go around a grave.

And there, in the middle of the arc, pressed hard enough into the oilcloth to emboss it, Efraín had marked a cross.

Beside the cross, in letters smaller than anything else on the map, in a hand tighter than the rest, as though written by a different man or by the same man at a different age:

**AGUA**

One word. No depth, no flow rate, no date. Just the claim itself, asserted the way a starving man asserts hunger — absolutely, and without evidence offered, because the evidence is the body.

Orsen stood before the map for a long time with his arms folded, feeling the cold come up through the floorboards, and understood that he was afraid.

Not the ordinary fear of the field — the fear of weather, of bad water, of a mule going down in broken country. He had lived inside those fears so long they were less emotions than colleagues. This was another animal. This was the fear of understanding something backward, the way you understand a landslide only after it has moved: seeing, all at once, that a thing he had walked past for twelve years had been standing behind him the whole time.

Because if the 1901 line was sound — if the grades were buildable, if the country was crossable — then the railway's reason for the long way round was a lie, and lies of that size are never free. Companies do not spend millions bending a railroad around a plateau for nothing. They bend it because bending it serves something. A port, perhaps. A coal concession. A monopoly on freight rates that a shorter line would have broken. Orsen didn't know the shape of it yet. But he knew the weight of it, the way he knew the weight of a bridge girder by the sound it made under load, and the weight said: *this lie has people paid to maintain it.*

And a man who walks onto the meseta carrying proof of that lie should expect company.

* * *

He slept badly, which was usual, and woke before dawn, which was habit, and made tea on the small spirit stove he traveled with because hotel tea tasted of the tin it came in. Then he took the key from under the false bottom of his instrument case — the bottom he'd fitted himself in Puerto Deseado, the one hiding place he trusted because he had built it out of distrust — and drew out his copy of the 1901 survey summary.

He had made it eleven years ago, in the records office, in ninety minutes of feverish tracing while Bianchi smoked by the window and pretended not to watch. Why he had copied it he could not have said at the time. Habit, maybe. The compulsion of a man who measures things to also keep them. He had traced the route, the profile, the level notes, and the penciled marginal line, and he had carried the sheets through eleven years and three postings without once looking at them, the way a man carries a stone in his pocket without weighing it.

Now he spread them beside the oilcloth and confirmed what the night had told him. Leg for leg. Angle for angle. The dead man's map and the company's abandoned dream were the same line, separated by forty years and by everything that had happened along it in between.

The marginal note stared up at him. *Line sound. Water is the question.*

Water was the answer too, then. That was the arithmetic Efraín Salas had spent fifty years doing. The company had declared the plateau waterless — had built its whole justification on that declaration — and somewhere out there, six miles off the line it refused to build, was a cross and a single word saying otherwise. AGUA. Enough water, presumably, to unmake the declaration. Enough water to make the 1901 line not merely possible but inevitable. Enough water to cost men their positions, the company its charter terms, and whoever carried the proof his comfort, possibly his life.

Orsen looked at the word a long time. Then he folded his traced copies along their old creases, took them to the spirit stove, and fed them to the flame one corner at a time.

He was not a man who destroyed records lightly. Measurement was his sacrament; paper was its scripture. But he had learned one thing in twelve years of walking other people's forgotten lines: a document is a confession waiting for a search. If the company's men came to his room — and after yesterday's questions at the provisioning house, he no longer considered that a paranoid construction but a reasonable forecast — he could survive their finding the oilcloth, because the oilcloth was a dead prospector's keepsake, a curiosity, a thing a sentimental engineer might reasonably carry. What he could not survive was their finding his own hand tracing their own suppressed survey. That was not a keepsake. That was intent, documented in his handwriting.

The paper curled and blackened. The pencil lines went first, faint gray ghosts lifting off the burning sheet like heat shimmer, and then the ink of the marginal note — *line sound, water is the question* — flared briefly green-copper before it became ash. Orsen held the last fragment with his fingertips until the fire reached them, and dropped it into the stove, and stirred the ash with a matchstick until nothing remained that a patient man could read.

Then he opened the window and let the wind take the smell of it out over the town, toward the sea, where the company's coal port stood loading ships with fuel dug from the same interior the railway claimed it could not cross.

* * *

Tomás came at eight, punctual as a timetable, with the provisioning lists and the particular brightness of a young man who has slept well on the eve of adventure. Orsen watched him spread the lists on the table and felt the strange tenderness he always felt around the boy — the tenderness of a man looking at his own younger self, before the miles had taught him caution, before whatever it was that had sent him walking decommissioned spurs at dawn had happened to him.

"Señor Nystrom." Tomás smoothed the list. "I've done the feed calculations for eighteen days out, with margin. But I keep arriving at the same problem." He looked up, and his face did the honest thing his face always did, which was to show exactly what was behind it. "Eighteen days assumes we know the route. Do we? Truly? Because the men at the corral were saying the meseta has no—"

"I know the route," Orsen said.

It came out flatter than he intended. Tomás heard the flatness — the boy missed nothing, that was why Orsen had taken him on — and paused with his pencil in the air.

"You've been over it?"

"No."

"Then—"

"The route exists," Orsen said. "It was surveyed. It's sound. That's all you need to hold in your hands for now."

Tomás looked at him for a moment longer, and Orsen watched the boy decide — visibly, the way a switch decides a train — to trust the older man rather than press him. It was a good decision and a dangerous one, and Orsen both admired it and grieved it, because he knew what it cost a mind like Tomás's to set down a question unanswered, and he knew what answering it honestly might someday cost them all.

"There's one more thing," Orsen said. "Buy the water barrels today. Not tomorrow. Six, the iron-hooped ones, and fill them at the municipal standpipe, not the well behind the provisioning house — the well water here tastes of coal, and coal water tells the body lies." He counted money from his roll. "And Tomás — buy them separately. One barrel here, one at the ferretería, one at the chandlery by the pier. Spread it out. Don't let any single seller see the whole picture."

"The whole picture being...?"

"Six barrels," Orsen said, "is a lot of water for three men and fourteen animals on a route that officially doesn't exist."

He said it mildly, almost as a joke, and Tomás laughed the way the young laugh at their elders' little paranoia. But Orsen saw him glance at the oilcloth, still pinned to the wall above the bed, and saw the laughter pause somewhere behind the boy's eyes — the first small splinter of the larger understanding that would come to him later, out on the plateau, in country where understanding arrives too late to be comfortable.

After Tomás left, Orsen took down the oilcloth, folded it along Efraín's creases, and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where a man carries the things he has decided to be responsible for.

Through the window he could see the street below, waking in its ordinary way: carts, gulls, a boy sweeping the hotel steps. At the corner opposite, leaning against the telegraph office wall with the unhurried posture of men paid to wait, stood two figures in good coats that did not quite fit the climate.

They were not looking at the hotel.

That was what made Orsen's hands go cold as he buttoned his coat over the map. Amateurs watched the door. Professionals watched the street — the approaches, the sightlines, the whole web of ways in and out — and these two were reading the morning the way he himself read a contour sheet: patiently, completely, missing nothing.

One of them shifted his weight, and the light caught his face, and Orsen recognized the jaw and the neat gray mustache of Señor Ballesteros, who bought harness cord at the provisioning house and asked friendly questions about routes.

Ballesteros did not look up. He lit a cigarette, and the smoke rose straight in the still air, a thin vertical line drawn against the horizon — the only plumb line in the whole visible world — and Orsen understood that the question of whether he was being watched had been answered some days ago, and that the only remaining question was what the watchers intended to do when he rode out past the last sleeper.

He checked the loads on his revolver, an act so habitual it required no thought, and went down to breakfast.
