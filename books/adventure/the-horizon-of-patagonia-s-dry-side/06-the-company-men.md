## 6. The Company Men

The provisioning house of Puerto Deseado smelled of burlap, tallow, and the particular sweetness of dried apples gone slightly past their prime. Orsen Nystrom had been in a hundred such buildings across twelve years — the supply rooms and trade posts that clung to the rail lines like barnacles to a hull — and he could read them the way he read track beds: by their wear patterns, by what was stacked where, by which clerk watched the door and which watched the ledger. This one was run by a Scot named MacAllister who had come out for the sheep boom and stayed for reasons he'd never explained, and it was MacAllister's boy who came running down the street at midmorning to find Orsen measuring the spacing of the hotel's veranda posts, an old habit, his hands needing something to count even when his mind was elsewhere.

"Señor Nystrom. There are gentlemen asking after you."

Orsen straightened. "Trade men?"

"No, señor." The boy hesitated, and in the hesitation Orsen felt the first cold thread of it, the thing he would carry with him from this day forward like a splinter worked under the skin. "They're from the railway. They have a motorcar."

A motorcar. In Puerto Deseado. The town had perhaps four hundred souls, two hotels, a church, and a pier that groaned like an old man in any swell above half a meter. A motorcar here was not transportation; it was a statement. It said: we came deliberately, and we can leave whenever we choose, and you cannot.

He followed the boy back along the street, past the chandlery and the shuttered offices of a wool broker, and saw the car before he saw the men — a long black touring machine with brass fittings gone dull in the salt air, parked with the precision of a man parking who has never once been told to move along. It sat outside the provisioning house with its engine ticking as it cooled, and standing beside it were two figures who might have been cut from the same sheet of paper.

The taller one wore a suit that had been made in Buenos Aires and had traveled badly, or rather had traveled exactly as well as its owner intended, because there was a kind of creasing in good wool that only comes from genuine use on genuine roads, and this man had it. He stood with his weight settled, hands clasped behind him, surveying the street the way Orsen surveyed a right-of-way: not looking at anything in particular, taking in everything. The shorter one was rounder, softer, with a face built for apology, and he was the one who turned first when Orsen's boots sounded on the packed earth.

"Señor Nystrom." The round one extended a hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake conducted by a man who had shaken ten thousand hands and remembered none of them. "Ballesteros. Southern Patagonia Railway, engineering division. And this is Señor Ferrer, of our board."

Ferrer inclined his head without offering his hand. His eyes went over Orsen once, unhurried — boots, coat, the leather case slung at his shoulder, the dust line on his hat brim — and Orsen understood that he had just been measured, and that the measurement had been recorded somewhere behind those flat gray eyes.

"Nystrom," Ferrer said. Not a question. Confirming a fact against a list.

"You know my name."

"We know all our engineers' names," Ballesteros said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of a railway, isn't it? Knowing your people." He gestured toward the provisioning house with an open palm, a man inviting another man into a conversation that would be comfortable for both of them. "We've been sent down to look at some of the older spur surveys. Decommissioned work. You'd know more about that stretch of country than anyone living, I'm told. MacAllister says you've been walking the closed lines for years."

"I walk them," Orsen said. "It keeps me honest about distances."

"And do you find much?" Ferrer asked. His voice was quiet, almost idle, and that was how Orsen knew it was not idle at all. "Out there. On the dead spurs. Anything worth finding?"

* * *

They took a table at the back of the provisioning house, among the barrels and the hanging coils of rope, because Ballesteros insisted on coffee and MacAllister's back room had a stove and a pot. The round man did the talking; Ferrer sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, and let his silence do its own kind of work. Orsen had seen that arrangement before, in negotiation rooms in Buenos Aires years ago, when he'd been young enough to sit on the other side of tables like this one. One man talks. One man watches. The talker makes you feel flattered; the watcher makes you feel weighed.

"So," Ballesteros said, wrapping both hands around his tin cup as though the coffee were precious, though Orsen doubted either of these men had been truly uncomfortable in a decade. "Tell me about yourself. Twelve years on the dry side — that's devotion. Most men transfer out after three. The plateau breaks them. The wind, the nothing, the quiet." He smiled. "What keeps you out there?"

"The work," Orsen said.

"There is no work. That's the joke of it, isn't it? The lines are closed. The company wrote them off. There's no salary attached to walking a dead spur." Ballesteros leaned forward, confiding now, a man sharing a small conspiracy. "Between us — and I mean no disrespect — the board considers your position something of an anomaly. An engineer retained to survey routes nobody intends to build. There are those upstairs who call it waste. I call it foresight. Because someday the south will matter again. Coal, wool, water if we ever find it. And when that day comes, the man who knows the ground will be worth more than the whole board combined."

Water. The word landed in Orsen's chest like a dropped tool. He kept his face still and drank his coffee, and was grateful for the cup, for something to do with his hands.

"You've surveyed for water before," Ballesteros went on, watching him now with a pleasant, patient attention. "1908, wasn't it? The wells along the Deseado line. Your reports were thorough. Very thorough." He paused. "Almost too thorough, some said. Finding water where the geological section said there couldn't be any. Do you remember that?"

"I remember being right," Orsen said.

"Yes." Something moved behind Ballesteros's pleasantness, quick as a fish under ice. "Yes, you were. That's precisely why I'm here."

* * *

The offer, when it finally arrived, came dressed so carefully that Orsen almost admired it. Ballesteros laid it out over a second cup of coffee, in the tone of a man doing a favor rather than conducting business, while Ferrer sat silent by the door and occasionally consulted a slim black notebook that he never fully opened, only tilted toward himself, as though checking entries already made.

"The company is reorganizing its engineering department," Ballesteros said. "Head office, Buenos Aires. They want experienced field men for the planning side — route selection, hydrology review, cost estimation. Desk work, yes, but senior work. Chief assistant to the chief engineer himself. Salary doubled. Housing provided. A pension, Nystrom. A real one, with your name on it, vesting from the date of appointment." He spread his hands. "You've given the firm twelve years of your life on the worst ground it owns. Nobody downstairs will say it, but everybody upstairs knows it: you've earned a chair by a window."

"That's generous," Orsen said.

"It's overdue." Ballesteros produced papers then, actual documents, drawn up and signed and waiting — which meant they had been drawn up before anyone in Buenos Aires could have known whether Orsen Nystrom would prove agreeable, which meant the offer was not contingent on his answer but on his absence. "We sail Thursday. There's a berth for you on the same steamer if you want it. All you'd need to do is close out your field notes, hand over your local materials to Señor Ferrer here — standard practice, housekeeping really — and come north with us."

"Hand over my materials."

"Surveys, maps, correspondence. Everything relating to the closed lines." Ballesteros's smile never wavered. "You must see how it looks from the board's perspective. A man alone on the meseta for twelve years, keeping private files on company property — well. It's not an accusation, it's administration. Clean hands, clean records, everyone protected. Including you."

There it was. Orsen set down his cup and looked across the table at the round, reasonable face, and felt the oilcloth map in his hotel room three streets away as though it were pressed against his skin, warm as a living thing. Efraín Salas was dying in the room above this very floor, or had died in the night — Orsen had left him at dawn breathing in shallow pulls, and did not know which way the balance had tipped since. And these two had sailed from Buenos Aires, or ridden up from Comodoro, carrying a promotion in a folder, to collect the papers of a man whose name they should not have known mattered.

"How long have you been looking for me?" Orsen asked.

Ballesteros blinked. "Looking? We weren't—"

"You said you were sent to examine old spur surveys. But you came with a signed appointment and a request for my files. That's not examining country, Señor Ballesteros. That's collecting a man." He kept his voice level, almost gentle. "So I'll ask again. How long?"

The silence lasted three seconds. Ferrer's pen, which had been moving softly in the notebook, stopped.

"A fortnight," Ballesteros admitted, and his pleasantness had acquired a hairline crack through which something colder showed. "Since a report reached head office regarding certain... irregularities. Old matters. Nothing to concern you."

"What sort of irregularities?"

"Prospecting activity on the Meseta del Viento," Ferrer said, speaking for the first time since the table, and his voice was exactly as idle as it had been before, which was not idle at all. "An old prospector. Salas, the family name. Do you know him?"

Orsen looked at Ferrer, and Ferrer looked back, and between them passed the whole length of the problem: that a lie here would need to survive scrutiny, and that the truth, spoken plainly, would kill a dying man's last request before the man himself was cold.

"I know the name," Orsen said. "Every name on the dry side crosses my desk eventually. Half of them are dead. The other half owe money."

"Has he approached you?" Ferrer asked. "Recently. With anything. Papers, claims, maps."

"He's a dying man in a hotel room," Orsen said. "I visited him yesterday out of common decency, because I visit every old man on my lines. He talked about weather and bad knees. If he had papers, Señor Ferrer, they're papers about weather and knees."

Something flickered in Ferrer's gray eyes — not belief, but the suspension of disbelief that power permits itself when it hasn't yet decided otherwise. He made a note. The pen scratched once, twice, and stopped.

"Well," Ballesteros said brightly, and the brightness was effortful now, a lamp turned up to cover a draft. "Then there's nothing to discuss, is there? The offer stands regardless. Thursday's steamer. Think of it, Nystrom — Buenos Aires by the weekend. Trees. Rain that falls downward instead of sideways. A pension." He rose, extending his hand, and Orsen took it because refusing a hand was itself a kind of confession. "You'll give us your answer by tomorrow evening. We're dining at MacAllister's table. We're easy to find."

"I don't imagine you're easy to lose," Orsen said.

Ballesteros laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and full, and Orsen thought: this man has laughed his whole life in rooms where laughing was cheaper than shooting, and he has never once needed to learn the difference.

* * *

They walked him out. That was the detail that stayed with him afterward, the one he turned over that night like a stone with something underneath it: they walked him to the door, and Ballesteros clapped him on the shoulder with the familiarity of an old colleague, and Ferrer paused on the threshold and looked down the street toward the hotel — toward, Orsen noted, the particular window of the particular room where a dying man lay with an oilcloth map folded beneath his mattress — and then Ferrer looked back at Orsen, and smiled a small smile that contained no warmth whatsoever.

"Twelve years is a long time to be alone," Ferrer said. "Men alone get ideas. It would be a shame if ideas got you killed out there. The plateau is unforgiving. Accidents happen. Even to careful men." He settled his hat. "Especially to careful men."

Then they were gone, into the motorcar, which started on the second crank and pulled away northward with the insolent ease of machinery, leaving a rope of dust hanging in the windless street.

Orsen stood on the provisioning house step for a long time. MacAllister came and stood beside him, arms crossed, saying nothing, because Scotsmen of that coast had learned that silence was often the safest currency.

"They'll watch the hotel," MacAllister said at last.

"I know."

"They'll watch the pier, and the road out, and they'll pay the boy at the stable to tell them who buys feed."

"I know that too."

MacAllister spat thoughtfully into the dust. "And yet here you stand, not packing."

Orsen watched the dust settle back onto the street, grain by grain, the wind erasing the motorcar's passage the way the company erased towns, the way it erased wells, the way it erased forty-one names from a payroll and called it administration. He thought of Efraín's cracked voice: *tell her nothing except that I kept it.* He thought of the word AGUA inked in a dead man's hand on oilcloth. He thought of a chair by a window in Buenos Aires, and a pension with his name on it, and twelve years of miles stretching behind him like a track to nowhere.

"I'm not packing," Orsen said, "because I'm leaving tonight."

MacAllister looked at him sharply. "Tonight? The tide—"

"Doesn't matter. We go inland, not by sea. Comodoro first, then the plateau." He was already calculating it, the engineer in him laying out the route the way he'd once laid rail: provisions bought quietly, in small lots, from three different houses; mules shifted to the corral behind the chandlery after dark; departure before the moon rose. "MacAllister. Did those men ask you what I'd purchased?"

"Barrels," MacAllister said. "They asked about barrels. Water barrels. I told them you were shipping salt fish south." He shrugged. "I lied. It's a habit of mine with men who arrive in motorcars."

Orsen put out his hand, and the Scot took it, and held it a moment longer than commerce required.

"One more thing," MacAllister said as Orsen turned away. "The tall one — Ferrer. When you were talking, he wrote in that book of his. Three times. I counted. Once when you gave your name, once when the girl's father came up, and once—" the Scot paused, and his weathered face did something complicated, "—once when you said you'd been to visit the old man. Whatever you're carrying, Nystrom, that man has already written it down somewhere. You won't outrun the book. Only the men holding it."

Orsen walked back toward the hotel through the middle of the day, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be, counting his own footsteps without meaning to — forty-one steps to the corner, he noticed, and almost laughed, and didn't. Above the street, behind the window of a room that smelled of kerosene and dying, Efraín Salas waited with his secret, and below the street, in a black motorcar rolling north toward Comodoro, two company men carried Orsen's name in a notebook and an offer in a folder, and between those two weights — a promise and a pension — Orsen Nystrom began, for the first time in twelve years, to measure something that could not be measured in miles.

By nightfall he would be gone. By morning, they would know it. And somewhere between those two facts lay the rest of his life.
