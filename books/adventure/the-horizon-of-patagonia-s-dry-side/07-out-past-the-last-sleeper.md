## 7. Out Past the Last Sleeper

The last sleeper of the world lay at Orsen Nystrom's boots, and he was counting it.

Ingrid watched him do it — saw his lips move slightly as he stepped from tie to tie along the dead spur, the way a man might count beads on a rosary he no longer believed in but could not put down. The rail had been pulled up years ago; only the creosote-blackened sleepers remained, marching off into the scrub like the vertebrae of some enormous animal that had lain down here to die and been picked clean by wind.

*Fourteen animals,* she thought. *Five people. One man who counts.*

She had agreed to come — she was still not entirely sure why — and now, on the first morning out from the railhead, with her father's oilcloth map folded somewhere inside Orsen's saddlebag, she found herself studying these strangers the way she had once studied telegraph traffic: for pattern, for rhythm, for the lie under the message.

Tomás Ferreyra, nineteen and all elbows, was already twenty yards ahead, walking with a surveyor's chain over one shoulder and an expression of pure joy that seemed to Ingrid almost obscene this early in the morning. The two muleteers — the elder called Sarmiento, the younger one simply Cholo, though his real name, he'd told them at the provisioning house, was Anselmo — were loading the last of the water barrels with the wordless efficiency of men who had done this their whole lives and expected to die doing it.

And Orsen Nystrom finished counting his sleepers, straightened, and looked east at nothing at all.

"Ready?" he said.

"Since yesterday," said Tomás.

Ingrid said nothing. She mounted the mule Sarmiento held for her, arranged her skirts the way her mother had taught her before her mother stopped teaching anything, and waited.

Orsen swung up into the saddle last, the way a captain leaves a ship, and gave the railhead one long look behind him — not sentimental, Ingrid decided, but accounting. A man closing a ledger.

Then the land took them.

* * *

It happened faster than she expected. Within an hour the scrub thickened and then thinned, the sleepers ran out altogether, and the country opened like a hand unclenching: flat, gray-green, endless, stitched at every horizon with wind. Not gusts — wind as a condition, wind as weather's permanent opinion. It came out of the west with a sound like cloth tearing very slowly, and it moved through the coiron grass in silver waves so that the whole plain seemed to be flowing toward some sea that wasn't there.

Ingrid had grown up on stories of this country. Her father had come back from trips like this one smelling of dust and kerosene, his hands cracked, his eyes lit with something he never quite shared, and he would sit at the kitchen table in Comodoro Rivadavia drinking maté and saying things like *the plateau sings if you let it* while her mother rolled her eyes and Ingrid, eight years old, believed every word.

She had stopped believing at fifteen. She was thirty-one now, and she had come out here to find out what a man does with fifty years of believing, when he finally decides to spend it.

He spends it on strangers, apparently.

She urged her mule alongside Orsen's. He glanced at her — a quick, assessing look, the look of a man checking an instrument — and then returned his attention to the horizon.

"You counted the sleepers," she said.

"Habit."

"How many?"

"Two hundred and six." A pause. "There used to be four hundred and twelve to the mile. I know that without thinking about it. That's the trouble with twelve years on closed lines. Your head fills up with numbers nobody wants anymore."

"And what do you want?"

He didn't answer right away. The wind filled the silence for him, pressing against them both, flattening the grass, carrying the small dry smell of thorn bush and distance.

"Water," he said at last, and she couldn't tell whether he meant it plainly or otherwise, and she hated him a little for that, and admired him a little too, and understood that those two things were going to be living together inside her for the length of this journey.

"My father," she said. "What did he find out there? On the meseta."

Orsen's face did something complicated and quick, like weather changing over the sea, and then settled into careful blankness. She had seen that exact maneuver before — on the faces of telegraph clerks passing along messages they'd been ordered not to read, on the face of the company doctor who had told her, gently, that her father's lungs were finished and there was nothing more to be done. It was the face of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"He found what he was looking for," Orsen said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I can give you."

"Why? Because you promised him?"

"Because I promised him." He said it steadily, without apology, and she realized that this was the first honest thing anyone had said to her since Efraín Salas died in a hotel room smelling of kerosene, pressing an oilcloth packet into a stranger's hands.

"What exactly did you promise him?" she asked. "Word for word. I want the words."

Orsen rode in silence for perhaps half a minute. When he spoke, it was slow, deliberate, the cadence of a man reciting something he had gone over so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone.

"I promised to bring you home safe. I promised to tell you that he kept it — whatever *it* turned out to be. And I promised not to speak of what the map shows until we stand where it leads." He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were pale gray and very tired and absolutely level. "Those are the words. I'll keep them to the letter, Señorita Salas. You have my word on that. But I won't give you more than the letter, because the letter is what I own. The rest belongs to a dying man, and I don't have the right to spend it."

Ingrid looked away first. Out across the plain, a ñandú — a great flightless bird, taller than a man — broke from the cover of a thorn clump and went sprinting away in indignant strides, neck outstretched, until it became a smudge against the shimmering air.

"He kept it," she repeated. "Kept *what*?"

"A promise of his own, I think. Fifty years' worth." Orsen shifted in his saddle, wincing slightly — city bones, she thought, or old ones anyway. "You knew him better than I did. What kind of man was he, your father?"

The question caught her off guard. She had spent three days being angry at Orsen Nystrom for knowing her father's last days and refusing to share them, and it had not once occurred to her that he might be curious about the other forty-nine years.

"He was a liar," she said, and heard how young and bitter her voice sounded, and kept going anyway because bitterness was the only fuel she'd brought. "Not a cruel one. A kind one. He lied beautifully. Every time he came home he'd tell us the trip was nothing, easy work, good pay, and my mother would look at his hands and say nothing, and I'd believe him because I wanted to. He lied about how hard it was. He lied about why he kept going back. And at the end—" Her throat tightened. She made herself finish. "At the end he sent for a stranger instead of me."

"He didn't send for me," Orsen said quietly. "I was just the nearest man who walked decommissioned lines. He'd been waiting for someone to pass through for weeks, from what the hotel woman said. He asked after every traveler." A pause. "I think he'd have preferred it to be you. I think he couldn't bear it to be you."

"Why not?"

"Because of what he had to ask." Orsen's voice dropped, and even the wind seemed to lean in to hear it. "A man doesn't hand a stranger his life's work unless he's afraid of what handing it to family would cost. Shame is heavier than water, Señorita Salas. I've carried a little of it myself. Enough to recognize the weight on another man."

They rode on. The sun climbed. Somewhere behind them Tomás was asking Cholo the name of a bird and Cholo was giving him three different names for it, enjoying himself enormously, and the sound of their laughter drifted forward and away like something from another country.

*Shame is heavier than water.* Ingrid turned the phrase over. Her father had come home thinner every year, quieter every year, and in the last decade he had taken to standing at the window of an evening looking south toward the meseta with an expression she had filed, in her anger, under *guilt*. Maybe it had been shame. Maybe there was a difference, and maybe the difference was everything.

"Tell me about the disgrace," she said suddenly.

Orsen's head turned. "What disgrace?"

"The one you never mention. The stationmaster in Deseado mentioned it — 'after what happened,' he said, and then went red and changed the subject. My father must have known. Men who live out here know everything about each other." She held his gaze. "If you're asking me to trust you with my father's secret, you can at least tell me why you're trustworthy. Fair trade."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. His jaw worked once, twice, the way a man works a bit he doesn't like. Then, to her surprise, he laughed — short, dry, without much humor in it, but a laugh nonetheless.

"Fair trade," he said. "All right. Twelve years ago I signed a survey report that wasn't mine. I signed it because the man who wrote it was my friend, and he was dying, and his widow needed the completion bonus, and the line was sound — I checked it myself, every gradient, every culvert. Sound work, wrong signature. Someone found out. The company didn't fire me. They did something worse: they kept me, and they took me off anything that mattered, and for twelve years they've paid me to walk lines they've already decided to close." He shrugged, a small bitter motion. "So now you know. I'm a man the company owns a piece of. Which is either the best or the worst possible recommendation, depending on what we find out there."

"And what do we find out there?"

"You already asked me that."

"And you already gave me the letter, not the spirit." She surprised herself by smiling — a thin one, but real. "I'm learning your rules, Señor Nystrom. I'm going to hold you to every single one of them."

"I'd expect nothing less," said Orsen Nystrom, and for the first time since she'd met him, he smiled back.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a low rise, the mules hobbled in a rough crescent, the water barrels stacked where Sarmiento could watch them, because Sarmiento had announced at midday that water on the dry side was worth more than silver and he intended to guard it like a bishop guards his relics. Tomás built a fire of coiron roots and sheep dung, and the smell of it — sweetish, resinous, ancient — rose into a sky so crowded with stars that Ingrid felt briefly dizzy looking up, as if the ground had become unreliable and the stars were the only fixed points left.

They ate stewed goat and hard biscuit. Cholo produced a battered guitar from somewhere among the packs — Ingrid never learned which pack, and suspected she was not meant to — and played softly, songs about women in Valparaíso and horses that died well. Tomás sang along on the choruses, badly and happily. Sarmiento dozed sitting up, guitar or no guitar, one eye on the barrels.

Ingrid sat apart, wrapped in a blanket, watching the fire and turning her father's letter over in her mind for the hundredth time. *He kept it.* Three words. She had read the letter itself perhaps forty times since Orsen handed it to her in Comodoro Rivadavia, standing in her doorway with rain running off his hat brim, and each reading yielded the same stubborn handful of sentences:

*I did not forget you. I did not forget any of them. If a man named Nystrom brings you this, go with him, and forgive me the manner of it. Some debts are paid in person or not at all.*

*Any of them.* Any of whom?

She became aware that Orsen had settled onto a stone near her, a respectful few feet away, a tin cup of maté in his hands. He didn't speak. He just sat, offering the companionship of silence, and after a while she found she didn't mind it.

"Your apprentice," she said eventually. "He believes in the company."

"He believes in wages," Orsen said. "At nineteen that's the same thing. Give him ten years."

"And you? Do you believe in the company?"

Orsen looked into his cup for a long time. The firelight moved on his face, carving the lines deeper.

"I believe in miles," he said. "The company gave me miles. Whether that makes me loyal to it or just grateful, I honestly don't know yet." He glanced sideways at her. "Ask me again when we're home."

"If we're home."

"When," he corrected, mildly, and passed her the cup.

She drank. The maté was bitter and perfect. Around them the wind combed the dark plain, and somewhere far off a fox screamed once and fell silent, and the stars burned on in their terrible multitudes, indifferent and beautiful, and Ingrid Salas — who had slammed a door on this man twice, who had come on this journey out of spite and grief and a debt she hadn't examined — felt something loosen in her chest, very slightly, the way ice loosens in spring: not melting, not yet, but no longer sealed.

She did not trust him. But she was beginning to believe he could be trusted, and she understood that these were different things, and that the second one might be enough.

* * *

On the third day the land changed its mind about hills.

They had been climbing so gradually that none of them noticed it — a rise of inches to the mile, imperceptible except to Tomás, who announced it at noon with the delight of a boy reporting snow: "We've gained two hundred meters since the railhead! Two hundred!" — and then, all at once, the scrub gave out and the horizon flattened into the true plateau, the meseta proper: a vast table of gray stone and gravel and wind-scoured nothing, running to every edge of the world.

The wind out here was different. Harder. It came off the western distances with nothing to slow it for a hundred leagues, and it smelled of nothing at all — no grass, no animal, no rain. Just distance, made breathable.

Sarmiento spat to leeward and muttered something about *el viento que borra* — the wind that erases. Cholo laughed at him, but Ingrid noticed that neither muleteer strayed far from the others after that, and that the mules themselves pressed together into a tight, nervous herd, ears swiveling.

It was late afternoon when Ingrid saw it.

She had fallen a little behind — her mule had developed opinions about a loose girth strap — and so she was alone when she crested a shallow swell of gravel and looked down into a broad, shallow depression in the plateau floor, perhaps half a mile across, ringed with stones.

Stones placed by hands.

They were low, half-buried, wind-worn, most of them — but they were in a pattern. A circle. And at the circle's center, barely visible, a darker patch of earth where nothing grew, in a country where everything else grew stubbornly low and gray.

She reined in and stared. The wind poured over her, cold despite the sun.

"Señor Nystrom," she called, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

He was beside her within a minute, and Tomás after him, and the muleteers after that, and for a long moment all five of them sat their animals in a row and looked down at the ring of stones and said nothing at all.

"Old camp," Sarmiento said at last. "Fogón ring. Fire circle." He crossed himself, quickly, the way men do who aren't sure they believe but aren't sure enough to skip it. "Somebody camped here a long time. A lot of somebodies."

"Forty-one somebodies," Ingrid said.

Every head turned to her.

She hadn't known she was going to say it. The number had simply risen in her, unbidden — out of her father's letters, out of the ledger pages Orsen had shown her by lamplight in Deseado before they left, out of the payroll record for a well-digging crew that official history insisted had never existed. Forty-one names. She had copied them out herself, one night, unable to sleep, writing them in her neat telegraphist's hand as though transcribing a message that had been waiting fifty years for someone to take it down.

"How do you know that?" Tomás asked.

Ingrid looked at Orsen. He looked back at her, and she saw the struggle in him — the promise pulling one way, the plain evidence of the stones pulling the other — and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that whatever her father had found, it began here, in places like this, with rings of stones and numbers that had been erased.

"He kept it," she said quietly, to Orsen, to the wind, to the ring below. "Whatever it was — he kept it for fifty years. And I think I'm starting to understand why he couldn't give it to me."

Orsen said nothing. But he took off his hat — a small gesture, absurd and enormous, there on the empty plateau — and held it against his chest for the space of three breaths, facing the ring of stones, before putting it back on and giving the order to move on.

They rode down past the circle without stopping. Ingrid was the last through, and she made herself look at it properly as she passed: the fire ring, the dark center, the way the wind had scoured everything else away but had somehow, in fifty years, failed to erase this.

That night, making her bedroll under a sky like spilled salt, she caught Orsen standing apart from the fire, looking back the way they had come, toward the depression, invisible now in the dark.

"Señor Nystrom," she said.

He turned.

"When we get where we're going," she said, "you'll tell me everything. All of it. Not the letter. The spirit."

He held her gaze across the firelight for a long moment. And then he said, in a voice so quiet the wind nearly took it:

"Yes. I think I will."

Far off to the west, beyond the edge of the firelight, something glinted once in the last of the sun — metal, perhaps, or glass, or nothing at all — and was gone.
