## 8. Wind Without Water

Tomás Ferreyra had learned surveying in a classroom in Comodoro Rivadavia, where the instruments behaved themselves and the horizon stayed politely where you put it. He had assumed the plateau would be more of the same — a bigger classroom, a duller one. Five days out from the railhead, he understood that he had been taught geography by men who had never been lied to by the land.

The Meseta del Viento did not rise. That was the first deception. It simply began, somewhere behind them, and unrolled itself northward like a bolt of grey-brown cloth that some careless god had left stretched between the mountains and the sea. There were no landmarks on it worth the name. A basalt ridge here, a dry arroyo there, a lone ñire tree bent into a permanent apology toward the east. The wind came off the Andes all day long with nothing to slow it, and by the second day Tomás had stopped trying to talk except by shouting, and by the third he had stopped wanting to.

He walked at Orsen Nystrom's shoulder because that was where an apprentice walked, and because watching the engineer read the ground was like watching a man read a language Tomás only half knew. Orsen counted things. Sleepers, paces, mule-steps, hours. His lips moved sometimes when he thought no one could see. Twelve years of measuring had worn grooves in him the way water wears limestone, and Tomás, who was nineteen and still mostly ungrooved, found it both admirable and faintly frightening — the idea that a man could become his own instrument.

* * *

It began on the fourth morning, at a place where the land dipped without seeming to decide to.

They had made camp in the lee of a low scarp, and before dawn Tomás took his bearings as he did every morning, kneeling with the prismatic compass open on its flat board. The needle swung. He tapped the glass, the way he'd been taught, assuming dampness or a bubble or his own cold fingers. The needle settled — then drifted, lazily, a degree east, back west, like something drowsing.

"Señor Nystrom." His voice came out rougher than he intended; they had all been breathing dust for days. "My compass is drunk."

Orsen came over, crouched, held out his hand for the instrument without a word. He turned it slowly through the full circle of the horizon. Tomás watched the older man's face rather than the needle, and what he saw there was not annoyance. It was attention — sharp, total, the look of a man who has heard his name called from a great distance.

"Again," Orsen said.

Tomás set the compass down. The needle wandered again, agreeably, aimlessly, pointing at nothing in particular with great confidence.

"And your barometer?"

Tomás fetched it. The aneroid's brass face showed a pressure half a line higher than it should have been for their elevation — he had shot the depression's height twice yesterday and knew the numbers — while his spare barometer, a cheaper thing he carried as backup, insisted on a reading lower still. Two instruments, made by two different houses in two different cities, disagreeing about the same air.

"They can't both be right," Tomás said.

"No," Orsen agreed. He was looking not at the instruments but at the ground, at the shallow dish of the depression, perhaps three kilometers across, rimmed with pale soil that glittered faintly when the sun caught it. "But they're both telling the truth about different things."

"I don't understand."

"Neither did I, the first time." Orsen stood, knees cracking, and shaded his eyes against the rising light. "Iron," he said, as if that explained everything, and then, seeing Tomás's face, he explained anyway, and Tomás would remember that explanation for the rest of his life, because it was the first time anyone had treated him as a colleague instead of a boy with a chain and stadia rod.

"The water under this plateau isn't sitting still," Orsen said. "It moves — slow, slower than anything you've ever seen, but it moves, and where it moves through old basalt it picks up iron. Dissolved iron, magnetite fines. Enough of it, pooled under a basin like this one, and your compass doesn't know which master to serve. Needle wants north; the ground wants the iron. As for your barometers—" he shrugged, "—air over standing groundwater sits differently than air over dry rock. Subtle. Your good instrument feels it and lies about the elevation. Your cheap one is too stupid to lie consistently."

"So the map—" Tomás stopped himself. They had all learned to be careful about the map. It lived rolled in oilcloth inside Orsen's saddlebag, and none of them spoke of it in the open, though Tomás had caught Ingrid looking at the saddlebag more than once with an expression he couldn't file anywhere.

"The map knew," Orsen said quietly. "Salas knew. He didn't have a compass worth a damn, I'd wager, and didn't need one. He navigated by this." He nudged the glittering pale soil with his boot. "Caliche with iron bloom. You can see it from a ridge if the light's right. First sign I've seen since the railhead that we're not walking on faith alone."

* * *

Wonder, Tomás discovered, was a thirsty business.

All that fourth day he kept stopping to take readings, and Orsen let him, even slowed the column for him, and each time the needle swung and sulked and drifted, Tomás felt something lift in his chest that had nothing to do with science. The depression deepened by degrees until they were walking on its floor, a vast shallow saucer of hardpan cracked into polygonal plates like the skin of something enormous and sleeping. The mules' shoes rang on it. Their own voices sounded wrong — flattened, close, as if the air itself were thinner here.

Ingrid rode with her father's letter folded inside her jacket and her eyes on the glittering ground. She had said little in five days beyond what courtesy required, but that evening, when they halted, she dismounted beside Tomás while he packed the instruments and asked, without preamble: "You believe him? About the iron?"

"It's real. I can feel it — the instruments don't lie about *that*, only about everything else."

"Then we're near whatever he found." She said it flatly, but her hands were not steady as she tightened her girth strap. "Near, or far. On this ground you can be either and it looks identical."

That was the trouble with the plateau, Tomás was beginning to understand. It gave you wonders and withheld the ordinary. It would show you a compass gone mad with buried iron — proof of hidden water, proof of Efraín Salas's truth — and the same hour it would show you the water barrel hoops creaking as the staves dried and shrank, and the mules nosing at empty ground where grass should be, and the wind coming through camp at night with a sound like a freight train that never arrived.

* * *

On the fifth day the thirst got personal.

Orsen rationed from the first morning — two liters per person per day, drawn from the barrels with a tin measure and poured out in front of everyone so no one could doubt the arithmetic — but arithmetic and thirst are different languages. By noon Tomás's mouth tasted of copper and caliche dust. By mid-afternoon he had begun doing something shameful: calculating. How many liters remained. How many days to the marked point, if the map's distances were honest. How many days back, if they weren't. He caught himself licking the dew off the mule lines at dawn and made himself stop, and then, an hour later, caught himself doing it again.

The others were managing better, or hiding it better. The muleteers, Domínguez and the silent one called Chico, chewed leaves and said nothing. Orsen drank last and least and pretended not to notice anyone noticing. Ingrid had gone quiet in a way that Tomás was learning to distinguish from her usual quiet — this was the quiet of a woman conserving something, and he couldn't tell if it was spit or anger.

It was late afternoon when Orsen reined up on a low swell of ground and sat looking ahead for a long time without speaking. Tomás rode up beside him and followed his gaze.

Ahead, the depression floor rose gently into another swell, and beyond it the land lay flat and featureless to the horizon — the same grey, the same scrub, the same merciless sky. But along the base of the swell, maybe a kilometer distant, there was a line of darker earth. Vegetation, sparse but unmistakable: coirón grass in tufts, a scatter of quilembay bushes, green-tinged where nothing else on the plateau was green.

"Water," Tomás breathed. "There's water there—"

"There's water *under* there," Orsen corrected. "Close enough to the surface to feed those roots. Which means—" and now his voice changed, took on the careful weight of a man handling something fragile, "—we are close enough that Salas's mark should lie within a day's walk. Maybe less."

He turned in the saddle and looked back the way they had come, and Tomás, following his glance, saw nothing at all — just the empty saucer of the depression, the shimmering air, the trackless miles. But Orsen looked at it the way other men looked at a room they suspected of holding listeners.

"We camp short tonight," he said. "Dry camp, no fire. And Tomás—" the engineer's eyes came around, tired and level, "—keep your instruments packed after dark. If your compass starts wandering worse than usual, I want to know what direction the needle favors. Not north. The *other* direction. Understand?"

Tomás nodded, though he didn't entirely, and it was only much later — lying awake under a sky crowded with stars, listening to the wind comb the coirón grass a kilometer away and the mules stamping restlessly in the dark, his throat lined with sand, his head full of wandering needles and buried iron — that he understood what Orsen had been looking for on the trail behind them.

Not water.

Riders.

And far off, at the very edge of hearing, beneath the wind, Tomás fancied — only fancied, he told himself, only fancied — he heard the thin ring of shod hooves on stone, keeping pace with theirs.
