## 9. The First Marker

Ingrid had learned to read men the way she had learned to read telegraph tape: by the small irregularities, the hesitations between the letters. Her father had been all hesitation in his last years — long silences on the rare occasions he wrote, sentences that stopped halfway and never resumed. Orsen Nystrom was different. His silences were deliberate, load-bearing, like the beams of a bridge. He said little because he trusted what he said to hold weight.

Tomás Ferreyra said too much, but she found she didn't mind it. At nineteen, the boy carried enthusiasm the way other men carried tools — visibly, noisily, always within reach. He talked about gradients and mule temperaments and the proper way to pack a water barrel so the bung wouldn't work loose, and beneath all of it ran a single bright thread: *I have never been this far from anything, and I am not afraid.*

The two muleteers, Arriaga and Sosa, said almost nothing at all, which Ingrid understood as its own kind of language. Men who took coin for uncertain work learned early that questions were a currency they couldn't afford.

Five days out from the railhead, the plateau had flattened them all into silence anyway. There was only so much one could say about scrub and wind and the particular gray-green monotony of coirón grass, and they had said most of it by the second day. Now the land spoke for itself: a low endless hum of wind over stone, the creak of leather, the metronome clop of mule hooves on ground that had never known a wheel.

Ingrid rode with her father's oilcloth map folded inside her jacket, against her ribs, though Orsen kept the working copy in his saddlebag. Efraín Salas had drawn it in ink gone brown as old blood, and she had spent enough nights since Puerto Deseado studying her own tracing of it to know every line by heart. The route ran northwest across the Meseta del Viento in a series of long, patient legs, each one terminating at a small cross — eleven crosses in all, spaced like stations on a line that had never been built.

Orsen called them markers. He would not call them anything else, and when Tomás once asked whose they were, Orsen looked at him for a long moment before answering.

"A dead man's," he said. "We'll know more when we find one."

They found the first one on the sixth morning.

* * *

It was Tomás who saw it, because Tomás saw everything — that was his gift and his affliction both. He stood up in his stirrups half a mile ahead and pointed at a low rise where the wind had combed the grass into pale waves.

"There. On the crest. Stones."

At first Ingrid took it for geology — the plateau was littered with outcrops, with glacial erratics dropped by ice ten thousand years dead. But as they drew closer the shape resolved itself, and her chest went tight in a way she could not immediately name. It was a cairn. A human hand had stacked those stones: waist-high, carefully corbelled, the flat slabs fitted together with a mason's patience. Someone had wanted this pile to last. Someone had built it against the wind itself.

Orsen dismounted first. He walked around the cairn twice without touching it, the way a man circles a sleeping animal, and then he knelt and began lifting stones from the top with both hands, setting them aside in order, left to right, like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Help me," he said, not looking up. "Carefully. If there's something inside, I want it the way he left it."

Ingrid swung down and joined him. The interior stones were smaller, packed tight, and beneath them — a hand's depth down — lay a tin box the size of a biscuit tin, its lid soldered shut, wrapped in waxed canvas that had gone stiff and yellow with age.

She held it while Orsen cut the canvas away with his knife. The tin was rusted at the corners but sound. Someone had cared for it. Someone had checked on it, maybe more than once, over years — she could see the brighter metal where the solder had been reworked, two repairs, three, each one neater than the last.

"He came back," she heard herself say. "He came back to maintain it."

"Of course he did." Orsen worked the lid loose with the flat of his knife. "A promise is a thing you keep visiting."

Inside, on top, lay a scrap of oilcloth with a single word in her father's hand: *CUENTA.* Account. Beneath it, wrapped in a wool sock gone to lace, was a ledger.

It was a cheap stationer's book, the kind sold in any company store from Río Gallegos to Bahía Blanca, its marbled cover bleached nearly white. The spine had cracked and someone had sewn it back with copper wire. Ingrid opened it with hands she told herself were steady.

The first page bore a heading in a formal, schoolroom script:

**SOUTHERN PATAGONIA RAILWAY — POZO NUEVO WORKS**
**PAYROLL, WELL No. 1 — COMMENCING OCTOBER 1901**

And below it, in columns ruled by hand, forty-one names.

She counted them twice. She counted them a third time because the number would not sit still in her mind, because some part of her kept insisting she had misread, that a well crew of forty-one men was a large undertaking, that large undertakings leave records, that records are how the world remembers things.

Forty-one names. Wages entered weekly in pesos and centavos. Deductions for tobacco, for boots, for remittances home — to Concepción, to Santiago, to a village near Punta Arenas, to an aunt in Carmen de Patagones. Whole lives laid out in debit and credit. A man named Anselmo Riquelme who sent money every week to a *señora Riquelme* and was docked twice for lost tools. A boy listed as *El Chileno, menor* — a minor — earning half wages. A paymaster's signature at the bottom of each page, growing more confident as the months turned: *E. Salas.*

Her father's signature.

"Efraín Salas," Orsen said quietly beside her. He had read it over her shoulder, or perhaps he had known already. She looked up and found him watching her face rather than the book, and there was something in his expression she might have mistaken for pity if she hadn't known better. It was recognition. He knew exactly what it was to find your father written somewhere you'd never thought to look.

"This is impossible," Tomás said. He had come up behind them and was staring at the open ledger as if it might bite. "There was no works at Pozo Nuevo. There's no Pozo Nuevo. I've memorized the company surveys — the '01 expedition reported the meseta waterless. That's why the western branch was abandoned. It's in the minutes."

"Then explain the payroll," Orsen said.

"I can't—"

"Forty-one men don't dig a well in secret, boy. Somebody fed them. Somebody paid them. Somebody signed their wages every Saturday for—" Ingrid turned pages, counting weeks, "—eleven months. And then."

And then. The entries stopped in September 1902. Not gradually, not trailing off. They simply ended, mid-column, the final week's wages entered and initialed and nothing after. The last page bore a single additional line in a different hand, larger, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

**CLOSED BY ORDER. RETURN ALL BOOKS TO CENTRAL OFFICE.**

Except one book had not been returned. One book had traveled fifty years in a waxed canvas wrap, from central office to wherever her father had hidden it, and back again, and out here to a cairn under the wind.

Ingrid closed the ledger. She did not remember deciding to close it. Her hands did it on their own, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

"Ingrid?" Tomás's voice, careful.

She shook her head. She meant to say *I'm fine,* which was what she said to everyone, which was the whole architecture of her life — fine, fine, perfectly fine, the door slammed, the message received, nothing further. But the words wouldn't assemble. Something in her had gone quiet the way the telegraph line goes quiet at night: not dead, just listening, every wire strung taut and humming with traffic that hadn't arrived yet.

Because she knew these names. Not the names themselves — she had never heard of Anselmo Riquelme or the boy called El Chileno. But she knew the *shape* of them. She had grown up on the shape of them. Her father had drunk alone sometimes, in the years she'd been old enough to notice, and on those nights he would say strange things she had filed away as the eccentricities of an old prospector: that paper was heavier than gold; that the company could make a man disappear without moving him; that somewhere on the dry side there was a debt that had never been collected and never would be, and he was the only creditor left alive.

She had thought it was drink talking. She had thought a great many things about her father, most of them unkind, all of them convenient, and now here was his handwriting in a dead man's book, paying living men to dig water out of a plateau the railway swore was dry, and the world she had organized around her anger at him tilted several degrees and did not right itself.

"Ingrid." Orsen again, closer now. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." Her voice came out flat and strange. She opened the ledger again, deliberately, and made herself look at the wage columns instead of the names. Numbers were safe. Numbers were her trade, in a way — pulses and intervals, information reduced to its skeleton. "Eleven months of payroll. Forty-one men. Boots and tobacco deducted. Remittances sent. This isn't a survey crew, Orsen. This is a town being born. This is families expecting money every week. Where did they sleep? Where did they—"

She stopped, because the answer was standing right in front of her, and it was the map folded against her ribs. Eleven crosses. Eleven markers. And her father had drawn the route past all of them, which meant he had walked this ground knowing what lay at the end of the line, and he had asked her to come see it without telling her why, because telling her why would have required him to say aloud whatever it was he had carried for fifty years.

"He knew," she said. "When he gave you the map, he knew what we'd find."

"I suspected," Orsen said. "He confirmed nothing. He made me promise two things and neither of them was an explanation."

"What were they?"

"That's between me and him." He said it without apology, and she hated him a little for it, and respected him a little more, and understood that this was going to be the shape of the man for the length of the journey.

Arriaga and Sosa had hung back by the mules, the way they hung back from everything, but she caught Sosa squinting at the cairn with an expression she filed away: not curiosity exactly. Calculation. She would remember that later, though she did not yet know what for.

Tomás was still turning the impossibility over. "The minutes say the '01 survey found nothing. If there was a works — if there was a *well* — the survey wasn't wrong, it was *falsified*. Do you understand what that means? That's not error. That's—"

"It means somebody wanted the western branch dead," Orsen said. He took the ledger from Ingrid with a care that surprised her, wrapped it back in the ruined sock, and weighed the tin in his hands. "And it means somebody else wanted proof it had lived. Fifty years, this box has waited out here. We're the first people he trusted with the address."

"Why us?" The question escaped Ingrid before she could weigh it. "Why not a newspaper? Why not a magistrate? Why spend fifty years maintaining a cairn and then hand it to a stranger on his deathbed?"

Orsen looked at her for a long moment. The wind moved through the coirón grass and made a sound like the sea, though the sea was two hundred miles east and a lifetime away.

"Because a newspaper prints and forgets," he said. "And a magistrate files and forgets. He needed someone who would walk the route. All of it. To the end. You can't testify to a country you haven't crossed." He paused. "And because he wanted his daughter to see it. Whatever *it* is. That part he didn't have to say. It was the only reason he'd give the map to me instead of mailing it to you."

The words landed harder than she expected. She turned away and busied herself restacking the cairn — Orsen handed her the stones in reverse order and she fitted them back with more force than the work required, pressing each slab down as if she could press the whole morning into place. When the last stone sat level, she stood with her palm flat on top of it, feeling the cold of it, the age of it, the patience.

Forty-one men. Forty-one names in her father's handwriting. And a final entry, *closed by order*, in a hand that had never once had to explain itself to anyone.

"We camp here tonight," Orsen said behind her. "Next marker's two days on, if the distances hold. Eat, water the animals, and nobody talks about the book in front of the muleteers. Not yet."

"You think they'd sell us?" Tomás asked.

"I think hungry men sell whatever's nearest," Orsen said. "Including stories."

That night Ingrid sat apart from the fire with the ledger open on her knees, reading it by the last light the way she had once read telegrams — fast, complete, missing nothing. She copied the forty-one names into the back of her own notebook, in order, spelling them exactly, because names were the one cargo that must not be damaged in transit. Riquelme. The boy called El Chileno. A foreman named Bustos whose wages jumped in March, promotion noted in the margin. The paymaster, E. Salas, who drew the same salary throughout and never took a deduction for anything, not even tobacco.

Near midnight, when the fire had burned to orange eyes and Tomás slept with his instruments cradled like a child, she felt Orsen settle onto a stone nearby, unhurried, keeping the polite distance of a man who has decided not to intrude.

"The last page," he said quietly. "*Closed by order.* You noticed the date?"

"September 1902."

"And the cemetery records for every settlement on the dry side begin again in 1903. I've checked. There's a gap. A year with no births registered, no deaths, nothing — as if forty-one men and whoever loved them never existed at all." He looked into the coals. "Your father didn't hide a payroll, Ingrid. He hid a population."

She closed the notebook on the names and held it against her chest, and out on the dark plateau the wind kept up its long one-note song, and two days south of them, though none of them knew it yet, riders were breaking camp before dawn with rifles wrapped in sacking and a wagon loaded with shovels.
