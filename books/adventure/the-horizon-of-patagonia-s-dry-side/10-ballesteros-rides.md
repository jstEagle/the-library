## 10. Ballesteros Rides

The decision to cut back along their own trail came to Orsen the way most of his decisions did — not as a thought but as a habit of mind asserting itself. A surveyor's instinct: you do not trust a line until you have walked it twice. They had come four days out from the railhead, and the country behind them had been rising in long, almost imperceptible swells toward the shoulder of the meseta, and something about the geometry of their backtrail had been bothering him since noon without ever announcing itself as a worry.

It was the wind, finally, that gave him the excuse. It had swung north-northwest overnight, which meant it now blew at their backs instead of their faces, which meant that for the first time since leaving the railhead they could move quickly and quietly over ground they had already broken. He told Tomás he wanted to re-shoot a bearing on the second cairn line — which was even true, or true enough — and told Ingrid nothing at all, because she would have asked better questions than Tomás did.

"I'll take the glass," he said, slinging the field binoculars across his chest. "Keep the column moving slow. I'll catch you up before the water halt."

Tomás looked up from his notebook with the particular brightness he reserved for anything resembling technique. "You want me to leave a marker at the turnoff?"

"No markers." Orsen heard how sharply it came out and softened it by half. "We're not building a line here, muchacho. We're just passing through."

He turned the mule off the trail and rode back the way they had come, alone, the scrub opening around him like a conversation he was interrupting. The land out here did its work in grays and olive-drabs — coirón grass bent flat by the wind, spiny cushions of neneo, the occasional gaunt skeleton of a calafate bush. There were no trees to speak of, no stone worth the name, no feature a man could point to and say *there, that is where I am*. Distance on the dry side was not measured in landmarks but in hours, and hours could lie.

Two miles back, he found what he had half-known he would find.

The wagon tracks came up out of the southeast, cutting their trail at an angle, and then — this was the part that sat cold in his stomach — turned and followed it. Not crossing. Following. The wheel ruts were a day old, perhaps less; the edges still stood crisp, uncrumbled by wind. And overprinting the wheel tracks, spaced like punctuation, the round stamped signatures of shod horses. Four horses, maybe five. Heavy shoes, recently set. He dismounted and crouched over one print longer than any engineer needs to crouch over a hoofprint, and told himself he was reading depth and moisture when really he was giving his hands something to do while his mind finished the arithmetic it had already done.

A wagon meant supplies. Supplies meant intention. Nobody hauled a wagon onto the meseta for an afternoon.

He climbed the nearest swell, bellied down against the coirón at its crest, and put the glasses to his eyes.

They were small yet — dark shapes strung along the trail three miles back, near the low saddle where they themselves had camped two nights ago. But the glass pulled them close enough to count: four riders, and behind them the wagon, canvas-topped, drawn by two heavy draft animals. Even at that distance there was no mistaking the cut of the men — not gauchos, who ride like they were poured into the saddle as children, but town men on horseback, sitting stiff, rifles slung across their backs in the manner of men who have been told to carry them rather than men who have carried them all their lives. Except the lead rider. The lead rider sat well enough, and even at three miles Orsen knew the set of those shoulders, the way the man checked his flanks every few minutes with a smooth quarter-turn of the head, economical, unhurried.

Señor Ballesteros.

Orsen lowered the glasses and lay in the grass a long moment, feeling the wind move over his back and thinking about the provisioning house in Deseado, and the questions asked too casually, and the offer made too gently — *a man of your experience, Nystrom, we'd hate to see you wasting yourself out there* — and how smoothly he himself had declined, and how smoothly, he understood now, he had been permitted to decline. Ballesteros had never needed his answer. The answer had been a formality, like a signature on a document already written.

He should have felt fear. What he felt instead, lying in the flattened grass with the whole gray sky leaning on him, was a strange and bitter clarity — the same clarity he remembered from the one other time in his life the ground had shifted under him, twelve years ago, on a night he did not permit himself to visit. *So,* he thought. *This is what I am now. A man followed.* He got up, brushed the seeds from his coat, and turned the mule toward his crew with the deliberate calm of a man walking into a room where he intends to lie.

* * *

He caught them at the water halt, in the lee of a shallow claypan where the wind dropped and the mules drank with the greedy, gulping patience of animals that know thirst as a permanent condition. Ingrid watched him come in and said nothing, which was worse than a question. Tomás said everything.

"Well? Did you get your bearing?"

"Better than that." Orsen swung down and kept his voice level, pitched under the wind. "There's a party behind us. Four mounted men and a supply wagon, about three miles back, following our trail exactly. They're moving slower than us, but they don't need to move fast. They know where we're going."

Tomás's face went through several expressions in quick succession — disbelief, excitement, and then, arriving late but landing hardest, fear. "Following our trail? How? We left the railhead quiet—"

"Quiet isn't invisible. Fourteen animals leave a scar a blind man could follow." He glanced at the muleteers.

That was the mistake, or the necessity — he was never sure afterward which. The muleteers, Sosa and Cabrera, were standing by the water barrels, and they had stopped pretending not to listen. Sosa was a leathery, silent man in his fifties who had driven stock across half the territory and trusted nothing he couldn't eat; Cabrera was younger, talkative, with a gambler's cheerfulness that Orsen had privately assessed as either courage or a lack of imagination. Both of them were looking at him now with the flat attention of men who had been waiting four days for exactly this conversation.

"Company men?" Sosa said. Not a question. He spat, carefully, away from the barrels.

"Railway men," Orsen said. "Riding for the Southern Patagonia."

"Then I'll ask it plain," Sosa said, "since nobody else seems inclined to." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Four days ago you pay us for mules and guide work to the meseta and back, fair enough, strange route but fair wages. Since then I've counted three things that don't fit. One: we buy more water than any survey party ever carried, and you weigh it yourself each morning like a jeweler. Two: the señorita there" — a tilt of his chin at Ingrid — "asks nothing about gold, and you tell nothing about anything. Three: this morning you ride back alone with the glasses, and you come back with a face like a man reading his own death certificate." He folded his arms. "So. What are we carrying, señor? Because if it's only instruments, we can laugh about it tonight. And if it isn't, my brother-in-law and I would like to know what price we've actually agreed to."

Cabrera hadn't spoken, but he had moved — subtly, unconsciously — to stand beside Sosa, and Orsen noted it the way he noted everything: two men becoming one unit, the unit no longer his.

Ingrid saved him, or condemned him; the distinction seemed thinner every day. She stepped forward, wind pulling at her hair, and said, "Tell them, Señor Nystrom. Whatever you decide to tell them, tell it straight. I'm tired of being managed too."

The wind moved through the coirón grass with a sound like paper turning. Orsen looked at the four faces turned toward him — the old muleteer's suspicion, the young one's uneasy cheerfulness gone stale, the apprentice's naked anxiety, the telegraph operator's hard, grieving patience — and he understood that he had arrived at the place every expedition arrives at eventually, the place where a leader's authority runs out of road and has to be paid for in coin of another kind.

He bought time the way he always did: precisely.

"Sosa's right that the water is unusual," he said. "I'll say that much. We may need every liter before we're home, and I won't explain why until I can explain it fully, because half an explanation is worse than none — it makes men argue with arithmetic." He held up a hand before Sosa could object. "What I will tell you is this. The men behind us are riding for people who offered me money four days ago to stay off this plateau. I declined. They didn't accept the decline. That means they consider whatever we're doing worth a wagon, four rifles, and a long ride into empty country to stop. I don't yet know whether they mean to stop us with words or with the other thing. I hope to find out before it matters."

"And what we're carrying," Sosa said, unmoved, "is?"

"Instruments, provisions, and a dead man's promise. That's the truth entire, as far as I'm free to give it."

"As far as you're *free*," Cabrera repeated, and laughed, but without any of the morning's music in it. "You hear that, Sosa? Free. We signed on to a secret and didn't get told the fee."

"The fee," Orsen said quietly, "is that if you turn back now, you keep your wages and lose nothing but the story. If you stay, you stay knowing you're followed, and knowing I can't promise you it ends well. I'd rather you chose with open eyes than stayed out of stubbornness and hated me by Friday."

Silence, except the wind and the mules shifting, chains clicking, breath steaming faintly in the cold. It was Ingrid who broke it, and she broke it in a way Orsen did not expect and never afterward forgot.

"They killed my father," she said. "Not with bullets — with silence, with years, with whatever it is that eats a man alone in a room in Puerto Deseado until there's nothing left but a map and a stranger to give it to. Those men behind us are riding for the same silence." She looked at Sosa, then Cabrera, and her voice didn't rise at all. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm telling you what's chasing us, so you can decide if you want it catching you too."

Sosa studied her for a long moment, then Orsen, then the northern horizon as though the answer might be posted there. "My brother-in-law and I will talk," he said at last. "Tonight, at camp. You'll have your answer at first light, and you'll live with it either way." He turned back to the barrels, conversation closed, and began cinching a load strap with unnecessary force.

Cabrera lingered. "For what it's worth, señor," he said, lower, "I don't like being followed. But I like being lied to less, and you haven't quite lied to us yet. Mind you keep it that way." He grinned — the old grin, mostly restored — and went to his mule.

* * *

They made six more miles before dusk, putting distance between themselves and the wagon with the methodical, unspectacular pace that Orsen had learned was the only honest speed on the dry side. He set the camp in a hollow below a rim of basalt, fires small and shielded, animals picketed close. Then he did what he had been postponing since the claypan: he took the oilcloth map from its oilskin wrapper and studied it by the last gray light, alone, while the camp sounds settled around him.

Efraín Salas's hand was steady even dying — a draftsman's hand, or a man who had once been taught to draw. The route ran northwest across the meseta in long confident strokes, annotated in a cramped Spanish full of abbreviations: distances in kilometers, bearings in degrees, waterholes marked with small careful circles, some crossed out. And at the head of the sheet, in letters pressed deep enough to read with a fingertip, a single word Orsen had taken for a prospector's boast:

**AGUA.**

He had assumed it named a destination. Lying on his belly in the wind-shadow of the basalt, tracing the line of the route with one finger, he suddenly saw it differently. The word wasn't at the end of the route. It was at the beginning — a title, not a landmark. *Water* was the thing itself. Water was what the whole map was *about*, and everything else — the cairns, the distances, the dying man's promise — was only the road to it.

And company men were following them across forty miles of the driest plateau in the territory, carrying a wagonload of supplies, to stop them from finding it.

He wrapped the map away and sat with his back to the stone and let himself think the thought he had been refusing since the crest of the hill: *Ballesteros knows what AGUA means. That's why he offered me a promotion.* The firm had sent casual questions and a polite salary after him, and when those failed, rifles and a wagon. You did not spend that kind of money preventing a survey unless the survey threatened something worth more than the money. Twelve years he had given the Southern Patagonia Railway, twelve years of sleepers and gradients and lonely miles, and somewhere in all that loyalty there had apparently been a floor beneath which he had never looked.

From the fire, low voices: Tomás explaining something with his hands, Ingrid listening with her chin on her knees, the firelight moving on her face. Sosa apart, whetstone ringing slow and regular against a blade, the sound carrying in the cold air like a clock.

First light, then. He would have his answer at first light, and whichever way it fell, the expedition would change shape. Fourteen animals or ten. Five souls or three. He found, examining himself honestly, that he could not decide which outcome he dreaded more — abandonment, or the loyalty of men who stayed knowing the price, because loyalty freely given under threat was a debt he would owe until the sea.

The whetstone rang. The wind rose and fell. Somewhere south, beyond three miles of darkening grass, a wagon lamp burned or didn't burn, and men sat around their own smaller fire discussing him.

At first light, Sosa walked to Orsen's bedroll before the sun cleared the rim, and stood over him, and said the thing that ended the easy part of the journey forever:

"We're staying, señor. But you should know — Cabrera talked to the horse trader in Deseado before we left, and the horse trader talks to everybody." He paused, and his old face was unreadable as the plateau. "Ballesteros already knew your route before you bought your first barrel. Somebody in town sold it to him. So before you decide who's loyal to you out here—" he tipped his head, very slightly, toward the fire, toward the sleeping shapes of the girl and the boy, "—you might want to decide who's been loyal to *him*."
