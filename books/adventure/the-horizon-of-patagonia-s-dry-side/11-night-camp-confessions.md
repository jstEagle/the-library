## 11. Night Camp Confessions

Tomás Ferreyra had learned to read Orsen Nystrom the way he was learning to read instruments: by watching for small deviations from a known baseline. The old engineer had exactly three moods in the field. There was the working mood, all economy and precision, hands moving before words did. There was the weather mood, when the wind came up off the plateau and he went silent as a fence post, eyes on the horizon like it owed him money. And there was the third mood, rarest, which Tomás had only seen twice — a kind of listening stillness, as though the man were hearing something under the ground.

Tonight, sitting at the fire with his boots off and his hat brim pulled low, Orsen was in none of those moods. He was in a fourth one Tomás had never seen, and it frightened him more than any of them.

They had made camp early, in the lee of a basalt rise that broke the worst of the wind. The mules stood hipshot in their picket line, ears swiveling. The two muleteers — broad, sun-cured men named Chávez and Ruiz who had signed on for wages and said little else — were seeing to the animals. Ingrid Salas sat cross-legged on her bedroll with her father's oilcloth map unrolled across her knees, studying it by firelight the way she had studied it every night since they'd passed the last sleeper of the abandoned spur, though she never asked outright what it meant. She just looked. And Orsen let her look, which Tomás understood now was its own kind of answer.

The barometer readings had been wrong again that afternoon. Tomás had checked the instrument three times against the aneroid in Orsen's kit, and both disagreed, and both disagreed with the boiling-point estimate he'd taken at noon. On the meseta, everything that measured pressure seemed to be lying by a little, and always in the same direction — as if the land itself were pressing upward on the needles, or holding its breath beneath them.

He'd written it in his field book: *Instruments inconsistent. Suspect temperature effect. Will recalibrate.* It was what a good apprentice wrote. What he actually thought was: *Something down there is bigger than we are.*

Orsen fed a stick of scrub into the fire and watched it catch.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we'll come to a place where I have to tell you why we're really here."

Tomás looked up. Ingrid didn't; she kept her eyes on the map, but her thumb stopped moving along its edge.

"You've been paid to carry water barrels across a plateau," Orsen went on, in the flat voice he used for reading elevations aloud. "That's what you were told, and it's true as far as it goes. But there's a reason the route is where it is, and you deserve to hear it tonight rather than stumble into it tomorrow."

Chávez had drifted back from the picket line, wiping his hands on his thighs. Ruiz stood behind him, arms folded. Neither sat down, but neither walked away.

Orsen reached into his coat and took out the oilcloth map — not the original; that stayed wrapped in waxed canvas inside his saddlebag, and Tomás knew because he'd helped pack it. This was a copy Orsen had drawn himself, in pencil, on butcher's paper, and it showed the same route they'd been following for five days: a long diagonal line across the Meseta del Viento, running nowhere, connecting nothing, ending at a point marked with a single word.

*AGUA.*

"I've been with the Southern Patagonia Railway for twelve years," Orsen said. "Before that, six years with the Andino surveys. Eighteen years total. I've chained every mile of dry side this company owns, and most of what it doesn't." He laid the copy on his knee. "I know these lines better than the men who sign the payrolls. So when I saw this route, five days ago, I recognized it. Not because I'd walked it. Because I'd read it."

"Read it where?" Tomás asked.

"In the 1901 survey files. Puerto Deseado. A reconnaissance party came out here twenty-two years ago looking for a rail alignment to the interior. They found something better than an alignment." Orsen turned the paper so the firelight fell across it. "They found water. A shallow aquifer under this section of the plateau — iron-rich, which is why your compasses have been wandering, Tomás, and why your barometers lie. The company sank a well. Dug it properly, timbered the shaft, put a crew on it through the winter. Forty-one men."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a mule shifted and the picket rope creaked.

"And then," Orsen said, "the company erased it."

Tomás felt the words go past him and then, a half-second later, arrive. "Erased it how?"

"The survey file is gone. The well record is gone. The crew's payroll doesn't exist in any ledger the company keeps. The alignment was formally rejected in 1902 on grounds of insufficient water — *insufficient water*, on a plateau where they'd dug a producing well — and the whole project was moved south toward the coast." He said it evenly, without heat, the way he'd recite a chainage. "I've spent four nights going through my memory of those files, and there's no honest engineering reason for it. None. The numbers worked. The water was real. Somebody decided it should never have been found, and made it so thoroughly unfound that even the men who dug the well were struck out of the company's books."

"Why?" Ruiz said from the shadows. It was the first thing either muleteer had volunteered in five days.

"That's the part I don't know yet," Orsen said. "And the part I intend to find out."

Tomás became aware that he was gripping his field book too hard, the cover bowing under his fingers. He made himself set it down.

"That can't be right," he said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it aloud, but now it was out, and nineteen years of loyalty stood up inside him and refused to sit back down.

"The company wouldn't do that," he went on. "You don't erase a survey. Surveys cost money — thousands of pesos, months of work. If the well produced, they'd have used it. Water is worth more than coal out here and everyone knows it. There has to be an explanation. Bad samples. Contamination. Maybe the aquifer tested brackish and—" 

"It's fresh," Ingrid said quietly.

Tomás turned to her. She hadn't raised her voice. She was still looking at the map, but she wasn't reading it anymore; her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely.

"How would you know?"

"Because my father spent fifty years walking out here." She finally lifted her head. Her face in the firelight was all planes and shadow, and her eyes had the fixed brightness Tomás had learned to be careful around. "Fifty years, Mr. Ferreyra. Do you understand what that is? He left home when I was nine years old. He came back maybe once every two years, stayed a week, filled the house with silence, and left again. My mother died waiting for him to stay. I stopped writing to him when I was twenty because every letter came back with the same sentence in it — *soon I'll have what I'm looking for* — and I decided I hated the sentence more than I loved the man." She looked down at the map. "Now I know what the sentence meant. He wasn't looking for gold. He was looking for proof. And if he spent fifty years buying it piece by piece, then whatever's buried under this plateau is fresh enough, and important enough, that a man gave up his entire life to document it."

"That proves he believed it," Tomás said. "It doesn't prove the company—"

"Mr. Ferreyra." Ingrid's voice didn't sharpen; it got quieter, which was worse. "My father is dead. He died in a hotel room in Puerto Deseado with this map in his hands, handing it to a stranger because he didn't trust anyone the company could reach. Whatever the truth is, it killed him slowly over fifty years, and I'd thank you not to defend the people who may have loaded the gun."

The silence after that was enormous. The wind moved through the scrub with a sound like sand poured over paper. Tomás stared into the fire and felt his face burning in a way that had nothing to do with the flames.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: the company had fed him. Not in some abstract way — literally, specifically, with names and dates. His father had died in a tunnel collapse on the Comodoro branch when Tomás was six, and the Southern Patagonia Railway had paid the widow's settlement without a fight, had kept his mother on the books as a station laundress for eleven years, had taken Tomás himself off the streets at sixteen and put a chain and a level in his hands and told him he had a good eye. Every meal he'd eaten since he was a child had come, one way or another, out of the company's kitchen. When people in his mother's town said *la empresa*, they didn't say it the way Ingrid said it. They said it the way you say a father's name.

"They're not perfect," he said finally, and heard how young his own voice sounded. "I know they're not perfect. But you don't build eight hundred miles of line through nothing and keep towns alive on the edge of a desert by being monsters. My mother gets her coal from the company. Half the estancias between here and the coast drink because the company drilled for them. If the firm erased a survey, there's a reason, and the reason might be ugly, but it isn't — it can't be what you're implying. Companies don't spend money digging wells and then pretend the wells don't exist unless—"

"Unless someone above them ordered it," Orsen said.

Tomás stopped.

Orsen hadn't moved. He sat with his forearms on his knees, firelight catching the grey in his beard, and he looked, Tomás thought suddenly, very tired — tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day's march.

"I've served this firm for eighteen years," Orsen said. "It gave me my first chain. It taught me everything I know about gradients and grades and how to keep men alive in country that wants them dead. I am not here to burn it down, Tomás. Hear me clearly on that. But I am here to find out what happened in 1901, and I will not pretend I didn't see what I've seen, because pretending is how things like this stay buried for twenty years." He picked up the pencil copy, folded it, and handed it — not to Tomás, not to Ingrid, but to Ruiz, standing at the edge of the light. "You two are carrying our water and our lives. You should hold a copy of the route. If anything happens to me, get them to the marked point and then get them home. That's all I ask of you."

Ruiz took the paper without a word and passed it to Chávez, who folded it into his shirt. Something in the small ceremony — the passing of the paper hand to hand, the fire cracking, the mules stamping softly in the dark — made Tomás's throat tighten. It felt less like a camp conversation than like a will being read.

Chávez spat thoughtfully into the dirt. "Company men followed us out of Deseado," he said. "Two riders, a day back, keeping our dust in sight. You want to tell us what they're following us for?"

Orsen's face didn't change, but Tomás saw his hands go still.

"I want to tell you it won't matter," Orsen said. "And I want you to believe me, and I'm not sure I'd believe me."

Chávez grunted, which might have meant anything, and he and Ruiz withdrew to the picket line, leaving the fire to the three of them.

For a while nobody spoke. Ingrid rolled the original map closed and tied it with its cord, her fingers quick and precise, a telegrapher's fingers, and Tomás realized he was staring and made himself stop.

"You think I'm wrong to defend them," he said at last.

"I think you're honest," Ingrid said. "Which is rarer." She set the map aside. "But I'll tell you what I know about erasure, Mr. Ferreyra, and then I'll leave it alone. Where I grew up, there was a family called the Acevedos. Four brothers, a mother, a father. One winter the father took work on the coast and never sent for them, and the mother took sick, and the town — everyone — simply stopped speaking of them. Not cruelly. Just completely. By spring the house had been pulled apart for firewood, and by the next year if you said the name Acevedo people looked at you blankly, and the ones who remembered looked away." She held her hands out to the fire. "Nobody killed them. Nobody had to. The town just agreed, all together, that they had never existed, and that agreement was enough. They starved inside their own name." She looked at him. "A payroll with forty-one men on it, and no record anywhere that those men ever lived — that's not paperwork, Tomás. That's the same agreement. Erasure is a kind of killing. It's just the kind where you don't have to attend the funeral."

Tomás opened his mouth and closed it again. He wanted to say that a company wasn't a town, that ledgers weren't neighbors, that ink and intention weren't the same thing. He wanted to say it badly. And underneath the wanting was the other thing, the stone in his chest, turning over: *they kept my mother on the books.* Eleven years of laundry wages. Had that been kindness? Had it been the same hand that struck forty-one names from a payroll, deciding, entry by entry, who existed and who didn't? He couldn't make the two facts fit in the same company, and he couldn't make them fit in two different companies either, because there was only one, and it had signed both entries.

"I need to check the chronometer against the stars," he said, which was true, and also a retreat, and he knew Ingrid knew it.

She nodded, gracious enough not to say so.

He carried the instrument case out beyond the firelight, into the blue-black cold, and knelt with the sextant box open on his knees, and did not immediately begin measuring. Behind him the fire was a low orange murmur. He could hear Orsen's voice, low and continuous, and Ingrid's answers, shorter, and though no individual words reached him, the shape of the conversation was legible enough: two people negotiating the terms of a trust that had cracked down the middle and might, if they were careful, be rejoined along the fault.

His hands were steady on the instrument. That surprised him. Inside, nothing was steady at all.

He sighted on Polaris, brought it to the horizon glass, read the altitude, and worked the correction with numb fingers. Then he compared the result to the chronometer and found, as he'd found every night for three nights, that the watch had gained — a few seconds, no more, but consistent, directional, wrong. He wrote it down. *Chronometer fast. Cumulative error now 11 seconds. Will apply correction.*

Eleven seconds. At fifteen miles a day, eleven seconds of time was nearly three miles of longitude. He stared at the figure and felt the desert tilt faintly beneath him, the way a deck tilts. All their positions, all week, had been quietly drifting eastward of where they truly were, and he had been applying corrections, and the corrections themselves depended on the failing watch, and somewhere in that elegant circular arithmetic an error might already be nesting, growing, compounding — patient as water finding its way through caliche.

He closed the case and sat back on his heels and looked up. The sky over the Meseta del Viento was obscene with stars, more stars than sky, the Milky Way lying across it like spilled flour. Somewhere out in that darkness were two riders with a day-old trail and a reason of their own. Somewhere ahead was a point marked AGUA, and whatever Efraín Salas had spent fifty years buying.

When he came back to the fire, the conversation was over. Orsen lay on his bedroll with his hat over his eyes. Ingrid sat awake, wrapped in her blanket, watching the coals.

"He's a good man," she said, without looking at Tomás. "Your chief. He told me the truth tonight in the exact order a careful man tells it — the parts that protect us first, the parts that endanger us last. That's not nothing."

"No," Tomás said. "It's not nothing."

"But tomorrow," Ingrid said, "when we come to whatever we come to — remember that I asked you a question tonight and you defended the company before you'd seen a single grave." She pulled the blanket higher. "I'm not saying you were wrong. I'm saying write it down. You're a surveyor. Write down where you stood."

Tomás lay down and did not sleep for a long time. The wind rose after midnight and moved over the basalt with a long exhaled sound, and once, near the coldest hour, he heard Orsen sit up abruptly and stand, motionless, facing north into the dark — and after a moment Tomás heard it too, far off across the plateau, thin and almost lost in the wind:

Hooves. More than two. Coming on at a walk, unhurried, like men who knew exactly where they were going.
