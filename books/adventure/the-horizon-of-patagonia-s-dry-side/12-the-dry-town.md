## 12. The Dry Town

Ingrid saw it before anyone else did — not because her eyes were better than Orsen's or Tomás's, but because she had spent nine years reading telegraph tape in a dark room, and she had learned to see what was almost not there: the faint interruption of a pattern, the dot where a dash should have been. The land ahead of them had been flat for hours, scrub and stone and wind, and then it wasn't quite flat. There was a wrongness to the horizon, a softness where the evening light should have run clean.

"Orsen," she said.

He reined up beside her and looked where she pointed. For a long moment he said nothing, and she watched his face do something complicated — a tightening around the eyes, a settling of the jaw, as if he were bracing himself against a weight he had known was coming but had hoped would arrive later.

"Yes," he said finally. "That's it."

"That's what?" Tomás asked from behind, standing in his stirrups to look. "I don't see anything."

"You will," Ingrid said.

They came down off the low rise as the sun went the color of old brass, and the town assembled itself out of nothing, the way towns do in that country — first a corner of wall, then a roofline without a roof, then the whole sad geometry of streets laid out in confidence by men who believed they knew where water would be. Adobe, mostly, dissolved back toward the earth it had been dug from. Walls stood waist-high or shoulder-high or not at all. Doorways opened onto rooms full of sand, and the sand had drifted into shapes like frozen water, like the town had drowned in the ground itself rather than under it.

Nobody spoke. Even the mules went quiet, ears forward, picking their way through what had once been a street wide enough for two carts.

Ingrid had imagined this moment on the trail — she admitted that to herself now, riding down the main street of a dead town at dusk. She had imagined anger, mostly. Her father's name had been a closed door her whole life, and she had built her resentment into a structure as solid as any of these walls, and she had thought that whatever they found out here would give her ammunition. Proof. Something she could hold up and say *this is why you left me, this is what mattered more than your own daughter.*

She had not imagined silence. She had not imagined that the truth might arrive not as an accusation but as a presence, heavy and patient, that made all her prepared speeches feel like paper blown against adobe.

* * *

The church was the only building still taller than a man, and even it had shrugged. One wall leaned outward at an angle that seemed to defy argument; the others had crumbled to about chest height, so that from the street the church looked like a cupped hand holding nothing. The bell — Ingrid saw it when they dismounted — had come down from its tower years ago and half-buried itself in the drift at the church's threshold, tilted, its mouth filled with sand. Someone had tried to dig it out once, long ago by the look of it; there were old scrape marks on the bronze, and then the scraping stopped, as if the digger had lost interest or hope or simply run out of time.

Tomás touched the bell's rim and drew his fingers across it, and a sound came out — not a ring, more a breath, a dull exhalation of metal, like a voice too tired to finish a sentence. He snatched his hand back as if scalded.

"Sorry," he said, to no one, to everyone.

"It's all right," Ingrid said, though she didn't know why she said it, or why her voice came out so low.

Orsen walked the street slowly, and she watched him do the thing she had watched him do every day since Puerto Deseado: measure. His eyes went along the walls, counting, pacing, reading the ground the way she read tape. But today the measuring had a different quality. He moved like a man walking through a house where he had once been happy, touching things he remembered. At one doorway he stopped and stood for a long time with his hat in his hands, looking at nothing, at a floor of sand and a wall with a faded stripe of whitewash at shoulder height.

"What is it?" she asked, coming up beside him.

"A house," he said. "A family lived here." He turned the hat over once in his hands. "You can tell. They painted. People don't paint walls they mean to leave."

Ingrid looked. The stripe of whitewash ran around the interior of the room at exactly the height a standing man could reach, neat and even, done with care. Below it the adobe was bare. She thought of her father's kitchen in Comodoro Rivadavia, the one room in the house he had kept painted, repainting it every few years in the same pale green, never explaining why, never letting her help. She had always assumed it was control, another form of his distance. Standing in this ruined room she understood suddenly that it might have been memory. That some men repaint a wall because they cannot rebuild a town.

"Whose town was this?" she asked.

Orsen didn't answer right away. He looked east, toward the darkening plateau, then down at the street, at the wheel ruts baked hard into the caliche, ruts that rain had never softened because rain had stopped coming here, or had been made to stop.

"Pozo Nuevo," he said. "New Well." He said the name carefully, the way you handle something with a broken edge. "It isn't on any map I've ever carried."

"I know," Ingrid said. "I've read maps my whole life. My father kept them. Railway surveys, land office plats, everything. There's no Pozo Nuevo."

"No," Orsen said. "There wouldn't be."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the church wall, because it was the tallest thing standing and because none of them, Ingrid suspected, wanted to sleep inside a house. Tomás got a small fire going with scrub wood, feeding it sparingly — Orsen's rationing discipline had become second nature to all of them now — and the muleteers picketed the animals near the old corral, whose posts had rotted to stubs but whose shape was still legible in the ground, a rectangle of stakes and habit.

It was while the fire was small and the sky going from brass to iron that Ingrid noticed the cemetery.

It lay beyond the last buildings, on a slight rise, fenced — or fenced once — with wire and crooked posts, most of the wire long gone. She saw the crosses first, white in the last light, dozens of them, ranked in rows with a care that hurt to look at. Not scattered, not jumbled the way death in a bad season scatters people. Ranked. Someone had buried these people in order, in rows, with space between, the way you plant a field you expect to harvest.

She walked up the rise alone. Behind her she heard Tomás say something to Orsen, and Orsen answer quietly, and neither of them followed her, and she was grateful.

There were forty-one graves.

She counted them twice, because the number arrived in her mind before she finished counting, arriving from somewhere she couldn't name — from her father's letters, maybe, which she had read four times now on the trail, the last letter especially, the one that said *I kept it, mija, I kept it all these years, and keeping it was the whole of my life.* Forty-one crosses. Some were wooden and weathered to the color of bone; some were stone, rough-cut, the letters chiseled deep enough to survive. And every grave had a date, and the dates were nearly all the same.

1902.

One after another after another. Enero 1902. Febrero 1902. Marzo. Abril. Mayo. They marched across the cemetery like months on a calendar, like pages torn from one year and nailed to the ground. A few graves carried two names, side by side, a man and a woman, or a woman and a child — the child's cross smaller, the date the same as the mother's, sometimes within days.

Ingrid stood among them with her hands hanging empty at her sides and felt the horror arrive the way cold arrives, not all at once but upward, from the ground, through the soles of her boots.

This was not a mining accident. This was not fever, not bandits, not any single catastrophe a town survives and rebuilds from. This was a town dying in sequence, month by month, through a whole year, and being buried in order by whoever remained alive to do the burying. And then the burying had stopped, because there was no one left to stop it, and the town had been taken off the maps, and fifty years of wind had done the rest.

She heard footsteps behind her and did not turn. Orsen stopped a few paces away, and for once he did not measure anything. He just stood, hatless, his gray hair moving in the wind.

"Forty-one," she said.

"Yes."

"The ledger we found in the cairn. The well crew. It listed forty-one workers."

"I know," Orsen said.

"Do you think—" She stopped. Her throat had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with thirst. "Do you think these are them? The same forty-one?"

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved the scrub at the edge of the fence with a dry whispering, like paper, like tape ticking out of a receiver bearing a message no one would send.

"The dates are 1902," he said at last. "The payroll ledger is dated 1901. A crew digs a well in '01. A town grows around the well. And in '02—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. In the country they had crossed for five days, there was only one thing that ended a town in order, month by month, gently, without violence.

The water.

"They died of thirst," Ingrid said. She said it flatly, testing the words, the way you tap a rail to hear if it's cracked. "Here. On top of a well."

"We don't know yet," Orsen said. "Not for certain. Tomorrow I'll—"

"Don't." She turned and faced him, and she saw him take a small step back, surprised by her. "Don't manage me. Don't say *we don't know yet* the way the company says it. I've been a telegraph operator for eleven years, Señor Nystrom. Do you know what I learned, tapping other people's words all day? I learned that everything true gets sent twice. Once in the message and once in what the sender leaves out. My father left out a town. You've been leaving one out since Puerto Deseado — I've watched you do it, giving me the letter of your promise and not the spirit, telling me what route we're taking and never why. Well." She gestured at the graves, at the forty-one crosses going blue in the dusk. "Here's what was left out. Here it is, in the ground, in order, with dates."

Orsen took it. She watched him take it — the rebuke, the grief, both — and he didn't defend himself, and somehow that was worse and better at once.

"You're right," he said. "I'll tell you everything tomorrow. What I can read from the ground, I'll read aloud, in front of everyone. You have my word."

"Your word," Ingrid said, and laughed, and hated the sound of it. "My father gave a man his word once, too. Look how far it carried him. Fifty years alone with a secret, and a daughter who thought he simply hadn't loved her enough to stay."

She hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out of her the way water comes out of a cracked barrel — all at once, unstoppable, and then it was gone and the ground drank it. Orsen said nothing. He put his hat back on, slowly, and looked out over the graves, and the last light left the plateau while they stood there, and neither of them moved to go back to the fire.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to a red eye and Tomás slept with his instruments case under his head like a pillow, Ingrid sat awake and listened to the town.

That was the thing no one warned you about a dead place: it wasn't quiet. Wind found every broken wall and played it. Sand ticked against adobe. Somewhere a piece of loose tin — a roof scrap, a sign, something — lifted and dropped and lifted again in a rhythm almost like speech, almost like someone tapping out a message from inside a collapsed building, *still here, still here, still here.* She told herself it was metal and wind. She was eleven years a telegraph operator; she knew the difference between a signal and noise. But knowing did not stop her from listening, hour after hour, for the word underneath.

Near midnight she gave up on sleep and walked the main street with the moon high and white, and the town showed her its details the way faces show their details in bad light — honestly. A boot heel, leather gone stiff, half-sunk in a doorway. A child's clay marble, perfect and round, in the middle of the street where it had rolled and been abandoned mid-game. On one standing wall, at the height of a seated person, a handprint in faded ochre, small, a child's, pressed there by someone who loved it or by the child itself, and Ingrid pressed her own hand against it and her hand was larger and the fit was wrong and she took it away quickly as if from a stove.

At the end of the street stood a building bigger than the rest, its walls thicker, its doorway framed in cut timber that had cost someone money. The company store, she thought. Or the paymaster's office. Above the door, carved into the lintel, letters worn shallow but still readable:

POZO NUEVO — FUNDADO 1901

Founded 1901. Struck from the maps sometime after 1902. Two years of existence, compressed into a lintel. She stood before it a long time, and she thought about her father at fifteen — fifteen, the age Tomás nearly was — somewhere in a town like this one, watching it die, carrying something away from it that he would spend fifty years paying interest on. She thought about the oilcloth map folded against Orsen's ribs, the route drawn in a dying man's careful hand, the point marked AGUA. She had asked Orsen three times what her father had found out there, and three times he had given her a sentence with nothing inside it, a shell of an answer, and she had catalogued each evasion like a debt.

Standing under the lintel, Ingrid understood something that rearranged her anger without dissolving it: her father had not chosen the map over her. He had chosen it *because* of her — because shame is a thing a man cannot hand his daughter, and so he handed it to a stranger with strong legs and a railroad man's habits, and paid for the delivery with the only coin he had left, which was the truth about where he'd been all those years.

It didn't forgive him. She held onto that. Forgiveness would have to be earned, argued, fought for — she had promised herself an argument and she intended to have it, with him or with his ghost. But it explained him, and explanation, she was discovering, is a heavier burden than grievance. Grievance you can carry in one hand. Explanation takes both.

Behind her, gravel shifted. She turned. Orsen stood at the corner of the company building, a shadow against shadows, and she realized he had been awake too, and had let her walk alone, and had followed only to be near in case the night required it.

"There's something else," he said quietly. "Something I should show you tonight, before morning. Before the others."

"More than forty-one graves?"

He hesitated. In the moonlight his face was unreadable, and then it wasn't — and what she read there frightened her more than the cemetery had, because it wasn't horror. It was recognition.

"The well," Orsen said. "Come and see what was done to the well."

He led her past the last wall, out beyond the corrals, to a depression in the ground where the caliche had been cut and squared a generation ago — a wellhead, stone-capped, engineered, and sealed. Not dried up. Not collapsed. Sealed, with fitted stones and mortar and, bolted across the cap, a plate of iron stamped with letters she had to kneel and trace with her fingers to read, the moonlight thin, her heart loud:

SOUTHERN PATAGONIA RAILWAY — CLOSED BY ORDER

And beneath the stamp, smaller, almost an afterthought, the date.

The date was not 1902.

The well had been sealed in March of 1902 — while the cemetery on the rise was still filling, month by month, row by row, while someone was still digging graves in hard ground for people who had come to a town called New Well to drink.

Ingrid knelt in the dust with her fingers on the iron plate and did not move for a long time. Behind her, Orsen waited, silent. Ahead of them, east across the dark plateau, the wind was rising, and somewhere out in that darkness lay the thing her father had marked with a single word — AGUA — and she understood now, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the map had never been leading them to water at all.

It had been leading them to the reason there was none.
