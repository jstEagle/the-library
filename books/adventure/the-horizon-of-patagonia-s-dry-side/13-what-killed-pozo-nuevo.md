## 13. What Killed Pozo Nuevo

Orsen Nystrom had spent the better part of thirty years reading ground the way other men read ledgers, and so it was that he read Pozo Nuevo — not as a ruin, not as a tragedy, not even yet as a crime, but first and always as an engineering problem. That was his shame, though he would not know to call it shame until much later. He stood in the gray light of dawn among the collapsed adobe walls with his notebook open and his pencil moving, and what he felt was not horror. It was comprehension. The horror came afterward, arriving like a delayed telegram, and by then he had already written down everything.

The town had been laid out by someone who understood water. That was the first thing. The streets ran on a gentle grade toward a central plaza — not for drainage, for gathering; every roof in a town like this was a catchment, every gutter fed a cistern, and the plaza would have held the communal tank. Adobe walls, thick at the base, plastered against the wind. The church stood where the church always stood, facing the prevailing weather, its bell now half-swallowed by the drift of caliche dust that had come in over the years like a slow tide. A town built by people who expected to stay.

He walked the main street with Tomás pacing behind him, uncharacteristically silent, and Ingrid somewhere off to the left near the cemetery fence, and he let his eye do what twelve years of closed lines had trained it to do. Read the ground. Find the water. Every abandoned settlement told you why it died if you knew how to ask, and Pozo Nuevo was shouting.

* * *

The wellhead sat two hundred paces north of the plaza, behind what had been the company storehouse, and Orsen knew it before he saw it because of the masonry. In a town of adobe, the well housing was brick — fired brick, hauled out here at cost over country with no road worth the name — and nobody spent that kind of money on a well they meant to abandon. The structure was circular, maybe four meters across, walls a meter high, capped now with a concrete plug poured in one continuous operation. He put his palm flat against the cap. It was still sound. Fifty years of frost-heave and sun and it had not cracked more than hairline. Whoever poured it had been good at their work.

That was the second thing, and it was worse than the first.

He crouched at the rim and looked at the joint where the cap met the old brick. There was a flange — a raised lip cast into the concrete's edge, sitting inside a channel cut into the wellhead masonry. He had seen that detail exactly once before in his life, in 1909, on a pressurized artesian installation outside Bahía Blanca, where the engineers had sealed a flowing bore against back-pressure. It was not a plug for a dry hole. It was a valve cover. You did not pour concrete like this over a well that had failed. You poured it over a well that was still producing, that might someday be reopened, that you wanted *kept* — kept the way a man keeps money he does not want his wife to find.

"Señor Nystrom." Tomás's voice came from behind the storehouse, tight and strange. "You should look at this."

Around the corner, cut into the caliche hardpan, ran a channel. Not dug — bored, with a machine, a clean cylindrical trench perhaps sixty centimeters across, running dead straight toward the southeast. It emerged from beneath the wellhouse apron and went out past the last wall of the town and up the shallow rise beyond, and along its length, at intervals, were the rusted collars of pipe joints, most of them pulled, some of them left in place and cut off flush. Orsen followed it on foot for nearly a kilometer without breaking stride, because he already knew where it went, and he wanted to be wrong, and he was not.

It crested the rise and descended toward a depression in the plateau's edge — and there, half-buried, was the pump station. A small building, steel-framed, its corrugated skin mostly gone, but the bedplate remained: anchor bolts in a pattern he recognized, the mounting footprint of a three-stage centrifugal set, the kind the Southern Patagonia Railway bought by the dozen for its coastal installations. And beyond it, marching away into the haze of morning, the pipeline itself — large-bore, riveted sections, running arrow-straight toward the coast. Toward Puerto Deseado. Toward the coal port and the coaling wharves and the company's ships, forty leagues of iron vein carrying water out of the meseta's heart to feed boilers and wash coal and fill locomotive tanks at a terminus that official surveys described as served by "inadequate local sources."

Orsen stood on the rise with the wind pushing at his back and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the only prayer he knew.

A crew of forty-one men digging a well on the Meseta del Viento in 1901. A well that struck the aquifer — the same aquifer whose signature had made Tomás's compasses wander five days ago, the iron-rich groundwater that pooled under the caliche in quantities no survey had ever admitted to. A company town built around it, families brought out, a church bell shipped overland. And then, sometime in 1902, a decision made in a boardroom in Buenos Aires or a manager's office in Puerto Deseado: the survey had said there was no water on the meseta. The charter, the land grants, the whole financial architecture of the Southern Patagonia Railway rested on that survey. If there was water — a lake of it, a buried sea of it — then the railway's claim to have been forced onto its ruinous coastal alignment collapsed, and with it the compensation claims, the land revaluations, the entire paper edifice. So you did not report the water. You took it. You capped the well, you drove a line to the coast, and you let the town drink the difference.

Forty-one graves dated 1902.

* * *

He found Ingrid at the cemetery. She was standing very still among the headstones with her hands folded in front of her like a woman at a funeral she had been dreading for fifty years, and when Orsen came through the gate she did not turn around.

"They're all the same year," she said. Her voice was level in the way ice is level. "Every single stone. March through November, 1902. Do you understand what that means? They didn't die of anything sudden. No fever, no accident. They died *slowly*, all year long, one after another, while somebody in an office counted the cost of keeping them alive."

"Some of these are children," Tomás said quietly. He had followed Orsen without being asked, and his young face had gone the color of the caliche. "There are small stones. Four of them."

"The paymaster stayed." Ingrid crouched beside the largest stone in the row, brushing dust from the carved letters with her sleeve. "Look. *Ramón Salas, pagador.* The others say *obrero*, *capataz*, *esposa de*. But he's named by his office. As if the stone wanted to record that he was still doing his job at the end. Still drawing the payroll. Still paying men who were dying."

Orsen looked at the name and felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing thing settling a centimeter out of true. *Salas.* Efraín Salas, who had put a map in his hands in a kerosene-smelling hotel room and asked for one thing only: bring my daughter home, tell her I kept it. Efraín Salas, who had marked a point on oilcloth labeled AGUA and burned the word into Orsen's memory like a brand. Efraín Salas, fifteen years old in 1902, the paymaster's boy, walking out of a dying town while his father stayed to pay the dying.

"He was the last one," Ingrid said. She was still crouched at the stone, and her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "My father never said any of this. Not once in my whole life. He talked about the dry side like it was scenery. He taught me to read the sky and find water and he never — " She stopped. Her jaw worked. "He carried this alone for fifty years. He carried it so far that when he finally set it down, all he could lift was me."

Tomás turned away and walked a little distance off and stood with his back to them, and Orsen let him go, because there are things a nineteen-year-old cannot watch a woman learn about her father without needing to be elsewhere.

* * *

By midday Orsen had done what he could do, which was his work. He measured. He paced the streets, shot bearings on the pipeline alignment, sketched the wellhead and the pump station bedplate into his notebook with dimensions and dates and the cold, damning precision of his trade. He found the schoolhouse foundation and the doctor's house and the company office, where a strongbox lay open and empty in the rubble, its lock punched cleanly — the paymaster's last act, perhaps, or the company's first act after. He found the cistern in the plaza, dry, its floor silted, and scratched into the plaster of its inner wall, low down where a child would have written it, a name: *M. OYARZÚN*, and beneath it, in a different hand, *se fue a Puerto Deseado, que Dios lo guarde* — went to Puerto Deseado, may God keep him. Someone had gotten out. Someone had walked or ridden forty leagues to the port where the water was going, and had lived close enough to the pipes to hear them running full.

That was the detail that broke something loose in him. Not the graves. The graves were grief, and grief belonged to other people. But the child's handwriting on the cistern wall — that was evidence, and evidence was his language, and in his own language he heard the sentence plainly:

*They knew.*

Not the board, perhaps. Not all of them. But the engineers knew. The division engineer who approved the pump station knew. The man who signed the requisition for forty-one workers' rations in 1901 and stopped signing them in 1902 knew. Somewhere in the company's files — in the very offices where Orsen had spent twelve years being useful, being loyal, being the man who mapped the closed lines and asked nothing — there was a folder with the Pozo Nuevo alignment in it, and a memo, and a signature, and the signature was not going to belong to some distant director. It was going to belong to someone in the chain of men who had trained him, promoted him, forgiven whatever it was he had fled into the miles to escape. The firm that gave him his miles had drunk this town. He had spent his career walking the arteries of a body that had done this, and he had never once felt a pulse fail.

He sat down on the church steps with his notebook closed on his knee and let the wind move over him, and for the first time since Puerto Deseado he understood that this expedition had stopped being Efraín Salas's promise and had become something he owed. Not to the dead — the dead were past owing. To the living. To the forty-one names that existed nowhere except on limestone in a cemetery no map admitted to. To the boy who had walked out and spent half a century buying the truth back piece by piece, tin box by tin box, cairn by cairn, because shame is heavier than water and he had carried both.

"You've figured it out," Ingrid said. She had come up the street without him hearing her, which frightened him a little; he had thought himself a man who noticed everything. She stood looking down at him with her father's eyes, and there was no anger in them now. Anger would have been easier. What was in them was the terrible patience of someone waiting for a man to say a true thing out loud.

"Yes," Orsen said.

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it said by someone from the company. By someone whose hands are clean."

"My hands aren't clean." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "I've worked for them twelve years. I've surveyed lines they closed and filed reports they shelved and I never once asked why a firm that loses money on every league of track keeps buying more of it. I read the ground everywhere I go except the one place I work." He looked out at the ruined street, at the bell in its shroud of dust, at the small stones in the cemetery. "They found the water. The survey that justified the whole coastal route said there was none, and the well proved the survey wrong, and instead of correcting the record they corrected the town. They capped the well, they ran the pipe to the port, and they waited. Eleven months. The desert did the rest. And then they struck the name off the maps, and everyone who could remember the name was either dead or paid or fifteen years old and too ashamed to speak."

Ingrid nodded slowly, once, as if he had confirmed a figure she had already computed.

"And my father watched them do it," she said. "At fifteen. From the last wagon out."

* * *

They made camp that night inside the shell of the company storehouse, out of the wind, and Orsen did not sleep. Near midnight he got up and walked out to the wellhead with a lantern, because there was one more thing he needed to know, and knowing it would change everything that came after.

The concrete cap had a fitting. Small, brass, corroded green, set flush near the rim — a sampling port, the kind used to draw test volumes from a sealed installation. He had walked past it twice without registering it, the way a man walks past his own guilt. Now he knelt, worked the fitting loose with his knife blade and a folded rag, and put his ear to the opening.

Wind, first. Then, underneath the wind, faint and steady and utterly impossible after fifty years, a sound he knew from a hundred pump stations on a hundred closed lines: the deep, patient note of water standing under pressure in a confined space. The aquifer had not forgotten. The lake below the caliche was still there, still pressing upward against the plug, still waiting behind a concrete lid poured by good engineers doing bad work.

Efraín's AGUA. Not a marker of something lost. A marker of something *hidden* — hidden so well that only a man who had watched them hide it could ever find it again.

Orsen sat back on his heels in the dark with the lantern guttering, and the last piece of the truth assembled itself in him with the quiet click of a chronometer reaching the hour. The company had not merely killed a town. It had buried a lake — enough water to make the whole dry side bloom, enough to answer every drought and every debt on the plateau — and it had buried it under the weight of its own lie, and the lie was the charter, and the charter was the law, and the law was guarded by men with rifles who were, at this moment, somewhere behind them on the trail.

He closed the fitting, snuffed the lantern, and stood a moment in the black wind listening to the water breathe under the stone.

Then, from the darkness out on the old southern road, thin and clear across the ruins, came the sound he had been listening for since the ridge yesterday without letting himself name it: the far-off complaint of a wagon axle, and under it, once, the metallic clink of a rifle bolt being worked.

Ballesteros had found the town too.
