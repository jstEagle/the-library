## 14. The Buried Lake

Tomás Ferreyra had learned, in his nineteen years and his one season of field work, that the plateau spoke a language of small things. A change in the color of the gravel. The way the coiron grass leaned — always east, always leaning, as if the wind had made a habit of it and the grass had never found reason to argue. A cairn of stones that was not a natural pile of stones, because nature did not stack; it scattered.

So when Orsen Nystrom stopped the column at midmorning on the fourth day out from Pozo Nuevo and pointed his chin at a shallow basin ahead, Tomás looked for the small thing first.

"There," Orsen said. "See the rim."

The basin was perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by a low lip of pale stone, and inside it the ground was different — smoother, darker, swept clean of the scrub that grew everywhere else in stubborn gray tufts. It looked like nothing so much as a dry lakebed, except that no lake had ever sat here, four hundred meters above the nearest river, on a meseta where rain fell three days a year and evaporated before it finished landing.

"That's caliche," Orsen said. He said it the way he said most things, flatly, as though reading from an instrument. "Hardpan. Water carries lime down through the soil for ten thousand years and leaves that crust behind." He swung down off his mule without waiting for questions. "Efraín's last marker should be at the center."

Ingrid Salas dismounted more slowly. She had been quiet since the cemetery — quieter even than grief usually made her, which was saying something, because Ingrid's silences were never empty. They were full to the brim. Tomás had begun to think of them as full glasses being carried carefully across a moving deck.

They walked the basin's floor together, the three of them and the two mules they'd brought down from the rim, leaving the rest with the animals picketed above. The caliche rang faintly under their boots. Not hollow exactly — but not dead either. Tomás knelt and put his palm flat against it and felt something he could not name, a coolness rising through six inches of stone, patient as anything underground.

"Here," Orsen said.

The marker was almost nothing: five stones arranged around a depression, and beneath them, when Orsen scraped away the gravel with the edge of his boot, a rusted iron rod driven into the hardpan at an angle, its top worn smooth as if someone had gripped it many times over many years. Beside it, half-buried, a shovel head gone to lace-thin rust and a coil of what had once been rope.

"He came back," Ingrid said. Her voice came out strange, compressed. "He came back more than once."

"More than twice," Orsen said. He crouched, working the iron rod loose with a rocking motion, and when it came free there was a hole beneath it — narrow, hand-dug, the walls slicked smooth with some kind of sealant that had dried black. "And every time, he filled it in again. Covered his own tracks. Fifty years of coming back to look at something he couldn't afford to leave open."

Tomás looked at the hole. It was barely wider than a man's shoulders. It went straight down into darkness, and the darkness breathed — a slow, cold exhalation, smelling of wet stone and something mineral, something old.

"It's a shaft," he said, and heard his own voice go thin. "Someone dug this."

"The company dug this," Orsen said. "1901. Efraín just kept the door."

* * *

They spent an hour clearing it. This was engineering, and Tomás threw himself into it gratefully, because engineering was a language he could speak when everything else had become too large. They widened the mouth with picks, careful, controlled strokes, Orsen calling the angles, Ingrid hauling broken caliche up in a canvas sheet, her sleeves rolled, her face set in the expression she wore when she refused to feel something yet. The caliche came away in plates, in shelves, and beneath it the shaft opened into older rock — volcanic tuff, soft enough to dig with knives, hard enough to have held its shape for half a century.

At three meters the air changed.

Tomás noticed it first as a sound — or rather the absence of one. The wind, which had been a constant presence since the railhead, a thin whistle in the wire grass and a pressure against the left ear, simply stopped. Below three meters the plateau's voice could not follow them. What replaced it was silence of a density he had never encountered, a silence with weight and temperature, and inside that silence, very far down, a sound like a single drop of water falling into a room.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

Orsen heard it. Tomás watched the older man go still, watch him close his eyes for a moment the way a man does when an instrument confirms what he has feared or hoped. Then Orsen took the lantern from its hook on his saddle, lit it, checked the flame, and lowered it into the shaft on the end of the rope.

The light sank. Ten meters. Fifteen. The walls of the shaft slid past, marked with old chisel scars, and then — Tomás lying flat on his stomach at the rim, peering down past the rope — the light stopped sinking and began to swing, gently, and its reflection came back up to meet it.

Water. The lantern hung above water, and the water gave the light back.

Nobody spoke. Ingrid had both hands pressed over her mouth. Above them on the rim the mules shifted and blew, sensing nothing, knowing nothing, and Tomás thought absurdly that the animals were the only honest creatures present, because they alone were not afraid of what they felt.

"We'll need the tackle," Orsen said at last, and his voice was rougher than Tomás had ever heard it. "Rig the block on the tripod. And someone stay topside with the animals."

"I'm going down," Ingrid said. It was not a request.

* * *

The descent took twenty minutes and rearranged something in Tomás that he suspected would never be arranged back.

He went second, after Ingrid, feet braced against the chiseled walls, the rope humming faintly with her weight above him and Orsen's instructions drifting down like weather. At twelve meters the shaft opened sideways — a horizontal gallery, cut by men with picks and blasting powder, tall enough to walk upright. The lanterns found the tool marks immediately: overlapping crescents in the tuff, thousands of them, the signature of hands. Men had been here. Men had cut this corridor into the dark heart of the plateau, and then, apparently, the world had agreed to forget it.

"Mind your footing," Orsen called down. "The floor may be—"

But the floor was not slippery. The floor was dry, packed pale clay, and it sloped gently downward, and forty paces along the gallery it ended at a shelf of rock a meter above the waterline, and beyond the shelf the water began.

Tomás stood at the edge of the shelf and understood that he had no adequate words, and that this failure was itself a kind of evidence.

The cavern opened around them like the interior of a cathedral that geology had built slowly and without an architect. The lantern light reached perhaps thirty meters and gave out, and beyond that reach the darkness continued with the particular quality of darkness that contains space — you could feel the volume of it, the way you feel the size of a room by the delay of your own echo. The water lay absolutely still. Not a ripple. Not a breath of current. It was black where the light didn't touch it and deep green-black where it did, and it reflected the cavern roof so perfectly that for a moment Tomás's eyes insisted there were two caverns, one above and one below, joined at the waterline like a page folded shut.

"How big," Ingrid whispered. Her voice crossed the water and came back changed, rounded, enormous.

"Big," Orsen said. He had come down last and now stood at Tomás's shoulder, and he was doing what Tomás had seen him do a hundred times on the decommissioned lines: measuring. His eyes moved along the walls, timing distances, estimating spans. "The gallery runs at least two hundred meters before the light quits. The echo says the main chamber is wider than that. Call it a hectare of surface, conservatively. Probably more." He paused. "Depth unknown. Could be fifty meters. Could be two hundred. The aquifer feeding it underlies the whole southern meseta — I've read the old geological bulletins. They all say the same thing. *No significant groundwater reserves.*"

"They wrote that," Ingrid said, "while standing on top of this."

"They wrote that," Orsen said, "knowing about this."

Tomás crouched at the shelf's edge and did the thing he knew how to do. He dipped his fingers in the water and touched them to his tongue.

It was cold enough to ache. It tasted of stone, faintly, and of nothing else — none of the bitter alkali of the seeps, none of the salt that poisoned half the wells on the dry side. It was sweet. It was the sweetest water he had ever tasted, and he had grown up on a cattle station where the well water was so mineral-heavy the kettle crusted white in a month.

"It's fresh," he said. "Señor Nystrom, it's completely fresh. Cold, too — that means depth, that means volume—" He stopped, because the arithmetic was doing something to him. A hectare. Even at ten meters' average depth that was a hundred thousand cubic meters, and it would not be ten meters, it could not be ten meters, not fed by an aquifer that spanned a province. Enough for a town. Enough for forty towns. Enough for the railway's coal port and every estancia between here and the coast, forever, in a country that killed people for lack of a bucket of it.

Enough for a province. Exactly where the maps said there was nothing.

He said some of this aloud, the numbers tumbling out of him the way numbers did when he was excited, and Orsen listened with his lantern raised, and Ingrid stood a little apart with her arms wrapped around herself, looking at the water as if it were a face she recognized.

Then Tomás started to cry.

* * *

It embarrassed him deeply and he could do nothing whatsoever about it.

It was not sobbing. It was worse than sobbing — a silent, unstoppable leaking, tears running down his face while he stood at the edge of the buried lake holding his hat in both hands like a boy at a funeral, and the worst part was that he did not know why. He searched himself for the reason the way he'd search a misprinted table, line by line, and found nothing wrong anywhere and the tears coming anyway.

Ingrid looked over. He braced for her mockery — she was capable of it, he'd learned, especially with him, especially when he was earnest, which was always. But she only looked at him for a moment with an expression he couldn't read, and then she turned back to the water, and she said, quietly:

"Don't fight it. My father used to say the desert makes you pay for beauty in advance. Maybe this is the payment."

That did not help. That made it worse, or better, he couldn't tell.

Orsen said nothing at all. He had moved off along the shelf with his lantern, following the wall, and Tomás heard him stop and call softly: "There's equipment here. Bring a light."

Twenty paces along, tucked into a recess in the rock, they found what the men of 1901 had left behind. A winch frame, its timber gone silver and soft. A hand pump, seized solid with corrosion, bolted to a stone plinth. Stacked crates stenciled with faded lettering — *SOUTHERN PATAGONIA RAILWAY, PUERTO DESEADO* — and inside the one Orsen pried open, wrapped in tar paper that still held: drill steel, a brass gauge, and a ledger bound in oilcloth, its pages furred with damp but legible.

Orsen turned the pages with the care of a man handling eggs. The lantern light trembled slightly, and Tomás realized the older man's hands were not entirely steady.

"Well logs," Orsen said. "Daily records. Depth readings, strata notes, flow tests." He read in silence for a long moment. "They drilled the main shaft to sixty-two meters. Flow test in November 1901: sustained yield sufficient for a town of two thousand, plus locomotive resupply." He looked up, and in the lantern light his face was terrible and calm. "Two thousand people. They proved it could support two thousand people, and then they walked away and let the town die at forty-one graves."

"Why?" Tomás asked, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Why would they—"

"Because admitting the survey was right meant admitting the survey they'd already published was wrong," Ingrid said. She had come to stand beside them, and her voice had the flat clarity it got when the anger underneath froze over. "The charter. The land grants. The whole company is built on the story that this plateau is worthless, that the port route is the only route worth anything. If there's a lake under the meseta, the whole map of Patagonia changes, and everyone who signed the old map becomes a liar or a criminal." She looked at the water. "So they buried it. Twice. Once in rock, once in paper."

Tomás looked at the pump. Somebody had installed that pump. Somebody had believed, for a while, in the morning of that year 1901, that this water would rise through it and green a town called Pozo Nuevo, and children would drink it, and the forty-one graves would never be needed. And then somewhere in an office in Buenos Aires or Puerto Deseado, men with good handwriting had done arithmetic of a different kind, and the pump had been abandoned mid-hope, and the shaft had been sealed, and the town had been struck from the maps so thoroughly that even the wind seemed to have agreed to forget it.

His tears had stopped. What remained was heavier than tears.

"My mother's town drinks from a well that goes dry every February," he said. "Every February the cattle die at the edges of the district, and every year the company sends a man to say the aquifer is exhausted, it's the drought, it's God." He heard his own voice shake. "It wasn't God. It was a decision. It was a *minute* in a meeting."

"Careful," Orsen said quietly.

"Careful?"

"You're nineteen and you're furious and that's correct. But fury spends fast out here. We're four people and six mules and whatever we can carry, and what we know is worth killing for — we've known that since Ballesteros sat down at our fire. So be furious, Tomás. But carry it like water, not like fire. Water you can ration. Fire burns the carrier first."

It was the longest speech Tomás had ever heard him make that wasn't about gradients. He nodded, and found that he meant it, and found also that the nod cost him something, because part of him had wanted to stand in the beautiful cold dark and shout until the cavern answered.

Ingrid crouched at the waterline and let her fingers trail the surface, making the only ripples the lake had maybe ever known, rings of lantern light expanding and dying against the black.

"Papá stood here," she said. "Fifty years. He stood here and he watched this, and he went home and he never told me, and he died in a hotel room smelling of kerosene with a map folded in his coat." Her voice caught and steadied. "He kept it safe. That was his way of loving things. Keeping them safe instead of keeping them close."

Above them, faint through the shaft, came the sound of the wind resuming — the plateau's thin whistle, indifferent, eternal. Tomás thought: it blows over all of this every day and doesn't know. The whole country walks around on top of its own drowned truth, and the wind doesn't know, and the maps don't know, and only four people alive do.

Orsen closed the well log and wrapped it in the tar paper and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where a man carries the things he has decided to defend.

"We document everything," he said. "Depths, dimensions, photographs if Tomás's plates will take the light. Then we seal this shaft behind us the way Efraín did, and we decide, carefully, with clear heads, what we do next." He raised the lantern, and the light swung out over the still water, and for a moment all three of them were doubled in it, standing on the underside of the world. "Because I promise you, gentlemen and lady — the moment we surface, we are no longer surveyors. We are witnesses. And witnesses on the dry side of Patagonia have a life expectancy measured in days unless they choose their ground very, very well."

They worked another hour in the cold cathedral, measuring the immeasurable, and it was Tomás who climbed last, and Tomás who paused at the lip of the shaft with the daylight pouring down past him, and looked back one more time at the black mirror below — the lake that had waited fifty years in the dark, patient as the dead, holding enough life to forgive a province.

When he reached the surface, squinting, the wind hit his face and the mules blew and stepped, ordinary as morning.

Ingrid was already at the rim glass, sweeping the eastern horizon where the land ran flat and featureless to the edge of sight.

She lowered the glass slowly. She did not hand it over.

"Smoke," she said. "Due east. Two columns. Someone's camped on our back trail — and they've lit fires they don't mean to hide."
