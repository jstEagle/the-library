## 15. Riders on the Rim

Orsen saw the smoke before he saw the men.

It rose from behind a ridge of black basalt perhaps two kilometers east of the cavern mouth — a thin gray thread standing straight up in the windless evening air, the way smoke only stands when it is being fed deliberately and watched carefully. A careless fire smokes. A signal fire is tended. He had spent twelve years reading the country from the back of a mule, and he had never once been wrong about tended smoke.

He was at the rim above the lake's entrance, gathering brush for the concealment work Tomás had begun that afternoon, when he saw it. He did not move for a long moment. He simply stood with an armload of thorny coiron grass against his chest and let the fact of it settle into him the way cold water settles into boots.

Ballesteros had found them.

Not caught up to them — found them. There was a difference, and the difference mattered. A man who catches you has followed your trail and can be shaken by breaking it. A man who finds you has guessed where you were going, which means he knows what you know, which means every hour Orsen had spent believing himself ahead had been an hour of being exactly where his enemy expected.

He set down the brush without letting any of it scatter and walked back along the rim, keeping low, the way he'd learned to move along embankments as a boy watching trains he wasn't supposed to watch.

* * *

The cavern mouth opened onto the plateau through a fold in the caliche like the seam of a coat turned inside out. From twenty paces away it read as nothing — a shadow among shadows, a wrinkle in the pale ground. From fifty it disappeared entirely. That was their luck, and Orsen intended to spend all of it before nightfall.

Tomás was at the entrance with the last of the brush, stacking it into the gap with a patience that would have pleased Orsen on any other day. Ingrid sat nearby on a flat stone, Efraín's second letter open across her knees even though the light was nearly gone. She had been reading it in pieces for three days, the way one eats something precious slowly to make it last. She looked up when Orsen's shadow fell across her page.

"You've stopped walking like a man," she said. "You're walking like a cat. What did you see?"

"Smoke," Orsen said. "East ridge. Two kilometers, maybe less."

Ingrid closed the letter. She didn't ask whose smoke. She had understood the shape of their situation faster than Tomás had, faster perhaps than Orsen himself; she had grown up on stories of the plateau, and stories of the plateau were all, in the end, stories about men who wanted things buried to stay buried.

"How many?" she asked.

"I counted one thread of smoke. Could be four men. Could be ten." He crouched, took a stick, and drew in the dust: the rim, the cavern mouth, the basalt ridge, the shallow arroyo that ran between. "They'll have glass. If they have glass, they've already seen our fire."

Tomás straightened from his brushwork. "Our fire's small—"

"It's not the size. It's that there is one." Orsen looked at the boy — no, not a boy anymore, he corrected himself, not after Pozo Nuevo, not after the lake; whatever Tomás Ferreyra was now, he had been manufactured in the last six days — and saw the question forming behind his eyes before it reached his mouth. "If they can see our fire, they know we're here. The only questions left are whether they know about the water, and whether they're coming tonight or waiting for morning."

"And if they're waiting for morning?" Ingrid said.

"Then they're confident. And confidence means numbers."

* * *

They doused the fire with sand, working by feel, and then doused it again because the first sand had only banked the coals and a dull red pulse still showed through. Orsen remembered a stationmaster in his early years on the lines who used to say that a fire put out carelessly was a message sent carelessly, and he thought of that man now with an odd, unwelcome tenderness, because the man had been company through and through, decent within the limits of the company's decency, and those limits had apparently always included forty-one graves.

When the pit was cold enough to touch, they scattered the ashes wide and dragged brush over the bare circle.

Then Orsen made his dispositions, and making them he felt something shift in his chest that he recognized from years ago and had hoped never to feel again: fear turning over and becoming arithmetic. It had happened to him once before, long ago, on a night he never named, on a bridge he never named, and the feeling was horrible and clarifying at once, the way a fever is clarifying. Fear alone makes a man freeze. Fear converted to numbers makes him useful.

"Here's how it stands," he said quietly, the three of them crouched together in the dark like conspirators, which was what they were. "Fourteen animals, we're down to nine. Two hundred liters in the barrels, less what we drank today. One rifle, my revolver, Tomás's shotgun with eleven shells. Against a party that chose its own ground and got there first."

"We could go out the back," Tomás said. "Through the cavern. You said yourself the far passage might open—"

"Might. Into country I've never seen, in the dark, with mules that won't walk into a cave mouth without fighting us, and noise carries underground worse than it does up here." Orsen shook his head. "No. We stay. But we make this place look like nothing. Like nobody was ever here. Brush over the entrance, deep. Mules moved into the hollow below the rim, downwind, hobbled tight and muzzled so they don't call to each other. No light after full dark. No talking above a whisper. We sit quiet until I say otherwise."

"For how long?" Ingrid asked.

"Until they leave or until they come. Whichever comes first."

She was silent a moment. In the last blue light he could see her face, and it was her father's face, he realized — the same set jaw, the same way of holding anger in the mouth rather than the eyes. Fifty years Efraín Salas had carried this plateau inside him, and here it was again, expressed in his daughter's silence.

"They're going to come," she said. "Aren't they. They're going to come and take it and fill it in or guard it or do whatever companies do with truths, and everyone who saw it will have an accident."

"That's one outcome," Orsen said.

"What are the others?"

He didn't answer, because there weren't others worth saying aloud, and she knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a fourth person at the council.

* * *

Full dark came down over the meseta the way it always did — absolutely, without twilight's lingering courtesy, the wind rising as the sun went and the temperature falling through it. Orsen took the first watch at the rim, lying flat behind a shelf of rock with the rifle beside him and his hat off so the brim wouldn't catch starlight. Below him the land was a black sea. Above it the stars came out in their terrible Patagonian multitudes, more stars than sky some nights, and he had loved that sky for twelve years and could not love it tonight.

At some point Ingrid came and lay down beside him without asking. She brought the second pair of eyes, and she brought something else too, though she didn't say what it was for a while.

"I want to go down there," she said finally. "Tonight. While they're sitting around their fire feeling proud of themselves. Walk right into their camp."

"No."

"I'd carry a white rag. Unarmed. I'd tell them who I am — Efraín Salas's daughter. Tell them I know what happened at Pozo Nuevo, that the whole story will reach Buenos Aires whether anything happens to us or not. Make them hesitate. Men hesitate when a thing stops being abstract."

"Ingrid."

"Forty-one graves, Orsen. They're sitting on my father's whole life down there, cooking supper over it, and you want me to lie in the dirt and hide from them."

"Yes," Orsen said. "I do."

She turned her head and looked at him, and he felt the look like a hand pressed flat against his chest.

"Tell me why. Not the tactical reason. The real one."

He was quiet long enough that a nighthawk called somewhere out over the dark, a strange hollow sound, like the plateau breathing.

"The real reason," he said, "is that confrontation is a luxury of people who can afford the consequences. Ballesteros can afford them. He has a board behind him, lawyers behind the board, and a charter behind the lawyers, and if a telegraph operator dies on the meseta, there will be an inquiry that concludes exposure. If you walk into that camp, you give him a problem, and men like him solve problems. You don't weaken him. You simplify him."

"And hiding?"

"Hiding keeps us complicated. Complicated is the only weapon we have that weighs nothing and costs nothing." He nodded toward the concealed cavern mouth, invisible now even to him, though he knew its position to the pace. "That lake is worth more than every rail line south of the Colorado. Do you understand? Not sentimentally. In money. In power. Whoever holds it holds the dry side. Ballesteros knows it. That's why he's here and not in Puerto Deseado signing papers. And a man who has ridden five days to hold a thing does not negotiate with the people already holding it. He removes them."

"He removed forty-one people once already," Ingrid said softly. "By doing nothing. Just closing a valve."

"Just closing a valve," Orsen agreed. "The cheapest massacre ever committed. No orders anyone can find. No signature. Just an engineering decision, filed and approved." He heard his own voice go flat and stopped it. "Which is why we don't give them a body to file. We stay alive, we stay hidden, and we get these photographs and that ledger to a magistrate if it takes the rest of the year."

Ingrid lay back against the rock. For a long time neither spoke. Then she said, "My father hid for fifty years. Look what it bought him."

"It bought him the truth," Orsen said. "Alone, but intact. Hiding isn't surrender, Ingrid. Sometimes it's just refusing to spend yourself on the enemy's terms."

She didn't answer, but she didn't argue either, and from Ingrid Salas that was a treaty.

* * *

Past midnight, Tomás crawled up to relieve the watch, moving with a competence that still surprised Orsen — the boy had learned the country's grammar quickly, the low movement, the weight placed on the outer edge of the foot. Orsen briefed him in whispers and stayed a while anyway, unable quite to let go of the rim, and it was during that overlap of watches that they saw the lantern.

It appeared on the basalt ridge like a slow orange planet, bobbing, stopping, bobbing again. Then a second light joined it. The lights worked their way along the ridgeline with the unhurried methodicalness of men sweeping a field, and Orsen understood: they were glassing the ground between the ridge and the cavern country, looking for sign. Looking, specifically, for the trail the expedition had cut coming in from the west — a trail that ended, if Orsen had done his work, nowhere at all.

"Are they going to come down?" Tomás breathed.

"Not tonight. Tonight they're confirming. Tomorrow they'll ride the ground." Orsen watched the lanterns pause at the ridge's northern end, hang there a long time — pointed, he judged, almost directly at the hollow where the mules were hidden — and then withdraw. His heart was going hard and evenly, a pump doing its work, and he noticed distantly that his hands were perfectly steady, and wondered when he had become a man whose hands steadied under exactly this kind of pressure, and what that said about the twelve years he had chosen instead of another kind of life.

"They know we're close," Tomás whispered.

"They knew before we did." Orsen began backing away from the rim. "Get Ingrid. Wake her gently. We're going to move the mules deeper into the hollow, now, tonight, while their lanterns are on the ridge and their eyes are full of light."

* * *

Moving nine hobbled mules in the dark, muzzled and murmured-to, was an hour's work that felt like a night's. Twice an animal tried to bray and got a palm over the nose and a shoulder leaned into its neck until the sound died unborn. The stars wheeled. The cold came up out of the ground through boot leather. And when it was done, the four of them — Orsen, Ingrid, Tomás, and the secret beneath the brush — settled into the hollow with their backs to stone and waited for the sky to lighten, which in Patagonia in that season it did late and grudgingly.

Dawn showed them the ridge empty of lanterns but not of men. Small figures moved along it, and glints of glass flashed where the sun struck lenses. And then, as the light strengthened, Orsen saw what he had been afraid of since the first thread of smoke: a rider detaching from the ridge, descending at a walk into the flat, riding a line that would pass within half a kilometer of the cavern mouth. A scout. Alone, unarmed-looking, unhurried.

Behind the brush, Ingrid's hand found Orsen's sleeve.

"Let me guess," she whispered. "We stay complicated."

"We stay complicated," Orsen said, and put his eye to a gap in the branches, and watched the rider come on — and saw, as the man passed close enough for detail, that he raised one hand in a lazy salute toward the hidden hollow, as if he knew precisely where they lay, and rode on.
