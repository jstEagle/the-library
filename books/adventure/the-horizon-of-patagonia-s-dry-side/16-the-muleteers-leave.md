## 16. The Muleteers Leave

Ingrid woke to the sound of money being counted.

Not coins — the muleteers had no use for coin out here, where a peso bought less than a bullet — but paper, the soft rasp of banknotes drawn from an oilskin wallet and folded into smaller and smaller squares. She lay in her bedroll beneath the wagon canvas and listened to Domingo Aguirre count in a low voice, twice through, the way men do when they do not trust their own arithmetic or their own courage.

The fire had burned to a red eye. Beyond it, the plateau went on being the plateau: black scrub, black stone, a horizon that had not moved in six days no matter how far they walked toward it. Somewhere out there, Orsen had said, riders were following them. She had seen them herself at dusk yesterday, three points of dust on the southern rim, patient as vultures.

She sat up. The canvas smelled of mule sweat and creosote from the waterproofing, and under it all the faint mineral smell of the water barrels, which she had begun to think of as the smell of life itself.

Domingo looked up when she stirred. He was a small man, wiry, with a face like a dried riverbed — all cracks and channels, everything that had once flowed through it long gone. His partner, Rafael, slept sitting up against a wheel with his hat over his eyes, which was how he did everything: sleeping, arguing, praying, if he prayed at all.

"You're leaving," Ingrid said. It was not a question. She had known it since supper, since Rafael had asked for his pay two days early and then hadn't eaten.

Domingo finished folding the last note into his shirt, buttoned the shirt, and only then met her eyes. "Señorita."

"How much?"

"Enough." He said it carefully. "Enough to start again somewhere the wind doesn't know my name."

* * *

They woke the others properly at first light. Orsen was already awake — Ingrid suspected he was always already awake, that sleep for him was a formality observed briefly and without conviction — and Tomás came stumbling out of his blankets with his hair flattened on one side and his instruments cradled against his chest like a sleeping child.

Orsen heard Domingo out without interrupting. That was the thing about him, Ingrid was learning: he let silence do work that other men did with words. He stood with his hands behind his back, weight even on both boots, and listened to the whole speech — the reward Ballesteros's men had shouted across the arroyo at dawn, fifty pesos gold for the oilcloth map, more for the man who carried it; the arithmetic of fourteen animals and four unarmed men against rifles and a wagon; the wife in Gaiman and the debt on the team.

"We took your money knowing it was strange work," Domingo finished. "We didn't know it was dying work. That's the difference, señor. I'll carry that difference to my grave, but I'll carry it living."

"You'll carry it however you like," Orsen said. "The pay is yours. Take eight mules."

"Six." Rafael pulled his hat off his eyes and spoke for the first time, voice gravelly with sleep. "You'll need what you have. We'll take six and walk them easy. Eight would leave you lame before the week's out."

It was, Ingrid thought, the strangest kind of kindness: men deserting you and doing the sums of your survival better than you had done them yourself.

Tomás had gone very still by the cold fire. He was nineteen and he wore every feeling on his face the way the plateau wore its stones — nothing hidden, everything weathered. "You could stay," he said, and hated himself for saying it; Ingrid watched him hear his own voice and flinch. "I mean — the reward. If they catch us anyway—"

"They won't catch us because of you staying," Domingo said gently. "And if they catch us because we stayed, we'll be two more graves and no fewer rifles against you. No, muchacho. The best thing these hands can do for you now is hold reins going the other way."

He spat, considered, and spat again, which among the muleteers of the dry side was a form of oath-taking.

"One thing more." Domingo lowered his voice, though there was nothing within ten miles to overhear but wind and stone. "The man who shouted the offer — I knew his father. Worked the Deseado line with him. These company men, they hire local boys to hunt local people, because a local boy knows where the water is and where the bodies go." He tapped his own chest. "Fifty pesos is a year of my life. But some of those boys out there, the reward isn't money to them. It's permission. Understand me? Money you can refuse. Permission is harder."

Ingrid felt the words go into her like a splinter and sit there.

"Thank you," she said, because Orsen said nothing, and someone had to.

* * *

They loaded six mules in the grey half-light, and it went quickly because there was not much left to load. Half the barrels. Half the fodder. The canvas lean-to, which Rafael folded with the neatness of a man packing up a decade rather than a week. When it was done the expedition stood in the wind and looked at itself: Orsen Nystrom, tall and grey and unreadable; Tomás Ferreyra, nineteen, clutching his theodolite; Ingrid Salas, who had come on this journey to argue with a dead man and had not yet managed to finish the argument; and six mules, and a secret worth killing for, and between them and Comodoro Rivadavia a desert that did not care what any of them carried.

Rafael shook hands all around. His grip was dry and strong and brief, like everything about him. With Tomás he lingered half a second longer. "Keep your numbers honest, boy. Numbers are the only witnesses that can't be bought."

"Or the first ones sold," Tomás said, trying for bitter and landing somewhere near frightened.

"Then keep them honest anyway." Rafael swung up onto the lead mule. "It's the keeping that counts."

Domingo paused beside Ingrid. Up close she could see the riverbed cracks of his face were wet at the corners, though whether from wind or something else she could not say and he would never permit anyone to say.

"Your father," he said quietly. "The old man Salas. I ran freight past his claim once, years back. He stood at the track and watched the train go by like a man watching a woman leave." He shook his head. "I don't know what he found out there. But I know this: a man who spends fifty years walking back toward a thing isn't walking toward gold."

Then he turned his mule and followed Rafael out onto the plateau, and Ingrid watched them shrink — two dots, then one dot, then a shimmer where heat made the ground lie about itself — until the land closed over them the way water closes over a stone.

* * *

They did not speak for a long time after.

Orsen repacked the remaining loads himself, redistributing weight with the economy of a man who had spent twelve years learning exactly what a mule could carry and exactly what a man could not. Ingrid helped him without being asked, hauling barrels, cinching straps, and the work was good because work was the only language grief and fear could both speak without lying.

It was Tomás who broke first. He was crouched over his instrument case, checking the chronometer against the sun, and his hands were not steady.

"Four people," he said. "Six mules. And they know our trail, señor. They've seen our camps. Even if they don't sell us, they'll sell the shape of us — how many, how slow, which way. Ballesteros can read a camp like you read a survey."

"He can read a camp," Orsen agreed, tightening a strap. "He can't read a map he doesn't have, and he can't read intent. Those are ours."

"And the reward?" Ingrid kept her voice level. "Fifty pesos gold. Domingo says some of those boys out there—" She stopped. The splinter was still in her. "Permission, he said. Harder to refuse than money."

Orsen straightened and looked south, along the line of their own backtrail, where the land rose in long dull swells toward the rim. His face did what it always did, which was nothing, but his eyes did something else — a narrowing, a settling, the look of a man taking a bearing on something farther away than the horizon.

"Then we stop being readable," he said. "No fires after tonight. Cold food. We break camp before light and make camp after dark, and we stop following our own line home. From here we travel like the guanaco travel — no pattern, no promise."

"That's desert country," Tomás said. "Off the line, there's no water for—"

"I know the arithmetic, Tomás."

"Then say it." The boy's voice cracked and he pushed through the crack. "Say the numbers out loud. Four people, six mules, X liters a day, Y days to the nearest seep. If we're going to die out here, I'd rather die of honest sums than of secrets."

The wind moved through the scrub with its long dry whisper, the sound the plateau made instead of speech.

Ingrid looked at Orsen and saw him weigh the boy's anger, and saw — this was new, this was the thing she would remember later — saw him decide to answer it honestly.

"Two liters a day per person," Orsen said. "Less for the mules. Twenty days if we find the seep Efraín marked. Fifteen if we don't and live on what the barrels hold. After that—" He spread his hands, empty. "After that the desert does the accounting."

Silence. Tomás wrote the figures in his notebook with a pencil worn to a stub, pressing hard, as if the numbers might otherwise blow away.

Ingrid turned her face into the wind so neither of them could see what it did to her expression. Her father had spent fifty years buying back a truth piece by piece, alone, counting pesos in rooms that smelled of kerosene, and he had never once asked another soul to share the weight of it. Now she was carrying his secret through the country that had killed forty-one people to bury it, with a boy who did sums to keep from being afraid and a man who measured miles to keep from remembering, and six mules, and twenty days of water, and somewhere behind them a man named Ballesteros offering permission.

*We turn south at noon,* she thought. *We become unreadable. We live.*

She picked up the last barrel herself — heavier now, everything heavier — and carried it to the mule, and said, in a voice that surprised her with its steadiness:

"Well then. Let's not waste daylight being few."

Orsen almost smiled. Almost. On his face it was a seismic event, registered only at the corners.

But as she cinched the last strap, Ingrid looked back once, south along their own dead trail, and saw it: not dust, not riders, nothing so definite. Only a shimmer on the far swell of the plateau that did not move with the wind, and did not move against it either. Something standing still, watching, the way her father must once have stood at the track watching trains leave.

Waiting to see which way they'd run.
