## 17. Efraín's Second Letter

The lead tube had been waiting for them the way certain things wait — not hidden, exactly, but set aside with a patience that outlasted its maker. Tomás found it while they were filling the water skins at the lake's edge, his hands in the shallows where the cavern floor shelved down into darkness. He struck it with his knuckles before he understood what it was, and came up out of the water holding it aloft like something rescued from a drowning.

"Señor Nystrom," he called, and then, remembering himself, "Orsen. There's metal here. Under the stones."

They gathered around him on the flat shelf of rock where Efraín's last cairn stood guard over the water. The tube was the kind surveyors used for carrying field notes through river crossings — lead, threaded at both ends, each cap sealed with wax that had gone amber and brittle with age. Someone had wound wire around the middle of it, twice, and twisted the ends into a crude loop, as if the thing had once hung from a nail or a neck.

Orsen turned it over twice in his hands. His face did the thing it always did when he was measuring something — eyes narrowing slightly, jaw setting, the whole man becoming an instrument.

"It's heavy," Ingrid said, though she could see that it was.

"Lead doesn't rot." He picked at one wax seal with his thumbnail. It flaked away in curls, releasing a smell of old oil and older air. "Fifty years, maybe more, and it kept whatever's inside dry."

Inside was oilcloth — the same heavy, waxy fabric as the map her father had pressed into Orsen's hands in that kerosene-smelling room in Puerto Deseado. But when Orsen unrolled it against a stone, flattening the corners with two rocks the way a man spreads a chart on a plotting table, Ingrid saw immediately that this was no map.

It was writing. Columns of it, dense and small, the letters leaning hard to the right the way handwriting leans when the hand is old but stubborn. Pages of it, sewn edge to edge with waxed thread into a single long strip. At the top, in letters larger than the rest, pressed so deep into the cloth that the stylus had nearly torn through:

*Para mi hija, si el agua la lleva hasta aquí. Y si no, para quien sea honesto.*

*For my daughter, if the water brings her here. And if not, for whoever is honest.*

Nobody spoke for a moment. The lake made its small sounds against the rock — that soft, ceaseless lapping that had seemed comforting yesterday and now seemed like breathing, like the place itself was alive and patient. Somewhere above them, filtered through however many meters of caliche and stone, daylight must have been falling on the plateau, but down here the light came only from their lamp, and it made the oilcloth glow the color of old honey.

"You read it," Ingrid said. Her voice came out strange. She cleared her throat. "No. Wait."

She held out her hand for it.

Because she had spent three days now watching these men read her father — Orsen interpreting the cairns, Orsen decoding the route, Orsen explaining what Efraín Salas had meant by this marker or that bearing — and something in her had begun to rebel against it. He was *her* father. Whatever he had left in this world that could still speak, it should pass through her first.

But when the strip of oilcloth was in her hands and the lamp was brought close, she found she couldn't begin. Not because the writing was difficult — it wasn't; her father's hand was as familiar to her as her own name, though she hadn't seen it in eleven years. She couldn't begin because beginning would make it real, and there was a part of her that wanted to stand in this impossible blue-lit cavern under a dead town's plateau and believe, for one more minute, that she still didn't know what her father had done with fifty years of his life.

Tomás drifted toward the mules, making a business of checking their hobbles. Orsen stayed, but he turned his back — deliberately, she saw, giving her the same privacy he'd given her father's room.

She read.

*My daughter Ingrid.*

*You will be angry. I have counted on your anger the way other men count on rain. If you are reading this, you found the tube, which means you found the lake, which means Orsen Nystrom kept his promise to me, which means at least one promise in my life was kept entire. That is more than I expected of myself when I started.*

*I was fifteen years old in Pozo Nuevo. You will not find the name anywhere now. They took it off the maps the year after I left, the way you'd wipe a stain off a table. My father was the paymaster. Forty-one men dug the well that fed our town, and my father paid their wages every Saturday from a leather bag that smelled of coins and sweat, and I carried water for the crew because a boy who carries water hears everything.*

*Here is what the boy heard.*

*The well was good. Understand this first, because everything after depends on it. The well was GOOD. Two hundred feet into the best aquifer between the coast and the cordillera. The engineer who sank it wept when he saw the flow — I saw him weep, a big Chilean named Fuentes who wore his hat in the rain because he said a hat should work for a living. The company's own surveys said there was no water on the meseta. Fuentes proved the surveys wrong. For six months we were going to be rich, all of us, the whole plateau. A rail spur was promised. A grain elevator. My father bought my mother a stove with a proper oven.*

*Then the company found coal at the coast, and coal needs a port, and the port needed water, and there was only one water within two hundred kilometers, and it was ours.*

*They didn't send soldiers. I want you to understand that, because you will want someone to hate cleanly and there is no one clean to hate. They sent engineers. Good ones. Quiet ones. They capped our well with iron and concrete in four days and told us the aquifer had shifted, that the desert had taken it back, acts of God, nothing to be done. Then they bought our land for almost nothing, because land without water is worth almost nothing, and moved the spur forty miles south to the port. My father signed last. He signed because if he didn't, the men who worked for him would get nothing at all, and he stood in our kitchen afterward and washed his hands until my mother made him stop, and he never told me why.*

*Forty-one graves behind the church. Dated 1902. Not all at once — the desert is patient, it takes a family every few months, the old ones first and then the babies, always the babies. My mother went third. She never knew what the well-capping crew looked like. I did. I carried their water too.*

*You asked me once, years ago, why we left Comodoro. I told you work. Here is the truth: I left because I met one of those engineers in a bar in 1911, drunk, weeping about a town he couldn't remember the name of, and I realized I had spent nine years pretending I didn't remember it either. Shame is heavier than water, Ingrid. I have carried both across every dry mile of this province, and the shame always rode the heavier animal.*

*So I went back. Piece by piece. A ledger here — I paid a widow in Gaiman twenty pesos for her dead husband's payroll book. A survey sheet there — bribed a clerk, traded a horse, sat three winters outside a records office until a man died and his papers scattered. Fifty years, buying back the truth gram by gram, because no one would sell it to me by the kilo. The map Nystrom carries is the last piece. The lake itself is the last piece. Everything else I have already given away, hidden, buried, seeded along that route like a man planting a field he knows he won't harvest.*

*I never told you. I know how that reads. You will say: Papa, I would have helped you. You will say: Papa, I deserved to know whose blood runs in me. Both true. But I watched what knowing did to my father — how he went quiet as a closed door for the last thirty years of his life — and I swore no child of mine would inherit a weight before she chose to lift it. That was cowardice dressed as kindness. I am old enough now to call it what it is.*

*If you are angry, stay angry. Anger keeps a person walking. Just walk toward the truth and not away from it, and when you have delivered it — to a court, a newspaper, a magistrate, whoever will hold still long enough to receive it — then put it down. Put it down, hija. It was never yours to carry. I only ever wanted you to see it once, so you would know I was not the man you thought, and so you would know that the man I actually was spent half a century trying to become the one you thought.*

*The water is real. Drink it. Live. Forget me slowly if you must, but forget me facing forward.*

*Your father,
Efraín Salas
who was a boy at Pozo Nuevo and owes forty-one debts*

Ingrid read it twice. The second time she read it aloud, quietly, because some part of her refused to let the words exist only inside her own head — as if speaking them made them evidence, made them real in a way that ink alone could not. By the end her voice had worn down to almost nothing, and the lake kept lapping its patient rhythm beneath the words like a slow heartbeat under a doctor's stethoscope.

When she finished, the silence had weight. Orsen had turned back around at some point; she didn't know how long he'd been listening. Tomás stood at the edge of the lamplight with a water skin forgotten in his hands, dripping onto the stone.

"He wrote that for me." She heard her own voice as if from a distance. "Before he died. He sealed it and he waited — he must have known he was dying — and he waited for me to come all this way rather than send it to my house in Comodoro. Eleven years I lived eleven hours away by train, and he—"

Her throat closed. She pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth and breathed through it, the way her father had taught her to breathe through the worst of a fever when she was small. Some habits survive estrangement. That was the cruelest thing about them.

"He couldn't post it," Orsen said quietly. "A letter can be intercepted. This—" he nodded at the tube, "—could only be found by someone who walked the whole route. He built the delivery into the journey. If the company stopped you, the letter stayed safe. If you gave up, you'd never have to read it. He made the truth cost exactly as much as it was worth."

"That's not kindness," Ingrid said. "That's engineering."

"Yes," Orsen agreed. "He was that too, in his way. Fifty years of surveying his own guilt."

She laughed — one short, broken sound that surprised all three of them — and then the laugh became something else, and she sat down hard on the stone with the oilcloth spread across her knees, and cried the way she had not cried at her father's deathbed or his funeral or any of the eleven gray years between. Not neatly. Not privately. Down here under the earth, in front of two men and a buried lake, with the light of one lamp shaking on the water.

Tomás came and sat near her, not touching, just present, the way you'd sit with a spooked animal. Orsen stood watch at the cavern mouth, which was its own kind of tenderness — a man arranging the world so a woman could grieve without audience.

It lasted a long time. When it was over she felt scraped clean and hollowed, the way the plateau looked after wind.

"I owe him an argument," she said finally, wiping her face with her sleeve. "I told you that, back in Comodoro. That's why I came. Because he owed me explanations and I owed him anger, and I thought we'd settle accounts face to face someday."

"And now?"

"Now he's settled them himself, the bastard. Every account. Paid in full, itemized, fifty years of receipts." She folded the oilcloth along its old creases, carefully, the way her father folded his maps. "And I can't even tell him I forgive him. Do you understand? He spent fifty years believing he didn't deserve forgiveness, and he died believing it, and the one letter that could have told him otherwise is addressed to *after*, to *whoever is honest*. Forgiveness arrived too late for delivery. That's what this is. A parcel returned to sender, addressee deceased."

"Not too late," Tomás said. He was nineteen and usually careful with words the way he was careful with instruments, but he said it plainly. "You're reading it. He wrote it knowing you'd read it. That's delivery. Late isn't never."

Ingrid looked at the boy for a long moment. Then she reached over and squeezed his arm once, hard, saying nothing, and he flushed in the lamplight like a stove door opening.

Orsen came back from the cavern mouth. He moved differently than he had an hour ago — she noticed it the way she noticed weather changes, some surveyor's instinct she'd developed on this trail without meaning to. Tighter. Quieter. A man who has received information he hasn't finished processing.

"There's something else," he said. "In the letter. Did you catch it?"

"Fifty years of buying back documents. Yes. I caught it."

"No — past that." He crouched beside her and touched the folded oilcloth without unfolding it, respectfully, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "*Hidden, buried, seeded along that route.* He didn't keep what he collected, Ingrid. He distributed it. Which means somewhere between here and the railhead there are more caches. Ledgers. Survey sheets. Possibly names." He paused. "And Ballesteros is behind us with rifles and a wagon, and he doesn't yet know how much there is to find."

The lake lapped its small, eternal rhythm against the stone. Ingrid looked down at the honey-colored oilcloth in her lap, at her father's stubborn right-leaning hand, and felt the grief in her chest shift shape — not leaving, never leaving, but compacting into something denser, harder, load-bearing.

"All right," she said. "Then we'd better decide, very quickly, what we do next."

Above them, faint as memory, the plateau held its silence — and somewhere out on that silence, she would think later, wheels were already turning toward them through the dark.
