## 18. The Choice of Routes

They laid the map out on a flat stone in the last of the light, weighted at the corners with four pebbles, because the wind up here had opinions about paper and expressed them constantly.

Orsen crouched over the oilcloth with his dividers — the same brass dividers he'd carried for twelve years, their points worn to needles by a thousand measurements across a thousand miles of country that most men would call empty and that he had never once been able to see as anything but full. Full of gradient. Full of distance. Full of the arithmetic that had ordered his life since the disgrace he did not name even to himself.

Three lines home. He drew them in his head first, the way he always did, before committing anything to dirt or discussion.

The first line ran northwest, back down the way they'd come — the fast trail, the one with water every second day, the one whose dust Ballesteros's wagon wheels had already written their intentions upon. Orsen had seen the riders from the rim two days ago, six of them, camped comfortable in the lee of a basalt ridge like men who had all the time in the world and all the water too. They sat astride the trail the way a bailiff sits in a doorway. You could go around them, but going around meant leaving the trail, and leaving the trail out here was its own kind of dying.

The second line ran south and east, a long loop through the desert margin below the meseta — country so dry the maps simply gave up and left it blank, two hundred miles of it, maybe more, with perhaps three seeps along the way and no guarantee any of them still wept. Efraín's map marked one of them with a small cross and a word in pencil gone nearly ghost-grey: *quizás*. Perhaps. The old man had hedged his bets even in ink.

The third line ran due south, toward the Chilean frontier — toward territory where Ballesteros's writ ran thin, where the company's men could not ride with rifles quite so openly. But the frontier was also a border, and borders meant officials, and officials meant questions, and questions meant the map being taken from him by men in uniforms instead of men in company coats, locked in some customs house in Coyhaique while lawyers on both sides of an imaginary line argued about jurisdiction for a year. And somewhere in that year, Orsen did not doubt, the evidence would develop a way of disappearing. Paper drowned as easily as towns.

He straightened up. His knees cracked — fifty-one years old, and the plateau knew it.

"Show me," Ingrid said.

She stood with her arms folded against the wind, her father's coat buttoned to the throat. She had not taken it off since they'd read the second letter. Orsen understood. Some garments stop being clothing.

Tomás hovered at his shoulder, chronometer in hand, the boy's face lit orange by the low sun and shadowed everywhere else. He had been quiet since the lake. Not frightened-quiet — something else. Awe sits on a young man strangely; it looks like silence from the outside.

Orsen set the dividers' points on the cavern mouth and walked them northwest along the fast trail. "Two days to the railhead if we push. Water at the Estancia Nueva every second day. Ballesteros knows we know he's there." He moved the dividers southeast. "The desert loop. Eleven days, twelve if the seeps are dry. We carry what we have and pray the *quizás* is generous." He moved them again, south. "Chile. Six days riding, then whatever the border gives us."

"And what does the border give us?" Ingrid asked.

"A desk," Orsen said. "A man behind it. Forms in triplicate. And time — months of it — while our evidence sits in a drawer and the company's lawyers negotiate its funeral arrangements."

"The company can't reach into Chile."

"No," Orsen agreed. "But it can reach into Buenos Aires, and Buenos Aires reaches into every consulate on the coast. Ballesteros doesn't need to shoot us there. He only needs to write letters." He looked at her steadily. "I've spent twelve years watching how institutions kill things, Señorita Salas. They rarely use guns. They use patience."

Tomás cleared his throat. "The fast trail, then. If we ride hard—"

"They have horses and we have mules. They have a wagon and we have barrels. They have been resting for two days and we have been digging through caliche." Orsen folded the map along its old creases. "If we take the fast trail, we meet them at a place of their choosing, at a time of their choosing, with the numbers exactly what they want them to be. I didn't spend thirty years reading gradients to walk into the one valley where they hold the high ground."

"So the desert," Ingrid said. It wasn't a question. She had read him already; she was her father's daughter, and reading men who thought they were being unreadable was evidently a family inheritance.

"The desert," Orsen said.

* * *

Tomás objected properly an hour later, when the fire was small and blue and the wind had dropped enough that voices could travel without being stolen.

They sat around it — the three of them now, plus the six mules hobbled in the shelter of the cavern's shoulder, plus everything they owned in this world packed onto those animals' backs. The muleteers had ridden out that morning with half the herd and their silence-money sewn into their belts, and their absence had a shape, a cold spot in the circle where two men used to be. Camps are smaller than they look. Take away two bodies and you take away half the warmth.

"I want to say it plainly," Tomás began, "because I think you'll respect plainness, jefe, even when it's insubordination."

Orsen almost smiled. "Say it."

"The instruments are failing." The boy held up the chronometer. "You've seen it. Two days ago it lost forty seconds against my star sights. Today it lost another twenty. I've cleaned it, I've warmed it, I've slept with it against my chest like a hen with an egg. It keeps losing time anyway. And the compass—" He pulled it out, a good instrument, London-made, and held it level. The needle swung lazily, settled, trembled, drifted a degree, settled again. "There's iron in the ground here. There's iron under half this plateau. My readings since the lake have been lies told politely. If we go into the desert without reliable bearings—"

"We won't have reliable bearings anywhere," Orsen said. "That's not an argument against the desert. That's an argument against the world."

"Then let me finish the argument." Tomás's jaw set, and Orsen saw the man the boy would become standing briefly inside the man he was. "Eleven days. Dead reckoning the whole way. Every hour of error compounds. On the fast trail, if we drift, we drift toward a river. On the loop, if we drift, we drift toward nothing, and nothing is very large out there, jefe. I've seen it. We were in it today."

"He's right," Ingrid said quietly.

Orsen looked at her.

"He's right about the arithmetic," she said. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying say it honestly. You're choosing the route where the land itself might kill us because you're more afraid of the men than the land. That's a real choice. But it should be made with its true price attached."

The fire popped. One of the mules shifted and clinked its hobble chain, and the sound went out into the dark and came back changed.

"You're right," Orsen said at last. "That is the price. So let me attach the other prices too, and then we'll decide together, because there are only three of us now and I won't command men into a desert on my authority alone. Authority is for garrisons. Out here we're just three people and a secret."

He drew in the dirt with a stick, the three lines, the way he'd drawn them in his head.

"The fast trail costs us the truth. Either we fight — six rifles against three, and they're rested and we're not — or we surrender the map, and everything Efraín Salas bought with fifty years of his life goes into a drawer and dies there. I won't pay that price. I've paid versions of it before." He did not elaborate. Ingrid watched him and, mercifully, did not ask. "Chile costs us time, and time is the company's favorite weapon. And it costs us the border itself — I've seen what happens to evidence that crosses a frontier. It develops complications. Complications develop lawyers. Lawyers develop years."

"And the desert?" Tomás asked.

"The desert costs us comfort and possibly our lives, and it buys us the only thing worth buying: arrival. We arrive home with the map intact, the ledger intact, the photographs intact, and nobody between here and Comodoro having touched us. Ballesteros will follow. Of course he will. But following a man across two hundred miles of waterless country is a different profession than waiting for him at a water hole. His horses drink twice what our mules drink. His wagon cannot cross the sand margins at all. He'll have to split his party or abandon it, and either way he becomes slower and angrier, and angry men make errors." Orsen set the stick down. "I don't like the desert. I fear it more than I fear Ballesteros, if you want the whole ledger. But I fear it as an honest enemy. It won't pretend to be anything but what it is."

Silence, except the wind working at the brush screen they'd built over the cavern mouth.

"My father crossed it once," Ingrid said. "In 'ninety-four. He told me about it when I was small — I thought it was a story. He said the trick isn't knowing where the water is. Everyone looks for the man who knows where the water is. He said the trick is knowing how much you drink, and lying to yourself about it last of all."

Orsen felt the words go into him like a splinter, deep and clean. *Lying to yourself last of all.* He thought of the barrels, and of what he already knew he would do in three days' time, and said nothing.

"Then it's decided?" Tomás asked. "We go tomorrow?"

"We go tomorrow," Orsen said. "South by east until we clear the basalt, then east along the old guanaco trails if we find them. Twelve days. We ration from the first morning, not the fifth — rationing on day five is just arithmetic for a funeral." He looked at each of them in turn. "And Tomás — your instruments fail, we navigate by the land. Wind direction, dune shape, the slope of the ground, the birds at dusk. I've done it before. It's slower and it's humbling and it works. Your chronometer will lie to us. The country won't. The country has no reason to."

Tomás nodded slowly, wrapping the chronometer back in its wool. "My father used to say something like that," he said, and stopped, and did not finish, and Orsen did not press, though he noticed — the way he noticed everything about gradients and loads and the small structural weaknesses of things — that the boy's sentence had broken exactly where a grief would sit.

* * *

He took the middle watch, as he always did, because sleep came to him reluctantly and left him early, and the hours between midnight and moonrise belonged to him by long treaty.

The plateau at night was a black sea with a floor of stars. The wind had died entirely, which happened out here maybe one night in ten, and in the stillness Orsen could hear the mules chewing, and Tomás breathing in his bedroll, and beneath everything — or imagined beneath everything, he could not tell — a sound like the buried lake turning over in its sleep. Water under caliche. Enough water for a province, sitting under a desert, while forty-one men lay in a cemetery a day's ride north because the men who found it chose a coal port over a town.

He turned Efraín's words over again. *Shame is heavier than water.* Fifty years the old man had carried it, buying back the truth piece by piece — a ledger here, a marker there, a map drawn in secret on oilcloth that would outlast the paper it contradicted. And what had it bought him? A hotel room smelling of kerosene, and a stranger to give it to, because he could not give it to his own daughter.

*Because shame is heavier than water.*

Orsen looked at his hands in the starlight. Measurer's hands. Loyal hands, he had called them for twelve years — loyal to the firm that gave him his miles, that gave him his first job and his disgrace both, that had taught him that a man could survive anything if he kept moving and counted carefully and never, ever looked directly at the thing behind him.

In eleven days, if the desert permitted, he would stand in front of a magistrate, or a journalist, or a mob, and the thing behind him would finally be in front of him. He found that he was not afraid of it. He found, instead, that he was tired in a way miles had never cured, and suspected years might.

The eastern sky had not yet begun to consider lightening when he heard it: hooves. Far off, north by northwest, many of them, moving at a walk. Then stopping. Then the small sounds of a camp making itself — canvas, iron, a voice pitched low, carrying anyway across eight miles of cold air.

Ballesteros, settling in for the night on the fast trail. Patient as a bailiff.

Orsen stood a long time in the dark, listening to his enemy choose the wrong door, and when the moon rose he woke the others gently, and they struck camp by feel, and by first grey light the three of them and six mules were walking south by east, off every map, into the blank space where the pencil had written *quizás*.

Behind them, faint and fading, the company's camp stirred awake to an empty horizon.

Ahead, the desert waited with its honest face, and Orsen Nystrom, who had never in his life begun a journey by doubting his own figures, caught himself doing the water arithmetic twice — and getting two different answers.
