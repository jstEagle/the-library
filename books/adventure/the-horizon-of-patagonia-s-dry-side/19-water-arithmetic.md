## 19. Water Arithmetic

Ingrid learned the arithmetic on the third morning of the desert loop, and she learned it the way she learned everything that mattered — by counting what other people preferred to leave uncounted.

The barrels sat in a row against the wagon's shadow, six of them now, the muleteers having taken two when they went. Orsen had chalked a mark on each stave at dawn, a small vertical stroke like a telegraph dash, and each evening he measured the water down with a tin dipper and a length of knotted cord, murmuring figures to himself in the flat voice he used for distances. Two liters per person per day. A liter and a half for Tomás, who was young and sweated generously. Less than a liter for each mule, which was not enough and everyone knew it, and the mules knew it best of all, standing with their heads low over the empty troughs in the blue hour before dawn.

Two liters. Ingrid had grown up in a telegraph office; she thought in units, in rates, in the cost per word of everything. Two liters was the weight of a heavy parcel. Two liters was what she'd once spilled on the office floor in Comodoro Rivadavia without anyone scolding her, because the company shipped water in by the tank-load and waste was only a word in a ledger. Out here the same quantity was a day of life, measured out by a man with a cord and a dipper, and the man was measuring himself last.

She noticed it on the third evening, and she might not have noticed at all if the wind hadn't changed.

They had made camp in the lee of a low basalt rise, the kind of featureless swell the plateau offered instead of shelter, and Orsen had gone through his ritual: the dipper, the cord, the chalk, the quiet figures. He filled four canvas bags and hung them from the wagon's rail — one for Tomás, one for herself, one for the cook-pot, and one he set apart, smaller than the others, and said nothing about. Then he walked out to check the mules' hobbles, as he did every evening, walking the perimeter of the camp with the unhurried gait of a man inspecting a right-of-way.

Ingrid lifted the fourth bag. It was light. She knew the weight of a liter the way she knew the weight of a key; her hands had been trained by a thousand parcels and a thousand telegraph keys, and this bag held perhaps a liter and a quarter where the others held two.

She set it back exactly where it had hung, and she waited.

The wind came up from the southwest as the light failed, carrying the dry mineral smell of the interior, and it flattened the scrub and made the canvas bags swing gently on their rail like hanged men, which was a thought she pushed away firmly. Tomás was asleep already, or pretending to be, curled in his blanket with his chronometer cradled against his chest like a doll — the boy slept holding that instrument the way other boys slept holding dogs. The fire was a hand's width of coals. Orsen came back from the mules, sat down across from her, took the small bag from the rail, and drank from it in three measured swallows, and Ingrid watched his throat move and understood that she was watching a man spend himself at a rate he had not disclosed to his investors.

"You're drinking half," she said.

He didn't startle. She had learned that about him — nothing startled him, or rather everything startled him and he had built a wall of stillness so high that no startle could get out. He lowered the bag and looked at her across the coals, and his face in the firelight was all planes and patience.

"I'm drinking my share."

"Your share is two liters. That bag holds one and a quarter, and it was full this morning because I watched you fill it, and the others hold two because I weighed them against each other while you were hobbling the mules. I'm a telegraph operator, Señor Nystrom. Weighing things is most of the job."

The coals ticked. Somewhere out in the dark a mule shifted its weight and the hobble-chain murmured against stone.

"You counted my water," he said at last. There was no anger in it. That was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over later — no anger, only a kind of weary respect, as if she had passed an examination he hadn't known he was administering.

"Someone has to. You count everyone else's."

"That's my work."

"Then let me do yours for one evening, and you tell me what I find." She reached over and took the bag from his hand before he could decide whether to surrender it, and she held it up so the firelight shone through the wet canvas, and she turned it slowly, the way the assayer in Comodoro used to turn ore samples under his lamp. "One and a quarter liters, minus what you just drank. You've had perhaps a liter today. The mules have had more than you. The *mules*, Señor Nystrom."

"The mules carry us."

"And you carry the map, and Tomás carries the instruments, and I carry my father's letters, and none of that matters if the man who knows the route dies of his own arithmetic." She heard her own voice rise and did nothing to lower it. Twelve days in the wilderness had stripped the office manners off her like old paint. "You gave us two liters each. You wrote the ration on the inside of the wagon lid in pencil. I've read it every morning. It says two liters. It doesn't say *except Orsen*."

"It doesn't have to," he said quietly. "I wrote it."

* * *

They sat with that for a while. The wind moved the scrub with a dry whispering, the sound the plateau made when it had nothing else to say, which was always. Ingrid turned the canvas bag over in her hands and found she was angry in a way that had layers to it, the way her anger at her father had had layers — anger at the surface and something else underneath, something that hurt more and was harder to name.

"You think I don't see it," she said. "You think none of us see it. Tomás is nineteen and in love with the idea of you, so he doesn't look. But I look. You gave the muleteers their silence-money out of your own kit — I saw the weight leave your saddlebag. You take the last watch every night and you sleep maybe four hours, and you think because you do it silently it's invisible. It isn't invisible. It's just quiet, and you've confused the two."

Orsen looked into the coals. When he spoke, his voice had the level tone he used for figures.

"If I drink two liters and the water runs out a day early, we die a day early. If I drink one, we have a day extra. That's all the philosophy there is in it."

"That's not philosophy. That's bookkeeping. And it's wrong bookkeeping, because it doesn't count the cost of what happens when the rest of us watch you fade." She leaned forward. "I've watched a man do this before. My father. He went out for six weeks when I was nine and came back twenty pounds lighter and would not say where, and he ate last at his own table for the rest of his life, and I thought — I was a child, I thought that was what strong men did. It took me thirty years and his deathbed to understand that it wasn't strength. It was penance. He was paying for something, a little every day, and he never told me the debt."

The words were out before she could weigh them, and she saw them land. Orsen's stillness changed quality — not a movement, nothing so crude as a movement, but the stillness of a man behind glass becoming the stillness of a man behind a door he has just unlocked from his side.

"Efraín," he said.

"Don't." She held up the bag like a stop signal. "Don't say his name gently. Don't do the thing where you handle my grief like it's a surveyor's instrument and you're afraid of breaking the calibration. He sent you to me. He sent you to me and told you to tell me nothing except that he kept it, and you have kept that promise so faithfully that I have crossed a desert with a man who is rationing himself to death and I still don't know what he *kept*." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and went on anyway. "So here is what I know instead. I know you drink less than you ration. I know you sleep less than you need. I know you look at me sometimes like I'm a letter you're afraid to open. And I know — this is the part that keeps me up at night, Señor Nystrom, this is the arithmetic that doesn't balance — I know that you are doing to yourself exactly what my father did, and I have to ask myself what kind of man collects two of us in one lifetime."

The wind gusted. A coal lifted, glowed, and died.

Orsen took the bag back from her. She let him. He held it a moment, weighing it — and she saw that he weighed it, that the habit of measurement went all the way down into him like grain into wood — and then he did something she had not seen him do in twelve days of travel.

He poured the remainder of the water into the cook-pot.

It was perhaps half a liter. It made almost no sound, a soft collapsing gurgle, and then the bag hung empty on the rail, and he took down the full bag instead, the two-liter bag, the one with his name on it in his own pencil, and he drank from it openly, in front of her, two swallows, three, four, until Ingrid realized she was watching a man surrender and that the surrender looked, on him, exactly like everything else he did: deliberate, measured, complete.

"Two liters," he said. "As written."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Argue with me. You said you owed my father an argument. It seems I've inherited it."

And something in her chest, some brace she had been holding since Puerto Deseado, since the hotel room that smelled of kerosene and the dying man's hands, let go a fraction — not all the way, a fraction — and she heard herself laugh, a short rough sound with no humor in it and a little relief.

* * *

They talked after that, and it was the first conversation they had had that was not about distance or water or the mules' feet, and it went on past the first watch and into the second, down to embers, while Tomás slept his instrument-clutching sleep and the plateau breathed its dry breath around them.

He told her about the spur lines. That was where it started — she asked him why a man would spend twelve years walking decommissioned track, and he was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer, and then he did, in the flat voice, but the flat voice had a floor to it now and she could hear the depth.

"Because a line that's been closed still remembers what it carried," he said. "You can walk an abandoned spur and read the whole story in the sleepers. Where the loads were heaviest. Where the brakes sang. Where someone spilled grain and it sprouted for a season. The company thinks a closed line is a deleted line. It isn't. The land keeps the ledger." He turned his hands palm-up, engineer's hands, scarred and square-nailed. "I've spent twelve years reading ledgers the company thinks it burned."

"And what did your ledger say about you? Before. The disgrace you don't name."

He almost smiled. In the ember-light it was barely a movement of his mouth, but she caught it, and it changed his whole face the way a single word changes a telegram.

"Tomás told you there was a disgrace?"

"Tomás told me there was something. The stationmaster told Tomás there was something. Men in this country can't keep a secret, they can only keep it loudly." She drew her knees up. "I'm a telegraph operator. I've read other people's messages for eleven years. You learn that everyone has one line they never send."

He looked at her for a long moment. The wind had dropped; the silence had the particular density of the plateau at night, as though the whole world were listening through a keyhole.

"There was a bridge," he said finally. "Twelve years ago, on the Deseado line. A trestle over the Cañadón de los Fósiles. I inspected it in the spring and I passed it. I passed it because the schedule was behind and because the man who taught me everything — the man who gave me my first chain and my first level — told me the timbers had another season in them, and because I wanted to believe him more than I wanted to believe my own instruments, which had said, in the small careful way instruments say things, *not sure*." His hands closed. "In November it went down under a work train. Four men. One of them was sixteen. His name was Aurelio Sandoval and I have written it so many times in so many ledgers of my own that the letters have worn smooth."

Ingrid didn't move. She had the sense that any movement would cost him the thread.

"The company paid. The company always pays — that's what the company is *for*, it's a machine for converting grief into quiet. The widow got a pension and a house in Bahía Blanca and a request to sign a statement. I got reassignment. And I understood, standing in the office while a clerk explained my new route to me in the tone men use for damaged goods — I understood that I could spend the rest of my life being the engineer who once killed four men, or I could spend it being the engineer who walks closed lines where no one can die. So I chose the miles." He looked up. "That's the disgrace. Not the bridge. The twelve years. The bridge was an error. The twelve years were the sin."

"Orsen." She used his first name for the first time and heard it land between them like a dropped tool. "My father spent fifty years walking back to a town that died of thirst, buying back the truth piece by piece, and he never once said the word *sorry* out loud where I could hear it. You just handed me your worst thing across a fire on the second night of a desert crossing because I caught you cheating at water." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know what that is? That's not confession. That's the first honest hour I've had with another human being since my father died."

"The ration holds," he said, and she laughed again despite herself, despite everything, because it was exactly what her father would have said, and she understood in that moment — with a completeness that hurt — why Efraín Salas had chosen this man out of all the men on the dry side of Patagonia. Not for his engineering. For this: that when you caught him shorting his own share, he poured it into the common pot.

"Two liters," she agreed. "As written. But I'm checking the bags every evening now. Consider yourself audited."

"Consider yourself hired," he said, and for a moment — a real moment, firelit and unguarded — he looked young, or rather he looked the age he actually was, which she realized she had never once seen him look.

* * *

She took the middle watch, as she had taken it every night since the muleteers left, and she walked the perimeter with the wagon between her and the wind, and the plateau lay around her black and enormous and indifferent, and she found that she was not afraid of it for the first time.

That was the strangest part of the whole evening, she thought. Not the confession, not the water poured into the pot, not even the sound of her own voice saying *thirty years* out loud to a stranger. The strangest part was that the desert had stopped being an emptiness. Somewhere behind them lay a town with forty-one graves and a bell half-buried in its own square. Somewhere ahead lay whatever her father had spent his life walking toward. And here, in between, on a featureless plain under a spill of stars, a man had handed her the one line he had never sent, and she had read it, and the world had not ended, and the water was still in the bags, and tomorrow they would walk again.

She understood, suddenly, what her father had meant in his last letter — the letter she had read on the second slamming of the door, the letter that had brought her here — when he wrote that he had spent his life learning the difference between a secret and a weight, and that the only way to set a weight down was to hand it to someone with the strength to carry it.

He had handed Orsen the map. But he had handed *her* the argument, and the argument, it turned out, was the heavier thing.

Near dawn she woke Tomás for his watch and lay down in her blanket, and the last thing she saw before sleep was Orsen's silhouette against the graying east, standing at the wagon rail with the knotted cord in his hand, measuring the water — measuring it honestly now, she was sure of it, or sure enough, which in the desert was the same thing.

The wind rose an hour after full light, and by noon it had a taste to it.

Ingrid had lived her whole life on the dry side and she knew the plateau's weathers the way she knew telegraph codes, and this one she knew worst of all. It came out of the west as a brown smudge no thicker than a pencil line, and Tomás saw it first and shouted, and Orsen stood up from the instruments and looked at it for a long moment, and then he said, very quietly, in the flat voice she now understood had a floor and a depth and a whole drowned country underneath it:

"Tie down everything. It's going to be three days."
