## 20. The Sandstorm

Tomás Ferreyra had heard the old muleteers talk about the viento blanco the way sailors talked about drowning — with a kind of professional detachment, as if it happened only to other men who had made other mistakes. *The white wind,* they called it, though there was nothing white about it at first. It came out of the west the color of dried blood, a wall of dust standing on the horizon like a mountain range that had decided to walk.

He saw it first, on the fourth morning of the desert loop, because he happened to be facing west with the theodolite against his eye, trying to shoot an azimuth to a butte that Orsen had named — without ceremony, without any of the poetry Tomás was beginning to expect from him — *Cerro Tres*. The butte was gone. Not hidden, not hazed over. Gone. And where it should have been there was a brown immensity, slow and total, swallowing the sky from the ground up.

"Señor Nystrom," Tomás said, and his voice came out smaller than he intended. "The horizon's wrong."

Orsen was beside him in four strides. He looked for perhaps five seconds — Tomás counted, because counting was what he did when fear arrived, numbers were the stones he threw at it — and then said, quietly, in the tone he used for facts that had already stopped being negotiable: "Viento blanco. We camp. Now."

The camp went up in eleven minutes. Tomás timed that too, because Orsen called the minutes aloud as he moved among the mules, and the two muleteers — the two who remained, after the others had taken their silence-money and gone — worked with the grim fluency of men who had survived this before. The canvas came down off the packs and went up as a windbreak, doubled, staked with iron pins driven at a lean against the coming force. The animals were hobbled close and turned tail to the west. The water barrels were lashed down with every strap they had.

Ingrid stood at the edge of the camp with her back to the wind, her braid already whipping loose, watching the wall come on. "My father told me about one of these," she said, and she had to raise her voice, though the storm was still twenty minutes away. "He said it lasted two days and when it was over he couldn't find the horse he'd been holding by the bridle."

"Did he find it?" Tomás asked.

"He found its bones a year later, forty miles south." She said it without drama, the way she said everything now, and went to help Orsen with the last of the lashings.

The wind arrived not as a gust but as a decision. One moment the air was moving; the next it had become a solid thing, a brown ocean laid on its side, and the world contracted to the size of the camp — then smaller. Tomás crouched under the windbreak with the chronometer case hugged to his chest and felt the whole plateau lean against him. The sound was beyond sound. It was as if the land itself had begun to speak, in a voice made of everything loose and light and dead: sand, dust, the powdered bones of a million years of Patagonian nothing, flung against the canvas hard enough to make it drum.

Grit found every seam. It got into his teeth, his ears, the creases of his eyelids. He learned to breathe through a dampened cloth, as the muleteers showed him, and to keep his back to the wind and his eyes shut when he had to move. In the first hour he crawled the twelve feet from the windbreak to the cook box and came back with a face like a man who had been weeping dust.

* * *

The chronometer was the thing he thought about most, those first hours, because the chronometer was his.

It had been his father's — no. He corrected himself, as he corrected himself every time, with the small ache that never quite dulled: it had been bought with his father's death-money, the settlement the company had paid out in 1909 for a man crushed between a locomotive and a loading platform at Puerto Gallegos, a man whose accident had been entered in the ledger as *worker error*. The company had paid for the funeral and the watch both. Tomás had chosen the watch. He had told himself it was practical. He had known, even at sixteen, that it was a way of keeping the wound where he could feel it ticking.

It was a good watch, a Swiss deck chronometer, and Orsen had trusted it from the first day, because Orsen trusted instruments the way other men trusted saints — provisionally, but with real devotion. Longitude lived in that watch. Every fix Tomás took, every line of position he laid across the map with his parallel rule, depended on the watch knowing, absolutely and without argument, what time it was in Greenwich.

He checked it under the canvas by shielded lantern light, twice a day, against the ritual Orsen had taught him: the watch was rated against the stars, and the stars did not care about storms.

On the first evening of the storm, the watch was four seconds slow.

On the second morning, eleven seconds.

Tomás sat with it in his cupped hands and felt the fear change character. A watch could be fast or slow by accident. But this — this steady, insistent drift, like a man walking off a road with his eyes open — this was the watch dying. Something in the balance, something in the oil, something the grit had reached that his cleaning could not. He cleaned it again anyway, with the little kit, with breath held so as not to blow dust into the works, and when he closed the case it was already losing again, a second every few hours, patient as erosion.

He did not tell Orsen. He told himself he would, that evening, or the next morning, when there was something to report instead of something to confess. He told himself a four-second error was nothing, was a mile at worst, was a rounding. He told himself the storm would end and they would see Cerro Tres and every error would be erased against a visible landmark, the way errors always were, the way they had been on every survey he'd ever worked.

The storm did not end.

* * *

It ran three days, and Tomás learned that a sandstorm is not one experience but three.

The first day was terror. The wind screamed, the canvas strained at its iron, and twice they lost a windbreak panel entirely and had to rebuild it on their knees in a brown blindness, shouting instructions no one could hear. The mules screamed too, a sound Tomás had never heard from an animal, high and human, and one of them — the gray, the gentle one, the one that had carried the theodolite since Puerto Deseado — tried to bolt against its hobbles and tore skin off its pasterns doing it. Ingrid held its head for an hour in the worst of the wind, her whole body wrapped around its neck, her mouth at its ear, saying things Tomás could not hear and understood anyway.

The second day was tedium, which was worse. The wind settled into a steady roar and the world became a canvas room the size of a grave. They ate cold biscuit and charqui and drank measured water — Orsen pouring it himself, his face unreadable, giving each man and woman and animal its exact ration from the barrel. Tomás watched the water level in the barrel and did arithmetic he did not want to do. Two liters a day, per person. Six days of reserve, maybe, if the storm broke tomorrow. Less if it didn't.

He worked on the watch. There was nothing else to do. He lay in his bedroll with the case open against his chest and the lantern hooded, and he watched the second hand, and he thought about his father's hands, which had been huge and gentle and had smelled of coal oil, and about the platform at Puerto Gallegos, which he had never seen, and about the ledger entry he had read at nineteen in the company archive in Comodoro, hunting for the truth of how his father died and finding instead a sentence: *Worker error. No further action.* He had sat in that archive with the sentence in front of him for a long time, and what he had felt was not anger. It was the vertigo of realizing that a man could be erased in eleven words, that his whole weight and warmth and the sound of his laugh could be filed away under a heading, and the company would go on, and the trains would run, and no one would ever say his name again except a boy with a settlement watch ticking in his pocket.

*Worker error.* He had believed the company, then. That was the part that shamed him now. He had believed it the way he believed the theodolite, because it was official, because it was written, because believing it was easier than believing that the world could be that careless.

The third day was despair, and despair, Tomás discovered, was also a kind of weather. It moved in slowly, the way the storm had. It had a pressure. It made the inside of the canvas room smaller than the outside. On the third day he caught himself watching the second hand not to check the watch but simply because the second hand was the only thing in the world still moving in an orderly way, and he hated it, and he could not stop watching it.

Ingrid found him like that, on the third afternoon. She crawled over and sat close, shoulder to shoulder, and shouted into the cloth over his ear: "You've checked it forty times. It isn't going to improve with attention."

"Nothing improves with attention," he shouted back, and heard how it sounded, and was ashamed.

She was quiet a moment. The wind filled the silence for her, the way it filled everything. Then: "My father spent fifty years alone with a secret. Do you know what he told me about that? In the letter I didn't want to read?" She didn't wait for him to answer. "He said the secret was easy. It was the *alone* that ate him. He said a man can carry almost anything if he can put it down in front of someone once a day." Her hand found Tomás's wrist in the dark and squeezed, hard, once. "Put it down, Tomás. Whatever it is. Once a day. That's all."

He didn't tell her about the watch. Not then. He told her about his father instead — about the platform, the eleven words, the watch bought with blood-money — and she listened with her face close to his and her eyes shut against the grit, and when he finished she said, "Eleven words," in a voice that had forty-one graves in it, and he understood that she was not only hearing his grief but adding it to a ledger of her own that would never balance.

* * *

The storm broke at dawn on the fourth day, the way a fever breaks — all at once, leaving the world weak and washed and strange.

Tomás crawled out of the camp into a silence so total it rang. The wind was gone. The air was clean, cold, almost sweet after three days of dust. The sun came up through a haze of fine suspended particles that turned everything the color of weak tea, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless.

He stood up and turned a slow circle, and the despair that had been weather inside him became weather outside him instead, and it was worse.

There was nothing.

Not featureless in the way a plain is featureless — the desert loop had been plain, but it had been a *legible* plain, a text of buttes and dry washes and the long dark seams of lava flows that Orsen read like a timetable. That country was gone. The storm had not merely buried the trail; it had buried the grammar of the land itself. In every direction the surface had been reorganized into the same low, rippled sameness, dunes of grey sand lying across the scrub in long parallel waves like a sea that had been told to hold still. The buttes were gone. Cerro Tres, which should have stood off their western shoulder, was gone. The lava seam they had been following south was gone. Even the sun's haze erased the horizon; earth and sky met in a smear, no line, no edge, no *anything*.

"Señor," Tomás said. His voice cracked on it.

Orsen was already standing at the edge of camp, and Tomás watched him do the thing he had watched him do a hundred times: the slow quarter-turn, reading the land, the eyes moving with the confidence of a man consulting a document only he possessed. And Tomás watched the confidence fail. It was small, the failure — a hesitation, a second turn, the eyes narrowing — but he saw it, and seeing it was like watching a church lean.

Orsen turned back to the camp. "Saddles," he said. "We move south by compass until we hit ground I know."

They moved. Tomás walked behind the mules with the compass in his hand and the chronometer in his pocket, and he did the work, and the work was all there was. Compass bearing south by south-west, corrected for the variation Orsen recited from memory. Dead reckoning: pace count, time, estimated speed over the sand — but the sand was wrong for reckoning, the storm had left drifts that slowed them and hollows that sped them, and every pace was a guess wearing the uniform of a measurement. He kept the numbers anyway. He kept them in the little notebook, in his small careful hand, and each column of figures looked exactly like the columns that had always kept him safe, and he no longer believed a single one of them.

At noon he took the watch out and checked it against his sun sight, and the sun sight gave him a longitude that put them forty minutes of arc east of where the dead reckoning said they should be — and the dead reckoning, he knew, had its own accumulated error, and the watch had its own, and he could not tell which lie was lying.

Forty minutes of arc. He did the arithmetic without wanting to. At this latitude, forty minutes was better than fifty miles. Fifty miles of *somewhere*, and he could not say whether the somewhere was the watch's fault or the sand's or his.

He stood in the tea-colored light with the instruments in his hands — compass, watch, the notebook with its beautiful lying columns — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had spent his whole life believing that the world could be measured into safety. That if the numbers were kept carefully enough, nothing true could be lost. His father had died inside a company's numbers. The forty-one of Pozo Nuevo had died inside a survey that said there was no water. And now the numbers were failing him in a featureless desert, and the land his instruments were supposed to read had been erased like a ledger line, like eleven words, like a town struck from every map.

"Tomás." Orsen had come up beside him, quietly, the way he did. He was looking not at the instruments but at Tomás's face. "What's the error?"

Tomás could have lied. He thought about lying. He thought about the four seconds he hadn't reported, and the eleven, and the forty minutes of arc, and how each silence had been a small stone laid on the next, a cairn of concealment, and how he was suddenly, desperately tired of carrying it.

"Maybe fifty miles," he said. "East or west, I can't tell you which. The watch is losing time. It's been losing since the storm started, and I didn't—" His voice went. He made himself finish. "I didn't tell you. I kept checking it like that would fix it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, señor."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. The silence had no horizon either. Then he said, not unkindly, in the voice of a man reading a fact that had already stopped being negotiable: "Fifty miles. And how much water?"

"Four days," Tomás said. "Maybe five, for the mules. Less."

Orsen looked south, into the smear where the horizon should have been, and Tomás saw him do something he had never seen him do in all the weeks of the expedition: he took out his own notebook, and he looked at it, and he closed it again without writing anything.

"Then we're lost," Orsen said. He said it the way he said *viento blanco* — a fact, already past arguing. And then, almost to himself, in a voice Tomás had never heard him use, quieter than the dead air: "Twelve years on these lines, and I have never once been lost."

Behind them, Ingrid had stopped walking. She was standing very still among the mules, her head turned east, her whole body listening — not to the wind, which was gone, but to something under the silence. Tomás watched her drop to one knee in the sand, and press her palm flat against it, the way her father must have taught her before she could write.

"Orsen," she called, and there was something in her voice that made both men turn. "The ground here. Come and look at the ground."
