## 21. Lost

The ninth morning came up gray, without a sunrise worth the name. Orsen Nystrom stood at the edge of camp with his sextant in one hand and the sun nowhere to be found in the other, and for the first time in thirty years of measuring the world he understood that the instrument was not broken.

He had checked it twice in the night by lamplight — the mirrors true, the horizon glass clean, the index arm moving smooth as a well-oiled thought. The trouble was not the brass. The trouble was that there was no horizon. The land ran out flat and pale in every direction like a sheet of paper someone had forgotten to write on, and above it a sky the color of dirty wool pressed down until earth and heaven met somewhere out past seeing, and where they met there was no line, and without a line there was nothing to shoot.

He lowered the sextant anyway. Habit is a kind of prayer, and he said his.

Behind him the camp was waking badly. Six mules stood hobbled in a loose crescent, heads low, ears flat against the wind that never stopped here, that had not stopped since the second day of the loop. Tomás crouched over the water barrels doing arithmetic with a stick in the dirt, and even from twenty paces Orsen could read the boy's shoulders: they were the shoulders of a man carrying something heavy and trying to decide whether to set it down.

Ingrid was already saddling her mule. She did that now before anyone asked, before the light was good enough to see the buckles by, as if motion were a form of hope she could put on like a coat. She had led them for two days since the seep, following her father's letters and her own stubborn reading of the land, but this morning the letters had run out of country to describe. Efraín Salas had never crossed this stretch. There was nothing left between them and Comodoro but dead reckoning and a nineteen-year-old's figures.

Orsen walked back through the camp, counting as he went, because counting was what his hands did when his mind would not hold still. Fourteen animals when they'd left the railhead; six now. Four people. Water: two full barrels and half of a third, which sounded like a number until you divided it by mouths and days and the merciless thirst of mules. He had done the division last night three times and gotten the same answer each time, which was the answer he refused to say aloud: four days, if they were careful, and five if they were lucky, and luck on the dry side of Patagonia was a currency nobody carried.

"Señor Nystrom." Tomás's voice. Not the bright voice that had signed on at the stationmaster's office, eager as a dog at a gate. A smaller voice.

Orsen turned. The boy was standing over his scratchings in the dirt, and he didn't brush them away, which told Orsen everything before a word was spoken. Tomás Ferreyra protected his figures the way other men protected their reputations. If he was leaving them exposed, it was because he wanted them seen. Wanted them judged. Wanted, maybe, to be told he was wrong.

"Say it," Orsen said.

Tomás wiped his hands on his trousers. "I've been keeping the reckoning from the seep. Dead reckoning — course and distance, hour by hour." He swallowed. "You know how it's done."

"I taught you how it's done."

"Yes, sir." The *sir* had come back into his speech somewhere around the sandstorm and stayed there, and Orsen hated it, because it was the sound of a boy putting distance between himself and a man he was about to disappoint. "The problem is compounding. Every hour's error sits on top of the last one. Wind pushing us off course while we think we're holding the bearing. Mule pace — I've been estimating it from time, but the ground changed after the storm, soft going, and soft going eats distance. And the chronometer—" He stopped.

"The chronometer," Orsen said.

"It lost eleven minutes during the viento blanco. I corrected it. But I corrected it against my own estimate of how long we'd been stopped, and if that estimate was wrong, then every longitude I've computed since is wrong by the same amount, and we wouldn't know. We can't know. There's nothing out here to check it against." He looked up, and his eyes were wet-bright with a misery that had nothing to do with fear of dying and everything to do with fear of having failed. "Sir, I may have walked us off our own map. I may have been walking us off it for three days."

* * *

They held council around the water barrels, because that was where all their councils happened now; the barrels were the government of the expedition, its treasury and its senate both.

Ingrid heard Tomás out without interrupting, arms folded, wind pulling strands of hair loose from under her scarf. When he finished she looked at Orsen. "How wrong? Worst case."

Tomás answered before Orsen could. "Forty miles. Maybe fifty. South or southeast — the error runs that way, if it runs anywhere."

"Fifty miles," Ingrid repeated. She said it the way she said most hard things now, flatly, weighing it rather than flinching from it. "At our pace that's four more days. We have water for four."

"If we're careful," Orsen said.

"We have been careful." She let that sit. She knew about the liters he poured back into the common barrel when he thought no one watched; she had caught him at it once and they had fought about it in low voices while Tomás pretended to sleep, and the fight had ended not in victory for either of them but in a truce so fragile neither dared speak of it again. "So the question isn't whether we're careful. The question is which way careful points."

And that was the question, and Orsen Nystrom had spent the whole gray dawn avoiding it, and now it stood in front of him wearing a boy's ashamed face and a woman's level eyes, and he could not put it off any longer.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud in thirty years of railroading and surveying and walking the closed lines of the dry side: he had never been lost. Not once. Other men got lost — good men, men who could shoot a sun and read a barometer — and Orsen had always found them, or found himself, or found some feature, some escarpment, some dry arroyo whose shape matched the shape in his memory, and turned it into a fix. It was the one thing he had believed about himself without ever examining, the way a man believes in his own heartbeat. Twelve years ago he had lost everything else — the disgrace he kept locked in a room in his mind, the room he never opened — but he had never lost *the land*. The land had always known him. He had always known it back.

And this morning he had stood at the edge of camp with a perfect instrument and no horizon, and turned slowly in a circle, and found — nothing. No landmark. No line of hills remembered from a survey fifteen years old. No guanaco trail, no salt-crusted pan, no shape of anything at all. The viento blanco had taken the trail and two mules and three days of position, and it had left behind a country so featureless it might have been the inside of his own skull.

"I don't know which way," he said.

It cost him more than the wound had. Ballesteros's bullet had torn his ribs and he had barely made a sound. This — four words — and his voice came out sanded down to the grain.

Tomás stared at him. Ingrid didn't stare; she watched, which was different and worse.

"You don't know," she said, "or you won't guess?"

"There's a difference?"

"There's always a difference with you." She crouched at the edge of Tomás's scratched figures, studying them upside down. "My father used to tell me about men like you. Railroad men. He said the company trained them to believe the land was numbers, because numbers obey, and land doesn't. He said the day a surveyor admits the land has beaten him is the day he starts telling the truth." She looked up. "So admit it properly, Orsen. Not *I don't know which way.* Say what you actually know."

What he actually knew. He made himself do it, the way he'd once made himself stand in front of a magistrate and answer questions he'd spent twelve years dodging. "I know the wind has blown from the west-southwest for nine days without backing more than a point. I know soft ground slows mules more than men estimate — call it a fifth, maybe a quarter. I know the chronometer error, if there is one, throws us east or west, not north or south. And I know that south of us, however far, the land has to change. The plateau can't run forever. Somewhere south there are the basalt edges, the canyon country, the first sheep stations along the Santa Cruz road. Terrain means water. Flat means death."

"So we go south."

"So we go south," Orsen agreed, "and we stop pretending we know where we are while we do it."

* * *

They broke camp and went south, and the country did nothing to reward them.

All that day it was the same: scrub flattened by wind into bronze-colored mats, gravel, the occasional bone of some animal that had died facing the wrong direction years ago and been picked clean by a patience larger than hunger. The sky stayed low and lidless. Twice Orsen climbed the tallest thing in sight — a rise that could not have claimed ten feet of prominence — and twice the view was the view they were already standing in.

He caught himself doing the old things. Counting paces under his breath. Estimating distances to mirages and correcting the estimates downward. Building the country in his head out of habit, laying an imaginary grid over emptiness, and every hour the grid fit worse, and every hour he understood a little more deeply what Tomás had tried to tell him at dawn: error compounds. It was true of figures and it was true of men. One mistake, uncorrected, becomes the foundation of the next. He had built twelve years of life that way — one silence laid on another silence, each one load-bearing — and he had called the structure discipline.

Toward evening Ingrid rode up beside him. For a while she said nothing, matching his mule's pace, watching the west where the sun was trying and failing to find a gap in the cloud.

"My father was lost once," she said. "Before I was born. He told it as a joke, usually, at parties he never went to." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Three weeks in the sierras, no instruments, eating his boots. And he said the worst part wasn't the hunger. The worst part was the third week, when he stopped being afraid. Because he realized he'd stopped believing anyone was looking for him. He said being lost isn't a place. It's deciding no one knows where you are."

Orsen was quiet for a long time. "I know where you are," he said finally. "That's not nothing."

"No," she said. "It's not nothing." She reached over and took his rein, not guiding the mule, just holding it, the way you'd hold a man's wrist to feel that the pulse was still there. "But hear me, Orsen. If your pride needs this country to forgive you before you can navigate it, we die of that. You've spent nine days being the man who's never lost. Be the man who's lost. It's a smaller man. He fits through doors."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow that gave nothing except a little shelter from the wind, and Orsen sat up after the others slept, with the oilcloth map spread across his knees though he no longer needed it — the route home was not on it; it had never been on it; Efraín Salas had drawn the way *in*, and the way out was the part of every journey no one maps.

He took out his notebook instead, the one he'd kept for thirty years, and by the light of the shaded lamp he wrote down what he actually knew, item by item, the way he'd write a report for a line he intended to build. Wind: WSW, nine days. Ground: soft since the storm. Chronometer: suspect. Position: unknown within fifty miles. Course: south. Water: three days, strict.

Then, underneath, in smaller letters, he wrote the thing he had never written in any notebook: *I am lost.*

It looked small on the page. It looked almost peaceful.

He was closing the book when he heard it — or thought he heard it. Under the wind, which never wholly slept, something else. A rhythm. Faint, irregular, coming and going the way sounds come and go across open country at night, riding the gusts.

He stood. Listened until his ears ached from listening.

Hooves. Many of them, or one animal circling — impossible to say. From the south. From the direction of tomorrow.

He woke Ingrid with a hand on her shoulder, and her eyes opened all the way, already awake, already knowing, and together they knelt at the rim of the hollow and listened to the dark, where the sound rose and fell and rose again, closer now, or perhaps only clearer — and beneath the hooves, thin as a wire drawn through the night, there was something else.

A bell.

Small, cracked, tolling unevenly, like the half-buried bell of a church in a town that had died of thirst forty years before anyone drew a map that admitted it existed.
