## 22. Ingrid Reads the Land

The morning of the ninth day came up gray and without wind, which was somehow worse than the wind. Ingrid had learned that on the plateau the wind at least told you something — it had a direction, a temper, an opinion. This stillness was like being inside a closed room the size of a province.

She stood at the edge of camp with her hands around a tin cup of water she was not drinking. Two liters a day, Orsen had said, and he measured it out with the gravity of a priest distributing communion, and she had caught him — three days ago now — tipping half his own ration into Tomás's canteen when he thought no one watched, and she had not said anything about it yet because she did not know yet whether saying it would be a kindness or a wound.

Behind her, the camp made its small sounds: mules shifting, canvas snapping in that dead air as if trying to remember what wind was, Tomás's voice low and apologetic, explaining his figures to Orsen for the third time since dawn.

"—if the drift began on the second day, then everything after is compounded," Tomás was saying. "I've run it four ways. The chronometer lost eleven seconds during the storm, maybe more before I noticed, and eleven seconds is—" He stopped. She heard him swallow. "Eleven seconds is miles out here."

"And the compass?"

"The compass is drunk, señor. It's been drunk since the depression. You know that."

Ingrid turned. Orsen Nystrom sat on an overturned provision crate with the oilcloth map — no, not the map; the map was gone from their keeping, or hidden, or decoyed, depending on which lie they were all pretending to believe — with instead a sheet of paper covered in Tomás's careful arithmetic, and he was looking at the numbers the way a man looks at a doctor's face.

Twelve years, Ingrid thought. Twelve years this man had walked the dry side of Patagonia and never once been lost. He had told her that on the third night out, in the tone other men used to describe their mothers — not boasting, just stating the one fixed thing in a life that had otherwise come apart. *I have never been lost. The land tells me where I am if I listen.* And now the land had stopped speaking, or had started lying, and she could see what that cost him. It was not fear exactly. It was something more private than fear. It was a man discovering that the one sense he had trusted above all others might have been a habit all along.

"We are here," Orsen said finally, and put his finger on a point in the middle of nothing, "or we are here." His finger moved perhaps forty kilometers west. "Or anywhere on the line between."

"Then we walk the line until it stops being a line," Ingrid said.

Both men looked at her. Tomás with relief, because he was nineteen and wanted someone older than himself to be right. Orsen with something harder — the look of a man who has been handed back his own authority by someone who does not want it and cannot afford to refuse it.

"Water?" he asked, and it was not really a question about water. It was *do you still trust me.*

"Two days," Ingrid said. "Maybe two and a half for the mules." She looked at the animals standing hipshot along the picket line, heads low, ears at that flat angle of endurance. "Less than that for them."

* * *

They walked the line. That was the whole of the plan, and everyone knew it was barely a plan at all: hold a bearing that might be wrong, across ground that offered nothing, toward a horizon that changed only when the light changed and pretended to change even then.

Ingrid rode last, on the spare mule, because someone had to watch the trail behind them, though there was no trail anymore — the viento blanco had taken it, and the days since had laid down new sand over the old, so that they moved through country with no memory. She watched the men's backs. Orsen rode like a plumb line, upright and economical, and she had come to understand that this too was discipline rather than nature, that somewhere under the straight spine was the same disgrace he never named, riding with him always, and that he kept his back straight so it would not climb any higher.

Tomás rode badly and always had, all elbows and apology, but he rode, and he had not complained once since the storm, which worried her more than complaining would have. A boy who stops complaining has started hiding things.

By midday the sun had burned off the gray and the plateau lay open in every direction, featureless except for scrub — coironé in silver clumps, neneo like grey-green cushions, the occasional thornbush standing alone as if it had been placed there by someone with a cruel sense of arrangement. The light flattened everything. Distance became a rumor.

It was Tomás who broke first, near the fourth hour.

"I should say it plainly," he called forward, and his voice cracked on *plainly* and he hated himself for it, she could see that from behind. "If my error runs east instead of west, then we're walking away from everything. From the seep, from the Escalante, from—"

"From Ballesteros," Orsen said. "Yes. There's that mercy in it, at least."

"There's no mercy in dying lost."

"No," Orsen agreed quietly. "There isn't."

The silence that followed had weight. Ingrid let it sit for a hundred paces, two hundred, watching the ground move beneath her mule's feet, and it was the ground that saved them, though none of them knew it yet — because she had been raised on her father's stories, and her father had never once in his life described gold. He had described ground. Fifty years of letters home, and then fifty years of the same stories told to a daughter who pretended not to listen: how the dry side keeps its accounts in small print, how nothing out here announces itself, how a man who looks for the big thing dies and a man who reads the small thing eats.

*Look down, niña,* he used to say, tapping the table with one finger. *Everyone looks at the horizon. The horizon is a liar. The truth walks on the ground.*

She reined in.

"Stop," she said.

Orsen turned his mule. "Ingrid?"

"Stop the column. Please."

He held up a hand and the little procession halted, six mules and three people and one secret, standing in the middle of a sameness so complete it seemed to press on the ears. He rode back to her. Up close she could see the rationing in his face, the fine drawn look around the eyes of a man drinking less than he allowed himself to admit.

"What do you see?"

"That's what I'm deciding," she said, and swung down, and knelt.

The ground here was no different from the ground an hour ago — that was the first thing, and she made herself say it aloud so the men would not think she had seen some marvel. Same gravel, same crusted sand, same grey scrub. But between the clumps of coironé, where the sheep-soft dust lay undisturbed, there were lines. Not paths. Not yet paths. Traces. Pale threads worn into the gravel, each one narrower than her hand, running — she turned slowly on her knees, following them with her eyes — running not south with the prevailing wind, not east-west with the old lakebeds, but converging. Ahead of them, off toward the northwest, dozens of these faint threads drew together the way loose strands draw together when you begin to braid.

"Guanaco," she said.

Tomás slid off his mule and crouched beside her. "I don't see anything."

"You're looking for a road. Look for a habit." She touched one of the threads, a groove worn maybe a finger's depth into the caliche crust, polished smooth at the bottom. "Guanaco don't wander. They commute. They walk from bedding ground to water and back, the same route, generation after generation, until the ground remembers it even after they stop using it. My father showed me once, years ago, near Gobernador. He said—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, dropped into an imitation of Efraín Salas's cadence, and she let it, because the men needed her father more than they needed her pride, "he said the guanaco are the oldest surveyors in Patagonia. They ran their lines before the company ever existed. And their lines are always true, because their lives depend on it."

Orsen had dismounted too. He was standing over them, and she watched him do the thing he did — the engineer's translation, turning instinct into gradient, habit into hydrology. She could almost hear the machinery turn over.

"Converging lines mean a destination," he said slowly. "And a destination worth commuting to, in this country, in this season—"

"Is water," Ingrid finished. "Not a lake. Not a well. A seep, a spring, something. Guanaco can go three days without drinking, four in cool weather. These trails are old — years old, maybe decades — but they were cut by thirst, and thirst doesn't draw fantasy maps."

Tomás was already pulling out his notebook, sketching the convergence, measuring angles between the faintest threads with his pencil held at arm's length. Then he stopped, pencil hovering, and looked up at Orsen with an expression Ingrid recognized: the look of a young man about to say something disloyal to his own training.

"The instruments say northwest is wrong," Tomás said. "My dead reckoning says northwest is wrong. If we follow these—"

"If we follow your reckoning," Ingrid said, not unkindly, "we will arrive nowhere, precisely, and die there with excellent arithmetic."

Tomás flinched. She had meant it to sting a little — grief sharpened into anger had been her way of moving through the world since Puerto Deseado, and old habits argue even when the argument is settled — but she saw immediately that the sting had landed somewhere deeper, on the bruise of the storm and the lost days and the eleven seconds, and she put her hand briefly on his arm.

"That wasn't fair," she said. "Your numbers got us this far alive. But numbers need a witness, Tomás, and out here the ground is the only witness that doesn't panic. Your father—" she stopped herself, because that wound was not hers to touch, not yet, not out loud in the open country. She changed course. "My father spent fifty years learning to read this land after the company taught him to read ledgers. He used to say the ledgers lied and the land couldn't afford to."

Orsen had been silent through all of it, standing with his hat in his hands, and now Ingrid saw him do something she had never seen him do in the weeks since Puerto Deseado. He looked at the ground, then at the converging threads, then at her — and he deferred. Not graciously, not performatively. With visible effort, the way a man sets down a weight he has carried so long his arms have forgotten any other shape.

"Miss Salas," he said. "Lead us."

* * *

They followed the braided traces northwest through the afternoon, and Ingrid discovered that leading is a different animal from navigating. Navigation is done with instruments and ink. Leading is done with your body — you ride ahead and every eye behind you rides on your back, and you feel that weight between your shoulder blades like a hand, and you understand why Orsen's spine was always so straight.

She stopped often. That was the first lesson, and she gave it to herself sternly: stop and read, stop and verify, because a leader who guesses twice has spent her authority. At each halt she dismounted and walked the threads, confirming the convergence held, watching for the places where the trails split around a stony rise and rejoined beyond it, the way water splits and rejoins, the way, she thought, families split and sometimes rejoin and sometimes don't.

Once, crossing a shallow pan of white alkali clay, the traces vanished entirely and her heart went with them, and she stood in the blinding flat while the men waited, and forced herself to do what her father's stories had trained her to do: not look harder, but look differently. She widened her focus until the pan became a shape, and saw that the shape was a funnel — the trails entered wide on the south side and must exit narrow on the north, funneled by the clay that guanaco would not cross except at its driest margin. She walked the northern rim, found the threads again within ten minutes, and heard Tomás exhale behind her like a boy released from a schoolroom.

"How did you know?" he asked, riding up alongside.

"I didn't know. I believed." She shrugged, uncomfortable with the theology of it. "My father used to say knowing is for towns. Out here you believe, and then you check, and then you believe a little further."

"He sounds like he was quite a man."

"He sounds like a man I argued with for ten years," Ingrid said, "and I would give both my hands for one more argument." She said it flatly, without self-pity, and saw Tomás absorb it the way young men absorb hard things — silently, and forever.

Toward evening the land began, almost imperceptibly, to argue in their favor. The scrub thickened. The coironé gave way in patches to greener growth — not green the way a meadow is green, but green the way a promise is green, a darkening at the tips of the neneo bushes that only a thirsty eye would register. A solitary bandurria, a bird that does not range far from water, lifted off from a stone and flapped away heavily, offended at being disturbed. Tomás saw it and said nothing, but he wrote it in his notebook, and Ingrid saw him write it, and understood that the boy was beginning to learn the second kind of surveying.

Orsen rode up beside her as the light went long and copper.

"You've done this before," he said. It was not a question, and it was not quite a compliment. It was a man recalibrating his map of another person.

"My father took me out when I was fourteen. One summer. He said if I was going to hate him properly I ought to know what I was hating him for." She kept her eyes on the trails. "We followed guanaco lines for three days to a spring that smelled of sulfur. I complained the entire time. He never said a word, just let me complain, and at the spring he drank first and offered me second, and I remember thinking — this is the most anyone has ever given me, and I will never tell him so."

"And you didn't."

"And I didn't." She glanced at him. "You understand that, I think. Giving without announcing it. Shorting your own water ration and hoping nobody counts."

Orsen's jaw tightened. For a moment she thought he would deny it, and she watched him decide not to, and that decision — small, tired, honest — did more to close the distance between them than anything in the weeks since Puerto Deseado.

"They'll need me counting again tomorrow," he said. "Rations. Whatever we find or don't find."

"Yes," Ingrid said. "Count. That's yours. But the finding—" she nodded ahead, to where the braided traces ran on into the dusk, drawing tighter, tighter, a rope of habit pulling them toward whatever the ground had promised, "the finding is mine tonight."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a low lava ridge, cold-hobbling the mules close because there was nothing for them to graze and everything to conserve. No fire. The stars came out in the fierce Patagonian way, crowded and indifferent, and Ingrid lay on her bedroll listening to the sounds of the night: mules breathing, Tomás murmuring in a sleep that already had graves in it — she had heard him talking in his sleep since the seep, names and numbers, *forty-one, forty-one* — and Orsen awake, as she knew he was awake, sitting his watch with his rifle across his knees and his eyes on the darkness to the southeast, where Ballesteros was, or was not, following.

She slept in pieces and woke before dawn to a sound she could not name, and lay still, and named it: birdsong. Faint, sparse, coming from the northwest — the direction of the braided trails, which in the first gray light she could see had tightened to a true path now, worn shoulder-wide in places, descending gently along the flank of the ridge toward a line of darker vegetation a few kilometers out.

She woke the men without ceremony. Orsen came to the light fast, rifle first, then relaxed by degrees when she pointed not at danger but at the path.

"Descends," she said. "All the way down. Water runs downhill, and so do guanaco trails at the end of their line. We'll know by midmorning."

They broke camp in the blue half-light and started down, and the land received them the way a sentence receives its final word. The scrub grew taller. The soil darkened, took on a smell — faint, mineral, unmistakable, the smell of damp earth in a country that had forgotten damp. A chingolo sang somewhere and was answered. The mules, sensing it before any human instrument could confirm it, quickened on their own, ears forward, and Tomás laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound — and Orsen reached over and gripped the boy's shoulder once, hard, and said nothing, because there was nothing that needed saying.

Ingrid rode at the head of the column down the last slope, and at the bottom, screened by a stand of molle shrubs, they found it: a seep, broader than the last one, a shelf of black basalt weeping a slow clear film of water into a basin of stones, fringed with rushes going gold, and around it — this was the detail that stopped her breath — around it, pressed deep into the mud at the water's edge, hundreds of prints. Guanaco, yes. But also sheep, decades old, crusted hard. And one print she knelt over for a long time, her heart going strangely, a boot heel with a broken nail, pressed into ancient clay and preserved like a fossil.

People had known about this water. People had drunk here. Her father's world, or the company's, or both, had passed this way once, long ago, and left only a heel-print and the sheep.

"It's good water," Tomás was saying, testing it, professional to the bone even with his voice shaking. "Clear. Barely mineral. Señor — Ingrid — it's *good*—"

"Fill everything," Orsen said, and his voice was rough. "Everything we have. And boil double rations for the mules tonight. We drink full, we wash, we rest a day. Miss Salas has bought us that much."

They worked, and the work was joy, the small sacraments of survival — filling barrels, stripping the bitter outer leaves from the rushes, letting the mules drink until their bellies rounded. Ingrid knelt at the basin and cupped water and drank, and it was cold and tasted of stone and of a very long patience, and she found she was crying without drama, the way the seep itself wept, just a steady quiet overflow, and she let it come because there was no one to perform strength for and because her father had found this place too, once, or places like it, and had carried the knowledge alone for fifty years, and she understood him a little better kneeling there, and forgave him a little more.

It was Tomás who found the thing that ended the rest.

He had wandered upstream along the seep's outflow, following his habit of mapping everything, and he called out — not alarmed, but puzzled — and they came to him through the molle scrub, and stopped.

A cairn. Small, old, tumbled partly down, built in the style she had learned to recognize on this journey, the style of Efraín Salas. But this one was different from the others. Beside it, driven deep into the caliche and rusted to a red lacework, stood an iron stake with a plate bolted to its face, and on the plate, legible under a century of corrosion, stamped letters:

**S.P.R. — POZO Nº 3 — 1901**

And below, smaller, almost obliterated:

**PROPIEDAD DE LA COMPAÑÍA — PROHIBIDO TOCAR**

Company property. Forbidden to touch.

Orsen crouched before the plate for a long time. When he straightened, his face had gone to the place it went when the past became personal — she had seen it at the ledger, at the cemetery, at the lake — but this time there was something new in it, something colder and more awake.

"Pozo number three," he said quietly. "The company marked its wells. All of them. Which means—" He turned and looked northwest, where the land rose in long swells toward a horizon that was no longer empty, because now they knew how to read it: the greener scrub, the converging game trails, the old sheep prints, all of it braiding together toward one point, the way the guanaco paths had braided, the way evidence braids when you finally follow the right thread.

"Which means there's a number one and a number two," Ingrid said.

"Which means," Orsen said, "that the company surveyed this water a year before Pozo Nuevo died of thirst — and marked it, and walked away." He picked up his hat from where he'd set it by the seep and put it on, and when he spoke again his voice had the flat calm she had learned to fear for other people's sake. "Pack everything. We rest one day, no more. Tomorrow we follow those trails to their end."

"And at the end?" Tomás asked.

Neither Orsen nor Ingrid answered. But the iron stake stood in the morning light with its prohibition rusting on its face, and forty kilometers to the northwest, at the end of the oldest survey lines in Patagonia, something waited that the company had ordered the world to forget — and had forbidden anyone, on pain of its displeasure, to touch.
