## 23. The Seep

Tomás Ferreyra had learned, in the nine days since the viento blanco buried the trail and two of their mules with it, that thirst was not one sensation but many. There was the first kind, the honest kind, a dryness in the mouth that water cured. Then there was the second kind, which arrived on the second day without water and lived behind the eyes, a pressure like a hand pressing gently inward from both temples. The third kind came later. It did not live in the body at all. It lived in the landscape, which began to lie.

He had stopped trusting his instruments on day seven. The barometer read a storm that never came; the compass swung lazily toward magnetic nothing; the chronometer — his father's chronometer, brass-cased, bought with death-money he had never once thought of as death-money until this journey — had begun losing seconds as though it too were tired. He carried it against his chest now, wrapped in wool, as if warmth might persuade it. It gained nothing from his affection. Nothing gained anything out here. That was the arithmetic of the dry side: everything spent, nothing returned.

But Ingrid had found the guanaco paths. That was the miracle he kept turning over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit but hoped wasn't. While he and Orsen had stood squinting at featureless horizon, arguing in low voices about dead reckoning and compounded error, she had simply walked fifty paces off their line, crouched, and said, *Here.* A thread of packed earth among the scrub, then another, then a dozen, all converging southeastward like the spokes of a wheel whose hub lay somewhere beyond sight. Animals do not walk to nowhere, she said. Animals walk to water.

So they followed her instead of him, and Tomás found he did not mind. Or rather: he minded, and the minding itself frightened him more than being wrong. A surveyor who resents being right is a surveyor already halfway to being useless.

* * *

They smelled it before they saw it — or rather they smelled what was not there, an absence of dust, a faint mineral coolness rising off the ground like breath from a cellar. The lava shelf appeared in the late afternoon as a long black shoulder of rock thrust up through the scrubland, its underside hollowed where ancient gas had bubbled out of cooling stone. Beneath the overhang the sand darkened, then glistened, then pooled into a shallow basin no wider than a wagon bed, ringed with a crust of white salt and green-black algae that looked, in the slanting light, almost like velvet.

"Brackish," Orsen said, kneeling, dipping two fingers, tasting. His face worked through a small private negotiation. "Salt. Alkaline. Drinkable in small amounts. Not pleasant." He looked up, and for the first time in days Tomás saw something in the older man's eyes other than the flat vigilance of command. Relief, maybe. Or the memory of relief. "It will keep the mules standing. It will keep us walking. It will not make either of us comfortable."

Ingrid was already down on her knees at the edge of the seep, cupping water in her hands, letting the first mouthful roll across her tongue without swallowing — testing it the way her father must have tested a hundred springs, Tomás thought, and the thought arrived with a strange tenderness. She spat, rinsed, tried again. "It tastes like drinking a church," she said. "Stone and old prayers."

The mules understood before the people finished negotiating. They crowded the basin, jostling, and had to be held back by halter and voice so they wouldn't churn the seep into mud. Orsen organized the watering like a stationmaster organizing departures: two animals at a time, three minutes each, buckets filled and set aside for the humans, everyone else standing back in the shade of the shelf where the rock threw a band of shadow as wide as a railway platform.

Tomás drank last, because he was youngest and because no one made him drink last and he wanted, obscurely, to have chosen it. The water was warm, metallic, faintly rotten with sulfur, and it was the finest thing he had ever tasted. He drank until his stomach sloshed, then filled the canvas bags, then sat back against the lava rock and felt the third kind of thirst begin, at last, to loosen its grip behind his eyes.

That was when the shivering started.

* * *

It began politely, as a chill that could be blamed on the evening. By full dark it was a conversation his body was having without him, teeth chattering while sweat stood on his forehead in the cold air. Orsen pressed the back of his hand to Tomás's neck and held it there a moment longer than necessary.

"You've been drinking from the stock barrels," he said. It was not quite an accusation. It was worse: a diagnosis delivered with the weariness of a man who has seen the same mistake kill better-prepared men. "The barrel water went bad two days ago. I told you to let me take the fouled share."

"I didn't taste anything wrong."

"No. You wouldn't, yet. Bad water is polite that way." Orsen sat back on his heels. "It's in your gut now. Tonight will be ugly. Tomorrow you ride, you don't walk. We'll boil everything from here on, even if it costs us fuel."

Ingrid appeared out of the dark with the kettle already steaming over a fire built small and smokeless, of dried scrub, fed stick by stick. She had boiled willow bark — or something she insisted was willow bark, gathered from a drainage they'd crossed at noon — and she pressed a tin mug into Tomás's hands and watched him drink it with the stern tenderness of a woman dosing a child. It was bitter enough to make his eyes water.

"My father used to say," she said, settling beside him with her back to the rock, "that the plateau doesn't try to kill you. It just stops trying to help you. Everything after that is your own business." She pulled the blanket higher around his shoulders. "You made it your own business very thoroughly, apprentice."

He wanted to answer with something clever. What came out instead was, "I'm sorry about the calculations."

Orsen, standing at the edge of the firelight with his hat in his hand, turned. "Say that again?"

"The drift. When we came out of the storm. I've gone back through my figures every night since. The chronometer was losing time — I knew it was losing time, I corrected for it, but I think I corrected in the wrong direction on the second day, and then every bearing after that inherited the error." His voice sounded strange to himself, thin and formal, the voice of a boy reciting a failure at school. "Nine days lost, señor. Nine days of desert because I wrote down a number and believed it."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire ticking and the mules shifting in their pickets. Then Orsen crouched down so that his face was level with Tomás's, and in the orange light Tomás saw that the older man's eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and entirely without anger.

"When I was twenty-two," Orsen said, "I signed a gradient profile for a spur line above Lago Argentino. I signed it because my chief told me to sign it. The grade was wrong. A locomotive went off it in the spring thaw. Two men died, and the inquiry asked me why I had certified a figure I hadn't verified myself, and I had no answer that anyone wanted to hear." He paused. "Twelve years ago. You know that much of the story, I think. Everyone on the dry side knows some version of it."

Tomás hadn't known. Or had known only the shape of it, the way everyone knew the shape — Nystrom the precise man, Nystrom the man who counted miles, and somewhere behind the counting a door that never opened.

"So hear me," Orsen went on. "An error in your figures is a wound. It bleeds, it scars, you learn where the edge is. But an error you hide — that's the infection. You gave me yours on the first night you suspected it. Do you understand what that's worth? Most men carry theirs to the grave, and the grave is often someone else's." He put a hand briefly on Tomás's shoulder, heavy as a rail iron. "Sleep now. Fever does its worst work in the dark, and we'll be here to see it doesn't get far."

* * *

The worst work did come in the dark.

Tomás dreamed, and the dreams were not dreams so much as relocations. He was at Pozo Nuevo again, except that in the dream the town was whole — whitewashed walls, the church bell swinging in a tower that in waking life lay half-buried in its own rubble, women beating blankets on lines strung between adobe houses. Forty-one people moved through the streets going about the ordinary business of an afternoon, and Tomás walked among them carrying his father's instrument case, and no one remarked on him, because in dreams the living and the dead share the road without comment.

Then the water failed. He watched it fail the way you watch weather change: the well rope coming up shorter each morning, the bucket scraping sand, the faces at the doors of houses turning first puzzled, then patient, then not patient. And the bell began to toll — though no one was ringing it — and each stroke of it was a name, and he knew the names, though he had never known them, forty-one names tolling out of bronze, and he was trying to write them down, trying to record them in his field book, but his pencil had no lead and the pages were caliche and the names would not stay written, they slid off the page like water off stone—

"—the third grave from the wall," he heard himself say, from a great distance, in a voice cracked and foreign. "The paymaster stayed. Someone should tell them the paymaster stayed—"

Hands on him. Cool cloth on his forehead. Ingrid's voice, close, saying his name the way you'd say it to bring a man back across a river. He surfaced. The stars were wrong — too many, too bright, arranged in constellations he didn't recognize, and then he blinked and they resolved into the southern sky he'd known all his life, and he was lying under a blanket against warm lava rock with Ingrid kneeling over him and Orsen standing beyond the fire with a rifle across his arms, watching the darkness past the shelf.

"You were talking," Ingrid said quietly.

"Was I."

"All night, near enough." She wrung the cloth over the sand. "About the town. About the graves. You counted them. You got to thirty-seven and started over twice."

Tomás lay looking up at the underside of the lava shelf, black and blistered, like the roof of a burned house. Shame rose in him, hot and specific. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to — I don't even remember the town properly. I saw it for half a day."

"That's how it works," Ingrid said. "My father carried it fifty years and never spoke it aloud. You carried it nine days and couldn't hold it in your sleep." She folded the cloth and laid it fresh across his brow. Her hand lingered a moment. "Neither of you chose it. It chose you. That's what a place like that does — it puts itself inside whoever comes, because it can't put itself anywhere else. No maps. No records. Just visitors."

"The paymaster," Tomás said. The word came up out of the fever like a cork surfacing. "In the dream. Grave forty-one, or the third from the wall — I couldn't tell if they were the same. He stayed with his crew. Everyone left and he stayed, and he kept the payroll open, and—" He stopped, because he realized he was describing something he had no way of knowing, and the not-knowing was worse than any of it. "Why do I know that? Señorita Salas, why do I know that?"

Ingrid was silent for a long time. Beyond the fire, Orsen had not moved, but Tomás saw the set of his shoulders change, the way a man's shoulders change when a sentence lands in an old wound.

"Because Efraín Salas was the paymaster's boy," she said finally. "And because grief is a current, Tomás. It runs underground, like the water. It comes up in strange places. Sometimes in strangers." She smoothed the blanket over his chest. "Drink this. Sleep if you can. If the dreams come back, let them come. They're only trying to deliver something."

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, which somehow felt more ominous than wind. Tomás woke weak but lucid, the fever broken or at least retreated to some garrison in his bones, and found the camp already in motion around him. Orsen had walked the rim of the lava shelf at first light and come back with his face carefully arranged into neutrality, which Tomás had learned, in ten days, to read as alarm.

"We're filling every container," Orsen announced. "Boiled and double-boiled. We rest the mules until midday, then move. Ingrid's paths run southeast toward a chain of salt flats — there may be more seepage along the fault line, or there may be nothing. Either way we cannot stay. This water is a loan, not a gift."

Tomás sat up slowly and took inventory of himself: legs present, head attached, dignity dented but recoverable. He helped fill containers because sitting still was worse than working, and no one ordered him to stop, which he understood to be the expedition's way of expressing confidence.

It was while he was crouched at the seep's edge, filling the last canvas bag, that he saw it. Not with instruments. With the plain eye, trained now by nine days of lying landscape to look for what didn't want to be seen: a glint on the southeastern horizon, at the limit of distance where heat shimmer dissolves hard edges. Metal. Moving. More than one point of it, in loose formation, keeping to the low ground the way men keep to low ground when they don't wish to be counted.

He did not call out. He marked the bearing against a notch of the lava shelf, memorized it, and walked — unhurried, he was proud of that, unhurried — back to where Orsen stood checking a mule's hoof.

"Señor Nystrom." He kept his voice level, pitched under the wind. "How good are company men at following guanaco paths?"

Orsen went very still. Then he looked southeast, along the line of Tomás's glance, and Tomás watched twelve years of discipline pass across the older man's face like cloud-shadow over a hillside — and settle, at last, into decision.

"Saddle up," Orsen said. "All of you. Now."
