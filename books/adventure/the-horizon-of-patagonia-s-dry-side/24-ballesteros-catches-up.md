## 24. Ballesteros Catches Up

The seep gave them what the desert would not: a thin, mineral-tasting trickle beneath a shelf of black lava, pooling in a basin no wider than a wagon wheel. It was not water in any way that flattered the word. It came up greenish, smelling of iron and old stone, and it had to be strained through Tomás's shirt before the mules would touch it. But it was water, and after nine days of arithmetic — two liters per person, less for the animals, every mouthful weighed against every mile — Orsen Nystrom watched it rise in the basin and felt something in his chest loosen that he had not known was clenched.

They had been at it since dawn. Ingrid worked the straining cloth while Tomás filled the canvas bags, and the two of them had developed a rhythm that needed no words: tilt, pour, wring, pass. The fever had burned down in Tomás to something like embers; he still talked in his sleep about the graves at Pozo Nuevo, forty-one names recited in an order only he knew, but by daylight he was himself again, or nearly, squinting at his instruments and muttering corrections.

Orsen stood apart, as he usually did, and did what he always did with water: he counted it. The basin refilled at roughly a liter every four minutes. They could fill everything they carried and still leave the seep running, which meant Ballesteros — if Ballesteros was still behind them — would find it too. That thought sat in him like a swallowed stone.

He had stopped pretending, somewhere back on the ninth day, that the riders were a guess. Twice he had climbed the low ridges east of their trail and seen dust where dust had no business being: a column of it, patient, moving at the pace of men who did not need to hurry because they knew the desert would do their work for them. Better horses. Better barrels. A wagon, probably, with spare shoes and spare rifles and a man whose job was patience.

"They'll reach us here," Ingrid said. She had come to stand beside him without his noticing, which happened more often now. She held a tin cup of the strained water and did not drink from it. "This is the only water between here and the Escalante. If they're following our trail, this is where the trail stops being ours."

"I know."

"We could be gone before dark. Push through the night, put twenty miles—"

"On half-filled barrels and mules that haven't rested?" He shook his head. "We'd break an animal by morning, and then we'd be three people and five mules carrying water for six. No. We drink, we fill, we rest until the moon is up, and we move."

"And when they come?"

Orsen looked out at the flat horizon, at the heat shimmer standing on it like glass. "Then we find out what they want," he said. "I don't think they want a fight. Not yet. A fight makes noise, and noise makes witnesses, and witnesses are the one thing the company can't afford." He said it with more certainty than he felt. "Ballesteros has been reasonable so far. Reasonable men make offers."

"Reasonable men buried a town," Ingrid said, and walked back to her work.

* * *

They came at the hour when the light goes long and gold and everything looks kinder than it is.

Orsen heard them first — not hooves, which the wind ate, but the creak of leather and the clink of a farrier's tools in a wagon bed, sounds that carry when the land carries nothing else. He was on his feet before the others understood why, and by the time the riders crested the eastern rise, the camp was already arranged in his mind: Tomás near the mules, Ingrid by the fire with a kettle in her hands as if she were nothing but a woman making tea, and himself standing in the open, empty-handed, where a man with a rifle could see there was nothing in his hands worth shooting over.

There were eight of them. He counted the way he counted everything, sleepers and liters and now guns: eight riders, one wagon, four long arms visible and probably four more under coats. They came down off the rise in good order, spread wide enough to look casual and close enough to close fast, and reined up thirty yards out, which was exactly the distance a careful man chooses — inside conversation range, outside knife range.

Señor Ballesteros rode at the front on a gray mare too fine for the country. He had shaved recently. That was the detail Orsen's mind seized on, absurdly, while the rest of him went cold: a clean jaw, a pressed collar, a man who had packed a razor across two hundred miles of plateau because a negotiation deserved respectability. He dismounted, handed his reins to a rider, and walked forward alone, palms slightly out, smiling the smile of a man arriving at a neighbor's fence.

"Señor Nystrom." His voice was warm gravel. "You've made excellent time. I confess I lost your trail for two days after the storm and feared you'd gone into the Escalante. That would have been a tragedy. That country kills better men than either of us."

"You followed well enough," Orsen said.

"Your muleteers left a trail a child could read, I'm afraid. Good men, those two. They took my money and my advice in equal measure." Ballesteros glanced past him at the seep, at the basin, at the straining cloth hung on a bush to dry. "Brackish, I'd guess. You look like men who've been drinking brackish water." His eyes moved on: Tomás, thin and watchful; Ingrid, still holding the kettle, her face arranged into perfect blankness; the mules; the packs. He did not stare at any pack in particular, and Orsen noted the discipline of that. A man who doesn't look at the thing he wants.

"What do you want, Ballesteros?"

"To talk. May I sit? My knees are fifty-four years old and the mare has opinions about hills." Without waiting for permission he lowered himself onto a flat stone near the fire, unhurried, and accepted — Orsen watched this happen with a kind of distant admiration — the cup of tea that Ingrid poured and handed him without being asked. She had decided, apparently, that the scene would go better if she played hostess, and she was right. Hospitality disarms. Orsen had learned that in hotel rooms all over the dry side.

For a while Ballesteros simply drank his tea and remarked on the weather, the mules, the quality of the light at this latitude, and let the silence do its slow work. Then he set the cup down, turned it once in his fingers, and said, pleasantly:

"The company knows you found Pozo Nuevo."

The name dropped into the camp like a stone into still water. Tomás went white. Ingrid's face did not change at all, which told Orsen she had been expecting it longer than he had.

"There are records," Ballesteros continued, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "Incomplete ones. A survey file from 1901 that was supposed to have been destroyed and wasn't, entirely. A payroll ledger that a dead prospector bought piece by piece over fifty years, God rest him, because grief makes collectors of strange men. And now, presumably, whatever you found in the ground out here." He looked up, and for the first time the pleasantness thinned, and Orsen saw the other man underneath, the one who had ridden a hundred miles to sit on a rock in the desert. "I'm not going to insult you by asking what you have. I'm going to assume you have everything. Efraín Salas was thorough, and thorough men raise thorough daughters."

Ingrid's knuckles whitened on the kettle handle. She said nothing.

"So here is what I've ridden out here to say." Ballesteros folded his hands. "The board met in extraordinary session eleven days ago. They know the situation is... delicate. They also know that you, Señor Nystrom, are not a radical, not a newspaperman, not a politician. You are an engineer. Twelve years of service. Twelve years of surveys nobody else could have done, on lines nobody else would walk. There are men on that board who signed your first appointment letter." He paused. "There is a man on that board who remembers your father."

Orsen kept his face still. Inside, something old and bruised turned over.

"The offer is this." Ballesteros counted it off on his fingers, gently, like a man laying cards face-up. "Reinstatement. Not to your old post — above it. Chief of surveys for the whole southern division, with the salary that implies. A pension accrued from the date of your original hiring, arrears included. Full safe passage for you, the young man, and Señorita Salas to Comodoro Rivadavia, escorted, provisioned, honored guests. The muleteers' silence-money stands. And in exchange—" he opened his hand, palm up, empty, "—the map, the ledger, the photographs if there are photographs, and your silence. Not forever. Five years. Five years of quiet, and then the company will open the lake itself, publicly, as a discovery of its own engineering department. There will even be credit in it for you. A reservoir named after a man is not nothing, Señor Nystrom."

The wind moved through the scrub. Somewhere behind Orsen, a mule shifted and stamped.

"Five years," Orsen said.

"Five years. During which the company compensates the descendants of the Pozo Nuevo workers — quietly, generously, through intermediaries who will call it a benevolent fund. Forty-one families. The board has already voted the sum." Ballesteros's voice softened. "You think I came out here to protect shareholders. I came out here because this is the best outcome anyone will ever get. Go to Buenos Aires with your papers and your photographs and what happens? Lawyers. Years. The charter suspended, the railway sold, the line closed, and every town from Deseado to the cordillera loses its freight and its coal and its telegraph while the courts argue. The lake sits under a locked plateau for a decade while the province thirsts. And you — you'll be famous for a season and unemployable for the rest of your life, and the forty-one will still be dead, and the water will still be underground." He spread his hands. "Or take five years of quiet, and the water flows, and the families are paid, and you spend those five years as chief of surveys with a pension waiting. Which of those serves the living?"

It was a good speech. That was the terrible thing about it. Orsen had spent twelve years around railway men and he knew a good speech when it was laid across him like a blanket, warm and heavy and meant to keep him still. Every number in it was probably true. The benevolent fund might even exist already, drafted in some lawyer's office, waiting. That was how the company worked — it never lied when the truth, properly arranged, would serve. It had surveyed a lake and reported none; it had capped a well and recorded a dry hole; every document true, every document arranged.

And part of him — he could not pretend otherwise, not with Ingrid ten feet away watching him — part of him wanted it. Not the title. The ending. The idea that there existed a version of this story in which nobody else bled, in which Tomás went home whole, in which the miles he had walked meant something official, stamped, forgiven. Twelve years ago he had lost his name over a matter the company had paid to forget, and he had spent every mile since trying to outrun it, and here was a man offering to give the name back, with interest, for the price of one more silence. One more. He had been keeping silences so long they fit him like his own coat.

He looked at Ingrid. She was looking at the fire, and her face was the face she'd worn in the cemetery at Pozo Nuevo — horror held very still — and he understood that she was not going to argue. She was going to let him choose, and live with whatever he chose, and that was worse than any argument.

"Until the next water," Ballesteros said, rising, brushing off his trousers. "That's my deadline, and I mean it plainly. There's a spring in the Escalante, three days hard ride from here. Reach it before me and take the southern fork, and I'll assume your answer is no, and I'll do what I'm paid to do, and none of us will enjoy it. Stop at the spring, send the boy out with a white rag and the map, and everything I've described begins tomorrow morning." He put on his hat. "You're a measuring man, Nystrom. Measure this: I've got eight rifles and full barrels, and you've got brackish water and a fevered boy. But I'd rather not use any of it. I've read your file. I don't think you're the kind of man who lets other people die for his principles." He smiled again, and it almost reached his eyes. "Prove me right. It's easier on everyone."

He mounted the gray mare. At the edge of camp he reined in once more and looked back, and his gaze found Ingrid.

"My condolences on your father, señorita," he said. "He was a stubborn man. It ran in the town, I'm told." And he turned and led his eight rifles back up the rise, and the gold light took them, and the dust of them hung in the air long after the sound was gone.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Tomás sank onto a crate by the fire, and his hands were shaking, and he wrapped them around a mug of tea he didn't drink. Ingrid set the kettle down with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it.

"He knew about the photographs," Tomás said finally. His voice came out cracked. "How does he know about the photographs? I never told anyone about the—"

"He guessed," Orsen said. "A surveyor's apprentice buys plates and chemicals in a provisioning house, people remember. It doesn't matter. He was listing everything he believes we have so we'd believe he already knows. It's the oldest trick in negotiation: name the price before the seller does."

"Was it true?" Tomás asked. "What he said. About the fund. About the families."

"Some of it. Probably most of it." Orsen crouched by the fire and fed it a stick of scrub, watching the flame take it. "That's what makes it dangerous. A lie you can see through is just weather. This is a door, and behind it is a room where everyone lives and nothing changes and you get your life back."

"And behind the other door?" Ingrid asked quietly.

"Behind the other door is the Escalante, and Ballesteros's rifles, and maybe none of us." He looked up at her. "I won't pretend I didn't want it. What he offered. For about a minute and a half I wanted it more than I've wanted anything in twelve years." He said it plainly, because he owed them that much, and because saying it out loud took some of its power away, the way naming a pain sometimes does. "But I measured it. That's the one thing I know how to do. And the arithmetic doesn't hold. Five years of quiet means five years of the company deciding what the truth is worth and paying it in installments it controls. The fund gets delayed. The lake stays theirs. And in year five, when the last witness is tired and scattered and bought, they write the history however they like, and Pozo Nuevo goes back to being nothing, and Efraín Salas spent fifty years buying a truth that his own daughter helped bury." He shook his head slowly. "No. I've been a silent man for twelve years. I know exactly what it costs. He's offering to buy it at a discount."

Ingrid came and sat beside him, close, not touching. "He said he'd rather not shoot us."

"He'd rather not. He will if he must. But he's right about one thing — he needs us to stop, not to die. Dead men don't hand over maps, and dead men's papers end up in newspapers. He wants us to choose the room with the warm blanket." Orsen stood, and his knees ached, and he looked east toward the darkening plain where the Escalante waited like a held breath. "So we don't stop. We fill every barrel tonight, we rest the mules until the moon clears the ridge, and we ride south through the dark and the cold, and we beat him to the spring."

"With what margin?" Tomás asked. "His horses are fresh. Ours are—"

"Our mules know this country and his horses don't. We travel light and he travels with a wagon. And we have something he hasn't accounted for." Orsen looked at Ingrid. "Her father taught her to read land that has no landmarks on it. When the trail runs out, she finds the next one. That's our margin."

Ingrid said nothing, but she straightened slightly, the way a person does when a weight is handed to them and they decide to carry it.

Tomás stared into the fire a moment longer. Then, quietly, almost to himself: "My mother's town drinks company water. If the charter falls, the pumps—"

"If the charter falls," Orsen said, "the water authority that replaces it will pump more, not less. That's what the lake is *for*, Tomás. Not for the board. For the towns." He put a hand briefly on the boy's shoulder. "Get the barrels filled. All of them. Tonight we count every liter twice."

They moved. The camp came alive around him — canvas rattling, mules complaining, Tomás at the seep with the straining cloth, Ingrid checking girth straps by feel in the failing light. Orsen stood a moment at the edge of the firelight, looking east, where the dust of eight riders had long since settled back into the plain.

Until the next water, Ballesteros had said. Three days. Maybe less, if the company men rode through the nights too, and they would; they were paid to.

Orsen checked his watch against the first star, wrote the figure in his head alongside the liters and the miles, and went to help load the mules.
