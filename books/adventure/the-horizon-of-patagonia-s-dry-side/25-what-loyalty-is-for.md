## 25. What Loyalty Is For

The fire was small by Orsen's order — no bigger than a hat brim, fed with dry ñire wood that burned hot and gave off little light. Ballesteros's camp sat two miles back along the seep's drainage, and on a plateau as flat as a held breath, a fire could be seen from farther than a man wanted to believe.

They ate in silence at first: boiled guanaco, tough and gamey, and the last of the flour fried into something Tomás called bread with an optimism that made Ingrid almost smile. The seep water they'd carried up in the canvas bags tasted of iron and sulfur, but it was wet, and wet was everything now. Four days to the next reliable water, if Orsen's arithmetic held. Five if it didn't.

He sat with his back against a mule pack, his ribs still stiff from the graze at the narrows, and watched the fire do its small work. Across the flames, Tomás turned his cup slowly, the way he did when he was building toward something. Ingrid sat apart, knees drawn up, her father's second letter folded into her jacket over her heart like a splint holding a broken rib in place.

Tomás set down his cup. "I've been thinking," he said, "about what happens after."

"After?" Ingrid didn't look up.

"When we get out. When this goes wherever it's going — the newspapers, the courts, whatever Señor Orsen means to do." Tomás's voice had that careful quality it took on when he was handling an idea heavier than his years. "I keep thinking about my mother's town."

Orsen knew it without being told. Puerto Santa Cruz country, a company coal town, the kind that grew up around a railhead like moss around a pipe. Half the plateau's towns were company towns; the other half wished they were.

"Everything there comes from the firm," Tomás said. "The water system — company built. The schoolhouse — company timber. My mother's pension when my father—" He stopped, adjusted. "When we needed it. It came through the company office, in a company envelope, signed by a company clerk who never once asked us a question beyond the form. There are four hundred people in that town who would not eat tomorrow if the Southern Patagonia Railway woke up dead. And you want to hand it to a newspaper."

"I want to hand it to the truth," Orsen said quietly.

"It's the same thing only if the truth doesn't cost anything." Tomás leaned forward, firelight moving on his young face. "You've seen what happens when a line closes. I've seen it too, sir — you taught me. Compañía leaves, and inside two years the town is dogs and rust. So tell me: forty-one men died in 1902 because of a decision somebody made in a boardroom. If we burn the company down for it, how many more than forty-one go hungry? Where does the arithmetic stop?"

Ingrid unfolded from her corner of the dark. She moved like her father must have moved, Orsen thought — economical, deliberate, nothing wasted.

"Let me see if I have your arithmetic right," she said. Her voice was level, which was worse than shouting. "A company kills forty-one men by capping their well so its coal port can drink instead. Your answer is that we should be quiet about it, because the same hands that drowned them feed other people, and we wouldn't want to interrupt the feeding."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? You're saying the murder is regrettable but the payroll is sacred. That the forty-one are a rounding error against four hundred."

"They're not a rounding error." Tomás was on his feet now, and the fire threw his shadow huge against the scrub. "Nobody's saying they don't matter. I'm saying they're *dead*, Ingrid. They've been dead for fifty years. Burning the company won't raise one of them. But it can bury four hundred living ones. You want to weigh grief against bread, and you've already decided which side of the scale matters because the grief is yours."

The silence that followed had edges. Orsen saw Ingrid take it — saw the words land where her father's death had already opened her — and saw her refuse to bleed in front of the boy.

"My grief," she said, very evenly, "is that I learned three weeks ago that my father spent fifty years carrying a town's death around in his chest and couldn't tell his own daughter because shame is heavier than water. That's mine. But the forty-one aren't mine, Tomás. They're nobody's. That's the whole crime. A company erased them so thoroughly that even their own grandchildren think they died somewhere else. Erasure is a kind of killing, and it doesn't end until someone says the names out loud."

"And if saying the names starves a town—"

"Then the town deserves to know who's been feeding it," Ingrid said. "That's all I'm saying. Not that it will be painless. That they deserve to choose with open eyes. Anything less — keeping them fat and ignorant because ignorance is comfortable — that's just the company's method performed with better manners."

Tomás looked at Orsen. It was the look of an apprentice asking a master to rule on a dispute of instruments — which reading was true, which error to correct. And Orsen found that he had nothing to give him.

Because here was the thing he had not said aloud in twelve years, the thing he kept below the waterline of himself like ballast: he owed the company everything, and he owed it a debt of the same coin twice.

The first time was simple. Don Aurelio Mendoza, chief engineer of the southern division, had pulled a nineteen-year-old chainman out of a ditch gang in 1908 and put a transit in his hands. "You count true, Nystrom," the old man had said, tapping the survey book against Orsen's chest. "Most men round off. Rounding off is how bridges fall. You count true and the firm will carry you anywhere." And the firm had. Twelve years of miles, of closed spurs and open desert, of a life measured in sleepers and stakes and kilometers of steel — a life that had shape and meaning precisely because the firm had given him the ruler to measure it with.

The second debt was darker. Because it was also Don Aurelio who had stood in the Puerto Deseado office eleven years ago, gray-faced, sliding the papers across the desk, and it was the firm's discipline — the same clean, exacting discipline that had made Orsen worth anything at all — that had taken everything else away. He had never told Tomás. He had never told anyone. He had walked out onto a decommissioned spur the next morning and begun counting sleepers because sleepers were honest, because a sleeper did not lie about what it was or pretend it had never been laid down, and because a man who measured things all day did not have to measure himself.

Loyalty, he thought now, watching the fire eat its small meal. Everyone in this circle claimed loyalty to something. Tomás to the company that fed his mother. Ingrid to the dead. Ballesteros — and Orsen believed this, had come to believe it out on the plateau watching the man work — Ballesteros to the institution itself, to the charter and the lie it floated on, because he genuinely could not imagine the world standing up without it. And Orsen? He had spent twelve years calling his loyalty to the firm by a nobler name. Discipline. Craft. Gratitude. But walking out here, following a dying man's map past the last sleeper into the company's oldest secret, he had begun to suspect the truth: his loyalty had been a wound dressed as a virtue. He had stayed faithful to the thing that broke him because staying faithful meant he hadn't deserved breaking. Because if the firm was good, then the disgrace was his, and a disgrace he could carry. It fit in a saddlebag. It traveled light.

And what would it mean, then, to betray the firm? Maybe it would mean finally admitting the firm had betrayed him first — not with malice but with the same cold arithmetic Tomás was defending, the arithmetic that weighed four hundred against forty-one and always found the dead lighter. Don Aurelio had given him a transit and taught him to count true. The firm had spent fifty years rounding off.

"Señor Orsen." Tomás again, gentler now. "You've been quiet. You know the company better than any of us. Tell her it isn't simple."

Orsen looked into the fire. The coals had gone the color of the caliche at Pozo Nuevo, the color of the crust they'd broken through above the buried lake, and he thought of the water down there in the dark, patient as testimony, waiting fifty years for someone to ask it a question.

"It isn't simple," he said at last. "But she's right."

Tomás's face fell the way a boy's does when a master rules against him.

"The company feeds towns," Orsen went on, slowly, feeling each word like a stake driven into ground he wasn't sure would hold. "It fed me for twelve years. I'm not going to stand here and call it a monster, because it isn't. It's a machine, and machines don't murder — they just don't notice. Forty-one men weren't killed, Tomás. They were *expensed*. Somebody in Buenos Aires balanced a column and Pozo Nuevo fell off the ledger, and every year since, the column has balanced again, and every year the missing men stay missing. That's what your mother's envelope is written on. Not evil. Arithmetic." He paused. "And I have loved that arithmetic my whole adult life, because arithmetic never asks what you did before you started counting."

Ingrid was looking at him now with an expression he couldn't read — something between recognition and warning, as if she'd heard the shape of the thing beneath the words and understood he wasn't ready to say it plainly.

"So what do we do?" Tomás asked. His voice was smaller. "If the truth starves people—"

"Then we make sure it feeds them instead," Orsen said. "There's a lake under this plateau big enough for a province. The company lied about it because admitting it meant admitting Pozo Nuevo, and admitting Pozo Nuevo meant admitting the survey was wrong, and the charter floats on the survey. But the lie and the water aren't the same thing. We can drown the lie and keep the water. Towns don't need the company, Tomás. They need what the company controls. That's a different animal, and it can be caught."

"And if it can't?" Tomás whispered. "If pulling the thread unravels the whole coat, and my mother is standing in the cold?"

Orsen had no answer that wasn't a promise, and he had learned long ago not to make promises he couldn't survey first. He reached over and put his hand on the boy's shoulder — the shoulder that had carried negatives of forty-one graves across a desert, that had flinched under rifle fire and held steady under a rockfall.

"Then we clothe her ourselves," he said. "That's what all of this is. That's why we're carrying it home instead of selling it."

Tomás nodded slowly, unconvinced but obedient, the way apprentices are, and went to check the mules' picket line. Ingrid stayed. For a while she said nothing either, and the two of them sat with the fire burning itself down to memory.

"You didn't tell him the rest," she said finally.

"No."

"About why the company's arithmetic is personal to you."

Orsen looked at her. The firelight was nearly gone; her face was mostly shadow and outline, and somehow that made it easier. "No," he said. "Not yet."

"He gave you something once, whoever he was. The man whose voice you use when you talk about the firm. And he took something too. I watched you decide not to say it tonight, and I recognized it, because I did the same thing with my father for twenty years." She rose, brushing grit from her skirt. "Shame keeps, Señor Orsen. But it doesn't stay buried. Ask the lake."

She walked to her bedroll, leaving him alone with the last coal and the wind coming off the plateau, thin and dry and full of distance.

Orsen sat a long time after the others slept, listening to the mules shift and the wind work the scrub, and he understood that the question had stopped being whether to betray the company. That question had been answered somewhere back along the trail — at the cemetery with its forty-one stones, maybe, or in the cavern where Tomás had wept at the sight of still black water, or possibly twelve years ago in a Puerto Deseado office, though he hadn't known it then. The question now was whether he could testify to his own ledger. Whether, when the lawyers in Buenos Aires peeled his life open looking for leverage, he would round himself off the way the firm had rounded off Pozo Nuevo — or count true.

Don Aurelio's voice, in memory: *Count true, Nystrom, and the firm will carry you anywhere.*

He realized he had been counting true for the firm for twelve years. It might be time to try counting true for himself.

He banked the fire and lay down, and was nearly asleep when Ingrid's hand closed on his shoulder — gentle, urgent, awake.

"Orsen," she whispered. "Wake up. Look at the picket line."

He rolled over. Down the slope, where the six remaining mules stood hipshot in the starlight, a lantern was moving — small, low to the ground, coming toward their camp out of the dark from the direction of Ballesteros's fires.

Someone was walking in.
