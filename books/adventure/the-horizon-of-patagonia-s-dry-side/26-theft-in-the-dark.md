## 26. Theft in the Dark

Tomás Ferreyra had never stolen anything in his life — not so much as a pencil from the survey office, not a fig off the tree that leaned over the neighbor's wall in his mother's courtyard, though he had wanted one every August of his boyhood and had stood beneath it some evenings with his hands in his pockets like a man at a funeral. So when the plan came to him, fully formed and terrible, in the blue hour before dawn while he lay awake listening to the mules shift their weight against the picket line, he did not recognize it as theft. He recognized it as arithmetic.

They were camped in the lee of a basalt rise two days south of the seep, close enough to Ballesteros's fire to smell their tobacco on the wind when it turned, far enough that the smoke was only a smudge against the stars. Four people now. Six mules, three of them thin enough that Tomás could count ribs by moonlight. And between Orsen Nystrom's saddlebag and the company men's rifles, the whole truth of the plateau rolled up in oilcloth and string.

He had thought about it for two nights before the answer arrived, and when it arrived it came with the calm of all bad certainties, settling over him like dew. The map had to go back. Not burned, not buried — *returned*. Carried across the dark ground to Ballesteros's camp and laid where the man would find it in the morning, along with whatever words Tomás could manage. Because Ballesteros would follow them forever otherwise. Because Orsen was still carrying the graze on his ribs like a second heartbeat, hot and slow, and Ingrid had stopped singing to herself at night, which frightened Tomás more than the rifles did. Because the photographs were safe, split among three packs, and the map was only paper after all — Efraín Salas's careful ink, fifty years of a dead man's patience, yes, but paper, replaceable by testimony, by the ledger, by forty-one names already riding toward Buenos Aires in Ingrid's memory and a journalist's cipher.

The map was a thing. The people were not things. He was nineteen years old, and this was the first equation of his adult life, and it balanced.

He got up quietly, the way you learn to get up around animals, moving with the wind instead of against it, and he knelt by Orsen's saddle.

* * *

Orsen slept the way Tomás imagined soldiers slept: on his side, facing out, boots within reach of his hand even now, even here, as if the habit had grown into the bone during twelve years of sleeping alone along closed lines where no one came and therefore anyone might. His breathing was long and even. His hat covered his face. The saddlebag sat against a stone, its buckle turned inward, because Orsen was careful about everything except the possibility that the danger was inside his own fire.

Tomás's hands shook. He noticed this with a kind of distant professional interest, the way he'd once noted that a level bubble drifted left in cold weather, and he pressed his palms flat against his thighs until the shaking slowed. He unbuckled the strap. The leather whispered. Somewhere out on the plain a fox called twice, high and thin, and Tomás flinched so hard he bit his tongue, and then laughed at himself without any sound at all, a silent convulsion of guilt, and went on.

The oilcloth roll was there, under the spare shirt and the tin of tea, wrapped and tied exactly as Orsen always tied it, with the knot doubled against rain. It weighed almost nothing. That was the obscene part — fifty years of Efraín Salas's life, forty-one graves, an entire town struck from the world, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread. Tomás held it and felt the weight of it anyway, a different weight, the kind instruments couldn't measure, and for one moment — one long, cold moment in which the stars seemed to lean down and look at him specifically — he nearly put it back.

Then he looked across the dark at the smudge of Ballesteros's fire, and he heard again the rifle shot at the narrows, and he saw in memory the guide folding sideways off his mule with a sound Tomás had not known a body could make, and his resolve came back to him like blood into a numb limb, painful and warming.

He replaced the roll with something of equal bulk — his own spare shirt, folded small, wrapped in a square of oilskin he cut from the lining of his coat with his pocketknife, working blind by feel. If Orsen opened the bag tomorrow and reached in casually, his hand would find cloth and oilskin and be satisfied. It would hold until the next water hole. Until the parley. Until whatever Tomás could arrange in the morning light, alone, walking out under a white flag of his own making.

He retied the buckle. He stood. He walked out of camp with the map inside his jacket, against his chest, where he could feel his heart hammering at it like a fist on a door.

* * *

The ground between the two camps took an hour to cross, and Tomás spent the hour learning things about himself he did not want to know.

He learned that fear has a taste, coppery, like sucking on a coin. He learned that the plateau at night is not empty but crowded — with wind working through the scrub, with stones ticking as they cooled, with the enormous patient silence of a land that had watched men lie to each other for fifty years and had kept every secret and forgiven none of them. He learned that he could walk a straight bearing by dead reckoning with his eyes half-closed, counting paces, correcting for drift, and that this skill — the skill Orsen had drilled into him since their first week together, *the land lies, lad; the numbers don't* — was the very skill he was using to betray the man who taught it.

That thought stopped him mid-stride, out in the open, exposed on a swell of gravel with the sky enormous above him.

Because that was the unbearable shape of it: he wasn't betraying Orsen at all. That was what he kept coming back to, turning it over like a stone looking for what lived underneath. Everything Tomás had — the trade, the field craft, the strange new vocabulary of gradients and grades and water-tables that had made his mother cry with pride when he wrote home about it — everything had come from Orsen Nystrom's hands. And those same hands, Tomás believed with his whole chest, were going to get all of them killed, because Orsen had made loyalty into a religion and then converted, late and violently, to the church of truth, and now he was trying to serve both gods across a desert with a wounded side and four mouths to feed, and the mathematics did not work. Tomás had run the numbers every way he knew. Someone was going to die. Probably more than someone. The muleteer at the narrows was only the first installment.

But if the map went back — if Ballesteros got what the company wanted, if the pursuit ended, if they were allowed to walk out of this with their lives and their negatives and their story intact — then everyone lived, and the truth lived too, because the truth had never been in the oilcloth. The truth was in the graves at Pozo Nuevo, in the payroll ledger, in the photographs Tomás had been taking since the day he'd stood among those forty-one stones with his camera sweating in his hands and understood that he was holding the only witness that couldn't be bought, shot, or lost in a sandstorm.

He wasn't stealing the map, he told himself, walking again. He was rescuing it from being worth anyone's life. He was paying a ransom with counterfeit money — giving up the symbol to save the substance.

It sounded right. It kept sounding right, the way a lie keeps sounding right when you repeat it fast enough, and somewhere underneath both sounds was another one, quieter, that said simply: *you are afraid, and you are nineteen, and you want your mother, and you cannot watch another person die.*

He let that quieter voice speak too. He figured it had earned it.

* * *

Ballesteros's camp was strung along the base of a dry wash, four fires banked low, six men visible in the ember-light, horses hobbled beyond them. A sentry sat propped against a rock near the eastern approach, rifle across his knees, hat tipped forward — asleep or performing sleep, impossible to tell.

Tomás stopped thirty meters out, behind a shelf of rock, and considered.

His original plan — the plan he'd rehearsed for two days — had been simple to the point of childishness: leave the map on a stone at the edge of their camp, weighted with a note, retreat, done. But standing there now, close enough to see the coffee pot hanging over the nearest fire, close enough to hear one of the men mutter in his sleep, the plan curdled. Because a map found on a stone proved nothing except that someone had been within rifle range of their camp in the night, and Ballesteros was not a man who would read that as surrender. He would read it as clumsiness. He would tighten the net.

No. If this was going to work, it had to work as an act of faith. It required a face. It required him.

He stepped out into the open, hands raised, and said, in a voice that cracked on the second word, "Señor Ballesteros. It's Tomás Ferreyra. I've come to talk."

Every dog-quiet thing in the night broke at once. Men rolled upright, grabbing for rifles; the sentry came off his rock with astonishing speed; a horse screamed somewhere and kicked its hobble. Torches coughed alight. And through it all Tomás stood with his arms out and his heart going like a telegraph key, saying, "Don't shoot — please — don't shoot — I'm alone — I've brought something."

Ballesteros emerged from his tent pulling on his coat, unhurried, gray-bearded, his eyes moving over the darkness behind Tomás with the thoroughness of a man checking a ledger. "Alone," he repeated. "Boy, do you understand how many ways that can be a lie?"

"I understand," Tomás said. "Search me. All of me. There's no one out there. Señor Orsen doesn't know I've come. Nobody knows." He took the oilcloth roll from inside his jacket and held it out in both hands, and the gesture was so absurdly formal, so much like a schoolboy presenting an essay, that one of the riflemen actually lowered his weapon a few degrees and looked embarrassed.

Ballesteros came forward slowly and took it. He turned it over once, weighing it, reading the string, the doubled knot, the grease-stained oilcloth, and Tomás watched recognition move across the older man's face like cloud-shadow across the meseta.

"Salas's map," Ballesteros said softly. "The old paymaster's brat finally paid his debts." He looked up. "Why?"

"Because it isn't worth dying for," Tomás said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Any of us. You, us, your men. It's paper, señor. Fifty years of paper. What we have is bigger than paper and it's already gone past you — it's on a wire to Buenos Aires, or it will be, and burning us won't unsend it. Take the map. Call it even. Let us ride home."

The fires popped. One of Ballesteros's men shifted, and the sound of it was very loud.

"And Orsen Nystrom knows nothing of this?" Ballesteros asked again, and there was something in the question that Tomás would spend the rest of his life unpacking — not triumph, not suspicion exactly, but a kind of tired professional curiosity, a man who had spent decades buying men and was genuinely interested to meet one selling himself cheap.

"He'll hate me," Tomás said. "When he finds out. He'll probably never forgive me. But he counts miles, señor, and I count people, and somebody on this expedition has to do the second sum." His voice broke again, and this time he let it. "There's a fever coming on him from that graze. I've seen it start. If we keep running and fighting, he dies on this plateau and the map dies with him and you lose everything anyway, and my mother gets a letter, and your men bury a stranger. Nobody wins. I'm offering the only version where somebody wins."

For a long moment Ballesteros said nothing. Then he rolled the map open a hand's width, glanced at the inked line running across the meseta, and closed it again, and Tomás watched him arrive at the same understanding that had come to Tomás in the dark hours — that this object was a decoy now whether it meant to be or not, because the real evidence had already slipped its cage.

"You're either the most honest young man I've met in this business," Ballesteros said at last, tucking the roll under his arm, "or the best liar, and I genuinely cannot tell which frightens me more." He gestured, and the rifles went down fully, all around the circle. "Go back to your camp, boy. Tell them nothing. We'll see what the morning thinks of your arithmetic."

Tomás turned and walked away into the dark, and it was only when the torchlight fell behind him and the plain took him back that his legs began to shake properly, great shuddering waves of it, so that he had to stop and stand with his hands on his knees in the middle of Patagonia, laughing and crying in the same breath, a thief who had given the thing away, a traitor who had never loved anyone more than the people he had just betrayed.

* * *

He was twenty minutes back, moving slower now, letting the fear drain out through his feet into the cold ground, when he saw the figure standing at the edge of their own camp.

Not sitting. Standing. Facing outward, into the night, exactly where a man would stand who had woken, reached into his saddlebag, and understood in one touch of folded cotton and cut oilskin what had happened in his sleep.

Orsen Nystrom did not call out. He did not raise a lamp. He stood with his hat in his hands — Tomás had never seen him without his hat outdoors, not once in a year and a half — and waited, and the waiting was worse than shouting, worse than a rifle, worse than anything, because it gave Tomás every step of the approach to rehearse his confession and discard it, rehearse and discard, until he arrived in front of the old engineer with nothing prepared at all.

"Where is it?" Orsen asked. Quietly. The wind took the edges off the words.

"I gave it to Ballesteros." Tomás's voice came out small and flat, a child reporting a broken window. "Just now. Tonight. I walked it over. He has it."

The silence stretched. Out on the plain, the fox called again, twice, and neither of them moved.

"You went out," Orsen said at last, "past a sentry, in the dark, alone, to hand the one thing that man wants most directly into his hand." He was not angry. That was the horror of it. He sounded like a man reading a survey report, confirming elevations. "And you did this why? Say it plainly. I'll know if it isn't plain."

"Because you're bleeding inside and won't say so. Because Ingrid has stopped sleeping. Because we've got six mules and four guns against theirs and no water margin for a battle. Because—" Tomás's throat closed, and he forced it. "Because at Pozo Nuevo I stood in that cemetery and I swore that no one else was going to die for a piece of paper. Even if the piece of paper was right. Even if it was *ours*. Señor — I did it to protect everyone. I swear to God I did it out of love."

Something moved behind Orsen's eyes — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever — but recognition, the terrible recognition of a man hearing his own younger voice argue an older man's mistake. Behind him, a bedroll rustled. Ingrid sat up, hair loose, eyes finding the two silhouettes against the starlight, and Tomás saw her go still in the way she went still at gravesides.

"The photographs," Orsen said. It was not quite a question. It was an audit.

"Safe. Split three ways. She has some, you have some, I have some. The map was always just the door, señor. The house is still ours."

Orsen put his hat back on, slowly, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register Tomás had learned to dread on the survey lines, the flat calm of a decision already made.

"Then saddle the mules, boy. Both of you. Because Ballesteros has the map now — and the map shows him the lake, and the lake shows him the one place on God's earth where a man can drown the truth with a shovel and a barrel of concrete. He won't wait for morning. Neither will we."

Out toward Ballesteros's camp, faint on the wind, came the sound of a whistle — two notes, then three — and hooves beginning to move.
