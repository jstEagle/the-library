## 27. Pursuit

Tomás had not planned to be a thief.

He told himself that all through the first hour of the ride, and then he stopped telling himself anything at all, because the wind off the plateau was in his teeth and his thoughts kept shredding like paper in it. The oilcloth map lay against his chest, folded into quarters inside his shirt, warm from his own body, and every time the mule stumbled on the moonlit scree he felt it shift against his ribs like a second heartbeat — a guilty one, a borrowed one.

He had taken it an hour after midnight, while Orsen slept with his bandaged ribs rising and falling too fast and Ingrid sat the last watch with her back to him, her face turned toward the black line of the escarpment where Ballesteros's fires had burned earlier and now did not. He had knelt at Orsen's saddlebag and his hands had known exactly what they were doing, which was somehow worse than if they had shaken. He had lifted the map out and put the folded survey sheets of the new water authority — blank ones, worthless ones — into the space it left, so that the bag would not feel empty to a man who measured everything. It was a stupid kindness. He knew that even as he did it. But his hands had wanted to leave something behind, some proof that he had not simply stolen, that he had *traded*, that there was still a transaction between himself and the people who trusted him.

The mule's ears swiveled back at him. Tomás leaned forward and pressed his cheek briefly against its dusty neck.

"You don't understand," he whispered to it. "Nobody understands. That's the whole point."

Because here was what Tomás Ferreyra knew, the thing he had been carrying since Pozo Nuevo, since the forty-one graves with their dates like a row of pulled teeth: Ballesteros was not going to stop. Orsen thought he was; Orsen believed in the arithmetic of pursuit, that men with rifles and wagons would weigh distance against reward and turn back when the numbers went bad. But Tomás had watched Ballesteros's face across the firelight at the parley by the seep, and he had seen something Orsen's eyes had slid past because Orsen was watching the rifles. Ballesteros had not looked angry. He had looked *afraid*. And afraid men with companies behind them do not turn back. They do whatever the company needs done, because the alternative is becoming the next thing the company needs forgotten.

And Tomás's father had been such a thing once. That was the other thing he carried, the thing he had never said aloud, not to Orsen, not to Ingrid, not to his mother in her kitchen in Gaiman where she still set out a cup for a man eleven years dead. His father, Aurelio Ferreyra, brakeman, third class, had died on a night in June when a cut of ore cars ran away down the grade above Comodoro and the company's investigation had lasted four days and concluded, in language Tomás could recite from memory because he had read the file through a clerk's keyhole at seventeen, that "operator error contributed materially to the incident." There had been no incident. There had been a brake shoe worn past tolerance, reported twice in writing by Aurelio Ferreyra himself, both reports missing from the file, both replaced by a single sentence about operator error. The company had paid his mother a settlement that bought the house and the silence together, one price, no itemization. Tomás had been eight years old. He had learned early that the railway was a machine that fed towns and also ate them, and that you could live your whole life inside its shadow without choosing which.

He had chosen, though. That was the thing. When Orsen had come to the provisioning house and asked for a young surveyor eager for field work, Tomás had signed on partly for the miles and partly because Orsen Nystrom was famous for being straight — straight lines, straight answers, a man who had walked out of some old disgrace nobody spoke of and rebuilt himself out of measurement. Tomás had wanted to stand near that. He had wanted to become the kind of man whose instruments did not lie.

And then the instruments had begun to lie, or the land had begun to lie to the instruments, and then there had been the cairn and the ledger with forty-one names, and then Pozo Nuevo itself, the bell half-buried in sand like something the earth was trying to swallow before anyone heard it ring, and the cemetery with its forty-one graves dated 1902, and Tomás had stood among them with his camera — his father's camera, the folding Kodak Aurelio had won in a raffle three months before he died, the only inheritance that came without conditions — and he had photographed the graves because photographing them was the only prayer he knew.

He had been photographing everything since. He told himself it was documentation, evidence, the workmanlike instinct of a surveyor's apprentice. But part of him, the eight-year-old part, understood that he was building the file his father never got. A true file. One where the reports stayed in.

So when Ballesteros had sat across the fire and offered reinstatement and pension and safe passage for the map and silence, Tomás had done the arithmetic differently from everyone else. Orsen heard temptation. Ingrid heard insult. Tomás heard a door — a narrow, ugly, humiliating door, but a door, the only one in sight. If the map went back, the pursuit ended. Nobody else bled. The muleteers were already gone with their silence-money; the decoy pack had already cost them one ambush and a graze along Orsen's ribs that had gone bad-smelling by morning despite Ingrid's poultices. Every day they carried the map was a day someone could die for it. And Tomás had done the sums on death already, standing in the cemetery at Pozo Nuevo. Forty-one. He knew how quickly a number like that could accumulate when a company decided a truth was more expensive than a town.

He would give the map back. He would tell Ballesteros nothing else — not the lake's exact position, not the negatives, not the ledger hidden in the pannier under the salt pork. Just the map. Buy the road home with it. Let the photographs do the work the map couldn't, later, in a courtroom, in a newspaper, wherever truth went to be weighed by people who weren't shooting at it. It was a plan. It was maybe even a good plan. It was also theft from two sleeping people who had fed him and taught him and bled beside him, and as he rode north by the stars with the map against his heart, Tomás found that the plan and the theft refused to fit inside him at the same time, and so he rode with a hollow where his certainty used to be, and the mule's hooves struck sparks of flint in the dark, and the plateau rolled away on either side like a sea that had died standing up.

* * *

They caught him at the dry wash an hour before dawn.

He heard them before he saw them — two sets of hooves behind him, moving fast, no attempt at quiet, and he knew in his stomach before he turned that it was Orsen and Ingrid, because only they would come alone. Ballesteros would have sent six. Tomás turned the mule and stood in the sandy channel of the wash, the banks rising waist-high on either side, the stars enormous and indifferent overhead, and waited, because there was nowhere to go that two riders couldn't follow.

Orsen came down the bank first, and even in the starlight Tomás could see the wrongness of him — riding stiff, one arm pressed to his side, his face gray under its weathering, a man running a fever on horseback through the coldest hours of the night because a boy had robbed him. Ingrid came down after, and she did not look gray at all. She looked like the telegraph operator he'd first imagined her to be, all wire and current, and when she swung down from the saddle she crossed the wash floor at a walk, which was somehow more frightening than a run.

"Give it to me," she said.

Tomás's hand went to his chest without his permission. "Ingrid—"

"*Give it to me.*"

"It's not—" He made himself take his hand away. Made himself stand straight, the way his father had stood when he came home smelling of coal oil and explained things patiently to an eight-year-old. "It's not what you think. I'm taking it *back*. To Ballesteros. That's all. Just the map, just the map, and he lets us go — all of us — and nobody else gets shot. Señor Nystrom, tell her. You know the numbers better than anyone. We can't outrun them to the frontier. We can't outlast them on this water. The map is the only coin we have and I'm spending it before somebody dies holding it."

For a long moment Orsen said nothing. He sat his mule in the bottom of the wash with his hat pushed back, and Tomás saw him breathe carefully, shallowly, around the wound, and saw something worse than anger in his face — disappointment, the specific kind a teacher feels, which is heavier than any rifle.

"You went through my saddlebag," Orsen said quietly.

"Yes."

"While I slept. While Ingrid kept watch over both of us."

"Yes." His voice cracked on it. He hated the crack. "I left papers in its place. I wasn't — I didn't want you to think—"

"That you stole from me?" Orsen almost smiled, and it was the worst expression Tomás had ever seen him wear. "What would you call it, Tomás? Help me find the word. You're the one with the education."

"I'm trying to save our lives!"

"You're trying to buy back your fear," Ingrid said. "It's different."

She was close now, close enough that he could see her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, grief ridden hard and converted to fuel, and Tomás felt the argument he had built all night — the careful, reasonable, arithmetic argument — begin to slide apart in his hands like wet rope.

"Ballesteros will kill us anyway," he said. "You know that. He said we'd never reach a court alive. So the map buys nothing unless we spend it. Unless we spend it *now*—"

"And then what?" Ingrid stepped right up to the mule's head, and the animal shifted nervously, and she put a hand on its cheek to steady it, gentler with the animal than with him. "Then we go home with nothing. No lake. No ledger. No names. Forty-one people stay dead in an unmarked town forever, and my father spent fifty years buying back the truth piece by piece, and I burn the last piece of him to buy three train tickets." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about Ingrid Salas; she got quieter as she got more dangerous. "Is that your engineering solution? Because I've read your numbers, Tomás. Your numbers are always beautiful and always wrong."

"They're not wrong," he said, and heard the whine come into it and couldn't stop it. "They're *practical*—"

"The well at Pozo Nuevo was practical," she said. "Capping it. Rerouting the aquifer. Somebody sat in an office in Buenos Aires and worked it out with figures just like yours, and it saved the port money, and it killed a town, and every man who signed it probably told himself he was being practical." She took her hand off the mule. "My father wrote me a letter I never answered. Fifty years, and he couldn't send it until he was dying, because shame is heavier than water — that's what he wrote, word for word. And you want to hand the shame back to the men who made it. You want to make his fifty years worth nothing so you can sleep tonight."

"I need to sleep sometime!" It burst out of him, ugly and loud, echoing off the wash banks. "We all do! I'm nineteen years old, I'm the youngest one here, I've been shot at and lost in a sandstorm and I dug my fingers into caliche over a lake that isn't supposed to exist, and every night I close my eyes I see those graves, all forty-one, in rows, dated, *dated*, like the company at least had the decency to number them — and you two carry it like it's yours to carry, like grief is a job title, and I never asked for any of it! I signed on to survey a line! I signed on for field work!"

The echo came back off the banks and died, and the three of them stood in the ringing silence it left, and Tomás realized he was shaking, and realized also that he had said too much and not nearly enough, and that the map was burning against his chest like a coal.

Orsen dismounted. Slowly, badly, one-handed, refusing help that Ingrid offered anyway. He came and stood in front of Tomás in the sand, close, looking up slightly because Tomás had grown taller than him sometime during the expedition without either of them noticing.

"You took it," Orsen said, "because you believe giving it back protects us. Say it plainly. All of it. Why."

"Don't," Tomás said. "Please don't make me—"

"Why."

And it was the word, the plain flat measuring word, Orsen's word, the word he used on rivers and grades and distances, that broke it open. Not anger. Measurement. Asking for the true figure.

"My father worked for the company," Tomás said. The words came out of him like teeth being pulled, one at a time. "Aurelio Ferreyra. Brakeman, third class, out of Comodoro yard. Eleven years ago. There was a runaway cut on the grade above the port and he went down the hill after it on a handcar to throw the switch at the bottom, because if it reached the curve it would have gone through the coaling stage with forty men on shift, and he threw the switch, and it took the handcar with it." His voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, and he let it. "The company report said operator error. My father filed two written reports about that brake shoe. Both of them disappeared. My mother got a settlement and a clause, and the clause said she didn't talk, and she signed it because there were three children, and I was the oldest, and I was eight." He looked up at Orsen, and his eyes were streaming now, hot and sudden, the tears of someone who has not cried in years and is furious about crying. "So when I stood in that cemetery at Pozo Nuevo with his camera in my hands, I understood Efraín Salas completely. Shame is heavier than water. I know exactly how heavy. I've been carrying it since I was eight years old, and I thought — I thought if I gave the map back, if I made the deal, if I ended it — I thought I was protecting you. But that's not true. That's not the true figure, señor. The true figure is that I am tired of being the son of a man the company erased, and I would rather hand them the map than watch them erase two more people and add me to the list of sons who let it happen."

The wash held the silence. Somewhere up on the rim a fox screamed once, thin and lonely, and was gone.

Orsen reached out and gripped Tomás by the back of the neck, the way an older man grips a younger one, firm and unceremonious, and pulled his forehead down until it rested against his own, and held it there. Tomás felt the fever heat coming off him, and the roughness of his beard, and beneath it all a steadiness like a rail driven home.

"Aurelio Ferreyra," Orsen said quietly. "I remember the name. I read the file. Eleven years ago, when I still had access." He pulled back and looked at him. "I was already out by then. But I read it. And I knew it was a lie, and I did nothing, because doing something cost, and I had paid enough." His voice roughened. "You've been carrying my sin too, boy, along with your own. That's why it weighs so much."

Tomás made a sound that was not a word. Ingrid came around the mule's head and put her arms around both of them, awkwardly, the map crushed between their bodies, and the three of them stood in the dry wash in the dark, holding each other up, while the stars wheeled toward morning and the wind moved the sand in long whispers across the banks.

After a long time, Ingrid stepped back. Her face was wet but her voice had found its level again.

"He'll be waiting for you," she said. "Ballesteros. You told him you'd come?"

Tomás wiped his face with his sleeve, and nodded, and hated himself, and nodded again. "Before the sun clears the rim. At the fork below the red buttes. He said — he said if I came alone, no one follows me in."

Orsen looked east, where the sky had begun, very faintly, to bruise gray at its lowest edge. Then he looked at the map, still crushed between them, and held out his hand — not to take it.

"Then we don't waste the meeting," he said. "Give me the oilcloth, Tomás. And get out your father's camera."
