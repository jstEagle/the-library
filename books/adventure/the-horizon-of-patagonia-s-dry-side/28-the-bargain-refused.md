## 28. The Bargain Refused

The water hole was not a hole at all but a shallow pan of clay and brackish seepage where the ground remembered a river that had died before any of them were born. The mules drank with the desperate patience of animals that had learned not to trust water, their nostrils working, their ears swiveling toward the rim of the basin as though the horizon itself might be lying to them.

Orsen let them drink first. That was arithmetic too — an animal that refused water was worth nothing, and an animal that drank badly told you something about what it smelled on the wind. He crouched at the edge of the pan while they drank, watching the surface for the tremor of distant hooves, and saw nothing but his own face looking back at him: thinner than it had been three weeks ago, gray at the temples in a way he hadn't noticed before, and wearing an expression he did not like — the expression of a man rehearsing.

Because Ballesteros would come. Of course he would come. A man who offered a pension and reinstatement at a seep in the middle of nowhere was a man who intended to collect an answer, and Señor Ballesteros struck Orsen as the sort of man who kept appointments the way other men kept ledgers — meticulously, and with an eye to the bottom line.

"You're going to say yes."

Ingrid's voice came from behind him, low, pitched under the wind. She had filled the canvas bags and now stood wringing one of them out, her hands red from the cold water, her eyes on him with that directness she had inherited from no one he'd ever met — certainly not from Efraín Salas, who had spent fifty years perfecting the art of saying nothing.

"I haven't decided," Orsen said.

"That's a lie." She hung the bag over her shoulder. "You've been deciding since yesterday. I watched you do it. You sit differently when you're deciding. You get very still, like a man listening for a train."

He almost smiled. Twelve years of walking rail lines and she had learned to read him in three weeks. "And how does a man about to say yes sit?"

"Like a man about to say no," she said. "But sorry about it."

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a basalt shelf that broke the wind into two streams and gave them a pocket of still air the size of a chapel. Tomás hobbled the mules and built a small fire of scrub — small because smoke carried, and all of them knew by now exactly how far smoke carried across flat country. Ingrid boiled tea that tasted of iron and clay. Nobody spoke much. The conversation they were waiting for sat among them like a fourth traveler, taking up room, drinking their water.

Tomás broke first. He was nineteen and had never yet learned to let silence age properly.

"Señor Nystrom. If you take it — the pension, the reinstatement — what happens to us? To me? He said safe passage for *all* of us. Does that mean—" He stopped, turned his tin cup in his hands. "Does that mean we go back to pretending?"

"It means whatever I agree to," Orsen said. "That's what a bargain is. One man's word covering everyone under it."

"And if your word covers something rotten?"

"Then my word is rotten," Orsen said quietly, "and so am I."

Tomás looked at him then with an expression Orsen recognized and hated, because he had worn it himself once, long ago, standing in a stationmaster's office in Puerto Deseado with a report in his hand and a superior's hand on his shoulder, being told — kindly, reasonably, in the tone men use when they are doing you a favor — that some things were better left unsurveyed. The boy was asking him to be the kind of man the boy needed him to be. It was a terrible weight to put on anyone, and Orsen had been carrying its twin for twelve years without once setting it down.

Ingrid said nothing at all. She sat with her back against the basalt, her father's oilcloth map folded in her lap — she had taken to keeping it near her since the theft and the recovery, since Tomás came back to them crying at the dry wash, since the three of them had become the kind of unit that could survive a night of shared grief — and her thumb moved slowly along its folded edge, back and forth, back and forth, like a woman counting beads.

The light went. The stars came out in the brutal quantity that only the dry side produced, whole armories of them, and the temperature fell the way it always fell, as though the plateau were exhaling everything it had borrowed during the day.

They heard the riders an hour after full dark.

* * *

Ballesteros came into the firelight with four men and left two on the rim, which was either courtesy or arithmetic, and with Ballesteros it was always arithmetic. He had shaved. That was the detail Orsen noticed first and could not stop noticing — the man had ridden two days across broken country to a brackish seep, and he had shaved, and his coat was brushed, and he dismounted as though arriving at a club in Buenos Aires rather than the end of the world. It was a performance, and it was meant to be read as one. *I am a man who controls circumstances,* the shave said. *Even here.*

"Señor Nystrom." He extended his hand. His gloves were clean. "You found water. I confess I had my doubts about this particular hole. My maps show it dry most years."

"Your maps show a great many things," Orsen said, and did not take the hand.

Ballesteros withdrew it gracefully, converting the refusal into a gesture toward the fire, as though he had merely been indicating how pleasant the flames were. "May I?" He sat without waiting for permission, which was its own answer. One of his men brought a leather case and set it down between them with the care of a man handling something that might explode or might simply be expensive. "Before we speak plainly — and I would like to speak plainly, I'm tired of the desert and I suspect you are too — I want you to know that nothing in what I'm about to propose requires you to like me."

"That's fortunate," Ingrid said from the shadows.

Ballesteros turned his head toward her voice and smiled, unhurried. "Señorita Salas. Your father and I never met, you know. I regret that. He must have been a remarkable man, to have held a secret for fifty years." He let that sit in the firelight. "Fifty years, señora. Think of the discipline. Think of what it cost him. And for what? For a town nobody remembers, full of people nobody mourned, dead of a mistake that was corrected before most of them were born." He spread his gloved hands. "I don't say it was *right*. I say it was *done*, and done cannot be undone, and the only question remaining — the only question that has ever remained — is whether more people suffer when it comes to light."

"The forty-one already suffered," Ingrid said.

"Yes," Ballesteros agreed, with terrible mildness. "And they will suffer again, in a different way, when their names are dragged through newspapers and their descendants learn their town was a lie. Whereas if the water simply appears — discovered, say, by a new survey, a modern one, conducted by a company with the resources to develop it responsibly — then towns bloom. Children live. The plateau fills with farms that would otherwise be dust. You may weigh forty-one dead men against ten thousand living ones, señorita, and tell me which scale tips." He turned back to Orsen. "That is the honest version of my offer. There is also the dishonest version, which is simpler: I pay you, you disappear, and the lake belongs to whoever finds it next. But I think you're a man who prefers the honest version, even when it's ugly. Am I wrong?"

Orsen had spent the day rehearsing, and every rehearsal had collapsed into the same realization: there was nothing to negotiate. Ballesteros had made sure of that. The offer was generous precisely so that refusing it would look like madness — a man turning down a pension, a career, safety for his companions, for the sake of names on a ledger half a century old. The generosity was the trap. Not the money. The *reasonableness*.

So Orsen did not refuse. Not yet. He reached instead for the only thing he had ever trusted more than the company: the numbers.

"Let me counter," he said.

Ballesteros's eyebrows rose a fraction — genuine surprise, or its excellent imitation. "Please."

"Publish it," Orsen said. "All of it. The 1901 survey, the payroll ledger, the forty-one graves, the capping of the well, the rerouting of the aquifer. Every document, every name, in every paper from here to Buenos Aires. And then — *then* — the railway develops the lake. Openly. Under its own name, with the truth attached to it like a bill of lading. You build the pumping stations, you run the pipeline, you sell the water, and you make more money than the old lie was ever worth, because a province that trusts you will buy from you for a hundred years, and a province that finds out later will burn your coal ports stone by stone." He leaned forward. "You keep the charter. You keep the profit. You lose only the lie. And the lie, Señor Ballesteros — I've carried it for three weeks, and Efraín Salas carried it for fifty years, and I can tell you what it weighs. It weighs more than the truth. It weighs more than anything. It is the heaviest cargo your company owns, and you are paying freight on it every single day, and you don't even own it. It owns *you*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere on the rim, a horse shifted its weight.

For a moment — just a moment — Orsen thought he had reached him. It was in the eyes, in the way Ballesteros looked into the fire rather than at Orsen, the look of a man doing sums he had done before and gotten answers he didn't like. There was a human being in there. Orsen was almost certain of it. He had worked for the company twelve years; he knew the species; he knew that the men who enforced the lie were rarely the men who invented it, and that some of them lay awake at night doing exactly these sums.

Then Ballesteros laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh, which would have been easier to bear. It was a *tired* one — short, genuine, almost affectionate — the laugh of a man hearing a child explain how the world works.

"Oh, Nystrom." He shook his head. "You think the charter is a piece of paper. It isn't. The charter is the lie. They are the same object. Do you understand what the Southern Patagonia Railway *is*? It is a promise made to English bondholders in 1898 that this territory contains enough coal, enough traffic, enough *certainty* to repay capital at six percent. Certainty, Nystrom. That is the product. Not coal. Not water. Certainty. The moment we publish that our founding survey was wrong — that we buried a town rather than admit an error — every bondholder in London learns that our certainty was theater. The bonds collapse. The charter collapses. The courts in London do not care about forty-one Patagonian graves; they care about six percent. We would not be punished for killing those men. We would be punished for *admitting* it. Do you see? Honesty would not save the company. Honesty is the one thing the company cannot survive." He picked up a stick and fed it to the fire, gently, almost tenderly. "Your counter-offer is not a bargain. It is a eulogy. You are offering to bury us with full honors."

"Then let the company die," Ingrid said, "and let the water live."

"And who buries it with us?" Ballesteros asked, and for the first time his voice lost its polish. "You've seen the plateau, señorita. Who builds the pipeline when the railway folds? The government? The government cannot pave a street in Comodoro. The lake sits under a hundred miles of caliche until someone with capital comes for it, and the someone who comes will not have read your newspapers, and will not care about your forty-one, and will not leave a ledger behind for a prospector's boy to find. You will not have saved the water. You will have merely changed its future owners to worse ones." He stood, brushing off his coat, and the performance returned, seamless, the shave and the gloves and the club-room posture reassembling themselves around whatever had briefly shown through. "I came to make you a reasonable offer. I leave it open until tomorrow's water. After that—"

"After that what?" Tomás said. His voice cracked on it, young and furious. "You'll shoot us? Over a lake nobody's drinking from?"

Ballesteros looked at the boy for a long moment, and something in his face suggested he was filing the face away for later reference.

"No one will shoot anyone," he said. "Shooting makes martyrs, and martyrs make newspapers, and newspapers make exactly the certainty problem I described. No, Tomás Ferreyra — yes, I know your name, I know all your names — no one shoots anyone. Things simply become difficult. Permits are delayed. Mules are impounded. Telegraph messages are lost. Witnesses discover reasons to be elsewhere. The desert is very large and very patient, and it has already eaten one town for us." He gathered his reins. "Until the next water, gentlemen. Señorita."

He mounted and rode out of the firelight, and his men followed, and the hoofbeats faded northward along the rim until the wind took them, and then there was only the fire and the stars and the four of them — three of them — sitting in the ringing quiet.

* * *

"He'll never allow it," Tomás said finally. "The court. He means it. We'll never reach a court alive."

"He means it," Orsen agreed.

"Then what do we do?"

Orsen looked into the fire. He thought about the man who had given him his first job, thirty years ago, a stationmaster with ink-stained cuffs who had said, *the rails don't lie, Nystrom, that's why I trust them and no one else* — and he thought about the disgrace, the unnamed thing, the reason he had fled into miles, and he understood suddenly that he had been walking toward this fire his entire adult life, that every sleeper he had counted, every gradient he had measured, had been a rehearsal for the moment when measuring would not be enough and a man had to simply stand somewhere and be counted himself.

"We do what water does," he said. "We keep moving downhill. Toward Gobernador. There's a telegraph line there."

Ingrid lifted her head. In the firelight her face was exhausted and absolutely awake.

"How far?"

"Four days," Orsen said. "If the desert lets us."

Behind them, far off on the northern rim, a single lantern swung once and went dark — and Orsen, who had spent twelve years reading signals in empty country, understood that Ballesteros's men were not riding away at all.

They were settling in to watch.
