## 30. The Photographs

They made camp that night in the lee of a rockfall, where the canyon walls pinched together and the wind, which had bullied them for nine days without pause, could only reach them in thin, whistling threads. Tomás built the fire low and small — Orsen had taught him that much, to build it so the flame never rose above the stones, so the light died within arm's reach — and while the others ate their ration of hardtack and cold mutton fat, he sat apart with his pack between his knees and did something he had been doing every night for weeks without telling anyone.

He developed photographs.

The apparatus was his father's. That was the first thing anyone would have asked about, if anyone had thought to ask, and it was the reason he had said nothing. A folding wooden camera with brass fittings gone green at the joints, a leather case of glass plates wrapped in black paper, and a developing box no bigger than a hatbox, all of it carried out of Comodoro Rivadavia in the bottom of his kit beneath spare shirts and a coil of surveyor's twine. His father had been a portrait photographer in Gaiman before the railway took him — before the company took him, though Tomás had spent nineteen years learning to say *the railway* instead of *the company*, the way you learn to say a dead man's name gently. The camera was the one thing of his father's that had survived the accident and the settlement and the silence afterward. Tomás had learned the wet-plate process at his knee, or rather at his elbow, standing on a stool in the darkroom while his father's hands moved over his own hands, guiding them: *slowly, slowly, the emulsion forgives nothing.*

He had brought the camera because he could not explain why. He told himself it was for the survey — a record of terrain features, useful for the report they would surely write someday, whenever someday arrived. But when they had stood among the forty-one graves at Pozo Nuevo and Ingrid had gone quiet in that way she went quiet, still as a held breath, Tomás had known why. His hands had known before his head did. They had lifted the camera off his shoulder and begun to work.

*Slowly, slowly,* his father's voice said in his memory. *The emulsion forgives nothing.*

Neither, he was beginning to understand, did the truth.

Now, in the red glow of the shielded lantern inside the developing box, he watched another plate come up out of its chemical bath. He worked by touch mostly, counting seconds in his head the way Orsen counted sleepers, and when the image surfaced he held it up against the firelight for the few moments he allowed himself each night — a single look, never two, because looking cost him something he couldn't name and he had learned to ration it like water.

It was the ledger. Plate after plate of it: Efraín Salas's payroll book from 1901, opened to the pages listing forty-one names, forty-one men paid in company scrip to dig a well that official records insisted had never been dug. Tomás had photographed it in three exposures at the dry town, bracing the camera on a saddle, waiting for the wind to drop between gusts. He had photographed the graves too — all of them, working down the rows in the dying light while Ingrid stood watch and Orsen pretended not to see what he was seeing. And later, at the cavern, the lake itself: the impossible still black water, the caliche roof glittering where their lamps caught it, the shoreline smooth as poured metal.

And last night, at the parley, Ballesteros's face.

Tomás had taken that one without raising the camera at all. He had simply stood beside Orsen with the instrument slung at his hip under his poncho, angled outward, and opened the shutter twice during the long minutes when Ballesteros sat his horse and made his reasonable offer — reinstatement, pension, safe passage — with the sun full on his features. It was a poor photograph by any professional standard. Half the frame was Orsen's shoulder. But Ballesteros's face was there, sharp and unmistakable, the mouth mid-sentence, the eyes doing what company men's eyes do when they are being generous with other people's lives.

Forty-one plates in the leather case now, give or take. Tomás knew the number the way he knew his own pulse.

He was sliding the last plate into its paper sleeve when the shadow fell across the opening of the developing box.

Orsen crouched beside him. The old man's face in the firelight was the color of tanned hide, the graze along his ribs already scabbing into a dark seam beneath his shirt, and his eyes went from the box to the camera case to Tomás's hands, and then he did something Tomás had never seen him do in four months of trail companionship.

Orsen Nystrom laughed. Quietly, once, without much breath behind it — but he laughed.

"All this time," he said, "I've been sleeping ten feet from the only witness we've got."

"I didn't know how to tell you." Tomás closed the box. "At first it didn't seem important. Then it seemed — I don't know. Presumptuous. Like I'd decided for everyone that there'd be something left to show."

"There'll be something left to show," Orsen said. It wasn't comfort; it was engineering, stated flat as a gradient. Then he paused, and Tomás saw the thought move through him like a train moving through a cutting — you could track it by the sound. "Unless we don't."

"Unless we don't," Tomás agreed.

Orsen held out his hand, and Tomás gave him the ledger plate. The old engineer turned it against the light, tilting it so the emulsion caught orange fire, and studied not the image but the object itself — its weight, its fragility, the way a lifetime could be fixed onto a rectangle of glass thinner than a windowpane.

"Glass breaks," Orsen said at last. "You know that better than I do."

"Everyone knows that."

"And everything we have that matters is sitting in one leather case in the bottom of your pack. The map they can take. The map's a drawing — drawings can be disputed, redrawn, claimed to be forged. But these—" he tapped the plate against his knuckle, gently, the way you'd tap an egg you didn't want to crack, "—these are the graves themselves. These are the names. This is Ballesteros's face making his offer. Nobody un-photographs a thing."

"Nobody un-breaks it either."

"No." Orsen set the plate down between them on the flat stone. "So we don't keep them in one place."

* * *

He woke Ingrid and laid it out before her, because Orsen Nystrom did not make dispositions of other people's property without asking, even property he hadn't known existed until ten minutes ago. She came and knelt by the fire with her blanket around her shoulders, and when she understood what she was looking at — what had been riding behind her on the mule trail for three hundred miles — she put her hand over her mouth for a moment, and Tomás thought she was going to cry, and she didn't. She lowered her hand and said:

"You photographed my father's ledger."

"The whole thing."

"He kept it in oilcloth for fifty years." Her voice was strange, level and thick at once. "Wrapped it like a relic. Carried it through every winter. And you — you made it into light."

"That's what photography is," Tomás said, awkward. "Light, fixed."

Ingrid looked at him for a long moment across the fire. "My father thought shame was heavier than water. He carried both. And you just — put it down on glass and made it weigh nothing at all."

"It doesn't weigh nothing," Tomás said. "It weighs more now. Now it weighs enough to matter."

That was when Orsen took over, because Orsen could bear grief but not pauses in it, and he began laying out the plan the way he'd lay out a route on a drafting table, with little sticks and stones for the packs and mules.

Three sets. That was the arithmetic. The originals stayed split — never together, never in one saddlebag, never in one camp position. Tomás would carry the lake and the town plates in his survey kit, inside the false bottom where the spare levels lived, because nobody robbed a surveyor's instruments; they were worthless to thieves. Ingrid would carry the ledger plates sewn into the lining of her coat, flat against her back, because — Orsen looked at her when he said this — no one searched a woman thoroughly, and everyone searched a man. And the third set, the duplicates Tomás could print from the negatives in a night's work with the developing box, would go into the decoy: the oilskin packet that looked exactly like a map case, heavy with a folded chart and weighted papers, riding openly in the most obvious saddlebag they owned.

"If they take the decoy," Orsen said, "they'll think they've won, and men who think they've won stop shooting."

"And if they search all three of us?" Ingrid asked.

"Then we're done regardless, and it won't have mattered where we kept them." He said it without cruelty, the way he said everything arithmetical. "But they won't. Ballesteros wants the map. Men who want a specific thing see that thing everywhere. It's the oldest weakness there is — you find it in every surveyor worth his salt. You go looking for what you expect, and the land gives you what you expected, and the real country goes past unseen behind your back."

Tomás printed the duplicates that same night, working by feel inside the box while the others slept in shifts, and toward dawn Orsen took the finished set and the decoy packet and walked the length of the camp in the gray half-light, redistributing weight, shifting the obvious saddlebag from the lead mule to the lame one, moving Tomás's instrument case to a different pannier, changing everything and explaining none of it, until the camp bore no relation to any logic a stranger could read from it. Then he came back and sat down heavily across the fire from Tomás, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"My father worked for them," Tomás said finally. It came out of him the way blood comes out — pushed by pressure he hadn't chosen. "The company. Section crew, near Sarmiento. There was a coupling failure. Eleven tons of rail shifted. The firm paid my mother a sum and a letter, and the letter said the accident was due to unforeseeable circumstances, and my mother believed it because believing it was easier than not, and I grew up in a house where the company was spoken of the way you speak of weather. Something enormous and impersonal that takes things and cannot be argued with." He turned a stone over in his fingers. "When I signed on with you, I thought I was choosing the railway. I'm only now understanding I chose against it. Against the part that writes letters like that."

Orsen fed a stick into the fire. "I knew a man once who lost someone to those letters," he said. "He spent twelve years walking miles so he wouldn't have to count years. I won't tell you his name." The ghost of the laugh again. "But I'll tell you this, boy, and mark it, because I'm your superior and it may be the last order I give that matters: evidence is heavier than grief, and it travels farther. Grief dies with the griever. Evidence gets found. Your father's letter said *unforeseeable*. Your photographs say otherwise. When we get out of here — *when*," and he put the word down like a spike, "—you're going to write your own letter. To whoever has authority to read it. And you're going to enclose a print."

Tomás nodded, and discovered his eyes were burning, and looked at the fire until they weren't.

Above the canyon rim, far off to the east, a bird called once and went silent. Orsen's head came up at the sound — not at the bird itself but at the quality of the silence after it, the wrongness in it — and he rose without hurry, the way he did everything, and went to wake Ingrid, and stood a long time listening at the canyon mouth with his hand raised for quiet.

"They're moving," he said quietly, when he came back. "Before first light. Ballesteros has stopped being patient — the parley was his courtesy, and courtesy's run out. We break camp now, in the dark, and we're in the narrows by sunrise or we're nowhere at all."

Tomás packed the developing box with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere in the night and hadn't started again. The glass plates rode against his spine, cool and secret, forty-one names' worth of fixed light. Whatever waited in the narrows, he thought, tightening the last strap, the emulsion forgives nothing — and neither, from tonight forward, would he.
