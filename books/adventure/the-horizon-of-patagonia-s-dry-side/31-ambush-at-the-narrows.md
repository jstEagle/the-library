## 31. Ambush at the Narrows

Orsen had counted the narrows before he ever saw them. That was the habit of thirty years: you read a canyon the way you read a rail bed, looking for the places where the walls pinched and the options died. The Escalante ran south and east in a series of switchback gorges, and somewhere ahead — Tomás's map said eleven kilometers, Ingrid's reading of the water said less — the two main walls drew together until a man on a mule could touch both with his outstretched hands. A railroad man would call it a choke point. A soldier would call it a killing ground.

He called it the only way through, and that was the trouble. Ballesteros would know it too.

They rode early and cold, before the sun cleared the eastern rim, because Orsen wanted the light behind them and the shadows long across the canyon floor. Six mules now, after the losses in the storm and the river gravel. Four people. Three packs carried negatives, split according to the plan they'd made around the fire two nights ago — Tomás's photographs distributed like seed grain, so that no single loss could end them. One pack, the fourth, carried the oilcloth map.

Or the decoy of it. Orsen had spent an evening by lamplight copying Efraín's route onto a sheet of canvas from a tent fly, aging it with ash and mule sweat, drawing the cairns and the AGUA mark in a hand that shook convincingly. It was not forgery exactly, he told himself. It was engineering redundancy. You never ran a single line where a failure would cost everything. He had learned that lesson young, from a bridge engineer who said a good design assumes the worst day of its life.

The decoy rode in the left saddlebag of the lead mule, which was led by Domingo.

Domingo and his cousin Raúl had come back to them at the seep — not for loyalty, Domingo made clear, but because Ballesteros's reward meant nothing if you were dead collecting it, and the company men had already shot at Raúl once in the dark without asking his name. They guided for wages doubled and paid half up front, and they knew the Escalante the way Orsen knew sleepers: by feel, by story, by the accumulated gossip of everyone who had ever driven stock through it. It was Domingo who told him about the narrows.

"Two hundred meters," he said, spitting into the dust. "Maybe three hundred where you can't turn around. My uncle lost a cargo of wool there in 'nineteen when the flash flood came down. No room to climb. No room to run." He looked at Orsen with the flat appraisal of a man pricing a horse. "If I wanted to trap four people and six mules, señor, that is where I would do it."

"Thank you," Orsen said, meaning it.

"Don't thank me. Ride faster."

So they rode faster. The canyon walls rose around them, red and black banded rock, streaked white where minerals wept down the stone like old candle wax. The sound of their passage came back to them doubled and tripled — hoofbeats arriving from directions no hoof had struck, so that the canyon seemed full of another party riding just out of sight. Tomás kept glancing over his shoulder. Ingrid didn't look back at all; she sat her mule with her father's stubbornness, eyes forward, one hand resting on the rifle scabbard as though it were a railing she'd grown used to holding.

Orsen counted. He couldn't help it. Two hundred meters of narrows. At a walk, four minutes exposed. At a trot, two and a half, but a trot risked a mule going lame on the broken floor, and a lame mule in the Escalante was a death written in slow ink. He weighed it the way he'd once weighed gradients and axle loads, and chose the walk, and hated himself for choosing arithmetic over instinct.

The sun cleared the rim while they were still a kilometer out. Light poured down the eastern wall in a single golden sheet, and every shadow on the canyon floor swung west like a compass needle finding its pole. Orsen squinted up at the rimrock and saw nothing — scrub, stone, a twisted ñire leaning out over the drop — and understood that seeing nothing proved nothing. A man lying flat behind a low rock at two hundred meters was invisible until the moment he wasn't.

"We could climb out here," Tomás said quietly, reining close. "Domingo says there's a goat track on the west side. We'd lose half a day."

"And Ballesteros would watch us climb out all afternoon and simply follow along the rim, fresh, while our animals tire on the scree." Orsen heard his own voice and recognized the tone: the one he used with junior engineers who proposed elegant solutions to problems that had already been priced. "We go through. Fast, quiet, spread out. If shooting starts, you get to the far side no matter what happens to anyone else. Do you understand me? No matter what."

Tomás's jaw worked. "And the packs?"

"The packs are on mules. Mules are faster than men when they're frightened." He almost smiled. "Pray for frightened mules."

* * *

The narrows announced themselves as a narrowing of sound first — the wind, which had been running down the canyon all morning, suddenly compressed between the closing walls and began to moan, a low organ note that rose and fell. Then the light went. The walls leaned together overhead until only a ribbon of sky remained, blue-white and very far away, and the canyon floor fell into a cool violet shadow that smelled of damp stone and ancient bird droppings.

Domingo went first with the lead mule and the decoy pack. Then Raúl. Then Ingrid, then Tomás with two mules on a lead, then Orsen on the last animal with the true negatives sewn into his bedroll, against his ribs, where a graze would have to go through him to reach them.

He thought about that as he rode into the throat of the rock. He thought: this is where the cost stops being theoretical. He had spent weeks now speaking of cost in the language of provision lists — liters per day, kilograms per mule, hours of daylight. But there was another ledger, and Ballesteros kept it too, and somewhere above them, in the shadowless gold of the rim, a man was doing the same arithmetic Orsen did, and had decided what four lives weighed against a charter.

The walls closed to arm's reach. Hoofbeats became a confusion of echoes. Orsen watched the ribbon of sky and counted meters under his breath — fifty, eighty, a hundred and twenty — and at a hundred and forty the first rifle spoke.

There was no warning shot. He would think about that later, how there was no warning shot, how Ballesteros had stopped playing patient sometime in the night and had decided that the conversation was over. The crack came off the eastern wall multiplied, sourceless, everywhere, and the bullet hit the canyon floor two meters ahead of Domingo's mule and threw up a fist of stone splinters. The mule screamed — a human sound, horribly human — and bolted.

"Domingo!" Raúl was shouting, hauling his own animal against the wall. "Get off! Get off and push it!"

Another shot. The echo turned it into three shots, five, a fusillade that existed mostly in the stone. Orsen saw, in the strobing instant of understanding, how it would go: the lead mule panicking in the narrowest part, jamming the passage, the rest of them bunched behind it like coal cars behind a derailment, and rifles working the rim above with all the time in the world.

"Forward!" he roared, and his voice came back at him from both walls, FORWARD, orward, ward. "Through! Don't stop, don't bunch — *through!*"

Domingo did the bravest thing Orsen saw in all the years he counted afterward. He let go of the panicked mule entirely — let the decoy pack go, let everything go — and dropped flat against the western wall and began waving the others past him, past the screaming animal, slapping mule rumps as they broke by, shouting directions into the chaos of echo. The lead mule wheeled in the narrows, the oilcloth decoy flapping from its saddlebag like a wounded wing, and then it found the gap and plunged forward, and the whole column compressed and surged like water through a sluice.

The third shot took Domingo.

Orsen saw it happen from twenty meters back and forty meters too far away to change anything. Domingo was turning — turning toward the rear, toward Orsen, maybe to shout something, maybe just to see — and the bullet came off the rim high on the eastern wall and came down at the angle bullets come down, and it caught him below the collarbone and spun him halfway around. He sat down against the rock the way a tired man sits down, deliberately, one hand pressed to his chest, and looked at his palm with an expression of mild professional disappointment, as if the blood were a bad reading on a gauge.

Raúl was screaming his cousin's name. Ingrid had dismounted and was dragging her rifle free of the scabbard. And Orsen made the choice that the next year of his life would be built around, the choice he would explain to magistrates and to himself and finally, one evening, to Ingrid, standing in her doorway with his hat in his hands:

He did not stop.

"Raúl — get him up, get him on your mule, we are *leaving*—" Another round cracked off the wall above his head and stone dust stung his cheek. He put two shots up at the eastern rim without aiming, without hope, purely to make the shooters keep their heads down, and then he was moving again, because the column was moving, because the narrows were bending left around a shoulder of black basalt and the far mouth was visible, a slash of ordinary daylight, and the only mercy in the world that morning was that rifles fire slowly and fear fires fast.

The last hundred meters passed in fragments. Tomás's hat gone, torn from his head by a bullet he never heard. Ingrid firing upward with the calm economy of a woman who had grown up around guns and grief and had sorted out which one to use. Raúl hauling Domingo bodily onto the lead mule — the decoy mule, the map still strapped to it, blood sheeting down the animal's foreleg where shrapnel from the first shot had opened the skin. And Orsen himself, at the bend, feeling a blow like a hot poker laid briefly against his left side, not even a proper impact, more an insult — a round skipping off the basalt and kissing him along the ribs, opening a furrow that he would not fully see until nightfall and would carry, faint as a survey line, for the rest of his life.

Then they were through. The walls fell away, the canyon widened into a gravel wash running south, and the gunfire behind them stuttered, argued with itself in echo, and stopped.

* * *

They went two kilometers before Orsen allowed a halt, and even then only because Domingo was dying and a dying man deserved ground that wasn't moving under him.

They laid him in the shade of an overhanging slab, and Raúl knelt over him making sounds that weren't words, and Ingrid tore open the man's shirt with steady hands and looked at the wound and then looked up at Orsen, and her face told him everything before she said anything at all.

"It's deep," she said. "It's in the lung, I think. There's blood in his mouth." She pressed her palms flat over the wound anyway, because that was what hands were for. "I can't fix this. Nobody can fix this out here."

Domingo's eyes found Orsen. They were clear, which was worse than if they hadn't been. Clear-eyed men know exactly what is being taken from them.

"The pack," Domingo said. Each word cost him air and he spent it anyway, a paymaster to the last. "The map. On my mule. Take it — take it and ride, señor. They'll follow the blood. They'll follow us. Leave me the rifle and leave me here and tell my wife—" He coughed, wetly, and Raúl made a sound like something tearing. "Tell her I was coming home. Tell her whatever gets her through. That's what stories are for."

Orsen knelt beside him. He had stood at deathbeds before — his father's, and once, terribly, a brakeman crushed between cars on the Deseado line while Orsen held the lantern — and he knew there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie or a theft. So he said the only true thing.

"You bought us the narrows with four minutes of courage. I will count those minutes for the rest of my life. That is a promise, and I keep promises to Salases and to muleteers alike."

Something moved in Domingo's face that might have been a smile. "Salas," he breathed. "The old man. This is his doing, isn't it. All this." His hand lifted an inch off the gravel and fell. "Fifty years, and he makes other men bleed for it."

"He bled for it first," Ingrid said quietly. "Fifty years of it."

Domingo looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw there satisfied some final accounting, because he closed his eyes and said, "Ride, then," and did not open them again.

They buried him shallow, because the ground was caliche and iron-hard and the light was failing and the shooters might return. Raúl piled stones. Ingrid cut a strip from her skirt hem and tied it to a stick of dead ñire, a marker no surveyor would ever log. Tomás stood apart with his camera in his hands and did not raise it, and Orsen saw him make that decision — saw the boy look at the grave, look at the instrument, and put the camera away — and understood that Tomás Ferreyra had learned today the difference between evidence and dignity, which was a thing no university taught and which some men never learned at all.

Raúl would not leave the grave. They did not ask him to twice. He took the rifle, took two mules, took his cousin's hat, and told them he would hold the wash behind them until dark and then come on slow, and whether he intended to follow them or simply to stand guard over stones, none of them asked. Some debts are paid alone.

* * *

They rode into the side gorge as the light went out of the sky.

It was Ingrid who found it — a seam in the southern wall, invisible from the wash, a crack barely wide enough for a loaded mule that opened, forty meters in, into a slot canyon of impossible narrowness, winding up into the mesa like a vein. Orsen would have ridden past it in the dark. He admitted it freely, sitting in the black cold of that stone corridor with his side stiffening and his thoughts circling the same grim orbit: he would have ridden past it, and the riders behind them, following the blood trail by lantern light, would have found four people camped in the open like a gift.

Now the blood trail ended at the seam. Let Ballesteros read that signature and wonder.

They made no fire. They ate cold biscuit and shared a liter of water among three, and Orsen sat with his back against the stone and finally, by the match-light Ingrid struck to check the wound, saw what the day had done to him. A furrow along the lowest rib, left side, eight centimeters, raw and ugly but shallow — the bullet had spent most of its fury on the basalt. Ingrid cleaned it with water he told her not to waste and she told him, in a voice that permitted no appeal, that a fevered engineer was worth less than a dry one, and bound it with a strip of shirt boiled earlier over the seep-fire and saved, she said, for exactly this, though she would not say how long she had been saving it.

"You should have stopped," Tomás said in the darkness, after a long silence. "At the narrows. For Domingo. If we'd stopped—"

"We'd be dead," Orsen said. "All four of us, and the negatives with us, and forty-one graves that stay anonymous forever. That is what stopping would have bought." He heard how it sounded — like arithmetic, like the cold ledgers he despised Ballesteros for keeping — and he made himself finish it honestly. "I will live with it. That is not the same as being right about it. Both things are true, Tomás, and you will learn to carry both, because out here and in every life worth the name, the choice is never clean. You choose the version you can survive, and then you pay."

Ingrid's voice came out of the dark, low and even. "He paid too. Domingo. He knew what he was buying when he waved us past." A pause. "That doesn't make it lighter. But it means it wasn't stolen from him. He gave it."

Orsen leaned his head back against the stone. Above the slot of the gorge, stars were coming out in their cold thousands, and somewhere south, over the miles of broken country between them and Gobernador, he imagined Ballesteros standing at the mouth of the narrows, holding a seized saddlebag, unfolding a canvas map aged with ash and mule sweat, and reading — line by patient line, cairn by marked cairn — a route drawn by a dead man's shaking hand, leading nowhere at all.

Let him follow it. Let him spend three days discovering the forgery. Every hour Ballesteros spent chasing ash was an hour purchased with Domingo's blood, and Orsen intended to spend every hour of it.

His ribs throbbed in time with his heart. Tomorrow they would climb out of the Escalante onto the high pampa and turn for the telegraph station at Gobernador, two days' hard ride if the animals held, three if they didn't, and the negatives rode against his ribs in the dark like a second heartbeat.

"Ingrid," he said quietly, into the black. "How fast can you tap?"

She was silent a moment. When she answered, he could hear the ghost of her father in it — the same dry steel.

"Faster than they can ride."
