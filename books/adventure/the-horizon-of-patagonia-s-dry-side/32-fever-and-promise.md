## 32. Fever and Promise

The fever came on the second night after the narrows, the way bad weather comes to the plateau — first a stillness, then a change in the air that you feel before you understand it.

Ingrid noticed it in the way Orsen held his left arm. He had stopped favoring the graze along his ribs, which was itself a warning; men who hurt stop pretending they don't. He rode with his shoulders squared and his jaw set, and when she asked how the wound looked he said *clean enough*, in the flat voice of a man reading from a document he did not believe. By midnight, camped in the lee of a basalt ridge with the wind combing over the top of it like water over a weir, he was shivering so hard the ground beneath him seemed to tremble.

She knelt beside him and peeled back the bandage by the light of a shielded candle stub. The skin around the furrow had gone the color of old brick, streaked with red lines that ran toward his heart like roads on a map leading somewhere neither of them wanted to go. The flesh was hot. When she pressed it, he made no sound at all, which frightened her more than any cry would have.

"Tomás," she said quietly. "Bring me the kit."

Tomás came awake all at once, the way nineteen-year-olds do, as if sleep were a coat he could shrug off. He saw her face and said nothing about it. He had learned that much in three weeks of desert.

They cleaned the wound with the last of their good spirits — a decision Ingrid made without consulting anyone, because there was no one to consult, because the muleteers were gone and Ballesteros was somewhere behind them and the arithmetic of what they carried had narrowed down to this: alcohol for wounds or alcohol for courage, and courage was a luxury now. Orsen watched them work with an expression of polite detachment, as though they were tending someone else.

"It's nothing," he said. "I've had worse from a sleeper spike."

"You haven't," Tomás said. It wasn't insolence. It was fact, delivered gently, and Orsen blinked at him as if the boy had spoken in a language he was still translating.

Ingrid bound the wound tight and gave him a double ration of water, which she did not enter in the ledger she kept in her head. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at him — this man who had walked into her father's life out of a telegram and out of hers into a fever — and understood, with the cold clarity that grief had taught her, that the expedition had just changed hands.

* * *

By dawn he was raving.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. He lay in his bedroll with his eyes half open, speaking in the reasonable tone of a man giving testimony, and none of it belonged to the present. He told the scrub brush about gradients. He apologized to a mule named after a stationmaster. And then, as the sun cleared the ridge and threw his shadow long across the sand, he turned his face toward Ingrid and said:

"You shouldn't be standing out there. Come inside, señora. The well's capped but the cistern still has some."

His eyes focused on something past her shoulder. Something forty years gone.

"I'm not—" she began, and then stopped, because arguing with a fever is like arguing with the wind; you only exhaust yourself and it doesn't notice. She crouched where he could see her. "I'm here, Orsen."

"You're not her," he said, with sudden, heartbreaking precision. "She died in March. March of '02. They buried her under the number eleven." His hand rose from the bedroll, trembling, and pointed at the empty air beyond the fire ring. "You're wearing her shawl. Why are you wearing her shawl?"

Ingrid looked down. She was wearing her own gray wool shawl, the one she'd brought from Comodoro, and she understood then that he was seeing through her — through her body, through twenty-three years, through everything — into a town she had never seen and he had only just left, and the dead of that town were walking around in his fever wearing whatever clothes the dying light provided.

Tomás stood at the edge of the camp holding two water skins, watching, and she saw the boy's throat work.

"He thinks I'm one of them," she said. Not a question.

"He keeps counting," Tomás said. "Under his breath. I think he's counting graves."

He was. She listened: *thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty* — and each time he reached forty-one he started over, as if the last grave refused to be counted, as if it stood outside the arithmetic the way Efraín had stood outside every family photograph, the boy who lived, the paymaster's son who walked away carrying a town in his chest.

Ingrid took command the way her father must once have taken command of a payroll line: quietly, completely, without asking permission.

"We move at first light or we don't move at all," she said. "He can't ride alone, so I ride behind him and hold him on. Tomás leads with the compass and my father's letters. We follow the route south-southwest until the letters say otherwise. We don't stop for anything that isn't water or blood."

Tomás nodded. Then, hesitating: "And if he gets worse?"

"Then he gets worse and we keep moving," Ingrid said, and heard her own voice go flat and hard, her mother's voice, the Salas voice, the one that had slammed doors in Comodoro Rivadavia. "Grief can wait. Fever can't."

* * *

They moved.

The plateau had softened into broken country here — lava flows worn into low ridges, washes filled with coarse yellow grass, the horizon no longer a straight line but a jagged suggestion. Ingrid rode pillion behind Orsen with her arms around him, one hand flat against his chest where she could feel the fever burning off him like heat off stone at dusk. He weighed more than she expected. Solid men, she thought, burn longer.

He talked almost continuously, in a low murmur pitched for her ear alone, and she learned things no testimony would ever contain.

"The company sent me the survey in '99," he murmured once. "Before the well. Before everything. There was water marked on it — a good aquifer, shallow, clean. And then the coal port needed it more, and a man I respected sat me down and explained about charters, and I signed the reroute order because he told me it was for the greater good, and forty-one people drank dust." A pause. His breathing went ragged. "I signed it, señora. I signed it in ink."

"You didn't," Ingrid said softly. "You were a child. You were the paymaster's boy."

But he had already drifted elsewhere, and she understood that it didn't matter which man he believed himself to be — the engineer who signed, the boy who survived — because both of them were trying to carry the same weight, and neither could put it down.

Near midday he surfaced briefly, lucid as a window wiped clean, and gripped her wrist with startling strength.

"Ingrid." Her actual name. Her actual self, recognized. "The letters. My saddlebag, oilskin packet, third from the top. If I'm not—"

"You're going to be insufferable for another fifty years," she said. "I've decided. I don't take arguments from sick men."

A ghost of a smile crossed his cracked lips. "Your father argued too. At the end. He argued that shame is heavier than water." His eyes closed. "He was wrong. Shame is lighter. That's why it floats up out of you when you're dying, and why it sinks everyone else."

Then he was gone again, down into the country where the dead wore shawls, and Ingrid sat holding him upright in the saddle and wept without sound, because sound cost water and water was life.

* * *

That evening they made camp in the mouth of a dry wash, and Ingrid opened the oilskin packet.

Her father's letters — the real ones, the ones Efraín had written over fifty years and never sent, hoarded like coins — were written in a hand that changed decade by decade: young and slanted, middle-aged and cramped, old and enormous, the letters of a man whose eyes were failing but whose purpose was not. She read them by candlelight while Tomás checked the animals, and she read them aloud sometimes, because Orsen answered better to a voice than to silence.

*"The seep under the black shelf runs even in drought,"* one letter said. *"Two days' walk from the split rock, bearing southwest as the guanaco goes. Mark it. Mark everything. One day someone will come who needs the marks."*

Someone had come. She was someone. The thought settled over her like the shawl he'd mistaken for a dead woman's — warm, borrowed, heavier than it looked.

Orsen woke near midnight, and this time he knew her, mostly, and this time what he asked for was not water.

"Promise me," he said.

"What?"

"That you'll finish it. Whatever happens to me. That the names get read. All forty-one. Even the ones nobody remembers, even the paymaster." His hand found hers in the dark and gripped it. "Promise me, Ingrid. Promise me like you mean to keep it."

"I promise."

"No." His grip tightened. "Say it whole. Say it like a telegraph operator sends it — every letter, nothing abbreviated. Promise me you'll deliver the message."

She leaned close, so he could feel her breath, so there could be no mistake about which living woman was speaking.

"I promise," she said, distinctly, word by word, "that every name will be read. That your testimony will be heard if you live to give it, and given for you if you don't. That Pozo Nuevo will be a place again, with its name on maps, and its dead under stones instead of numbers. I promise it whole. Nothing abbreviated."

For a long moment he lay still. Then: "Good," he whispered. "That's... that's a clean transmission." And he slept, truly slept, the fever sweat finally breaking across his forehead like rain on ground that had forgotten it.

* * *

The second day was worse and better.

Worse, because the infection had spread its red roads further despite the spirits and the binding, and twice he fell from delirium into a sleep so deep she had to hold a mirror to his lips to be sure. Better, because between the plunges there were longer intervals of clarity, and in those intervals he was himself — dry, precise, secretly kind — and he used them to teach her.

"Bearing two-two-five off the split rock," he rasped, propped against a saddle, pointing with a finger that shook. "But don't trust the bearing. Trust the grass. Grass that yellow means roots below. Your father knew that. He wrote it in the margins, look — *the land tells you what the map forgot.*"

"I see it."

"And the mules — watch the near-side gray. She drinks less than the others. She knows something about this country we don't. Animals have been reading this plateau for ten thousand years." He coughed, winced, kept going. "When I'm done being a burden—"

"You're not a burden."

"—when I'm done being a burden," he repeated, with the stubbornness of a man laying rail, "you'll want to know about the gradient ahead. There's a rise, false, then a true descent into a broad basin. If the letters are right, the seep is at the basin's far wall, under black stone. Blacker than the rest. You'll know it when the shadows don't match the sun."

She knew it the next afternoon.

They had crossed the false rise at dawn, descended through grass that whispered like paper, and come out into a basin so wide the far side seemed a rumor. And there, exactly as the letters promised and the shadows confirmed, a shelf of black lava jutted from the basin wall, darker than anything around it, holding its shade like a secret.

Beneath it, in a hollow lined with wet clay and dark green moss — green, actual green, obscene and beautiful in all that yellow — water seeped from the rock in a slow silver thread and gathered in a pool the size of a table.

The mules smelled it before they reached it and nearly broke discipline. Tomás laughed out loud, a cracked, disbelieving sound. Ingrid slid down, cupped the water, tasted it — brackish, mineral, faintly bitter, and the finest thing she had ever put in her mouth.

They drank. They filled every skin. They stripped the gray mule's pack and let the animals drink until their sides rounded. Tomás built a small fire and heated water for washing the wound properly, the way Orsen had shown him in better days, and Ingrid boiled cloth and rebandaged the ribs with hands that only shook a little.

Orsen lay in the shade of the black shelf, fevered but quieter now, his body finally fighting on ground it could defend. His eyes opened once, found her, and focused.

"You found it," he murmured.

"Your father's friend found it," she said. "Fifty years ago. I just read the mail."

Something moved through his expression — amusement, gratitude, and underneath both, a tenderness he would never say aloud, because men like Orsen Nystrom counted miles precisely so they would never have to count the other thing. But she saw it. She had spent three weeks learning to read him the way he read land.

"Rest," she said, and touched his forehead, and did not take her hand away as quickly as she should have.

* * *

It was near dusk, with Tomás asleep and the animals hobbled and the seep whispering its small eternal thread, that Ingrid sat beside Orsen and made the promises she hadn't been asked for.

He was drifting again, but gently now, surfacing and sinking like a swimmer learning the current. When he surfaced, she spoke.

"When you're well," she said, "you're going to testify. I've decided that too. You're going to stand in front of whoever will listen and tell them about the well and the cistern and the forty-one, and you're going to be so dry and so precise that they won't be able to dismiss you, and afterward you're going to hate every minute of it, and I'm going to sit in the front row."

He made a sound that might have been protest.

"And when it's over," she went on, relentless, gentle, "you're going to stop counting miles. You're going to learn to count years. Mine, if you're fortunate. Tomás's. Even your own — I know you've been avoiding those." She smoothed the blanket over his chest, over the bandage, over the heart that was beating fast and hard beneath her palm. "My father spent fifty years buying back the truth and never once let anyone carry it with him. I won't let you make the same error. That's not a promise, Orsen. That's a plan."

His eyes were open now, fever-bright, fixed on her face.

"Why?" he whispered. "Why would you—"

"Because you kept his map," she said simply. "Because you walked into a stranger's door and took on a dying man's debt for nothing. Because you shorted your own water ration for nine days and thought no one noticed." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Because I slammed a door on you twice, and you stood in the street like a fool, and you came back anyway. Nobody comes back anyway, Orsen. Nobody I've ever known."

The wind moved over the basin, stirring the yellow grass, carrying the smell of wet clay and stone.

"So rest," she said. "Get well. Be stubborn. We have a long way yet to run, and Ballesteros is still out there, and the truth is still in three packs and one stubborn head, and I intend for all of it — all of us — to reach a courtroom alive." She bent close, so that her words landed on him like a transmission meant for one receiver only. "Sleep now. I have the watch. I have the letters. I have the route."

And Orsen Nystrom, who had spent twelve years alone on the dry side of the world, closed his eyes and obeyed — and in the morning, when the sun rose gold over the black shelf and the seep sang its thin song, it was Ingrid Salas who stood first, scanned the southern rim with her father's field glasses, and went very still.

There were riders on the rim. Many riders. And they were not riding away.
