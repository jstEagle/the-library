## 33. The Telegraph at Gobernador

Ingrid had learned to read a man's condition by the way he sat a mule. Her father had taught her that much without meaning to — that everything a body carried showed in its posture eventually, and that silence was only pain waiting for permission. So she watched Orsen across three days of broken country and counted what she saw: the way he no longer bothered to straighten in the saddle when they crested a rise; the way his left hand stayed pressed flat against his ribs as though holding a door shut from the inside; the way he had stopped giving orders and started agreeing with whatever anyone proposed, which for Orsen Nystrom was the surest sign of dying there was.

The fever had come up out of the graze along his ribs like water rising through adobe. Tomás had lanced it on the second night by firelight, hands steady, face gray, and packed it with the last of the carbolic, and still the heat climbed. By the third morning Orsen could not mount without help. He rode slumped forward like a sack of mail, and when he spoke at all he spoke of things that were not there — sleepers laid out across the scrubland, a station platform where there was only stone, a woman's voice calling names into wind.

"Names," he said once, turning his head toward her with eyes too bright. "You have to write down all forty-one. If you don't write them down, they never—"

"I know," Ingrid said. "I know them."

She did not, not yet. But she would.

* * *

They smelled Gobernador before they saw it — woodsmoke and horse dung and, underneath both, the mineral stink of standing water in a stock tank, which after nine days of brackish seep was nearly enough to make her weep. The settlement was less a town than an argument between the railway and the desert: a water tower leaning on its legs, a store with sand drifted against its door, a corral, four houses, and — rising above all of it like a church spire over a graveyard — the telegraph line, one wire running north and south along poles that marched off toward either horizon until the distance dissolved them.

Ingrid reined in and looked at that wire for a long moment.

Twelve years ago she had been a girl with ink-stained fingers in the Comodoro office, taking down train orders and private griefs at forty words a minute, learning that every message that crossed her desk passed through other hands before it reached its destination and other hands after, and that a telegraph operator who forgot this did not last. She had left the trade angry at it — angry at the click and the waiting and the way men dictated their hearts in telegraphese because plain speech cost extra per word. She had sworn she'd never sit at another key.

Now she sat looking at the wire the way a drowning woman looks at a rope.

"There'll be an operator," Tomás said beside her. "Station's manned. It has to be — the water stop, the stock trains."

"There will be," Ingrid said. "And whatever I send will pass through him first."

Tomás frowned. "So?"

"So nothing goes out in plain language." She turned in the saddle to look back at Orsen, who had slid half out of the saddle horn and was being held upright by his own stubbornness and little else. "Nothing goes out that a company man can read."

* * *

Gobernador's operator was a boy of perhaps seventeen named Esteban, all elbows and Adam's apple, who came out of the station house wiping his mouth on his sleeve and went white when he saw the state of them. Ingrid let Tomás handle the lying — survey party, bad water, fever, need of a bed and a doctor if the district had one, which it did not, which meant the nearest one was two days north by the supply wagon and might as well have been on the moon. Esteban gave them the station's back room, a cot, a stove, a bucket of tank water that tasted of rust and iron and was, to Ingrid's tongue, indistinguishable from grace.

Orsen lasted long enough to be got onto the cot. Then the fever took him properly, and he began to talk.

He talked to people who were not in the room. He talked about gradients and about a man named Salas and about a well that had been capped with concrete while men were still drinking from it. He said "forty-one" eleven times in the first hour, and each time his voice cracked on the number as though it were a rail joint his wheel had struck.

Ingrid sat on a stool beside the cot and held his hand and did not correct any of it, because correcting a fever is like arguing with the wind, and because some part of her understood that he was not talking nonsense at all. He was testifying. He was rehearsing, in the only courtroom open to him, the thing he would have to say later when his mind was his own and the stakes were real.

"You promised," he said at one point, gripping her wrist with a strength that surprised her. "You promised you'd bring her home."

"She's here," Ingrid said gently. "I'm here."

"No," he said, frowning at her as though she'd misread a signal flag. "Her. The daughter. You have to tell her I kept it. Tell her I kept it the whole way."

Ingrid's throat closed. She bent close so her hair hid her face from Tomás, who was boiling water at the stove.

"Efraín Salas kept his word," she said. "Every mile. And his daughter knows it now. I'll tell her again when she asks."

Orsen studied her face for a long moment with eyes that were seeing someone else, or several someone elses, layered over hers like double-exposed plates. Then he nodded, satisfied, and slept — really slept, the fever banked for the moment — and Ingrid stood up and walked out into the cold blue evening because if she had stayed one more second she would have made a sound she couldn't take back.

* * *

Esteban the operator ate his supper of goat stew across the table from her in the station's front room, and Ingrid watched his hands.

That was the old habit, the one she'd thought dead. Operators knew each other by their fists. A good sender's key hand told you everything — whether he was nervous, whether he was copying by ear or by rote, whether he'd been on the wire five years or fifty. Esteban's hand was young and quick and a little sloppy, and he sent his evening traffic to the division point at Puerto Deseado in bursts, the way boys do, proud of speed over clarity.

Puerto Deseado. Where the Southern Patagonia Railway kept its offices. Where Señor Ballesteros had a desk, or had had one, and where word of a survey party gone missing off the Meseta del Viento would already have arrived, dressed up however Ballesteros liked to dress things.

"How often does the line check?" she asked, casual, ladling more stew.

"Hourly, more or less. Division sends the time signal and weather." He grinned. "Mostly it's weather. Nobody ever wants to send anything interesting."

"Any traffic today besides routine?"

His grin faded a notch. He was young but not stupid; the question had a shape he recognized even if he didn't understand it. "There was a wire for the store manager. And—" he hesitated, and Ingrid saw the hesitation and knew, "—a circular. From the division. About persons traveling without permits in the southern districts. Asking stations to report."

"Report what, exactly?"

"Parties. Armed parties. Survey equipment moving without authorization." He shrugged, trying to make it small. "It's routine. They do it every year around stock season."

"They do it every year," Ingrid agreed, "around stock season. Is it stock season?"

Esteban looked at his stew.

"Señorita," he said quietly, "my father works for the railway. Everyone's father works for the railway. What is it you want to send?"

And there it was — the whole frontier in one sentence, the whole country: everyone's father worked for the railway, and the railway fed them, and the railway had buried forty-one men under a lie, and the boy asking the question deserved better than either answer she could give him plainly.

"I want to send a private message," Ingrid said. "To Buenos Aires. And I want to send it in a code of my own, because the people I'm sending it about read wires the way you and I breathe."

* * *

She worked through the evening by lamplight, and the work was the sweetest she had done in twelve years.

Not the composition — the composition was agony, compressing everything into the fewest possible words, choosing what survived the compression. Coordinates she could give as figures disguised as freight rates. The ledger numbers, the payroll totals, the dates from the graves at Pozo Nuevo — those became a column of sums that would look like bookkeeping to anyone glancing over the tape. But the forty-one names could not be disguised as anything. Names were names. Forty-one names at even ten words apiece was four hundred words of traffic, hours on the wire, and every name legible to every hand it passed through.

Unless the names weren't names.

She sat back and looked at her father's letters spread on the table — the ones from the lead tube at the lake's edge, softened now by handling, the ink going brown at the folds. Efraín had spent fifty years buying back the truth piece by piece, and he had done it the way a poor man does everything: slowly, in disguise, hiding value inside worthless things. His cairns had looked like prospectors' markers. His map had looked like a treasure map, so that anyone who stole it would hunt gold and find water instead.

He would have hidden the names inside something worthless too.

She took a blank sheet and wrote out the alphabet of the trade — the commercial codes every telegraph office kept bound in canvas covers, the five-letter groups that turned "cotton, baled, 40 pounds" into a single word and saved the sender money. Every office had the codebook. Every operator knew it. And buried in the middle of every edition, in the section nobody used because nobody shipped such things, were the commodity groups for items that existed nowhere on earth except in the minds of clerks: obsolete goods, discontinued lines, entries kept in the book for decades after the last shipment because reprinting was expensive.

She remembered, suddenly and completely, a slow afternoon in Comodoro when she was nineteen, and an old operator named Ferrer — dead now, surely, or pensioned — showing her the joke of it. *Look,* he'd said, tapping the page. *Here's a code word for whalebone, uncut. When did Patagonia last ship whalebone? Never. These words are ghosts. You could send a whole letter in ghost-words and no one would ever read it, because no one reads the pages that are never used.*

Ingrid pulled the station's own codebook off its shelf — Esteban fetched it without being asked, having decided something in his own mind that he did not share — and turned to the dead sections, and began.

Each name became a ghost-word. Each ghost-word was preceded by a figure group that gave its position in a sequence only she and one other person alive could reconstruct, because the key was a date: the date on grave forty-one, the date her father had carved into his memory at fifteen and carried for fifty years. The journalist in Buenos Aires — Ramona Etcheverry, who had written three pieces on the railway's land dealings before the paper's owner got a visit and killed the fourth — had never met her father, but her father had written to her twice, and Ingrid had found the carbon copies among his papers, and in the second letter Efraín had mentioned, in passing, a date that had meant nothing to anyone but himself.

Ramona would see the date. Ramona would understand. And if she didn't, the message would sit in her drawer looking like a clerk's error until Ingrid arrived in person to explain — and either way, no company man reading the tape in Puerto Deseado would see anything but a freight inquiry full of discontinued merchandise.

By midnight the draft ran to ninety-one words of cipher and covered everything: coordinates, ledger totals, the count of graves, the lake, the well, the cap of concrete, and beneath it all, folded into ghost-words like bones wrapped in oilcloth, forty-one names.

She read it through four times. Then she copied it clean, burned the drafts in the stove, and went to the door of the back room to listen to Orsen breathe.

* * *

He was worse. The fever had come back up during the evening, and Tomás sat where she had sat, wringing out a cloth, and when he looked up at her his face asked the question his voice wouldn't.

"He'll live," Ingrid said, because saying otherwise was not permitted yet. "Fever like this burns or breaks. He's too mean to burn."

Tomás almost smiled. Then: "The message. Did you send it?"

"At dawn. The line's quietest then, and Esteban sleeps worst at dawn — he told me himself. I'll work the key myself. He'll stand aside."

"Will he? Why?"

Ingrid considered how to answer. "Because I asked him what his father does for the railway, and he said track maintenance, section nine, and I asked him if his father had ever heard of a place called Pozo Nuevo, and he said no, and I said exactly." She touched the folded message in her pocket. "He doesn't know what he's carrying. That's the safest thing a courier can be."

"And if the company traces it anyway?"

"Then they trace it to a freight inquiry about whalebone," Ingrid said, "and someone in Puerto Deseado spends a very confused afternoon with a codebook." She paused. "But Tomás — it won't be enough. A wire is a spark. Sparks go out. The photographs, the ledger, the negatives split across three packs — those have to reach Buenos Aires in person, in our hands, or none of this matters. The telegram buys us time. Maybe it buys us a witness on the platform when we arrive. It doesn't buy us safety."

Tomás nodded slowly. He had aged a year since the dry town; she could see it happening day by day, the boy evaporating out of him.

"Ingrid," he said. "When Orsen wakes up. Does he know? About your father — that you're—"

"He knows," Ingrid said. "Somewhere under the fever, he's known since the seep. He keeps making me promise things. I keep promising." She looked down at the man on the cot, at the gray stubble and the sunken cheeks and the hand curled loosely near his ribs, guarding the wound even in sleep. "He spent twelve years counting miles so he wouldn't have to count anything else. And now he's lying there counting graves."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin.

The station room was cold enough to see breath. Esteban, hollow-eyed, handed her the key seat without a word and retreated to the stove with a mug of coffee, and Ingrid sat down before the instrument the way she had sat down at fifteen the first time — spine straight, feet flat, the brass familiar under her fingers as a door handle in her own house.

She called the line. Puerto Deseado answered in four minutes, a sleepy operator sending his opening formula with the mechanical boredom of a man beginning another identical day. Ingrid copied his time signal, acknowledged, and then, with her draft propped against the switchboard base, began to send.

The rhythm came back to her hands like water finding an old channel. She sent cleanly, slower than the boy would have, each character placed rather than thrown, and the ghost-words went out one after another into the dark morning — whalebone, uncut; tallow, inferior; hides, undressed, damaged in transit — forty-one lives dressed as dead inventory, streaming north along a wire strung by the very company they accused. There was a poetry in it that she suspected only her father would have appreciated, and the thought of him steadied her hand through the hardest stretch, the coordinates, the figures that put a freshwater lake on a record somewhere outside her own skull.

At word sixty she felt it: a hesitation on the line from Deseado. Not a break — a pause, the receiving operator letting his key rest a half-second longer than rhythm allowed. Reading something. Wondering.

Ingrid did not pause. She sent on, level as a rail, and the pause resolved itself into the standard acknowledgment, and the words went out, and at last she signed the traffic with a fictitious shipper's mark out of the codebook's sample page, and requested confirmation, and received it, and cut the circuit.

She sat for a moment with her hands flat on her knees, listening to the wire hum its empty note.

It was done. The truth was out of her hands — out of all their hands — running north at the speed of electricity through towns and offices and other people's fists, past operators who would copy it without reading it and clerks who would file it without understanding it, toward a third-floor room in Buenos Aires where a journalist who had already lost one story to the railway would unfold a strip of tape and begin, slowly, to smile.

Anything could happen to it now. That was the terror and the mercy of it. She could not recall it, protect it, or follow it. All she could do was what her father had done: keep walking toward the thing itself, and trust the message to travel faster than the men chasing them.

Behind her, from the back room, came a sound — Orsen's voice, hoarse but level, fevered no longer, calling her name with the particular clarity of a man surfacing from deep water.

"Ingrid. Is the wire up?"

She stood, and her knees ached, and she found she was smiling.

"It's up," she said. "And the message is gone."

From the cot, weak but unmistakable, Orsen Nystrom began to laugh — a dry, cracked, astonished laugh, the laugh of a man who has just realized he is, for the first time in twelve years, telling the truth faster than the company can bury it.

"Then get my boots," he said. "We're leaving before they read it twice."
