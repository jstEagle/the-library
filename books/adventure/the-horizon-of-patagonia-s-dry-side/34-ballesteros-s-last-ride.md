## 34. Ballesteros's Last Ride

Tomás heard the horses before he saw them — that particular sound a tired animal makes when it is being made to work anyway, the hollow knock of shoes on the station's stone apron, and beneath it the creak of leather held too long in one man's hands. He was standing at the window of the telegraph office with a cup of mate going cold in his fist, watching the light come up gray over Gobernador, and he thought, with a strange calm, *so this is what it sounds like when it ends.*

They came out of the north in a loose line of six, riding through the dust of their own approach like men who had stopped caring whether anyone saw them. Ballesteros led. Even at two hundred meters Tomás knew the set of his shoulders, the way he sat his horse slightly forward, always leaning into the next thing. Behind him rode the five who had followed him off the plateau and down out of the Escalante: Herrera, who never spoke; the brothers Quiroga; old Fuentes with his white-streaked beard; and a young one whose name Tomás had never learned, who had a way of looking at the ground when rifles were mentioned.

Six riders. And inside the telegraph station: Orsen Nystrom feverish on a cot behind the operator's desk, his ribs wrapped in linen gone yellow at the edges; Ingrid Salas with her hand still resting on the telegraph key as though the message she had sent were a bird she was afraid would fly back; and himself, Tomás Ferreyra, nineteen years old, holding a cup of cold mate and a satchel full of glass negatives.

He set the cup down.

* * *

The station at Gobernador was not much — a single room of unplastered brick, a counter, the telegraph apparatus, a stove that smoked, and a lean-to stable where their three remaining mules stood with their heads low. The operator, a half-asleep man named Delgado who had taken Ingrid's message an hour before dawn without fully understanding what he was transmitting, had retreated to his quarters behind a curtain and did not emerge. Outside, the settlement consisted of a store, a corral, four houses, and a windmill turning slowly against a sky the color of unpolished silver.

Ballesteros reined up ten meters from the door. He did not dismount. That was the first thing Tomás noticed, and it mattered: a man who meant to talk sits down; a man who means to end something stays in the saddle where his horse can carry him away from it.

"Señor Nystrom," Ballesteros called, and his voice carried easily across the flat morning air, pitched for the doorway rather than the sickroom. "I know you can hear me. I've ridden three days to say this once."

Tomás stepped out onto the apron. Behind him he heard Ingrid move — the soft scrape of her boots, then her hand closing on his sleeve, then letting go. She had spent the night sending forty-one names into the dark toward Buenos Aires, tapping them out twice for certainty while Orsen burned and muttered on the cot, and now there was nothing left to do but stand in front of what they had done and see if it was enough.

"Orsen is ill," Tomás said. "You can say it to me."

Ballesteros looked at him the way a man looks at furniture that has unexpectedly spoken. Then, past him, at Ingrid in the doorway. Then back.

"The boy," he said. "Of course. The boy with the camera." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Yes. I'll say it to you, since apparently you're the one keeping the accounts now."

* * *

What Ballesteros said, Tomás would remember for the rest of his life in almost perfect order, because he had trained himself — without knowing why until that moment — to remember things exactly. It was the surveyor's habit: record the bearing, record the distance, record the error. Later, giving testimony, he would recite it and watch men in the courtroom write it down, and understand that everything he had ever measured had been practice for this.

"You have caused a great deal of trouble," Ballesteros said. "More trouble than six men and a dead prospector should have been able to cause. But trouble is my trade, Señor Ferreyra, and I am here to settle accounts." He gestured with one gloved hand, a small economical motion, as though laying cards on a table. "The map. The ledger. Whatever papers the girl transmitted last night — and yes, we know about the transmission; the wire runs through Puerto Deseado and Puerto Deseado is ours. Give them to me, all of it, and every one of you rides home whole. Nystrom gets his position back with seniority restored. The girl gets passage anywhere she wants and money enough to start over. You—" the eyes settled on Tomás with something that was almost, horribly, kindness— "you get a career. A real one. I've read your field notes; don't look surprised, I've read everything about this expedition since the day you left the railhead. You're the best instrument-man the company has failed to hire. Come work for people who appreciate precision."

"And if we refuse?" Ingrid said from the doorway.

Ballesteros did not answer her directly. He looked instead at his own men, ranged behind him in the gray light, and Tomás watched him do it — watched him check them the way a driver checks his team — and saw something in their faces that made Ballesteros's jaw tighten almost imperceptibly before he turned back.

"If you refuse," he said, "then this station has an unfortunate accident. Fires are common on the frontier. So are bandits. So are mules that bolt with essential supplies and leave travelers to die of thirst, which is how your muleteers will tell it, because I have already paid them to tell it that way." He paused. "I'd hoped not to be crude. Crude is expensive. But you've left me very little country to maneuver in."

* * *

Here is what Tomás knew that Ballesteros did not know he knew: that the oilcloth map in Orsen's saddlebag — the map Ballesteros believed was the whole game, the map his men had seized in the ambush at the Narrows along with the decoy pack — was Efraín Salas's third copy, drawn in Efraín's own hand, deliberately wrong by eleven kilometers at the final bearing. Orsen had made it himself on the third night out, copying the original with a steady lamp and a steady hand, and had swapped them without telling anyone except, eventually, Ingrid. The real map — the original, water-stained, smelling faintly of kerosene and fifty years of a dead man's patience — was folded inside Tomás's shirt, against his skin, where it had ridden since the Narrows.

And here is what Tomás knew that even Orsen did not fully know: that in the leather case under his poncho were thirty-one glass plate negatives, wrapped in wool, each one developed in a black cloth tent by the light of a candle stub, each one a small square of absolute fact. The cemetery at Pozo Nuevo with its forty-one crosses. The ledger page with its columns of names and wages. The church bell half-swallowed by the earth. The cavern mouth. The lake itself, black and still, with Ingrid kneeling at its edge and the lantern making a small gold world around her. And — taken from cover at the parley by the seep, through a gap in the rocks, hands shaking so badly he'd had to brace the camera against a stone — Ballesteros's face, unmistakable, offering his reasonable bargain.

Negatives could not be argued with. That was the whole of it. Words could be bought and burned and buried, but a negative was light that had passed through a real thing and been fixed forever. Tomás had understood this only gradually, the way you understand water is rising only gradually, and now, standing on the stone apron with six armed men ten meters away, he understood it completely.

Ballesteros was still talking — something about the expense of crude solutions, something about how no one wanted tragedy — when Tomás reached under his poncho and took out the first photograph.

Not a negative. He had made prints. Three nights ago at the seep, by firelight, using paper from his dwindling stock, he had printed six copies of six images, and he had not told anyone, not even Ingrid, because some part of him had known there would be a morning like this one.

He held up the print of the cemetery.

* * *

It was a good print. He had washed it properly. Forty-one wooden crosses stood in rows on a rise above the ruined town, leaning at the angles of fifty years of wind, and the morning light had come across them low and long so that each cross threw its shadow toward the camera like a second army. In the foreground, sharp and unavoidable, the nearest cross bore its tin plate, and the plate bore its number and name: *MIGUEL ANGEL SALAS, 1902.*

Tomás did not speak. He simply held it up, arm extended, the way a signalman holds a lamp.

The effect was not immediate. For perhaps five seconds nothing happened at all; the windmill turned; a mule stamped. Then young Quiroga — the younger brother, the one who was barely more than a boy himself — stood in his stirrups to see better, and Tomás watched the recognition travel across the distance and land.

"That's Pozo Nuevo," Quiroga said. Not loudly. As though he were confirming something he had already suspected in his sleep for years.

"It's Pozo Nuevo," Tomás said. "Forty-one graves. Dated 1902. The year your company says there was no town."

"There was no town," Herrera said automatically, and then stopped, because saying it aloud, in front of the photograph, in front of the boy from the plateau, made it sound exactly like what it was.

"My father drove freight out of Deseado," Fuentes said suddenly. His voice was rough, unused. Everyone looked at him, including Ballesteros, and something in the old man's face suggested he had been waiting years for permission to finish a sentence. "In '02. He hauled well-boring equipment up the meseta trail and came back with empty wagons and wouldn't say where. My mother asked him once and he drank for a week." He looked at the photograph in Tomás's raised hand the way a man looks at a mirror he isn't ready for. "There were names on those wagons' manifests. I've seen them. Company manifests."

"Ballesteros," said the older Quiroga quietly, "my family has run sheep below that escarpment for sixty years. My grandfather talked about a town that went dry. We were told he was confused." He shifted in the saddle, and his rifle — slung, Tomás noted, slung and not held — swung harmlessly against his back. "He wasn't confused."

Tomás took out the second print. The ledger page. Names, wages, weeks worked, the paymaster's signature at the bottom — *E. SALAS, hijo, por su padre* — a fifteen-year-old boy signing for a father too sick to hold the pen.

Then the third. The bell in the earth.

Then the fourth. The lake.

And then the fifth, and this was the one that ended it: Ballesteros at the seep, hat brim shadowing his face imperfectly, one hand extended palm-up in the gesture of a man making an offer, his five men visible behind him, the whole composition legible to anyone as exactly what it was — a company officer negotiating for silence over forty-one graves.

"I have copies," Tomás said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Copies exist in three places. If anything happens to any of us — anything at all, a fire, bandits, bolting mules — the photographs go to Buenos Aires with a written account, and the newspapers print them at full size. Every rider in this picture is identifiable. Every grave. Every name." He lowered his arm at last. "You can't drown this one, señor. It's already fixed."

* * *

What happened next happened slowly, which Tomás had not expected. He had expected a crack, a collapse, a sudden breaking. Instead power came apart the way caliche comes apart — grain by grain, invisibly, until the whole slab is sand.

Ballesteros sat his horse and looked at the photograph of his own face, and Tomás watched him calculate. He was good at it; you could see the machinery running behind his eyes, fast and clean, weighing the reward against the risk, the charter against the newspaper, the six men behind him against the six men behind him. Because that was the arithmetic now, and everyone on the apron knew it: his men were no longer behind him. They were merely near him. Herrera stared at a point above Tomás's head. The Quiroga brothers had turned their horses a few degrees, casually, the way men turn when they no longer wish to be facing the same direction as someone. Fuentes had not moved at all, but his stillness had changed quality — it was no longer obedience; it was judgment.

"Those plates," Ballesteros said finally, and his voice was still level, still reasonable, and that was the most frightening thing about him, that he could remain reasonable while the ground opened. "Are company property. Taken on company territory of records the company holds."

"No," said Ingrid, coming forward off the doorstep for the first time. She walked past Tomás and stood beside him, and Tomás saw that she had put on her father's coat sometime in the night, and that it hung on her like a flag on a pole that didn't quite fit it. "They're evidence. There's a difference, and you know the difference better than anyone, because your entire career has depended on nobody learning it." She held up her own hand, and in it was the flimsy — the telegram tissue, the carbon copy of what she had tapped out to Buenos Aires. "This left here an hour before you rode in. Coordinates. The ledger numbers. Forty-one names. It's in Buenos Aires now, or nearly, and no fire and no bandits and no bolting mules can reach it. You lost last night, señor, somewhere on the wire between here and the coast, and you've been losing ever since. Everything since is just the sound of it."

For a long moment Ballesteros looked at her. And Tomás, watching, saw the exact instant the calculation finished — saw it close like a ledger being shut. Whatever sum it produced, the man's face did not show it. He had been built, Tomás thought, out of the same material as the railway itself: something that could keep running on rails that led nowhere, out of pure institutional momentum, long after the destination had ceased to exist.

"Nystrom," Ballesteros called past them, toward the dark doorway and the smell of fevered linen. "You've won nothing. You understand that. A charter suspended is a charter rewritten. Trustees can be advised. Newspapers forget. I'll be in Buenos Aires before your journalist finds a printer." He gathered his reins. "But not today."

He turned his horse.

"Herrera," he said. "Fuentes. We ride."

Herrera turned his horse. Fuentes did not.

The old man sat motionless for the space of three heartbeats, and then he said, in that rough unused voice, "No, señor. I'm going to look at those graves myself. All forty-one. I think I may have uncles there." He swung down from his horse, deliberately, heavily, a man dismounting from thirty years of company service, and stood on the stone apron with his rifle slung and his hat in his hand like a mourner.

The younger Quiroga followed him down. Then the elder. Then, after a pause that seemed to cost him something physical, Herrera — who never spoke — simply turned his horse sideways, out of the line, and sat there, a wall of one.

Ballesteros looked at his dissolved posse with an expression Tomás never forgot: not rage, not fear, but a kind of professional disappointment, the look of an engineer watching a bridge fail at a load it was rated to carry. Five men who had followed him off the plateau and down the canyon and across three days of hard country stood on the stones or sat apart from him, and every one of them was now, in the only sense that mattered, on the other side of the photograph.

"Sentiment," Ballesteros said. "It's the great weakness of frontier labor. It's why the company prefers immigrants." He said it to no one, or to everyone, and then he put his heels to his horse.

He rode north alone, at a walk, not hurrying, his back straight, growing smaller against the gray-gold flat until the heat shimmer took him, and Tomás stood with the prints in his hand and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the Narrows.

"He took the map," Ingrid said quietly. "The wrong one. He'll spend months having it copied, surveyed, argued over — and it leads eleven kilometers from the water. Let him keep it."

Tomás reached inside his shirt and drew out the original oilcloth, warm from his skin, and held it up to the strengthening light. Fifty years of a dead man's patience, folded into a square the size of a hymnal.

"Not this one," he said. "This one goes where it was always going."

Behind them, from the dark interior of the station, a voice cracked with fever and disbelief called out — Orsen, awake at last, demanding to know why there were strange horses outside and why Fuentes was taking off his hat.

And down the wire, somewhere east of the mountains, a telegraph key was already chattering forty-one names toward the presses.
