## 35. Homecoming, Interrupted

They came down off the last rise at dusk on the ninth day out of Gobernador Gregores, and Comodoro Rivadavia lay below them the way a wound lies under a bandage — you could not see it, but you knew its shape.

Ingrid had imagined this moment for three hundred kilometers. In most versions she was arrested at the edge of town. In some, worse. Ballesteros had promised them they would never reach a court alive, and though his posse had dissolved in the canyon when Tomás held up the photographs of the graves — when men who had grown up drinking from windmills their grandfathers had dug looked at forty-one crosses and could not make themselves point rifles at the people who found them — Ingrid did not believe the company's reach ended where its riders' courage did. Companies were patient. Companies sent letters instead of bullets when letters would do.

So she rode into her own town braced for the knock on the door, the polite man with a warrant, the long arm of the Southern Patagonia Railway reaching all the way from Buenos Aires to close her mouth beside her father's.

What she got instead was a boy on a bicycle.

He came pedaling up the coast road as they descended, waving one arm over his head like a man signaling a ship, and he was shouting something the wind took apart before it reached her. Orsen reined in. Tomás's hand went to the rifle scabbard. But the boy only skidded to a stop in the dust, breathless, fourteen years old maybe, with ink on his fingers and a newspaper folded under his arm like a schoolbook.

"Are you them?" he gasped. "Are you the ones from the meseta?"

Orsen looked at him the way he looked at all things — measuring, weighing, counting — and said, "That depends who's asking."

"Everybody." The boy thrust the paper forward. "Everybody's asking."

The *El Chubut* had gone to press four days ago, and the front page carried a headline set in type so large it seemed to shout: **FORTY-ONE NAMES THE RAILWAY BURIED**. Below it, two columns of small print, and Ingrid read the names standing in her stirrups, reading faster than she had ever read anything, because her father's name was not among them — Efraín Salas had been fifteen in 1902, too young for the payroll, too young to die, old enough to remember — but the name *Salas* appeared anyway, in the fourth paragraph, in a sentence the journalist had clearly coaxed out of her coded telegram: *the account comes by way of the surviving paymaster's line, carried across fifty years by a dying man's map.*

"They printed everything," the boy said, bouncing on his bicycle seat. "The magistrate put it in the *Gaceta*. There's going to be an inquiry. Señor Ferrer — the journalist — he's been at the telegraph office every day waiting for word you'd come through. He told the operator to watch for anyone asking about Pozo Nuevo." He grinned, gap-toothed, triumphant, as if he personally had broken the story. "You're famous."

Famous. Ingrid turned the word over and found nothing inside it. She was filthy, sun-blackened, eleven kilos lighter than when she'd left, with a scar of crusted salt along her jaw where the seep water had dried and cracked her skin like drought-cracked mud. Her father was dead in a hotel room in Puerto Deseado and had been dead for two months, and no headline could print that back to life.

"Where is Señor Ferrer now?" Orsen asked.

"The *Comercial* hotel. He's been there a week. He says—" the boy dropped his voice, suddenly solemn, "—he says the railway lawyers arrived yesterday. Two of them. From Buenos Aires."

Of course, Ingrid thought. Of course they came. Not with rifles. With briefcases.

* * *

They came down into the town proper after dark, which was a mistake, because half the town was still awake and someone recognized Orsen Nystrom — twelve years mapping the closed lines makes a man's face known at rail yards and provisioning houses from Deseado to Esquel — and by the time they reached the *Comercial* there were thirty people in the street holding lamps, and by the time they dismounted there were sixty, and the noise of them was like surf.

Ingrid had never been the subject of a crowd. She had spent nine years in a telegraph office translating human feeling into dots and dashes, invisible behind a window, and here were people pressing against her mule to touch her sleeve, calling her *señorita*, calling her *la hija de Salas*, as if her father's fifty years of shame had been transmuted in a single news cycle into something like sainthood. An old woman pushed through and gripped her wrist with hands like tree roots and said, "My tío dug that well. My mother cried for him forty years and we never knew where. You gave us back his bones." And she was crying, and Ingrid found she could not cry, could not do anything but stand there with the woman's grip on her wrist and the lamplight swimming, because grief does not obey headlines, and hers had been waiting two months for a quiet room and would not be rushed into a street.

Señor Ferrer met them in the hotel lobby, a compact man with spectacles and ink-stained cuffs and the particular exhaustion of someone who has been awake for a week on purpose. He shook Orsen's hand, then Tomás's, then took Ingrid's in both of his and held it a moment too long, studying her face as if comparing it to something.

"You look like your father," he said. "Around the eyes. He'd have hated all this."

"He hated everything," Ingrid said. "It was how he loved."

Ferrer almost smiled. Then the smile went away and he became a professional again, brisk, lowering his voice, steering them toward the back stairs. "Two things. First: the magistrate has summoned you. All three of you. Formal inquiry, day after tomorrow, the courthouse on Rivadavia street. The company's lawyers are already in town — I've seen their telegrams; they're requesting the summons be quashed on grounds of *irregular evidence*. They'll come for your characters next. That's what they do. They can't dispute the ledger, so they'll dispute the people who found it." He glanced at Orsen. "Especially you, señor. I pulled what records exist. There's a gap in your file. Twelve years ago. They will find it too."

"I know," Orsen said, and his voice was flat as a rail bed, and Ingrid heard in it the thing he had never explained, the disgrace he carried like ballast, and understood that in two days' time he would have to open it in public or let it sink them all.

"Second thing," Ferrer said. He hesitated, which journalists, in Ingrid's experience, did not do. "There's a woman been asking after you. Not company. Older. Says her family worked the port at Puerto Deseado. Says Efraín Salas stayed with them, off and on, these last years." He produced a card, soft-cornered, much handled. "She left this. She says she has his things. What he couldn't carry."

* * *

Her father's house stood on the same street it had always stood on, three blocks up from the telegraph office, adobe walls whitewashed once a decade and going grey again, a windmill turning slow in the yard with the sound it had made her whole childhood — *tick... tick... tick* — a sound she had thought she'd forgotten until it reached her across the dark and undid something in her chest.

The neighbors had kept it. That was the custom of the plateau: you kept the house of the absent, you drew water, you swept sand from the doorway, and you asked no questions about why they were absent. Doña Rosa from next door had the key and pressed it into Ingrid's hand without a word, only a look that contained, compressed into a single glance, condolence and curiosity and the plateau's fierce refusal to pry.

Inside, the lamp oil was still good. Ingrid lit the lamp and stood in the room where she had grown up and let it look at her.

Everything was where he had left it. That was the terrible thing. The cup upside down on the drainboard, drying since March. His coat on the nail by the door. On the table, a spread of papers she recognized at once as the geology of his obsession — well-boring reports bought piecemeal over decades, copies of copies, marginal notes in his cramped hand: dates, depths, sums. Fifty years of a man buying back the truth a piece at a time, exactly as his second letter had described, and here was the workshop of it, the bench where the obsession had been machined.

And on the wall above the table, pinned with rusted tacks, a child's drawing. A train. Crayon, the perspective wrong, the wheels floating free of the rails. Signed, in a wobbly hand: *Efraín, 5 años.*

He had been five when the railway came through promising water. He had pinned his hope to the wall at five years old and never taken it down, and Ingrid stood looking at it while the windmill ticked outside and the lamp hissed, and she understood that she had come home to a house that was also a museum, and that she was its last living exhibit.

She had not cried at the hotel room in Puerto Deseado where he died. She had not cried on the meseta, not at the cairn, not at the forty-one graves, not even at the lake, where Tomás had wept without knowing why and she had envied him the release. She had not cried in the canyon or the storm or the seep, because there had been no room for it — grief is heavy freight and the desert allows no excess load — and she had told herself it would come later, and later had kept arriving without bringing it.

Now it came.

It came not as tears first but as anger — she swept the papers off the table with her forearm, the whole fifty-year archive, and stood shaking amid the drift of them, furious at him, furiously at the room, at the crayon train, at the man who had watched a town die at fifteen and then chosen fifty years of silence over one honest conversation with his daughter, who had handed a stranger a map and a promise rather than tell her, *I am ashamed, I ran, I owe the dead a debt and I paid it in secret because shame is heavier than water.* He had let her learn his life from a lead tube at the bottom of a hole in the earth. He had made a railroad engineer her confessor and left her a stranger in her own inheritance.

And then the anger broke, the way caliche breaks, all at once along a seam, and she was on her knees in the scattered papers and the sound she made was not dignified and did not try to be, and she wept for her father and for the boy of five with the crayon train and for the paymaster who stayed with his crew and for the forty-one whose names were now in a newspaper being read aloud in kitchens across the territory, and she wept for herself, who had slammed a door twice on the last messenger her father ever sent, and who would never be able to unslam it, never be able to say *I read it, Papá, I came, I finished it for you.*

Doña Rosa must have heard, or guessed, because somewhere in the middle of it there was a knock and then arms around her, an old woman's arms, smelling of bread and lamp oil, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the windmill's ticking wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Near dawn, red-eyed, wrung out, strangely lighter, Ingrid sat at the cleared table with the papers re-sorted into stacks and the card from Ferrer in front of her. The woman from Puerto Deseado. His things. What he couldn't carry.

Tomás appeared at the door as the sky went grey, hat in hand, looking like a boy who hadn't slept either. Behind him in the yard, Orsen was already up, doing what he always did — walking the fence line, pacing off distances, measuring a world that had stopped making sense to him. She watched him through the window a moment. Twelve years of miles, and in two days the hearing would demand years instead, and she wondered if he knew yet which of them he could actually count.

"There's a crowd starting outside the telegraph office," Tomás said quietly. "People waiting for the morning dispatches from Buenos Aires. Someone chalked POZO NUEVO on the depot wall last night. Big letters. Nobody's washed it off." He shifted. "Señor Ferrer sent word. The company lawyers filed this morning. They're asking the magistrate to bar you all from testifying — conflict of interest, they say. And they've requested Orsen's personnel file. The whole file."

Ingrid looked at the card in her hand. At the crayon train on the wall. At the door where her father's coat still hung, sleeves empty, waiting for shoulders that were done coming home.

"They can have the file," she said. "We have the ledger. We have the photographs. We have forty-one names that won't go back in the ground." She stood, and picked up her father's coat off the nail, and held it a moment, and then hung it back exactly where it was, because some things should stay where the dead left them. "Tell Ferrer we'll be at the courthouse. Both days. However long it takes."

Tomás nodded and went out, and Ingrid stood alone in the museum of her childhood as the sun came up over Comodoro Rivadavia and lit the chalked word on the distant depot wall, visible even from here, three white letters and six, un-washed, un-ashamed: the name of a town the maps had murdered, written now on the side of the railway's own station for the whole morning shift to read.

Somewhere south, she knew, Ballesteros was riding, or scheming, or writing telegrams of his own. Somewhere in a drawer in Buenos Aires, a lawyer was opening a personnel file with a twelve-year-old wound in it. And here, in her hand, a card with a stranger's address in Puerto Deseado, and her father's unfinished business folded inside it like a letter he'd never had the courage to send.

She put on her hat. There were two days until the hearing, one woman to visit, and a truth that had only begun to cost what it was worth.
