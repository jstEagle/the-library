## 37. Verdict

The hearing room in Comodoro Rivadavia had once been a wool warehouse, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin and salt, as if the building itself remembered what it had been before the law moved in. Orsen noticed such things. He had spent twelve years noticing things — gradients, sleepers, the way wind combed a plateau — and now, on the second day of testimony, he noticed that the magistrate's bench was built from the same yellow quebracho as railway ties, and that somewhere behind the paneled wall a pipe ticked like a cooling locomotive.

He sat in the witness chair with his hands flat on his knees, the posture of a man waiting for a train he did not expect to come.

* * *

They had dismantled him for two days, and they were good at it. The company's lawyers — three of them, silver-haired, patient, expensive men flown up from Buenos Aires — did not raise their voices. They did not need to. They took his character apart the way a wrecking crew takes apart a bridge: bolt by bolt, politely, with the road closed and flags set out.

The first day had been the surveys. Orsen had brought his field books, twelve years of them, and for four hours he had walked the court through them page by page: the 1901 reconnaissance line across the Meseta del Viento, the well logs that existed in duplicate and then existed nowhere, the payroll ledger with its forty-one names, the engineering of Pozo Nuevo read plainly from its ruins — the capped casing, the diversion cut that bent the aquifer east toward the coal port at Cabo Raso, the arithmetic of thirst performed by men who signed their initials and went home to houses with water in the taps.

The senior counsel, a man named Vergara with a voice like dry gravel poured slowly, had listened to all of it without blinking.

"You are telling this court," Vergara said, "that the Southern Patagonia Railway deliberately murdered forty-one people."

"I am telling this court," Orsen said, "that the company knew the water was there, knew the town depended on it, capped the well anyway, and struck every record of both from its files. What you call it is above my pay."

A murmur ran through the warehouse. The magistrate let it die.

The second day was worse, because the second day was about him.

Vergara had waited, the way good lawyers wait, until the court was tired and the light through the high windows had gone the color of weak tea. Then he had produced a folder — thin, but a folder is always thinner than the man it concerns — and asked Orsen Nystrom to tell the court about the year 1913.

Orsen had known it would come. He had felt it circling since the summons arrived, the way you feel weather coming off the meseta long before the first dust moves. Twelve years ago. The Santa Cruz extension. A bridge over a wash that his figures said would hold and did not hold, and a gang of tracklayers caught on it when the flash flood came, and two men carried downstream and never found, and an inquiry that found no fault in anyone except quietly in him, and a company that offered him a choice: accept reassignment to the dead lines of the dry side, where nothing he signed would ever carry weight again, or resign and carry the fault openly into a world that had no use for a disgraced engineer.

He had taken the miles. He had taken twelve years of miles, measuring sleepers at dawn because counting was the only prayer he trusted, and he had told himself the disgrace was paid for, amortized, written down year by year like depreciation.

"Tell the court," Vergara said, "why a man with your history was permitted to wander the company's closed lines unaccompanied for twelve years."

"Because I was harmless," Orsen said. "Because I counted things and reported them and asked for nothing. Because a broken engineer is cheaper than a pension."

"And are we to believe," Vergara said, spreading his hands toward the gallery, toward Ingrid and Tomás sitting very straight on the bench, "that this same man — this man whose miscalculation drowned two workmen — is now the sole reliable interpreter of a map drawn by a dying prospector? That the court should suspend a charter, seize a lake, ruin an industry, on the word of—"

"On the evidence," Orsen said. "Not my word. The ledger. The graves. The photographs. The lake itself, which any surveyor in this province can walk to and put a line in. I am not asking you to believe me, señor. I am asking you to believe the water."

For a moment — one moment — something moved behind Vergara's gravel face, something that might have been respect, or might have been the recognition of a man who has fired a shot and heard it strike nothing he aimed at.

Orsen had answered it plainly. For the first time in twelve years he had said the whole of it aloud, in front of strangers: the bridge, the flood, the two names — Sosa and Lindqvist — which he still wrote in the back of every field book, on the last page, where other men kept prayers. He said he had been wrong. He said the company had known he was wrong and buried him instead of burying the mistake, because burying mistakes was what the company did, and he had not understood until a dying prospector pressed an oilcloth map into his hands that he had spent twelve years standing inside the same grave they had dug for Pozo Nuevo.

The warehouse was silent when he finished. Ingrid's hand found Tomás's sleeve. Orsen looked at the floor between his boots and saw, absurdly, sleepers — the spacing of them, even here, even now — and made himself stop counting.

* * *

The verdict came nine days later, on a Thursday, delivered by the magistrate in a voice pitched for the telegraph wires more than for the room.

The charter of the Southern Patagonia Railway was suspended pending criminal investigation. The lake beneath the Meseta del Viento — the buried water, the AGUA that Efraín Salas had marked with a single word fifty years too early — passed into public trusteeship, to be developed for the towns and estancias of the interior under a new provincial water authority. An order of exhumation was issued for the cemetery at Pozo Nuevo; the forty-one names read aloud in court would be carved where the wind could find them.

And Señor Ballesteros.

Ballesteros had testified the week before, and everyone in the warehouse knew what his testimony had cost him and what it had bought. He had come in gray-faced, immaculate as ever, and he had given them his superiors the way a drowning man gives up his gold: board minutes, memoranda, the 1902 directive authorizing the capping of the well, the instruction — initialed, dated, preserved in a lawyer's fireproof cabinet for half a century because lawyers keep everything — that the town be "relocated" without further expenditure. He had named three directors, two of them dead and one of them, Don Ernesto Aguirre, very much alive and very much in Buenos Aires. He had done it in a level voice, looking at no one, and when the magistrate asked whether he understood the consequences for himself, Ballesteros had said, "I have considered them, señor, at some length, in the dark," and the stenographer had recorded it and the room had gone cold.

Immunity, partial. Disgrace, total. It was more than Orsen expected from him and less than justice required, and he supposed that was what justice usually was: a bargain struck in the dark, wearing its best coat.

* * *

The dismissal came by letter, because the company was punctual even in its dying.

Orsen read it standing on the platform at the Comodoro station, in the wind, because it seemed right that it should happen among rails. The language was exquisite. *The Board wishes to express its gratitude for your many years of service.* Gratitude, from a board that had sent men with rifles after him across a waterless plateau. *Your conduct during recent events has rendered your continued employment incompatible with the interests of the firm.* There it was, plain at last, the truest sentence the company had ever written him: the truth was incompatible with its interests. He almost laughed. He folded the letter along its original creases and put it in the pocket over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to keep, and stood a while watching a freight consist take the curve south, twelve years of habit reading its wheelsets by sound alone — and then, deliberately, not reading them, letting the sound just be sound.

His career. Gone in a paragraph. And yet walking back from the platform he felt lighter than he had since 1913, lighter than he had any right to feel, as though the company had not dismissed him so much as finally set him down.

* * *

Tomás came to him that evening at the hotel, still in his good jacket, holding a document with a blue ribbon on it like a boy holding a bird he wasn't sure wouldn't fly away.

"They hired me," he said. "The water authority. Junior surveyor. They want me on the pipeline reconnaissance in the spring — the line from the lake to Gobernador." He stopped, embarrassed by his own joy, because joy seemed indecent in a season full of graves. "Señor Nystrom. Orsen. They asked who trained me. I told them the truth there, too."

"What did they say to that?"

"That truth seems to be in fashion." Tomás grinned, and for a moment he was nineteen again, signing on at the railhead with his instruments wrapped in flannel. Then the grin faded into something older. "It isn't right, what they did to you. You should be chief engineer of the whole authority. They know it. Everyone in that room knows it."

"The room knows many things," Orsen said. "Rooms aren't asked to live with them." He put his hand briefly on the young man's shoulder — the shoulder that had carried negatives through a sandstorm, that had wept over a lake without knowing why, that had betrayed him in the dark out of love and been forgiven in a dry wash out of the same. "You'll build the line better than I would have. That's not consolation, Tomás. That's arithmetic."

* * *

Ingrid found him later, on the hotel's narrow balcony, where the wind came off the harbor carrying coal smoke and gull noise. She didn't speak at first. She had learned, on the plateau, when silence was a tool and when it was a gift, and she gave it to him now.

"They gave me my father's effects today," she said finally. "From the hotel in Puerto Deseado. The magistrate released them as evidence, and then un-released them." She held up a small canvas roll — a prospector's kit, worn soft as cloth. "There was nothing in it but a spoon, a tin cup, and a photograph of me at six years old, taken the year he left. He carried it fifty years, Orsen. Through all of it. He never once wrote to tell me, and he never once put it down."

"He kept you the only way he could," Orsen said. "Shame is heavier than water. I've testified to that myself, in a way."

She leaned on the rail beside him. Below, the harbor lights came on one by one, and beyond them the dark sea worked at the coast with its old patience.

"What will you do?" she asked. "Now. With no miles to count."

Orsen considered the question honestly, which was new for him. Twelve years he had answered every version of it with the same currency — distance, tonnage, sleepers per mile — and the currency was worthless now, and he found he did not miss it.

"There's talk," he said, "that the trusteeship will need route surveys for the pipeline. Independent ones. Men the company can't lean on." He turned the thought over like a stone, checking its underside. "I'm told I'm available."

"And the rest?"

"The rest—" He stopped. On the plateau, feverish, he had mistaken her for the dead of Pozo Nuevo and made her promise things, and she had promised anyway, and neither of them had spoken of it since, because some promises are load-bearing and you don't inspect them while the roof still stands. But the roof was standing now. The verdict was in. The wind was ordinary wind.

"In Buenos Aires," he said carefully, "there is a journalist who owes us a great deal and has offered more. And there is a house in this town with a telegraph key in it that hasn't been sold, according to the papers. And there is a meseta that will be green in places, next spring, where water comes up out of the earth for the first time in fifty years." He looked at her. "I find I'm counting years now instead of miles. It changes the arithmetic considerably."

Ingrid smiled — the first full smile he had seen from her since the dry town, since grief had sharpened her into someone who led men across deserts by reading guanaco paths. She reached into her pocket and set something on the rail between them: the oilcloth map, Efraín's map, returned from the evidence lockers, creased white along its fifty-year folds.

"The magistrate released this too," she said. "Said it belonged to whoever kept the promise." She looked at him. "You haven't decided yet, have you. Where it goes."

"No," Orsen admitted. "I haven't."

"Well." She pushed it gently toward him and left her hand on it a moment longer. "When you do decide — don't decide alone. That's the whole lesson of the last year, if you ask me. Nothing on that map was ever meant to be carried by one man."

Below them the harbor lights burned steady against the tide, and far inland, invisible past the dark hills, the meseta lay quiet over its buried water — waiting, the way Patagonia waits, patient as a ledger, for spring.
