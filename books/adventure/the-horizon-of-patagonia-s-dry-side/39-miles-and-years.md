## 39. Miles and Years

The morning Orsen Nystrom turned fifty-three, he woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, lay in the dark of his rented room above the hardware store in Comodoro Rivadavia, and discovered that he could not remember how many miles he had walked in his life.

This should have frightened him. For twelve years the number had been his ledger, his prayer, his penance — every sleeper counted, every chain measured, every decommissioned spur walked end to end with a surveyor's chain and a mind that refused to stop adding. The miles had been the wall he built between himself and the thing he would not name, and he had stacked them so high and so carefully that he had forgotten there was anything on either side of it.

Now the number was gone. Not misplaced — dissolved. And in its place, unbidden, came other figures: it was spring; Ingrid's birthday was in nineteen days; Tomás had written twice since the equinox and the second letter had contained a sketch of a pump housing with an arrow pointing at a bolt and the words *this one is wrong, isn't it?* It was wrong. He could see it from memory, the way you can see the face of someone you have begun to love.

He got up, put the kettle on, and did not count anything at all.

* * *

The pipeline route ran northwest out of Comodoro, forty leagues of trench and flume that would carry water from the new pumping station at the edge of the meseta down to the towns the railway had starved and the towns it hadn't, which were sometimes the same towns wearing different clothes. The water authority had hired him as a consultant — a word that made him laugh the first time he heard it applied to himself, a dismissed engineer with a ruined name, consulted. The young men from Buenos Aires who ran the authority had read about the hearing. One of them, a pale earnest fellow named Duarte, had shaken his hand for too long and said, "Señor Nystrom, we want the route done *right*, not done fast," and Orsen had felt something turn over in his chest that he recognized, after a moment's confusion, as being trusted.

So he walked. He walked the route in sections, pacing it out with the old habit living in his legs even though he no longer kept the tally, and where the line crossed a dry wash he stood for a long time looking at the gradient, and where it climbed the shoulder of a stony hill he knelt and pushed his fingers into the soil to feel for caliche, because caliche meant hard digging and hard digging meant money the authority didn't have.

The land was the same land. That was the strange mercy of it. The scrub still bent all one way under the wind, the guanacos still watched him from the ridgelines with their idiot dignity, the sky still went on forever in every direction like a promise nobody intended to keep. A year ago this country had been a map of his failures — every mile a step away from something. Now it was just country. It had water coming to it. In a year, maybe two, there would be a valve stand outside Pozo Nuevo, and children who had never heard the name of the town would drink from it without knowing they were drinking an apology.

He had learned to count differently. That was the whole of it, the change no one could see from the outside. He counted seasons now: this was his second spring on the route, and he knew the way the coirones silvered when the rains came late, and he had marked in his notebook — actually marked, in ink, like a man writing down something worth keeping — the day the first swallows arrived at the windmill near Km 14. He counted birthdays: his own, which he had let pass with a cake Tomás had carried out to the works camp balanced absurdly on a mule's pack; Ingrid's, which was coming; the anniversary of the seep beneath the lava shelf, which none of them celebrated aloud but all of them remembered, the way you remember the day you almost died and didn't. He counted letters. Letters were the true currency now. He had more of them than he had ever had in his life, and he answered every one by hand, slowly, in the evenings, with the lamp turned up bright because his eyes were starting to complain and he had decided he was allowed to grow old.

Unemployed and lighter, Ingrid had called him once, teasing, and he had not corrected her. Lighter was right. The disgrace he had carried unnamed for twelve years — the error in the Deseado surveys, the bridge crew that paid for it, the man who had signed off on both — it had come out at the hearing the way a splinter comes out, badly, with blood, and then the wound had closed. He had answered the lawyers plainly. *Yes, I falsified the bearing reports. Yes, I knew the ground was unstable. I was twenty-nine years old and afraid of the man who gave me my first job.* Twelve years of silence, spent in four sentences. He had lost his career over four sentences and gained, somehow, a life.

He was thinking about all of this — or rather, letting it sit in him the way water sits in a cistern, still and available — when he crested the low rise above the Senguerr wash and saw the two figures waiting for him by the survey stakes.

One tall, one shorter, standing close together the way people stand when closeness has become their native language. Even at half a mile he knew them. He found he was walking faster, and then faster than that, an old man hurrying across the scrub like a boy, and he did not care who saw.

* * *

They had ridden out from the pumping station site on two borrowed horses, leaving the wagon with the work crew, and they had brought food, which was how Orsen knew it was an occasion and not merely a visit: cold roast guanaco, bread still warm from the station bakery wrapped in cloth, a jar of the bitter honey the plateau bees made from the flowering scrub, and — produced from Ingrid's saddlebag with ceremony — a bottle of wine with a label from Mendoza, which she said her father had drunk on the night Orsen met him, according to a story Efraín had told her so many times she'd stopped believing it until recently.

"He toasted the end of the line," she said, pouring into three tin cups because they had brought nothing else. "He said the end of a line is always the beginning of something, but he wouldn't say what."

Orsen looked at the wine, then at her. She was browner than a year ago, harder in the hands, softer around the eyes — field work and grief and its slow resolution had done all three things at once. She wore her hair back with a leather cord and she sat on the ground the way her father must have sat on the ground for fifty years, folded and patient, part of the landscape.

"You look well," he said, which was inadequate, and she smiled because she knew it was inadequate and accepted it anyway.

"I'm going to be married," Tomás said.

It came out of him sideways, the way everything important came out of Tomás — mid-sentence, blurted, followed immediately by a blush visible even in the flat plateau light. "In September. Her name is Rosa. She's the telegraphist at the pumping station — well, she will be, when there's a station. She's been teaching me Morse." He said this last part as if it were the scandalous detail, as if the marriage itself were the ordinary part.

"Rosa," Orsen repeated gravely. "Does she know you photograph everything?"

"She says I'll photograph the wedding and she'll allow exactly one picture."

"Then she's sensible. Marry her quickly before she reconsiders."

Tomás laughed, and Orsen heard how the laugh had changed — deeper, less eager to please, the laugh of a man who had buried two mules in a sandstorm and wept at the edge of a buried lake and testified, at nineteen, before a magistrate with the whole room watching his hands shake. He was twenty-one now. He had a scar on his forearm from the rockfall in the Escalante that Ingrid had pulled him out from under, and he touched it sometimes when he was thinking, unconsciously, the way Orsen touched the old rib graze when the weather changed.

They ate. The wind moved through the coirones with its long dry whisper, the sound that had been the only voice of this country for ten thousand years and would go on being so long after the pipeline rusted. Ingrid told him the news from the station site in the clipped efficient manner of someone who had learned engineering vocabulary out of self-defense: the borehole casing seated true, the head frame up, the boiler tested twice. "Duarte wants to name it," she said. "There's a committee. They're arguing between *Nueva Esperanza* and something with *Libertad* in it. Tomás voted for naming it Pozo Nuevo."

Orsen set down his cup.

"Did he."

"The committee didn't understand why." She looked at him steadily. "I did."

"It's a good name," Orsen said finally. "Better than they know. New well. That's all it ever wanted to be." He turned the tin cup in his fingers. Forty-one graves under the adobe dust, a church bell half-buried, a paymaster who stayed with his crew when the water failed — and now a pump house with a fresh coat of paint and a telegraph key clicking the news south. The dead would not know. But the living would walk past a signboard every day that said the truth in two words, and some of them, eventually, would ask.

"They've set the date for the first water," Tomás said. "November the ninth. There'll be a ceremony. Speeches. Duarte has asked—" he hesitated, glanced at Ingrid, and pressed on, "—he's asked if you'd speak. At the opening. Officially. They want your name on the plaque too, both of yours, and mine, but mine doesn't matter—"

"Yours matters," Orsen said, more sharply than he intended, and Tomás stopped. "You dug the evidence out of the dark and carried it through a canyon under rifle fire. If there's a plaque, your name goes on it, and you'll stand in front of it at the ceremony and let people shake your hand, and you will not slouch, because slouching tells the world you think you stole something. You didn't steal anything. You *paid* for something everyone else owed."

Tomás stared at him. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod.

"And the speech?" Ingrid asked.

Orsen looked out at the wash, where the survey stakes ran away to the northwest in a straight line that would, within the decade, be a trench full of singing water. He thought about speeches. He thought about the hearing, the two days of testimony, the lawyers' careful dismantling and his own plain answers, and how afterward a reporter had asked him what it felt like to lose everything, and he had said, honestly, *lighter,* and the reporter had printed it as if it were wit.

"I'll speak," he said. "I'll say forty-one names. That's the speech. It'll take four minutes and it'll be better than anything Duarte's committee writes."

Ingrid reached into her coat then, and brought out an envelope, and handed it to him without a word.

He knew the handwriting before he opened it. He had seen it on one letter only, a year and a lifetime ago — the spidery, determined script of a dying man writing against the clock in a kerosene-smelling hotel room. But this envelope was newer. The paper was newer. Someone had written it recently.

"When?" Orsen asked.

"Two months ago. The lawyer held it — Father left instructions, letters to be delivered at certain times. This one was to be given to you 'when the counting changes.'" She shrugged, and her eyes were bright and wet at once. "I don't know what he meant. But you told me last month about the swallows at Km 14, and the birthdays, and I thought — that's a man whose counting has changed."

Orsen opened the envelope with hands that were not quite steady.

* * *

*Nystrom —*

*If you are reading this, the girl judged the moment, which means she is smarter than both of us, which I always suspected.*

*I owe you a second letter because the first one was a trade and trades are cheap. This one is a gift and gifts cost.*

*You counted miles because you were running. I know the stride. I walked it myself for fifty years — every league away from Pozo Nuevo a league I pretended was toward something. We are the same animal, you and I, except you got out of the desert alive and I got out of it as a boy carrying a payroll box and a shame that fit me worse every year I grew.*

*Here is what I never told anyone, and tell it now because the lake keeps secrets better than I do: I did not survive because I was clever. I survived because my uncle the paymaster put me on the last mule and stayed. Forty men stayed with him. They dug at the capped well until their hands broke, knowing — knowing by the end — that the company had sealed it from the other side. My uncle's last words to me were: count the names, boy. Somebody has to count the names.*

*I counted for fifty years. Ledgers, graves, court records bought one page at a time. And then I found you walking a dead spur at dawn measuring sleepers, and I thought: here is a man who counts. Give him the map. Give him the girl. Let him finish the arithmetic.*

*He did. So this is the last entry, and it is short:*

*You were never disloyal to the firm by telling the truth. The firm was disloyal to you first — it took your best years and your best self and taught you to call the taking discipline. I am telling you what I needed someone to tell me at fifteen and no one did: the miles were never the debt. The debt was always the names. And it is paid. Paid in full, with interest, in water.*

*Count seasons now. Count birthdays. Count the letters. When you run out of things to count, come stand on the rim above the lake and count nothing at all. The water has been keeping my map. Maybe it should keep yours too.*

*Your friend in the arithmetic,*
*Efraín Salas*

*P.S. Tell my daughter the argument she owed me is settled. She was right about everything. Tell her I said so in ink.*

Orsen read it twice. The second time his vision blurred and he let it, sitting in the scrub with the wind moving through the coirones and two young people watching him with the particular courtesy of those who understand that a man needs a minute.

When he could speak, he said, "He wrote this two months ago. He died fourteen years ago."

"The lawyer says he wrote the letters in his last year and dated the deliveries ahead." Ingrid's voice was rough. "He planned them like a railway schedule. He always said shame is heavier than water, but paper floats."

Orsen laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, that turned halfway through into something else, and Ingrid put her hand on his shoulder and left it there, and Tomás looked away politely at the horizon, which was doing what the horizon always did, which was refusing to be the edge of anything.

* * *

They rode back toward the station site in the long gold light of late afternoon, Orsen on the borrowed horse he'd been lent for the inspection, the letter inside his coat over his heart. At the top of the last rise before the works camp, Tomás reined in and pointed.

"There. See it?"

Below them, at the edge of the meseta where the land dropped away toward the old dry town, the head frame of the pumping station stood black against the sunset — iron legs braced like a man planting his feet, the wheel still, the boiler shed small and neat beside it. Beyond it, faint in the haze, the scrubland ran gold to the rim of the plateau, and beyond the rim, thirty leagues of stone and silence, the buried lake waited with its cargo of light and freshwater and one oilcloth map, keeping Efraín's secret the way it had kept everything else.

"November ninth," Ingrid said. "Forty-one names. And afterward—"

"Afterward," Orsen said, "I'm going to ride out to the rim. Alone. There's something I have to leave there."

Neither of them asked what. They had learned, all three of them, that some deliveries you make yourself, on foot, at dusk, and you don't explain them, because the explaining belongs to a different kind of counting — the kind he had finally retired.

The sun went down behind the head frame and lit the wheel's spokes like a compass rose, and below, in the camp, someone struck a match and a lamp bloomed in the cook tent, and Orsen Nystrom sat his horse between the two people he loved best in the world and counted, silently, the days until November — and found, for the first time in his life, that the number was not a wall, not a debt, not a distance to be endured.

It was a door.
