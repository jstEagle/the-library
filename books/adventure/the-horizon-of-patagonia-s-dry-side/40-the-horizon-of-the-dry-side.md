## 40. The Horizon of the Dry Side

He left Puerto Deseado on the fourteenth of March, a year and eleven days after the hearing, riding in the cab of a water-authority supply truck as far as the old railhead and walking from there.

The driver, a young man named Cifuentes who had been a boy of nine when Pozo Nuevo's name was still spoken aloud by no one at all, offered to take him farther. Orsen declined. There were things a man ought to measure with his own feet, and this was one of them: the distance between the last sleeper and the rim of the meseta, which he had walked twice before — once with fourteen animals and a secret worth killing for, once in his memory every night since — and which he wanted to walk now carrying nothing heavier than a canvas roll and a letter he had written and rewritten eleven times.

"You'll want water," Cifuentes said, meaning it kindly, meaning it the way everyone meant everything now that the story had been printed and reprinted and argued over in the newspapers of three countries. The man who found the lake. The engineer who told the truth. Orsen had learned to let the phrases pass over him like weather. "There's a tank at the seep now. And a cairn with a tin box, if you want to leave word."

"I know," Orsen said. "I helped build both."

The truck pulled away in a fan of dust and he stood a while listening to the engine fade, because silence out here did not begin until you could no longer hear anything man-made at all, and he had missed that silence the way other men missed music.

Then he shouldered his pack and began to count.

Not miles. He had given up counting miles. He counted instead the things Tomás had taught him to notice without instruments: the way the scrub thickened where groundwater ran shallow beneath the caliche, the particular green-gray of coirón grass that meant moisture within reach of roots, the flight lines of birds that had no business being this far from the coast except that now there was a reason for them to come inland. A year ago there had been no birds here. Now there were flamingos, absurdly pink against the dun plateau, standing in the shallows of the first holding pond the new authority had dug at the edge of the old rail spur. He stopped and watched them for ten minutes and did not write the time down anywhere, and the not-writing felt like a kind of wealth.

* * *

The walk took him four days. He was fifty-one years old and his knees had opinions about caliche slopes, and he permitted himself the slowness. That was another thing the year had taught him: that slowness was not failure. For twelve years he had moved through Patagonia at the pace of a schedule, measuring sleepers by habit, laying each day end to end like rail, and he had called the habit discipline because he had never dared call it what it was, which was hiding. A man can hide inside a routine more thoroughly than inside a wilderness. He had hidden so well that when Efraín Salas opened that kerosene-smelling hotel room door and looked at him with eyes already going yellow, Orsen had understood immediately why a dying stranger had chosen him: not because he was honest, but because he was empty. A dying man does not entrust his life's work to a full man. He gives it to whoever has room.

He had room then. He did not have room now, and he found he did not mind. Fullness sat differently than it used to — less like weight, more like ballast.

On the second evening he camped at the dry wash where they had caught Tomás, three years ago now, though the arithmetic still surprised him; it felt like two years and also like twenty. He ate his supper sitting on the same flat stone where Ingrid had stood with her hand fisted in Tomás's collar, both of them crying, all three of them crying, the oilcloth map crumpled between them like a dead bird until Orsen smoothed it out and said the only wise thing he said that whole year, which was: "We are not going to be the people who break this over a boy's good heart." He thought about writing to Tomás from here and decided against it. Tomás would be at the pumping station by now — the first one, the one they'd named after Efraín Salas over Ingrid's fierce objection and secret pleasure — and letters written from the field always arrived smelling of apology. He would tell them everything in person, in a month, when he came down off the plateau. Some news wanted a face attached.

He slept badly, as he always did at that wash, and dreamed of gravel sliding, and woke before dawn with his ribs aching on the old scar where Ballesteros's bullet had grazed him, which was nonsense, of course, wounds did not keep anniversaries, but he lay there anyway in the gray light letting the ache speak, because he had learned that some pains were just the body remembering what the mind had agreed to carry forward.

* * *

The third day he climbed onto the meseta proper, and the wind met him the way it always met him, like an old colleague with no manners.

It had nearly killed them, that wind. Three days of viento blanco, two mules buried, the trail erased, Tomás's chronometer drifting and his calculations compounding their error until on the ninth day Orsen — Orsen Nystrom, who had never been lost, who had crossed the dry side eleven times without a landmark missed — had stood on a featureless plain under a rinsed-blue sky and admitted aloud, in front of his crew, in a voice that cracked: "I don't know where we are."

He had expected the admission to destroy him. It had been, after all, the second disgrace of his life, and the first one — the one he never named, the one the company's lawyers had dragged into the hearing like something exhumed — had cost him twelve years of exile into routine. But Ingrid had simply looked at him across the trampled grass and said, "Good. Now we can start looking properly," and had knelt and begun reading the ground the way her father had taught her without ever knowing he was teaching: the converging threads of guanaco paths, the faint darkening of soil, the logic of thirst written in animal grammar. She had led them to the seep. She had led them through the Escalante when his fever burned at a hundred and four and he mistook her, over and over, for the dead of Pozo Nuevo, and she had answered every delirious demand with the same terrible patience: "I promise. I promise. I promise." She had tapped out forty-one names on a frontier telegraph key with hands that shook only after the last name was sent.

And when it was done — when the charter was suspended and the lake passed to trusteeship and Ballesteros stood in the witness box trading his superiors for his own neck with the smooth efficiency of a man changing trains — she had stood in her father's empty house in Comodoro Rivadavia among fifty years of unopened parcels and finally wept for the man who had watched a town die at fifteen and spent half a century buying back the truth piece by piece because shame, he had written, is heavier than water.

Orsen had read that line — *shame is heavier than water* — perhaps two hundred times since. He had copied it into the front of his field notebooks. He had said it aloud at the dedication of the pumping station while Tomás held the microphone too close and Ingrid stood with her jaw set the way she set it when she was refusing to cry in public. He understood it now better than he had at the reading, because he had spent the year learning the weight of his own shame, the old one, the unnamed one, and had discovered that a shame spoken weighs almost nothing. It is only the carried ones that crush.

His disgrace, for the record — and it was on the record now, printed in the transcript, read aloud by a lawyer with a voice like oiled leather — was this: at thirty-six, as junior surveyor on the Sierra Grande extension, he had signed a gradient calculation he knew to be optimistic, because the chief engineer asked him to and because the chief engineer had given him his first job and Orsen had confused gratitude with loyalty. A bridge settled. No one died. A foreman lost his leg. The company paid quietly, transferred Orsen to the dead lines of the dry side, and let him understand, without ever saying it, that his career would proceed exactly as far as his silence. Twelve years later a dying prospector handed him a map and a promise and Orsen had recognized the transaction instantly, because he had been living inside its mirror image his entire adult life.

That was why he had burned his copy of the 1901 survey in chapter five of his own life, so to speak — though nothing in a life has chapters until afterward. Not because he feared the company. Because he recognized, in the act of burning, what he had been doing for twelve years: destroying evidence of what he knew.

* * *

He reached the rim above the buried lake in the middle of the fourth afternoon, and the wind, for once, fell away.

The cavern mouth looked smaller than he remembered. They had concealed it with brush during those bad nights when Ballesteros camped within rifle range on the opposite rise, and afterward the authority had debated at length whether to seal it, fence it, or leave it open, and had finally compromised on a low stone wall and a bronze plaque that Orsen had personally insisted be worded plainly: *Here lies water enough for a province. Here lay forty-one people the railway let die of thirst rather than admit a mistake.* No poetry. Poetry was for afterward, and afterward belonged to other people.

He sat on the wall with his back to the sun and looked down into the dark.

The lake was still there. Of course it was; lakes do not go anywhere. But he had needed to see it, the way a man needs to visit a grave not because the dead require it but because the living do. The water lay black and utterly still below him, forty feet down the sloped passage, holding the plateau's secret the way it had held it for ten thousand years — patient, indifferent, enormous. Enough water for a province. Exactly where the railway's official surveys said there was none, because saying otherwise would have meant admitting the 1901 well, the 1901 payroll, the forty-one workers who dug it, and the town of Pozo Nuevo whose adobe walls they had capped the well beneath and rerouted the aquifer away from, choosing a coal port over four hundred souls with the tidy arithmetic of men who never had to look at the graves.

Forty-one graves, dated 1902. Forty-one names, sent by telegraph from Gobernador by a woman whose father had been the paymaster's boy. One headstone, placed last spring by that same woman, on grave number nineteen — her grandfather's brother, the paymaster himself, who stayed with his crew when the water failed and was buried among them. Ingrid had planted a fence post where the well was. She had told Orsen about it afterward in a letter of uncharacteristic gentleness: *I put up a fence around nothing, Orsen. It is the best thing I have ever built.*

He took the canvas roll from his pack.

Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth that had been replaced twice but was folded the same way Efraín Salas had folded it fifty years ago, was the original map. The real one — not the decoy Ballesteros seized at the narrows, not the copies distributed to the tribunal and the newspapers and the water authority's archives. The route drawn in a dying man's shaking hand across the Meseta del Viento, following a line the railway surveyed in 1901 and never built, to a point marked with a single word: AGUA. The map that had started everything. The map Orsen had promised to bring home and tell no one about, and had kept both halves of the promise in ways Efraín could never have predicted — bringing home not the daughter alone but the daughter plus a truth, telling no one until telling became the only honest act left.

He had thought about this for a year. He had drafted and discarded eleven letters explaining his decision, and in the end he had understood that the decision needed no explanation, which was why the twelfth letter was only four sentences long and fit in his breast pocket:

*Efraín — I am leaving your map with the water. You spent fifty years buying it back piece by piece, and I think you bought it for the lake, not for the archives. The lake kept your secret when no one else would have. Let it keep this too. Your daughter sends her love, which arrived too late to deliver and has nowhere else to go, so I am putting it here with the map. — O.N.*

He went down the passage slowly, one hand on the rock, his boots finding the ledges his memory had preserved better than any notebook. At the water's edge he knelt. The air was cool and mineral and absolutely silent, the silence of underground, which is different from the silence of the plateau — older, denser, the silence of things that have never been interrupted.

He unwrapped the map and held it a moment in the lamplight he'd brought, and saw again what he had seen the first night in Puerto Deseado, studying it by kerosene flame with unease wrapped in obligation: the careful line, the patient route, the single word at its terminus. A man's whole life reduced to a route and a destination. He had thought then that it was a treasure map. It had taken him a year and a desert and forty-one graves to understand that it was a confession — and that confessions, unlike treasures, want to be delivered.

He weighted the oilcloth with three stones at the water's edge, where the spring floods would never reach but the damp could work slowly into the fabric, and where anyone who came after — a surveyor, a historian, a child of the plateau on some school excursion fifty years hence — might find it and understand that the water had been loved before it was useful.

Then he did something he had not planned. He put his hand flat on the surface of the lake, just for a moment, the way you touch a shoulder. The water was cold beyond cold, ancient, moving with currents too slow to feel. He left his hand there while he said the words he had said at the hearing and at the dedication and alone in his rooms on bad nights, the forty-one names, all of them, in order, from the ledger in the cairn — Ambrosio, Baltazar, Catalán — and when he finished he said one more name, the forty-second, the one that was never on any payroll: *Efraín Salas, who survived, and paid for surviving.*

The ripples from his hand traveled out and did not come back. Underground water does not return what it takes. He found that fitting.

* * *

He climbed out of the cavern in the last hour of daylight, and the sun was doing what the sun did here on clear autumn evenings: setting the scrubland gold.

Not metaphorically gold — actually gold, the coirón grass lit from the side so that every tussock threw a shadow twice its height and the whole plain seemed to be made of struck metal, of value, of the thing prospectors cross deserts looking for and never find because they are looking down. Orsen stood on the rim and let the light pour over him and thought about horizons.

For thirty years he had treated the horizon as a measurement. The farthest visible point, the limit of survey, the place where the known ended and estimation began. He had walked toward horizons the way he walked toward everything — counting paces, fixing bearings, converting distance into progress and progress into worth. The horizon had been the edge of the world and therefore the edge of the job, and he had believed, without ever articulating it, that if he reached enough edges he would eventually arrive somewhere.

But a horizon, he understood now, standing in the gold with the wind beginning again at his back, is not an edge at all. It is an agreement between the land and the eye. Move toward it and it retreats, not out of cruelty but out of geometry; it is the one destination that exists only in the approach. Every horizon he had ever measured was simply the place where his seeing stopped, not where the world did. The world went on — behind the ridges, under the caliche, beneath the black still water — going on whether or not anyone surveyed it, holding its aquifers and its graves and its fifty-year confessions in the dark, complete and unmeasured and sufficient.

The horizon was never the edge of anything. It was an invitation. He had just spent thirty years misreading the invitation as a boundary, and one year learning to read it rightly, and the tuition had been everything: his career, his certainties, two mules, a graze along the ribs, and the comfortable fiction that loyalty means keeping silent for the people who pay you. What he had bought with the tuition was harder to itemize. A daughter-figure who argued with him about water arithmetic and won. An apprentice who betrayed him out of love and was forgiven out of the same. A town with its name restored to the maps. A pumping station named for a dead man who would have hated the ceremony and loved the water. And this: an evening in gold, alone on a rim, wanting nothing.

He shouldered his pack — lighter now by one map, by one confession delivered — and turned east, toward the coast, toward Comodoro, toward Tomás and Ingrid and the month of ordinary days he had promised himself.

Before he took the first step he looked back once, not at the cavern but at the long line where the plateau met the sky, burning now from gold into copper into the deep violet that comes just before the stars. Somewhere out there, past that line, the desert loop ran its two hundred waterless miles where they had nearly died and been saved by guanaco paths and a woman's eye. Somewhere behind him, under his boots, a province's worth of water waited in the dark for the pipes that would carry it to towns that would never know how near it had come to staying a secret forever.

He stepped off the rim and began to walk, and because he was who he was, he counted — but this time he counted seasons, birthdays, letters, the small accumulating units of a belonging chosen rather than assigned. The sun went down behind him and did not need him to watch it. The land went on in every direction, edged by nothing, and Orsen Nystrom walked out into the gold with the horizon ahead of him, retreating gently, invitingly, the way it always had, the way it always would —

— and somewhere far to the south, past the line where the light failed, a lamp was already burning in a window he could not yet see, and someone who knew his stride was setting a third plate at the table.
