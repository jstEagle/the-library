## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning fog still clung to the valley when Petra Ishikawa brought the Cessna down onto the dirt strip at Kaliwatu, wheels kissing earth so gently that the chickens scratching near the fuel drums didn't even look up. Three hundred and twelve kilometers from the coast, eleven hundred meters above the sea, and she could put a plane down on a ribbon of red clay narrower than her father's boat had been long.

"Show-off," Milo said from the right seat, not looking up from his checklist. He said it every time. It was their version of good morning.

"Somebody has to be," she said, and killed the engine.

The highlands came up to meet them in green terraces, coffee and cassava stitched across the slopes like someone had sewn the mountain into a quilt. The air up here smelled different than the coast—cooler, woodsmoke and wet earth instead of salt and diesel—and for the first ten minutes after landing, every time, Petra let herself breathe it like it was new. That was the trick of a life lived in motion: everything was new if you never stayed long enough to wear it smooth.

Nurse Ayu was already waiting at the edge of the strip with two crates of vaccines on a hand cart and a boy of maybe nine holding the umbrella against a sun that hadn't fully committed yet. Petra signed the cold-chain log without taking off her headset, checked the temperature strips out of habit even though the box hadn't been opened since Sari packed it at dawn, and helped load the crates onto the clinic's ancient motorbike sidecar while Milo flirted with the boy about whether airplanes or water buffalo were faster.

"Buffalo," the boy said solemnly, "if the airplane is tired."

"The airplane is never tired," Milo said gravely. "Only the pilot."

Petra snorted and turned away before anyone could see her smile.

This was the run she knew best. Kaliwatu twice a month, vaccines out, sputum samples back, sometimes a patient strapped into the second seat if the clinic needed an evacuation—once a woman in labor, once a man who'd lost an argument with a chainsaw, both of them alive now because Petra flew like the weather owed her money. She knew the cloud patterns over the saddle between the twin volcanoes, knew which ravine funneled wind at midday, knew that if she left the coast by six she'd be back on the water by noon with the whole afternoon still hers.

Hers. That was the word she kept using, and the word that kept not meaning anything.

"You're doing the thing again," Milo said.

They were walking back to the plane, preflight done by memory, the fog burned off and the strip steaming faintly. Petra looked at him. "What thing."

"The counting thing." He tapped his temple. "You get this look. Like you're running arithmetic behind your eyes. Kilometers to the next strip, hours to the next job, days until the next monsoon window. You count everything except what normal people count."

"And what do normal people count?"

"Birthdays. Anniversaries. How long they've lived somewhere." He swung himself up into the right seat and grinned at her sideways, that easy grin that made half the coastal towns assume they were married and the other half assume they were siblings. "Three years I've flown with you, Petra. I know your birthday is in there somewhere because you bought me beer on mine, but I couldn't tell you the date. I don't think you've told me."

"It's a date," she said, checking the magnetos. "It happens once a year. Like yours."

"Cagey." He settled back, hands behind his head, watching her work. "One day I'm going to figure out what you're running from, and then I'm going to buy you a cake so big you'll have to land somewhere long enough to eat it."

"I don't run from things." She said it lightly. She'd had three years of practice saying it lightly. "I fly toward things. There's a difference. Aerodynamically speaking."

"Aerodynamically speaking," Milo agreed, "you're the best pilot in the Triangle. Humanly speaking, you live out of a duffel bag in a hangar and you flinch whenever anyone says the word *home* like it's a hornet."

She ran up the engine to cover whatever her face was doing. The propeller washed the morning flat and loud, and then they were rolling, lifting off the red clay, and the mountains fell away beneath them in folds of green, and there it was—the feeling, the one she never named, the one that arrived every single time the wheels left the ground. Relief. Pure, chemical relief, like a held breath finally released.

That was the problem with flying, she'd decided long ago. Everyone thought pilots loved takeoff because of the view. But Petra loved it because for four seconds, every time, she got to feel like she was escaping something. And then the plane leveled off and the something turned out to be still aboard, sitting quietly in the seat beside her, wearing her own face.

* * *

They crossed the coastline an hour later, the sea coming up blue and enormous, and Petra felt the shift in herself that always happened over water—the loosening, the quickening, the way her hands went sure on the yoke as if the ocean were a language she'd been born speaking and had sworn off speaking aloud.

Below, the Coral Triangle unrolled itself: reef systems glowing turquoise through the shallows like veins of light under skin, fishing boats trailing white wakes, an island chain strung out toward the horizon like beads on a wire. Somewhere down there were wrecks nobody had ever mapped, channels nobody had ever named, and a boat called the *Kestrel* lying on the bottom of a reef she did not allow herself to think about, which meant she thought about it roughly every forty minutes.

"Coastal strip's got traffic," Milo said, scanning. "There's the ferry, there's the dive boats... hey. Is that Sari's cousin's junk? Tell me Sari didn't actually buy that floating coffin."

"If she did, we're not helping her fix it. We're having an intervention." Petra banked toward the strip, eyes doing the sweep—windsock, runway surface, other traffic—and snagged on something that didn't belong.

A vessel anchored well off the point, too large for a pleasure craft, too clean for a working trawler. White hull, dark glass, a crane on the aft deck folded neat as a sleeping insect. Survey vessel, her mind supplied automatically. Hydrographic, maybe. Or salvage.

She looked away from it the way you look away from a door you don't want to open.

By the time they were tied down and the vaccine return crates were loaded, the heat had come on full, and Sari was waiting for them under the hangar awning with an expression Petra had learned to dread. Sari had two expressions: the one where the engine was fine, and the one where it wasn't. This was the second one, but worse—it was the one where the engine was fine but the bank account wasn't.

"Don't say it," Petra said.

"Our number two cylinder is eating metal," Sari said anyway. "I can baby it another month if we don't fly heavy. After that, we ground her, and grounding means no income, and no income means—" she gestured elegantly at the hangar, the plane, the whole precarious architecture of their lives, "—this stops being a business and starts being a memory."

"How much?"

"For a proper overhaul? Numbers I don't want to say out loud in front of the plane. She's sensitive."

Milo whistled low. Petra stood very still, doing the arithmetic behind her eyes, the counting thing, miles and rupiah and flight hours all stacking into columns that refused to balance. They had enough for fuel and fees and Sari's wages through the end of the month. Maybe. If nothing broke. Something had broken.

"There's a man been asking after you," Sari said, and her voice changed, careful now, watching Petra's face the way you watch weather. "Came by yesterday evening. Well-dressed. Said he represents a client wanting to charter us—medevac capability plus recovery support, two weeks minimum, terms generous enough that I made him repeat them."

"We don't do recovery work." Petra heard her own voice go flat and hard, a door closing. "We fly medicine. That's the whole business model."

"He said the client knows that. Said the client specifically wants *you*. Said—" Sari hesitated, and pulled a folded sheet of paper from her pocket, "—he left paperwork. Charter agreement, insurance riders, the lot. All drawn up proper. Signature page already executed by the client's principal."

Milo leaned in first, because Milo had no survival instincts where interesting things were concerned. "Two weeks, medevac and recovery, that rate is triple our—okay, wow. Okay." He looked up. "Who signs like that? Look at it. That's fountain pen. Who uses fountain pen on a charter contract?"

Petra took the paper because not taking it would have been worse.

She knew the handwriting before she read the name. She would have known it upside down, underwater, in the dark. A certain forward slant on the capitals, the V formed in two strokes like a bird's wing, the way he crossed his T's slightly left of center as if always reaching back for something. She had watched that hand draw reef charts across a galley table by lamplight. She had worn a ring chosen by that hand. She had spent three years making sure she never saw it again.

**E. Vane**, the signature line read. *Elias Vane.*

The strip seemed to tilt. Somewhere behind her a gull screamed, and the tide breathed against the seawall, and Petra Ishikawa—who could hold a Cessna steady in crosswinds that made grown charter pilots sweat—stood in the shade of her own hangar and could not make her hands stop shaking.

"Petra?" Milo's voice, distant. "You know this guy?"

"No," she said.

It was the oldest lie she owned. She kept it oiled and ready, and it worked exactly as badly as it always had.

Sari and Milo exchanged a look over her head. Then Sari cleared her throat and said, gently, in the tone of a mechanic delivering bad news about an engine: "The fee covers the overhaul. All of it. Plus six months' operating costs. Whatever history you've got with this client, Petra—we vote tomorrow. But tonight, sleep on what it costs us to say no."

Petra looked out past the hangar doors, past the strip, to the white ship riding at anchor off the point, dark-glassed and patient, like something that had been waiting three years and could wait three more.

The sea said nothing. It never did. That was the thing people loved about it, and the thing Petra had never forgiven it for.
