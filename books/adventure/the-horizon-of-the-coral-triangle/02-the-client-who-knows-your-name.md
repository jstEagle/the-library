## 2. The Client Who Knows Your Name

The coastal strip at Talisay Point was a scar of crushed coral and heat-shimmer, and Petra Ishikawa had landed on it forty-one times without once calling it home. She knew the number because she counted everything now—landings, fuel drums, nautical miles, the days since the last time she'd thought about the past on purpose instead of in her sleep.

The *Albatross*—her father's name for her, never the plane's—settled onto the strip with the tired grace of an old bird coming in to roost. Milo killed the throttle and immediately began his post-flight liturgy: flaps up, mixture lean, brakes tested twice because he'd once seen a man roll a perfectly good aircraft into a fish pond.

"Forty-two," he said, tapping the logbook. "You're keeping score again."

"I keep records. It's called aviation."

"You keep *scores*. There's a difference." He grinned at her, that easy nineteen-year-old grin that made him look like he'd never lost anything, which was more or less true. "Forty-two landings this month. You know what I did on my birthday? Nothing. Because nobody remembered it except you, and you remembered it by telling me how many miles we'd flown since then."

"Eleven thousand, two hundred and six," Petra said, unclipping her harness. "Statute."

"*Statute.* See? That's not a person, that's a spreadsheet with a pilot's license." He hauled their medicine crates toward the shade of Sari's open-sided hangar while Petra stood a moment longer in the propeller wash, letting the wind flatten the tall grass at the strip's edge. Beyond it, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky already stacking clouds to the east. Early monsoon, the radio had said. Days ahead of forecast.

She didn't mind storms. Storms were honest. They came when they came, wrecked what they wrecked, and left. It was the quiet things—the polite things—that had taught her otherwise.

* * *

Sari was under the *Albatross*'s port engine when Petra found her, lying on a creeper with a flashlight clenched in her teeth like a pirate's knife, muttering in three languages about cylinder head temperatures.

"How bad?" Petra asked.

Sari rolled out, sat up, and pushed her welding goggles onto her forehead, leaving a pale band across a face brown as teak. She was fifty if she was thirty-five, built like something salvaged and refitted, and she had been keeping the *Albatross* alive on prayer and scavenged parts for three years.

"Bad enough that I'm glad you're sitting down." She nodded at the crate beside the workbench. "Sit."

Petra didn't sit. "Sari."

"Cylinder four's cracked through the jug. Not cracked *at*. Through. And the spare we've been cannibalizing is worse. We need a factory overhaul kit, or a whole engine, and both of those cost more than the plane's worth on paper." Sari wiped her hands on a rag that had started life white sometime during the previous administration. "I can keep us flying for maybe six weeks if I baby her. After that, she's a very expensive lawn ornament."

Petra looked at the engine—at the hairline seam of corrosion she could just see from this angle—and felt the particular vertigo of a woman whose entire life was held together by one machine and two people who loved it more than they loved sense.

Six weeks. She'd flown out of tighter margins. She'd find another donor engine, another favor, another strip where somebody owed somebody. There was always another—

"There's mail," Sari said, too casually.

"We don't get mail. We get fuel receipts and malaria advisories."

"This came by launch this morning. Courier said hand-deliver, signature required." Sari produced a heavy cream envelope, sealed with actual wax, which on a coastal strip in the Coral Triangle was roughly equivalent to receiving a live flamingo. "It's addressed to *Albatross Air Services*, care of the strip. Which is interesting, because until last month we weren't even a services company. We were a favor with wings."

Petra took it. The paper was cool and dense, the kind of stationery that got used in boardrooms and embassies. Her name wasn't on it. That alone should have told her everything.

She broke the seal.

Inside: a charter contract, three pages, typed except for the signature block. A medevac-and-recovery operation, seven days, hazard rates doubled, a retainer that would have made her laugh if the number hadn't been large enough to buy a new engine outright, overhaul kit included, plus six months' operating capital. Destination listed only as a set of coordinates. Vessel coordination provided by the client's own survey ship.

And at the bottom, in ink, signed:

**E. Vane**

Petra didn't read the name first. She read the handwriting.

Three pages of typed text, but the initials in the header block—*E.V.*—were handwritten, and handwriting doesn't change. Not in three years, not in thirty. The *E* with its long deliberate tail. The *V* struck like a keel cutting water. She'd watched that hand draw survey charts across a galley table by lamplight, tracing depth contours around her father's boat while Elias talked about currents and she pretended she wasn't watching his fingers.

Elias Vane.

The strip seemed to tilt. Somewhere behind her a generator coughed and caught; gulls argued over fish guts; Milo laughed at something only Milo found funny. The world went on being ordinary, and Petra stood holding three pages of generous terms like a letter bomb with the fuse already burned down.

"He knows where we are," she said.

"Apparently," Sari said. "Which means he's known for a while. You don't courier wax-sealed contracts to a strip unless you've done your homework."

"I changed the company registration twice."

"And he still found us." Sari's voice was gentle, which was how Petra knew she was about to be outvoted. "Petra. Look at the numbers."

"I'm looking at the name."

"The name pays for an engine. The name pays for six months of medicine runs. The name keeps Milo fed and keeps me in parts and keeps this outfit alive through monsoon season, when nobody charters anybody." Sari stood, arms crossed, and fixed her with the look she usually reserved for engines that were lying to her. "So before you tell me no, tell me what your no costs."

* * *

They convened the crew council that evening, which meant the three of them and a mosquito coil on the hangar floor, the contract spread between them like a card game none of them wanted to deal.

Milo read it twice, lips moving. "Medevac-and-recovery. So we fly support for a salvage op. We're not diving, right? We're not going down there?"

"The client's vessel handles recovery," Petra said. "We provide air cover, casualty evacuation capability, and logistics. Seven days."

"Where?"

"Coordinates off the eastern approaches." She kept her voice level. She'd had three years of practice keeping her voice level, and it worked on everyone except herself.

Milo looked at the coordinates, then at her face, then back at the coordinates, and she watched him do the arithmetic—watched him realize he didn't have the map knowledge to do it, and decide not to ask. That was Milo. Loyal first, curious second, and smart enough to sequence them that way.

"Retainer clears our debt at the fuel dock," he said. "Twice over. And the hazard clause—if anyone gets hurt, medevac's pre-paid, unlimited. Petra, we've been flying medevacs on promises for a year. Half our clients pay in chickens."

"One client paid in a canoe," Sari said.

"A *good* canoe."

"It's not the money," Petra said.

"No," Sari agreed. "It's the money, and it's also not the money. Both things are true. Welcome to being an adult with employees."

"I have a history with this client."

"You have a history with *everyone*, or you'd have an address." Milo said it without malice, which was the worst way to say it. "Three years, Petra. Three years and I still couldn't tell anyone where you're from. You've got a birthdate you won't celebrate, a surname you shorten on every manifest, and a reaction to that signature like it bit you. I'm not asking for the story. I'm asking if the story's going to sink us, or if it's just going to make you miserable for a week while we get paid."

Petra looked at the contract. At the coordinates she could feel through the paper like heat. Somewhere east of those numbers lay a reef she had sworn off the way other people swear off drink, and at the bottom of the water near it, a boat with her father's name still legible on the transom, probably, if the coral hadn't taken it. If the syndicate hadn't stripped it. If three years of currents hadn't finished what three years of silence had started.

If she said no, they'd limp into monsoon season on a dying engine and good intentions, and good intentions had a service ceiling.

If she said yes—

"Yes," she heard herself say, and hated how much of her relief wasn't about the engine. "Conditional. We take the charter, but I negotiate the terms face to face, and I set the operating boundaries. Nobody dives but their people. We stay out of the water. And if I say we leave, we leave, mid-job, no refunds, no arguments. Those go in writing before wheels-up."

Milo pumped his fist. Sari just nodded slowly, watching Petra with the expression of a mechanic listening to an engine she suspected was running hot.

"I'll send confirmation tonight," Sari said. "Courier waits till dawn."

"Fine."

"Petra." Sari gathered the contract, then paused. "For what it's worth—he wrote your company's name correctly. *Albatross Air Services.* Full legal registration, including the amendment you filed under the name you don't use anymore."

The mosquito coil sent up its thin gray rope of smoke. Outside, the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon began ticking against the hangar roof, days ahead of schedule, the way the worst things always arrived.

"He didn't sign it *Elias Vane*," Petra said quietly. "He signed *E. Vane*. Like he knew I'd recognize the hand before I could stomach the name."

Sari said nothing. There was nothing to say that the rain wasn't already saying, drumming harder now, washing the dust off the strip, erasing the day's landings one drop at a time.

That night Petra slept in the hammock she strung wherever they happened to be, and dreamed—not of the reef, not yet—but of a galley table, lamplight, and a hand drawing depth contours around a boat called the *Kestrel*, while a voice she'd spent three years forgetting said, *the currents are shifting, Petra. Whatever's down there won't stay buried.*

Dawn brought the courier back with a reply, one line, that same unmistakable hand:

*Thank you. Rendezvous instructions enclosed. — E.V.*

And beneath it, a single sentence that turned her blood to cold seawater:

*Bring your dive gear anyway. You'll want to see this yourself.*
