## 3. Cargo That Breathes

The harbor at dawn smelled of diesel and salt and the green rot of fish scales scraped off the pier by men who'd been working since before the light. Wren had been awake since three, folded into the space behind the medical crates where the foam padding made a kind of nest, listening to the island wake up around them: generators coughing, a rooster with delusions of grandeur, the slap of water against hulls in a rhythm that felt, for the first time in months, like something close to safety.

They'd learned to sleep anywhere. That was what three years of moving did to you—taught you that a bed was a rumor and warmth was a skill you practiced. The hold of this little cargo plane-turned-boat-runner was warmer than most places Wren had slept, smelling of antiseptic and burlap and someone's spilled coffee, and the vibration of the loading crew's boots overhead came through the frame like a heartbeat.

Wren kept their eyes closed and counted the footsteps. Two heavy ones—the mechanic, by the drag of her left boot—and one lighter, quicker, stopping often. The pilot. Petra Ishikawa. Wren knew the name the way you know the name of a storm: from other people's mouths, dropped carefully, always in low voices near harbors. *She flies medicine out to the islands nobody else will service. She doesn't talk about herself. Don't ask her about her father.*

Everyone said that last part. Nobody said why.

Wren hadn't planned on being discovered asleep among the crates. It just wasn't the worst opening they'd ever had. Better than being pulled out of the water at midnight by fishermen who wanted payment; better than swimming the last channel with a dive bag held over their head. At least this way, when the moment came, Wren would be rested, dry, and holding something valuable.

The crate beside them shifted. Light poured in—a blade of it, gold-white, cutting across Wren's face.

"Well," said a voice above them, female, dry as sun-baked rope. "Either my medical supplies have learned to breathe, or I've got a stowaway."

Wren opened their eyes. Against the glare stood a woman in a faded flight jacket, dark hair tied back without ceremony, a clipboard held loosely at her side like she'd forgotten it was there. Her face was harder than the harbor gossip had promised, but her eyes were doing something interesting: cataloguing. Exits, hands, weight, threat. Wren recognized the exercise because they performed it too, every day, on everyone.

"Good morning," Wren said, and sat up slowly, hands visible, movements unhurried. Trust was a thing you built in the first five seconds or never. "I can explain."

"You're sitting on two crates of amoxicillin."

"I'm sitting *beside* two crates of amoxicillin." Wren lifted one hip to show the gap. "I checked before I lay down. I'm not an animal."

The woman didn't smile. Behind her, a broad man with a sunburned neck leaned into the hold—Milo, the copilot, if the harbor talk was right—and let out a low whistle.

"Petra," he said. "We've got a situation."

"We've got a *passenger*," Wren corrected. They reached into the canvas bag at their side, unhurried, watching both of them track the movement, and brought out a small cloth pouch. They loosened the drawstring and tipped it: pearls rolled into the light, six of them, silver-pink, catching the dawn like small captive moons. "Passage to the reef at Ternate Deep. I pay up front."

Silence. Milo whistled again, quieter this time, reverent.

Petra didn't look at the pearls. She looked at Wren—longer, harder, the way you look at weather coming over the horizon. "How old are you?"

"Old enough to dive."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I give for free."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement exactly, but its distant cousin. She crouched, bringing her eyes level, and Wren saw the tiredness under the iron, three years deep at least. "Ternate Deep is not a tourist site. It's a wreck site. And it's not open to passengers."

"It's open to me." Wren closed the pouch, drew the string tight. "You're going there anyway. I know your contract. Medevac-and-recovery, signed by a surveyor named Vane. Everyone at the fuel dock talks; they think nobody listens." They shrugged. "I need to get to that reef. You need money—I heard your mechanic say the engine part alone costs more than the plane's worth. So here we are."

"You've been in my hold since when?"

"Since you went to dinner last night. Your watchman is very thorough. He checked twice." A pause. "I was under the tarp the third time."

Milo laughed despite himself, then smothered it when Petra turned her head a fraction of an inch.

"Get out of my plane," Petra said.

Wren had expected this. They'd rehearsed it on the beach at low tide, all the versions. But rehearsal was nothing against the actual weight of her voice, flat and final, and for one unsteady second Wren felt the old vertigo—the one that came whenever a door started closing, the one that meant another night in the shallows, another cold camp, another week further from the reef where their whole life had come apart.

So they did the only thing they had left. They told the truth.

"My mother dove that wreck," Wren said. "Before it was a wreck. She was deckhand on a boat called the Kestrel."

The words landed in the hold like something dropped from height. Petra went very still. Not shocked—Wren watched her fight not to be shocked—but still, the way water goes still over something enormous underneath.

"That boat sank three years ago," Petra said quietly. "There were no survivors."

"There was one survivor." Wren held her gaze. "He wasn't on the boat. He was sixteen, he was ashore buying fuel, and he's been trying to get back to that reef ever since. My mother was Ana Reyes. She wore a red bandana on deck because she said the sea should know her coming." Their voice caught, just barely; they let it. Honesty was currency, and they were spending all of it. "I'm not asking you to take me to a wreck, Captain. I'm asking you to take me to my mother's grave."

The harbor noise filled the silence—gulls, diesel, the far shout of men hauling nets. Milo had stopped breathing entirely. Somewhere above them, Sari's boots crossed the fuselage and paused, listening.

Petra rose. She looked at the pearls in Wren's palm, then out through the hold door at the water, gray-gold and endless, and Wren watched her do the arithmetic—not of money, but of grief. Recognizing it was easy. Wren had seen the same calculation in mirrors.

"The pearls stay with me either way," Petra said finally. "If I put you off at the next strip, you don't get them back."

"Fair."

"If you cause trouble on my boat—any trouble—I'll tie you to a mooring buoy and let the tide decide."

"Also fair."

"And you dive only when I say, where I say, with gear I inspect myself." She held out her hand, palm up, not for the pearls—for agreement. "Those are my terms. Take them or swim."

Wren took the hand. Her grip was calloused and strong and brief, and in the half-second of contact Wren felt her pulse jump once, quickly, like a bird startled off water. She was more afraid of this trip than she wanted anyone to know. That was useful to know. That was also sad enough that Wren decided not to use it.

"Milo," Petra called, turning away. "Log our passenger. Wren Reyes. One berth, no meals guaranteed, full fare paid."

"In pearls," Milo said, grinning, weighing the pouch in his big hand. "Captain, we are officially the fanciest medicine boat in the Triangle."

"Sari will want to check the pearls aren't glass," Petra said. "They usually are."

"These aren't," Wren said.

Petra paused at the hold door, silhouetted against the brightening water, and looked back—one last catalogue, exits and hands and weight and threat. But this time something else moved behind her eyes, something Wren couldn't name yet, and it unsettled them more than any suspicion could have.

Because Wren had spent three years learning to read people on water, and what they saw in Petra Ishikawa's face just then wasn't doubt about a stranger in her hold.

It was recognition. As if Wren's ease on the water—as if the way they'd slept through engine noise and woken calm in a rocking hull—had shown her something she'd spent years trying not to see.

* * *

Sari found them an hour later, coiled in the rigging of the beached pontoon, testing a regulator against their lips the way other people tested fruit. The mechanic was squat and strong, with grease worked so deep into her knuckles it looked like tattooing, and she examined Wren the way she examined engines: without mercy, without hurry.

"Dive bag," she said. "Open it."

Wren opened it. Mask, fins, knife, slate, spare O-rings, a waterproof tube sealed with wax. Sari took the tube, weighed it, shook it next to her ear like a child with a present.

"What's inside?"

"Maps," Wren said. "My mother's. Channels around Ternate Deep that aren't on any chart you own."

Sari's eyebrows climbed. "Uncharted channels."

"Charted once. By someone the charts forgot." Wren held out a hand, and after a moment Sari gave the tube back. "She drew them herself. Reef passages, current windows, a mangrove cut that runs behind Ternate Island. She used to say the official maps are written by people who've never been afraid."

"And you're afraid?"

Wren looked out at the harbor, at the little plane-boat riding its mooring, at Petra on the dock talking in low tones with a fuel agent who kept glancing at a letter in his hand. Even from here Wren could see the way she stood—shoulders squared toward the paper like it might bite.

"Terrified," Wren said. "That's how I know the maps are good."

Sari snorted, wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands, and jerked her chin toward the boat. "Come on, then, ghost-child. If you're diving my wrecks, you'll help load my crates. And tell me about these channels while we work. The captain says monsoon season's coming early this year, and I don't like the look of that sky."

Wren slid down from the rigging, landed soft on the sand, and fell into step. Overhead the gulls wheeled, and beyond the breakwater the sea lay flat and shining and patient, holding everything it had ever taken.

Three years, Wren thought, buckling the dive bag across their shoulders. Three years of harbors and cold camps and other people's boats, all of it aimed at one patch of water where a red bandana had gone down with a ship that everyone said sank by accident.

Tomorrow they'd be there. Tomorrow the sea would have to start giving things back.

They just had to survive the crossing—and whatever it was that Petra Ishikawa wasn't saying, whatever had made her go white at the name *Kestrel*, was already waiting for them all at the end of it.
