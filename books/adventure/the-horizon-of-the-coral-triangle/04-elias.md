## 4. Elias

The coordinates put us thirty miles off Ternate, in water the color of hammered pewter. Milo spotted the survey vessel first—a blunt gray slab of a ship riding low on her own machinery, cranes folded like sleeping insects along her deck. The name on her stern was *Meridian Star*, and she sat over a patch of ocean I had spent three years flying around the way you walk around an open grave.

I brought the Otter down onto the swell with hands that knew the work and a mouth gone dry as paper. Sari said nothing from behind me, which was its own kind of mercy. Wren leaned against the cargo netting, watching me with those flat sea-colored eyes, and said less than nothing.

"You could still turn us around," Milo offered.

"No," I said, and cut the engines, because if I let myself speak again I would have turned us around.

* * *

They sent a tender for us. Of course they did. Elias Vane had never once in his life made anyone come to him when he could arrange the approach himself—that was the whole architecture of the man. He stood at the rail of the tender as it came alongside, one hand on the line, and three years collapsed into the space between our hulls.

He was grayer at the temples. Thinner through the jaw, like grief or discipline had pared him down. The same salt-and-pepper field jacket he'd worn the day everything ended, patched at the elbow now. His eyes found mine before the fenders even kissed and held on, and I hated him for looking, and I hated myself more for the thing that happened in my chest—this awful, traitorous loosening, like a knot I'd tied too tight finally slipping.

"Petra." Just my name. Not Ishikawa, not the callsign the strip rats used, not any of the aliases I'd flown under. He said it like it had been sitting in his mouth for three years, waiting.

"Elias." My voice came out level. Small miracle. "You've got a lot of nerve chartering a plane instead of calling."

"A call gets hung up on." He handed me up over the gunwale, and his hand closed around my forearm for half a second—warm, dry, steady—and I wanted to break every finger of it and hold it at the same time. "A contract makes people show up."

"People showed up because we were broke."

"I know." No apology attached to it. That was Elias. He wore his ruthlessness openly, like a tool belt. "Come below. Weather's turning early this year and I don't want to do this twice."

* * *

His cabin smelled of coffee, chart ink, and machine oil—the same smell his old office had, back when he was a surveyor with a government grant and I was a girl with a father's boat and a ring I hadn't learned to wear yet. He spread the charts across the desk without asking permission, and there it was: the reef, marked in his precise drafting hand. And beside it, a hull profile labeled with letters I hadn't seen since the inquiry.

*Kestrel.*

My father's boat. Forty feet of teak and stubbornness, lost with four souls aboard three years ago this monsoon season. Lost with me supposed to be aboard, and not, because of a fight I'd had with my father about cargo weight, about shortcuts, about Elias. The last fight. I'd taken the mail flight instead. I'd lived.

"You want us to dive her," I said.

"I need instruments recovered from the site before the currents shift." He tapped the chart. "Current meters, sediment traps, two pressure housings we deployed eight months ago. They're moored inside the wreck structure itself. When the monsoon turns—and it will turn days ahead of forecast, the way it's building—the sand moves off that shelf and everything not pinned down goes into the deep channel. Buried past recovery. Past anything."

"So hire a salvage crew. You're sitting on a vessel full of them."

"I'm sitting on a vessel whose divers can't be trusted with what's down there." He looked up at me then, and something moved behind his eyes—not quite guilt. Something adjacent to it. "And because you know that wreck better than any diver alive. You grew up on her decks. You'll read her."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than I meant. "Don't dress it up like sentiment. You put my father's name on a contract and expected me to sign it?"

"Yes," he said simply. "Because you needed the fee, and I needed someone who wouldn't stop at the first deck plate."

Sari shifted by the door. Milo had gone very still. Even Wren, who had no stake in any of this—or so I'd believed—had stopped pretending not to listen.

"The Kestrel went down with four people," I said. "My father. Tomas Reyes. The Ade brothers. You surveyed these waters, Elias. You were the one who certified that route safe for the run she was making. So tell me how you sleep, and tell me why your instruments are moored to her bones."

For a long moment the only sound was the hull ticking against the swell and rain beginning to stutter on the deck above.

"I sleep badly," he said. "And my instruments are there because I've spent three years trying to prove the sinking wasn't weather. Current data doesn't lie, Petra. Sediment cores don't lie. Somebody forged that manifest, somebody cut that hull, and the syndicate that did it is still operating out of Bitung with new boats and old friends. I couldn't prove it from a surface station. So I put sensors where the truth sank." He straightened. "And yes. I knew the risks when I stayed silent after the inquiry. I chose the investigation over telling you. It wasn't the only choice I regret."

"You didn't stay silent. You vanished."

"I worked." Flat, unapologetic. "Three years of current tables and hydrophone records while everyone else got to grieve. You want an apology for that? Fine. I'm sorry I didn't come to you. I'm not sorry I kept digging. One of us had to."

The cabin felt suddenly small, the walls pressing in with all the things I'd rehearsed saying to him across three years of empty cockpits—*you could have called, you could have warned me, you could have come to the memorial*—and none of them fit anymore, because he'd just laid out a version of those years that made my hiding look like what it was: flight dressed up as work.

Milo cleared his throat. "Boss. The terms are good. Better than good. Covers the engine rebuild, the annual, and six months' strip fees."

"I know what the terms are, Milo."

"Then maybe look at them instead of at him."

That earned him a flicker of something from Elias—almost respect. I pulled the contract across the desk and read it properly, because whatever else Elias Vane was, he was honest on paper. Clean terms. Recovery of specified equipment, hazard pay, silence clauses that were broader than they needed to be but not criminal. And at the bottom, in the client signature block, that handwriting I'd known since before I could drive: the tall ascenders, the way the V crossed into the next letter like a man always moving toward something else.

E. Vane.

I signed. The pen scratched loud in the quiet. Relief went through me like warm water and I despised every degree of it—relief that the crew was paid for, relief that the decision was made for me, relief, God help me, at standing close enough to him to smell the coffee on his breath after three years of telling myself I'd forgotten it entirely.

"There's a briefing at 0500," Elias said. "Dive plan, wreck orientation, comms protocols. Your stowaway dives too, or stays aboard. Everyone signs the silence clause."

Wren stepped forward out of the shadow by the bulkhead and took the pen before I could pass it. Signed their name—just Wren, no family name—in a quick slanted hand, and slid the paper back across the desk.

"My parent worked that boat," Wren said quietly. "Tomas Reyes. Deckhand. I've been diving toward that reef since I was sixteen." A pause, eyes on Elias with no fear in them at all. "So don't brief me on orientation, Mr. Vane. Brief me on what you know that the inquiry didn't."

Elias looked at Wren for a long moment, and then at me, and I watched him decide—visibly, deliberately—to open a door he'd kept locked for three years.

"All right," he said. "0500. Bring everything you think you know." He turned to the porthole, where the rain had thickened into sheets and somewhere south of us, dark water moved over the Kestrel. "Because tomorrow we find out which of us has been lying longest."

* * *

We crossed back to the Otter in the tender through falling dusk, and nobody talked until the wreck of the light caught us—my father's anchor light, Milo remembered, the way I'd told the story once, drunk, two years ago. Green, blinking slow, visible for miles. The last thing the search planes saw.

Wren stood at the bow with spray coming over the rails, and when I joined them, they spoke without turning.

"He's lying about something," Wren said.

"I know."

"But not about the syndicate."

"I know that too."

Wren nodded slowly, watching the green blink swallow itself behind the rain. Below us, thirty meters down, the Kestrel waited with her secrets bolted to her ribs—and tomorrow morning, I was going to swim back into the worst day of my life and pull it apart bolt by bolt.
