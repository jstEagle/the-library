## 5. The Reef Remembers

The water off Saddle Cay was the color of bottle glass, and Wren had been awake since before the generators, watching it from the rail while the sky went from ash to apricot.

They knew this reef. Not this reef exactly—no one knew *this* reef but the dead—but reefs like it, the way a diver knows the grammar of coral without having read every sentence. The bommies rising in stepped shelves. The sand channels that ran like pale rivers between them. Somewhere down there, folded into a cut in the shelf where the current combed everything loose away, lay the Kestrel.

Wren's mother had died on a boat with a name like a bird. Wren had spent eleven years not thinking about that, which was its own kind of diving: controlled descent, equalize early, don't rush the bottom.

"You're up early." Milo's voice, thick with sleep, a mug of something steaming clutched in both hands. He leaned on the rail beside them. "Or you never went down."

"Never went down," Wren said. It was mostly true. They'd slept two hours in the crook between the fuel drums and the wheelhouse, the way they'd slept their whole life, one ear open for weather and worse.

Milo sipped his drink. "Petra's been up since four. Checking the dive plan over and over like it'll change if she stares hard enough." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on the water. "You know whose boat that is down there. Her father's."

"I know," Wren said, and did not say the rest of it.

Below them, the shadow of the wreck waited. Above them, Petra Ishikawa stood at the bow with her arms crossed, watching the reef as if it might confess to something.

* * *

They briefed at seven. Elias Vane's survey vessel sat anchored a quarter mile east, white and efficient, its crane arm folded like a sleeping insect. His voice came through the radio crisp and rehearsed: target depth eighteen meters, hull resting on its port side against the shelf, visibility expected three to five meters, current mild until midday.

"Recovery priorities are the instrument array aft and the data core in the wheelhouse," Elias said. "Touch nothing else. Photograph anything anomalous, but don't disturb it. This is an archaeological site as much as a salvage job."

"An archaeological site," Sari repeated flatly, coiling rope. "Three years old."

"Three years is enough for a reef to take ownership," Elias said. "Respect that."

Wren watched Petra's face during all of it. She gave nothing away—the same stillness she'd worn since the rendezvous, a face like a locked hatch. But when Elias signed off, her thumb lingered on the transmit button a half second too long, and Wren thought: *she's listening for him even when he's gone.*

Four divers. Petra insisted on going down herself, which surprised no one. Sari would run the surface line. Milo, who dove like a man apologizing to the water, would stay shallow and work the instrument array. That left the deep survey of the hull to Petra—and Wren volunteered before the silence could settle into refusal.

"I'm the strongest swimmer aboard," Wren said. "And I don't need much bottom time to map a hull."

Petra looked at them for a long moment. There it was again, that flicker Wren had seen at loading dawn—the wariness, the sense that Wren fit into water too easily for someone claiming to be nobody. "Stay within sight of me," Petra said finally. "If you see anything that isn't hull or coral, you mark it and you come to me first. Not the radio. Me."

"Why?"

"Because radios can be heard by anyone," Petra said, "and I'd like to decide what we know before everyone knows it."

Wren held out a hand, and after a beat Petra shook it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief, like a woman closing a door gently so as not to wake anyone.

* * *

The descent was a slow drowning in light.

Eighteen meters came fast along the shot line, the blue deepening around them from glass to ink, and then the Kestrel materialized out of the haze the way wrecks always did—not gradually but all at once, as if the sea had been hiding it behind a curtain and grew bored of the game.

She lay on her port side against the shelf, exactly as promised, and the reef had indeed taken her. Soft corals furred the railings like frost. A school of fusiliers poured through the broken wheelhouse windows in a silver ribbon. Anemones had colonized the winch housing, clownfish standing guard in their pink city. Three years, and the boat had become a place rather than a thing.

But the hull—

Wren swam the starboard face slowly, running their light along the plating, and the cold that moved through them had nothing to do with water temperature.

There were cuts in the hull. Long, clean, deliberate cuts, the kind made by an exothermic torch or a plasma lance, and they ran *inward*. Wren had grown up around salvagers and smugglers and the whole floating economy of people who took boats apart; they knew what breach patterns meant the way farmers knew clouds. Grounding tears tore outward, ragged, following stress lines. Collision damage punched inward in a single ugly bloom. These cuts were surgical. Parallel. Spaced like fingers.

Someone had opened this boat from the inside.

Wren looked across the haze at Petra, who was crouched at the wheelhouse door with her light fixed on the frame, utterly still. Even through the mask, even at distance, Wren could see the shape of what she was seeing. Her hand rose and pressed flat against the steel beside the door, palm open, the way you touch a wound to learn how deep it goes.

They surfaced together in silence, and only when both were aboard and masks off did Petra speak, and her voice came out level in the way that cost the most.

"The manifest," she said. "Elias sent the original cargo manifest last night. I checked it against my father's own load sheets—I still have copies, I kept everything, I—" She stopped. Breathed. "The tonnage doesn't match. He was carrying forty percent more than the manifest declared. And the declared cargo doesn't match what the hold was built to carry. Compartments that should have been empty were sealed."

Sari set down her wrench. Milo stopped pretending to check the tanks.

"So it wasn't weather," Milo said quietly. "It wasn't the storm."

"It was never the storm," Petra said. "The storm was just the alibi. Someone loaded my father's boat with something worth sinking her for, and then someone cut her open from the inside and let the sea do the paperwork." She laughed once, a sound with no humor in it at all. "Three years I've flown medicine over this water telling myself the ocean took him. The ocean didn't take him. People did."

Nobody touched her. Wren understood why. Some griefs you couldn't approach directly; you circled, the way sharks circle, keeping respectful distance until the moment was right or past.

"We tell Vane?" Sari asked.

Petra was quiet a long time. "Not yet," she said. "I want to know what he already knows first."

* * *

Wren went back down alone.

They told themselves it was for the survey—to finish the hull mapping, to photograph the cuts properly, to be thorough. All true. But underneath the truth, in the sealed compartment of themselves they rarely opened, was another reason entirely: the deckhand.

Their mother had served on boats all through the Triangle, and in the last year of her life, on a boat called the Kestrel, she'd written letters home that Wren still kept in a waterproof pouch inside a hollowed-out copy of a navigation manual. Letters about a captain who sang off-key. About a daughter back home the captain asked after by name. About cargo she wasn't allowed to ask questions about.

*Cargo she wasn't allowed to ask questions about.*

Wren descended past the wheelhouse, past the fusiliers, following the hull's fallen flank down the shelf to where the sand began, deeper than the briefing line, deeper than Petra had told them to go. At twenty-four meters the light turned the color of old bruises. Their computer chirped a warning; they thumbed it quiet.

And there, half-buried at the base of the hull where the current had excavated a small trench, was a box.

Steel, maybe sixty centimeters long, stenciled with markings that the reef had tried to eat and failed. Wren brushed the sand away with careful hands, and the stencil resolved letter by letter, and their heart began to hammer against their ribs like something trying to escape a hold.

A stylized wave breaking over a hook. Beneath it, a serial number.

Wren knew that mark. Everyone in the gray economy of the Triangle knew that mark, the way children know the shape of the monster under the bed—it was the seal of the Meridian Combine, the syndicate that owned harbors and harbormasters, that moved anything through anything, that no one named aloud in daylight. Their mother had never written the name in her letters. She'd drawn the mark once, in the margin, and crossed it out so hard she'd torn the paper.

The box was sealed. Padlocked, and the padlock wrapped in plastic sheeting that had somehow held—someone had intended to recover this. Someone had sunk the boat knowing the box was aboard, planning to come back for it when the heat died, and either the plan had failed or the plan had *changed*, and either way the evidence of everything had been sitting here in the dark for three years, patient as bone, waiting for someone to dig.

Wren's gauge read twenty minutes of no-decompression left. Their hands wanted to haul the box up right now, to break the surface screaming with it. Instead they did what their mother had taught them, the first rule of every kind of diving: *slow is smooth, smooth is fast.* They photographed the stencil. They photographed the serial number. They rigged a lift bag from their kit, clipped it to the box's lifting eyes, and kicked for the light.

* * *

Petra met them at the ladder, and when Wren broke the surface and saw her face, they understood that she had known—had felt them go deep, had counted their bubbles, had stood at the rail the entire time with her arms crossed and her jaw set.

"What did you find?" she said. Not angry. Worse than angry. Braced.

Wren hauled the box onto the deck, streaming seawater, and turned the stencil toward her. "Do you recognize the mark?"

Petra went very still. Wren watched recognition move through her like a current under ice—the flinch, the denial, the terrible arrival. Her hand came up and hovered over the wave-and-hook without touching it, the way you hover over a burn.

"That's—" she started, and couldn't finish.

"Meridian Combine," Wren said. "Your father wasn't carrying their cargo by accident, Petra. He was carrying it because someone paid him to—or because someone put it aboard whether he liked it or not. Either way, they sank their own shipment. They sank *him*. And this box has names in it. Boxes like this always have names."

The afternoon had gone gold around them. On the eastern horizon, Elias Vane's survey vessel sat white and silent at anchor, and Wren saw Petra look at it—really look at it—with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors, from years of practicing suspicion in shop windows and ferry terminals.

"He sent us the manifest," she said slowly. "He told me to photograph anything anomalous. He told me to report to him first." Her voice dropped. "Either Elias Vane has no idea what's down there. Or he knows exactly what's down there, and he needed someone else's hands to bring it up."

The box sat between them on the deck, dripping, patient, full of the past.

"And which do you think it is?" Wren asked.

Petra didn't answer. Behind her, the sun slid behind a cloud, and the shadow of the syndicate's mark fell across the whole deck like a hand.
