## 6. What Was Sworn Off

The survey vessel's name was *Meridian Grace*, and she smelled like diesel and old coffee and someone else's ambition. Petra stood in the corridor below her wheelhouse with salt drying white on her forearms and the strongbox's photograph burning a hole in her shirt pocket—Wren had taken it with a waterproof camera on that second dive, before anyone could stop them, and now the syndicate's stamped mark sat in her pocket like a coal.

Elias had said *come below, we should talk*. Three years ago he'd said the same thing outside a registrar's office in Sorong, and what followed had been the longest silence of her life.

The cabin was small, chart-strewn, lit by a single caged bulb that swung with the swell. He'd made coffee. Of course he had. Elias Vane negotiated with other people's grief over coffee, always had, pouring two cups like the ritual itself was an argument for his innocence.

"Sit," he said.

"I'll stand."

He set one cup down anyway, pushed it an inch toward her across the chart table, and she hated how precisely she remembered this—the economy of his gestures, the way he never wasted a motion, as if the world were a survey grid and everything in it had coordinates he'd already taken.

"Wren found something down there." She kept her voice level. Below them, through the hull, she could hear Milo laughing at something Sari said, the two of them splicing line on deck, ordinary sounds, sounds from a life she'd built out of the wreckage of another one. "A strongbox. Stamped with the Halcyon Consortium's mark. Bolted to what's left of my father's engine room."

Elias didn't flinch. That was the first betrayal of the evening—that he didn't flinch.

"You knew," she said.

"Petra."

"You knew the wreck wasn't an accident." The words came out of her like water finding a crack, pressure behind three years of dammed silence. "You knew when you sat across from me at that table in Sorong. You knew when you told me the Coast Guard findings were conclusive. Hull failure. Weather. Bad luck." Her hand found the edge of the chart table and gripped it, because if she let go she thought she might put that coffee cup through his teeth. "My father died believing he was a bad sailor, Elias. I spent three years believing I came from a family of bad sailors."

"Your father was the best seaman I ever—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles briefly against his mouth. When his hand came away, the gray at his temples looked less like age and more like frost. "Sit down, Petra. Please."

She sat. Not because he asked. Because her knees had quietly resigned.

* * *

He talked for eleven minutes. She knew because she counted, the way Milo counted miles, because counting was the only arithmetic that kept her from screaming.

Halcyon had been running untaxed cargo through the eastern passages for a decade—fuel, mostly, and worse than fuel—and the Kestrel had been in the wrong channel on the wrong night with a hold full of legal fish and a father who saw too much. The manifest in the harbor office had been forged after the fact. The cuts in the hull were made from inside, by men who boarded under the pretense of a distress inspection and left through the engine room with the sea coming in behind them. Six crew. Five bodies recovered. One deckhand lost to the current and never found.

And Elias had known all of it.

"I was working the coastal survey contract," he said. "Halcyon was a client. A subsidiary layer, but a client. I pulled the sonar archives for the incident report and the numbers didn't match the official story, and I took them to my director, and my director took them to—" He laughed, short and airless. "They went up the chain and came back down as a directive. Sealed. Ongoing investigation, do not contact the families, do not speculate, Mr. Vane, your commission depends on your discretion."

"So you were discreet."

"I was twenty-nine years old and they told me that talking would get you killed." His voice cracked on *you*, and some animal part of her leaned toward the sound before the rest of her locked it out. "Not suspected. Killed. They named the method. They named the channel. They said the deckhand who disappeared wasn't an accident either—he'd seen the boarding party's faces. Do you understand? Everyone who knew was already being subtracted. Your father. Aris. And then there was you, asking questions in every port from Bitung to Dili, and I had a choice between telling you the truth and keeping you alive long enough to hate me for it."

"So you chose duty."

"I chose *you*." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "I chose the version where you got to be angry at me instead of dead next to him. I have replayed that choice every day for three years and I want to tell you it gets easier and it does not. It does not get easier, Petra. It just gets quieter. Like a hull settling."

She stared at the coffee going cold between them. The bulb swung. Shadows swung with it.

"You could have told me last week," she said finally. "When you signed the charter papers. You could have written one sentence in that contract—*the wreck was not what you think.*"

"And you'd have flown straight into their waters alone, with no cover story, no recovery operation, nothing but grief and a fuel budget." He shook his head. "I needed you here. I needed all of you here, with witnesses, with the instruments as pretext, with Wren—"

The name landed in the cabin like a dropped wrench.

"—with Wren," he finished, too late, and Petra watched him realize what he'd just handed her.

* * *

She found Wren aft, sitting on the anchor windlass with their knees drawn up, watching the rain come across the water in gray curtains. They didn't startle when she climbed the ladder. They'd heard her coming; Wren always heard everyone coming, the way small animals did, the way children of dangerous households did.

"Elias says you're not a freelance diver," Petra said. "He says I should ask you about the Kestrel's crew list."

Wren turned the camera over in their hands. In the low light their face was all planes and patience, younger than their hands, older than their eyes.

"Aris Mende," they said. "Deckhand. Lost overboard, presumed drowned, body never recovered. My mother waited four years before she let the court declare him dead. I was nine." They looked up. "I've been diving that reef since I was sixteen. Every permit season. Looking for anything with his initials on it. And then three weeks ago a broker in Sorong offered me more money than my mother makes in two years to dive the site early and bring up a sealed box and not open it."

Petra felt the deck tilt, or imagined she did. "You took the job."

"I took the money and I stowed away on the only boat in the harbor flying toward the same reef." Wren's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I figured whoever wanted that box buried badly enough to pay me was the same someone who'd buried my father. Best way to find a grave-robber is to be standing on the grave when they come back."

Below them, faintly, Milo's laugh again. Sari's voice rising over the wind, arguing about splice tension. The little family Petra had assembled out of strays and second chances, all of them unknowing passengers on a shipwreck's orbit.

"My father," Petra said, and her voice came out strange, sanded down, "was Tomas Ishikawa. He used to feed half the harbor's kids off the stern of the Kestrel. If Aris worked for him, Aris ate at our table. You might have—I mean, before, you might have—"

"I know." Wren said it gently, which was somehow worse. "I did the math the first night, sleeping among your crates. Aris wrote home about the skipper's daughter who was learning to fly. He called you Little Compass. Said you always pointed true north even when you were scared."

Petra turned away and gripped the rail and breathed the rain-smell until her eyes stopped stinging. *Little Compass.* She hadn't heard that name since she was nineteen. She had buried it in the same grave as the boat, and here it was, dredged up by a stranger with her father's deckhand's jawline, delivered like a gift she had no right to accept.

"Three people," she said to the water. "One lie. It orphaned all of us."

"Not one lie." Wren slid off the windlass and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. "One truth, kept by cowards and killers. There's a difference. Lies rot. Truths just wait."

* * *

She went back below decks without deciding to. Her feet made the decision, carrying her down the corridor to Elias's cabin, where he still sat exactly where she'd left him, the untouched coffee gone cold in front of him like an offering at a shrine.

"Wren is Aris Mende's child," she said.

He closed his eyes. "I wondered. The moment they surfaced with that box and wouldn't give it up. The jaw. The way they read water." He opened his eyes. "I'm glad, actually. Glad they found the reef. Glad they found—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me what you're glad about. You don't get to curate my feelings anymore, Elias. You lost that license three years ago and you forged the renewal."

He nodded slowly, accepting it, and that acceptance frightened her more than any argument would have, because it meant he'd rehearsed this surrender for years and she was only now arriving at the scene.

Outside, the light changed. Not sunset—wrong color for sunset, wrong hour. Green-gray, bruised, the particular light that Sari called *monsoon teeth*. Petra heard boots on the deck above, quick ones, and then Milo's voice losing its laughter entirely:

"Sari! Barometer's falling off a cliff—get the hatches—"

Elias was already on his feet, reaching for the radio log, and when he glanced at the porthole his face did something she'd never seen it do in all the years she'd loved him and all the years she'd hated him.

It went afraid.

"That front," he said quietly, "wasn't due for four days. Somebody paid to make sure we were all still here when it arrived."
