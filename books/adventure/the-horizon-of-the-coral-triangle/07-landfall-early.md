## 7. Landfall Early

Sari felt the storm before she saw it, the way she felt everything wrong with a boat—through her hands, through the soles of her feet, through a low hum in her back teeth that had never once lied to her.

She was under the deck plate with a wrench in her fist, chasing a fuel line that had developed opinions, when the swell changed. It came up out of the southeast, long and wrong, a swell that had traveled too far to be local. She slid out, wiped her hands on a rag already more grease than cloth, and looked at the sky.

The horizon to the east had gone the color of a bruise going bad. Not the scattered gray of an ordinary squall. This was a wall, low and wide, and it was eating the islands behind it one by one.

"No," she said, to the sky, to the barometer bolted by the companionway, to the universe in general. The barometer's needle was falling like it had somewhere better to be.

She climbed the two steps into the cockpit, where Petra stood at the helm with the kind of stillness Sari had learned to read over three years of flying and floating together. Petra didn't fidget. When she went quiet, the world had usually just gotten louder.

"Petra." Sari pointed east. "That's not forecast. That's not forecast for three more days."

Petra looked, and Sari watched her do the math—the same math she'd done, the same ugly answer. The monsoon had jumped its schedule. Or the forecast had lied. Either way, the survey vessel *Meridian* was still anchored a mile off the wreck site with its crane down and its tender in the water, and Elias Vane's carefully plotted recovery window had just been torn up by the sky.

"Radio the *Meridian*," Petra said. "Tell them it's coming. Then get everything on this boat tied down twice."

Sari keyed the VHF and got Milo instead, who had the *Meridian*'s channel up on the second set. His voice came through thin and crackling: "They're already hauling the tender. Elias says forty minutes to break anchor."

"Tell him he has twenty," Sari said. "Tell him the sea doesn't negotiate."

She heard Milo relay it, heard the pause, heard Elias's reply come back clipped and controlled: *Understood. We'll rendezvous clear of the shoals. Keep your heading south until the front passes.* A beat. Then, softer, though the radio made no secret of it: "Petra—be careful."

Petra's jaw did the thing it did. "Tell him to worry about his own boat," she said, and turned to the wheel, and Sari pretended not to have heard either half of it.

* * *

The front hit them at four in the afternoon, and it did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

One minute they were running south through building chop with the *Meridian* a gray shape astern, wallowing as it fought its anchor chain. The next, the rain came sideways, a solid gray curtain with weight and intent, and the wind went from a shove to a fist. Sari, lashed to the rail with a safety line, watched the visibility drop to fifty meters and the *Meridian* vanish as if it had never existed.

"Radio's gone," Milo shouted from the cabin, and his voice had a flatness she didn't like. "Not weak. *Gone.* Antenna's snapped—line's dead, I've got nothing but the handheld."

"The handheld's line of sight!" she shouted back. "That's ten miles on a good day!"

"Then let's hope the coast guard's having a good day!"

Wren appeared out of the rain like they always did, silent as spray, and started doubling the lashings on the dive gear without being asked. Sari had stopped being unnerved by that—mostly. The kid moved around a boat like they'd been born on one, hands finding cleats and lines in the dark without looking. There was a story there. There was always a story. Right now Sari didn't have time for stories.

She had time for water. Specifically, for water that was where water shouldn't be.

She felt it at six, or thought she felt it—the bilge pump cycling too often. She went below with a flashlight clenched in her teeth, popped the engine cover, and there it was: six inches of gray-green sea sloshing around her boots, and a thin bright thread of it jetting from the stern gland where the propeller shaft passed through the hull. The pounding swell had worked something loose. Not a gash, not yet. A whisper. But whispers in a hull become screams, and she knew it the way she knew everything about engines: in her spine.

"Petra!" She was up the companionway steps before she'd decided to move. "We're taking water. Shaft gland. I can slow it, maybe stop it, but I need the engine off for an hour and I need calm water, and I'm looking at—" she gestured at the entire ocean, "—*that*."

Petra's face went through three expressions in one second and settled on none of them. "How long do we have if you can't fix it?"

"With both pumps running? Hours. Not many."

"Then fix it." Petra looked at the compass, at the gray chaos, and Sari saw her make the call that Sari would have made, the only call there was: "We run south as far as she'll take us. Milo, get the pumps going, both of them. Wren—"

"Already on the bail bucket," Wren said, and was gone below.

* * *

The engine died at nine that night.

Not fouled, exactly—Sari would argue the word later, in calmer weather, over something stronger than coffee. *Drowned* was closer. The intake had gulped a wave somewhere in the black hours of the run south, and the whole fuel system had swallowed seawater, and now the engine sat below decks sulking like a patient with fluid in the lungs. She had it stripped to its heart in the rolling dark: injectors out, lines drained, everything that touched fuel flushed and dried and flushed again. Milo worked beside her by flashlight, passing tools before she asked, because after three years he knew her hands better than she did.

Above them, Wren bailed. The sound of it came down through the deck in a rhythm—*slosh, slosh, heave*—steady as a heartbeat, steady as a metronome, and Sari found herself timing her work to it. Petra steered with no engine, no radio, no instruments she trusted, riding the swells by feel and by the sound of the water on the hull, keeping the boat's stern to the worst of it the way her father must have taught her, the way someone had taught Sari, the way the sea teaches everyone eventually or kills them.

Around midnight, Milo stopped passing tools.

"You hear that?" he said.

Sari froze. Under the wind, under the rain drumming the deck like a fist on a door, under Wren's bucket—engines. More than one. Far off, out in the rain to the west, a low diesel growl that came and went with the wind, circling.

The syndicate boat. The fast one that had been shadowing them since the reef. Out there in a monsoon, because whatever was in that strongbox in their hold was worth more to those people than their own hulls.

"It can't see us," Milo said. "Nobody can see anything."

"It doesn't have to see us," Sari said quietly. "It just has to wait. Storm ends, we're a leaking boat with no engine and no radio, and they're a fast boat with a full tank." She handed him the injector she'd just cleaned. "So we don't be here when the storm ends. Pass me the twelve-millimeter."

They worked. That was the whole of it, the whole secret of the night, the thing Sari would remember years later when people asked her what it was like: they worked. Nobody cried, nobody screamed, nobody wasted a single breath on anything that didn't move water out of the boat or salt out of the engine. Milo's hands shook for the first hour and then stopped. Wren bailed for six straight hours and never once asked how much longer. Petra held the helm through swells that stood the boat on its stern and dropped it into troughs like a hand closing, and when Sari came up at two in the morning to check the deck lashings, she found her captain soaked to the bone, hair plastered flat, humming—*humming*—some off-key fragment of a song, and understood that it was not calm. It was defiance. It was Petra Ishikawa telling the ocean, note by off-key note, that she was still here.

Sari went back below and worked faster.

* * *

The engine caught at 4:17 in the morning.

Sari would remember the time because Milo wrote it on the inside of the engine cover in grease pencil—*4:17, she lives*—and because when it caught, all three of them below decks made a sound that was not quite a cheer and not quite a sob, and Wren's bucket, for the first time in six hours, went silent.

"Petra!" Milo yelled up the companionway. "We have propulsion!"

The boat answered before Petra could—surged forward under them, the hull biting into the swell, and the whole vessel changed character from *dying thing* to *going somewhere*. Sari climbed up into the cockpit, dripping, filthy, half-blind with exhaustion, and found Petra grinning at the gray dawn like a woman possessed.

"South-southeast," Petra said. "There's a channel."

"A channel," Sari repeated. "Out here? There's nothing out here but reef and drowning."

"There's a mangrove channel." Wren had come up too, and their voice was hoarse from six hours of shouting over wind, but steady. "Behind the barrier islands. My mother ran cargo through it. It's not on any chart."

Sari looked at the kid—at the salt-crusted face, the hands blistered raw from the bucket handle, the eyes that had seen the syndicate's boat out in the rain and gone on bailing anyway—and felt something in her chest crack open a little. Three years she'd flown with Petra, wrenched with Milo, and she'd thought she understood what a crew was. She hadn't understood anything until a stranger bailed seawater beside her through a monsoon without one question about why.

"Uncharted," she said. "In a monsoon. Through mangroves."

"Yes," said Petra, and Wren, and Milo, all at once, all of them grinning like lunatics.

Sari sighed, and checked the fuel lines one last time, and took the throttle. "Then somebody keep an eye on my temperature gauge, because if I cook this engine in a swamp, I'm haunting all of you."

They ran south into the lightening east, the syndicate's growl fading somewhere behind them in the rain, and for twenty minutes Sari let herself believe the worst was over.

Then Wren, at the bow, went very still, and pointed, and said, "There's the entrance. And there's—Petra. *Petra.* Look at the water."

Beyond the barrier islands, where the channel should have opened into calm green shallows, the sea was boiling white—and out in the gray rain behind them, low and fast and closing, came the shape of a boat with no lights at all.
