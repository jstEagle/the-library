## 8. The Stranger's Help

The channel was a rumor my mother used to tell me, back when I was small enough to believe the sea kept secrets out of kindness.

*There's a way through the mangroves,* she'd say, mending net in the lamplight of our houseboat, her hands never still. *The old smugglers cut it. The charts don't show it because the charts are written by people who never needed to disappear.* She'd tap my chest, right over the sternum. *Remember it, anak. One day you'll need a door the world forgot.*

I was seven. I thought she meant it as a lullaby.

Now, with the monsoon chewing the horizon into white shreds and the syndicate's boat somewhere behind us in the rain, I stood at the bow of the *Sampaguita* and remembered every word.

* * *

The leak had started an hour after the survey vessel vanished into the storm's western skirt. Sari found it with her bare feet before any gauge could warn her—water cold and insistent over the deck plates, rising through a seam aft of the fuel tank. Milo killed the engine to keep the fouled intake from tearing itself apart, and for ten minutes the *Sampaguita* was just a tin shell rolling in the swell, radio drowned, rain hammering like a fist on a door that would never open.

"We can't make the harbor," Sari shouted over the noise, bailing with a cut-down jerrican. "We can't even make the shipping lane."

"Then we don't make the shipping lane," I said.

They all turned to me. Petra's face was gray with salt and something older than salt. She'd been at the helm all day, hands welded to the wheel, and when she looked at me now there was no suspicion left in it—only the raw arithmetic of a woman running out of ocean.

"You know a way through?" she asked.

"My mother did."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." I pointed east, where the mangroves stood up out of the water like a drowned forest holding its breath. "There's a channel. Cut by hand, sixty, seventy years ago. Smugglers, pearl divers, people who didn't want to be found. It goes through the root mazes to a basin on the far side—shallow, sheltered, invisible from open water. My mother ran it in a boat half this size."

"Is it deep enough for us?"

I hesitated. The truth was I didn't know. The channel lived in a seven-year-old's memory, translated through a dead woman's voice, and the sea had had seventy years to forget it.

"It's deep enough," I said, and heard my own mother's certainty in the lie.

Petra looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin. Behind her, Milo and Sari had stopped bailing to listen, and I understood that whatever she decided, she'd decide for all of them—that this was the shape of her, the woman who carried everyone's weight and called it cargo.

"Take the bow," she said. "Call the turns. If you kill us, I'll haunt you personally."

"That's fair."

Milo got the engine turning over on the second try—rough, coughing, half its power gone to the fouled intake—and we crept toward the green wall while the storm came down on us like a verdict.

* * *

The entrance was where my mother said it would be, or close enough that I chose to believe it was: a break in the mangroves no wider than a church door, hidden behind a curtain of nipa that hung into the water. I saw it the way you see a face in a crowd—not with the eyes first, but with something older. The roots on the left bank grew in a spiral. My mother had told me that. *Look for the spiral, anak. The smugglers planted it so their own would know.*

"Hard to port," I called. "Slow. *Slow.*"

Petra brought the *Sampaguita* around with a delicacy I wouldn't have believed of her an hour ago, and we slid through the curtain of nipa into a green twilight where the storm became a sound instead of a thing—muffled, enormous, like the sea breathing in another room.

The channel was beautiful and terrible. Roots arched overhead and plunged into black water on both sides, close enough to touch, close enough to take the paint off. Mud squirmed with crabs. Every fifty meters the passage bent, and every bend looked like the last one, and every bend wasn't.

"Port," I called. "Then starboard, hard. There's a fallen trunk—you'll have to shave it."

"I see it."

She didn't slow. She threaded the trunk with maybe a hand's width on either side, and Milo made a sound in his throat that might have been a prayer, and we went on.

"Your mother," Petra said, not taking her eyes off the channel. "She's the deckhand. From the *Kestrel*."

It wasn't a question. I'd told them below decks, in the terrible quiet after Elias's confession, but I think Petra needed to hear it again in a place like this—needed to understand that the person calling her turns had been orphaned by the same lie that orphaned her.

"She was," I said. "Alma. She went by Wren because she said a real name is something you spend, and she never liked spending." I watched a root slide past close enough to graze. "I spent mine instead. It didn't help."

"No," Petra said quietly. "It doesn't."

We ran the channel for forty minutes. Twice we touched bottom—once a soft kiss of mud that Sari swore at, once a grinding three seconds against a sandbar that stopped my heart and then released it. The rain found us through gaps in the canopy in cold needles. Milo bailed without being asked, passing the jerrican hand to hand with Sari, and nobody spoke except me, calling turns in my mother's cadence without meaning to, her words coming up through me like water through a well.

And then the green closed behind us and we were through—into a basin no wider than a football pitch, ringed entirely by mangroves, the water flat and brown and still as a held breath. Above us the storm raged, but down here the wind couldn't reach. The *Sampaguita* drifted to a stop in the middle, and for a moment nobody moved.

"We made it," Milo said.

"We're trapped," Sari said, at the same moment.

They were both right. The basin was shelter, and it was a cage. The channel behind us was too narrow to run at speed in the dark with a fouled engine, and the water ahead—Sari was already sounding it with the boat hook—was barely two meters over soft mud. We could hide here. We could not leave. Not until the storm eased, not until the tide turned, not until the sea decided to forgive us.

I looked at Petra. She was standing very still at the helm, and I saw her doing the same arithmetic I was: the syndicate's fast boat had been behind us in the rain. Fast boats have radar, and charts, and paid men who know every local passage worth knowing. Sooner or later, one of those men would remember what my mother knew.

"We've bought time," I said. "Not safety."

"I know," Petra said.

* * *

They came ninety minutes later, in the worst of it.

I heard the engine first—a deep, arrogant throb that had no business being in the mangroves—and then the sweeping blade of a searchlight cutting through the nipa curtain at the basin's eastern edge, painting the roots white, sweeping, sweeping, patient as a lighthouse.

"Kill everything," Petra breathed. "Lights, engine, everything."

We went dark. The *Sampaguita* drifted against the mangroves at the basin's western rim, tucked into a fold of roots where the overhang hid us from above, and we crouched on the deck like animals in a burrow while the light probed and the engine idled somewhere beyond the green wall, close, so close, close enough that I could hear voices carrying flat across the water.

"—channel comes out here, I'm telling you. Old woman's chart. We ran it in '19."

"Then run it again."

"Storm's peaking, you idiot. We'll tear the hull out. We wait for the tide and we go in on foot if we have to."

A pause. Then a voice I hadn't heard before, smoother, the kind of voice that gave orders instead of arguments—the broker, or someone near him.

"The box is on that boat. The woman is on that boat. Nothing else matters. When the tide turns, we go in."

I felt Sari's hand find mine in the dark and grip it. Milo had gone very pale. And Petra—Petra stood up.

She moved to the console, and I saw what she was doing a half-second before I understood it: her hands were on the radio, the drowned, dead radio, and she was stripping its housing with a multitool, working by feel, rain and river water dripping off her chin.

"It's fried," Sari whispered. "The antenna's snapped, the—"

"I don't need the antenna." Petra's voice was low and absolutely steady. "I need power and a transmitter. The antenna's a courtesy." She pulled a cracked panel free and began splicing wires with her teeth and her fingers, and I remembered, suddenly, that this woman had spent three years flying medicine into places with no infrastructure and no mercy—remembered that people like her knew machines the way my mother knew water.

"What are you doing?" Milo whispered.

"Channel sixteen. Open band. Every coast guard station from Palawan to Timor monitors sixteen." She twisted two wires together and the radio crackled, half-dead, spitting static. "And every boat in the Triangle. Including theirs."

"They'll hear you," I said. "They'll know exactly where—"

"I know." She looked at me then, and what was in her face wasn't fear. It was the opposite of fear. It was the look of a woman who had spent three years burying her name and had just decided to dig it up with her own hands. "Wren. Give me the box."

I dragged the strongbox from under the tarp. Syndicate markings, salt-scoured, heavier than it looked. I'd carried it through a storm and half a mangrove maze without once asking what was inside, because some part of me had understood that the answer would change everything.

It changed everything.

She popped the latches with a flathead screwdriver, right there in the dark, and lifted the lid, and even in that green twilight I saw it: bundles of documents, ledgers, payment records with names and dates, and at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a logbook with a water-stained cover I recognized before my brain finished recognizing it.

The *Kestrel*'s log. My mother's handwriting on the spine label. *Alma R.*

Petra's hands shook once, and stopped.

"My father's name is in there," she said, to all of us, to no one, to the storm. "The men who sank his boat are sitting a hundred meters from us, and they think I'm going to run until I die." She keyed the transmitter. Static shrieked, then settled into a thin, ragged carrier wave. "They're wrong."

"Petra," Milo said. "Once you do this—"

"I know what I'm doing." She drew a breath. "I'm done being no one."

She keyed it open and spoke into the storm, her voice carrying across the dead radio in fragments and static, half-swallowed by the rain, but clear enough, clear as a bell through a cracked window:

*"All stations, all stations, this is Petra Ishikawa, master of the vessel Sampaguita, broadcasting on open channel. I am in possession of documentary evidence of the sinking of the fishing vessel Kestrel three years ago—hull breach from within, forged manifest, payments itemized. Names. Dates. Accounts. The parties responsible are currently in the mangrove basin east of—"*

She gave the coordinates. Our coordinates. She gave them plainly, like a woman reading a shopping list, while the syndicate's searchlight swung wild across the canopy and the smooth voice beyond the green wall started shouting.

*"—repeat, evidence of murder and fraud, in my possession, offered freely to any coast guard authority. My position is—"*

The first shot cracked across the basin and took a splinter of mangrove root a meter over her head.

Petra didn't flinch. She read the coordinates again, slower, and keyed off, and in the ringing quiet that followed, the storm seemed to lean in and listen.

"They know where we are now," Sari whispered.

"So does everyone else," Petra said. She closed the strongbox and put her hand flat on its lid, over her father's logbook, over my mother's name. "Now it's a race."

Beyond the nipa curtain, the syndicate boat's engine roared to full throttle, and somewhere far to the south, through kilometers of rain and black water, I chose to believe that a coast guard radio was crackling awake.

The tide was turning. The water in the basin began to move—slowly, surely—toward the channel, toward the open sea, toward whatever came next.

Petra looked at me over the strongbox.

"Your mother's channel," she said. "How fast can you run it in the dark?"

I gripped the bow rail as the searchlight found us at last, white and total, and the smooth voice called out across the water, promising us nothing good.

"Faster than they can," I said. "Hold on."
