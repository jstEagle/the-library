## 9. Eye of the Storm

The mangroves closed around them like a fist.

Wren had threaded them through channels no chart admitted to—water the color of steeped tea, barely a meter deep, roots rising on both sides like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. The *Alcedo* sat low in the water, patched twice, her hull groaning with every swell that reached even this far into the green. Rain came down in ropes. Somewhere behind them, beyond the wall of storm, the syndicate's fast boat was still out there, patient as debt.

Petra stood at the bow with the strongbox lashed to the deck at her feet and a flare gun in her hand, watching the channel mouth they'd come through.

"They'll have to follow us in single file," Wren said beside her, voice pitched under the rain. "Shallows will slow them."

"Slow them," Petra repeated. "Not stop them."

"No." Wren didn't pretend otherwise. Water ran off their hood in sheets; beneath it their face was calm in a way Petra was beginning to understand wasn't peace but inheritance—the calm of someone who'd grown up on a boat that sank. "But we bought the night. That's what my mother used to say about these channels. You don't escape them. You buy the night."

Milo appeared aft, soaked through, holding the radio handset like it might still confess something. "Nothing. Antenna's half-drowned. I got maybe a kilometer of range if I hold it up and pray."

"Then pray loud," Petra said. "When they come, I want every coast guard station from Ambon to Davao hearing this."

She knelt beside the strongbox. Salt-crusted syndicate markings, the same three-bar sigil she'd seen burned into crates at ports she'd landed at for three years without knowing what she was looking at. Her father's boat had carried that sigil's cargo in its last week of life. She hadn't known that either. Three years of not-knowing, stacked like ballast.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake when this moment finally came, and instead they were pilot's hands, doing the next thing, and the next.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, when the rain slackened just enough to hear engines.

The syndicate boat couldn't follow them deep into the channel—it grounded once, hard, with a sound like a bone breaking—but it didn't need to. Four men came over the roots in the grey half-light, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had done this before, and the fifth man who followed them did not wade at all. He stepped off the bow onto a sandbar as though the storm were a formality he'd chosen not to attend.

Petra knew him before he spoke. Not his face—she'd never seen his face—but the way Milo went rigid beside her, the way Sari's wrench stopped turning in her hand. Some men you recognize by the weather they bring.

"You're difficult to find," the broker said. He was small, neat, dry under an umbrella one of his men held. His voice was almost gentle. "Three years, Ms.—ah. Forgive me. What do you go by now?"

The question landed exactly where he'd aimed it. Petra felt the old reflex rise—to give him nothing, a false name, a shrug, motion. She'd been no one for so long that being no one had started to feel like safety.

Then she looked down at the strongbox between her boots.

"My father," she said, "was Tomas Ishikawa. Captain of the *Kestrel*."

Something flickered across the broker's face—not surprise. Satisfaction. "Yes," he said softly. "He was."

"You cut his hull from the inside."

"I signed a document," the broker said, with the mildness of a man discussing freight rates. "Other men held the tools. This is how the world works, Ms. Ishikawa. It worked that way three years ago, and it works tonight. Now. The box, please. My clients are sentimental about their paperwork, and I am authorized to make your departure from this archipelago very comfortable. A new name. A new country. No past at all."

It was, she thought, the same offer she'd been making herself for three years. He was simply better funded.

Behind the broker's men, at the edge of the mangrove screen, the water moved.

Elias came through it like something the storm had decided to return. He was bleeding from a cut above his eye, his survey jacket gone, and he was holding the one thing Petra had told him never to bring near her again: his commission papers, the syndicate retainer that had paid for his vessel and his silence and his careful, dutiful grief. He walked them straight up the sandbar and dropped them in the standing water at the broker's feet.

"The instruments were never the job," Elias said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "There were no instruments. There was only ever the box, and the woman who could open the site, and I knew that when I wrote your contract. I've known for three years what happened on the *Kestrel*, and I chose the commission because I told myself keeping you ignorant would keep you alive." He turned and looked at her, rain streaming down his face, and there was no surveyor's steadiness left in him at all. "I'm done choosing wrong."

The broker sighed, the way a man sighs at a delayed shipment. "Mr. Vane. You are a contractor. You have no standing here."

"I have standing," Elias said, "because I'm standing here."

One of the four men shifted his weight, and a pistol came out from under a wet jacket, and everything after that happened in the space of a single breath.

Petra moved first—not toward the gun, away from it, dragging Wren down behind the strongbox as the shot went wide and tore a strip of bark off the mangrove root above them. Sari threw the wrench. It caught the gunman across the wrist with a sound Petra felt in her teeth. Milo was shouting into the radio, holding the handset high over his head, screaming coordinates and names and *coast guard, coast guard, all stations* into a storm that had, without any of them noticing, begun to thin.

And then the sky answered.

Not thunder. Rotors. Two grey shapes came low out of the breaking cloud cover, searchlights swinging white blades across the water, and a voice amplified to godhood rolled over the mangroves: **VESSEL IN THE CHANNEL. THIS IS THE INDONESIAN MARITIME PATROL. STAND DOWN AND REMAIN WHERE YOU ARE.**

The broker did not run. That was the worst part, Petra thought later. He simply stood in the rising water among his fallen men and looked at her with something like professional regret, as if she were a line item that had failed to reconcile.

"You understand," he said, as the patrol boats came grinding through the shallows and men in orange began wading toward them, "that a box is only paper. Names can be buried deeper than boats."

"Then I'll dig," Petra said.

* * *

They opened the strongbox on the deck of the *Alcedo*, in the grey-gold light that follows a storm, with coast guard officers standing witness and the syndicate boat riding at tow-line behind them like a caught fish.

Inside: ledgers. Payment schedules in three currencies. Photographs of port officials whose faces Petra had trusted at fuel docks for three years. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that made her chest hurt, a logbook with a cracked spine and a coffee ring on the cover that she recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

Her father's last logbook. The real one—not the forged manifest, not the sanitized copy the inquiry had accepted. His entries ran to within two days of the sinking. Cargo discrepancies. A name circled. A final entry, dated the night before the end:

*Something wrong with this load. E.V. agrees—we both sign off tomorrow or we don't sail. If anything happens, tell Petra the truth is in the numbers. She'll know where to look. She always counts.*

Petra read it twice. Then she handed it to Wren, whose hands shook as they took it, and then to Sari, and then to Milo, because it belonged to all of them now—it belonged to everyone the *Kestrel* had ever carried.

Elias stood apart, dripping, unclaimed. When she finally crossed the deck to him, he didn't reach for her. He just waited, which was the first right thing he'd done in three years.

"Elias Vane," she said, and heard her own voice come out level and full, carrying across the water the way Milo's radio call had carried. "My name is Petra Ishikawa. Daughter of Tomas Ishikawa, master of the *Kestrel*. Pilot of the *Alcedo*. And I am done being a rumor."

The officers were writing it down. Let them. Let it be on record in every language the Triangle spoke.

Elias exhaled like a man surfacing. "Petra," he said, just the name, and it undid something in her chest that three years hadn't touched.

She didn't forgive him. Not yet—maybe not cleanly, maybe not ever entirely. But she took his hand anyway, salt-wet and shaking now at last, hers too, and together they watched the sun come up over the mangroves while the coast guard loaded the broker aboard and the ledgers lay open to the light.

Above them, gulls were already circling the channel mouth, and the *Alcedo* rocked gently on water going calm, as if the sea itself had finally exhaled.

Sari appeared at the companionway, wiping her hands. "Hull'll hold," she said. "Barely. Somebody want to tell me where we're headed?"

Petra looked at her crew—her crew, hers by choice—and smiled for the first time in three years without checking over her shoulder.

"Home," she said. "Wherever we decide that is."
