## 10. Anchorage

The coast guard station at Tanjung Selatan smelled of diesel and old paper, and Petra sat in it for eleven hours giving her name.

Not a name—a *name*. Petra Ishikawa, daughter of Kenji Ishikawa, master of the *Kestrel*, deceased three years and four months as of the monsoon that nearly took the rest of them. She said it into a recorder. She said it to a lieutenant with kind eyes and a stenographer's patience. She signed it at the bottom of fourteen pages of testimony, and when the pen lifted from the last page her hand did not shake, which surprised her more than anything else that week.

The syndicate broke the way rotten things break—quietly, all at once. The strongbox had done its work: ledgers of payments routed through three shell companies, a manifest forged in a harbor office that no longer existed, and names. Names of officials, names of brokers, and at the center of it all a man named Corvo whose face she would carry longer than she wanted to—the flat calm of him in the mangroves, the way he'd said *your father was a rounding error* as if grief were arithmetic. He was in custody now. So were six others. Two had run for open water and been caught by weather instead, which felt like justice of an older make.

The lieutenant had asked her, near the end, why she'd hidden for three years. She'd thought about lying, found she didn't have the energy, and told the truth: because hiding felt like safety, and safety felt like something she'd forfeited on the night the *Kestrel* went down with her father aboard and her future aboard, and she hadn't known yet that they were different losses requiring different grieving.

He'd nodded like he'd heard it before. Maybe he had.

* * *

They flew out two days later, the *Tern* patched and re-riveted, Sari having argued with the engine until the engine conceded. Milo rode copilot with his feet up until she made him put them down. Wren sat in the back among the empty med crates, turning one salvaged pearl over and over in their fingers—not payment now, just a thing to hold.

The reef came up green-black under them, and then the water shallowed and turned the color of bottle glass, and there it was: the site. The survey buoys were gone, hauled by the coast guard's divers, but the memorial was new. Elias had arranged it before he'd even finished his own testimony—six markers set on the reef crest in bronze that would green with salt, one for each soul the *Kestrel* had carried, including a deckhand named Aris Wren, and including, at the end of the line, KENJI ISHIKAWA, MASTER.

She brought the *Tern* down low—not landing, just passing, the way you pass a grave—and Wren leaned between the seats to look.

"He'd have hated the fuss," Petra said.

"He'd have hated being dead more," Wren said, and she laughed, sudden and wet, and Milo whooped for no reason at all, and that was how they crossed the reef: loud, alive, counting nothing.

They landed on the strip at Batu Lompat where it had all started, and there was a crowd—small, but real. The clinic staff she'd flown medicine to for three years without ever letting them learn her surname. The harbormaster. A woman from the highlands settlement holding a baby Petra had once flown through a storm at midnight, back when she was nobody, back when flying medicine was just a way to keep moving.

"You're the Ishikawa girl," the woman said. Not a question. "Your father ran supplies up the coast when I was young. Everyone knew the *Kestrel*'s horn."

Petra looked at her hands. "I'm the Ishikawa," she said. "Yes."

It fit differently than she expected. Not like a mask going on. Like a coat coming home from storage—still hers, smelling faintly of salt and old cedar, sleeves a little short because grief had grown her in strange directions.

* * *

Elias found her at dusk on the seawall, where the strip met the water and the water met everything else.

He looked better than he had any right to. Gray at the temples now, lines around his eyes that hadn't been there at thirty, but standing the way he always stood—like a man braced against a current only he could feel. His commission was gone; he'd resigned it in writing before the boarding party ever reached the mangroves, choosing her over the institution that had taught him to choose wrong. He'd told her this on the boat afterward, rain still streaming off his jaw, and she'd said *we'll see*, and *we'll see* had carried them both here.

"Petra." Just her name. He'd earned the right to use it again, and he knew it, and he used it carefully, like a tool that had cut him before.

"Elias."

"I'm not staying," he said. "I want you to know that first. I'm not asking you to stay either. I learned my lesson about asking people to be things they aren't."

"Good start."

He almost smiled. "I've taken a contract. Hydrographic survey, independent. Six months in the eastern Triangle, mapping channels nobody's charted since the war." He turned to face the water. "I'll be moving. You'll be moving. Sometimes those lines will cross."

"Sometimes."

"Sometimes." He was quiet a moment. "Three years ago I chose duty over you and called it protection. Then I chose a commission over the truth and called it patience. I'm not going to stand here and call whatever this is love if love is just another word I use to keep you in reach." He looked at her then, fully, the way he had across a negotiation table in chapter four of their disaster, except his eyes weren't negotiating anymore. "So I won't ask for a future. I'll ask for an anchorage. Somewhere we can both put in when the weather turns. No chain on the anchor. No lease. Just—when you see my flag in a harbor, you know you're welcome at my table. And when I see yours."

Petra watched the last light go copper on the swell. She thought about cages, and how she'd spent three years building one out of motion and calling it freedom. She thought about anchors, and how everyone she'd ever loved had confused them with cages too, and how maybe the difference wasn't in the iron at all but in whether anyone remembered the anchor belonged to the boat, and not the other way around.

"An anchorage," she said. "That I can do."

"That's it?"

"That's everything. Don't ruin it by making it bigger." She held out her hand, and after a beat he took it, and they shook like captains, which was its own kind of vow, and then he pulled her in and hugged her like family, which was truer than the vow, and she let him, and the sea did what the sea does, which is continue.

"When your survey crosses the reef," she said into his shoulder, "come to the memorial. Tell my father what you told me in the mangroves. He'd want to hear it from you."

"I will." His voice cracked on it, just slightly. "Every year."

* * *

Wren announced it at breakfast, mouth full of rice, in the manner of someone who had already decided and was merely informing the room: "I'm staying on."

Sari didn't look up from the carburetor she was rebuilding on the table, which was a hygiene violation nobody commented on anymore. "We don't pay much."

"I don't need much. I have pearls."

"We don't fly exciting routes."

"I spent a week trapped in a mangrove swamp while armed men circled us in a monsoon." Wren swallowed. "I'm not looking for exciting. I'm looking for—" They paused, searching, and Petra watched them find the word the way you watch someone surface from a deep dive, slowly, holding air. "Belonging. My dad had it on the *Kestrel*. I read it in his letters. I thought the reef would give it back. It gave me a box instead." They shrugged. "You gave me a seat."

Milo pointed his spoon at them. "You get the jump seat. Forever. That's the price of stowing away."

"The jump seat has the best view."

"It does not."

"It faces backward. You can see everywhere you've been."

Petra, pouring coffee, felt something in her chest go loose and warm and unguarded, and realized with mild alarm that this was what people meant by happiness—not the big weather of it, just this, a kitchen table with an engine on it and four people arguing about seats.

"Wren," she said. "Crew rules. One: nobody counts miles. Two: everybody bails. Three:" she set the cup down in front of them, "you tell me when you're diving deeper than briefed. Not after. Before."

Wren grinned, and it was Aris Wren's grin off a photograph three years old, and Petra let it land without flinching. "Deal, Captain."

"Not captain."

"Deal, *Captain*."

* * *

The morning run left at first light, the way it always did: vaccines and antibiotics for the highlands strip, a spare alternator for the mission at Kilat, mail that was mostly letters and one parcel of mangoes wrapped in banana leaf that filled the whole cabin with sweetness.

Milo flew copilot with his feet down, mostly. Sari rode along to test the rebuilt engine and fell asleep in twenty minutes, which was the highest praise she knew how to give. Wren took the jump seat, facing backward, watching the coast unspool behind them—the reef, the bronze markers catching sun, the whole bright wound of the past becoming, mile by mile, simply geography.

Petra flew.

She did not count the miles. She did not count the days since the wreck or the months since the monsoon or the years since she'd last said her own name aloud. She counted nothing at all, and found that the sky did not fall, and that the horizon did not require measuring to hold her up.

Below, the Coral Triangle turned its colors—green water, blue water, the dark lace of reefs, a fishing boat dragging its white wake like a signature on the world. Somewhere east, a survey vessel was beginning its slow work. Somewhere south, a coast guard cutter patrolled waters that were, at last, only water.

"Milo," she said, "what's our heading?"

He checked, grinning, because she never asked, because she always knew. "One-nine-five. Same as always."

"Same as always," she agreed, and banked gently toward the highlands, toward the morning, toward wherever the crew landed next—which was, she understood at last, the only definition of home she had ever needed, and the only one that had never once asked her to stop moving, or stop grieving, or stop loving, or stay.

The *Tern* climbed into the light.

The sea went on below, holding everything, losing nothing.
