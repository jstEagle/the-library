## 1. The Tide That Owes Nothing

The tide went out at noon and did not come back.

Beatrix Galloway noticed it first as a silence — the absence of the lagoon's small, ceaseless conversation with the shore. For three days he had been running his levels along the closed spur east of Al-Hamra Junction, a line the company had struck from its books eleven years ago, and he had grown accustomed to the water's murmur beneath the wind. Now the wind had nothing to talk over. He set down his transit, wiped the grit from its lens with his sleeve, and looked out at a seabed lying naked in the sun.

The lagoon was gone. Not lowered — *gone*, emptied as if a hand had pulled a plug somewhere beneath the world. A mile of wet sand stretched toward the horizon, steaming faintly, ribbed with channels that gleamed like veins of quicksilver. Fish flopped in the shrinking pools, silver flashes of panic. And there, where no chart in his possession showed anything but open water, stood a wall of black rock.

He knew the coastline. That was the trouble. He had spent six weeks memorizing it from the inside of a company survey office, copying figures by lamplight for men who paid him to know things and then told him not to say them aloud. The charts showed this reach as shallow, shifting, unremarkable. The old Admiralty sheets showed less. His own erased map — the one folded now against his heart like a letter from a dead man — showed nothing at all. That was the point of it. The blank spaces on that map were not ignorance. They were decisions.

Someone had decided this stretch of coast should not exist.

Beatrix picked up his transit and walked down onto the seabed, boots sinking into sand that sucked at each step like something reluctant to let him pass. The air smelled of iodine and rot and older things — kelp dying in the sudden light, and beneath that, a mineral smell he could not name, cold stone breathing out of a wound. Gulls screamed overhead, wheeling down to feast on the stranded bounty, and even their noise felt wrong, too bright, like laughter at a funeral.

The rock wall rose ahead of him, thirty feet of basalt furred with weed, and Beatrix saw why the tide had never troubled it: it was not natural ground at all. The courses were too regular. Cut blocks, fitted without mortar, their joints sealed with a dark resin that had kept the sea out for — how long? Long enough for the sea itself to forget.

And in the center of the wall, exposed for the first time in living memory, was a doorway.

It was not carved so much as *refused* — an opening in the stone shaped like a mouth mid-scream, its lintel a single slab wider than two men lying head to foot. Around the arch ran lines of script, deeply incised, filled with something pale that caught the light: crushed shell, maybe, or bone ash. Beatrix stood before it with his notebook open and his pencil motionless, and understood that every character on that stone had been cut by someone who expected readers, and feared them.

He could not read a word of it.

That should have been a relief. It was not. Because below the script, at knee height where any man approaching would have to bow his head to enter, someone had carved a row of small figures — human shapes, dozens of them, each one crossed through with a single deep stroke. Counted. Struck off. Like entries in a ledger. Like names.

Beatrix had seen ledgers before. He had spent the last year learning what companies did with them.

He crouched, touched one of the crossed figures, and the stone was cold despite the noon sun, cold as if the rock behind it had never once been warmed. Behind him the pools ticked and settled, the last of the lagoon giving up its argument, and he became aware of the strangest quality to the emptiness: it was not empty. The drained seabed lay around him like a room after the guests have left — chairs still warm, glasses still full — and he had the crawling certainty that the tide had not simply failed to return. It had been *sent away*. Pulled back like a curtain. Like a sheet drawn off a body for identification.

His hands were steady. They always were; it was the one inheritance from his father worth keeping. But his breath had gone short, and when he straightened and looked up at the lintel slab again he found himself reading the blank spaces between the characters instead of the characters themselves, because blank space was his trade, blank space was where companies wrote their truest sentences.

Eleven years ago, the Coastal Extension had been graded through this country and then abandoned overnight. No bankruptcy, no reroute, no announcement. Surveyors dismissed with a month's pay and a warning. Maps recalled. His own recall had come later, quieter — a polite letter asking him to surrender his field copies, followed by a promotion that was also an exile, followed by the years of being useful and untrusted in exact measure. He had burned nothing. He had surrendered copies. Copies were not originals. An engineer who surrendered everything he knew was an engineer who could be discarded, and Beatrix Galloway had resolved, somewhere in the long gray years since, never again to be entirely discardable.

Now he stood on ground his map said was sea, in front of a door his map said was nothing, and felt the particular dread that comes not from danger but from *design*. The cave sat precisely where his erased map held its largest blank — not near it, not adjacent, but exactly, to the chain-link, as if the blank had been drafted around the cave the way a tailor cuts a coat around a shoulder. Whoever made that map had known this rock was here. Had measured it, plotted it, and then rubbed it out with the same care they'd used on everything else.

You do not erase a cave. You erase what a cave contains.

A fish died in a pool behind him, a small silver convulsion, and he turned too fast, heart slamming. Nothing there. Wind moving weed. His own shadow thrown long across the seabed, thin as a rail.

But standing there, he let himself do the arithmetic he had been avoiding since noon. Tides did not fail. Currents did not reverse on schedule. Unless — and here the thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome as a stranger at a sickbed — unless something offshore had shifted, some movement of the seafloor, some subsidence or blockage far below, and the sea had simply found a new arrangement and left this one dry. A freak. An accident. Nature owing nothing to anyone.

Or the company had done it. Or time had done it for the company. Eleven years of buried reasons, and the sea had finally stopped keeping their secret for free.

He took out his notebook — the real one, the one that lived inside his coat rather than the show copy in his satchel — and made his measurements quickly, working with the economy of a man who has learned that observation is a perishable good. Bearing to the cave mouth from the ruined watchtower on the point. Distance by pacing, corrected for slope. The depth of the cut blocks. A rubbing of three of the crossed figures, graphite whispering over paper. When he finished he looked at what he'd written and understood that he had just committed, in ink, the act his erasure had been designed to prevent.

The sun was lowering. Far out on the horizon, where the sea ought to have been climbing the shore by now, the waterline had not moved a hand's width. Whatever had taken the lagoon was keeping it.

Beatrix climbed back up to the shingle and stood a moment looking at the black doorway below, already gathering shadow, the shell-white script going gray with dusk. Tomorrow the expedition would arrive — the geologist, the journalist, the guide, and whatever the company sent along to keep the ledger balanced. He had read their names on the manifest with the wariness of a man counting exits. He would tell them about the cave, of course. He had been hired to survey, and the cave was a fact, and facts were the only currency he had ever been able to hold onto honestly.

But he would not tell them about the blank space. Not yet. Not until he knew which of them had been chosen for the same reason he had.

Behind him, in the darkening seabed, something shifted — a slide of gravel, soft, deliberate, like a foot testing its weight.

Beatrix did not turn around. He walked back along the shingle in the last light, unhurried, counting his steps, and the sea stayed out, and the mountain kept its ledger, and the tide, wherever it had gone, owed nothing to anyone — least of all to him.
