## 2. A Party of Convenient Strangers

They came to the coast the way weather comes—uninvited, in stages, and with a smell that arrived before they did.

Beatrix Galloway watched them assemble on the salt flats below the surveyor's camp, and he counted them twice, because the first count had troubled him. Five strangers, plus himself: six souls for an expedition into a cave no living Bedouin would enter. Six was a tidy number. Tidy numbers in Beatrix's experience were never accidents. They were budgets. Someone had done arithmetic before anyone packed a single crate.

The tide had drained the lagoon three days ago and not come back. It lay out there beyond the flats like a debtor who had finally fled town, exposing the black mouth of the cave and its carved lintel of symbols nobody could read. The company's telegram had used the word *opportunity*. Beatrix had spent eleven years learning what the company meant when it used words like that.

He descended from the ridge to meet them, boots crunching on salt crust so bright it made his eyes water, and the first thing he learned about his companions he learned from how they stood.

* * *

The journalist introduced himself as Ambrose Quill, which Beatrix did not believe for one second, and shook hands like a man pumping water from a well that might run dry at any moment. He had a camera in a leather case slung across his chest and a notebook already open despite the fact that nothing had happened yet.

"Galloway. The engineer." Quill said it the way other men said *the murderer* or *the miracle*, hungry for whichever it turned out to be. "They say you surveyed spurs out here that don't exist."

"They say a lot of things."

"Do you deny it?"

"I deny that they say anything worth printing."

Quill grinned, delighted, and wrote something down anyway. Beatrix watched him do it. A man who took notes on nothing was a man paid to find something—or paid to make sure something was never found. He couldn't yet tell which, and that uncertainty sat in his stomach like swallowed seawater.

The guide was easier to read and harder to trust, which Beatrix suspected was the honest arrangement. Her name was Halima, and she was older than she moved, which is to say her hands were sixty and her walk was thirty. She carried no rifle and no map, only a water gourd and a string of beads worn smooth as river stones, and she looked at the cave's black mouth across the flats the way a widow looks at a grave she was not allowed to attend.

"You've been here before," Beatrix said to her, in the trade Arabic he'd picked up along forgotten railheads.

"Everyone has been here before." She didn't look at him. "The mountain remembers everyone. That is why it charges admission."

"Charges what?"

She turned then, and her eyes were the color of the deep water the lagoon used to hold. "You will see what the songs charge. And then you will wish the tide had never gone out."

It was not a threat. That was the worst of it. It sounded like grief, delivered in advance.

* * *

Elena Marsh arrived last, from the north, alone except for a mule loaded with sample cases, and Beatrix knew she was trouble before she said a word, because she was the only one of the five who looked bored.

She was a compact woman in a dust-colored field coat, sleeves rolled with the practicality of someone who had stopped performing competence years ago and simply possessed it. She knelt at the edge of the salt flat without greeting anyone, cracked off a piece of crust with a small hammer, tasted it, frowned, and wrote a number in a ledger of her own.

"Company geologist?" Beatrix asked.

"Company geologist." She didn't stand up. "And you're the man whose map has holes in it."

"That's one way to describe my career."

"I've read your surveys. The 1899 coastal series." Now she stood, and she studied him the way she'd studied the salt—with a hammer ready. "There are corridors graded on those sheets that appear nowhere in the company archive. Grading costs money. Money leaves paperwork. Your paperwork was burned, Mr. Galloway, and I would very much like to know by whom."

"Would you."

"It's why I'm here." She said it plainly, without coquetry or conspiracy, and somehow that was more dangerous than either. "I dig where I'm told and record what I find. What I found this year is that I am never told anything true. So." She wiped her hand on her coat and offered it. "Elena Marsh. I suspect we're both liars, and I'd rather know yours early."

Her grip was dry and strong and over far too soon, and Beatrix felt the first pull then—not of attraction exactly, though that was there, immediate and unwelcome, but of recognition. Here was another person carrying documents that could ruin her. He understood the shape of her loneliness from the inside, and understanding someone that fast is the most dangerous thing a man can do in country like this.

He let go of her hand and made himself look at the cave instead.

* * *

Elias Voss arrived the way institutions arrive: already certain he was welcome.

He came up the coast road in a hired motorcar, white linen immaculate against the salt glare, and stepped out into the desolation as if stepping into a club room. He greeted Halima with respectful warmth. He greeted Quill with professional charm. He greeted Elena Marsh by name before anyone had introduced them—which Beatrix noted, and filed, and did not forget.

Then Voss turned to Beatrix and offered a smile that was flawless in every dimension except the one that mattered.

"Galloway. The company speaks highly of your work."

"The company burned my work."

"And yet here you are." Voss spread his hands, taking in the flats, the dead lagoon, the black mouth of the mountain. "Rehired. Trusted. Given the closed charts. That should tell you something about how the company treats its mistakes—it recycles them into purpose." He clapped Beatrix on the shoulder, and his hand weighed nothing and everything. "Six of us. A small party moves fast. You'll lead us to the spur alignments, Marsh will assess the strata, our friend will document, and Halima will keep us alive. Simple expedition. Nothing more."

*Nothing more* hung in the air like the smell of the motorcar's exhaust. Beatrix looked around the circle—at the journalist writing nothing down, at the guide counting her beads, at Elena watching Voss with an expression of polite blankness that Beatrix was beginning to suspect was her native language for *I see you*.

Six people. Chosen. Assembled on a coastline the company had spent twenty years pretending wasn't there.

He thought of his map—the true one, hidden in oilcloth beneath the false bottom of his instrument case, the one with the erased corridors inked back in by lamplight—and he thought about who else might have been shown a copy. About why a geologist would be sent who asked questions. About why a journalist would be sent at all.

You do not send witnesses to a place unless you want the place witnessed. And you do not want a place witnessed unless you fear what happens if it stays dark.

"Mr. Voss," Beatrix said, "who drew up the manifest?"

Voss's smile held perfectly. "Why, the company did. As always."

"And who at the company reads the manifest?"

Something flickered behind the smile—not quite a crack, more the shadow a crack casts from the inside. "Mr. Galloway. We are going to look at a cave. Let's not make it more than it is."

But that night, as the camp settled and the wind came off the dead lagoon carrying the mineral smell of the exposed seabed, Beatrix saw two things he was not supposed to see.

The first was Elena Marsh, awake past midnight, holding her own survey ledger to the firelight—and tearing a page out of it, slowly, deliberately, feeding it to the flames page by patient page until nothing remained but ash and her careful face watching him watch her. She did not startle. She met his eyes across the fire, and gave the smallest nod, as if to say: *now you know I also burn things.*

The second was Elias Voss, standing apart in the darkness with a telegraph key wired to a portable battery, tapping out a message into the empty night toward some station Beatrix couldn't see—a message long enough that it was not logistics. Logistics took ten words.

When Voss finished and turned, Beatrix was already lying in his blanket, breathing slow, eyes shut, heart going like a locomotive on a downgrade.

Six of them. One mountain. And somewhere inland, a corridor graded for rails that were never laid, waiting at the end of his erased lines like a sentence no one had finished writing.

In the morning they would march. And Beatrix Galloway, who had once believed maps told the truth, would spend the whole day trying to decide which of these four strangers had been sent to keep the truth buried—and whether the fifth had been sent for the same reason, and was simply better at it than all the rest.
