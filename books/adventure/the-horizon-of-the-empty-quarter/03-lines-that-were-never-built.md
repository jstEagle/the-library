## 3. Lines That Were Never Built

The map lied to him in the morning the way it always did — politely, in ink.

Beatrix Galloway sat on the tailgate of the supply wagon with the doctored survey spread across his knees, holding down one corner with a tin cup of tea gone cold. The wind off the Empty Quarter came in long, dry breaths that lifted the paper's edge and let it fall, lifted and fell, like something trying to read over his shoulder. He had redrawn this sheet himself, three years ago, in an office that smelled of pipe smoke and fear. The company's cartographer had stood behind his chair and said, *There was never a spur here, Galloway. There was never anything here.* And Beatrix had drawn the nothing exactly as instructed: a clean white corridor running inland from the coast, forty miles of blankness where his own instruments had sung with iron.

Now he rode through the blankness, and the blankness had bones.

The expedition had broken camp before dawn, Halima leading on a camel that moved like poured water, Voss riding well back with the geologist's instruments lashed to his saddle as though he owned them. Elena Marsh had taken the eastern line of the traverse without being asked, and Beatrix had found himself adjusting his own bearing to keep her in view — not so close as to be obvious, not so far as to lose her against the dun immensity of the gravel plain. He told himself it was professional. He was the surveyor; she was the geologist; their work overlapped like hands folded together.

He told himself a great many things lately, and fewer of them held weight each day.

* * *

They found the first cut at midmorning, where the plain rose into low stone ridges the color of old rust.

Halima stopped her camel and did not dismount. She looked at the ridge the way one looks at a face in a crowd that one hoped never to see again, and she began, very quietly, to sing — two notes, descending, over and over, a sound less like music than like a door being closed slowly.

"What is that?" the journalist, Okafor, asked, already unslinging his camera.

"A song for passing," Halima said. "My grandmother sang it crossing this ground. She said the ground remembers being opened."

Beatrix climbed the ridge alone. At its crest he stopped, and the breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a punctured tire.

Below, running dead straight toward the interior, lay a graded rail bed.

Not a track — no rails, no ties, none of the furniture of a living line. But the earth itself had been shaped: a corridor sixty feet wide, cut and filled and drained, its ballast shoulder still crisp after all these years because almost nothing rots in a desert this dry. Culverts of dressed stone crossed beneath it at proper intervals. A drainage ditch ran along the upland side, its profile still knife-edged. It stretched away southward, straight as a struck nail, until the heat haze took it, and Beatrix knew — the way a man knows his own signature — that it ran to the cave.

His map said there was nothing here. His map said it in his own handwriting.

He knelt and pressed his palm flat against the graded surface. The gravel was hot, but beneath the heat the ground felt dense, engineered, deliberate. Someone had moved mountains of earth along this line. Someone had surveyed it with instruments no better than his own and gotten it right, because the bed did not wander, did not bow around the ridges — it went through them, in cuts twenty feet deep, blasting powder and patient mule teams written into every wall of stone.

And then, overnight by the look of it, everyone had walked away.

Railroads did not do this. Railroads were greed made physical; they finished what they started or they died loudly, in bankruptcy courts and newspaper columns. A line abandoned mid-build left ruins, lawsuits, rusted locomotives sold for scrap. This left nothing. No equipment, no campsites he could see, no cairns of spoil beyond what construction required. It was as if the company had built forty miles of intention and then erased itself from the site more thoroughly than it had ever been erased from a map.

More thoroughly than *he* had erased it from a map.

"Mr. Galloway."

Elena's voice came from below and to his left. She had climbed the ridge without him hearing — a habit of hers he was beginning to suspect was not accidental. She stood with her sample hammer hanging loose in one hand and her hat pushed back, squinting down the corridor of empty earth.

"You've gone very still," she said. "For a man whose job is looking at ground, you're looking at it like it insulted your family."

"It's a rail bed," he said. "Graded. Never laid."

"I can see that." She came up beside him, close enough that he could smell the dust and the faint camphor of her field kit, and crouched to run her thumb along a culvert stone. "Dressed granite. Hauled, not quarried local — see the mica? Wrong geology entirely. They freighted stone forty miles to drain a ditch." She looked up at him, and the light caught the sweat at her temple. "That's not ambition, Mr. Galloway. That's obsession. Nobody spends like that on a spur to nowhere."

"Maybe it isn't to nowhere."

"No," she agreed softly. "I don't think it is."

She rose and stood shoulder to shoulder with him, both of them looking down the long straight throat of the thing, and Beatrix became aware of two truths occupying the same body at once: that he wanted, badly, to tell her everything — the erased map, the office with the pipe smoke, the cartographer's voice saying *there was never anything here* — and that telling her would put her in the exact current that had already pulled one engineer under. Duty said silence. Something warmer and considerably stupider said *she would understand.*

She turned her head slightly, not quite looking at him. "You knew this was here."

It was not a question, and he could not make it one by denying it.

"The company hired me to survey closed spurs," he said. "Closed lines. Lines that exist on no current map."

"And this is one?"

"This is worse than one. This is a line that was never allowed to exist." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he trusted less with every step. "Someone paid to have it forgotten, Miss Marsh. I'm standing on the receipt."

She was quiet a moment. Below them, faintly, Halima's two-note song drifted up from the plain, closing its slow door.

"Elena," she said.

He looked at her.

"If we're going to stand on buried things together," she said, "you may as well use my name. And I'll use yours, Beatrix, though I notice you didn't offer it." The corner of her mouth moved. "Geologists are trained to read strata. People deposit themselves in layers too. You're the most layered man on this expedition, and I include Voss, who is mostly just varnish."

Despite himself — despite the cold spreading in his chest where the map used to sit — Beatrix laughed. It came out rough, unused.

"Elena," he said, testing it. It fit in his mouth better than it should have.

"Good." She crouched again, broke a small fragment from the culvert stone, wrapped it in cloth, and wrote something in her notebook — he saw her date it, and saw her write, after the date, *corridor, unnamed.* Then she closed the book and looked up at him with an expression he would carry for the rest of his life: open, level, and quietly afraid.

"Whoever built this wanted to reach the cave," she said. "And whoever un-built it wanted the reaching forgotten. Those are two different groups of men, Beatrix, and we're standing in the argument between them." She paused. "Which side sent you?"

The question hung there, small and enormous. He could answer it honestly — *neither, both, I don't know anymore* — or he could answer it safely, and he discovered, standing in the heat with her eyes on him, that he no longer knew which answer was which.

"Ask me again tonight," he said. "When I've decided."

She held his gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed, then nodded once, and they descended the ridge together, not touching, closer than touching.

* * *

They followed the corridor inland through the afternoon.

Voss rode at the head now, having produced, from some pocket of authority, a compass bearing of his own. "The geology improves ahead," he announced to no one in particular. "Limestone. Cave country." He smiled at Beatrix when he said it, that smile that arrived at the mouth and stopped at the border, and Beatrix nodded and said nothing and counted.

Counted what, he wasn't sure yet. Steps. Men. Lies. A surveyor's habit: measure everything twice, trust nothing once.

In the late light they reached a place where the corridor bent — the only bend in forty miles, a wide, graceful curve around a hill of black basalt — and there, sheltered in the lee of the cut, Beatrix found the first artifact of the abandonment. A tie. Just one, half-buried in ballast, creosote-blackened hardwood, and stamped into its end in faded tar:

**C.Q.R. — 1891 — DO NOT REUSE**

Coastal Quarter Railway. A name that appeared in no ledger he had ever audited, on no map he had ever drawn or destroyed. He crouched over it while the others watered the camels, and ran his fingers into the stamped letters, and felt the shape of a word he couldn't yet pronounce.

Behind him, boots on gravel. Voss, stopping at his shoulder, looking down at the tie with an expression of mild, well-bred interest, like a man regarding a curiosity in a shop window.

"Remarkable craftsmanship," Voss said. "Shame about the climate." Then, pleasantly, turning away: "We make the cave by tomorrow noon, I think. Best get your bearings straight tonight, Galloway. Wouldn't want anyone wandering off the route."

Beatrix watched him walk back to the fire, and understood that the question Elena had asked on the ridge had just been answered for him — not in words, but in the particular way Voss had said *your bearings*, as though he already knew they were crooked.

That night Beatrix did not sleep. He lay with the doctored map under his bedroll and listened to the desert ticking as it cooled, and somewhere past midnight he heard, faint and rising on the wind from the direction they were headed, Halima singing in her sleep — not the two-note song for passing this time, but something longer, older, with names in it.

A great many names.

He got up quietly, took out his pencil, and on the blank white corridor of his own false map, in the smallest hand he could manage, he wrote a single word across the nothing:

*Why?*

By dawn they would break camp and follow the line to its end, and whatever waited there, Beatrix knew the question would stop being rhetorical.
