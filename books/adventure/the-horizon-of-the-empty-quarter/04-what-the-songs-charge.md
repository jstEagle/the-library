## 4. What the Songs Charge

Halima had known the mountain's name before she had known her own.

Her grandmother sang it to her the way other grandmothers sang lullabies — a low, grinding melody that moved like a camel's walk, patient and inevitable, with a refrain that always ended on a falling note, as though the song itself were descending into stone. *Jabal al-Dam*, the grandmother called it, and then laughed at her own daring, because no one said the whole name aloud near the coast. The fishermen shortened it. The company men who came in their white steamers never learned it at all; they called the headland by its survey number, as if a thing without a name could not hold a grudge.

Now Halima stood before the cave mouth with the tide still hissing out of it, and she understood, finally, why the song was sung low.

The inscriptions covered the rock in bands, like the strata of the cliff itself. Not painted — cut. Cut deep, cut clean, by hands that had wanted the work to outlast them. Her grandmother's song had words for this: *the mountain keeps what is given, and gives back only names.* Halima had thought it poetry. Standing here, with salt wind pulling at her veil and the morning light coming up the beach like something arriving late to a funeral, she understood it had been instruction.

"You can read these?" Beatrix Galloway stood a little apart from her, the way he seemed to stand apart from everything, as if he were perpetually waiting for the ground to contradict him. His eyes were on the carved lines, but his voice was careful, pitched for her alone.

"Some of it." She touched the first band of letters. The stone was cold even in the sun, cold as a thing recently drowned. "It is old script. My grandmother's people kept it alive in songs when the writing stopped being used. Songs are how we keep what the world wants to take."

"And what does the world want to take here?"

She looked at him. He asked questions like a man who already suspected the answers and needed someone else to say them first, so the weight would not be his alone. She had met his kind before — the railroad men, the survey men, men with instruments for measuring distance and no instrument at all for measuring consequence.

"Come," she said. "I will read it in order. It is meant to be read in order."

* * *

The first band was an invocation, and her voice found its old shape almost without her permission — the rising cadence, the held breath between phrases. Behind her, the others gathered. Elena Marsh stood closest, her notebook already open, her pencil moving before Halima had finished the first line. The journalist, Okafor, crouched with his camera bag hugged to his chest like a child. The geologist's assistant hovered at the edge, uneasy, glancing back at the sea as though the tide might change its mind and return early.

And Elias Voss stood furthest back, arms folded, smiling his small closed smile, watching not the stone but the readers of it.

Halima translated slowly, feeling each phrase settle into English like sediment into water:

*"Here the mountain was paid. Here the debt was counted. Let no one enter saying they were not told."*

"A warning," Elena said quietly.

"No." Halima shook her head. "A warning says *stay away*. This says *you may come, but you will know*." She moved her hand along the second band, where the letters grew smaller, denser, crowded together like people pressing toward a door. "This is different. This is..." She stopped. Read it again. Her mouth had gone dry, and not from the wind.

"What is it?" Beatrix asked.

"It is a list," she said. "Names. Hundreds of names. And beside each name—" she traced the small marks, the ones she had taken at first for decorative flourishes, the way songs decorate grief to make it bearable— "beside each name there is a word. The same word, over and over. *Adfa'a.* Paid. Settled. As one settles an account."

The wind came off the lagoon then, carrying the smell of exposed weed and old brine, and nobody spoke. Somewhere above them a gull cried once and thought better of it.

"Whose names?" Okafor asked. His camera stayed in its bag. Even he, hungry as he was, understood that some things should be read before they were taken.

Halima bent close. The cutting was fine work, finer than the coarse chisels the coastal masons used. Whoever had cut these names had cared about them — had cared enough to make each letter legible, each name distinct, in a language the dead workers themselves might have spoken. She sounded out the first one, then the next, translating the old forms into the living tongue.

"Yusuf ibn Daoud. Amal bint Rashid. Tariq, son of Tariq, son of none." Her voice caught on that last one, the formula for an orphan of orphans, and she pressed on. "Salim the well-digger. Musa the rope-maker. Fatima, whose trade is not given, only her mother's name, because that is all anyone wrote down."

"These are workers' names," Elena said. Her pencil had stopped moving. "Laborers. These aren't — these are navvies. Tunnel men."

"They are the names of the people who built the first line," Halima said. "My grandmother's song has a verse about them. I thought it was a story about a storm." She heard her own voice go flat and strange. "There was a tunnel, the song says, cut through the headland in the time of the first rails. The company wanted the coast road open before the season turned. They worked the men day and night, and the mountain—" she made herself say it plainly, because the song deserved better than ornament now— "the mountain came down on them. All of them. And the company sealed the tunnel with the men inside, because digging them out would have cost more than digging a new line elsewhere. So they dug elsewhere. And they wrote in their books that the men were lost to weather."

"How do you know that last part?" Voss asked.

His voice was pleasant. That was the terrible thing about it — it was always pleasant, a voice designed for boardrooms and dinner parties, a voice that had never once needed to rise. Halima turned and looked at him fully, and saw that his smile was exactly where it always was, and his eyes were exactly somewhere else.

"Because the song says so," she answered. "And because your company's own stone says so." She gestured at the third band, lower than the others, half-swallowed by centuries of salt bloom, and walked to it, and knelt, and cleared the crust away with the edge of her sleeve.

The letters here were different. Newer. Cut in the company's imported alphabet, in the company's imported style, by someone who had learned letters in a counting-house rather than a courtyard. But the words were in her language anyway, badly spelled, phonetically rendered, the way a foreigner writes down what he hears without understanding what he is hearing.

She read it aloud, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, because inside she was shaking like a rope in wind:

*"By order of the Meridian Coastal Railway. Sealed per instruction of Director Halloway-Brass. Losses attributed to weather event, November the ninth. Entry forbidden. Compensation disbursed to families per schedule B."*

"Compensation disbursed," Elena repeated. Her face had gone the color of the salt crust. "They paid them off. They paid the families off and carved the receipt into the rock."

"Per schedule B," Okafor said softly. He had his camera out now after all. He was photographing the band of letters with the methodical patience of a man loading a weapon. "That's a payroll term. I've seen it in freight ledgers. Schedule A is equipment loss. Schedule B is—"

"Personnel," Beatrix said.

Everyone looked at him. He had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing on a rail bridge hearing, far down the line, a vibration in the iron. Halima watched him read the carved order twice, three times, his lips moving around the director's name, and she saw something pass across his face that she recognized from her father's face the day the company bought their well: not surprise. Confirmation. The grief of a man who had suspected the shape of a thing and now had to live inside its exact dimensions.

"The song charges admission," Halima said, mostly to herself, standing, brushing the salt from her knees. "That is what my grandmother taught me. Everyone thinks the legends forbid the cave. They don't. They *price* it. You may enter, the song says, but you must carry out what you find. You must speak the names. Because the mountain already took its price once — it took the men — and it will not be robbed twice. If you go in and come out silent, you owe it. And the mountain collects debts."

"That's superstition," Voss said lightly.

"It is bookkeeping," Halima answered, and had the small satisfaction of seeing his smile flicker, just once, like a lamp in a draft. "Your company understands bookkeeping. So does the mountain."

* * *

They worked along the wall as the sun climbed, Halima reading, Elena transcribing, Okafor photographing, Beatrix holding the lantern-oil lamp against the shadowed bands even though daylight made it nearly useless — holding it anyway, as if the light were a courtesy owed. Halima lost count of the names somewhere past two hundred. They ran in families, in trades, in villages. Whole hamlets had gone into that tunnel. She found three women with her grandmother's family name and had to stop for a while, kneeling, her forehead nearly touching the stone, breathing the cold breath of the cave mouth until she could stand again.

Elena knelt beside her without a word and simply waited, which was its own kind of fluency.

Voss circulated. That was the thing Halima noticed most, the thing she would turn over later that night when the campfire burned low and everyone pretended to sleep. He did not read the wall. He did not ask her to translate anything specific. He drifted, pleasant and unhurried, and paused behind each of them in turn — behind Okafor longest, noting which plates the journalist exposed; behind Elena, noting which pages she filled; behind Beatrix, noting nothing visible at all, only standing, only watching, the way a man watches a fuse he has already lit.

When Halima reached the final band — a short one, set apart, cut deeper than the rest by someone whose hands had been shaking — Voss was suddenly beside her, closer than he had been all morning.

"What does that one say?" he asked.

She considered lying. She was good at lying; her people had survived three governments by it. But the mountain was listening, and the song charged admission, and she had already decided, somewhere between the second hundred names and the third, which side of the ledger she stood on.

"It says," she translated, "*We were not lost to weather. We were spent like coin. Count us.*"

Voss nodded slowly, as if approving the craftsmanship. Then he took out a small leather notebook of his own, and wrote something in it — briefly, neatly, without covering the page with his hand, without any shame at all — and put it away.

"Remarkable," he said. "Truly remarkable. The company will want a full accounting of what's been recorded here. For the archive, you understand." His smile returned, warm as lamplight, reaching everything except his eyes. "Do finish, Miss Halima. Take whatever time you need."

He walked back toward the tents whistling — actually whistling, a few bright bars of some music-hall tune, absurd and cheerful against the black mouth of the mountain — and Halima stood in the shadow of two hundred and forty-one names and understood, with the plain certainty of her grandmother's oldest verses, that the man had not come to see whether the massacre was true.

He had come to see who would tell.
