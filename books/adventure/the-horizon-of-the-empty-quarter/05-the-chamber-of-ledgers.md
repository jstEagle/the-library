## 5. The Chamber of Ledgers

The passage narrowed the way a throat narrows before speech. Beatrix went first with the carbide lamp held high, its flame bent sideways by a draft that should not have existed this far underground. Air was moving through the mountain — cold, mineral, tasting of iron and old stone — and moving air meant another mouth somewhere, an opening to the world above that no survey had ever recorded.

Behind him came the sounds of the others: Halima's bare feet whispering on rock, Elena's boots, the journalist Okafor's camera case knocking his hip at every step, and last, always last, Elias Voss, whose footsteps had a metronome quality Beatrix had learned to hate without knowing why.

"Feel that," Elena said quietly. "There's a chimney somewhere. The mountain is breathing."

"Mountains don't breathe," Voss said pleasantly.

"This one does."

Beatrix said nothing. He was watching the walls. The inscriptions from the entrance hall had followed them inward — not fading, as he'd half hoped, but changing character, the carved names giving way after fifty paces to columns of shorter marks, tally-like, deliberate. He ran his glove along them. Cut clean, cut by someone with time and a straightedge and a reason.

"Halima," he called back softly. "These aren't warnings anymore."

Her voice floated up the passage, low and even. "No. They're arithmetic."

* * *

The passage opened without warning, the way a held breath opens.

One moment Beatrix was ducking under a shelf of limestone; the next, the lamp's light fell into a chamber so large the beam simply gave up, swallowed by a darkness that had weight and texture. He stepped forward onto level ground — level ground, here, a hundred feet beneath a coastline that had never known a road — and raised the lamp in a slow arc.

The light found paper first.

It covered the near wall: hundreds of sheets pinned or pasted in careful rows, browned to the color of tea, their ink faded but legible. Company letterhead. The Galloway & Meridian Coastal Railway — his own company, the firm whose map sat folded against his ribs like a second heart. Payrolls. He crossed to them slowly, as one approaches something sleeping, and read.

*Al-Rashid, Yusuf — navvy, Second Section — 11 rupees.*
*Osei, Kwame — driller — 14 rupees.*
*Nakamura, Tetsu — powder man — 19 rupees.*

Names. Wages. Weeks of them, columns running down and down, the labor of men who had been told they were building a tunnel through the headland. Men whose names appeared nowhere in any record Beatrix had ever signed, audited, or archived. The company had listed them as lost to weather. Here, the weather had been written down instead, in its own hand, and kept.

"Beatrix." Elena's voice came from deeper in the chamber, and it had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of a geologist standing on ground she did not trust. "You need to see this."

He followed her light. Beyond the payroll wall, set into a natural alcove, stood a wooden crate — military make, brass corners, stenciled with a shipping mark he recognized because he had once processed its twin through the freight office at Meridian Junction. Inside, nested in waxpaper like preserved fruit, were telegrams. Dozens. Okafor reached past him and lifted the top one into the lamplight, and Beatrix read it over his shoulder, and the words went through him like a spike driven true:

*TO SITE ENGINEER, HEADLAND WORKS. FROM G&M COASTAL RAILWAY, OFFICE OF THE CHIEF ENGINEER. PROCEED WITH SECOND CHARGE AS DISCUSSED. NO FURTHER CORRESPONDENCE THIS MATTER. SIGNED HOLLIS.*

Second charge. As discussed. No further correspondence.

He knew what a second charge meant in a tunnel where the first charge had already been fired with men inside it. He knew what no further correspondence meant. Every engineer knew; it was the phrase they used when a thing passed from being an accident to being a decision.

"There are more," Okafor said. His voice was steady in the way of a man holding himself together with professional habit. "Dates, initials, requisitions for lime — lime, Beatrix, you order lime to deal with—" He stopped. He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

And then the lamps found the bones.

They were arranged. That was the thing Beatrix would carry out of this chamber if he carried nothing else: they were *arranged*. Not scattered as collapse leaves them, not heaped as hasty burial leaves them, but laid out in rows upon a shelf of smooth stone, each skeleton separate, each skull placed at the eastern end, each body oriented the same way, toward the sea. Someone had come down here afterward — someone with light, with patience, with grief or guilt or both — and counted them. Beside the rows, scratched into the stone floor in the same careful hand as the tallies in the passage, ran a final line of figures. Halima knelt beside it. She read it aloud, and her voice did not shake, which somehow made it worse.

"One hundred and fourteen," she said. "It says: one hundred and fourteen, and the ledger is closed, and the mountain keeps the account."

Nobody spoke. The draft moved through the chamber, stirring the dead papers, and somewhere above them the sea worked at the coast with its patient, indifferent sound.

Elena stood very still among the bones, her lamp lowered, her face in shadow. When she finally spoke, it was barely above a whisper. "They wanted them counted. Whoever did this — they couldn't stop the killing, but they could refuse to let it be anonymous. This whole room is a refusal."

"It's evidence," Okafor said, already unclasping his camera case. "It's a hundred and fourteen names and a signature and a shipping mark, all in one room, and I am going to spend every plate I brought."

* * *

He worked fast and well, kneeling at the payroll wall, framing the telegrams one by one, the flash pan flaring white and dying, white and dying, each burst printing the chamber onto Beatrix's eyes in negative — bones glowing, names blazing, darkness rushing back between. Voss stood apart during all of it, near the entrance throat of the chamber, arms folded, watching. Beatrix marked it without marking it, the way a surveyor notes a crack in a bridge: filed, weighted, waiting.

"You seem calm," Beatrix said to him, quietly, during one of the dark intervals.

"I'm a guest," Voss said. "Like everyone else."

"You've been quiet since the payrolls."

"I'm respectful of the dead." The smile arrived, brief and correct. "Aren't you?"

Beatrix turned away rather than answer. He crouched by the crate and began copying telegram dates into his notebook — Hollis's name, the site office, the requisition numbers, anything that could be cross-checked against files that still existed above ground. Evidence needed two legs to walk: what was buried and what was filed. He was writing the third date when he heard the sound.

A deep, grinding exhalation from the passage behind them. Stone shifting on stone. Not the small talk of settling rock — something structural, something committed.

Everyone froze. Then Okafor's lamp swung toward the entrance, and the beam found what the sound had made: half the throat of the chamber, the narrow neck they had all squeezed through, now choked with fallen slab, dust sifting down through the light like snow through a broken roof.

Voss was brushing dust from his sleeve.

"I leaned," he said, before anyone asked. His tone was mild, almost apologetic. "The rock up there is rotten. I put my hand on it and it went. I'm sorry — truly. But we all heard it coming; there was warning enough."

"There wasn't," Elena said flatly. "I felt no tremor. There was no shot, no charge—"

"People lean on things, Miss Marsh. Mountains oblige." He looked around at them all, the lamplight making his face gentle and unreadable. "The question isn't how it fell. The question is what we do now. And I'd suggest we do it calmly, because panic uses water faster than thirst does."

Beatrix rose slowly. He looked at the fall — at the fresh fracture faces, pale where the ancient stone was dark, at the way the slabs lay stacked with a neatness that gravity alone rarely managed — and then he looked at Voss, and Voss looked back, and for one long moment neither of them performed anything at all.

Then Beatrix smiled, and it cost him more than any climb of his life.

"Well," he said. "Good thing I'm an engineer."

* * *

They took inventory because inventory was the only prayer available. Water: six canteens, seven people counting Halima. Food: hardtack, some dried fruit, a tin of condensed milk Okafor had carried out of sentiment. Lamps: four, with carbide for perhaps thirty hours if they rationed the flame. Air: the draft still breathed through the chamber, faint but constant, which meant the mountain kept its other mouth open — and a moving draft, Beatrix knew, could be followed upstream like a river run backward.

"We split the water evenly," Voss said. "Ration strictly. Someone will go for help — the strongest climbers, back the way we came, over the fall if they must."

"The fall can't be climbed from below," Beatrix said. "Not with those slabs keyed like that. It was dropped, not shed." He let that sit in the air a moment. "But drafts rise. There'll be a shaft somewhere on these bearings — old workings breathe through chimneys, and this one breathes steadily. Give me until the lamps say midnight, and I'll find it."

"And if you don't?" Voss asked.

"Then we'll have plenty of time to discuss it."

While the others sorted rations, Elena drifted to Beatrix's side under the pretense of examining the fracture in the wall. Her shoulder nearly touched his. She did not look at him when she spoke, and her voice was pitched for him alone.

"Count the canteens again," she murmured. "Count them, and then count us."

He glanced across the chamber. Six canteens. Seven souls. And Voss, who had organized this expedition's stores personally, who had insisted on packing the supplies himself at the coastal camp, standing now with his hands clasped behind his back like a man whose accounts always balanced.

Seven people. Six canteens. Brought by a man who checked everything twice.

Beatrix felt the horror in the room change temperature — cool, settle, harden into something with edges. They had been meant to find this chamber. Enough to be awed. Enough to be complicit. Enough to stay quiet forever, with the mountain's silence sealed around them like a lid. What they had not been meant to do was speak — and the arithmetic of the water had been drawn up before anyone descended, in a camp tent, by lamplight, by a smiling man who packed for fewer guests than he invited.

Elena's hand brushed his, once, briefly — a touch that was not affection but transmission, a signal passed between stations. Then she moved away to help Halima portion the hardtack, her face composed, her eyes saying everything her mouth would not.

Beatrix opened his notebook again, and beneath the telegram dates he wrote three words in shorthand only a railroad surveyor would bother learning:

*Air shaft. Bearings.*

Above him, unseen, the mountain drew its slow breath, and somewhere in the dark above the chamber, a chimney waited — and Beatrix intended to find it before Voss decided the party could spare more than water.
