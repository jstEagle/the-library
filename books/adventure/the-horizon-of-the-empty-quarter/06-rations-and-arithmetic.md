## 6. Rations and Arithmetic

Elena Marsh had counted things her whole life. Strata, grains, degrees of dip and strike, the slow arithmetic of the earth. Numbers were the one language that never lied to her, and so, in the dark behind the collapsed mouth of the cave, with seven people breathing and the air gone thick as wool, she counted.

She did it the way she did everything — quietly, methodically, kneeling on the cold stone with the expedition's packs laid out before her in the light of two storm lanterns. The others had retreated into what passed for sleep: Halima sitting upright against the wall, lips moving through the old songs in a whisper meant for no one; the journalist, Aldous Reeve, cradling his camera case like a child; young Tam, the surveyor's assistant, shivering in a corner with his knees drawn up. Beatrix stood at the far wall where the entrance had been, running his hands over the rubble as though he could read the rock's intention by touch. And Voss — Voss slept, or arranged his body into the convincing shape of sleep, a few feet from the water.

That was the first thing. The water was with Voss.

She had noticed it hours ago, in the first confused hour after the collapse, when the shouting died and the arithmetic of survival began. The expedition's stores had been consolidated, as stores always were, into Voss's keeping — he had insisted, with that reasonable smile, that the company man should carry the company's provisions. Now four canvas water bags and the ration tins sat in a neat row beside him, and Elena, who had packed those stores herself in the coastal depot, knew their weight the way a mother knows the weight of her own child.

She counted again, to be certain. She wanted to be wrong.

Four bags. She remembered six. She remembered packing six, and she remembered the manifest — she had written it herself, in her small precise hand, *water, 6 bags, 4 gal. each* — and she remembered Voss signing for it at the railhead with a flourish, joking about the weight of civilization.

Six bags had come into the cave. Four bags remained, and the entrance was a wall of stone no one could move.

She sat back on her heels and made herself do the rest of it, because the rest of it was the part that mattered. Four bags, sixteen gallons. Seven people, and a body in heat like this needed — she ran the figures the way she'd run them in the field a hundred times — call it a gallon a day per person, generously, if no one exerted themselves, and they had already exerted themselves, and would exert themselves more if Beatrix found his way out. Two days. Perhaps three if they rationed cruelly, if the wounded drank little, if the air stayed cool, if a dozen mercies aligned.

Seven people. Two days. And Voss had brought — she made herself look at the tins now, counting those too — Voss had brought rations for exactly five. Not six. Not seven. Five.

She had packed for eight, with margin, because she was a geologist and margin was her religion. The packs had been in Voss's tent the night before they entered the cave. She had seen him repack them. She had thought nothing of it. A man organizing his kit.

A man organizing his kit.

Elena closed her eyes and let the number sit in her chest like a stone swallowed whole. Five rations. Seven people. A collapsed entrance that only one man had been positioned to cause, and only one man had been standing clear of when it came down. She thought of the telegrams she'd glimpsed in the chamber below, the ones ordering the tunnel collapse forty years ago, the bones arranged in rows by someone who had wanted the dead counted — and the cold, clean horror of it settled over her: the company had done this arithmetic before. Voss was not improvising. He was following a procedure.

*Enough for fewer people than are present.* The phrase assembled itself in her mind with terrible neatness, the way a fault line assembles itself across a map you'd thought was blank.

She opened her eyes. Voss was watching her.

Not obviously. His eyes were half-lidded, his breathing even, his face arranged in the slack innocence of a sleeping man. But Elena had spent six weeks in the field with this man, and she knew the difference between his sleep and his performance of it, and this was the performance. He was watching her count. He had perhaps been watching her count for some time.

She did not flinch. She picked up her notebook, wrote three lines of geological nonsense about the limestone bedding planes, and closed it. Then she yawned, elaborately, and lay down on her side facing the wall, and let her breathing slow into the rhythm of a woman who had counted nothing at all.

* * *

She found Beatrix an hour later, or he found her, at the back of the chamber where a seep of moisture darkened the stone in a thin, indifferent trickle — a cup of water a day, perhaps, if they scraped for it. He stood with his lantern held close, studying the rock face, and the light carved his profile out of the dark: the tired eyes, the jaw set at the angle she was beginning to know too well, the angle it took when he was carrying something he wouldn't put down.

"Seepage," she said, keeping her voice low. "Fracture-fed. It means the rock above us isn't sealed. Which means there may be more ways through this mountain than the one Voss closed."

He glanced at her. "You've been busy."

"I've been counting." She let that word hang between them, and watched him go very still. "Water. Rations. People."

The lantern flame bent in a draft from somewhere deep in the stone. Beatrix looked at her, and she watched him arrive at the arithmetic himself — she saw it happen, saw the numbers land behind his eyes one by one, and saw the moment he understood what she was telling him without telling him, because the walls of this cave had ears and one of them was pretending to sleep thirty feet away.

"Say it in numbers," he said quietly.

"Four bags. Sixteen gallons. Seven throats. Two days, if we're careful." She paused, and gave him the last figure the way you hand someone a live coal. "Rations for five."

The silence that followed was the loudest she had ever heard. Somewhere in it, Beatrix Galloway stopped being a surveyor and became something else — she could see the change happen, a settling, like a structure taking load.

"Five," he repeated.

"Five." She held his gaze. "I packed for eight. I wrote the manifest myself. The stores were in his tent the night before we came in."

"You're certain."

"I'm a scientist, Beatrix. Certainty is the only thing I own." And then, because her hands were shaking and she hated that they were shaking, she did the only thing she could think of — she reached past him to the seep, cupped her palm under the trickle, and let the cold water run over her skin. "There's a cup a day here. Maybe two if we channel it. It's not enough to save seven people. It's exactly enough to save four or five, if the right people drink."

He understood. She watched him understand — that she wasn't only reporting the arithmetic, she was proposing an answer to it, and the answer was that some of them would have to be gone before the water ran out, and the ones who left would need every gallon the others could smuggle to them, drop by drop, canteen by canteen, under the eyes of a man who had planned for exactly this.

"Tam can't climb," Beatrix said. "His ankle. Reeve's the only other one who knows the chamber layout for the photographs. Halima won't leave while the inscriptions are unread." He was thinking aloud, building the split the way she'd seen him build a survey, line by line. "That leaves—"

"It leaves us deciding who drinks," Elena said, "and who is decided against. And I won't do his arithmetic for him. That's the whole point of what he's done. He wants the cave to do the killing so his hands stay clean. The water runs out, the strong take from the weak, and when the relief party he's already telegraphed arrives, they find a tragedy. An accident. *Lost to weather.*" Her voice cracked on the last words, and she hated that too, and kept going. "The same entry they wrote forty years ago. He's not trapping us, Beatrix. He's *filing* us."

Beatrix turned from the seep and looked at her fully, and in the lantern light his face held something she hadn't seen in it before — not the wary courtesy of the railhead, not the guarded warmth of the graded rail bed where they'd stood too close together over a map that shouldn't exist. This was plainer and more frightening. This was a man deciding.

"Elena." He said her name like a man setting down something heavy. "If I get us out of here—"

"Don't." It came out harder than she meant. She softened it by doing the thing she'd been carrying in her hands all night without knowing it: she took her own canteen — her personal one, the one Voss hadn't consolidated because it had hung at her hip — and she pressed it into Beatrix's hands, and then she took one of the canvas bags' worth of trust and did the real thing, the thing words would have ruined. She shifted her bedroll closer to his. She sat down against the stone at his shoulder, where a watching man would see nothing but two exhausted people conserving warmth, and she said, at a volume calibrated to reach exactly one person:

"Drink when I drink. Pour when I pour. We'll be even, and no one will know what even is."

His hand found hers in the dark. He didn't squeeze it, didn't make a declaration of it. He simply held it the way you hold a bearing you've taken and intend to follow — and that, she thought, was the most honest love letter she had ever received: *I will match you gallon for gallon, and I will not let your arithmetic be the death of you.*

Above them, or below them, or wherever the mountain kept its dead, the seep went on dripping its small indifferent count. Elena closed her eyes and did not sleep. She listened.

* * *

She was still listening, two hours later, when she heard the sound that turned her blood to ice water: the soft scrape of canvas, the unmistakable gurgle of a water bag being opened in the dark.

She opened her eyes a slit. By the dying lantern light she saw Voss sitting up, unhurried, and she watched him tip one of the four bags and pour — not a ration, not a cup, but a long, greedy draft — before resealing it and settling the bags back into their row, adjusting one of them, she noted, by a hand's width, so that its slumped shape matched its neighbors.

Then he lay back down, arranged his sleeping face, and was, to all appearances, asleep again.

But Elena Marsh had counted. And she knew now, with the certainty that was the only thing she owned, that the man was already spending their water — that the two days were already one and a half, and shrinking — and that whatever Beatrix was going to do about the air shaft he'd been staring at, he was going to have to do it tomorrow.

She reached over in the dark and touched Beatrix's wrist. Once. Twice. The old surveyor's signal for *bearing confirmed* — and the bearing, they both knew, was *out*.
