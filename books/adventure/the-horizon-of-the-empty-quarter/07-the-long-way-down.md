## 7. The Long Way Down

The dark in the chamber had a weight to it, like wet wool laid over the mouth and eyes. Beatrix sat with his back against a crate of payroll ledgers — paper older than his career, soft as cloth where the damp had found it — and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since Voss's lantern had "slipped" and brought the entrance down behind them.

Six of them left. Halima, who sang low to herself when she thought no one listened, the old ledger-songs now, not the road songs. Elena, whose canteen had been lighter every time she handed it to him and heavier every time he handed it back. Okafor the journalist, whose camera plates were wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like an infant. Two of Halima's cousins, Musa and Tariq, who had come up from the coast with rope and patience. And Voss, sitting apart in the far dark, cleaning a pistol he never fired, humming nothing at all.

Beatrix turned his compass by feel. He didn't need it — that was the thing about bearings you'd memorized under threat of dismissal; they lived in your hands now, in the angle of your wrist. When the company erased those spurs from the maps, they'd forgotten they'd first been written into him.

North-northeast, eleven degrees off the tunnel axis. On his doctored survey there was a mark he'd never been able to explain: a ventilation shaft, drawn in someone else's hand, dated three years before the tunnel collapse. A hole cut down through forty meters of limestone so the men below could breathe while they dug the corridor that would become their grave.

He hadn't believed in it until tonight. Now belief was the only supply that mattered.

* * *

He found Elena first, because he found himself drifting toward her the way water finds its level.

"Air shaft," he said quietly, close to her ear so only she would hear. "Northeast bearing, maybe two hundred meters along the old grade. If it's open even a crack, we can send climbers up for help."

She looked at him in the lantern light, and for a moment neither of them was an engineer or a geologist or whatever careful shapes they wore for each other. She was just a woman counting days in her head; he could see the arithmetic behind her eyes, the same sums he was doing. Water for four days if they drank like prisoners. Six days if they drank like the dead already had.

"Who climbs?" she asked.

"Your cousins are strongest," he said. "Musa especially. And—" He hesitated, and hated himself for the hesitation.

"And?" Elena said, because she heard everything.

"Voss will insist on going. He'll frame it as protecting the company's interests on the surface." Beatrix kept his voice flat. "And he'll want company."

"Then whoever goes with him—"

"—doesn't come back. Yes. I know."

She put her hand briefly over his, there in the dark among the counted bones, and it was the most honest thing either of them had done all week. Then she drew it away, because trust, too, was rationed.

"We split," she said. "Some climb. Some hold the chamber. The photographs stay with the ones who stay — if the climbers don't make it, the evidence still does."

Beatrix nodded. That was the plan he'd already built and hadn't dared say aloud, because saying it made it real, and real things in this cave had a way of collapsing.

* * *

He called them together around the one good lantern, its flame standing perfectly straight in air that never moved.

"Two hundred meters northeast there's a ventilation shaft cut during construction. It may be blocked. It may be open. Either way it's our only way up." He unrolled the strip of tracing paper he'd copied from his map — the original stayed hidden in his boot heel, where it had lived since the tide went out. "I need two climbers. Strong, calm, able to sit in the dark without panicking. The rest hold this chamber. Nobody wanders. Nobody touches anything else."

Voss rose from the shadows like a man who'd been waiting for his cue, which, Beatrix thought, he probably had.

"I'll lead the climb," Voss said. His voice had the pleasant grain of polished wood. "Surface protocol is company business. There will be telegraph lines to manage, questions to answer. Better I go."

"Better you go," Beatrix agreed, and watched the small satisfaction settle over Voss's face like dust. Let him think he'd won the argument. Winning arguments was how Voss kept score. Beatrix had stopped keeping score and started keeping people alive, and the second job required letting the first man believe what he liked.

"Okafor," Voss added smoothly, turning to the journalist, "you'll want to file from the surface anyway. Come with me. We'll get your plates to a wire office before anyone else can muddy the story."

It was elegant. It was almost kind. Okafor looked at Beatrix — a long look, the look of a man who had photographed bones arranged in rows and knew exactly what kind of story killed the people who told it.

Beatrix should have said no. He should have said: the plates stay here, you stay here, we all stay together in the dark where at least the dark is honest. He opened his mouth to say it.

But Okafor had already begun wrapping his oilcloth bundle tighter against his chest, and there was something in his face Beatrix recognized from mirrors: the hunger of a man who had spent his whole career being told what not to print, finally holding the thing he'd been forbidden. You couldn't argue a man out of his own redemption. Beatrix knew that better than anyone in the room.

"Stay behind Voss on the ladder," is what he said instead. "Never above him. And if the shaft narrows, you go last."

Okafor smiled. "Mr. Galloway, I've interviewed warlords. I know how to stand behind dangerous men."

That was the trouble, Beatrix thought later. Everyone thought they knew. Standing behind a dangerous man only worked if the danger announced itself. Voss's whole talent was that his never did.

Halima touched Okafor's sleeve as the party gathered at the shaft mouth. She sang three notes under her breath — Beatrix caught them, a fragment of the ledger-song, a name ending in -mir — and pressed something into the journalist's hand. A knot of red thread. Whatever it meant, Okafor closed his fist around it like a man accepting communion.

They went up. The lantern light shrank to a coin, then a spark, then nothing. For a long time they could hear the scrape of boots on stone, growing fainter, growing higher, and then they heard nothing at all except the mountain breathing its slow mineral breath.

Elena stood beside Beatrix in the blackness. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the silence wasn't already saying better: *we just fed two men to the surface, and only one of them was sent.*

* * *

The hours after the departure were the longest of Beatrix's life, and he had once spent nine days walking a flooded rail bed with a broken wrist.

They held the chamber. That was the phrase he used in his own head, military and clean, because the alternative phrasing — *they waited to find out who was dead* — was unbearable. They inventoried the ledgers by lantern light, Elena reading telegram dates aloud in her clear geologist's voice, Halima copying names into a notebook with the terrible diligence of a woman writing an obituary for two hundred men at once. Musa slept. Tariq paced. Voss was gone, and the absence of him changed the air the way a lifted weight changes a scale.

Toward what might have been dawn, Beatrix climbed to the collapsed entrance and put his ear to the rubble. Stone doesn't carry voices well, but it carries picks. He listened until his knees ached.

Nothing. No rescue party. No sound of digging from above, which meant either the climbers hadn't reached help yet — or help had been reached, and told to wait.

Or meant, said a colder voice, that the climbers had reached the top and found the telegraph office already occupied by men who took their orders from the same place Voss took his.

He came back down and found Elena awake, waiting, her canteen already unscrewed and held out to him. Full. He stared at it, then at her, doing the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do: she had not been drinking. Days of it. Every handoff, every "you take it, I had some earlier," every lightness he'd attributed to carelessness had been engineering. She'd been running a private rationing scheme with a single beneficiary, and the beneficiary was him.

"You've been—"

"Drink," she said.

"Elena."

"Drink, and then tell me the plan for if nobody comes." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst part. She'd made her decision somewhere in the dark and filed it alongside the telegrams, another datum, another date.

He drank. It tasted like iron and gratitude. Then he sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and told her the truth he'd been carrying since the entrance fell.

"Nobody's coming the way we asked them to come," he said. "If Okafor reached the surface, Voss has already managed the story — roadblocks, telegraph, whatever he needs. Which means the only way out is the way I know and nobody wants me to use. The closed lines."

"The spurs on your erased map."

"There's a maintenance siding eleven miles north that isn't on any current schedule. Graded, ballasted, forgotten. If we can reach it, there are handcars. And if there are handcars, there's a junction, and if there's a junction—" He stopped. "There's a whole railroad out there built to move things secretly, Elena. Tonight it moves us."

She was quiet a moment. "And the photographs? The ledgers?"

"They come with us. All of them. We don't leave a page in this mountain." He realized, saying it, that he'd stopped thinking of himself weeks ago — somewhere between the tide and the tunnel collapse, between the map and the man with the smile that never landed. Somewhere in there, guiding an expedition had become something else.

"What are you smiling at?" Elena asked.

"Nothing. Everything." He looked at the lantern, at Halima's pen scratching names into witness, at the bones in their patient rows. "I used to think my job was drawing lines on paper. Turns out it's getting people across them alive."

* * *

Near what should have been midday, by Beatrix's pocket watch, they heard footsteps in the upper gallery.

One set. Light. Unhurried.

Everyone froze. Beatrix took the lantern and went to meet it, heart hammering a rhythm he was ashamed of — hope first, dread right behind it, the two of them racing like rival trains on a single track.

Voss came around the bend alone.

He looked exactly as composed as when he'd left, which was itself an answer. His sleeves were clean. His boots were clean. There was no dust on him that forty meters of climbing wouldn't also explain, but there was no rope burn on his palms either, and Beatrix had spent enough years around ropes to read hands the way other men read faces.

"Where's Okafor?" Beatrix asked.

"Topside." Voss smiled his upholstered smile. "Getting his story sorted. He sends his regards. Says the plates are in safe hands."

Something went cold and permanent in Beatrix's chest. Safe hands. Voss's hands. The photographs — two hundred names, a tunnel ordered collapsed by telegram, payrolls that proved the company had counted its workers and then counted them out of existence — all of it, forty meters up, in the custody of the one man alive with every reason to burn it.

"He'll rejoin us," Voss continued pleasantly, "as soon as arrangements are complete. In the meantime—" he gestured graciously at the chamber, at the ledgers, at the trapped and dwindling survivors, "—I suggest we all remain patient. Help is coming. Eventually. These things take time."

He walked past them to his corner of the dark and sat down and began, softly, to clean his pistol again.

Beatrix stood very still, listening to the little mechanical sounds of metal on metal, and understood with perfect clarity what he had known only in fragments before:

He wasn't guiding an expedition anymore. He hadn't been since the entrance fell. He was running a rescue inside a murder, under the nose of the murderer, with a compass in his boot and a killer's patience ticking down around him — and the next move wasn't waiting at the bottom of an air shaft.

It was eleven miles north, on a line that officially did not exist, and every hour he stood in this dark, Voss's arrangements grew more complete.

He caught Elena's eye across the chamber. She had heard everything. She gave him the smallest nod, the kind you give across a crowded room when the signal has to survive being witnessed.

Tonight, Beatrix thought. While the pistol is still being cleaned instead of used.

Tonight they would show Voss what a man could do with a map the company thought it had erased — and what a railroad built on secrets would carry, if you knew which keys turned which locks.
