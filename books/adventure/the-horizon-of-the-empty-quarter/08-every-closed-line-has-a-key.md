## 8. Every Closed Line Has a Key

The surface came up to meet them like a held breath finally released. Beatrix climbed out of the air shaft first, into a night so wide and star-scattered it seemed the desert had been keeping count of everything below and had decided, tonight, to look away. Behind him came Halima, then the two climbers from Voss's party who had chosen the wrong leader and knew it now — Karim with his split lip and his hands still shaking, and the boy, Tomas, who hadn't spoken since the chamber.

Elena came last, pale as paper in the moonlight, her satchel strapped tight across her chest. Inside it: the photographs. The payrolls. The telegrams with their neat, murderous phrasing — *proceed with collapse, weather will be blamed* — all of it folded in oilcloth like something living that might cry out if jostled.

Beatrix looked east, toward the coast road, and saw what he'd expected to see and dreaded anyway: a lantern swinging at the mouth of the wadi where the telegraph line ran down to the junction station. A rider, or a watcher. Voss had been down in the dark with them for six hours, but Voss's orders had been sent up before he ever went under. Somewhere ahead, every key, every crossing, every water tower between here and the border was being wired shut.

"He'll have telegraphed Al-Miraj by now," Elena said. She said it quietly, the way you name a diagnosis. "And Qasr Junction. And anyone else who answers to the company."

"Then we don't go to any of those places." Beatrix turned his back on the lantern light and faced the other way — west, into the empty quarter proper, where the land went flat and silver and utterly unlit. "We go where the company stopped looking twenty years ago."

Halima crouched beside him, drawing in the sand with one finger. "The closed lines."

"The closed lines." He knelt and took the stick from her. What he drew next he had never drawn for another living soul: the skeleton of the railway that existed only in his head and in the map burning a hole in his breast pocket. Graded beds never ballasted. Tunnels bored and then sealed with timber and shame. Spurs that appeared on no schedule, answered to no dispatcher, and — this was the part that mattered tonight — connected to the live network at exactly three points, because even the men who buried a massacre had needed a way to bring in the materials to bury it with.

"Here," he said, tapping a junction drawn as a small cross. "The old quarry spur meets the coastal main at Kilometer 212. There's a switch there the company thinks is rusted solid. It isn't. I oiled it myself, four years ago, when they had me surveying what they told me was nothing."

"You surveyed your own erasure," Halima said. It wasn't a question.

"I surveyed the shape of the hole they left in the map." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and let it stand. "They paid me to confirm that nothing was there. I confirmed instead that nothing was *written* there. There's a difference, and it runs two hundred kilometers through the desert."

Tomas spoke for the first time since the chamber, his voice cracked and young. "There's a supply train. I saw the manifest before we came up. Water cars, coal, provisions for the road camps — it runs the coastal main tomorrow before dawn, from Al-Miraj south."

Beatrix looked at him. "You know its timing?"

"I loaded crates onto it once," the boy said. "When I still thought I worked for a railroad and not a grave-digging crew." He swallowed. "It stops at Kilometer 212 to take water. Four minutes. Maybe five."

Five minutes. Beatrix felt the old engineer's arithmetic wake in him, cold and precise, the one part of himself the disgrace had never managed to touch. Five minutes to throw a switch, board a train, and bend it off the map of the world.

* * *

They moved through the hours before dawn the way water moves — around obstacles, along the low places, never rising high enough to be seen. Halima went ahead alone, wrapped in grey, carrying nothing but her voice.

Her work was misdirection, and she performed it like a woman singing to children. At the wadi watch-post she left word, passed in the accent of the coast tribes, that the survivors of the cave party had been seen heading north toward Al-Miraj itself — walking straight into the company's arms, poor fools, wanting only to surrender and be fed. Beatrix, watching from a dune's shoulder through borrowed binoculars, saw the lantern at the telegraph post begin to swing with purpose. Saw the rider mount and drive hard north.

"She has just bought us the whole night," Elena murmured beside him. "One lie, and every soldier they have is marching the wrong direction."

"One song," Halima corrected, rejoining them an hour later, breathless and bright-eyed. "The desert believes songs faster than it believes telegrams. Telegrams come from men. Songs come from the land." She glanced at Beatrix, and her expression gentled into something almost sorrowful. "Your company forgot that. That is why it buries things. It cannot make the land keep its secrets willingly, so it makes the land keep them dead."

They reached the quarry spur an hour before the train was due. The rails rose out of the sand like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and been too proud to finish. Beatrix walked the bed with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, reading the track the way other men read scripture — every joint, every tie, every place where twenty years of wind had driven drift against the rail web. The grade was sound. The steel was sound. Steel kept better than reputations did.

At Kilometer 212 the main line ran gleaming and alive under the stars, and the old switch stood where he'd left it, half-buried, its throw-bar furred with corrosion — except where his oil, four years old and renewed by no one, had kept a clean seam of bare metal like a scar that refused to heal.

He put his weight on the bar. It groaned, fought him, remembered itself, and threw over with a crack that sounded obscenely loud in all that silence.

"Every closed line has a key," he said softly. "They just never imagined anyone would want one."

Elena was already working. She had spread the stolen waybill forms on a crate lid, weighted with stones, and by lantern light she was building a document with the patient care of a woman who had spent years forging legitimacy in smaller ways — initials in the right corners, freight classifications in the right boxes, the particular smudge of authenticity that comes from handling paper the way clerks handle it, carelessly, hundreds of times a day.

"Coal and water for the road camps," she recited as she wrote. "Rerouted via the quarry branch due to washout on the coastal section south of the wadi. Authorized by—" She paused, pen hovering.

"Not Voss," Beatrix said. "Never give him a document with his name on it. He'll deny it by breakfast."

"Then someone senior enough that denial looks like cowardice." She wrote a name — a director whose signature she had studied for three years across a thousand shipping manifests — and signed it in a hand so close to the original that Beatrix felt a chill of admiration and unease in equal measure. "Every rule broken tonight," she said, not looking up, "is a rule that was written to protect the guilty. The waybill rules. The dispatch rules. The rule that says a surveyor confirms what he is paid to confirm." Now she looked up, and the lantern light made her eyes very steady. "I'm not sorry. I want that said aloud, once, so it's on the record somewhere."

"It's on my record," Beatrix said. "It's the first thing on my record I'm proud of."

Something passed between them then that neither of them named — there was no time to name things, and naming could wait until there was a country safe enough to name them in — but it settled over the work like a blessing, and after that their hands moved faster.

* * *

The train came at 4:40, exactly when Tomas swore it would, its headlamp carving a white corridor out of the dark. Beatrix stood at the switch with his hand on the bar and counted the cars as the engine thundered past — tender, three water cars, four boxcars, caboose — and on the fourth car he threw the switch.

The points sang. The whole train leaned into the curve with a shriek of flanged steel that must have woken every jackal in forty miles, and then it was rolling away down the dead spur, into the quarter where no dispatcher would ever think to look for it, hauling its cargo of water and coal and forged legitimacy toward the border and the people waiting there.

The crew — five men, subdued without a shot fired, thanks to the element of surprise and Karim's considerable size — sat on the ballast under guard, bewildered rather than afraid. Their fireman, an old man with a stoker's forearms, squinted at the waybill Elena handed him and grunted.

"Washout on the coastal, eh." He tapped the paper. "This authorizer's been dead to me since '09. But paper's paper." He looked up at Beatrix, and his eyes were shrewd and tired and not fooled at all. "You're the one they struck off the rolls. Galloway. The map man."

Beatrix said nothing.

"My brother was a navvy on the tunnel contract," the old fireman said. "Listed lost to weather, spring of the bad year. My mother waited eleven years for a body." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like the switch had cracked, and picked up his shovel. "I can fire a locomotive on this ghost track if the water's good. Can't say I ever liked the company's timetable anyway."

By full dark of the following night — no; by the grey hour before dawn of the second day, time having gone strange and syrupy — they were sixty kilometers down the closed line, the engine breathing easy, the desert opening westward like a book with its pages finally unpressed. Halima rode the cowcatcher platform and sang low, some old traveling song, and the rails carried it back along the train like a pulse.

Elena found Beatrix in the cab, hands on the throttle, eyes on the unwritten country ahead.

"There's a telegram office at the border station," she said. "And there's a man on this train who knows Voss's habits better than anyone alive, because Voss trained him." She nodded back toward the boxcars, where Tomas stood watch over the evidence and over Karim, who slept. "Tomas says Voss won't wire the border directly. He'll go there himself. He always cleans up in person — it's the one thing he doesn't trust to paper."

Beatrix watched the dead rails run out ahead of him, straight as a confession, toward a junction where a single line branched north — the last live connection, the last door between them and the world.

"Then he'll be standing on that platform," Beatrix said, "when we arrive."

The engine drove on into the dark, and far ahead, at the edge of sight, a signal lamp burned red at the last junction — though no dispatcher, on any map, living or erased, was supposed to know they were coming.
