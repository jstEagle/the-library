## 9. The Man Who Was Sent Ahead

Elias Voss had always believed that a man's character revealed itself in the last hour of the night, when the lamps burned low and the arithmetic of the soul came due. He had sat through many such hours in the company's service, watching men sign things they did not wish to sign, and he had learned to read the small betrayals of the body: the swallow before a lie, the hand that drifted toward a door that was no longer there.

Tonight he was the one being read.

The junction at Wadi Sarif was nothing — a water tower gone to rust, a switch stand frozen at an angle no wheel would ever forgive, the moon laying silver rails across gravel that had not felt a train in eleven years. Beatrix Galloway stood at the switch with a lantern in one hand and a bar of iron in the other, prying at the points as though the desert itself might be persuaded to change its mind.

Voss stepped out of the shadow of the water tower and let his boot heel ring once on the rail. The sound carried farther than it should have. In the Empty Quarter, sound traveled like gossip.

"Don't," Voss said.

Beatrix did not turn around. That was answer enough about what kind of man he had become in the last six days. A frightened man turns. A guilty man runs. Only a man who has already decided stands with his back to you and keeps working.

"The points are seized," Beatrix said. "Eleven years of sand. I need another hour."

"You don't have an hour." Voss brought the pistol up, not leveled, not yet — held low against his thigh, the way one holds a pen before signing. "And you don't need the points. I've already telegraphed ahead. Every station from here to the coast has your description and Elena Marsh's. There is no train coming for you, Galloway. There is only me."

Now Beatrix turned. The lantern light came up under his jaw and made him look older than his years, carved rather than aged. His eyes went to the pistol, catalogued it, and dismissed it — which was either courage or exhaustion, and Voss suspected they had become indistinguishable out here.

"Is that how you'll write it?" Beatrix asked. "Another entry in the ledger. *Lost to weather.*"

"Lost to bandits," Voss corrected mildly. "Bandits are very active this season. I have it on good authority."

* * *

He had rehearsed this conversation on the ride down from the escarpment, and like all rehearsals it fell apart on contact with the man himself. Voss had planned to open with sympathy. He opened instead with honesty, because honesty was the rarer currency and Beatrix struck him as a man who had stopped accepting paper.

"I was sent ahead," Voss said. "That's all I am. You understand that, don't you? Eleven years ago I wasn't even in this country. I signed papers in an office with carpet. The men who ordered the tunnel collapsed never saw the mountain. They saw figures. Two hundred and forty laborers were a rounding error between them and a dividend." He paused. "I'm the rounding error's errand boy. So are you, if you're honest."

"And Elena?"

"Elena is the reason we're having this conversation instead of none." Voss let the pistol rise now, slowly, until it pointed at the space between them rather than at either of them — a courtesy, a grammar. "Here is my offer, and I will make it once. Burn the photographs. Burn the ledgers' copies. Walk away from the story. In exchange: Elena's name comes off every list I control, and I control more lists than you'd believe. She keeps her position. Her pension. Her life. And you—" he smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful, "—you retire rich. There's a consulting firm in Alexandria that exists entirely to employ men who know where bodies are buried. You'd be very good at it. You already know the geography."

The wind moved through the wadi, lifting grit off the old rail bed and letting it fall again, patient as sediment.

"You're offering me money to forget two hundred and forty names," Beatrix said.

"I'm offering you a woman's life to forget two hundred and forty names. Let's be precise about what's actually for sale." Voss took a step closer, gravel crunching. "Because that's the truth of it, Galloway, and you know it better than anyone. Halima can't read a telegraph line. The climbers we pulled out of that shaft will swear whatever keeps them fed. The journalist is dead because he couldn't do simple sums. It comes down to you and her. And she works for them. She has always worked for them. Do you imagine the company forgives? Do you imagine there's a version of next year where Elena Marsh attends a hearing and testifies and goes home to her flat? There isn't. There's only the version where she's safe because you were sensible, and the version where she's a widow of a man who was never her husband, lost to weather."

He watched the words land. He had built a career on watching words land. And he saw — or believed he saw — the exact moment the offer found purchase: a flicker behind Beatrix's eyes, the pupil contracting around something private.

"There it is," Voss said softly. "You love her. Good. Love makes men reasonable."

"No," Beatrix said. "Love makes men honest. It's fear that makes them reasonable."

* * *

What Voss did not account for — what he could not have accounted for, because his whole education had taught him that people were ledgers and ledgers balanced — was the sound.

It came from the dark beyond the water tower: the soft double knock of a boot on steel, deliberate, almost polite. Then another, from the opposite side of the junction. Then a third, from somewhere near the ruined signal post, high up, where no ground-level man should be.

Halima stepped into the moonlight first, and she was singing.

Not loudly. Not the war-chant the legends promised. Something older and quieter, a walking song, the kind sung by women following a funeral bier across hard country — and Voss understood, with a cold that started in his teeth, that she had been singing it since the chamber, since the bones, since the ledger of names, and that she had been singing it *at him* for days while he noted who read what aloud.

Behind her came the climbers. Four of them, gaunt, dust-white, alive — men Voss had written off as ballast, as witnesses to be managed, as figures. They fanned out along the rails with the unhurried competence of men who had spent six days learning that patience was the only weapon the desert issues freely.

"Galloway," Voss said, and the pistol finally came level. "You set this up."

"I set up a train," Beatrix said. "They set up everything else. You spent the whole expedition counting who heard what. You never counted who was listening."

Voss's finger tightened — and stopped, because Halima had stopped singing, and in the silence her voice alone remained, speaking the language of the inscriptions, translating as she had in the cave:

"*The mountain keeps its count.*"

Then, in English, so there could be no mistake: "Mr. Voss. Say it again. All of it. What you offered him. What you did to the journalist. What you were sent to do."

"You're recording nothing," Voss said. "There's no machine out here."

"There's me," said one of the climbers — the youngest, the one with the broken wrist set in a splint of rail-splint and cloth. "I have a memory like my mother's. She remembered forty years of debts. Say it. We'll carry it out of here word for word, and words travel faster than trains."

Voss looked at the pistol in his own hand and understood its true weight for the first time: not the power to end a life but the certainty that ending one would accomplish nothing now. Kill Galloway and the song continued. Kill the climber and the song continued. He had been sent ahead to stop a story, and stories, he realized, were the one cargo that could not be derailed once the couplings were loosed.

He lowered the gun. Not all the way. Enough.

"The company will send someone after me," he said. "Someone worse than me. Someone who doesn't make offers."

"Let them come," Halima said. "The quarter is large, and it owes us nothing, and it remembers everyone who walks it. You taught us that, Elias Voss. You and your employers wrote it on the mountain in blood, and the mountain kept the receipt." She extended her hand, palm open, toward him. "The pistol."

For a long moment the junction held its breath — the rusted switch, the silvered rails running off into darkness toward a coast that had forgotten them, the moon making its indifferent audit of them all.

Voss thought of carpeted offices. Of figures. Of the rounding error. Of the look on Beatrix Galloway's face, which was not triumph, which was worse than triumph — it was pity, offered without cruelty, the way one offers water.

He put the pistol in her hand.

"It was me," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he had ever heard it, a man reading his own name from a ledger he had thought was closed. "I was sent ahead. I collapsed the entrance. I counted the rations for fewer men than were present. Write it down. All of it. I'll say it as many times as you need."

The young climber repeated each sentence back to him, word for word, and nodded at each confirmation like a clerk balancing a column.

Beatrix picked up the lantern and turned back to the switch, and began, at last, to work the points free.

"We have a train to catch," he said. "And a border to reach before dawn."

Above the wadi, far to the south, the horizon of the Empty Quarter lay black and patient — and somewhere beyond it, Voss knew, the company was already waking men whose names were not yet in any ledger at all.
