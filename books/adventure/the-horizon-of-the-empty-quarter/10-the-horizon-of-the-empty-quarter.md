## 10. The Horizon of the Empty Quarter

The border station at dawn was a single lamp burning against a wall of grey light, and Beatrix Galloway stood beneath it like a man waiting to be judged by something that had no face.

The town behind him had no name worth remembering — a scatter of whitewashed buildings at the seam between two territories, a water tower, a telegraph office with its shutters still closed against the cool. Ahead of him the rails ran out into the Empty Quarter the way veins run out of a hand, thinning, silvering, until the desert took them for its own. He had spent eleven days looking at that horizon from the wrong side of fear. Now he looked at it from the other side, and it had not changed at all. That was the thing about deserts. They did not care what you carried out of them.

The mail sack hung from his shoulder by a strap worn soft with use. Inside it: the ledgers, wrapped in oilcloth. The photographs, wrapped in the journalist's spare shirt, because it seemed right that something of Aldous Wren should carry what he had died trying to take. And beneath both, folded into quarters, the map.

His map. The one the company had paid to erase, line by line, spur by spur — the one he had copied in secret the night before they burned the original, because a man who builds a road cannot bear to watch it unmade. For six years that paper had been the only honest thing about him. It had gotten him hired onto this expedition. It had gotten four people out of a mountain that was built to keep them.

He weighed it now through the canvas, the way you weigh anything you are about to lose.

* * *

The postmaster arrived with the sun, an old man in a coat too heavy for the climate, keys jangling on a ring at his belt. He unlocked the little iron cage of a window without a word, without curiosity, which was the finest gift anyone had given Beatrix in weeks.

"Where's it going?" the old man asked, when Beatrix laid the parcel on the counter.

"A newspaper." He gave the name. A city three borders away, a paper whose editor had once gone to prison rather than print a railway company's version of a bridge collapse. "Registered. I'll pay for a receipt."

"They'll want a name for the sender."

Beatrix looked at the form. There was a line for it, a neat ruled space, and for a moment he saw all the names that could fill it — his own, Elena's, Halima's, the dead men in the ledger whose names had been written down once already by people who meant to lose them. *Lost to weather.* Ninety-one times. Ninety-one men entered into a payroll and then subtracted from the world, and the arithmetic signed off in a founder's hand.

He wrote his name. Not a false one. If they came for him, let them come to a name that had finally told the truth somewhere.

The old man stamped the receipt, tore off the carbon, slid it back under the bars. "Anything else?"

"No," Beatrix said. Then, because the lie would have been worse than the weight: "Yes."

He took out the map.

* * *

There was a brazier by the station platform where the night watchman had kept coals alive for the early trains, and it still held a red heart of heat. Beatrix crouched beside it with the map across his knees, and for a long moment he simply read it — the way you reread a letter from someone you loved who has since done you harm.

Every line on it was true. That was the obscenity of it. The graded bed that ran toward the cave and stopped. The spurs marked CLOSED in his own hand, closed not because they failed but because they succeeded too well at reaching a place the company needed unreachable. The tunnel bearings. The air shaft he had found by dead reckoning while Voss slept with a pistol under his coat. Six years of his life drawn in ink, accurate to the chain, and every inch of it a corridor to a massacre.

If he mailed it, some other engineer would read it someday. Some surveyor with restless hands and a hunger for lines that went nowhere. And the company — the company endured; he had watched it endure Voss's arrest, watched it begin rewriting the story even before the ledgers reached ink — the company would send someone down those lines again. Not to find the truth. To bury it deeper. Maps were how they had done it the first time. Maps were how they would do it again.

Elena had said it in the dark of the last camp, her voice low so the sleeping guides wouldn't hear: *You can't give them the road, Beatrix. The road is the weapon.*

He fed the corner of the map to the coals.

It caught slowly, the way old paper does, reluctant, the ink flaring green before the brown took it. He held on as long as he could stand to, watching the graded bed curl and blacken, watching the air shaft go, the junctions, the water stops, the whole secret geography of his six lost years lifting off the page in smoke that the dawn wind pulled apart and scattered toward the quarter. When the fire reached his own handwriting — *B. Galloway, Surveyor, 18—* — he let the last piece drop and pressed it flat with a stone until there was nothing left but a skin of ash shaped like nothing at all.

He had expected to feel lighter. What he felt instead was amputated, and then, slowly, underneath the loss, clean. Like a bone setting.

No one else would be sent down those lines. Not by way of him.

* * *

Halima found him there, kneeling at the brazier like a man at prayer who had forgotten the words.

She stood over him with her burnous already dusted for travel, and she did not ask what he had burned. She had known since the chamber — since she read the ledger aloud in a voice that shook and did not break, ninety-one names given back their weight in a language the mountain had been holding for thirty years. She knew what maps cost and what they were worth, and she knew that a man burning one was not destroying anything. He was paying a debt.

"The first train is on the board," she said. "Eastbound, an hour past sunrise. Hers is the second." She paused. "You're taking different trains."

"It's safer."

"I know why it's safer." Her eyes were dry and very old and very kind. "I'm telling you it's also a choice. Don't confuse the two, engineer. Safety is what the mountain gives no one. Choosing — that's the part that belongs to you."

She left him with that, the way she left everyone with things, and went to see the others onto the platform. Behind her, the horizon was turning the color of hammered copper, and the Empty Quarter lay under it vast and indifferent and, for the first time in Beatrix's life, quiet in a way that sounded less like silence and more like rest.

* * *

They said goodbye at the water column, where the steam rose and fell in slow white breaths.

Elena Marsh had her satchel over one shoulder and her specimen case in her hand — rock cores, mineral samples, the innocent-looking geology of a woman who had spent two weeks cataloguing evidence disguised as strata. Half of everything true. That had been the plan, argued out in whispers across three nights: split the archive between them, split the routes, split the risk. If one train was searched, one set of copies survived. If one of them was taken —

Neither of them had finished that sentence. Neither of them had needed to.

"You'll wire me from the junction," she said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite steady either.

"Coded. Halima's cipher. Weather reports." He almost smiled. "You'll be a great deal of rain."

"And you'll be fog." She looked at his hands, empty now, the map's ghost still faintly on them in ash and singe. "You burned it."

"This morning."

"Does it hurt?"

"Like pulling a tooth." He met her eyes. "And then it doesn't."

A whistle sounded down the line, far off, the eastbound making up its courage. Porters began to move along the platform. Somewhere behind them Halima was saying farewell to the guide-brothers in the old tongue, words that sounded like songs cut short.

Elena stepped close — closer than colleagues, closer than conspirators — and put her hand flat against his chest, over the pocket where the receipt for the registered mail lay folded. Through the cloth he could feel the pressure of her palm and, beneath it, his own heartbeat doing something unsoldierly.

"When this is printed," she said quietly, "they will come for everyone whose name is in it. Yours is in it. Mine is in it. We chose that on the mountain, both of us, and I don't regret it. But Beatrix—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—I don't want the company to be the only reason we never see each other again. Fear made us careful. Let something else make us brave."

"There's a valley," he said. "North of the coast. Where the lagoon drains twice a year and the cave mouth shows. No rail line goes there anymore. No line ever will." He covered her hand with his. "In a year. After the trial, after the papers stop printing our names. If you come, I'll be there at the spring tide. If you don't—"

"If I don't, it's because I'm dead or in prison," she said, "and either way you'd better be at that lagoon anyway, so someone remembers us both."

The whistle blew again, nearer, insistent. She kissed him once, brief and hard, like sealing a letter, and stepped back onto the second platform with her chin up and her eyes bright and wet, and Beatrix Galloway stood alone between two tracks as the eastbound came groaning in out of the copper light.

He boarded it. She did not. Through the window he watched her grow small on the receding platform, one hand raised, not waving so much as holding still — a fixed point he could steer by — until the curve of the line took her, and then the town, and then everything but the desert itself, unrolling beside the track, immense and patient, keeping its ninety-one secrets now safely given away.

Somewhere ahead, in a city he would never see, a postman would break the seal on an oilcloth bundle, and the world would tilt. Somewhere behind him, in a cell, Elias Voss sat with his recorded confession and his ruined career and the dawning understanding that the institution he served had already begun to forget him. And here, in between, in the rattling gold light of a third-class carriage, Beatrix took out the carbon receipt, read his own name on it — the first document in six years that told the truth and belonged to him — and folded it away next to his heart.

The train ran east. The quarter took back its silence.

And at the edge of the horizon, so faint it might have been heat-shimmer, a signal lamp blinked twice from a junction that appeared on no map at all — and went dark.
