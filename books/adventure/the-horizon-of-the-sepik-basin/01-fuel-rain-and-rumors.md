## 1. Fuel, Rain, and Rumors

The rain came at four o'clock, the way it always did in the wet season, arriving not as weather but as a fact—as certain as gravity, as indifferent as arithmetic. Jude Almeida heard it first on the tin roof of the fuel shed, a sound like gravel poured from a great height, and then it was everywhere at once, drowning the airstrip, the kunai grass, the brown ribbon of the Sepik visible far below through the haze.

Jude stood under the overhang of the hangar with a mug of black tea going cold in one hand and watched the water hammer the strip into submission. The Cessna 206 sat tied down at the far end, nose into the wind, its paint the color of old bone where the sun hadn't peeled it away entirely. VH-something-or-other; the registration letters had faded years ago and Jude had never bothered to repaint them. In this country, markings were a kind of vanity. Everyone who mattered knew the plane by the dent above the left doorframe, the patch of new aluminum on the wingtip where a cassowary had once objected to being nearly landed upon, and the particular cough of the engine when it was cold—two misses, then three, then a grudging smoothness that never quite convinced anyone, least of all Jude, that the machine was well.

Forty-one minutes past four. The rain would run for an hour, maybe ninety minutes, and then the clouds would tear open along some invisible seam and the light would come slanting gold across the basin, and the whole world would smell like steam and green rot and woodsmoke. That was the rhythm. Jude had learned it the way you learn a heartbeat—not by studying it but by living inside it long enough that you stopped hearing it except when it changed.

* * *

The airstrip at Ambunti had been cut into the river terrace sometime before the war, by men whose names were lost even to the mission records, and it had been maintained since by nothing more than stubbornness and the annual burning of the kunai. It was nine hundred meters of red-brown clay and grass, sloping gently toward the river, bounded on the north side by sago swamp and on the south by a ridge of secondary growth that swallowed anything rolled off the edge without complaint or trace. There was no tower, no beacon, no windsock anymore—the last one had rotted off its pole two seasons back and Jude had replaced it with a rag torn from a flour sack, which told the truth about the wind about half the time, which was better than most things in the Sepik.

Jude flew out of it because it was there, and because it was nowhere, and because nowhere suited.

The supply runs were the spine of the week. Mondays, medicine and kerosene upriver to Pagwi and beyond, sometimes as far as Yessan if the weather held. Wednesdays, mail and rice and outboard spares down to Angoram, where the trading stores lined the bank like tired teeth. Fridays were whatever anyone would pay for: coffins, chickens, a schoolteacher's trunk, a government patrol officer with a clipboard and a malaria tremor, once an entire wedding party including a bride who spent the whole flight laughing at the noise. Sundays Jude did not fly. Sundays belonged to the plane—oil, tires, the endless small war against corrosion—and to the long slow afternoon of doing nothing that Jude had come to regard not as laziness but as maintenance, the same as the oil.

Everyone knew Jude. That was the arrangement, and it had taken six years to build. The mission fathers at Kaminabit knew him; the trade-store Chinese at Angoram knew him; the councilors and the luluais and the crocodile hunters and the beer-crew boys who slept under the hangar when it rained—they all knew him, greeted him by name, argued with him about freight rates, trusted him with letters that had no stamps and parcels that had no receipts. And none of them knew him. Not really. They knew a pilot who kept his own counsel, who flew for everyone and took sides with no one, who drank one beer at the end of a hard day and never two, who could be relied upon absolutely and understood not at all.

It was a good life. Jude told himself that most mornings, standing in the doorway of the hut behind the hangar, watching the mist lift off the river. A good life, deliberate and self-made, a life with no entanglements in it, no debts, no history trailing behind like a tailwheel dragging through mud. The basin asked only that you be useful and discreet, and Jude was both, expertly, and if there were nights when the quiet pressed down like deep water—if there were moments, refueling at dusk, when the reflection in the drum's dark gasoline mirror looked like a stranger wearing a familiar face—well. Everyone ran from something. Some people just chose better scenery.

* * *

Father Anselm came up from Kaminabit on the Thursday boat, unannounced, which was unlike him. He arrived at the strip in the blue hour before dawn, while the mist still lay on the runway in ropes, and found Jude already at the Cessna with the cowling open, running fingers along the engine's hoses the way a doctor palpates a doubtful abdomen.

"You're flying early," the old priest said. His voice carried the flattened vowels of forty years in-country, English worn smooth as river stone.

"Medicine run. Fever at Yessan." Jude didn't look up. "You're walking early."

"I couldn't sleep." Father Anselm lowered himself onto an oil drum with the care of a man whose knees had opinions, and folded his hands, and said nothing for a while. That was the thing about Anselm—he wore silence like a vestment, comfortably, and he could make other people wear it too. Jude tightened a hose clamp that didn't need tightening and waited.

"There are men moving up the river," Anselm said finally. "From the coast. From Wewak, I think, though they came by Lae. Well-dressed. Very polite. They carry a company name—Kavera. Kavera Mining Consortium."

Jude closed the cowling. "Prospectors?"

"Prospectors ask questions about rocks." Anselm turned his pale eyes toward the river, where the mist was beginning to burn off. "These men ask questions about people. Who talks. Who remembers. Who is angry. At Ambunti they paid a man five hundred kina to tell them which villages used to fish the tributaries near the highlands. Five hundred kina for what any child could have drawn on a slate. The money wasn't for the information, Jude. The money was for the telling. They're buying the fact that people will talk to them—and marking, I think, the ones who won't."

The engine ticked as it cooled, small metallic sounds in the growing light. Somewhere across the strip a rooster made its argument, and was ignored.

"What do they want up there?" Jude asked. "There's nothing in the highlands but cloud and leeches."

"There is gold," Anselm said mildly. "There has always been gold. Alluvial, they say, in the streams that come down from the range. Enough of it that a company with lawyers and helicopters might find it worth the trouble. And enough of it," he added, and here his voice changed, became careful, "that two years ago somebody else found it worth killing for."

Jude looked at him then. Anselm sat very straight on his drum, hands still folded, face composed into the particular blankness he reserved for things he had decided to say and could not unsay.

"A village," the priest said. "Up near the headwaters. You won't find it on any map now. You won't find it in the government records, or the census rolls, or the mission registers—I've looked, Jude, I've spent three weeks looking, and it has been removed so thoroughly that I began to doubt my own memory. But I baptized children there. Twenty-two of them, over eleven years. I married four couples. I buried old Yawan beneath a pandanus tree, and I remember the ground, and I remember the names, and there is no village there anymore. Burned. Emptied. The survivors scattered downriver and told to keep their mouths shut, and they have kept them shut, because the men who did it are the sort of men who check."

"Who did it?"

Anselm smiled, and it was the saddest expression Jude had seen in a year of sad expressions. "That is the question, isn't it? No one says the name. But the name that pays the silence—that name is Kavera."

The mist was lifting off the strip now, unraveling into rags. Down at the far end, the flour-sack windsock stirred and hung slack again. Jude found the tea mug still in hand, cold and forgotten, and set it down on the wheel chock.

"Why are you telling me this?" Jude asked. "I fly freight. Rice and penicillin and outboard spark plugs. I'm not a policeman."

"No," Anselm agreed. "You're worse than a policeman. You're a neutral. You go everywhere, you talk to everyone, you belong to nobody. If I wanted to know what was happening in every bend of this river, I'd ask you." He unfolded himself from the drum, joints cracking like green firewood, and stood looking at Jude with those pale, patient eyes. "I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm an old man and I've learned what asking costs. I'm only telling you, so that when the day comes—and it will come, these things always come—you'll recognize it. Be careful upriver, Jude. Be careful especially of polite men with money. Politeness is expensive, and companies don't spend what they can't afford."

He walked away across the wet grass toward the boat landing, a small gray figure growing smaller, and the morning swallowed him.

* * *

Jude flew the medicine run anyway. Of course Jude flew the medicine run. A fever at Yessan didn't wait for rumors to resolve themselves, and neither did Jude; that was the whole architecture of the life, usefulness as a form of invisibility. Do your job perfectly, owe nothing, take no side, and the country will let you pass through it untouched, forever.

But all that day, between the strips, the words rode along in the cockpit like an extra passenger.

At Pagwi, loading quinine and a crate of nails, Jude noticed that the storekeeper wouldn't meet his eye, and that a stranger in a clean white shirt sat drinking tea in the corner with the particular stillness of a man listening rather than resting. At Yessan, delivering the fever medicine to the aid post, Jude found the orderly—a chatty man who normally narrated his entire life unprompted—quiet, watchful, glancing twice at the empty sky as though aircraft might overhear. On the return leg, climbing out over the river bends with the afternoon light going bronze on the water, Jude caught himself scanning the banks for white shirts, and realized it, and laughed aloud in the empty cockpit, a short ugly sound swallowed by the engine noise.

One beer at the end of the day. Never two. Jude sat on the hangar step with the bottle sweating in hand and watched the swifts come down over the strip to feed, hundreds of them, stitching the last light into ribbons. Behind the hangar, the beer-crew boys were singing something low and rhythmic around their fire, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless air, and everything was exactly as it had been yesterday and would be tomorrow, and none of it felt solid anymore. That was the trouble with knowledge. It got into the foundations. You could wallpaper over it, but the walls remembered.

Five hundred kina for the telling. Jude thought about that. Thought about how many conversations on this river had been purchased lately without anyone saying so—how many nods and shrugs and helpful directions had been quietly converted into inventory. Neutrality, Jude understood suddenly, sitting in the dark with the bottle going warm, neutrality was itself a commodity. It had a price like anything else. The Consortium hadn't come to buy the river's silence; it had come to buy the river's *voices*, and a neutral man was simply a voice that hadn't been priced yet.

Jude finished the beer and did not get the second one, and went to bed listening to the rain start up again on the tin roof, right on schedule, four o'clock in its own way, and lay awake longer than usual in the dark listening to the water hammer down and thinking, with a strange prescient unease, that the rhythm of things—the heartbeat learned so well it went unheard—was the sound of a life that could change without warning.

* * *

It changed on a Friday, eleven days later, at dusk.

Jude was under the Cessna with a grease gun when the sound came, and for a moment mistook it for the engine of the mission boat rounding the bend. Then the pitch dropped, and stuttered, and rose again in a way no boat ever made, and Jude was out and squinting west into the dying light before the conscious mind caught up with the ears.

An aircraft. Low, too low, coming down the river valley with its nose canted and one wing dipping like a wounded bird, trailing a thin gray feather of smoke. Not a plane Jude knew—not the mission's Cessna 185, not the government Pilatus, none of the handful of machines that worked this country like fish working a reef. This one was unfamiliar, battered, flying with the desperate precision of a pilot spending his last competence all at once.

It wobbled over the treeline. It leveled, more or less. It came down the strip in a flat, screaming slide that Jude would remember for the rest of a life that was about to become considerably more complicated, touched, bounced, slewed, and rolled to a stop sixty meters from where Jude stood, close enough that the propwash flattened the grass at Jude's boots.

For a moment, nothing. The evening insects resumed their work. Smoke curled thinly from the cowling and was torn apart by the breeze.

Then the pilot's door swung open, and a man fell out of it onto the clay, and did not get up.

Jude ran. Later there would be time to reconstruct the distance in strides, the grease gun abandoned on the ground, the strange slow quality of the light—but in the moment there was only the running, and the man on the ground, and the blood. So much blood, spreading under him into the red clay until the two reds met and became indistinguishable, as if the earth were simply taking him back ahead of schedule.

He was perhaps fifty, gray-stubbled, dressed in a bush shirt gone dark with wet. His eyes found Jude's with terrible clarity, the clarity of a man who has done his sums and knows the total.

"The map," he said. His hand moved inside his shirt, slow as a hermit crab. "Oilskin. You take it. You swear."

And Jude—who had built six years of careful, deliberate, load-bearing neutrality, who owed nothing to anyone, who had chosen this river precisely because it asked nothing of him but cargo and discretion—heard himself say, in a voice that seemed to come from somewhere outside the whole constructed life:

"I swear."

The man's hand pressed the bundle against Jude's chest, oily and heavy and final, like a heart being transplanted. His lips moved again, shaping a word—*Mara*, it might have been, or *Wewak*, or both—and then the clarity went out of his eyes all at once, like a lamp blown out by the draft of its own falling, and the blood went on spreading into the clay, and the swifts came down over the strip to feed, and the rain began at four o'clock, or whatever o'clock it was now, hammering down on the tin roof and the dead man and the map and the oath, indifferent as arithmetic, certain as gravity.

Jude knelt in the rising water and did not move for a long time.
