## 2. The Man Who Fell Out of the Sky

The storm had been building all afternoon behind the Hunstein Range, stacking itself up in blue-black towers that ate the light, and Jude Almeida had spent most of it doing what he did best on days like this: nothing at all, deliberately, with great attention.

He sat on the veranda of the strip house with his boots off and a mug of tea going cold in his hand, watching the sky perform its slow theater of menace. The Cessna sat tied down out on the clay-and-grass runway, wings glistening from the morning's shower, looking small and honest under the bruising clouds. Downriver, he knew, the mission stations would be counting their stores and finding them short. Upstream, the mining camps would be doing the same arithmetic with less patience. Somewhere between those two hung Jude's whole life: four hundred kilos of rice and diesel and penicillin and letters from mothers to sons, ferried back and forth across the basin in a forty-year-old airframe held together by safety wire, prayer, and the particular stubbornness of a man who had chosen, long ago, to be indispensable to everyone and answerable to no one.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of it. He knew every missionary's drinking habits and every camp boss's debts; he carried messages that were technically legal and parcels that were technically not; he accepted payment in cash, in chickens, in silence. In eleven years he had never once been asked whose side he was on, because everyone understood, without it ever being said aloud, that Jude Almeida was on no side at all. Neutrality wasn't a virtue here. It was a load-bearing wall.

The tea had gone cold. The first fat drops were starting to stipple the dust along the veranda boards when he heard the engine.

He heard it wrong at first — heard it as a sound like a chainsaw two valleys over, or a truck grinding through low gear — before his ear sorted it into the one thing it should not have been: an aircraft, coming in low, in weather no sane pilot would fly through, and flying badly.

Jude was on his feet before he'd decided to stand. He stepped out into the first real rain and shaded his eyes, searching the murk over the western tree line where the river bent away toward Ambunti. For a long moment there was nothing but cloud and the grey sheeting of water. Then the aircraft came out of it sideways, more falling than flying, a pale smear against the dark hills, and even at that distance Jude could see the wrongness of it — the nose-down attitude, the wing rocking like a drunk trying to walk a line, the engine coughing in a rhythm that said *I am dying and we both know it*.

"Come left," Jude said aloud, uselessly, the way he talked to aircraft in trouble. "Come left, you fool, you're drifting past—"

But the pilot had seen the strip. Of course he had; there was nothing else to see, just the long grey scar of mowed grass and packed clay running along the river terrace, and the little tin roof beside it, and perhaps — if God was generous — the memory of a chart annotation, *Almeida Strip, 640 meters, soft after rain*. Soft after rain. The rain was coming down now in ropes.

The aircraft flared too high, dropped, bounced once with a crack of metal that Jude felt in his teeth, then slewed sideways and ground-looped through the wet grass in a fan of spray and torn turf, coming to rest fifty meters short of the windsock with its tail cocked up and its propeller bent into a shape like a question mark.

Jude ran.

He ran the way he hadn't run since he was a young man with something to prove, out into the downpour, boots skidding on the slick clay, and by the time he reached the aircraft the door was already open and hanging crooked and a man was half out of it, face-down on the wet grass, one arm trailing.

"I've got you," Jude said, though he didn't know yet whether he had anything. "Easy. I've got you."

The man was heavy, sixty-odd kilograms of dead weight soaked in rain and something warmer and stickier that Jude's hands found before his eyes did. Blood. A lot of it, thinned by the rain to a pink wash that ran off the grass in braided streams. Jude got an arm under the man's shoulders and dragged him clear of the aircraft — some old instinct about fuel and fire, though the engine had already gone quiet in the way engines go quiet when they are finished forever — and laid him flat, and got his own jacket off and wadded it under the man's head, and only then looked at him properly.

He was maybe fifty-five, grey-stubbled, sunburnt down to a deep leathery brown, with the kind of face the basin makes out of people who stay too long: creased, weathered, half-erased. His shirt was open at the collar and beneath it, high on the left side of his chest, was a wound that Jude took one look at and understood completely. Not a gouge. Not a graze. A hole, small and neat at the front, made by something that traveled fast and left in a hurry without showing its papers.

A bullet hole. Out here, three hundred kilometers from the nearest policeman who mattered.

"You're shot," Jude said, stupidly, stating the obvious because the obvious was all that was holding still.

"No," the man said. His voice was a dry whisper, almost amused. "I'm *bleeding*. The shooting happened this morning. Near Pagwi." He coughed, and the cough cost him something visible, a shudder that ran through his whole frame. "You're Almeida."

"How do you know my name?"

"Everyone knows your name." The man's eyes were pale, a washed-out blue-grey, and they moved with difficulty, dragging themselves up to Jude's face as if each glance were a weight to be lifted. "Everyone on the river knows the name of the man who carries everything and asks nothing." A ghost of a smile. "Elias Wren."

"I don't know you."

"No," Wren agreed. "You don't. That was the idea."

*

Getting him to the house was ugly work. Jude went back for the utility tarpaulin and dragged him the last hundred meters on it, the wounded man biting down on sounds that wanted to be screams and mostly winning, and then they were inside, out of the hammering rain, and Jude lit the pressure lamp and put his hands on the wound and began doing the things you do when you are the closest thing to a doctor within a day's travel and the patient is already past helping.

He had done this before. Twice, in eleven years. Once for a chainman with a severed thumb, who lived. Once for a young constable opened up by a bush knife, who didn't. You boiled water. You packed what needed packing. You gave what painkillers you had, which tonight was a strip of paracetamol older than some of his tires. And you watched the color of the blood and the rhythm of the breathing and you made your private estimate, and you kept it to yourself.

Wren's blood was coming too fast and too dark, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it.

"Needle and thread," Wren said, when Jude had the wound packed and bound as well as it could be bound. "In your kit. I saw the look on your face. Sew me up anyway. Buy me twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes for what?"

Wren's hand came up — trembling, caked with dried mud and his own blood — and closed around Jude's wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "For what I flew here to do."

Jude should have refused. Later, in the long accounting he would keep of this night, he would return again and again to that moment, standing in his own kitchen with lamplight jumping on the tin walls and rain roaring on the roof like a congregation, and he would try to find the version of himself that said *no*, that said *I don't carry men's secrets, that's the whole point of me*, that walked out into the storm and let the night take its course. That version existed somewhere. It simply didn't show up.

He threaded the needle.

*

Wren talked while Jude sewed, because talking kept him anchored, and because — Jude understood this slowly, with a growing coldness in his stomach — he had rehearsed. Whatever speech this was, whatever confession or plea or transaction, he had been composing it since Pagwi, maybe since long before Pagwi, stitching it together during the hours of flight while his life leaked out of him into the seat padding, refining it, cutting it down, until what remained was lean enough to deliver in the time he had left.

"I prospected the high country for thirty years," he said. "Before Kavera, before the roads, before any of it. I know ground the way you know weather. And six years ago I found it. The real thing. Alluvial field in a high valley, gold in the gravels like pepper in rice, enough to make every claim staked in this country look like a child's sandbox." His breath caught; Jude tightened a stitch; Wren grunted and went on. "I did what fools do. I told people. Quietly. Carefully, I thought. But money smells these things the way sharks smell blood, and the Kavera Consortium came sniffing, and then they came leasing, and then—" He stopped. His eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere Jude couldn't follow.

"And then?" Jude said quietly.

"And then they cleared the ground." The words came out flat and terrible, stripped of everything. "There was a village in that valley. They said it was resettlement. Compensation. Papers were signed — or fingerprints were taken, which is not the same thing — and then one morning there was smoke, and by evening there was no village, and by the end of the year there was no record that a village had ever been there. I checked. I spent two years checking. I have names, dates, photographs, radio logs. I have testimony." His hand moved, weakly, toward the breast pocket of his ruined shirt. "I have it all. And they know I have it, and this morning near Pagwi their men stopped pretending to negotiate."

Jude set down the needle. His hands were steady — they always were, that was the trick of him, the gift of him, hands that stayed steady while everything else went to ruin — but inside, something was tilting, the way a loaded canoe tilts when you shift your weight one degree too far.

"Wren. Listen to me. I fly supplies. I'm not a courier for—"

"You're exactly a courier," Wren said. "It's the only honest job description in this whole basin. You carry things from one place to another and you don't ask what they mean. Well. Here is the thing I need carried." He fumbled at his pocket, and Jude, cursing softly, helped him, and together they drew out a bundle wrapped in oilskin, tied with cord, flat and rectangular and heavier than its size suggested, the way certain objects are heavier than physics can explain.

"The map?" Jude said. "Is that what this is? Because if it's a treasure map, mate, I've got news for you about the state of my—"

"It's evidence," Wren said. "And it's a map. Both. The valley's marked. The gold's marked. And so is the village — the village that isn't in any government ledger, that doesn't appear on any survey, that two hundred people lived in and died in while men in Port Moresby signed documents calling it voluntary relocation." His grip on Jude's wrist returned, tighter now, and his voice dropped to something raw and urgent, the rehearsal burning away, leaving only the man underneath. "My daughter doesn't know any of this. She thinks I'm a deadbeat who chose gold over her, and she's right, she's been right for fifteen years, and I will die with that on me. But this—" he pressed the bundle against Jude's chest, oily and heavy and final, "—this goes to her. Not to the police, they're bought. Not to the church, they'll sit on it for a decade. To *Mara*. Mara Wren. She works at the trading post in Wewak, the one on the point, you can't miss it. You tell her it's from her father, and you tell her I'm sorry, and you tell her—" his voice cracked, and reformed, and cracked again, "—you tell her whatever you think a dying man would say, because I can't think anymore, I can't think, so you swear it, you swear it now."

"I swear," Jude said, because there was no other word available, because the man's eyes were fixed on him with the last of his light, because refusing a dying man seemed suddenly like the one cruelty the basin had never taught him.

The hand pressed the bundle against Jude's chest, oily and heavy and final, like a heart being transplanted. Lips moved, shaping a word — *Mara*, it might have been, or *Wewak*, or both — and then the clarity went out of the pale eyes all at once, like a lamp blown out by the draft of its own falling, and the blood went on spreading into the floorboards' cracks, and outside the swifts came down low over the strip to feed on whatever the storm had driven up, and the rain hammered the tin roof and the dead man and the map and the oath, indifferent as arithmetic, certain as gravity.

Jude knelt on the floorboards and did not move for a long time.

*

Later — much later, when he had done what needed doing with the body, covering it with a clean sheet and laying it straight with its hands folded, because whatever else Elias Wren had been, he deserved the dignity of geometry — Jude sat at his table with the oilskin bundle in the circle of lamplight and did not touch it for the better part of an hour.

The rain had settled into its overnight rhythm, steady and businesslike. The wrecked aircraft sat out in the dark, filling slowly with water, its bent propeller pointing at the sky like an accusation. Somewhere downriver, men who shot prospectors near Pagwi in the morning were presumably having dinner, and sleeping well, and making plans.

Jude thought about neutrality. He turned it over in his mind the way you turn a stone to see what lives underneath. Eleven years of it. Everyone's friend, no one's man. And now there was a dead prospector on his floor and a dead man's promise in his hands and a name — *Mara* — sitting in his mouth like a stone he couldn't swallow or spit out.

He untied the cord.

Inside the oilskin, wrapped in a second layer of waxed cloth, was a surveyor's map, hand-annotated, worn soft at the folds, and beneath it a thinner packet: photographs, carbon copies of radio traffic, a sheaf of pages in a cramped, slanting hand. Jude looked at the map first, because maps were his language, and he read the contour lines and the river systems and the little penciled cross marking the high valley, and he felt the dread arrive before understanding did, the way thunder arrives before you consciously hear it.

Because he knew that valley. Every pilot in the basin knew the shape of that country. And he knew, from cargo manifests he'd signed with his own hand, from drums and crates he'd hauled without question to airstrips with consortium money in their gravel, exactly whose lease boundaries lay drawn around it.

Kavera Mining Consortium. Ground they already owned on paper. Ground that had been emptied of the people who lived on it.

Jude sat back. The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain went on falling on the whole drowned world, and he understood, with a certainty that felt like the ground dropping away, that the comfortable architecture of his life — the load-bearing wall of belonging to no one — had just taken a hit it was not built to survive.

He looked at the map, and then at the sheeted body, and then west, toward the coast, toward Wewak, toward a woman at a trading post on the point who had no idea what was coming for her.

"Well," Jude said to the empty room, to the rain, to the dead man. "Now what?"

The rain answered, and did not stop, and in the morning there would be wreckage to deal with and questions to deflect and a flight to plan that he had not yet agreed to make — and already, even then, even kneeling in the lamplight with a stranger's blood drying on his hands, he knew he was going to make it.
