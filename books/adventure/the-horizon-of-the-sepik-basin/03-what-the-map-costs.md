## 3. What the Map Costs

The rain came back an hour after dawn, the way it always did in the Sepik, as if the sky had only paused to catch its breath. Jude stood in the open doorway of the hangar with a mug of tea going cold in one hand and the oilskin bundle in the other, and watched the water hammer the corrugated roof like a congregation of impatient fingers.

Elias Wren lay on the cot beneath the window, dead twelve hours now, and the strip outside was a river of mud and standing silver light.

Jude had covered him with a mosquito net and a blanket, because there was nothing else to do until the weather lifted and he could fly the body somewhere with a priest or a registry or at least a hole in the ground that wasn't his own front yard. The Cessna sat in the rain with its cowling still off, and Jude had spent the night not sleeping, not opening the bundle, just listening to the water and to the silence where a dying man's breathing used to be.

Now the tea was cold and the bundle was warm from his hand, and there was no reason left to wait.

He sat on an upturned fuel drum, set the mug on the workbench among the wrenches and the spark plugs and the jar of screws that had no home, and unwrapped the oilskin.

It was wrapped the way river traders wrapped opium and the way missionaries wrapped communion silver — tight, deliberate, tied with a length of fishing line that had been knotted and reknotted so many times the ends were furred. Inside were three layers: a strip of waxed canvas, a sheet of plastic cut from a garbage bag, and then the thing itself.

A surveyor's map. Hand-annotated. Old paper gone soft and tea-colored at the folds, the kind that had been folded and unfolded so many times the creases had become part of the geography.

Jude spread it flat on the workbench and weighted the corners with a socket wrench, a spark plug, a mug, and his own palm.

The map showed a stretch of country he knew the way you know a scar — the upper tributaries of the Sepik's southern catchment, the country where the river systems tangled and the mountains began their long climb into the range. He'd flown over it a hundred times, hauling diesel drums and flour sacks and sick children, and from two thousand feet it was all the same green velvet, beautiful and anonymous. On the ground it was none of those things.

The base map was old — government survey, he guessed, from the style of the lettering and the way the contours were drawn by hand, someone's patient ink work from before the war or just after it. But over the printed bones someone had laid a lifetime of annotation in pencil and ballpoint and, in the earliest layers, in ink from a fountain pen, the lines browned and fine as spider silk. Elevations. Water sources. A trail marked in dotted line that climbed out of a river gorge and over a saddle and down into a valley that the printed map showed as blank green.

In the middle of that blank valley, in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, someone had written a single word.

*Alluvial.*

And below it, in Elias Wren's cramped, hard-slanted hand — Jude recognized it now from the freight manifests Elias had filled out at other strips, other years, back when he'd been a name on a cargo list and not a man bleeding out on a hangar cot — a string of figures. Weights. Probably grams. A private ledger of a private fortune, written in the margin of a stolen country.

Jude sat with it a long time. The rain kept on. Somewhere under the hangar, a rat moved through the fuel drums with the confidence of a landlord.

He should burn it. That was the first thought, and it was an honest one. A man with a bullet in him had pressed this into his hands at dusk and made him swear, and the cleanest way out of a swear was to destroy the thing sworn on. No map, no promise. No promise, no syndicate. No syndicate, no dead pilot on his cot, no daughter in Wewak who'd learn her father died the way he'd lived, reaching for something in the dirt.

He even got as far as standing, as far as picking up the map, as far as holding one corner over the kerosene lamp on the bench.

The paper trembled in the draft and the lamplight came through it, and Jude saw that the map was double-written. The annotations he'd been reading were the top layer. Beneath them, showing faintly through the soft old paper, was a second text — pressed hard, from the back, in a different hand. He turned it over.

The reverse was covered in writing. Dense, small, slanted, in the same fountain-pen brown as the oldest ink on the front, but this was not survey notes.

It was a statement.

Jude read it twice before his hands started to shake, and then he read it a third time, slowly, the way you read a thing you're going to have to say out loud someday to people who will want to know if you read it carefully.

It was dated two years ago, give or take — the ink was faded but the date was clear — and it was signed at the bottom with a name that meant nothing to Jude and a clan name that did, because he'd flown a medical evacuation out of that valley once, three years back, a boy with a broken femur, and the boy's father had given the clan name the way you hand someone your whole self.

The statement said: the Kavera Mining Consortium, through its contractors, had come to the valley in the dry season of that year with armed men and a company survey. It said the village had been told the ground was leased. It said the village had not signed anything and had not been asked to. It said that when the elders refused to move, the men burned the village and shot those who ran, and that the number of the dead was written out — names, ages, some of them — forty-one names in a column, in ink brown as old blood, in a hand that pressed so hard it had gone through to the front of the map.

It said the witness had seen it from the ridge above, and had taken this map from the survey camp, and had been running from Kavera ever since.

And at the very bottom, in a different ink, fresher, in Elias Wren's cramped hard slant, someone had added one line:

*I carried this for two years. I am tired of carrying it. It belongs to my daughter, and to them.*

Jude put the map down on the bench very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

Then he did what he always did when the world got too large for the hangar: he made tea he didn't want and thought about flying.

* * *

The trouble was that Jude Almeida knew that valley. Not the valley itself — he'd never landed there, never wanted to, the strip was a rumor and the country was a wall of green — but he knew its coordinates, because he knew every coordinate in his operating area the way a priest knows his parishioners' sins: intimately, involuntarily, and without approving of most of them.

And the coordinates of that blank green valley fell inside a lease block.

He'd seen the lease maps. You couldn't work the basin and not see them — Kavera's exploration licenses hung in the provincial lands office in Wewak like trophies, great colored polygons laid over the country, and every pilot who hauled freight for the company camps knew where the boundaries ran because the boundaries determined who you could carry for and who you couldn't. Jude had spent nine years keeping himself exactly on the safe side of those lines. He carried for missions, for traders, for the small alluvial men with their sluice boxes and their dreams. He did not carry for Kavera, and he did not carry against Kavera, and he did not ask questions about what Kavera did inside its colored polygons, because the answers to those questions had a way of requiring follow-up questions, and the men who asked follow-up questions in this basin tended to end up as fuel-depot rumors.

The valley on the map sat inside Kavera's EL-114 like a heart inside a ribcage.

And a village had been erased there. Forty-one names, pressed so hard into the paper they'd come through to the other side.

Jude carried his tea out under the eave of the hangar and stood in the rain-smell and tried to do the arithmetic that had kept him alive for nine years. The arithmetic was simple. It went: *I know nothing. I have seen nothing. A stranger died at my strip of wounds received elsewhere, and I will fly his body to Wewak and notify whoever needs notifying, and that is the whole of my involvement.* That arithmetic had worked for Father Anselm's contraband antibiotics and for the Timbuke traders' untaxed tobacco and for every gray thing that moved up and down the rivers, because gray things only stayed gray if nobody looked at them too hard.

This was not gray. This was black pressed so hard into paper it showed through to the other side.

And the swear — Elias had made him swear with a hand like a vise on his wrist, blood on both their hands, and Jude had said yes because you say yes to a dying man, the same way you say amen, because the alternative is to be alone in the room with the silence you made.

*Take it to my daughter. Mara Wren, Wewak, the trading post on the point. Tell her — * and here the man had coughed, and the cough had gone on too long, and when he came back to speech his voice had dropped into something quiet and terrible, the voice of a man spending his last coins — *tell her I wasn't running from her. Tell her I was running from this. Tell her — *

And then the blood, and the dark, and Jude holding his wrist and saying *yes, yes, I'll take it, I swear it,* into a face that was already mostly somewhere else.

He had regretted it before the man's breathing stopped. He regretted it now, standing under the eave with cold tea and rain-smell, and the regret was a live thing in his chest, and under the regret, unwelcome as a leech, was the other thing. The thing that had made him say yes in the first place. The thing that had kept him in this basin for nine years, hauling other people's medicine and other people's mail and never once his own weight.

Forty-one names.

He went back inside and dug the radio log out of the drawer under the workbench. Three years back, the medevac flight — he'd written it up the way he wrote everything up, date, strip, passenger, complaint. The boy with the femur. The village name in the father's mouth, and Jude had written it down phonetically, the way you do, and the village name was a name.

He checked it against the map. The coordinates matched. The village on the map — the erased one, the burned one, the one with forty-one names pressed through the paper — was the village he'd flown the boy out of. The village that, when he'd overflown it six months later on the Kavera mail run, had simply not been there. No smoke, no roofs, no gardens. He'd noticed, the way you notice a missing tooth in a smile, and he'd filed it under *none of my business* and flown on, because flying on was the whole of his religion.

He had been there. He had seen the absence and called it weather.

Jude sat down on the fuel drum again. The rain eased, as it does toward midmorning, into a fine drifting mist that turned the whole world the color of the inside of a shell. Out on the strip a heron picked its way through the puddles with the indifference of a creature that has never once been asked to take sides.

Neutrality, he thought, was a thing you could only hold if both sides agreed to let you. And one side had burned a village and pressed forty-one names through a piece of paper, and the other side was a dead man and a daughter who didn't know yet that she was an orphan, and a map that sat on his workbench like a coal that wouldn't cool.

He thought about the men buying silence upriver. Father Anselm's warning, two days old now and aging badly, like everything else. *They're paying for quiet, Jude. And quiet is the one crop you've been growing all these years.*

He thought about the fuel depot in Wewak, and who drank there, and who listened, and how fast a rumor traveled downriver compared to a Cessna with a sick engine and a full load of doubt.

And he thought — this was the thought that ended it, the thought that came in quiet as the mist and settled as permanent as the rain — of Mara Wren at the trading post on the point, whoever she was, whatever she looked like, spending her days selling rice and kerosene to men who might already have been paid to ask about her.

The map was evidence. The map was a fortune. The map was forty-one names. And it was addressed, in the only way that mattered, to a woman in Wewak who had no idea it existed, and every man with a gun in this basin would be looking for it soon, and the only thing standing between her and all of it was a pilot who had spent nine years perfecting the art of not being there.

Jude folded the map along its soft old creases, wrapped it back in its plastic and its canvas and its oilskin, tied the fishing line the way Elias had tied it, and put it inside his flight jacket, against his chest, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat.

Then he went out into the mist and started pulling the Cessna's cowling back on, because the weather would lift by afternoon, and Wewak was four hours' flying, and he had a body to deliver and a promise to keep and, for the first time in nine years, a side.

He was tightening the last of the cowling screws when he heard it — a sound that didn't belong to the mist or the herons or the dripping green world of his strip. An engine. Not his. High and steady and coming on fast out of the south, and painted on its side, when it cleared the tree line and leveled over his river of mud, was a logo he knew from the colored polygons in the lands office: the Kavera Mining Consortium, arriving, as it always did, with perfect courtesy and perfect timing, to ask its questions.
