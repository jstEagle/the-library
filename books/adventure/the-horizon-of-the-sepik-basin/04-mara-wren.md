## 4. Mara Wren

The tide came in at Wewak the way bad news did—slowly at first, then all at once, filling every low place you'd thought was dry.

Mara Wren stood on the veranda of the Kamber trading post with a ledger under her arm and salt drying white on her forearms, watching the water push up the mudflats in long silver sheets. Behind her, the store smelled the way it always smelled by late afternoon: kerosene, rice dust, dried fish, the sweet rot of a crate of bananas nobody would buy before they turned. A ceiling fan turned with the lazy conviction of something that had given up on its job years ago and was only going through the motions.

"Mrs. Wren," said old Simon from the doorway, using the honorific he applied to her out of courtesy rather than accuracy, because she was neither married nor, strictly, anyone's widow. "The *Kuranda* is coming up the channel. Two days late."

"Then we'll be two days short of flour," Mara said, "and I'll be two days explaining to Father Anselm why his mission order is sitting on a wharf in Madang." She wrote it down anyway, in the ledger, in her small square hand: *flour, 40 bags, delayed.* The ledger was the one thing in Wewak that behaved. Numbers went where you put them and stayed there. Numbers didn't get on a boat to the gold country when you were nine years old and promise to come back by Christmas.

She had stopped counting Christmases somewhere around the eleventh.

* * *

The trading post sat on stilts above a beach that was beautiful in the way of things nobody local had time to notice—a crescent of gray-gold sand, palms leaning over it like bored sentries, war relics rusting in the shallows offshore where the war had left them thirty years ago and the sea had been slowly digesting ever since. Mara had arrived in Wewak at nineteen with one suitcase and an address written on the back of a cigarette packet, and Old Man Kamber had hired her because she could add three columns in her head faster than he could with a pencil, and because she looked like someone who wasn't going anywhere.

He'd been right about that. He'd also been dead for four years now, leaving her the post in a will that surprised everyone except Mara, who had known exactly what she was worth to him: someone who showed up. Every day. In the rain. When the generator died and when the beer shipment came in warm and when the fishermen wanted credit against catches that might never come in. She showed up. It was the whole of her religion, and she practiced it devoutly, because showing up was the one thing Elias Wren had never once managed to do.

"Mara!" Simon again, from the front steps, and this time there was something in his voice that made her set down the pencil. "There's a plane. Small one. Coming in low over the point."

Planes came into Wewak regularly enough—the coastal strip took mail flights, charters, the occasional government patrol officer—but not to their beach, and not at this hour, and not flying the way this one was flying. Even from the veranda she could see it: a high-wing single engine, silver gone dull with age, one wingtip riding lower than the other as if the aircraft itself were tired. It came across the bay trailing the last light like something being dragged, circled the strip behind the town twice, and put down hard enough that she heard the tires complain all the way from the water.

Simon looked at her. She looked at the ledger, then closed it. Some part of her, the part that had spent twenty years listening for engines over ranges she'd never seen, already knew.

"I'll be back before dark," she said, which was a lie, and they both knew it.

* * *

The pilot was standing beside his aircraft when she reached the strip, hatless, shirt dark with sweat and something else, talking to the fuel man in a low voice that carried no further than it had to. He was younger than she'd expected—thirty maybe, lean in the way of people who worked for a living rather than exercised—and he had the particular stillness of a man carrying something heavy and deciding how to set it down.

"You're Mara Wren," he said, when she was close enough. Not a question.

"Who's asking?"

"Jude Almeida." He held out a hand, then seemed to reconsider what his hands had been doing lately and let it fall. "I fly supply runs out of the Sepik. Mission stations, mostly. Your father—" He stopped. Started again, and she watched him do it, watched him choose each word like a man crossing a river on wet stones. "Elias Wren came to my strip two nights ago. His plane was shot up. He'd been hit."

The sea sound filled the space where her voice should have been. Somewhere behind the fuel drums a dog was barking at nothing, at everything, at the smell of blood maybe, though she wouldn't learn until later that there was blood on this man's clothes, dried into the canvas of the seat behind him, that he'd flown two hours with a dying stranger slumped against his shoulder.

"He's alive," Mara said. It wasn't a question either, but it was a prayer, and God knew she hadn't prayed in years.

Jude Almeida looked at the ground between them, and the ground told him what to say, and he said it.

"He died on my floor at about four in the morning. I buried him yesterday, up at the strip, under the frangipani. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. There was nothing—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it, hating the crack, hating the apology, hating the whole errand. "There was nothing anyone could have done. The bullet was in too deep. But he was awake for a while, and he talked, and he made me swear I'd bring you something."

He reached inside his jacket and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilskin, tied with cord, stained dark along one edge in a way she understood without wanting to understand. He held it out flat on both palms, the way you'd hold something holy or something dangerous, and maybe it was both.

Mara didn't take it.

"No," she said.

Almeida blinked. "I don't—"

"No. You can put that back in your pocket and you can turn your plane around and you can fly back to whatever swamp you crawled out of, because I am not interested." Her voice was steady. She was distantly proud of that, the way you're proud of a building standing after the earthquake—impressed with the engineering, numb about everything else. "Twenty-two years. Do you know what twenty-two years is? I got a letter when I was nine. One letter. *Gone upriver, sweetheart, big strike, be home by Christmas.* And then nothing. Not a Christmas card. Not a radio message through the mission when my mother was dying—not even to tell him, I had to send word through three stations and a priest who knew him, and do you know what came back? Nothing. Silence. For eleven months she lay in that bed in Rabaul and her husband was somewhere in the hills digging yellow dirt out of a creek bed, and he couldn't spare one day. One day, Mr. Almeida. So don't stand on my beach and hand me his—his *last words*, his little package, his dying wish, because Elias Wren's wishes were worth exactly what he paid for them, and that was nothing."

The dog had stopped barking. The tide was coming in. She could hear it, that long silver hiss across the flats, filling the low places.

Almeida took it without flinching, which she resented; she'd wanted him to flinch. "I know," he said quietly. "I've read his file, effectively. I spent two days listening to him talk, and most of what he talked about was you. He knew what he'd done. He knew it was unforgivable. He wasn't asking you to forgive it."

"Then what was he asking?"

"He was asking me to make sure this reached you. That's all. What you do with it—" He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; she saw the exhaustion move through him like weather. "That's yours. I'll leave it with the mission if you'd rather. Father Anselm. You don't ever have to see me again."

And that—that was the thing that broke her open, though she'd have denied it under torture. Not the package. Not the death. The offer of the door. Because Elias had never offered her a door in his life; he'd simply walked out of one and left her holding the frame. And here was this stranger, this tired ferryman of other people's sins, saying *you owe me nothing,* and meaning it, and the meaning of it hurt worse than anything else had all evening.

She held out her hand.

He placed the oilskin bundle in it. It was lighter than she expected. Heavy things usually were, once you finally picked them up.

* * *

They sat on the seawall behind the post because she refused to let him inside, and because the light was going and she wanted to read it while there was light, and because sitting down had started to seem necessary in a way that had nothing to do with reading.

Inside the oilskin was a survey map, folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling. Pencil annotations in her father's cramped left-slanting hand crawled across it like ants: elevations, creek names she didn't recognize, distances measured in walking days. And in one corner, boxed in red grease-pencil, a set of coordinates and two words: *the field.*

Gold country. Of course. Twenty-two years and a corpse later, and the man was still sending her gold country.

"There's more," Almeida said. He was looking out at the water, giving her the privacy of his profile. "He talked, like I said. Some of it was delirium. Some of it wasn't. He said men were following him. Men from a company—Kavera. Kavera Mining Consortium. He said they'd killed people for that ground, Mara. He said he'd seen it happen, and that he'd run instead of telling anyone, and that he'd been running for two years, and that the map was the only proof of where it happened, and that they couldn't let it exist." He paused. "He said if they found out you had it, they'd come for you too."

Mara laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "Of course he did. Of course. He dies and leaves me a map, and the map leaves me a target. That's so perfectly him. He couldn't even die without outsourcing the consequences." She stared at the grease-pencil box until it blurred. "Do you know what I did when I got his name from the mission rolls, five years ago? I wrote to him. Care of every station I could find on the Sepik. Six letters. I told him—I told him Mom's grave doesn't even have the right dates on it because I couldn't remember the day he left, and I wanted the stone to mark the true anniversary of when our family ended. I asked him for a date, Mr. Almeida. That's all I wanted. A date. And now you're telling me he was hiding from murderers the whole time, and some part of me—the stupid part, the part that's still nine years old—is *relieved*, because at least silence has an excuse now, and I hate myself for being relieved, and I hate him for making me feel it, and I hate you for bringing it here."

"I know," Almeida said again. He said it the way people say things they mean.

"And what about you?" She turned on him, and the anger found a new target with the ease of long practice. "Why are you here? Don't tell me duty. Nobody flies a shot-up Cessna two hundred miles to deliver a dead prospector's parcel out of duty. What did he give you? What did he promise you?"

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Nothing. He tried. He offered me half of whatever the field was worth, and I told him to shut up and save his breath." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "I'm here because a man bled out on my floor calling his daughter's name, and I was the last person whose face he saw, and somebody had to be. That's all. That's the whole story. I fly things places. This was a thing that needed to go somewhere."

The tide had reached the pilings below them. She could hear the water working at the posts, patient, certain, the way it had worked at the war wrecks offshore for thirty years, taking everything eventually, keeping what it liked.

"The men who shot him," she said. "Are they still looking?"

"They were two days behind him when he reached me. They'll be less than that behind me now."

"So they'll come here."

"It's possible."

"To Wewak. To my shop. To the one life I built out of the wreckage he left." She folded the map along its worn creases, deliberately, precisely, the way she did everything, and put it inside her shirt against her skin, and hated herself for that too—for the instinct of it, the keeping of it, the nine-year-old's hand closing around the last thing her father ever gave her. "You should go. Tonight. Take off before dark and don't come back."

Almeida looked at her for a long moment. Out past the point, the first star was out, and the sea had gone the color of gunmetal, and somewhere in the town a generator coughed and caught and settled into its nightly drone.

"I can't," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because they'll search the strip tomorrow, and they'll ask the fuel man who flew in tonight, and he'll tell them, because everyone tells them everything, that's how Kavera works—they don't buy maps, they buy *people*, and there isn't a depot between here and the highlands they haven't bought." He stood, and in the failing light his face was all shadow and jaw. "Which means the moment I start the engine, I confirm where the map is. And the moment I don't, I'm just a supply pilot who stayed the night, and you're just a trader who knows me, and maybe we're nobody at all. Maybe." He picked up his hat, turned it once in his hands. "Or maybe a very polite man in a very clean shirt is going to walk into your shop tomorrow morning and offer you a great deal of money for something you'll swear you've never seen. And when that happens, Mara, I'd very much like to be standing next to you when you tell him no."

She should have said no to that. Every instinct she'd sharpened over twenty years of showing up, keeping books, keeping walls, keeping herself—every instinct said send him away, burn the map, be nobody, be safe.

Instead she heard herself say: "The post closes at six. If you're staying, you're stacking flour sacks. I don't feed volunteers who don't work."

Something moved through his face that might, in better light, have been the beginning of a smile. "I've stacked worse things," he said. "In worse places."

Behind them, out on the black water, the tide finished coming in, and the flats that had been dry all afternoon were gone, and there was no path back across them anymore.
