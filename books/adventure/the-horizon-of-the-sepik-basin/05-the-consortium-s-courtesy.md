## 5. The Consortium's Courtesy

The rain came to Wewak the way bad news came—sideways, without warning, and smelling of salt and something rotten underneath.

Mara Wren stood in the doorway of the trading post and watched it hammer the harbor flat. The water beyond the breakwater had gone the color of gunmetal, and the fishing boats that had gone out at dawn were hurrying back in like insects scattering from a lifted stone. Behind her, the post smelled of kerosene, dried fish, and the sweet rot of coconuts nobody would buy before they spoiled. She'd worked here eleven months. Long enough to know which crates held what without looking. Long enough to stop counting the days since she'd last thought of her father with anything other than a dull, banked anger.

Or so she'd believed, until three days ago, when a pilot named Jude Almeida had walked into this same doorway soaked to the ribs, holding an oilskin packet out to her like a man surrendering a weapon.

*Your father's dead,* he'd said. No preamble. He'd struck her once with the words and then stood there while she bled, watching her with those gray eyes that seemed to apologize for everything and promise nothing. *He crash-landed at my strip. I couldn't save him.*

She hadn't cried. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a stone in her palm: she hadn't cried, not in front of him, not after, not alone in her room above the post where the ceiling fan clicked like a slow clock. What she'd felt instead was something hotter and more shameful—a fury so complete it had nowhere to go, because the man who'd earned it was already dead. Elias Wren had spent her whole childhood half-present, his eyes always on some horizon she couldn't see, and then he'd spent her whole adulthood entirely absent, chasing gold up rivers that didn't appear on maps. And now he'd had the audacity to die before she could tell him what she thought of him. Before she could make him hear it.

And he'd left her this instead. A map. A dead man's war, wrapped in oilcloth and handed to a stranger to deliver.

She'd almost thrown it into the harbor. She'd stood on the seawall with the packet in both hands, the tide sucking at the pilings below, and she'd been one breath away from letting go when she'd seen, through the worn oilskin, the edge of her father's handwriting—the small, cramped letters he'd used for survey notes, letters she hadn't seen in fifteen years but recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Her hands had simply refused to open. Traitors, both of them.

So now the map lay hidden inside the false bottom of a tea chest in the storeroom, under forty pounds of rice, and Mara watched the rain and waited for whatever came next, because she was beginning to understand that something would.

It came at two o'clock, in a white Land Cruiser with mud on its flanks and government plates that didn't match the men inside.

* * *

He introduced himself as Mr. Voss, and he was the most carefully made man Mara had ever seen west of Port Moresby. Everything about him had been chosen: the pale linen shirt pressed to knife-edge creases, the silver hair cut short enough to be practical and long enough to be expensive, the way he removed his sunglasses indoors—not quickly, but slowly, folding them with both hands as though the gesture had been rehearsed in front of a mirror until it looked effortless. Even his sweat was dignified. In this heat, everyone glistened; Voss merely glowed faintly, like polished wood.

"Miss Wren." His voice was low and pleasant, a radio announcer's voice, the kind designed to be believed. "Thank you for seeing me on such short notice."

"I don't remember agreeing to see you," Mara said.

"You agreed by not leaving." He smiled at that, showing perfect teeth, and glanced around the trading post with the frank appraisal of a man pricing the building and everything in it. "Charming establishment. Family business?"

"It belongs to Mr. Okoro. I manage it."

"Of course." He set a leather folder on the counter between them, square and heavy as a hymnal. "I'll be brief, because I know your time is valuable and mine certainly is. I represent the Kavera Mining Consortium. We have interests in the highlands—substantial ones—and we've recently become aware that certain documents belonging to the late Elias Wren may have come into your possession."

Mara kept her face still. Eleven months of weighing fish and counting change had taught her that much: stillness was the only currency that never devalued. "May have come into my possession," she repeated. "That's very careful language, Mr. Voss."

"Careful language is a courtesy I extend to everyone." He opened the folder and turned it toward her. Inside, on cream paper, was a figure written in neat blue ink, and beneath it a line of smaller print that she read twice without comprehension because it was simply too large. "Kavera is prepared to offer you two hundred thousand kina for any survey materials, maps, or field notes of your father's. That is a life-changing sum, Miss Wren, and I'm authorized to go somewhat higher if the materials prove... comprehensive."

Two hundred thousand kina. It would buy the trading post outright. It would buy ten years of anywhere-but-here, which was all Mara had ever wanted from this coast. She looked at the number and felt her own face begin to betray her—not with greed, she hoped, but with the naked arithmetic of a woman who owned nothing but a duffel bag and a dead man's grudge.

"You're offering me more money than my father ever earned in his life," she said slowly, "for pieces of paper."

"We're offering fair market value for proprietary geological data." Voss said it smoothly, but she caught the seam in it—the half-second pause where a real answer should have gone. "Elias Wren did unlicensed survey work on ground adjacent to our leases. Anything he recorded has value to us purely as... housekeeping. Tidying the record. Nothing more sinister than that."

"And if I say no?"

Voss closed the folder. The sound it made was soft, final, like a door in a large empty house.

"Then I've wasted a flight, and you've lost a generous opportunity, and we part as strangers." He smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was somehow worse. "But before I waste the flight, Miss Wren, let me save us both some time. You're wondering how we knew to find you here. The answer is that your father was not a careful man in his later years. He talked. To traders, to pilots, to anyone who'd buy him a beer. He told at least four people that if anything happened to him, his daughter in Wewak should receive his papers. Four people, Miss Wren. Two of them work for us now. The other two—" he spread his hands, elegant, regretful, "—are no longer in a position to talk to anyone."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere out in the harbor a horn sounded, long and mournful, and Mara heard it as if from underwater.

"The other night," Voss continued, conversationally, "a Cessna left Wewak after dark without filing a flight plan. Unusual behavior. The pilot's name is Almeida—Jude Almeida. Perhaps you know him. He seems to know you." He tilted his head. "Wherever that aircraft goes, it will be reported. Every strip between here and the highlands has someone on our payroll, or will shortly. Fuel depots, mission stations, district officers. Kavera has been in this country a long time, Miss Wren. We are patient, and we are thorough, and we are extremely well funded. If you have the documents, you cannot keep them safe, because there is no *safe*. There is only a delay, and delays are expensive—for you, and for whoever helps you."

Mara became aware, distantly, that her heart was going hard against her ribs, and that her hands, resting on the counter, had gone white at the knuckles. She thought of the tea chest. Forty pounds of rice. Three days ago she'd have sworn no one on earth knew it was there.

"You're threatening me," she said. "In a shop. In daylight."

"I'm describing consequences." Voss picked up his sunglasses and did not put them on, holding them loosely, watching her with eyes the color of shallow water. "Threats are what angry men make. I'm simply reading you the terms. Consider them an act of respect—you strike me as someone who prefers the truth to comfort." He slid a card across the counter, heavy stock, embossed, nothing on it but a phone number and the Kavera logo: a stylized mountain range above a river, rendered in gold. "You have until Friday. After that, the offer expires, and so does my ability to protect the people around you. Mr. Okoro has a daughter in Lae, I believe. Boarding school. Lovely facility. Isolated roads."

Something moved through Mara then, cold and clarifying, the way a knife moves. Not fear—or not only fear. Recognition. All her life she'd listened to her father's stories about the interior, the gold fields, the men who fought over them, and she'd filed those stories under *exaggeration*, the way you file a fisherman's tales. But here it was, standing in front of her in pressed linen, polite as a churchwarden, and it was exactly as her father had described and nothing like she'd imagined. Corporate violence didn't shout. It didn't need to. It bought the silence around you, piece by piece, and then it simply waited for the silence to do the work.

"My father," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out, "witnessed something. Didn't he. On that ground you lease. Something you needed buried so deep you burned a village to bury it."

For the first time, something flickered behind Voss's composure—not guilt, nothing so human, but the recalibration of a machine encountering unexpected input. It lasted less than a second. When he spoke again, his voice was warmer than ever, and that warmth was the most frightening thing in the room.

"Elias Wren was a troubled man with a vivid imagination and a drinking problem," he said gently. "Whatever he told you, whatever he wrote down, it's the testimony of a sick mind at the end of a long decline. Kavera operates within the law in every jurisdiction where we work. We welcome scrutiny. We invite it." He picked up his folder and tucked it under his arm. "Friday, Miss Wren. The number is on the card. I do hope you'll call."

He put his sunglasses back on at the door, unhurried, and stepped out into the rain, where a second man—one she hadn't noticed, broad and quiet, standing beneath the awning like furniture—fell into step behind him. The Land Cruiser pulled away through standing water, wipers beating, and was gone.

Mara stood at the counter a long time, listening to the rain, feeling the shape of the trap close around the shop, around Okoro's daughter in Lae, around the tea chest full of rice. Then she turned the card over in her fingers, once, and dropped it into the kerosene lamp beside the register, where it curled and blackened and went out.

* * *

Jude was waiting in the covered passage behind the post, sitting on an overturned crate with his elbows on his knees, and he came off it fast when she pushed through the curtain.

"Well?" His eyes went over her shoulder, past her, cataloguing the street. "How long did he give you?"

"So you knew he'd come." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold and didn't care. "You knew, and you let me sit in there alone."

"He wouldn't touch you in there. Too many witnesses, too much daylight. That's not his style—his style is making you feel like there are no witnesses anywhere." Jude ran a hand through his damp hair. "I was twenty feet away the whole time. I heard every word."

"Then you heard him threaten a child. A girl in boarding school, because of papers she's never seen." Mara stepped closer, and she saw him hold his ground even though everything in his posture wanted to give way. "You heard him admit two men are dead. You stood in that passage and you *listened*, Almeida, because that's what you do, isn't it? You listen, and you watch, and you stay neutral, and you fly your little airplane for everyone and belong to nobody, and it's kept you alive, hasn't it? It's the whole trick of you."

"Yes," Jude said quietly. "It is."

The honesty of it stopped her. She'd expected deflection, argument, some pilot's charm deployed like a smoke screen. Instead he just stood there in the dimness and took it, and she saw that he wasn't calm at all—that underneath the stillness his jaw was working, and his hands, hanging at his sides, had curled into fists he seemed to be deliberately uncurling.

"But here's what I learned yesterday," he went on. "Neutrality isn't a position. It's just a slower way of choosing a side. Sooner or later the rain reaches everybody, and then you find out what you're made of." He nodded toward the street, toward the direction the Land Cruiser had taken. "Voss gave you until Friday because he thinks you're calculating. He thinks everyone's for sale at the right number, because that's the world he lives in, and mostly he's right. Which means every hour you don't call costs him certainty, and certainty is the only thing men like that can't buy back."

"And then what? We run?" She laughed, and it came out bitter. "He said it himself. Every strip, every fuel depot, every district officer. There's no *safe*, Almeida. He's right about that. I checked the map last night—all that ground he wants, it's inside their lease boundaries. My father spent years walking land that legally belongs to the men who killed him, writing down proof of what they did, and he never sent it to me. Never trusted me with it alive. Only dead, by courier." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated him, hated the dead man most of all. "Why should I carry it? Why should Okoro's daughter pay for it? Let them have the damn thing. Let it rot in a vault with everything else they've buried."

Jude was silent for a moment. Rain drummed on the tin roof, and in the half-light his face was all planes and shadow.

"Because he paid a village's debts," he said. "Did you know that? Upriver, years ago—an entire village's debts, quietly, no name attached. I flew the ledger once. I never understood it until this week." He met her eyes. "I'm not asking you to forgive him. God knows I'm the wrong man to preach forgiveness—I've been running from one promise or another my whole life. I'm telling you what I told myself at three this morning, staring at the ceiling: there are two things in that oilskin. A treasure map, and a testimony. Voss is paying for the treasure. But the testimony doesn't belong to Kavera, and it doesn't belong to us. It belongs to the people he wrote about. And they're still up there, Mara. Whatever's left of them. Somebody owes them the truth, and the somebody is us."

Outside, the rain was slackening. Through the gap in the curtains Mara could see the harbor emerging again, gunmetal going to silver, the fishing boats already putting back out because the sea didn't care about syndicates or Fridays and neither, ultimately, did she.

"Friday," she said at last. "He gave us until Friday."

"Which means we leave tonight."

She looked at him—at the tired gray eyes, the fists finally loose at his sides, the man who belonged to nobody deciding, apparently, to belong to this. She thought of the tea chest, and the rice, and the oilskin packet with her father's cramped handwriting sleeping inside it like something not quite dead.

"There's a fuel depot on the point," she said. "Okoro deals with them. They'll sell to me without questions—but Voss said they'd report us."

"They'll report a Cessna buying drums of avgas," Jude said, and for the first time since he'd walked into her life dripping rainwater and death, he smiled, and it changed his whole face. "So we won't buy drums. Go get the map, Mara. I'll handle the fuel."

Above the harbor, far off across the water, thunder rolled in from the open sea, and the light began to fail.
