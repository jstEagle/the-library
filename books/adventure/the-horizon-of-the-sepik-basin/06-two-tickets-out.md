## 6. Two Tickets Out

The rain came off the Bismarck Sea in the late afternoon, the way it always did in Wewak, and Jude watched it hammer the corrugated roof of the fuel depot office while pretending to read a weather sheet that had been pinned to the wall since before the war. The paper had gone soft as cloth. Somewhere behind him, a radio crackled through static, and the depot manager—a heavy, sweating man named Bruno whose loyalties were as fluid as the harbor—was taking his time with the pump.

"You want full tanks," Bruno said. It wasn't a question. His eyes stayed on the manifest he was filling out by hand, each letter drawn slow and careful, like a man writing something down for later. For someone.

"Full tanks," Jude agreed. "And I'm in a hurry."

"Everyone's in a hurry." Bruno licked his pencil. "You flying alone tonight?"

Jude felt the question settle against his skin like a mosquito he couldn't slap. *You flying alone tonight.* Three hours ago, no one in Wewak would have needed to ask. Three hours ago, Jude Almeida was the neutral man, the ferryman, the one who hauled diesel and penicillin and tinned beef up the Sepik and asked nothing of anyone and told nothing to anyone, and neutrality was worth more than gold in this country because it meant every faction could use you and none of them needed to kill you.

Then Mara Wren had thrown a coffee cup at a Kavera representative's head in front of six witnesses, and neutrality had died quietly on the floor of a trading post office along with the cup.

"I'm flying alone," Jude said. "Just me and some freight."

Bruno wrote it down. He didn't believe it, and Jude didn't expect him to. The Kavera man—the polished one, the one who smiled like a hotel brochure—had already been by the depot once today. Jude had seen his white Land Cruiser parked outside when he'd come to check the Cessna's tie-downs, and he'd seen the way Bruno stood a little straighter, talked a little faster, when the visitor left. Money moved through Wewak like the tide. You could watch it come in.

*Two hours,* Jude thought. Maybe less. The polite man from the Consortium had made his offer to Mara that morning, generous enough to be insulting, and when she refused he had made the threat explicit enough to be legal-adjacent: *Miss Wren, items of interest to Kavera Mining have a way of becoming company property. I'd hate for you to become an item of interest yourself.* And then he had looked past her shoulder, directly at Jude, and inclined his head a fraction—an acknowledgment, a courtesy, a promise—and walked out into the sunlight where his men were waiting.

Jude had spent the afternoon doing arithmetic. Fuel: forty minutes to pump if Bruno wasn't stalling, which he was. Pre-flight: fifteen minutes if he rushed it, which he would. Distance to the strip: eight minutes' walk, or four if they ran. Sunset: 6:41. The window between sunset and the point where someone with money and patience decided the polite approach had failed was exactly as wide as a knife blade.

"Mara," he said quietly, when she came in out of the rain, hood up, the oilskin map wrapped flat against her ribs beneath her jacket. She looked like she hadn't slept, which she hadn't, and like she was angry about everything, which she was. Grief had curdled in her over the last two days into something harder than sorrow—something with edges. Jude recognized it. He'd carried a version of it himself for years, back when running still felt like a plan.

"Bruno," she said, nodding at the manager without warmth.

"Miss Wren." Bruno's smile was wide and oily. "Terrible business, your father."

Something crossed her face—fast, quickly buried. "Yes," she said. "Terrible."

Jude counted the seconds while the pump ran. Forty gallons. Thirty. He watched the road through the depot's open front, where the rain came down in gray curtains and the potholes filled like small lakes. No Land Cruiser yet. But there would be. There was a version of this night where they simply walked out to the airstrip and took off and that was the whole story, and Jude did not believe in that version. Men like the Consortium's representative did not spend money on silence and then let the merchandise fly away unescorted.

"Tanks are full," Bruno announced, too loudly, and tore off the manifest copy. "That'll be—" and he named a figure thirty percent above the posted rate.

Jude paid it in cash without blinking, because arguing cost time, and because he wanted Bruno to remember him as a man who paid and asked nothing, a boring man, a forgettable one. "Keep the change for the rain," he said, and saw Bruno's eyes flick—just once, just briefly—toward the telephone on the desk behind him.

There it is, Jude thought. There's the sale.

* * *

They ran for the airstrip.

Not sprinting—that would draw eyes—but walking fast with purpose, heads down under the rain, Mara's hand gripping the strap of her single bag while Jude carried the rest. The Cessna sat at the far end of the apron where he'd parked it deliberately, nose into the wind, tied down under the one working floodlight that buzzed and flickered like something dying. The light was a problem. Light meant visibility, and visibility meant that anyone watching the field—which someone was; Jude could feel it the way you feel a stare through a wall—would see everything.

He untied the aircraft with hands that knew the knots in the dark, and Mara climbed into the right seat without being told, folding herself around the map the way she'd been doing for two days, like a woman guarding a wound.

"Door," Jude said, and she pulled it shut, and the rain drummed on the roof and turned the world outside the windshield into smeared yellow light.

He was halfway through the pre-start checks, running the flow by touch, when the headlights came.

They came fast up the access road—two vehicles, high-beams cutting through the downpour, no attempt at subtlety. Jude's stomach dropped through the floor of himself and kept going. He hit the master switch, heard the whine of the starter, felt the old Lycoming catch on the second blade and roar into life, rough and magnificent, shaking the whole airframe.

"What is it?" Mara shouted over the engine.

"Our escort." Jude pushed the throttle forward and the Cessna lurched off the tie-down spot, tail swinging, tires hissing through standing water. Behind them the headlights fanned across the apron, and in the mirror of the rain-streaked windshield he caught shapes moving—men getting out of the trucks, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who believed the night belonged to them.

"They're not shooting," Mara said. Her voice was tight but steady. He gave her credit for that. Most people's first gunfire-adjacent experience turned their voice to porridge.

"Not here. Too many witnesses, and Kavera likes its paperwork clean." He taxied hard toward the runway threshold, water spraying off the tires. "Out there, over the ranges, in the dark—different story. A missing aircraft, a missing pilot, a missing daughter. Very tidy. Very sad. Weather-related, probably."

Ahead, the runway lights—such as they were, three kerosene lamps and a faith-based arrangement—glowed dimly through the rain. And parked beside the threshold, angled carelessly across the grass shoulder, sat a third vehicle. A pickup. With a man standing beside it, smoking under an umbrella, positioned precisely where a departing aircraft would need to line up.

Jude understood it all in one cold flash. They weren't trying to stop the takeoff. They were making sure the takeoff happened on *their* terms—or making sure that if it happened badly, it happened very visibly. Or the man was simply there to confirm the departure, note the heading, radio it ahead. Every option ended the same way: somewhere ahead of them, in the dark, someone would be waiting to know exactly where a blue-and-white Cessna 185 went after leaving Wewak at night in the rain.

"He'll move," Mara said.

"He won't."

Jude lined up anyway. Full flaps. Mixture rich. The engine bellowed and the rain hammered and the man under the umbrella didn't move an inch, just stood there with his cigarette glowing, close enough now that Jude could see the wet shine of his face, the earpiece wire running down inside his collar. Professional. Patient. Certain that no bush pilot in the world would roll a loaded aircraft at a human being standing on his runway.

Jude advanced the throttle to the stop.

"Cessna," Mara said sharply. "*Cessna.*"

"I see him."

The engine howled. The airspeed needle crawled—ten knots, twenty, thirty—and the man's cigarette stopped moving, and Jude watched him understand, in the last four seconds, that this particular bush pilot was not a reasonable man tonight. The umbrella went sideways. The man dove for the ditch, and the Cessna's left main tire passed through the space where he'd been standing close enough to clip the pickup's mirror, and then the wheels left the ground and Wewak dissolved beneath them into rain and lamplight and the receding fury of headlights.

Mara exhaled a long, shaking breath. "You could have killed him."

"He could have killed us. We're even." Jude banked gently southeast, away from the coast, climbing into the murk. "Hold on. It gets worse before it gets better."

* * *

It got worse.

The storm cell sat over the coastal range like something with intentions, and Jude threaded them through it by instrument and instinct and twenty years of knowing these mountains the way other men know their own kitchens. Lightning strobed the cloud walls. Rain came across the windscreen horizontal. The old Cessna groaned and bucked, and twice the updrafts slammed them upward so hard the seat belts bit, and Mara—who had said nothing during the takeoff, who had said nothing through the worst of it—finally shouted, over the engine and the thunder:

"Are we going to die?"

"Statistically unlikely!"

"That's not comforting!"

"It's not supposed to be! Comforting isn't in my price range!" He fed in correction, rode a downdraft like a falling elevator, and punched out the far side of the cell into sudden, shocking starlight, the cloud deck falling away below them like a torn silver sea. The mountains rolled underneath, black and enormous, moonlit along their spines.

For a while neither of them spoke. The engine settled into its steady drone. Mara's hands slowly loosened on the armrests, finger by finger, and Jude pretended not to notice, the way he pretended not to notice most things, because noticing was a currency he'd learned not to spend.

"We should talk about the route," he said finally.

"Should we." Flat. Dangerous.

"The direct line to the highlands crosses two valleys where Kavera has exploration leases. If they've got radios—and they do—we don't cross their country. We go east first, up the Sepik, land at Ambunti, refuel, hire a guide."

"A guide." She turned in her seat to look at him, and even in the dim red glow of the panel lights he could see the exhaustion and the fury fighting for control of her face. "You want to add a stranger. To this. To the thing that six armed men tried to stop us from doing an hour ago."

"I want to add someone who knows the passes. Because I don't. Because your father's map marks ground above the snowline, and I've never been above the snowline, and neither have you, and the pass only opens when the glacier calves, which happens for maybe three days, and if we miss it we wait a year—if we're alive to wait."

"And you trust whoever we find in Ambunti?"

"No," Jude said. "I trust my ability to watch people. Mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"There's no version of this where we do it alone, Mara. I'm sorry. Your father drew that map for a reason, and the reason is that the highlands eat loners."

She looked away, out at the black mountains, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone quieter, and somehow that was worse. "He chose the gold over me. Fifteen years. One letter a year, if that, and half of them were about weather. And now he's dead in your airstrip shed, and I'm carrying his map through the dark for men with guns, and everyone keeps telling me what Elias wanted. Nobody has once asked what I want."

The honesty of it landed harder than any argument. Jude flew for a moment in silence, watching the moonlight run the ridgelines.

"What do you want?" he asked.

She was quiet so long he thought she wouldn't answer. "I want to know why," she said finally. "Before I decide anything else. Why the gold mattered more. Why he ran. What he saw out there that made him the way he was." Her jaw tightened. "And I want the men who shot him to know that his daughter carried it home. That's what I want."

It was the closest thing to a mission statement Jude had heard from her, and it scared him more than the storm had, because it meant she wasn't going to quit, and it meant he wasn't either, and it meant the two of them were now bound to the same reckless trajectory whether he liked it or not.

"Then that's the plan," he said.

"Don't patronize me."

"I'm not. I'm agreeing with you. It's disorienting for both of us."

Despite herself—despite everything—something twitched at the corner of her mouth. It vanished immediately, disciplined, but Jude had seen it, and he filed it away next to the memory of her voice staying level during the takeoff, and realized with a kind of dread that he was beginning to catalog her. That he was beginning to *notice*, spending that hoarded currency freely, and that somewhere over the Sepik Basin at eleven thousand feet he had stopped being a neutral man in every sense that mattered.

They argued about the rest. About whether to fly the river at night or hole up until dawn (night; the dawn belonged to Kavera). About whether the map should stay on her body or split between them (split; she hated the logic and accepted it). About Toma—whom they hadn't met yet, whom Jude had only heard described by a missionary with a taste for understatement—but Mara preemptively distrusted him on principle, and Jude defended the hypothetical stranger purely to be contrary, and both of them knew the argument was really about something else entirely, something with no name that sat in the cockpit between them like a third passenger, quiet and patient and growing.

Below them the Sepik appeared at last, a vast brown serpent gleaming under the moon, coiling through the darkness of the basin. Jude began the descent toward Ambunti, throttling back, the strip lights—two fires lit by the mission, God bless them—flickering ahead.

"Approaching the strip," he said. "Brace a little. It's grass, it's wet, and it's short."

"Is anything about you not short and wet?"

He laughed—actually laughed, a surprised bark of sound—and felt her glance at him, quick and assessing, and pretend she hadn't.

The wheels touched, bounced, touched again, and the Cessna rolled out through the wet grass under the stars while somewhere behind them, in Wewak, a man with an earpiece was already dialing a number, and somewhere ahead of them, in a river town that smelled of woodsmoke and fish, a guide with two names was waking to the news that strangers were coming and work was available.

Jude cut the mixture and let the propeller spin down into silence. In the sudden quiet, crickets roared from the darkness beyond the fires, and Mara sat looking out at the black wall of the highlands rising against the stars—the country where her father had died, the country that had erased a village and kept the secret—and Jude watched her watch it and understood that whatever waited up there, they were going to meet it together, and that neither of them had agreed to it, and that it no longer mattered.

"Welcome to Ambunti," he said.

She unbuckled. "It smells terrible."

"Wait till morning."
