## 7. The Guide with Two Names

The river town announced itself by smell before it appeared at all—woodsmoke and diesel and the green rot of the Sepik's backwaters, a smell Jude had come to think of as the true national anthem of the country. Then the bend opened and there it was: Angoram, strung along the river like beads on a wire, its stilt houses leaning over brown water, its single street a ribbon of mud and crushed coral, its market already loud even at seven in the morning.

Jude brought the Cessna down on the grass strip east of town with the particular care the strip deserved. It was soft in the middle after last night's rain, and the wheels sank an inch before the ground firmed up. Somewhere in the tall kunai grass at the edge of the clearing, something large moved away from them without hurry. Everything out here moved without hurry, except the river.

"You landed like you were setting down a sleeping child," Mara said from the right seat.

"I've landed worse things in worse places." Jude cut the mixture and let the propeller wind down into silence. "A piano, once. Don't ask."

"I was going to ask."

"Then don't."

It had become their language over the two days since Wewak: short sentences, dry as matches, struck quickly and thrown down while still burning. They had not spoken about the way Mara had gripped Jude's arm during the storm cell they'd threaded north of the coast, or the way Jude had watched her sleep against the window of a mission guesthouse, or any of the other things that were accumulating between them like silt in a slow current. There wasn't time for silt. There was only the map, folded now inside Mara's shirt against her skin because she didn't trust the aircraft, the fuel drums, or Jude—which was fair, since Jude wasn't sure she should.

They walked into town through the market, and the market did what markets in the Sepik always did: it looked at them, assessed them, priced them, and then went back to its business. A woman sold them hot tea in dented tins. A boy tried to sell Jude a cassowary egg the size of his own head. An old man selling dried fish looked at Mara's face for a long moment, then at the sky, then said nothing at all, which in Jude's experience was the loudest kind of statement.

"The guide," Mara said, when they'd found shade under the awning of the trade store. "How do we find one who isn't going to sell us?"

"You don't. You find one whose price you can afford, and you hope he likes you more than he likes money."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." Jude turned the tea tin slowly in their hands. "There's a man here. Toma. He's guided for missionaries, for survey teams, for a couple of expeditions I flew supplies for. He knows the high passes better than anyone on this stretch of river. He also has—" Jude hesitated.

"A second name," Mara said. "Everyone here keeps telling me about him. The man with two names. What does that mean?"

"It means nobody agrees on what his real name is. Some say Toma is the Christian name and the other one is the clan name. Some say the opposite. Some say neither is real and he collects names the way other men collect debts." Jude shrugged. "What I know is that three years ago he carried a sick patrol officer down from the Star Mountains on his back for six days, and the officer lived. And that last year he refused a contract from a mining survey team and nobody knows why, and nobody asks him twice."

Mara was quiet for a moment. "You want to trust him."

"I want to *need* him," Jude said. "Trusting him would be a luxury. We can't cross the range without someone who knows where the pass opens, and the pass opens when it wants to, not when maps say it should. Elias knew that. That's half of why he drew the thing the way he did—it's not a map so much as a set of arguments with the mountains."

* * *

They found Toma where everyone said he would be, which was nowhere in particular and everywhere at once: sitting on the veranda rail of the trade store like a man who owned both the rail and the store and possibly the river itself, talking with four people at once in three languages, laughing at something in Tok Pisin, answering a question in what might have been Ambunti dialect, and gesturing with a betel nut in his hand as though conducting an orchestra only he could hear.

He was perhaps fifty, built like the root buttress of a rain tree—broad, low, immovable—and he wore a faded rugby jersey with the number 9 cracked across the back, shorts, and rubber thongs that had been repaired with wire. His hair was grey-shot and cropped close, and his face had the specific geography of a man who had spent decades being looked at and had decided, long ago, exactly how much of himself to show in return.

When he saw Jude, he stopped mid-sentence, mid-laugh, mid-gesture, and looked at them the way a man looks at weather coming over a ridge.

"Jude Almeida." He said it slowly, tasting each syllable. "Pilot. Flyer. The one who belongs to no one." He slid off the rail and came forward, and his handshake was dry and strong and lasted exactly as long as it needed to. "I heard you were dead."

"You heard wrong."

"I hear many things wrong. It keeps life interesting." His eyes went past Jude to Mara, and something shifted in them—not recognition, but the *search* for recognition, the flicker of a man matching a face against faces he had known. "And this one?"

"Mara Wren."

Something happened then that Jude would turn over for days afterward. Toma's face did not change. But behind his face, something changed—the way light changes behind a closed door when someone walks past it in the corridor. He knew the name. Or he knew something adjacent to the name. And he filed it away in less than a heartbeat, behind a smile that arrived like a curtain being drawn.

"Wren," he repeated pleasantly. "Good name. Strong name." He turned back to Jude. "You need a guide."

It wasn't a question. Jude hadn't said a word about needing anything.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you came to Angoram instead of flying over it. Nobody lands here unless they need something the air can't give them." He spread his hands. "Also, you have the look. The look of people carrying something heavy that isn't in their bags."

Mara spoke for the first time. "We need to cross the range. Before the snows close the high pass."

Toma looked at her for a long time. Too long. Long enough that Jude felt the conversation tilt, like a canoe taking water on one side.

"The high pass," Toma said. "Nobody crosses the high pass. The high pass is a rumor the mountains tell to make fools walk."

"Elias Wren crossed it."

The name dropped into the space between them like a stone into a well, and Jude listened for the splash and heard—nothing. No echo. Toma's face gave back nothing at all now, smooth as river clay.

"Elias Wren," Toma said again, and this time the name sounded different in his mouth, handled differently, like an object he was turning over to check for markings. "Yes. I know this name too." He smiled, and the smile reached his eyes and stopped there, like a traveler reaching a border. "Come. Walk with me. If we are going to lie to each other, we should do it walking. It's healthier than sitting."

* * *

He led them along the riverbank, past canoes overturned like sleeping animals, past children who shouted greetings at him in a language Jude couldn't place and got shouts back in another. He talked the whole way—about the river, about the price of crocodile skins, about a missionary who had once paid him in canned herrings and been robbed blind by his own boat crew—but underneath the talk, Jude could feel him working, the way you feel a current under a calm surface. He was asking questions without asking them. Where had they flown from. When. How many people knew they were here. Whether the Cessna was fast. Whether it was watched.

And twice—only twice—he let a question surface all the way into the open air:

"Who else looks for you?"

"No one," Jude said.

Toma glanced at them sideways, and his expression was almost gentle. "In my experience, when people say *no one*, it means *someone*, and the someone is close enough that saying the name feels dangerous." He stepped over a mooring rope without looking at it. "I will take you across the range. But understand me. I do not guide liars, and I do not guide dead men, and the difference between the two is often only a matter of timing. So. Tell me true, or tell me nothing, and I will decide which I believe."

Mara stopped walking. She stood facing the river, arms folded, and Jude watched her make the calculation—the same one she'd been making since Wewak, since the polished man from Kavera had slid his card across the table and made his threat sound like a courtesy. Trust no one fully. Spend trust like ammunition.

"My father drew a map," she said finally. "It shows gold ground in the highlands. Ground the Kavera Consortium leases. Ground where—" her voice caught, steadied "—ground where a village used to be. Men from Kavera killed my father yesterday. Or the day before. They'll kill us too if they catch us. We're taking the map to people who can use it."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere downstream a motorized banana boat droned.

Toma looked at her for a long moment, and then he laughed—a real laugh, sudden and full, startling a flock of birds out of a riverside pandanus.

"You tell me everything," he said, delighted. "Everything! In the middle of a public path!" He wiped his eyes. "Either you are very wise or very foolish, and I have not yet decided which, and honestly, neither have you." He sobered as quickly as he'd laughed. "Kavera." He said the word the way other men spat. "Yes. I know Kavera. Everyone on this river knows Kavera. Kavera builds roads and closes mouths. Kavera pays school fees and buys silence. You know what they offered me last year? To guide their survey men to the same ground your father drew. Good money. Money with weight to it." He held up one finger. "I said no."

"Why?" Mara asked.

Toma was quiet for a moment. He looked out at the river, at the brown water carrying everything downstream—logs, leaves, a drowned frigate bird, things better left unnamed—and when he answered, his voice had changed, gone quieter, older.

"Because they asked me to guide them somewhere I had been before," he said. "Somewhere I had seen what men do when they think no law reaches them. And I said no. And they said, *think about your family, Toma. Think about your brother.*" He turned back to them, and his smile was back in place, but it sat on his face like a mask sits, slightly apart from the skin. "So. Now you know my price and my weakness, both. I am expensive and I am breakable. Every man is one or the other. Most of us are both."

Jude felt the words settle somewhere cold in their chest. *Think about your brother.* It was said lightly, tossed off like the rest of his talk—but Jude had spent a lifetime listening to what people said lightly, because the light things were usually load-bearing.

"We can pay you," Jude said. "Not much. Fuel money, some cash, and whatever the mission stations owe me in favors between here and the foothills."

"I don't want your money." Toma waved it away before it was even offered. "Money spends. Debts travel. I want a debt—from you, flyer, and from you, daughter of Wren—that follows you wherever you go. One day I will call it in. Maybe it costs you a flight. Maybe it costs you more. You will pay it without asking what it's for." He extended his hand. "That is my fee."

Mara looked at Jude. Jude looked at the hand—broad, scarred across the knuckles, steady as a bridge piling—and thought about every choice that had brought them to this riverbank, and how every one of them had been some version of *there is no one else.*

"That's a dangerous fee," Jude said.

"All good fees are." Toma's grin flashed white. "Do we shake, or do I go back to my veranda and you go back to dying somewhere inconvenient?"

* * *

They shook. Then they spent the afternoon preparing, and Toma prepared the way he seemed to do everything—at speed, in all directions at once, issuing instructions in three languages to people who appeared from nowhere and vanished the same way. A dugout canoe materialized, forty feet long, hollowed from a single kwila log, with a tarpaulin shelter amidships and an outboard engine that Toma kicked affectionately and called *his second wife*. Rice and tinned fish and betel nut appeared. A young man appeared with a coil of climbing rope and disappeared again. By late afternoon it was arranged: they would leave at first light, run the river two days up into the tributaries, then abandon the canoe where the water grew too thin and start climbing.

Jude spent the evening checking the Cessna a final time, tying it down against the coming rain, and found Mara sitting on the wheel chock in the last light, turning the oilskin packet over in her hands.

"You told him everything," Jude said. "On the path. After I spent two days teaching you to tell no one anything."

"I know." She didn't look up. "It felt like a test. Not of him. Of me. Like if I kept hedging, I'd become the kind of person who hedges forever." She looked up then, and the last light caught the exhaustion and the grief and the stubbornness all mixed together in her face. "Was I wrong?"

Jude sat down on the grass beside the chock. Somewhere across the strip, a frog began its evening argument with the world.

"I don't know," Jude said honestly. "I've been wrong about people in both directions. I trusted a man in Port Moresby once who sold my route schedule to a logging company for a case of beer. I distrusted a missionary for three years who turned out to be the only honest person in the valley." They picked a blade of grass and rolled it between their fingers. "Here's what I know about Toma. He carried a dying man six days through the Star Mountains. He turned down Kavera's money once. And just now, when he named his fee, he named a debt instead of cash, and debts mean he expects to see us alive again. Dead clients don't pay debts."

"That's either very comforting or very grim."

"Both. Welcome to the Sepik."

Mara almost smiled. Then it faded, and she looked out toward the dark line of the town, where a few kerosene lamps had begun to glow in the stilt houses, orange points reflected doubled in the black water.

"He knew Dad's name," she said quietly. "Before I said it. I saw it happen—something moved behind his face. And when I told him about the massacre, he didn't ask *which village*. Did you notice that? I never said the name. He never asked." She turned to Jude, and her voice was steady but her eyes weren't. "He already knew which village, Jude. Everyone on this river probably knows which village. Which means everyone on this river knows what Kavera did, and everyone on this river has decided to survive it instead of fight it. Can you blame them?"

"No," Jude said. "But I've noticed that surviving and living aren't the same thing, and the difference between them is usually a person who refuses to be quiet."

Across the strip, a lamp moved in the dark—one of the stilt houses near the water, a figure crossing a veranda, pausing, looking out toward the airstrip. Looking, Jude thought, for a long moment, directly at them. Then the lamp moved on, and it was probably nothing. Out here it was usually nothing, until suddenly it wasn't.

* * *

Toma did not sleep at the trade store that night. He told the boy minding the counter he was going to check his nets, which was true in the sense that all his lies were true in some sense; he did own nets, and he did intend to look at them, eventually, after.

He walked through the dark town to the little shack behind the fuel depot where the radio lived—the old HF set the Council had installed years ago and mostly forgotten, that the depot manager used to call barge schedules and, occasionally, other things. The manager owed Toma. Everyone owed Toma. That was the architecture of his whole life: a web of small debts so dense that no one could ever quite see what he himself owed, or to whom.

He keyed the mic and raised the frequency he had sworn, a year ago, he would never use again. Static, then a voice, flat and urban and wrong for this river, a Port Moresby accent wearing a work shirt.

"Identify."

"Toma." He said his other name then, the one nobody in Angoram used, the one that belonged to a younger man in a different life, before the six-day carry, before the word *no*. "I have something for you. Two travelers. They carry a paper. They fly a white Cessna. They move at first light, upriver, toward the tributaries."

Silence on the line, the crackle of distance. Then: "The woman. Describe the woman."

Toma closed his eyes. Behind them he saw a river, a burned village, a brother's face, a debt with teeth.

"Dark hair," he said. "She carries herself like her father. You'd know the blood if you saw it."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came back, it had changed—had gone careful, interested, hungry.

"Stay with them, Toma. Stay close. There's a bonus in it. A big one."

"There always is," Toma said, and cut the transmission, and stood a long while in the dark shack listening to the radio tick as it cooled, feeling the shape of what he had just done settle onto his shoulders like a wet tarpaulin.

Outside, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, patient, brown, carrying everything downstream. First light was five hours away. The canoe would leave with the dawn, three travelers aboard, and the fourth passenger—the one no one had invited, the one made of radio waves and money—would ride with them all the way into the mountains.

Toma walked home through the sleeping town, and if his step was heavier than usual, there was no one awake to see it, and no one, he told himself, who needed to.
