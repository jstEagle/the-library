## 8. Upriver

The river did not care about grief. That was the first thing Mara Wren understood about the Sepik, watching it slide brown and muscular beneath the dugout's bow, carrying silt from mountains she could not see toward a coast she had left behind. It moved with the patience of something that had been moving forever, indifferent to the small cargo of humans riding its surface—one white man at the stern paddle, one guide who smiled too easily, and herself, holding a dead father's map against her chest like a child holds a wound.

Three days out of the river town, and her arms had stopped burning. Now they simply ached, a deep dull throb that lived in the muscle the way the ache of Elias now lived somewhere behind her ribs—not sharp anymore, just present, a weight she would carry whether she wanted to or not.

"Water ahead," Toma called from the bow, where he crouched with the ease of a man born in canoes. "Big village past the bend. We take water, maybe trade for fish. You keep quiet about why we travel."

Jude said nothing from the stern. He'd said very little all morning. His paddle moved in long unhurried strokes, and his eyes never stopped working—reading the current, reading the banks, reading Toma's back in a way he probably thought was subtle and wasn't.

Mara had learned to read Jude Almeida too, these three days. She'd learned that his silence wasn't emptiness but fullness—a man constantly calculating, constantly weighing, and saying only a tenth of what he thought. She'd learned that when he looked at her, there was something in it that made her want to look away and something that made her want to be seen. Both of those things annoyed her intensely.

The village appeared around the bend the way villages on this river appeared—suddenly, as if the forest had exhaled and there they were: stilt houses leaning over the water, smoke rising thin through the palms, crocodile carvings on the gable posts staring down at the canoe with ancient indifference. Children ran along the walkways shouting. Dogs barked themselves hoarse and then lost interest.

Toma handled the trading. He was good at it—too good, Mara thought, watching him move through the crowd with his easy laugh and his pidgin that slid between languages like water between stones. Within twenty minutes they had water in jerry cans, two smoked fish wrapped in banana leaf, and a bag of ripe mangoes that smelled like heaven.

It was while Toma bargained over the fish that Mara made her mistake.

An old woman sat on the lowest platform of the nearest house, mending a net, her hands moving with the automatic grace of seventy years of knots. Her face was a landscape of ridges, and her eyes, when they lifted to Mara, were sharp as a bird's. Mara smiled at her—the reflexive smile of a stranger trying not to be a stranger—and the old woman smiled back, toothless and sudden, and patted the planking beside her.

Mara went. It was foolish. She knew it was foolish even as she sat down, because Jude was already turning from the canoe, already frowning, already three steps too far away to stop what happened next.

"You come from downriver," the old woman said. Her trade English was slow and precise, each word set down like a stone in a path. "You go up. Nobody goes up now."

"We're going into the highlands," Mara said. "Through the pass."

The old woman's hands stopped moving. The whole platform seemed to grow quieter, though nothing had actually changed—the children still shouted, the dogs still barked—but Mara felt the attention of the house shift, the way water shifts when something large moves beneath it.

"The pass is closed," the old woman said.

"Not yet. The glacier hasn't calved."

Something flickered across the seamed face—not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. "You know glacier words." The old woman studied her. "Who taught you the high country?"

"My father. Some." Mara heard her own voice go strange. "Elias Wren. He was a prospector. He knew this river years ago."

The name landed on the platform like a stone dropped into still water.

Around them, the village noise continued, but near them—within earshot of the house, within sight of the women mending nets, the men repairing a hull—it simply stopped. Conversations didn't end; they just lowered below hearing, the way birdsong stops before a storm. A man who had been walking toward the trading area changed direction without appearing to decide to. Two boys who had been staring at the strangers suddenly found urgent business elsewhere.

Mara felt the silence close around her name like a hand.

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she took up her net again, and her voice, when it came, was pitched so low that Mara had to lean close to catch it.

"You are his daughter."

"Yes."

"Elias Wren." The old woman said the name carefully, tasting it, the way you taste a word from a language you once spoke fluently and have half forgotten. "He came here. Long time now. Six years. Seven." Her hands worked the net. "He came with sickness in his leg. Bad cut, gone rotten. Our medicine man pulled the poison out of him. He stayed one moon."

"I didn't know that," Mara said. And she hadn't. In all the years of her childhood—years that had ended when Elias walked out of them—she had never known anything about where he went or what he did. He had been a man who existed in postmarks and occasional money orders and her mother's bitter silence. A ghost who sent ten dollars at Christmas.

"He paid," the old woman said.

"Paid?"

"Our debts. All the village debts. To the trade store at Angoram, to the mission, to the men downriver who lend money with teeth." The old woman's eyes came up and held hers. "Every family in this village owed somebody something. In one day, nobody owed anybody. Elias Wren paid it all. With gold dust, in a tin. He said—" she paused, searching for the shape of the memory in another language, "—he said he owed a debt too. Bigger than money. He wouldn't say to who."

Mara sat very still. The river moved behind her, enormous and indifferent. Somewhere a child laughed, and the sound seemed to come from another world.

"That doesn't sound like my father," she said finally, and hated how small her voice was.

The old woman shrugged, an eloquent lifting of shoulders that contained entire philosophies. "Men are rivers," she said. "What you see at the mouth is not what comes down from the headwaters." She bent again to her net, then paused. "But I tell you one more thing, daughter of Elias. When the Kavera men came—two years back, asking questions, showing papers, buying words the way they buy everything—we told them we never saw any prospector. Every family said it. Even the ones whose debts he paid, even the ones who forgot. We said it together, like one throat."

"Why?" Mara asked. "Why lie for him?"

The old woman's needle paused. She looked out at the river, at the brown water carrying everything toward the sea, and her face was unreadable as carved wood.

"Because the Kavera men burned a village upriver," she said. "A whole village. Old people, children, everyone who would not sign their paper. And because a debt paid in gold is a small thing, but a debt paid in silence—" she touched her own chest, "—that one stays paid."

She went back to her netting. The audience was over; Mara understood that the way you understand a door has closed. She rose, thanked her in the few phrases of courtesy Toma had taught her, and walked back toward the canoe on legs that felt borrowed.

Jude was waiting by the gunwale, and his expression told her he'd seen at least some of it—the silence, the way the village had closed around her like a fist.

"What happened?"

"She knew him." Mara climbed into the dugout and took up her paddle because her hands needed something to do. "My father. He stayed here once. Sick. And he—" She stopped. Started again. "He paid off every debt in the village. Every single one. With gold dust."

Jude was quiet for a moment. "That sounds like a lot of gold dust."

"It sounds like a different man." Mara drove the paddle into the water harder than she needed to. "The man I knew couldn't stay in a room for a month. Couldn't hold a job, couldn't hold a conversation, couldn't hold—" She stopped herself. The list was going somewhere ugly, somewhere that ended with *couldn't hold a daughter*, and she wasn't going to say that in front of Jude Almeida, not yet, maybe not ever. "He was a man who ran. That's what I knew. That's all I knew."

"And now?"

"Now I find out he was also a man who—" She gestured helplessly at the receding village, at the stilt houses and the crocodile posts and the old woman bending over her net. "Who carried a sick man's weight for a month and paid a village free of debt and never told anyone. Never told *me*."

The canoe slid out into the current. Toma took the bow again, humming something tuneless, and the village fell away behind them, its smoke thinning into the green haze of the shore.

They paddled through the afternoon, and Mara thought. That was the trouble with rivers—they gave you nothing to do but paddle and think, and thinking was the last thing she wanted company for.

Because here was the problem, and she turned it over and over like a stone with something underneath: if Elias had been capable of that—if he'd been capable of staying a month among strangers, of generosity on that scale, of *gratitude* deep enough to empty a tin of gold dust over strangers' debts—then the man who had left her mother, who had missed her graduation, who had sent money orders instead of himself—that man had been a choice. Not a condition. Not some incurable restlessness written into his blood. A choice, made over and over, year after year, and she had been on the losing side of it.

And yet.

And yet the old woman's face when she said his name. The village closing ranks like one throat. *We told them we never saw any prospector.* You didn't get that kind of loyalty from a transaction. You got it from something else, something slower, and Mara found she couldn't make the arithmetic work no matter which way she pushed the numbers.

"You're doing the thing," Jude said quietly, from behind her.

"What thing?"

"Where your shoulders climb up to your ears and you paddle like the river insulted you."

"My shoulders are fine."

"They've been fine for three days, which is how I know they're not." A pause. The paddle dipped and drew, dipped and drew, patient as breathing. "My mother used to say grief isn't one thing. It's a crowd. They all want a turn talking, and most of them are liars."

Mara almost laughed. "Your mother sounds smarter than you."

"She is. Everyone's mother is." Another pause, longer. "For what it's worth—the man who flew me that map, bleeding out on my strip—he wasn't running. Not at the end. He was heading *toward* something. Toward you. He just ran out of sky first."

Mara didn't answer. She couldn't. But her shoulders, traitorously, came down from her ears.

* * *

They camped that night on a sandbank island mid-channel, a sliver of pale ground between two arms of the dark water. Toma built a fire small enough to hide and cooked the smoked fish with wild ginger, and it was the best thing Mara had eaten since Wewak, and she ate it in silence while the river talked to itself in the dark.

Toma slept first, curled near the fire with the boneless ease of a man who could sleep anywhere, anytime—which, Mara was beginning to understand, was itself a kind of skill, the skill of a man with nothing heavy in him. Or nothing he admitted to.

Jude sat at the water's edge, cleaning the Cessna's spare fuel filter by lantern light, an act of maintenance so compulsive it was nearly devotional. Mara went and sat beside him. The sand was still warm from the day.

"The old woman said something else," Mara said. "Before I left. She said the Kavera men burned a village upriver. Two years ago. A whole village, because they wouldn't sign."

Jude's hands went still on the filter.

"And she said my father knew about it. That's why he ran from them. That's why he was hiding." Mara watched the firelight move on the black water. "Jude. What if the map isn't a treasure map? What if it's—"

"Don't finish that sentence yet." His voice was low, careful. "Not until we know. Because if it's what you're thinking, then it's not gold we're carrying into those mountains. It's evidence. And evidence changes what everyone will do to get it—including Toma."

Mara glanced at the sleeping guide. "You don't trust him."

"I trust that he wants to be trusted. That's different." Jude set the filter down and looked at her, and in the lantern light his face was open in a way she hadn't seen before—tired, and honest, and younger than his years. "I've spent ten years belonging to no one and nowhere, and telling myself that was freedom. Your father spent thirty years doing the same thing, and it got him killed alone on a dirt strip with a bullet in him, handing his last possession to a stranger." He shook his head slowly. "I don't want to be that story, Mara. I'm starting to really not want to be that story."

The river murmured. Out in the dark, something big surfaced and sank again, leaving a swirl of ripples that caught the firelight.

"Then don't be," Mara said.

It came out simpler than she meant it, and truer than she meant it, and for a moment neither of them breathed. Then Jude picked up the filter again, and Mara looked back at the fire, and neither of them said anything else, and nothing needed saying.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the river began to change.

The tributary narrowed. The banks steepened into clay walls furred with roots, and the current quickened, and the water—brown for days—took on a tea-colored clarity that meant mountain ground somewhere ahead. The air cooled. Mist hung in the gorge mouths in the early hours and refused to burn off.

And the villages changed too.

They passed three in a single day, and at each one the pattern repeated with variations that grew more unsettling. At the first, people waved from the walkways but would not come down to trade until Toma shouted assurances in a language Mara didn't know. At the second, the houses stood empty—not abandoned, Jude noted, pointing out the tended gardens on the ridge above, but deliberately vacated, the whole community gone silent and watchful somewhere in the trees. At the third, a single old man stood on the platform and called out to Toma in a cracked voice, and whatever he said made Toma's easy smile die on his face, and after that Toma paddled hard and didn't talk for two hours.

"What did he say?" Mara asked finally.

Toma's jaw worked. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "He asked if we were going to the gold ground." He kept his eyes on the channel ahead. "I told him no. We're going to the pass."

"Were you lying?"

Toma turned then, and gave her the smile—the real one, or what she'd decided was the real one, though she was no longer certain she could tell—and said, "In this country, missus, everybody lies a little. Question is what for."

But that night, camped under an overhang where the gorge pinched tight, Mara woke in the small hours to voices. Low ones. Toma's, and another voice answering from the darkness beyond the fire's dying glow—a voice with a radio's flat crackle buried inside it, or maybe she dreamed that part, maybe it was just the river talking in its sleep.

She lay still under the tarp, heart going hard, and listened until the voices stopped, and told herself in the morning light that she had imagined it.

Almost told herself.

Because when they broke camp, Toma checked his little transistor radio—checked it twice, turning the dial with his thumb—and then led them upriver toward the narrowing gorge where the map said the climbing began, and he walked, for the first time since she'd met him, like a man who already knew exactly where he was going.
