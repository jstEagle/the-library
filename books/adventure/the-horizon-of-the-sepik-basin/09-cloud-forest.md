## 9. Cloud Forest

The river gave them three more days and then took its toll.

They left the dugout at a bend where the Karawari narrowed into white water and the banks rose into country no engine could argue with. Toma beached the canoe beneath an overhang of pandanus, dragged brush across it with the unhurried competence of a man hiding something he'd hidden a hundred times, and pointed uphill with his chin.

"From here, we walk."

Jude looked at the hillside and saw no path at all — only a green wall rising into cloud, dripping, breathing, alive in the way that things are alive when nobody has ever asked them not to be. Somewhere above, the ridgeline they needed was invisible, wrapped in mist the color of old wool.

"How far to the tree line?"

Toma smiled, and it was the smile of a guide who has learned that tourists trust a smile more than an answer. "Two days if the weather is kind. Three if it isn't. In the cloud forest, the weather is never kind."

Mara said nothing. She had said very little since the river town, since the night she'd sat apart from the fire and Jude had heard her crying and pretended not to, because pretending not to hear was the only kindness she'd accept from him. She shouldered her pack without looking at either of them and started up the slope, and Jude watched her go and thought: she walks like him. Elias had walked like that too, the last morning, like a man with somewhere to be and no time to explain why.

* * *

The cloud forest did not welcome them. It absorbed them.

Within an hour Jude had lost all sense of direction. The canopy closed overhead in a cathedral of moss-hung limbs, and the light that filtered through came down green and sourceless, as though the forest itself were glowing. Everything was wet. Not rain exactly — the forest sweated. Moisture beaded on every surface and fell in fat, cold drops that found the gap between collar and neck with unerring accuracy. The ground was a foot of black rot over older rot, and each step sank to the ankle with a sound like a kiss.

The leeches found them by the second hour.

They came off the vegetation in silent ribbons, thin as threads until they fed, and Jude learned to stop every twenty minutes and strip them from his boots, his wrists, the back of his neck where he couldn't see. Toma carried a tin of salt and a cigarette, and burned the ones that had already attached, teaching Mara the trick of it with a flick of his lighter — "heat, not pulling, or the head stays in and the wound goes bad."

Mara flinched the first time, then didn't. By evening she was burning leeches off Toma's shoulders without asking, and Toma was telling her a story about a spirit that lived in the mist and stole the names of travelers, and Mara was laughing — actually laughing, a short, startled sound like a bird flushed from cover — and Jude, walking behind them with his pack straps cutting his shoulders, felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was knotted.

He didn't trust Toma. He wanted to, and that was worse. Wanting to trust a man was how a man got you.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of ground between two root buttresses, a tree so vast that its base was a wall. Toma built a fire under a lean-to of palm fronds, and the smoke rose straight up into the canopy and vanished, and the darkness beyond the firelight was so complete it felt structural, like the inside of the world.

Mara was asleep within minutes — the exhausted, graceless sleep of a body that has been overruled. Toma took first watch without being asked, settling against the root wall with his machete across his knees, and Jude lay staring up at the underside of the forest and found that sleep was not going to come for him.

The confession had been sitting in his chest for days. He could feel it the way you feel a splinter — a small wrong thing, working deeper.

He sat up. Toma's eyes caught the firelight, watchful, and Jude nodded toward the dark beyond the fire and said, quietly, "I need to tell her something. Wake me if anything moves."

He moved to where Mara lay, hesitated, then touched her shoulder. She came awake the way she did everything — instantly, completely, one hand finding the knife at her belt before her eyes were open.

"It's me. I have to tell you something. About your father. About the day he landed."

She sat up. In the firelight her face was hollowed, the cheekbones standing out, and Jude thought she looked less like Elias than she had in Wewak — the resemblance was in the hunger of her, not the geometry.

"You said he was bleeding out. You said he barely talked."

"He didn't. Not at first." Jude kept his voice low, under the sound of the dripping forest. "But he had maybe twenty minutes where the bleeding slowed, and he used them. And I want you to know what he said, because I've been carrying it wrong. I've been carrying it like a package. It's not a package. It's a message, and it's yours."

Mara pulled her knees up. "Tell me."

* * *

Jude had gone over it so many times in his head that the words had worn smooth, and saying them aloud felt like handling river stones.

"He came in low over the strip with his port wing dropping and smoke coming off the cowling, and he put it down on the worst landing I've ever seen anyone survive. I dragged him out of the wreck and he was gray, and I thought — I was sure — that was it. And he grabbed my shirt with both hands and he said, *'You fly for everyone out here. You fly for the missions, the miners, the crocodile men. You fly for everybody because you belong to nobody, and you think that keeps you safe.'*"

Mara's breath caught. Jude made himself keep going.

"And I said, 'Save your breath,' because I'm a coward, and he laughed. He actually laughed, with a bullet in him. And he said, *'Neutrality is just running with better manners, boy. I ran for twenty years and it caught me on a dirt strip in the middle of nowhere. Don't be me. Take the map to my daughter. Swear it.'*"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the black above them, something moved and went still.

"He made me swear," Jude said. "And I swore, because you don't refuse a dying man, and I've regretted it every day since — not the promise. The swearing. Because a promise you make to get a dying man to let go of your shirt isn't a promise, it's a transaction, and I told myself it was the same thing." He looked at his hands. "I've been telling myself a lot of things were the same thing for a long time."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. "What things?"

Jude had never said it out loud. Not to anyone. The missionaries knew he'd come up the coast twelve years ago with one duffel bag and no history, and they'd been too polite to invent one for him, and he'd let the silence stand, and the silence had hardened into a man.

"I had a life before the basin," he said. "Down south. I was engaged. Her name was Ruth. And there was an accident — not mine, not exactly mine, but I was the one flying, and there was a question about whether I should have been, and I never stayed to answer it. I left before the inquiry finished. Sold everything, flew north until the money ran out, and stayed where the money ran out." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it did on the radio when conditions were bad. "I told myself I was choosing a life of service. Supplying missions, hauling medicine. And some of that was even true. But mostly I was running, and the basin is a very good place to run to, because everyone here assumes you had your reasons and nobody asks what they were."

"Elias knew," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"Elias saw it in about four minutes. That's what the map was, partly — I understand that now. He wasn't just handing me coordinates. He was handing me a mirror. *You belong to nobody* — he said it like an accusation. Because he'd been that man, and he knew where the road went."

Mara stared into the fire. When she spoke, her voice was strange — stripped of the anger that had armored her since Wewak.

"He left when I was nine. He'd come back sometimes, between expeditions. He'd sit in the kitchen and tell me about rivers I couldn't picture, and I'd think, *he loves the rivers more than he loves me.* And now you're telling me he was running from watching people die, and he couldn't come home because home was where he'd have to explain, and explaining was the one thing he—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "God. We're the same. Both of us. He ran up here and I stayed down there and I never once went looking for him, and I told myself that was his failure, not mine."

"It's not the same," Jude said. "He had a massacre on his conscience. You had a father who was bad at being one."

"He had a daughter he was bad at being a father to, which is the same thing from where I sat." But there was no edge in it. She lowered her hand and looked at him, and in the firelight her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, something that might have been gratitude, fighting its way up like a shoot through rot. "Why are you telling me this? Now? In the middle of a cloud forest, halfway up a mountain, with men hunting us?"

"Because you trusted me with your life on that river today, and I haven't trusted you with the truth, and that's not a partnership, that's a hostage situation." He almost smiled. "Because I'm tired of being a man who belongs to nobody. And because if we die up here, I don't want the last honest thing I did to be a supply run."

Mara looked at him for a long time. The forest dripped. The fire settled.

"My father made you swear," she said finally. "And you've been carrying it like it was his promise, not yours. But you're here. You flew me out of Wewak with nothing but a wrecked map and a dead man's word. So whatever you were before—" she gestured at him, at all of him, "—you're not that man anymore. You can stop apologizing for him."

It was not forgiveness, exactly. It was something better — an accounting, entered in the ledger, the debt acknowledged. Jude felt it land in him like ballast.

"Sleep," he said. "Long climb tomorrow."

She lay back down. And just before sleep took her, in the smallest voice, she said, "Thank you for telling me," and Jude lay staring into the dark and understood that he had just traded the last of his invisibility for something he couldn't take back, and that he was, for the first time in twelve years, glad of it.

* * *

The river crossing came on the second afternoon, and it nearly ended everything.

The rain had found them in the night — not the forest's gentle sweating but real rain, a vertical ocean, the kind that turns a trail into a creek and a creek into a claim. They came down to the crossing point Toma had described as a wide brown torrent, swollen past recognition, dragging whole trees in its grip like a man shaking off dogs.

"We cross here or we lose a day going down to the gorge," Toma shouted over it, "and the gorge is worse."

"It doesn't look crossable," Mara shouted back.

"It isn't. But it's the shallowest place, and we have a rope, and we cross one at a time, sideways, upstream of the others. If you go down, you don't fight the water. You let it take you to the far bank and we find you."

Jude went first, because he was the heaviest and if the rope held for him it would hold for anyone. The water hit him at mid-thigh and kept climbing, and the cold of it was a physical blow, glacier-fed, high-country water that stopped the breath. He crossed facing upstream, leaning on the current, one shuffling step at a time, the rope singing with the force of the water, and when he dragged himself out on the far bank his legs were shaking and his hands were blue.

Mara went second.

She was lighter than him, and that was the problem. He watched her come out into the current and saw the moment the water found the flaw in her footing — a rounded stone, a fraction of an inch of lost grip — and the river simply took her. The rope snapped taut. She went under, came up twenty feet down with her arm hooked over the line, and the water was folding her over it, pressing her down, and Jude was already back in the current without deciding to be, Toma shouting behind him, the cold closing over his chest like a hand.

He reached her at the seam where the current split. She was coughing, still holding the rope, her eyes wide and white-rimmed with the pure animal surprise of drowning. He got an arm across her chest, got his feet under him on the cobbles, and the two of them leaned into the river together, and the river leaned back, and for a long moment — ten seconds, twenty, an hour — nothing moved at all, the whole world reduced to a rope and a grip and the roar.

Then Toma was there. He had come out on the line with a coil looped around his shoulder, and he threw the coil, and Jude caught it one-handed, and the three of them came out of the water on the far bank like something being born — gasping, streaming, collapsed on the stones.

Mara was shaking so hard her teeth chattered. Jude pulled her against him without thinking about it, and she let him, her face against his soaked shirt, her fists closed in the fabric.

"I had it," she said, when she could talk. "I had it until I didn't."

"Nobody has it," Toma said, wringing out his shirt. "The river has it. We are only borrowing ourselves from the river. Come — fire, before the cold gets into the bone."

They made camp early, shuddering around a fire Toma coaxed from sodden wood with a patience that seemed almost devotional. Mara's lips were still gray. Jude sat beside her and put his jacket around her shoulders, and she didn't refuse it, and that small surrender told him more than any words.

"That was stupid," she said quietly. "Going back in."

"Probably."

"You didn't even hesitate."

"No," Jude said. "I noticed that too."

She turned and looked at him, firelit, river-wrung, and whatever passed between them in that moment was not spoken and did not need to be. Toma, down the fire, was studying the dark with the studied blankness of a man giving privacy he had no business giving, and Jude thought: *I want to trust you. Tell me I'm not a fool.*

* * *

They climbed out of the cloud forest on the fourth morning, into a thinning of trees and a hard bright cold, and by noon they stood at the edge of the last forest, looking up at bare ridgelines stitched with snow.

But it was Toma who stopped them, an hour before that, at a place where the trail bent around a shoulder of rock — stopped them with an upraised hand and a stillness that was entirely new to him.

Below, in a fold of the hillside where the forest had never regrown, were the ruins.

Jude saw them first as shadows: the black rectangles of house posts, a score of them, fire-scarred. A garden gone to cane. A church bell hanging from a frame of charred timber, green with eight seasons of moss, silent.

Mara went very still beside him. "What is this place?"

Toma's face had closed like a door. "A village," he said. "Once."

"Which village?" Mara asked. "What was it called?"

And Toma, looking down at the burned posts with an expression Jude would think about for a long time afterward — an expression that was not surprise, and not quite guilt, and not quite grief — said:

"Nobody calls it anything anymore. That is the point of what happened here."

He started down the slope toward the ruins, and after a moment, Mara followed.
