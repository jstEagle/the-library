## 10. The Erased Village

They smelled it before they saw it.

Mara had been walking behind Toma for most of the morning, one hand on the wet bark of trees that seemed to sweat even in the thin mountain air, when the wind shifted and brought her a smell she knew from somewhere deep in childhood—burnt timber gone to rot, char soaked by two years of rain until the fire itself had washed out of it and only the ruin remained. It was a smell like a house after a kitchen fire, like the trading post's back room the year the generator caught. But bigger. Bigger than any kitchen, any room.

Toma stopped on the trail ahead of them without turning around. His machete hung loose at his side.

"From here," he said quietly, "we walk slow."

Jude came up beside her, breathing hard from the climb, and Mara watched his face as he caught the same smell. Something moved through it—not surprise. Recognition. He'd known what they were walking toward. He'd known since the map, maybe since before the map, and he hadn't said it out loud because saying it would make it real.

"How much farther?" she asked.

"Half an hour," Toma said. "Maybe less." He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—something almost like apology, though she couldn't have said what he was apologizing for. "You should know now, before you see it. This place has a name. The people who lived here called it Yambunai. The government maps call it nothing. There is no village here on any paper."

"No village," Mara repeated.

"The ground was leased," Toma said. "Before anything was burned. The company filed its papers in Port Moresby, and the papers said the land was empty." He turned and walked on. "The land was not empty."

* * *

They came down through a stand of casuarinas whose trunks were black to head height, and then the forest simply stopped, as if a giant hand had drawn a line across the mountainside and erased everything inside it.

Mara had imagined ruins before—she'd seen photographs of bombed towns in old magazines at the trading post, concrete skeletons, twisted iron. This was not that. This was worse, because this had been wood and sago palm and thatch, and wood and thatch do not leave skeletons. They leave stains. The village was a pattern of dark rectangles pressed into the earth where houses had stood, each one softened now by moss and colonized by ferns, so that the whole settlement looked like something growing rather than something killed. Here and there a ridgepost survived, charred upright, leaning at angles like men frozen mid-fall. A cooking pot sat in the doorway-shape of one house, fused into a lump of slag. A child's wooden bird lay in the grass, unburned, its paint flaked but its wings still spread.

Nobody spoke. Even the birds had come back wrong—there were honeyeaters working the ferns, but they kept their distance from the rectangles, skirting the house-places as if the shapes themselves were water too deep to cross.

Mara walked to the center of what had been, judging by the arrangement, a dancing ground. The earth there was packed hard still, worn smooth by decades of feet, and it was the only part of the village that didn't look wounded. It just looked abandoned. She stood on it and felt the strangeness of standing on a floor with no walls, under a sky where there should have been a roof.

"He was here," she said. She didn't know how she knew it, but she did—the way you know a thing about your own blood. "He stood right here."

"Mara." Jude's voice was careful. "Maybe we shouldn't—"

"He stood *here*," she said again, louder, and her voice cracked against the silence and came back at her off the blackened casuarinas. "Two years ago. And he walked away. He walked away and he never—" She stopped. Her hands had closed into fists without her permission, and she made herself open them. "Never told me. Never wrote. Never sent one word in six years, and all this time he was carrying whatever happened here inside him like a stone, and he let me think he just—chose gold over me."

Jude came and stood beside her, not touching her, which she was grateful for. Touching would have broken something.

"We don't know yet what he did or didn't do," Jude said. "We know what the map shows. We don't know the rest."

"I know my father," Mara said. "That's the rest."

But even as she said it, she heard the lie in it, because she had spent the last week learning that she did not know her father at all—that the man who'd taught her to read tide tables and left before her fourteenth birthday and sent exactly three letters in six years, each one shorter than the last, was a man assembled from fragments, and every fragment they found contradicted another. The father who paid a village's debts. The father who ran. The father who bled out on a stranger's airstrip pressing an oilskin packet into a pilot's hands, saying *my daughter, tell her I'm sorry, tell her I'm sorry*, over and over, until sorry was the last word he had.

"Toma," Jude called. "Is anyone—"

"There is someone," Toma said.

Mara turned. At the far edge of the ruined ground, half-hidden by a wall of ginger plants that had grown up wild since the burning, an old man was watching them.

She hadn't heard him approach. He stood very still among the leaves, a thin man in a ragged shirt and a lap-lap, gray-haired, with a walking stick of ironwood worn smooth as glass. He might have been sixty. He might have been eighty. His eyes were the oldest thing about him—they held the flat, patient attention of a man who had learned that watching was safer than being seen.

Toma spoke to him in a language Mara didn't know, low and formal, and the old man answered in the same tongue, and then, after a long pause, in Tok Pisin, directed past Toma entirely, straight at Mara.

"You are the daughter of Wren?"

Her heart stumbled. "You knew him?"

"I knew him." The old man came forward slowly, leaning on the stick, and stopped ten feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to run. "I am Ambai. I was the lavalava-man here. The keeper of stories." He looked around at the rectangles, the ferns, the slagged pot, and his face did not change at all, which was somehow more terrible than grief would have been. "Now I keep the story of how there is no village."

* * *

They sat, because Ambai insisted on it—he would not tell it standing, he said; a story told standing runs off the ground and is lost—and so they settled onto fallen logs at the edge of the dancing ground while rain began somewhere above them in the canopy and came down in fat, warm drops that struck the leaves like slow applause.

Ambai sat facing the ruined houses. He did not look at Mara while he talked. He looked at the village.

"Wren came first eight years ago," he said. "A white man alone, no company, no police, walking up the river with a pan and a pack. The young men wanted to turn him back—we had seen prospectors before, and prospectors bring surveyors, and surveyors bring bulldozers. But Wren sat in the men's house for three days and ate our food and worked with his hands, mended the school roof, helped pull a canoe over the rapids. And at the end he showed the elders his pan, with the color in it, and he said: this ground is yours. Whatever is under it is yours. I will help you take it out yourselves, small, slow, so no one comes. Or I will go away and say I found nothing. You choose."

"And they chose?" Mara asked.

"They chose him." Ambai's mouth moved in something that was not quite a smile. "Foolish. Wise. Both. For five years it was good. Small digging, hidden digging. Gold bought medicine, bought a diesel pump, bought the children's school books. Wren came and went like the weather. He was—" the old man searched, "—a man who could not stay anywhere long, but while he was here, he was fully here. Your father was like rain, girl. Never staying. Always feeding something."

Mara looked down at her hands. Rain. Yes. That was him exactly, and it broke her a little to hear a stranger say it, because it meant the truth of Elias Wren had been sitting in other people's mouths all these years while she starved on silence.

"Then Kavera came," Ambai said, and the warmth went out of his voice entirely, leaving something flat and mineral. "Not with guns at first. With papers. A man in a clean shirt, a helicopter, a briefcase. He said the lease was already signed. He said the signatures were ours. They were not ours, but paper does not care whose name it carries. We refused. We refused for a year. We wrote letters. The letters went into Port Moresby and did not come back. Then one night in the wet season—"

He stopped. For the first time, his composure bent. His hand tightened on the ironwood stick until the knuckles went pale.

"One night," he said again, quieter. "Men came up the river. Not police. Not company men in uniform—men paid to wear no uniform. They burned the village while everyone slept. They shot those who ran toward the river. My brother was the first. He slept nearest the water." The drops fell on the leaves. Somewhere in the ruined houses, a fern stirred in the wind, soft as breath. "Forty-one died. The rest scattered into the bush, into other villages, into the high valley beyond the ice. Those who stayed near were visited again. Warned. So we learned to be nowhere. The government wrote that the village had been abandoned voluntarily. Resettled. There is a file in Moresby that says we chose to leave our own dead."

Mara realized she was shaking. Not crying—she felt strangely dry-eyed, hollowed, as if the tears had been burned out of her along with the village—but shaking, her arms prickling with cold in the warm rain.

"And my father?" she managed. "Where was he?"

Ambai finally turned and looked at her directly, and what was in his old eyes made her want to look away and made her unable to.

"He was here," Ambai said. "Three days before. He had gone downriver to get medicine for the fever that was in the children. If he had been here, he would have died with us, and perhaps that would have been cleaner. Instead he came back to smoke." The old man gestured at the black casuarinas, the rectangles, everything. "He came back and found this. He dug graves for eleven of the dead who lay near the river, himself, alone, with his hands, for two days. Then he took things. Papers. Photographs. The company man's letter with the false signatures. A list. He said—I remember his words, I will carry them until I die—he said: *I will make them pay for this if it takes the rest of my life.*"

"And then?" Jude asked softly.

"Then he ran," Ambai said. "For two years, he ran. He sent word sometimes, through traders, through mission boats. Once money came, wrapped in oilskin, for the survivors in the high valley. But mostly he ran, and the company hunted him, because a dead witness is a closed story and a living one is a wound that will not close." He leaned forward, and the stick trembled slightly under his grip. "You have his map. I can see it in your pack, the shape of it, the oilskin. Show me."

Mara hesitated. Everything in her that had been trained by a week of flight and fear said *don't show anyone, don't trust anyone*. But this man had buried forty-one of his people and kept their names in his head for two years, and he had known her father when her father was worth knowing.

She unwrapped the oilskin and spread the map on the log between them.

Ambai studied it for a long time. His finger traced the ridgelines, the river bends, and stopped on a small square marked in Elias's cramped handwriting—a square Mara had taken for a claim marker, a treasure symbol, X marks the gold.

"That is not where the gold is," Ambai said. "That is where the men's house stood. Where the council met. He drew the village, girl. He drew the graves. Look—" his nail tapped a row of tiny marks along the river bend, marks she'd read as survey points, "—eleven. One for each grave he dug. This is not a treasure map. It never was. It is a testimony. Every mark on it is a fact he could prove: the false lease, the graves, the ground itself. He carried it so that someday, someone could stand on this earth and say: *this happened, and here is the proof, written in the land.*"

The rain fell harder. Mara stared at the map—at the little square, the eleven ticks, the ridgelines her father had walked and bled across—and felt the whole thing turn over inside her like a canoe in rough water. All week she had hated the map. It was the thing that had cost her father's life, the thing that had dragged her out of Wewak at gunpoint's distance, the thing that put her on this mountain with leeches in her boots and men with rifles somewhere behind her. She had thought of it as his last betrayal: even dead, choosing the gold over her.

It was never the gold.

It was forty-one names. It was a promise made over open graves with blistered hands. It was the reason he couldn't come home—because coming home meant stopping, and stopping meant dying, and dying meant the village stayed erased forever.

"Daddy," she whispered, and the word came out in her mother's accent, small and coastal, a child's word, and she did not care.

Ambai reached out and closed her fingers over the oilskin, gently, folding the map shut.

"Now you understand why they hunt it," he said. "Not for what it leads to. For what it says." He rose, leaning hard on the stick, and nodded up the trail, north, toward the white haze where the high peaks hid behind cloud. "There are others of us. In the high valley, past the ice. Elders who remember every name. If you carry that map, you must carry it to them—it belongs in their hands, not yours, not mine. But know this, daughter of Wren." His voice dropped, and the rain seemed to lean in to listen. "The company knows the valley road opens soon, when the glacier calves. They will not let your father's testimony reach the elders. They are already moving. And they are not the only ones who know you are on this mountain."

Toma had gone very still by the edge of the clearing. Mara noticed it the way you notice a change in the wind—peripherally, uneasily, without yet understanding it.

"What do you mean," she said slowly, "not the only ones?"

Ambai looked past her, up the trail they had come by, into the dripping green dark.

"Someone has been following you since the river," he said. "Since before the river. Walking your same footprints, half a day behind." He met her eyes. "And this morning, for the first time—they stopped following. Now they are hurrying."
