## 11. Toma's Night Market

The waystation at Kandep Saddle was a wound in the mountain that had never quite healed. Toma had known it in better years, when the trade store still had glass in its windows and the mission bell still rang for Sunday school, when the veranda posts were straight and the whole place smelled of kerosene and fresh coffee rather than rot and rust. Now the roof sagged like an old man's shoulders, and the rain came through it in slow, patient threads, and the only bell was the wind knocking a sheet of corrugated iron against the eaves all night long.

He sat on the veranda with his back against a post and watched Jude and Mara sleep through the doorway. They had taken the inner room, the one with a door that closed, because Mara's ankle had swollen to twice its size and she could no longer pretend otherwise. The white man had carried her up the last two hours of trail with her arms around his neck and her face pressed into his shoulder, and he had not once set her down to rest, and Toma had watched him do it and thought: *this is the kind of man who dies for people. That is the worst kind. Those are the ones who make everything complicated.*

Toma did not want complications. He wanted one thing only, and it had a name and a face and it was sleeping four hundred kilometers away behind wire.

* * *

His brother's name was Anis. He was twenty-three and he laughed like water going over stones, quick and bright and impossible to hold, and he had been gone from the village for three years now, which is how long it takes a boy to stop being a boy and start being a debt.

It had begun the way these things always begin — with a truck. A Kavera truck, blue and new, rolling up the valley road on a day so hot the tar had gone soft, and a man in a clean shirt stepping down to say that Kavera Mining Consortium was offering contracts. Good wages. Good food. Work for six months and come home with enough kina to buy a boat, a roof of real iron, a bride-price that would make the whole valley talk.

Anis had signed with a ballpoint pen borrowed from the man's own shirt pocket. Toma remembered the pen. He remembered thinking, even then, that a man who lends you the very instrument of your binding is either a fool or a fisherman, and this one was not a fool.

Six months became eight. Eight became a year. Letters came at first, dictated by other men because Anis wrote slowly, letters full of optimism and small lies — the food was good, the work was fair, the camp had a radio. Then the letters stopped. Then a different letter came, from a different hand, telling them Anis had extended his contract voluntarily and would send money soon, and there was something in the phrasing, some flatness, some absence of the boy's particular brightness, that made their mother weep without knowing why she wept.

Toma went looking. It took him eleven months and most of the money he had, moving upriver and over ridges, following the trucks and the rumors, until he stood on a hillside above a camp in the highlands and looked down through the rain at what a contract looks like when nobody is watching.

Wire. Watch towers. Men in lines. Not prisoners — the company called them workers, and there were papers, and the papers said they were free to leave, but the gate was far away and the road between here and there belonged to men with guns, and a free man who walks down a road owned by armed men is free exactly as long as it amuses them. Anis was thinner. Anis was slower. But Anis was alive, and Toma had learned that day the first great lesson of his life under the Consortium: that alive is a currency, and someone else sets the exchange rate.

* * *

The radio lived in the trade store's back room, behind a curtain of plastic sacking, in the keeping of a widow named Yalma who sold tinned fish and batteries and asked no questions, which was why everyone asked her everything.

Toma waited until the house was dark. He waited past the time when the rain softened, past the time when the last dog stopped barking, past the hour when even the mountains seemed to hold their breath. Then he crossed the wet grass in bare feet, because boots make noise and bare feet know how to be sorry, and he lifted the plastic curtain and stepped inside.

Yalma was awake, of course. She was always awake. She sat by the cold stove with a blanket around her shoulders like a queen holding court over nothing, and she looked at him with eyes that had seen forty years of men coming to do things at night.

"You want the radio," she said. Not a question.

"I want the radio."

"Kavera frequency." Also not a question. She knew. She always knew; that was the terrible tax of living in a place where everyone knows everything about everyone. "You have family in their hands."

Toma said nothing, which was answer enough.

Yalma rose, stiff as an old canoe, and lit a stub of candle, and set it beside the radio set — a Japanese transceiver, dented, lovingly maintained, its dial glowing a soft sickly green when she fed it power from the bank of batteries under the table. Then she went to the door and stood there with her back to him, a small square silhouette against the starlight.

"I will listen for the dogs," she said. "And I will not hear anything. You understand? Tonight I am deaf. Tomorrow also, if you need tomorrow."

"Yalma—"

"Don't thank me." Her voice was dry as seed husks. "Thanking makes it a favor, and favors are debts, and I am too old for more debts. Just go. And Toma—" she paused, half-turned, the candlelight catching one side of her face, "—whatever you are selling tonight, make sure you are not selling the wrong people. There are only two kinds of grief, boy. The kind you cause and the kind you suffer. Everything else is just weather."

Then she was out the door, and Toma was alone with the green light and the hiss of dead air.

* * *

He had rehearsed the call for three days. On the trail, while Jude slept and Mara dozed against her pack, he had mouthed the words silently, shaping them, testing them, discarding them. Every version ended the same way, though. Every version ended with him saying the coordinates.

Because that was the truth of it, the plain ugly arithmetic underneath everything: he knew where they were going. He knew the pass they meant to cross, the saddle below the glacier, the route through the scree that old men sang about and young men forgot. He was the guide. That was the whole reason they had hired him, the whole reason he had let himself be hired — because a man who knows the mountains is worth more to the Consortium than a hundred laborers, and worth more alive than any ransom.

They had found him in Wewak, or rather he had found them — no, that wasn't honest. Let it be honest, here in the dark, where honesty costs nothing because no one can hear it. They had not found him. He had arranged to be found. The meeting at the river town had not been chance; he had heard, through channels that run deep and quiet through every trading post in the Sepik, that a pilot and a woman were asking after the high pass, carrying something Kavera wanted, and he had put himself in their path like bait on a hook, smiling his best smile, telling his best stories about routes only he knew.

Some of the stories were even true.

That was the part that shamed him most, in the small hours. Not the plan itself — plans are just tools, and a knife is not evil because it cuts. What shamed him was how easy it had been. How naturally the lies had come, how warmly he had gripped Jude's hand, how sincerely he had said *I know the way*, and meant, underneath, *I know the way to sell you.* He was good at this. That was the discovery that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: he was good at betrayal, the way some men are good at carving or singing, a gift he had never asked for and could not give back.

The radio crackled. He keyed the transmit switch and spoke the call sign he had memorized in another life, the one that opened doors he wished stayed shut.

Static. Then a voice, flat and clipped, the voice of a man reading from a card: "Go ahead."

"This is Two-Kilo-Nine. I have a position report."

"Position."

He gave it. The saddle country, the waystation, the rate of travel. His voice sounded strange to himself — steady, professional, the voice of a man reporting cargo, and that was exactly what he was doing, wasn't it, reporting cargo, two items of live cargo moving toward the pass.

"The woman is injured," he added. "Ankle. They slow."

A pause. Somewhere far away, a man was writing this down, reducing Mara's swollen ankle to a line in a ledger, and somewhere beyond that man, other men were adjusting plans, moving vehicles, checking ammunition, and none of them would ever see her face or hear her voice or know that she laughed at bad jokes and swore at leeches with a creativity that had made even Toma grin.

"Understood. Continue to observe. Do not engage. Confirmation of package recovery triggers payment in full. Your brother's status will be reviewed upon completion."

*Reviewed.* Toma closed his eyes. In the language of the Consortium, *reviewed* was a word like a trapdoor. It meant: perhaps. It meant: we will see. It meant: your brother is a line in our ledger too, and lines in ledgers get erased when they become inconvenient, and right now your brother is useful precisely because you are useful, and when you cease to be useful—

"And after?" he said. "After completion. He comes out. Free passage. That was the agreement."

"That was the agreement," the voice agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing to weather.

The channel clicked dead. Toma sat in the green light with the handset still against his ear, listening to the static, which sounded, if you let yourself hear it, like the ocean, like rain on leaves, like all the indifferent vast noise of the world that does not care what you have traded.

* * *

He thought about Anis laughing. That was the danger of the night market, the true cost of it, the price hidden under the price: that in order to spend two lives you must first convince yourself they are only numbers, and numbers do not laugh. But Anis laughed. Anis had once spent an entire afternoon teaching a parrot to say their mother's name and then couldn't get it to stop, and their mother chased that bird around the village for a week, and Anis laughed until he fell off the platform of the haus win, and Toma had laughed too, helplessly, until his ribs hurt.

Numbers do not fall off platforms laughing. Numbers do not dictate hopeful lies in other men's handwriting. Numbers stay neatly in columns, and columns balance, and that is their whole virtue and their whole horror — that a ledger always balances, that something must always be entered on the other side, and tonight Toma had entered two names on the debit side and one name on the credit side and told himself the arithmetic was mercy.

One life bought with two. That was the bargain. That was what he had walked into the night to purchase, and the terrible clarity of it, standing there in the candle's dying light, was that he would do it again. If the positions were reversed — if it were Jude held behind wire and Anis walking free — he would sell them both again, and smile while he did it, and hate himself after, and that self-knowledge was the loneliest thing he had ever carried.

Unless.

The word arrived quietly, the way dangerous words do.

Unless the shot goes wide.

He had been turning it over for days now, the way you turn a stone in a stream, examining first one side then the other. When the ambush came — and it would come at the saddle, because the saddle was the only place the mountains allowed it — he would be expected to fire. To signal. To pin them down until the others closed in. And a rifle, in the dark, in the scree, among echoes — a rifle is an imprecise instrument. Bullets go wide. Warnings get mistaken. Seconds get lost, and seconds, in the high country, are worth more than gold.

He could save them and betray them in the same breath. He could spend his loyalty and keep his soul, or what remained of it. Fire wide, shout a false warning, give them the gap — and then run, and find Anis by his own means, the hard way, the way he had found the camp in the first place. Eleven months once. Maybe eleven months again. Maybe worse.

Or he could do nothing clever at all. He could simply be what he had contracted to be: a man who sells people, cleanly, efficiently, and collects his brother like a parcel.

Toma blew out the candle. The green light of the radio dial was the only color left in the world, and he stared at it until it seemed to float, until the little room seemed to drift like a canoe on dark water.

Outside, Yalma's silhouette reappeared in the doorway. She looked at him for a long moment, this old woman who heard everything and saw everything and forgave almost nothing, and whatever she read in his face made her shake her head slowly, once, like a woman watching a canoe put out into a storm.

"You have the look," she said. "The look of a man carrying two fires in one basket."

"One fire is for my brother."

"And the other?"

Toma looked at the floor. "For me," he said. "The one I'm burning myself on."

Yalma snorted, which from her was a benediction, and shuffled back inside, and Toma went out into the wet grass and the enormous wheeling stars and lay down between Jude's Cessna of a body and Mara's small fierce sleeping shape, and listened to them breathe.

Tomorrow they would climb to the saddle. Tomorrow the enforcers would be waiting in the rocks, patient as crocodiles. Tomorrow he would raise a rifle and choose, in the space of one trigger-pull, which kind of grief he could live inside.

But tonight — tonight there was only the breathing, and the rain starting again softly on the iron roof, and the thought that would not leave him, circling like a bird over open water:

*Two lives for one. Unless the shot goes wide.*

He closed his eyes and did not sleep, and far below in the valleys the rivers ran on toward the sea, carrying everything away, forgiving nothing.
