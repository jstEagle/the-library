## 12. Ambush at the Saddle

The saddle had a name, though no one alive remembered it. Toma called it only "the place where the wind changes hands," and Jude had learned not to ask for names the mountains kept for themselves.

They had climbed since first light, out of the moss-draped gullies of the cloud forest and into a world of grey stone and stunted pandanus, where the air thinned enough that each breath felt borrowed. Mara moved ahead of him with the stubborn rhythm of someone who had decided her body would obey her whether it wanted to or not. Her ankle had swollen badly two days ago at the river crossing; she had said nothing about it since, but Jude could read the limp the way he read weather—by the small deviations, the half-beat delay in her stride, the way she planted her left foot like a woman testing ice.

Toma ranged ahead, as he always did, a dark figure flickering between boulders, and Jude watched him with an attention he no longer bothered to disguise from himself. Three nights ago he had woken to find Toma sitting up, the radio handset pressed to his ear, speaking too softly for words—only the cadence of it, the hunched shoulders, the quick way he'd stowed the handset when Jude stirred. Jude had told himself it was a call to family. He had told himself a lot of things since Wewak.

The saddle itself was a shallow notch between two shoulders of the range, maybe two hundred meters across, carpeted in a coarse tussock grass gone bronze with the season. Beyond it the land dropped away into the high valley basin, and beyond that—when the cloud tore open, as it did now and then—the white wall of the calving glacier, so bright it looked less like ice than like a wound in the world. That was their door. Three days, maybe four, and the pass through it would be open. Toma had said so every evening, tapping the map with one finger, and every evening Jude had believed him a little less and needed to believe him a little more.

"Rest here," Toma said, sliding down off a boulder. "The wind changes at midday. Better to cross after."

Mara lowered herself against the rock with a gratitude she tried to hide. Jude crouched, unslung his pack, and worked the cap off the water bottle. The silence up here was enormous—not empty, but full, the way a held breath is full. Somewhere below, a bird made a sound like a hinge swinging.

He was lifting the bottle to his mouth when Toma stood up.

It was nothing, at first. A man stretching his legs, walking a few paces toward the eastern edge of the saddle, hands loose at his sides. But Jude had spent twenty years reading the small grammar of bodies in dangerous country—the missionary who stood too casually near the fuel drums, the trader whose eyes flicked to the door a half-second before the knock. Toma's body was speaking a sentence Jude had heard before, and the sentence ended in a word he didn't want to hear.

"Toma," Jude said quietly.

The guide didn't turn. "The wind," he said. "It changes."

"No," Jude said. "It doesn't. Not yet."

And then the world came apart.

It began as a sound from the eastern slope—a flat, dry crack, wrong for a branch, wrong for a rockfall—and then the tussock grass thirty meters to their left erupted in a geyser of dirt, and the crack came again, twice, and Jude was moving before his mind finished the arithmetic, his hand closing on Mara's pack strap, hauling her off the rock and down behind it as the third shot whined off stone somewhere above their heads.

"Down! Down, stay down—"

Mara was already flat, cheek to the frozen ground, her eyes wide and furious rather than afraid, which was so entirely her that some distant part of Jude's mind filed it away to marvel at later. Bullets cracked and snapped across the saddle in short, disciplined bursts—not panic fire, not hunting fire. Suppression fire. Men who knew their trade, keeping their targets pinned while the rest of the net closed.

"How many?" Mara shouted.

"Four, maybe five." Jude pressed his back to the boulder, feeling the cold of it through his shirt. "East slope. They've been behind us since the river."

"Since the *river*—"

"Since the river," he said, and did not say the rest, because the rest was a name, and saying it would make it true.

But it was already true. It had been true for three days, sitting in the back of his skull like a splinter. Toma at the fire, Toma on the radio, Toma walking to the eastern edge of the saddle with his hands loose and his back straight, and the first shot coming from the east.

Jude raised his head just enough to look.

Toma stood in the open, forty meters away, and he was not running. He was not crouching. He stood in the killing ground of his own ambush like a man at a funeral he had organized, and in his hands was his old lever-action rifle, the one with the worn stock he polished every night, and the rifle was up, and it was pointed—at nothing. At the sky. At the shoulder of the ridge where no one was.

He fired.

The shot went wide. Not wide the way a frightened man's shot goes wide—a hand's width, a body's width. It went wide by forty meters, cracking off the rocks high above the eastern slope, and Jude understood it in the space of a heartbeat, the way you understand a face across a crowded room. It was not a shot at anyone. It was a shot *for* someone. A warning, fired in the only language available to a man standing between two sets of guns.

The enforcers' fire stuttered, confused. In that stutter, Toma turned his head and looked straight at Jude, and across forty meters of open ground, with bullets singing past him, Toma shouted a word in his own language and then, in English, cracked and urgent:

"*Scree! West side—go, go now!*"

Jude didn't think. Thinking was a luxury the saddle didn't sell. He grabbed Mara's wrist and ran.

The western edge of the saddle broke off into a long fan of broken stone—grey scree running down three hundred meters into a fold of the mountain, the kind of slope that swallowed footsteps and gave nothing back. It was the worst ground he'd ever considered descending and the only ground the bullets couldn't follow. Behind them the fire resumed, sharper now, angrier, a round spanging off the boulder they'd abandoned, another kicking dust from the tussock at Jude's heels. Then they were over the edge and into it, and the mountain took them.

Scree climbing down is not running; it is a controlled catastrophe. Each step sent a hissing slide of stone ahead of them, the whole slope whispering and shifting like something alive. Jude went down twice, the second time hard enough to drive the air out of him, a slab of rock catching him across the ribs, and he rolled, gasped, and kept moving because Mara was below him and moving and that was the only fact that mattered. The gunfire faded above them, traded for wind and the endless rustle of stone, and then for nothing at all but their own ragged breathing and the boom of their hearts.

They came to rest in the fold of the mountain where the scree ran out into old moraine, a chaos of boulders and ice-worn gravel in the shadow of an overhang. Jude lay on his back with his ribs on fire and stared at the grey sky until it stopped tilting.

Mara crouched at the edge of the overhang, watching the slope they'd descended, her rifle—Toma's spare, the one he'd pressed on her at the waystation with a grin that now curdled in Jude's memory—clutched across her knees.

"He warned us," she said. Her voice was flat, scraped clean. "He shot wide on purpose. He *told* us where to go."

"I know."

"So which is it, Jude?" She turned, and her eyes had the bright, dangerous shine he'd learned to fear and love in equal measure. "Is he a traitor who lost his nerve, or a friend who sold us and then couldn't watch? Because those are different animals, and I need to know which one is out there."

Jude sat up, wincing, and pressed his palm against his ribs. Nothing moved that shouldn't. Bruised, not broken. The mountain's small mercy.

"Both," he said. "He's both. That's the whole problem with him."

* * *

They stayed in the shadow of the overhang while the light went long and the wind, true to Toma's word at last, changed hands. Jude took inventory the way he did after a forced landing: what survived, what didn't, what could be made to fly. They had water, half full. They had food for two days if they ate like monks. They had Mara's rifle, his machete, the map—God, the map—wrapped in oilskin against his chest where it had ridden since Wewak, and the second copy of Elias's testimony folded inside it. They had no radio. They had no Toma.

The arithmetic was ugly and he made himself do it anyway, because the mountain only dealt in numbers. Four or five enforcers on the saddle, well armed, well supplied, and now knowing exactly where their quarry had gone to ground. Behind Jude and Mara, the way they'd climbed for six days, was a country those men could sweep at their leisure. Ahead was the glacier, and the pass, and the three-day window that Toma alone had known the timing of—and if Toma had sold the route, he had sold the window too, and the enforcers would be waiting at the far side of the ice with the patience of men on salary.

Unless.

Unless a man who fired wide on purpose had left them one more thing.

Mara came back from the edge of the overhang and sat beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his, which was its own kind of arithmetic—the sum of everything they hadn't said since the whiteout on the ridge, since the night he'd carried her and told her the truth about Elias, about himself, about all the running he'd done. She didn't speak for a while. Below them the basin filled with evening, the shadows pooling blue in the folds of the mountain, and far off, through a tear in the cloud, the glacier burned pink and gold like something holy and terrible.

"My father ran from these people for two years," she said finally. "And it bought him a bullet in a stranger's airstrip at dusk." She turned the rifle over in her hands. "I used to think running was cowardice. Now I think it's just arithmetic. You spend something every day you run—pieces of yourself, pieces of other people—and you hope you have more than they do when the bill comes."

"Toma spent his brother," Jude said quietly.

Mara looked at him. "You know."

"I've known since the waystation. The radio calls, the way he talked about the labor camps—never as stories, always as places. He has someone inside one. A brother, I'd bet the plane on it." Jude rubbed his face with a grimy hand. "He believed he could trade us and buy one life back. Maybe he even could have. That's the thing about arithmetic—it doesn't care if the numbers are people."

"And then he stood in the open and fired wide," Mara said, "and gave us the seconds we needed to live. Which means somewhere between the radio and the saddle, he decided we were worth more than the trade." Her jaw tightened. "Or he decided his brother was already dead, and we were the only thing left worth saving."

"Either way, he's out there now with men who will shoot him as happily as they'd shoot us, if they saw that shot for what it was." Jude stared down the darkening slope. "And he knows this mountain better than they do. Better than we do. He knows where the scree funnels, where the water is, where the glacier thins."

"So we wait for him."

"So we wait for him," Jude agreed, "and we watch the slope, and we keep a round chambered, and we don't decide what he is until he shows us. That's not forgiveness. That's just the only strategy that keeps every door open."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then, without looking at him, she said, "You'd have done the same thing. Fired wide. If someone had a knife at the throat of everyone you ever—" She stopped. "If it were me," she corrected, very quietly, "and someone offered you my life for the map."

Jude looked at her. The light was nearly gone, but her face was close, and some things didn't need light.

"Yes," he said. "In a heartbeat. And I'd hate myself in exactly the order he's hating himself right now, somewhere on this mountain."

She leaned into him, just her shoulder against his, just weight and warmth and the small human heat that the whole cold range couldn't tax or seize. They watched the dark come down over the basin.

It was full night, the stars out in the brutal clarity of altitude, when Jude saw the light. Not a fire—a torch, shielded, moving down the western slope in the careful, weaving line of a man who knew exactly which stones would hold him. It paused at the lip of the scree. It blinked twice. It went dark.

Then, out of the blackness below the overhang, a voice pitched low and rough with exhaustion and something that might have been grief:

"Jude. Mara." A pause, long enough for a rifle to rise. "I brought medicine for the ankle. And I brought the route the soldiers will take through the pass." Another pause. "And I brought my brother's name, so you will know what I sold, and judge me for it, because I am done judging myself—it takes too long, and the snow is coming."

Mara's hand found Jude's in the dark and gripped it, hard, and Jude rose slowly to his feet with his heart hammering out the bitter, beautiful arithmetic of the mountain: mercy, or a trap; a guide, or a ghost; and three days of glacier between them and everything that mattered.
