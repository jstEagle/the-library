## 13. The Cold Above the Trees

The trees gave up before they did.

Mara felt it happen the way you feel a fever break—one moment the world was green and suffocating, moss and fern and the wet breath of cloud forest pressing against her skin, and then it simply stopped, as if a line had been drawn across the mountain by some authority she couldn't see. Beyond that line there was only rock and scree and a light so thin and white it seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere at once.

"Tree line," Jude said, unnecessarily. Her voice was hoarse from three days of climbing, from breathing air that had begun to feel like a rumor of air.

Mara stopped and looked back down. Below them the forest rolled away in waves, dark green going blue going grey going to nothing, swallowed by cloud. Somewhere under all that canopy were the gorges they'd crossed, the river that had nearly taken her, the burned village where an old man named Yawan had pressed his forehead against hers and told her, *Your father saw what we saw. Now he is yours to carry.* She had been carrying him ever since—Elias Wren, dead on a stranger's airstrip, alive in every step she took upward into this killing cold.

Her left ankle was the problem. It had turned on a wet root two days ago, and she'd walked on it because walking was the only option, and now it had swollen inside her boot until each step was a negotiation between her and the pain, conducted in a language with only one word.

Jude crouched beside her, unwrapping the last of the flatbread they'd bought in the waystation town, back when Toma was still with them, back when betrayal was still a thing that happened to other people. The bread had frozen into something like slate. Mara chewed anyway, because the body above the tree line made its demands without consulting the heart.

"How far to the pass?" she asked.

Jude squinted up the slope. Snow had begun to fall—not the fat, warm flakes of the lowlands but something dry and granular, hissing against the rocks like sand poured from a great height. "If the weather holds, tomorrow night. If it doesn't—" She didn't finish it. She never finished the bad sentences anymore. That was a habit Mara had noticed and hated and, in some part of herself she didn't examine, depended on.

"And if the snows come early?"

Jude looked at her then, and Mara saw her do the arithmetic she'd been doing silently for hours: the ankle, the altitude, the window of days when the calving glacier opened the pass, the enforcers somewhere behind or ahead or both. "Then we're on the wrong side of the mountain," Jude said, "when Kavera's men arrive."

*When*, not *if*. Mara had stopped arguing about that too.

They climbed.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the snow stopped being weather and became a place.

Mara had grown up on the coast, in Wewak, where cold meant a fan turned off or a beer gone warm in the sun. She had no vocabulary for this. The wind came off the ice fields above in long, screaming gusts that found every gap in her clothing and pressed fingers of absolute cold against her skin. The scree shifted underfoot with every step, a whole mountainside of loose stone that seemed determined to slide out from beneath humanity altogether. And the light—the light was the worst of it. White on white on white, sky indistinguishable from slope, horizon erased, so that she climbed through a blindness that wasn't dark.

She fell twice. The first time she caught herself. The second time her ankle folded under her and she went down hard on the scree, and something in her wrist cracked—a hot wire of pain—and she lay there for a moment, cheek against stone, watching snowflakes land an inch from her eye and vanish.

*Mara.* Jude's hands were under her arms, hauling her upright. *Mara, look at me.*

"I'm looking," Mara said, and discovered she was crying, which was absurd, because tears froze on your face up here and left salt tracks that stung for an hour. "I'm fine. I just—I need a minute."

"We don't have a minute." Jude said it gently, which somehow made it worse. She got Mara's arm over her shoulders and took her weight without comment, without ceremony, the way she did everything—as if carrying people were simply a skill she'd learned instead of a burden she resented. "We need to find shelter before full dark. There's supposed to be an overhang Toma marked on the map. Half a kilometer, maybe less."

Toma's map. Drawn in Toma's careful hand, before he sat by the fire at the waystation and spoke their names into a radio in exchange for his brother's life. Before his warning shot went wide and gave them the seconds they needed to run. Mara still didn't know what to do with that—whether to hate him or thank him or understand that both were the same thing, that a man could sell you and save you in the same hour and mean it both times.

They moved. Or rather, Jude moved and Mara was moved, her good leg doing half the work and Jude's shoulders doing the rest, the two of them lurching across the scree like a single wounded animal.

The overhang, when they found it, was barely shelter at all—a slab of rock leaning against the mountainside, leaving a wedge of shadow deep enough to lie in and not deep enough to matter. But it broke the wind, and breaking the wind was everything. Jude got Mara out of her boot—it took ten minutes, working the swollen ankle free inch by inch while Mara bit down on her own sleeve—and wrapped it in the spare shirt from her pack, and then wrapped the whole foot in the foil blanket, and then put Mara's feet inside her own jacket, against her own body, because heat was a currency now and Jude paid without counting.

"You should sleep," Jude said. "I'll take first watch."

"There's nothing to watch. There's nothing anywhere."

"That's exactly what needs watching."

Mara almost laughed. It came out as a sob instead, and she turned her face away, furious at herself, at the altitude, at the thin treacherous air that made every feeling too large for the container of her body.

"Mara." Jude's voice, very quiet. "Hey. Look at me."

She looked. In the grey failing light Jude's face was wind-scoured and exhausted, lips cracked, eyes red-rimmed from the glare, and Mara thought: *I have never seen anyone so beautiful in my life,* and the thought frightened her more than the mountain did, because beauty was a thing you could lose, and losing it would be worse than frostbite.

"Eat this," Jude said, and put the last square of chocolate in her hand—the one they'd been saving, the one Jude had been quietly not eating for two days. "All of it. Don't argue."

Mara ate it. It tasted like nothing and like everything. Then Jude pulled her in against her side, inside the jacket, inside the shared warmth, and Mara let her head drop onto her shoulder and closed her eyes.

The wind screamed past the mouth of their little cave. Somewhere below them, men with guns were climbing. Somewhere above them, a glacier was groaning toward the moment it would open like a door. And here, in a wedge of shadow the size of a coffin, Mara Wren—who had spent thirty years making sure no one got close enough to leave her—fell asleep against another person's heartbeat.

* * *

She woke to whiteness.

Not morning whiteness—there was no telling if it was morning. This was a whiteness with weight and texture, a whiteness that pressed against the entrance of the overhang like a living thing. A whiteout. The storm had closed the world.

"Jude?"

"Here." Jude's voice came from inches away, muffled, strange. "Don't move. I've got the rope on us. We're tied together."

Mara's hands found the cord at her waist, then followed it to Jude. Her fingers were clumsy, stupid with cold even inside the sleeping bag they'd zipped around them both. "How long was I out?"

"A while." A pause. "Your ankle's worse. I checked it while you slept."

"Worse how?"

Another pause, and Mara understood that pauses were Jude's way of lying. "It needs to stay wrapped. You shouldn't put weight on it today."

"Then we don't move today."

"If we don't move today, we lose the pass." Jude's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone reading instruments in turbulence. "The calving window is three days, maybe four. We've used one. If we sit here through the storm—"

"Then carry me."

The words came out of Mara before she thought them, and once they were out she heard how they sounded—imperious, childish, the demand of a woman who had been carried for two days and knew exactly what it cost the person carrying her. She started to take it back.

But Jude said, "Okay."

Just that. *Okay.* No argument, no negotiation, no wounded pride. As if Mara had asked her to pass the salt.

"Jude—"

"It's what I'm for," Jude said, and there was something underneath the flatness then, something raw. "Carrying things. Carrying people. It's the one thing I've ever been any good at. So okay. I'll carry you."

Mara reached through the dark and found her face. Cheekbone, jaw, the rough stubble she hadn't known was there until this week, the corner of an eye that was wet and freezing instantly. "That's not true," she said. "What you're for."

"Isn't it? Ask anyone. Ask Elias—he picked me precisely because I fly supply runs and ask no questions and belong nowhere. Neutrality. That was my brand." A sound that might have been a laugh. "And look where it got me. Tied to a bleeding stranger's promise, climbing a mountain with his daughter, hunted by a mining company. So much for belonging nowhere."

"You could have said no," Mara said. "Back at the airstrip. He was dying; nobody would have blamed you."

"Yes," Jude said. "I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

The wind filled the silence for a long time. When Jude finally answered, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, the voice people use for confessions and prayers.

"Because I've been running my whole life, Mara. From Port Moresby, from a marriage, from a debt, from every place I ever owed anybody anything. Flying away is easy—you point the nose at the horizon and the ground forgives you. And when Elias put that map in my hands and made me swear, my first thought—the honest, shameful first thought—was *how do I get out of this.*" Her breath shuddered. "And my second thought was that if I ran again, I would finally be the thing I've always suspected I am. Which is nothing. Nobody. A plane with no destination. And then I met you, and—" She stopped.

"And?" Mara whispered.

"And I didn't want to be nobody. Not to you."

Outside, the storm howled. Inside the overhang, in the dark, in the cold that was slowly killing them both, Mara Wren began to cry again, and this time she didn't turn away, because there was nowhere to turn, and because—for the first time since she was nine years old and watched her father's plane dwindle to a speck over the water—she didn't want to hide.

"My father chose gold over me," she said. "Twenty years. He chose a riverbed over his own daughter. And I swore I'd never be chosen second by anyone again, so I chose first—I left everyone before they could leave me. Every job, every friend, every—" Her voice broke. "Every man. I was so good at leaving, Jude. I was better at it than he ever was."

"I know," Jude said. "I recognized the technique."

Mara laughed through the tears, a broken, freezing, ridiculous sound. "God. We're a pair."

"The worst pair on this mountain."

"The only pair on this mountain." Mara turned in the sleeping bag, awkward with the bound ankle, until she was facing her, close enough that their breath mingled and froze in the space between. "Say it," she said. "Whatever it is you're not saying. Say it now, while we can't run anywhere. While leaving isn't an option. Say it in the worst possible place, so I'll know you mean it."

Jude was silent for a moment. Mara felt her trembling—not from cold, or not only from cold.

"I love you," Jude said. "I love you, and I have no idea how to do this, and I'm terrified, and I would carry you over every range on this island if you asked me to. I love you. There. Said in the worst possible place."

Mara kissed her. It was clumsy and desperate and their lips were chapped and tasted of salt and altitude, and it was the least worst thing that had ever happened to her.

When they broke apart, the storm had changed its voice. Lower. Steadier. Mara couldn't have said how she knew—but she knew, the way animals know.

"It's easing," she said.

Jude listened. "Maybe an hour. Maybe two." She began checking the rope, the packs, the foil blanket, her movements already shifting from shelter-mode to motion-mode, and Mara watched her with a feeling so large it scared her more than the crevasses did.

"Jude. When we get down—after the pass, after whatever happens with Kavera—"

"Don't jinx it."

"—I'm not leaving," Mara finished. "For the record. Whatever you decide to do, I'm not leaving."

Jude's hands went still on the rope. In the grey light her face did something complicated and young. "For the record," she echoed.

Then she stiffened. Her head tilted, listening past the wind, past the storm's long exhale—and Mara heard it too, a fraction later. Voices. Faint, down-slope, carried up on the dying wind in fragments: men calling to each other in the disciplined cadence of a search line.

Kavera's enforcers. On the mountain. Hours behind them—or less.

Jude's hand found hers in the dark and squeezed once, hard. "Up," she said. "Now. Help me get you on my back."
