## 14. What Toma Carried

Toma had lied to better men than himself and slept soundly after. He had cheated missionaries at cards, shortchanged miners who smelled of greed, talked his way past three police checkpoints with a smile that opened doors like a key. He had told himself, in the way liars do, that every lie was small, that the ledger of his life balanced out somewhere he couldn't see.

But the lie he'd spoken into the radio at the waystation — *I have them, come to the saddle, I will deliver them* — that lie had followed him up the mountain like a dog that would not be called home.

He sat now on a cold ridge above the scree field where Jude Almeida and Mara Wren had escaped his warning shot, chewing a piece of dried fish that tasted of nothing, watching the cloud boil up from the valley floor and thinking about his brother.

Anis. Nineteen years old, hands too gentle for the labor camp at Timunke, where Kavera put men who asked questions about their pay. Toma had seen the camp once, from a distance — the long tin-roofed barracks, the wire, the men moving in lines like ants that had forgotten why. He had not been able to eat for two days afterward.

*One life for two,* the voice on the radio had said. That was the arithmetic they offered him. Anis for the pilot and the woman. And Toma, who had spent his whole life trading — river passage for tobacco, stories for food, one name for another — had found, for the first time, an exchange he could not make.

Except that he already had.

* * *

The ambush had gone wrong in a way Toma still didn't fully understand. He had fired wide — deliberately, the bullet cracking off stone ten meters from where it should have gone — and shouted a warning that was half genuine panic, half gift. In the confusion, Jude and Mara had gone over the lip of the saddle into the scree, and the enforcers, six of them under a hard-eyed man named Voss, had spent forty minutes searching ground that should have taken five.

Toma had led them wrong after that. Not obviously — never obviously — but each fork in the trail, each decision point, he had chosen the path that bent away from the route the fugitives must have taken. He knew these mountains the way other men knew their mothers' faces. He could read a broken fern, a displaced pebble, the faint sweet smell of human sweat on cold air. And so he could also unread them. He walked the enforcers in circles through the high forest until Voss's patience thinned and the man's suspicion turned, slowly, like a knife being drawn, toward him.

"You found their sign easy enough before the saddle," Voss said on the second night, crouched over a fire that gave no warmth worth having. "Where did it go?"

"Scree eats tracks," Toma said. "You know this country?"

"I know people." Voss looked at him across the flames, and his eyes were the color of the glacier ice they'd come to cross — blue, ancient, and without any warmth in them at all. "You fired your first shot high. I counted."

Toma kept his face still. Inside, something turned over quietly, like a canoe shipping water.

They let him live because they needed him. That was all. The pass was closed to anyone who didn't know the ice, and Voss's men were lowlanders, valley men, men who looked at the white wall of the calving glacier the way children look at the sea. So Toma carried their radios, cooked their rice, marked their maps with routes that were true enough to be believed and false enough to cost them a day here, half a day there.

And on the third night, while the sentry dozed against his pack and the fire burned down to red eyes in the dark, Toma took the route plan out of Voss's map case, memorized it by the light of the coals, and walked away into the trees.

* * *

He climbed for a day and a half.

The trail Jude and Mara had left was old now, two days stale, but Toma had guided enough parties to know how certain people moved. The pilot was careful — a bush man, a man who understood that mountains keep accounts. The woman was tougher than she looked but her ankle was hurt; Toma had seen her favoring it at the river crossing, hiding it, the way proud people hide pain. Where she walked, the trail slowed. Where the pilot walked alone, scouting ahead, it quickened.

He found their cold camp above the tree line on the afternoon of the second day: flattened grass, a ring of stones, the ghost-smell of smoke. Beside the fire ring, pressed under a rock against the wind, someone had left a marker — a strip of red cloth torn from a pack strap, knotted around a stick. A signal for anyone following? Or a signal for him specifically?

Toma stood looking at it for a long time. The wind came off the ice fields and cut through his jacket like it wasn't there.

They knew he might follow. They knew what he'd done. And they had left him a sign anyway, which meant either forgiveness or a trap, and Toma, who had spent his life reading trails and faces, could not tell which. He thought about Anis again — Anis laughing, Anis teaching himself English from a missionary primer, Anis saying once, *Brother, when I have money I will buy a boat and we will fish the mouth of the Sepik and be rich in fish and nothing else.* One life for two. But the equation had failed. It had failed because the two lives he'd been asked to spend had trusted him, fed him from their own packs, taught him card games in the canoe, laughed at his jokes about the missionary and the crocodile. You could not spend lives that had become people.

Or you could. He had. That was the wound that wouldn't close.

Toma took the red cloth, folded it into his pocket, and walked on toward the glacier.

* * *

He found them at dusk in a hollow below the moraine, a shallow scoop of gravel where the wind couldn't reach, and he made sure they heard him coming — singing, actually, an old walking song his grandmother used to sing, loud and tuneless, so that no one would mistake his approach for anything but surrender.

It nearly got him killed anyway.

Jude came out of the rocks with a pistol — where he'd gotten it Toma never learned, some trade or salvage along the way — and the muzzle shook only a little, which was worse than if it had shaken a lot. Mara stood behind him with a climbing axe held like she meant it.

"Toma," Jude said. Just the name. The word hung in the thin cold air between them, and everything it contained — the waystation, the radio, the saddle, the shot fired wide — hung inside it.

"I brought medicine," Toma said. He set his pack down slowly, keeping his hands visible, and began laying things out on the gravel where they could see them. "For the ankle. Anti-inflammatory tablets, and a wrap. Also penicillin — the woman's cut on her hand, I saw it going bad at the crossing. You didn't see it. I saw it."

Mara's hand went unconsciously to her left palm, where the rope burn had darkened and swelled. She looked at the medicine the way starving people look at food, and then looked away from it, hating herself for looking.

"And this." Toma drew a folded sheet of paper from inside his shirt, where it had ridden against his skin for two days, warm as a second heartbeat. "Voss's route plan. When they move, which passes they watch, where they will put their men when the calving starts. There are six of them. Two are bad in the cold — lowland men, their feet are rotting. Voss has a satellite phone but the batteries are dying; he saves it for reports. He is afraid of his masters more than of you. That is useful to know about a man."

Jude didn't move. The pistol stayed level. "Why?"

"Why?" Toma almost laughed, and the laugh came out cracked, like ice under load. "Because I fired wide at the saddle. Because I led those men in circles for two days. Because—" He stopped. The mountain wind filled the silence. "Because my brother is in their camp at Timunke, and they offered me his life for yours, and I said yes. I said yes, Jude. I sat at that radio and I sold you. And then I couldn't sleep, and then I couldn't eat, and then I understood that I had already spent the money — there is no money, they don't pay, they just hold the thing you love over you forever — and that the only thing I had ever done in my life that was worth anything was that one bullet that went wide."

He ran out of breath. Above them, enormous and indifferent, the glacier groaned — a deep tectonic sound, the sound of a living thing turning in its sleep.

"So," Toma said. "Now you know everything. Use me or kill me or send me away. All three are fair. I ask nothing. I brought the medicine because the woman needs it, and I brought the paper because you need it, and I am done asking the world for things, because everything I have ever asked for has cost somebody else."

* * *

Mara took the penicillin. She did it without a word, without thanks, and Toma deserved exactly that and knew it. But later, when Jude had finally lowered the pistol and they sat around a fire no bigger than two cupped hands, she looked at him across the flames and said, "Your brother. What's his name?"

"Anis."

"If we get through this," she said, "we get him out. That's not a bargain. It's not a price. It's just what happens after, if there is an after." Her voice was rough with cold and exhaustion and something older. "I'm not forgiving you, Toma. I want you to understand that. My father died running from these people, and you helped them hunt us. But I've watched you walk behind us for two days — yes, I knew you were there, Jude didn't believe me, but fathers teach their daughters to listen for followers — and you could have led them straight to us and you didn't. So we'll use you. And maybe someday that becomes something else. Maybe not."

"That is more than fair," Toma said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much lighter the word *use* felt than the word *forgive*. Forgiveness was a debt erased. Use was a debt being paid. He could live inside a debt. He had lived inside one his whole life.

Jude said little. He studied the route plan by firelight for a long time, his lips moving slightly, committing it to memory the way bush pilots commit weather and fuel loads — absolutely, because lives depend on it. Then he folded the paper and handed it back.

"Keep it," he said. "If they catch us, they'll search me first. If they catch you—" He paused, and Toma saw him decide not to say what happened if they caught Toma. "You can talk your way past checkpoints. Talk yourself out of this too, if it comes to that."

"They will shoot me," Toma said simply. "That is fine. I have been dead since the waystation. Everything now is extra."

"Don't say that," Mara said sharply. "Don't you dare say that and then fight like a dead man tomorrow. Fight like a man with a brother."

Toma looked at her. The firelight moved on her face, and behind her the glacier stood pale under the rising moon, and he thought: *this woman has lost a father and gained a war in one month, and she is telling me how to live.*

"Yes," he said. "Like a man with a brother."

* * *

They slept in shifts, and Toma took the last watch, and near dawn he heard the glacier begin to speak.

It started as a crack, far off, high up in the seracs — a sound like the world's largest tree splitting — and then a long rumbling roar that rolled down the valley and set the gravel humming under his hands. Ice falling. The calving had begun.

By full light they could see it: the face of the glacier shedding itself into the meltwater lake at its foot, slabs the size of houses tilting and dropping, the boom of each impact arriving seconds late across the cold air. The pass beyond — the high notch through the range that was sealed by ice eleven months of the year — would be opening. For perhaps three days. Maybe less.

And on the far side, tiny with distance but unmistakable, figures were moving along the opposite moraine. Men with packs and rifles, setting themselves along the exit route like beads on a string.

Toma passed the binoculars to Jude and watched his face go still.

"They're not trying to stop us crossing," Jude said quietly. "They're waiting on the other side. Let the ice do the killing, and take whoever crawls off it."

"Then we don't crawl," Toma said. He pointed, not at the main pass where the enforcers waited, but lower and to the east, where the glacier bent around a shoulder of black rock. "There is another way. My grandfather took salt cattle through it in the old days. It is not on any map. It is not safe. Nothing is safe. But Voss does not know it exists, and his men are watching the wrong door."

Jude lowered the binoculars and looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the saddle — and whatever he saw in Toma's face, he nodded once.

"How long to reach it?"

"Half a day. We cross the ice tonight, in the dark, when the calving slows." Toma smiled, and for the first time in weeks the smile reached all the way up. "Bring the medicine, sister. Bring the paper. Tonight we spend everything we have left — and this time, we spend it together."
