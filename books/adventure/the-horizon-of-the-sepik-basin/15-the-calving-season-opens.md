## 15. The Calving Season Opens

The mountain told them first.

It was not a sound so much as a change in the quality of silence—a deep, subterranean groan that rose through the rock beneath their boots, through the bones of the scree field where they had made camp, up into the marrow of anyone standing still long enough to feel it. Mara felt it at dawn, lying in the shallow hollow Jude had dug for her against the wind, and for one disoriented moment she thought it was her own body failing: the cold, the thin air, the ache in her ankle that had never quite healed since the river crossing. Then she saw Jude sitting upright at the mouth of the overhang, utterly still, head tilted like a man listening to a conversation happening far below the floor of the world.

"It's starting," he said.

The glacier lay below them in the grey half-light, a river of ice frozen mid-fall down the throat of the range, two kilometers of fractured blue-white descending from the saddle they could not yet see toward the valley they could no longer remember. It looked inert. It had looked inert for six days, since Toma led them up out of the cloud forest and onto bare stone, since the trees gave way and the world became rock and ice and sky with nothing soft left in it. But now, listening, Mara understood that it had been moving all along—moving the way grief moves, imperceptibly, until the day it arrives all at once.

Another groan rolled through the morning, longer this time, and somewhere deep in the ice something cracked like a rifle shot.

* * *

They climbed to the lookout while the light was still coming up sideways off the snowfields—a shelf of granite Toma knew, three hundred meters above the glacier's shoulder, sheltered from the worst of the wind by a fin of rock streaked white with centuries of birdlime. From there the whole upper basin opened before them: the calving face where the glacier broke over a lip of bedrock into its lower tongue, the seracs leaning against each other like drunkards, and beyond them, past the shattered icefall, the notch of the pass itself—a dark V cut into the skyline, still holding winter, still choked with the avalanche debris that only the calving season's tremors would shake loose.

Toma reached the shelf last, breathing hard, and did not sit. He never sat anymore. Since the night he'd come back to them with medicine and the enforcers' route plan held out in both hands like an offering, he had carried himself like a man walking on a bridge he didn't trust. Mara watched him now, the way she had learned to watch everything above the tree line—the set of his shoulders, the direction his eyes went when he thought no one was looking. He took the binoculars from around his neck and handed them to Jude without being asked.

"Look at the far side," he said. "Below the pass. The rocks by the moraine."

Jude raised the glasses. Mara watched his face instead of the view, and what she saw there made her stomach drop before she'd seen anything at all.

"How many?" she asked.

"Count the fires," Toma said quietly. "Then double it."

She took the binoculars from Jude when he passed them back. Her hands were steadier than she expected. Through the lenses the far slope leapt close—grey scree, dirty snow, and there, tucked into the lee of the lateral moraine where the wind couldn't find them: orange nylon. A dome tent. Another beside it. A cook fire burning small and disciplined, smoke rising straight in the dead air. Figures moved between the tents with the unhurried economy of men who were not hiding, because they did not believe anyone was watching, or because they believed it didn't matter.

She counted the fires. Three. She doubled it.

"Twenty," she said. "Maybe more under the tarps."

"Kavera doesn't send twenty men for a map," Jude said. His voice was flat, the pilot's voice, the one that reported weather and fuel states and other facts that could not be argued with. "They send twenty men because they know what the map actually is."

Mara lowered the glasses. Her father's oilskin packet was inside her jacket, against her chest, where it had lived for weeks now—through the gorges, through the cloud forest, through the whiteout night when Jude had carried her on his back and spoken words into the storm that she would carry the rest of her life whether or not they survived to speak them again. The map had stopped being a treasure somewhere on the trail to the burned village. It had become testimony. Elias Wren had watched men die on this ground, had run from it for two years, had bled out on a stranger's airstrip rather than let Kavera bury what he'd seen—and now Kavera had come to finish the burial in person.

"They're waiting for the window too," she said.

"Of course." Toma crouched at the edge of the shelf, forearms on his knees, studying the icefall with the expression of a man reading a letter from someone he owed money to. "The pass opens for everyone or no one. Three days, maybe four if the weather holds cold. When the glacier calves enough to clear the debris choke, we cross—and so do they." He glanced back at her, and there was no apology in his face anymore; he had spent all his apologies on the night he came back. "My brother is down there. In that camp, or one like it. Every hour those men wait is an hour closer to him being freed or forgotten. I need you to understand that I will get you across. That is not in question. What happens after—" He stopped. Started again. "What happens after is why I brought you the route plan."

Jude took the binoculars again and swept them slowly along the glacier's length, reading it the way he read weather. Mara had watched him do this for weeks—the Cessna's engine, a river's current, a man's face—and she knew the look meant he was finding the truth underneath the surface. Below the calving face, the lower tongue of the glacier was littered with the season's first casualties: blocks of ice the size of houses, shed overnight, blue and fresh-fractured amid the older grey debris.

"She's shedding her skin," he murmured. "Every groan we heard last night was her letting go of the walls. By tomorrow the icefall will start moving in earnest. Day after, the choke at the pass should be loose enough to climb through." He lowered the glasses. "And the day after that, the meltwater rises and the whole thing turns into a killing floor. We go when it opens. Not before, not after."

"And them?"

Jude looked across the basin at the orange tents, and Mara saw something settle in his face—the thing she had come to recognize as the opposite of the man who had landed at Wewak carrying a dead man's promise and a lifetime of practiced invisibility. "Them we can't stop from crossing. But we don't have to cross behind them. We cross ahead."

* * *

They spent the day doing nothing, and it was the hardest work yet.

Above the tree line there was no gathering firewood worth the name, no water but what you melted from snow over a spirit stove, no task large enough to keep fear occupied. They melted snow. They ate the last of the rice Toma had carried up from the waystation, stretched with nothing, because stretching was over now. Mara rewrapped her ankle and found, to her surprise, that the swelling had finally gone down—that her body, which had seemed like such a liability in the whiteout, was quietly repairing itself in the thin cold air, as if the altitude burned away everything nonessential, including, sometimes, despair.

In the afternoon the wind died completely, and in that enormous stillness the glacier began to speak in earnest.

It was not like the night sounds. This was daylight violence, visible and undeniable. From the lookout they watched the calving face begin to work—first a shudder running through the seracs like a shiver down a spine, then a lean, a hesitation, and then a section of the face the size of a church simply let go. It fell without hurry, the way large things fall, and the sound arrived seconds later: not a crack but a concussion, a flat boom that slapped off the valley walls and came back and came back again, echoing long after the ice had finished becoming rubble at the base of the fall. The report rolled out across the basin and up the slopes, and somewhere on the far side, faint as a dog bark, Mara heard answering voices—men shouting, brief and sharp, then quiet again.

"They felt it too," she said.

"Everyone feels it." Toma stood at the edge of the shelf with his arms folded, watching the dust of ice crystals drift off the fresh collapse. "The old people say the mountain is counting. All winter it holds its breath, and in the calving season it counts aloud. Three days of counting. Then it closes its hand." He looked at her sidelong. "Your father knew these numbers. He crossed here twice, before. He told me once—he was drunk, at the waystation, years ago—he told me the pass was the only honest thing in the gold country. It opens for everyone the same. Rich, poor, guilty, innocent. The ice does not care who you are."

Mara turned that over as another serac leaned and groaned and held. Her father, drunk and honest, two different countries of the same man. She had spent the whole journey learning him in fragments—an old woman's memory of debts paid, a survivor's recognition of his handwriting, the testimony pressed into oilskin—and every fragment made the anger harder to hold and the love harder to refuse. He had run from this mountain. Now she was bringing him home across it, in pieces, in ink.

At dusk the light turned the glacier rose and gold, and for a few minutes it was almost beautiful—this vast cold engine grinding itself apart above a valley full of grieving people. Then the light went, and the temperature fell like a dropped stone, and they retreated to the overhang and lay in their bags watching their breath turn to frost on the rock an inch from their faces.

In the dark, Jude's hand found hers. No words. There had been words, on the worst night, in the whiteout, and neither of them had taken them back—but up here words were fuel, and fuel was rationed. His palm was cracked and cold and certain. She held on.

Sometime in the small hours, the glacier boomed again—twice, three times, a drumroll rolling up the valley—and beneath their backs the rock hummed like a struck string.

* * *

Morning came hard and clear, the kind of cold that makes the air feel manufactured, and Toma woke them with a hand on each of their shoulders and one word:

"Now."

He led them to the lookout at a pace just short of running. The basin below had transformed overnight. The calving face had retreated visibly, a raw blue scar where the ice had peeled away, and the lower tongue was unrecognizable—yesterday's orderly debris field churned into chaos, new ice blocks stacked on old, the whole surface broken open like ground after an earthquake. And at the head of it, high up near the pass, the debris choke that had sealed the notch all winter was moving. Even without the binoculars Mara could see it: a slow-motion avalanche in reverse, blocks shifting, settling, sliding aside as the ice beneath them melted and groaned and let go.

Through the glasses, the V of the pass showed threads of dark rock between the white. Passable. Today. Maybe tomorrow. After that—

She swung the glasses to the far side and her breath caught.

The camp was striking itself. Tents coming down. Men forming into a loose column at the base of the moraine, gear shouldered, weapons visible now—not even pretending. At their head stood a figure she recognized from Wewak, from the polished office with the bottled water and the contract on the table: the broad shoulders, the deliberate calm. Verrick. He was looking up at the pass the way a man looks at a door he has already paid for.

"They're moving," she said. "Now. This morning."

Jude took the glasses. A long silence. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet in the way she had learned meant the opposite of calm. "They'll reach the choke by midday. We'd have to cross behind them, in their tracks, with them waiting on the far side." He lowered the binoculars slowly. "Or we go now, straight down the glacier's shoulder, through the icefall before it's fully open, and beat them to the notch by hours. Hours, Toma. Can you do it? Is there a way?"

Toma was already looking at the icefall—not at the route but at the ice itself, reading the fractures, the lean of the seracs, the rhythm of the calving like a drummer reading a rhythm. The mountain boomed again as if to punctuate the question, and a sheet of ice the size of a tennis court sheared off the face and thundered down, and Toma did not flinch.

"There is a way," he said. "My father took cargo through it in the bad years, when the government patrols closed the main route. It is not a route. It is a series of decisions, very fast, and the ice makes most of them for you." He turned, and for the first time since the night market, since the betrayal, since the warning shot fired wide on purpose, Toma smiled—and it was not a comforting smile. "We go down the shoulder, across the tongue above the icefall, and up through the choke while it is still falling open. If we move now, we are through before Verrick ties his second boot. If we hesitate—" He shrugged. "The mountain closes its hand."

Mara touched the oilskin packet through her jacket. Somewhere below and behind them lay the burned village and its scattered survivors; somewhere ahead, beyond the pass, the hidden valley and its council of elders waited with a law older than any nation. Between the two stretched two kilometers of actively collapsing ice, and twenty armed men who wanted what she carried buried forever.

Jude was coiling rope with quick, economical movements, checking knots by feel, and when he glanced up at her there was no question in his eyes. They had argued about everything on this mountain except the things that mattered, and the things that mattered had been settled in a whiteout, in the dark, in the palm of a cold hand.

"The window's opening," he said. "Which means it's already closing."

Mara looked one last time at the far side—at Verrick's column beginning to move, small and inevitable as a shadow crossing a floor—and slung her pack.

"Then let's not be behind it," she said.

Toma led them onto the ice.
