## 16. The Crossing

The glacier began to speak before dawn.

Jude heard it first as a sound he couldn't name—deep, tectonic, like the earth clearing its throat somewhere beneath their boots. Then came the crack, sharp as a rifle shot, echoing off the valley walls, and after it a long rumble that rolled away down the ice like thunder learning to walk.

Toma stood at the edge of the moraine, his breath pluming white in the pre-light, and said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet in a way Jude had never heard from him before—not the salesman's warmth of the river towns, not the easy charm that had fooled them both at the waystation, but something older and stripped bare.

"It's opening," Toma said. "The pass. Three days, maybe two if the sun stays hard on it."

"And if we wait for it to close?" Mara asked.

Toma turned to look at her. In the grey half-dark his face was hollowed out, and Jude saw there what the mountain had already begun to carve: a man who had spent his last coin of safety and knew it. "If you're on it when it closes," Toma said, "the mountain keeps you."

They roped up in the blue hour before sunrise, when the ice was still frozen hard enough to hold and the shadows hadn't yet softened into lies. Toma went first, then Mara, then Jude on the anchor rope, and the three of them moved out onto the glacier like pilgrims onto a river they had no right to cross.

The light came slowly, the way it does at altitude—not rising so much as seeping, the darkness thinning until the world revealed itself all at once. And what a world. The glacier stretched ahead of them for nearly three kilometers, a frozen flood between two walls of black stone, its surface fractured into seracs the size of houses, its crevasses splitting open like mouths in the pale blue light. Somewhere beneath them, forty meters down or four hundred, water was running—the sound of it came up through the ice, a vast underground rushing that made the whole surface feel less like ground than like a roof over an abyss.

"Walk where I walk," Toma said. "Nowhere else. Not one step to left, not one step to right." He planted his probe pole, tested, planted again. "The ice lies. It puts snow over its wounds and dares you to find them."

They walked.

For the first hour it was almost bearable—hard going, but honest going, the kind of travel where your legs complain but your mind can rest. Then the glacier began to change under them. The crevasses widened. What had been cracks became chasms, and what had been chasms became bridges—snow bridges, arched over the voids like the spines of buried animals, white against the deeper white of the glacier's skin.

"The bridge is strong when it's cold," Toma said, stopping before one of them. It spanned a gap perhaps two meters wide, but the darkness below it went down and down without offering any bottom. "It holds us now because the night froze it. By afternoon—" He didn't finish. He stepped onto it instead, weight forward, poles spread wide, and crossed in six slow steps that took about a year off the length of everyone's life.

Mara followed. She had stopped speaking somewhere in the second hour, and Jude watched her go across the bridge with her jaw set and her eyes fixed on Toma's footprints, and he thought: *she has her father's stubbornness and none of his cowardice.* Elias Wren had run from this country for two years. His daughter was walking straight into its teeth.

Then it was Jude's turn.

He stepped onto the bridge and felt it give—not break, not even crack audibly, just *give*, a soft settling underfoot like stepping onto a mattress over an open grave. Every nerve in his body screamed at once. His training said *weight back, retreat*. Toma's voice, flat with command, said: *Forward. Slow. Don't stop. Stopping is death.*

Jude went forward. One step. Two. On the third step the snow sagged to his shin and he heard, beneath his boots, a sound like a giant sighing—a groan of compressed air being pushed through the arch of the bridge as its structure began to fail. He did not look down. He thought about looking down and decided, with a clarity that surprised him, that he would rather die ignorant of the exact depth of his grave.

Four steps. Five. Six.

His boot found rock-hard ice on the far side and he was across, shaking so hard he had to grip his own knees to make it stop.

* * *

By midmorning the sun was fully on the ice and the glacier had begun to die around them in real time. Water ran in a hundred silver threads. Serac towers groaned and settled. And once—only once—they heard the sound that would define the rest of the crossing: a deep boom from somewhere behind and above, followed by the crack-and-hiss of several tons of ice calving off a wall and sliding down onto the glacier's shoulder.

Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. They all understood the same thing at once: the window was closing faster than Toma had promised.

"We move faster," Toma said. "Faster means lighter feet. Lighter feet mean the bridges hold. We do not run—we flow. Like water."

They flowed.

And somewhere in the next hour, something happened that Jude would spend years afterward trying to describe and never quite manage. The terror didn't leave. But it changed. It stopped being a wall in front of him and became a current underneath him, carrying all three of them together at the same speed toward the same edge. When Mara stumbled on blue ice, Jude's hand was there before either of them decided it should be. When Jude's crampon strap worked loose, Mara knelt in the wind without a word and cinched it tight while he balanced on the slope, and neither of them thanked the other because thanks had become unnecessary—thanks were for strangers, and they were no longer strangers, not after the whiteout, not after the saddle, not after everything the mountain had taken and given back.

Even Toma had changed. The guide who led them now bore no resemblance to the charming fixer who'd met them at the river dock. This Toma moved like a man reading scripture, each step deliberate, each probe certain, and once, when they reached a junction of crevasses too tangled to read, he stopped and closed his eyes for ten full seconds—listening, Jude realized, listening *through* his boots—and chose the line that held.

"You've done this before," Jude said quietly, when they paused in the lee of a serac. "Not this glacier. Something like it."

"My grandfather's country," Toma said. "High valleys east of here. Ice country people." He looked out at the white waste and his voice dropped. "I forgot it for a long time. Chose to forget." His eyes flicked to Jude, and there it was again—that guilt, that ledger behind his eyes. "The mountain is teaching me to remember."

Jude let it lie. There would be time for accounts later, or there wouldn't. Here, on the ice, a man was only as good as his next step, and Toma's next step had been perfect for six hours.

* * *

It happened in the early afternoon, at the place Toma had warned them about from the beginning—the long traverse beneath the icefall, where the glacier bent around a spur of black rock and the crevasses ran diagonal to their path like the ribs of some drowned leviathan.

Toma crossed the first bridge cleanly. Mara crossed it cleanly. Jude was halfway across when the world ended.

There was no warning. No groan, no crack, no courtesy at all. One moment the snow beneath his boots was solid; the next it simply ceased to exist, collapsing inward like a breath knocked out of a body, and Jude dropped.

The rope caught him.

The fall was short—two meters, maybe less—but it emptied him completely. He swung once against the far wall of the crevasse, boots scraping blue ice, and looked down past his own dangling body into a corridor of light and shadow that descended farther than his eyes could follow, glowing that impossible glacial blue that photographers chase and never capture. Cold air rose out of it, ancient and absolute.

"Hold!" Toma was shouting. "Mara—anchor! Do not move!"

Above, Mara had thrown herself flat, driving her axe into the ice, and the rope went taut between them—a straight line of nylon holding Jude's whole weight over the abyss. He could hear her breathing, ragged with effort, and Toma's voice cutting through it, calm as a surgeon: "Jude. Listen to me. Put your feet on the wall. Boots flat. Now."

Jude put his boots on the wall.

"Good. Now walk. Walk up the wall while we take the slack. Small steps. The ice will cut you places for your crampons. Let it cut you."

Jude walked up the wall of the crevasse, step by step, his thighs burning, his hands screaming inside their gloves, while two people he had known for mere weeks hauled him back into the world of the living. When his waist finally cleared the lip and he rolled onto solid ice, he lay there gasping, staring at the sky, feeling his heart try to beat its way out through his ribs.

Mara's face appeared above him, framed against the glare, streaked with sweat and frost. She didn't say *are you okay*. She didn't say anything at all. She just gripped his jacket with both fists and pulled his forehead against hers, and they stayed like that for five seconds—an eternity, out here—in which everything that had gone unspoken since Wewak was said without a single word.

Then Toma said, "The bridge is gone. We find another way," and the moment folded itself away into whatever pocket of the soul holds such moments for later, for old age, for the telling.

* * *

They found another way. It cost them two hours and a detour that took them directly beneath the calving face of the upper icefall, close enough that the falling ice threw spray across their backs like sleet. Twice more they crossed bridges that groaned. Once, Toma probed a stretch of innocent-looking snow and the probe vanished to its full length, and he simply marked it with a wand and flowed around it, and no one said anything, because by now saying things had become a luxury they'd traded away for speed.

The far side came upon them gradually and then all at once. First the slope began to rise instead of fall. Then the crevasses narrowed, healed, disappeared beneath fresh snow. Then there was rock—real rock, black and solid and blessedly immovable—and Toma was pulling them up a scree gully with his hands as well as his feet, climbing out of the glacier's kingdom into the kingdom of stone.

At the top, they turned and looked back.

The glacier lay below them in the late light, vast and indifferent, already erasing their passage. Wind was filling their boot prints. Somewhere behind them, a serac the size of a cathedral leaned and fell with a boom that rolled up the valley like a door closing.

Toma stood with his hands on his knees, breathing hard, and then he laughed—a short, disbelieving bark of a laugh.

"We crossed," he said. "We actually crossed."

Mara sat down on the rock and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook—not with crying, Jude realized, but with laughter too, the wild kind that comes out of the body when the body has been told all day that it's going to die. Jude sat down between them and put an arm around each of them, and the three of them sat on the black stone above the ice and laughed like lunatics at absolutely nothing, at absolutely everything, while the glacier boomed and calved below and the sun slid down behind the peaks.

They were still laughing when Jude saw it.

Below them, far down the valley on the far side of the pass, where the glacier's meltwater gathered into the first brown thread of a river—a thread of smoke. Thin, deliberate, human. A cook fire, burning where no traveler had any reason to be.

Toma saw it a heartbeat after Jude did. The laughter died in his throat.

"That's not the valley," Toma said quietly. "That's the wrong side of the pass. Nobody camps there unless they're waiting for someone coming through."

Jude raised his binoculars. The fire resolved: three figures around it, small in the glass, and beside them—catching the last red light of the sun—a gleam of metal that could only be a rifle barrel.

"They're not waiting for us," Jude said. "They're watching the exit."

Mara stood. The laughter was gone from her entirely now, replaced by something harder and colder than fear.

"Then we don't take the exit," she said. "We take them by surprise. Tonight, before they know we're through."

Behind them, the glacier boomed again, sealing the way they'd come. Ahead, the fire burned in the gathering dark, patient as a spider.
