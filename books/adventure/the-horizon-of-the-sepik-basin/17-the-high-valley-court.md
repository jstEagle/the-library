## 17. The High Valley Court

The valley announced itself before they saw it — first as a change in the sound of the river, which stopped roaring through the gorge and opened out into something broader and slower, and then as a smell: woodsmoke, sweet potato gardens, pig, the green breath of a thousand growing things that had never been logged.

Mara came down the last stretch of trail behind Jude with her father's map wrapped in oilskin against her chest, and when the trees finally parted she had to stop walking, because stopping was the only honest response to what lay below.

It was a bowl of green set into the shoulders of the mountains, perhaps three kilometers across, ringed by ridgelines so steep they looked like the walls of a cupped hand. The glacier hung at its head, blue-white and ancient, calving off meltwater that fed a braided river running silver through the valley floor. Gardens climbed the lower slopes in neat terraces — kaukau, taro, bananas, all of it tended, all of it alive. Houses with iron roofs and thatch stood in family clusters around a central green where, even from this distance, Mara could see people moving: women bent over washing in a channel, children chasing a dog between the houses, men working timber under an open-sided shelter.

Smoke rose from a dozen cooking fires. Somewhere down there, someone was singing.

"It's real," she said, not meaning to say it aloud. "I thought—" She didn't finish. What she'd thought, after everything — the ambush at the saddle, Toma's warning shot cracking wide over their heads, four days above the tree line with frostbite whitening her fingertips and Jude carrying her on his back through a whiteout that erased the world — what she'd thought was that the valley might be a story. A rumor old men told to keep hope breathing. Elias had spoken of it once, drunk on cheap rum in Wewak years ago, a place beyond the law where wronged people kept their own accounts. She'd filed it away as one more of his beautiful lies.

But here it was, breathing smoke and song beneath the glacier.

"They've survived up here for two years," Jude said quietly beside her. "Kavera knows about them — has to. And hasn't come." He shaded his eyes against the high thin light. "That tells you something about how strong they are."

"Or how afraid."

"Both," Jude said. "Usually both."

Toma stood a little apart from them, the way he had since the saddle, since he'd fired wide and shouted *run* in a voice cracked down the middle. He carried the enforcers' route plan folded inside his shirt like a stone he couldn't put down. His face, when Mara glanced at it, held the expression of a man approaching a house where he owed a debt too large to name.

"The elders will ask who I am," he said. "What I did."

"Yes," Mara said. It wasn't cruelty. She'd learned, somewhere on the ice, that lying to these people — or letting them walk into a lie — would cost more than any truth. "And you'll tell them yourself. That's the only way this works."

Toma nodded slowly, once, and started down the trail.

* * *

They were seen long before they reached the green, of course. Word traveled ahead of them down the slopes — Mara watched it move, runners passing between garden plots, a boy sprinting toward the central cluster of houses, and by the time the three of them crossed a footbridge over the milky river a crowd had gathered, maybe two hundred people, standing in loose silence rather than the shouting welcome of a festival.

Mara felt the weight of every eye.

These were the survivors. She understood it without being told, the way you understand weather on your skin. The old woman at the front wore mourning bands of black orchid fiber gone gray with age; a man near her had a burn scar covering half his face and neck, shiny as poured wax. Children stared from behind their mothers' skirts. Two years since Kavera's men had come to the village on the map her father carried — the village whose name had been scraped out of every government register, whose dead had been entered nowhere — and these were the ones who had walked up into the clouds and built a life the world was not permitted to know about.

An elder stepped forward from the line. He was small and immensely upright, white-haired, with a walking stick of black palm wood and eyes that seemed to have finished being surprised by anything decades ago. Behind him stood others — a woman with a baby slung on her back, a younger man with a rifle slung casually the way farmers carry tools, an old woman whose gaze fixed on Toma and did not let go.

"You come from below," the elder said. His Pidgin was slow and formal, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You come through the high cold place. Only two kinds of people cross the ice. Fools, and those who are brought." A ripple went through the crowd — not quite laughter, but adjacent to it. "Which are you?"

"Brought," Mara said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "By a dead man's promise."

She unwrapped the oilskin.

* * *

They took her to the haus tambaran — the council house — a long building of pit-pile construction at the edge of the green, its ridgepole carved with figures whose faces had been smoked black by generations of fires. Inside it was dim and smelled of earth and ash and old wood, lit by a single doorway and slatted vents that laid bars of gold light across the packed floor.

Mara knelt — they indicated kneeling, and she knelt — on woven mats before a semicircle of elders. There were seven of them, five men and two women, and they arranged themselves with the unhurried gravity of people who had never once been asked to hurry by anyone they respected. Elder Kombai sat at the center, the small upright man from the bridge. The burned man sat to his right. To his left, the old woman with the unrelenting eyes, whose name, Mara learned, was Yawanisa, and who had lost, she would later understand, a husband, two sons, and a granddaughter on the night the syndicate came.

Jude knelt beside her. Toma knelt further back, apart, his hands open on his knees, palms up — a posture of surrender or supplication, Mara couldn't tell which, and suspected he couldn't either.

"Speak," said Kombai. "We have time. We are the only ones who do."

So Mara spoke.

She told it in the order it had happened to her, because that was the only order she trusted herself to keep straight through the shaking in her chest. A stranger crash-landing at dusk on a mission strip in the Sepik lowlands. A bullet wound. A man bleeding out on the gravel with his hands pressed flat to the earth as if trying to hold onto it, pressing an oilskin package into a bush pilot's grip and saying *my daughter, Wewak, swear it, swear it.* She told them about the map — how she'd spread it on a trading post table and found the gold field marked in her father's cramped hand, and found, overlapping it exactly, the boundaries of a lease block held by the Kavera Mining Consortium. How she'd dug through records until she found the hole where a village should have been: a census entry struck through, a mission station log with pages razored out, a patrol report dated two years past that mentioned, in a clerk's careless margin, *"disputed ground — resolved."*

Resolved. As if a village were a debt.

Then she unfolded the map itself, and with it the letter — Elias's testimony, written in the weeks before his death in a handwriting gone shaky, addressed to no one and everyone, and Mara read it aloud in the smoky light while the elders listened without moving.

Her father had been on the ground when Kavera's survey teams moved in. He'd been prospecting the same alluvial field, and when the villagers refused to leave their land, he'd been there. He described what happened — the shooting that began when a young villager threw a spear at a bulldozer, the burning of houses with people inside them, the bodies weighted and sunk in the river gorge, the official story filed in Port Moresby: inter-clan violence, regrettable, case closed. Elias wrote that he'd run, because running was the only thing he knew how to do, and that he'd spent two years telling himself he'd testify someday, and that someday was a coward's country. He wrote that he was sending the evidence to his daughter because she was harder than he was. He wrote, in the last lines, that he did not ask forgiveness, because he had learned that some things could not be asked for, only paid.

When Mara finished reading, the fire crackled. Somewhere outside, a pig complained. An elder shifted his weight, and the floorboards spoke softly beneath him.

Yawanisa rose.

Mara's whole body tensed. The old woman crossed the space between the council and where Mara knelt, moving with a deliberation that made each step feel like a footfall on Mara's own chest. She stopped directly before her. Up close her face was a landscape — deep-cut valleys, ridges of bone, and eyes that had looked at the smoke rising from her own burning village and had refused, afterward, ever to look away from anything again.

"You are the blood of the man who watched," Yawanisa said.

It was not a question, and Mara could not make it one. "Yes."

"He watched our daughters die and he ran to the coast, and he lived two years, and then he died in a bed of sky far from this ground." Yawanisa's voice was level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "And now his daughter brings us his paper and asks us to make it a weapon."

"I'm not asking you anything," Mara said, and heard her own voice break on the second word. She made herself keep going. "I'm not asking you to fight for him. I'm asking you to take what's yours. This is your ground on this paper. Your dead. Your testimony that no clerk ever wrote down. I just—" She stopped, swallowed. "He was a coward. I loved him anyway, and I hated him for it, sometimes both in the same hour. But the last thing he did with his life was point home. So I brought it home. Do with it what clan law says must be done. That's all. That's all I came to do."

Silence held the house the way water holds a stone.

Then Yawanisa reached out and touched the map — one finger, lightly, on the place where the village had been, the place the clerk had called *resolved* — and drew the finger down to the river gorge below it, tracing the route the bodies had taken, and Mara realized the old woman was showing the other elders, showing them in the old way, reading the ground aloud, and that the room had begun to murmur in a low collective voice like wind finding a cave mouth.

Kombai lifted his stick and tapped the floor twice. The murmuring stopped.

"The paper is true," he said. Not a question either. One of the younger elders had examined the letter's ink and folds; another had questioned Mara and Jude separately, in detail, for what felt like hours but was perhaps forty minutes, and their answers had matched. "We have waited two years for this ground to be spoken of honestly. We will speak of it now, and we will act, and our acting will be according to the law of this valley, which is the law of the ancestors, which does not care what is written in Port Moresby."

He turned his head slightly, and for the first time his gaze went past Mara to where Toma knelt with his palms open.

"And you," Kombai said. "You carried the enemies' road-plan inside your shirt. You led them to us once already with your voice on the radio. And at the saddle you fired wide and gave these two their lives." He paused. "Stand up, Toma. Walk to the fire. Tell us all of it — the brother they hold, the bargain you tried to make, what price you set on your own soul. Leave nothing out. In this house, a hidden thing rots faster than a confessed one."

Toma rose. His legs shook; Mara saw it, saw him see her seeing it, and saw him lift his chin anyway. He walked to the central hearth and stood in the firelight, and began to talk — about his brother Amos in the labor camp at Kiunga, about the radio calls, about the exact shape of the deal he'd offered the enforcers, two strangers' lives for one brother's freedom — and he talked until his voice gave out, and then he knelt again in the ashes' warm light and put his forehead nearly to the floor.

The elders conferred in a language Mara didn't know, voices low and quick, and the wait stretched, and outside the light changed from gold to copper to the first violet of evening.

At last Kombai spoke to the whole house.

"Toma will be judged with the others. He will carry water for the widows and stand watch on the cold ridge until the matter is settled — and if his heart is true, the matter will settle him too. Mercy is earned in labor, not words." Then the old eyes came back to Mara and Jude, and something in them shifted, a door opening a crack in a wall. "As for the daughter of Elias Wren, and the pilot who kept a dying man's promise across the ice — you have given us back our names. The village had a name. You will hear it tonight, spoken for the first time in two years, at the fire. Eat with us. Rest. Because—"

He stopped. From outside came the sound Mara had been dreading since the saddle: a runner's feet on the bridge planks, fast, urgent, and then the runner himself in the doorway, young, chest heaving, pointing back the way he'd come — toward the pass, toward the glacier, toward the three-day window that was already closing.

"Elders!" the boy gasped. "Men at the valley mouth. Armed men. Many. They're making camp in the rocks below the icefall — and they've sent a messenger ahead under a white cloth."

Kombai rose to his full small height, black palm stick in hand, and the firelight made his shadow climb the carved ridgepole like a spirit waking.

"So," he said calmly. "They have followed justice up the mountain. Good. Let them come and be judged where they cannot hide."
