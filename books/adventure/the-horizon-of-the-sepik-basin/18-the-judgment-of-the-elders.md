## 18. The Judgment of the Elders

Kombai had judged forty-one cases in the high valley, and he had learned long ago that the weight of a judgment was decided before the elders ever sat down to speak. It was decided by who came. By what they carried in their hands and their faces. By the silence they brought into the circle and could not put down.

This morning the silence was heavy enough to bend the pandanus roof beams.

He had risen before first light, as he always did on a judgment day, and walked the perimeter of the council ground alone, his bare feet finding the cold stone paths by memory. The mist lay in the valley like milk poured into a bowl. Below the terraces, the river ran gray-green with glacier melt, loud with its own urgency. Somewhere up at the ice line, the calving season had opened—the deep cracking reports rolling down the valley like distant artillery, each one a reminder that the mountain kept its own court, with its own sentences, and never adjourned.

Kombai was seventy-one years old. His hair had gone the color of wood ash, and his knees spoke to him in the cold language of old men. But his eyes were still good, and this morning they found what he expected to find: smoke from the watch fires at the valley mouth. Not cooking smoke. Signal smoke—three columns, close together.

Strangers were coming. Armed strangers. And they were not being quiet about it.

He stood for a long time watching the smoke, feeling the morning cold move through the woven fibers of his armbands, through the boar's tusk pendant at his chest that he had worn for every judgment since his father died. The tusk had belonged to four judges before him. It had heard everything. He sometimes wondered whether it remembered more than he did—the cases where mercy had been wrong, and the ones where severity had been a coward's disguise.

Then he turned back toward the great house and went to wake the others.

* * *

The council convened in the roundhouse at midmorning, when the mist burned off the terraces and the light came slanting through the smoke holes in long amber blades. Eleven elders sat in the circle. Kombai took his place last, as was proper, and looked around at the faces gathered there.

Old Sereva, who remembered the valley before the war planes crossed it, her hands folded over a walking stick carved with the names of her grandmothers. Mendi the younger, sharp-eyed, impatient, who had once argued that the valley should open itself to trade and had been wrong in ways that cost them two boys. Yawan of the river clan, whose brother had married into the lowland villages and brought news up the water every season like a man ferrying seeds. And the others—farmers, hunters, one woman who had been a teacher in Goroka before she chose to come home and stay.

And sitting outside the circle, because they were not of the valley and could not sit inside it, three visitors.

The pilot woman—Mara Wren—and the bush pilot Jude, both hollow-eyed and frost-burned from the crossing, wrapped in blankets someone had given them at the gate. And Toma, the guide, kneeling apart from them with his wrists bound loosely in front of him—not as a prisoner exactly, though everyone understood he was one. He had come down off the ice carrying medicine and the enforcers' route plan in a waterproof pouch, and he had laid it all at Kombai's feet without a word of explanation, and then he had knelt, and he had stayed kneeling.

Kombai had watched him kneel for a long moment before speaking. There were many kinds of surrender. This one had the smell of sincerity, but Kombai had been fooled by smells before.

"Begin," he said now, to Mara Wren.

She rose. She moved stiffly—one hand bandaged, frost-nipped fingers wrapped in gauze—and she carried a bundle wrapped in oilskin the way a soldier carries ammunition, close against the body, angled away from other people. She unwrapped it on the woven mat between the circles.

The map. Kombai had seen maps before—government surveyors had come through twice in his lifetime, drawing lines on paper that meant nothing to anyone who lived inside those lines. But this map was different. It was drawn in pencil and ink over an old aerial photograph, annotated in a cramped hand, and it marked the ground where the village of Kwaimbaita had stood.

Kwaimbaita. Two years dead. Erased so thoroughly that even the government rolls listed the place as "uninhabited at time of lease grant." Kombai knew better. Three survivors of Kwaimbaita lived in this valley. One of them sat at the edge of the circle this morning, an old man named Pidik, who had walked out of the burning with a broken arm and had not spoken above a whisper since.

Mara Wren told the story plainly, which Kombai appreciated. She did not weep for effect. She explained that the map had belonged to her father, Elias Wren, a prospector; that he had witnessed what happened at Kwaimbaita; that he had spent two years running from the men he called the Consortium, carrying this evidence and doing nothing with it; that he had died at a small airstrip in the basin with a bullet in him and the map pressed into a stranger's hands.

"He didn't have the courage to bring it here himself," she said. Her voice did not shake, but something under it did. "He traded it to save his own skin, and then he made someone else carry his debt. I'm finishing it because it should have been finished two years ago."

Silence. The fire cracked in the center pit.

Pidik stirred. The old survivor leaned forward on his stick, and Kombai nodded to him—an elder's permission, small but real—and Pidik began to speak.

His voice was a whisper, but the roundhouse was built to carry whispers. He told them what he had told no government officer, no missionary, no journalist, because none of them had ever climbed high enough or cared enough to ask. He described the day the Consortium's men came to Kwaimbaita—how they arrived saying they had lease papers, how the headman showed them the papers meant nothing because the gold in the river gravel belonged to the living and the dead together, how the men smiled and left and came back three nights later with gasoline and guns.

He named the dead. Forty-one names, spoken slowly, each one given its full weight, while the fire burned and the glacier boomed far overhead like a slow drum. Some of the names Kombai knew—cousins by marriage, trading partners, a girl who had come up to the valley for a festival two summers before the burning and had sung until dawn. When Pidik finished, he unrolled a bundle of his own: photographs, water-stained, of the village before. Children on the riverbank. A spirit house with fresh paint. A world.

"The man Wren," Pidik said finally, looking at Mara, "was there that night. Hiding above the gardens with his survey gear. He saw the trucks. He heard us screaming. And he packed his instruments and went downriver." The old man's eyes were not accusing; they were worse than accusing—they were simply factual, the way the glacier is factual. "But he wrote it all down. Every name. Every truck number. We know this because a mission teacher copied pages from his journal once, before he burned the rest."

Mara Wren closed her eyes. Kombai watched grief move across her face like weather, watched her master it, watched her understand—in the way of outsiders who arrive thinking they are delivering something—that she had carried not a gift but a confession, and that the confession was partly hers by inheritance.

* * *

They broke at noon for taro and smoked fish, and during the break, the runner came down from the valley mouth at a dead sprint.

Kavera's enforcers had reached the gorge below the falls. Nine men, maybe eleven—heavy boots, automatic rifles, satellite phone. They had stopped at the boundary stones and were waiting. Waiting, the runner repeated, as if the word itself were strange. One of them had fired a burst into the air an hour ago, but since then they had only waited, sending up signal smoke of their own, patient as crocodiles.

"They know we're here," Mendi said. "They're letting us know they know. That is a message."

"It is more than a message," said Yawan. "It is a test. They want to see if we will run, hide, or fight. Whichever we do, they have already planned for it."

Kombai ate his taro slowly and said nothing. He was listening to the glacier. It had been calving since dawn—the deep reports coming faster now, the mountain shedding its winter skin—and he had lived long enough to know that the ice did nothing without reason. The pass would be opening. The same meltwater that freed the crossing route also swelled the rivers below, and swollen rivers made the long way around impossible for another week at least. The enforcers at the gorge had one road in, and it led through the valley, and they knew it, and they were confident enough to wait in the open.

Confident men. Kombai had judged confident men before.

When the council reconvened, he had the visitors brought back in, and he asked the question he had been turning over since morning.

"The woman Wren. You came here through the ice, wounded, pursued. Why did you not go to the government? To the police at Wewak, or Mount Hagen? Why climb a glacier to bring paper to old men in a valley that appears on no map?"

It was Mara who answered, after a silence. "Because we tried the law," she said. "In Wewak, a Consortium representative offered me money for the map in a hotel lobby, in daylight, with lawyers' cards on the table. When I refused, his men followed us. The police station had a Kavera calendar hanging in the office. My father ran for two years instead of going to the authorities because he believed the authorities were bought, and I hated him for that belief—but I've seen enough now to stop hating him for it." She met Kombai's eyes. "You're the only court left that isn't owned."

Old Sereva made a sound in her throat—approval or contempt, hard to say. "And what do you want from this court?" she asked. "Vengeance? Compensation? Do you want us to make you feel clean?"

"I want the names read aloud where they can't be erased again," Mara said. "That's all my father owed, and all I owe. What happens after that belongs to you."

Kombai let the words settle. Then he turned to the kneeling man.

"Toma."

The guide lifted his head. Up close, Kombai could see the exhaustion carved into him—the sleepless nights on the ice, the guilt worn down to the quick. He had betrayed these two travelers to the Consortium, sold their route for the chance to free his brother from a labor camp at the mine. And then he had climbed back up the glacier after them with medicine and the enemy's plans, asking nothing.

"You sold them," Kombai said. It was not a question, and Toma did not treat it as one.

"Yes."

"Why do you kneel here now?"

Toma was quiet for a long moment. "Because the men in the gorge will kill my brother anyway, once they've finished with you. Because a bargain with them was always going to end this way. And because—" his voice roughened— "because the woman carried a dead man's promise over a glacier, and I have never seen anyone pay a debt like that. I want to be the kind of person who can stand in a circle like this one. Even if the circle judges me."

Kombai studied him. Around the ring, the elders exchanged glances—some hard, some measuring. Mendi wanted him bound and held. Sereva wanted him sent out of the valley before dark. But Kombai had noticed something neither of them mentioned: Toma had brought the route plan, which meant he knew the enforcers' timetable, their numbers, their approach. And he had offered it freely, knowing what it might cost him among his own people, whoever they were.

"Weigh him later," Kombai said at last. "First we weigh the greater matter."

* * *

The deliberation lasted through the afternoon, and Kombai conducted it the way his father had taught him: every elder speaks, in order, without interruption; no decision until the youngest has spoken, because the young see the future and the old only see the past; and when all voices are done, the senior judge does not vote—he listens to the room until the judgment becomes audible, the way a hunter listens until the forest tells him where the pig has gone.

The arguments circled the same ground for hours. The enforcers at the gorge were armed with weapons the valley could not match in open field. But the valley did not fight in open field—it fought in the narrows, in the fog, on ground its defenders had hunted since childhood. If the council gave the strangers to the government, the strangers would walk free within a month; everyone in the circle knew the calendar on the police station wall. If the council attacked first, they became the aggressors in any story the outside world might hear. If the council waited, the enforcers would come in on their own schedule, and their confidence would grow roots.

It was Sereva, oldest of them, who cut through.

"When the government men came after the burning," she said, "they asked us who did it, and we said the Consortium, and they wrote it down and left, and nothing happened. When the missionaries came, they prayed over our ashes and left. When the journalists came"—she glanced at Pidik—"one came. He wrote a story that no one published. The outside world has had two years to judge this crime and has declined to do so." She struck the floor with her stick, once, a sound like a door closing. "A court that refuses to sit has abdicated. The case reverts to the injured party. That is the oldest law there is—older than your governments, older than your churches. The people who were wronged keep the right of judgment until someone competent takes it from them."

"No one has taken it," Yawan said quietly.

"No one has taken it," Sereva agreed. "So it remains ours."

Kombai felt the judgment become audible then, the way he always did—not as an idea but as a pressure, a settling, the whole room leaning the same direction at once. He rose. The roundhouse went silent. Even the glacier seemed to hold its breath between calvings.

"The ruling of this council," he said, and his voice carried to the smoke holes and out, "is this:

"The crime committed against Kwaimbaita is a crime against the clans of this valley by marriage, trade, and blood. Its perpetrators are present at our boundary. Under the law of the ancestors, they shall be tried here—not driven away, not handed to a government that has sold itself, not ambushed in the night like thieves. They will be summoned. They will be given safe passage to the council ground under truce. They will face the survivors and the kin of the dead, and the evidence the woman Wren carried over the ice, and they will answer for it as men, in front of witnesses, according to custom.

"If they refuse the summons—if they come instead with guns and fire—then the valley will defend itself, and whatever befalls them in the narrows and the fog will be counted as lawful defense, and their bodies returned to their employers with the account written plainly on paper so that no one can say they were not warned.

"And the guide Toma"—he turned to the kneeling man—"will stand trial alongside them or beside us, as his actions between this hour and that hour determine. The council does not forgive in advance. The council watches."

He sat. For a moment nobody moved. Then old Pidik, the survivor, pushed himself upright on his stick, and his whisper filled the roundhouse: "Let the names be read at the trial. All forty-one. Let the men who silenced them hear every one."

"It will be so," Kombai said.

* * *

The summons was prepared before dusk—a strip of bark cloth bearing the marks of the eleven elders, sealed with ash and pig fat, carried down by two runners who would walk into the enforcers' camp unarmed, as the law required, and speak the invitation aloud whether they were shot or not. Kombai watched them go from the terrace, two thin figures descending the switchbacks into the blue evening shadow of the gorge, carrying nothing but words.

Behind him, the valley was already moving. Hunters checking bows and the few old rifles kept oiled against exactly such days. Women sealing the granaries and moving children up to the cave shelters above the terraces. The pilot woman and the bush pilot standing at the edge of the council ground, talking low, and Toma standing apart from them—unbound now, by Kombai's order, but not yet forgiven—watching the gorge with the expression of a man reading his own sentence in the landscape.

The runners would return before midnight with the answer. Kombai already knew what the answer would be. Men who waited at a boundary firing shots into the air were not men who accepted invitations to justice; they were men who mistook patience for permission.

Far up the valley, the glacier calved again—a boom that rolled down the walls and shivered the water jars on their shelves. The pass was open. The window would hold perhaps three days.

Kombai straightened his armbands, touched the boar's tusk at his chest, and turned toward the armory house, where the valley's old war spears hung in bundles, wrapped in barkcloth, waiting in the dark like sleeping animals.

"Light the watch fires," he told the runner at his shoulder. "All of them. Let them see how many we are."

Below in the gorge, unseen but not unfelt, nine or eleven men checked their rifles in the gathering dark, and waited for an answer to a question they had already asked with bullets.

Midnight would tell.
