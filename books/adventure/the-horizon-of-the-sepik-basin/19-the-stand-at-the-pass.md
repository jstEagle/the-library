## 19. The Stand at the Pass

The drums began an hour after full dark.

Jude heard them first as a tremor in the soles of his boots, felt through the stone of the pass before he heard them with his ears—a pulse rising out of the valley below, out of the great hidden bowl where the survivors of Yalimbung had rebuilt their lives beyond the reach of any map. Then the sound climbed to him, and he understood: not celebration, not warning, but something older than either. A summons. The valley was calling its men and women to the walls.

He stood at the throat of the pass where the glacier had spent ten thousand years carving a corridor through the range—a slot of rock barely forty meters wide, walled on both sides by ice-polished cliffs that rose sheer into the starless night. The glacier itself lay below them now, a pale river in the darkness, groaning as it calved somewhere down-valley. The air smelled of stone dust and cold and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the fires the defenders had lit low behind the rock walls.

Mara crouched beside him, checking the action on the old shotgun one more time, though she'd checked it four times already. Her hands were steady. Jude found that steadiness more frightening than fear would have been.

"How many?" she asked.

Jude looked back down the trail they'd climbed two days ago, the route Toma had led them through—up the moraine, across the ice bridge, along the shelf where the glacier's shoulder pressed against the mountain. The enforcers had reached the far side of the pass at dusk. He'd counted torchlight moving in the trees before the cloud swallowed it. Nine lights. Maybe twelve. Kavera didn't send tourists.

"Enough," he said.

Toma materialized out of the dark to their left, moving with that uncanny quiet of his, a rifle slung across his back and his face painted with ash in the style of the valley men. He looked different here. Harder. Older. The charisma was still there, banked like coals, but what burned over it now was something Jude hadn't seen in him before: shame worn long enough to become purpose.

"They will come at moonrise," Toma said. "Or they will come when the moon fails. Old Kombai says they will wait for dark because dark is what they trust." He spat toward the glacier. "They think this is like the village. They think people run from fire."

"Do they have guns?" Mara asked.

Toma almost smiled. "They have many guns. But guns do not know this ground." He tapped the stone beneath them with one finger. "The glacier knows. My grandfather's grandfather hunted pig in this pass when the ice stood higher than those cliffs. Every crack, every hollow—the elders remember them all. Tonight the mountain fights for us."

* * *

They had spent the afternoon preparing, and Jude kept returning in his mind to the strangeness of it—the ordinariness. Old men hauling stones up ladders of lashed sapling. Women boiling water over small fires screened by hides. Boys too young to fight running messages between positions, breathless and solemn as priests. No speeches. No bravado. Just work, done quickly and well, by people who had already lost everything once and had decided, with terrible calm, not to lose it twice.

Kombai himself had come to the pass at sundown, leaning on a staff of black palm wood, and had walked the line slowly, speaking to each defender by name. When he reached Jude, he had stopped and studied him for a long moment with eyes like river stones.

"You are the pilot," he said. "The one who carried the dead man's word."

"I'm the one who carried it, yes."

"The word was heavy?"

"Heavier than anything I've ever flown."

Kombai had nodded slowly, as if this were a reasonable answer. "Then set it down tonight. It belongs to us now." And he had moved on down the line, leaving Jude with the strange lightness of a man relieved of cargo he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Now, in the dark, the lightness was gone. In its place was the cold arithmetic of the hours ahead.

The defenders numbered thirty-one. Jude knew because he'd helped count rifles—nineteen, mostly relics, shotguns, a few hunting pieces, and one ancient bolt-action that had probably seen service in a war whose name nobody remembered. Against that: whatever Kavera had brought through the pass, professional men with modern weapons and no law above them but the one waiting in this valley, which they did not yet believe in.

That was the thing Jude kept circling back to, the thought that would not leave him alone: the enforcers were coming expecting a massacre. They had done one before, at Yalimbung, and nothing had happened to them. No arrests. No headlines. The world had simply closed over the dead village like water over a dropped stone. Why would they expect anything different now?

Because they don't know, Jude thought. They don't know about the council. They don't know the map has been read aloud. They don't know that every survivor in this valley has spent two years learning exactly where their dead are buried—and that some of those graves are shallow enough to point at the men who dug them.

The evidence was safe. That was the bargain Jude had made with himself when they'd come down off the ice: the map, Elias Wren's testimony written in his own hand, the photographs the survivor Ambai had produced from their oilskin wrapping—all of it sat now in the council house at the valley's heart, in the keeping of people who had waited two years for it. Whatever happened at the pass tonight, that paper would outlive them all. It would go downriver with the missionaries, to the journalists in Wewak, to anyone who could make the outside world look at what Kavera had buried.

But first the men who had buried it were climbing toward them through the dark.

* * *

The moon failed around eleven, sliding behind cloud that came down off the summits like smoke poured from a bottle. The temperature fell with it. Jude's breath hung in front of him and the stars went out one by one until the pass was black as the inside of a drum.

He heard them before he saw them—not footsteps, which Toma's men had learned to mask, but the small betrayals of men trying to be silent: the click of a sling swivel against a buckle, the scrape of a boot sole on wet stone, once, unmistakably, the muffled clink of metal on metal. They were coming up the moraine in single file, feeling their way along the wall of the pass, exactly where Toma had said they would come, because there was nowhere else. The glacier had left only one road, and the road ran straight into the teeth of the defenders.

A whistle sounded softly from the rocks below—two notes, then silence. The signal.

Old Kombai raised his arm, and Jude, watching, saw the gesture travel down the line in the dark like wind through grass.

The first volley was not aimed to kill. It was aimed to announce. Nineteen guns spoke at once from the walls of the pass, and the noise in that stone corridor was apocalyptic—a single hammer-blow of sound that rolled up the cliffs and came back doubled, tripled, avalanches of echo chasing themselves into the high country. Rock dust sifted down through the dark. Somewhere below, a man screamed, not from a wound but from the shock of it, the sheer physical violence of being inside that sound.

Then the pass went quiet again, except for the ringing in Jude's ears and the glacier groaning in the dark, and the enforcers' return fire began—wild, upward, stitching the cliff face with muzzle flashes that lit the gorge in stroboscopic bursts. Jude pressed himself flat behind the stone wall the defenders had built that afternoon, waist-high, mortared with mud, and felt rounds crack overhead close enough to part the air around his skull.

Beside him, Mara was shooting. Not often—she picked her moments, the way Elias must have taught her without ever meaning to—but when she fired, she fired at muzzle flashes, and at least one flash did not fire again.

"Tenth position!" a boy shouted from the shadows, relaying a runner's call. "They are at the bend! They carry a light!"

A flashlight beam swung up the pass, probing, and immediately three rifles answered it. The light died. In its afterimage Jude saw the shape of the attack: perhaps eight men bunched at the bend where the pass turned, pinned by the geometry of the ground, unable to spread out, unable to flank, funneling themselves one by one into a corridor their enemies owned completely. It was not a battle. It was a trap with a trigger.

And still they came. That was the thing Jude couldn't stop watching, couldn't stop admiring even as he loaded and fired and loaded again. These were professionals. They moved in short rushes, covering each other, dragging their wounded back down the slope, working by hand signals in the dark. Twice they tried grenades—one burst harmlessly against the lower wall, showering the defenders with gravel—and twice the valley men simply melted back and let the explosions spend themselves on stone.

An hour passed. Maybe two. Time dissolved into flashes and reloads and the endless cold. Jude's shoulder ached from the shotgun's kick; his ears rang continuously now, a high silver note that would probably never entirely leave. Beside him Mara passed him shells without being asked, her face a smear of ash and shadow, and every time their hands touched he felt the same electric jolt he'd felt crossing the ice—that certainty, terrifying and simple, that he would rather die in this pass than live anywhere she wasn't.

There was no time to say it. There was only time to mean it.

* * *

It went wrong near the end, the way things always go wrong at the end, when everyone has begun to believe the ending is decided.

The enforcers made one last push—a concentrated rush at the bend, six or seven men firing from the hip, screaming to spook the defenders, throwing everything forward at once. It was brave and stupid and nearly worked. Two of the valley men went down at the wall, and a gap opened on the right side where the stone was low, and through that gap came a single figure moving fast and low and very well, a man with a submachine gun who had clearly decided that if the position couldn't be taken by fire it would be taken by nerve.

He came up the rocks like something born to it. Jude swung the shotgun toward him but the angle was wrong, blocked by the wall, and the man was past the wall's end and into the open ground behind it where the reserve fighters stood—

Where Mara stood.

She had moved to help drag one of the wounded clear, and she was in the open, ten meters from the man, and Jude saw the whole geometry of it in a fraction of a second: the man raising the gun, Mara turning, the distance closing, no one else close enough, no one but—

Toma.

He came out of the dark between them the way he had come out of the dark all journey long, silently, impossibly, as if the mountain had opened and handed him through. He didn't shoot. There was no time to shoot. He simply put his body into the space where the bullets wanted to go.

The submachine gun hammered. Three rounds. Toma took two of them—in the chest, in the shoulder—and the impact spun him half around, and in that half-turn he fired once from the hip, almost casually, the way a man swats a fly, and the enforcer went down and did not get up.

Then Toma went down too, and the night seemed to tilt.

What happened next happened fast and slow at once. The valley men surged forward and the attack broke—not everywhere at once but all along the line, the way ice breaks, suddenly and then completely. Muzzle flashes retreated down the pass. Someone shouted an order in a language Jude didn't know, and the order was retreat, and the retreat became flight, and the flight became the sound of men running down the moraine with the drums of the valley pounding behind them like a heartbeat they could not outrun.

By two in the morning it was over. The pass belonged to the defenders. The cost lay on the stones.

* * *

They worked by lantern light among the rocks. Two valley men dead—Jude learned their names, Numbos and Kila, and made himself repeat them until he would not forget—and five wounded, none hopelessly. Of the enforcers, seven lay dead in the pass and three had been taken alive, disarmed and bound and sitting under guard with the blank faces of men whose professional world had just ended. The rest had fled down-ice into the dark, and the elders sent no one after them. The mountain would conduct that pursuit.

Toma lay behind the wall where they'd carried him, and Mara knelt over him with her hands pressed hard against his chest, and Jude knelt opposite her holding the lantern, and neither of them said anything for a long time because there was nothing to say that the blood wasn't already saying.

The wounds were bad. Jude had seen enough bullet wounds to know bad. But the round in the chest had struck the folded bundle Toma carried against his skin—the medicine packet from the waystation, the one he'd hauled over the glacier for them, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to his chest like a second heart. The bullet had gone through the tin of quinine tablets and spent most of itself there. The second round had torn through the meat of his shoulder and missed everything that mattered by fingers' widths.

Toma's eyes opened while the valley's healer worked. He found Mara's face first, then Jude's, and something like his old smile flickered at the corner of his mouth, cracked and bloodied as it was.

"My brother," he whispered. "Is he free?"

"He'll be free," Mara said. Her voice was wrecked and steady at the same time. "When the evidence goes downriver. When Kavera falls. I swear it."

Toma considered this with the gravity of a man weighing a trade, and then he nodded once, and let the darkness take him gently, into sleep instead of death, which was more than the night had promised any of them.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold to the pass. The cloud lifted off the summits and the light slid down the glacier and found the stones stained dark in places, and the defenders standing among them, silent, exhausted, unglorious. There were no cheers. Nobody raised a weapon to the sky. Old women came up the trail with food and water and knelt beside the dead and began, quietly, to sing, and the singing was worse than any gunfire Jude had ever heard, and better.

He stood at the wall with Mara, looking down the pass at the morning. Below them, far down the ice, the enforcers' abandoned gear glittered where it had fallen. Beyond that, the world—the rivers, the towns, the syndicate, the law that had never come. All of it waiting to hear what had happened here.

"It isn't finished," Mara said quietly. "The ones who ran. They'll report back. Kavera will send more."

"No," Jude said. He was watching the trail below, where figures were climbing toward the pass from the valley—elders, walking slowly, Kombai's black staff catching the early light. "They won't send more. They'll send lawyers." He exhaled, watching his breath drift. "But it doesn't matter anymore. What happens next doesn't happen here. It happens downriver, in courtrooms and newspapers, in places we can't follow." He turned to her. "The pass held. The map is safe. Everything else—" he took her hand, ash-stained and shaking at last now that it was over, "—everything else, we face together."

Below them, Kombai reached the wall and stopped, and looked out over the stones and the dead and the living, and then turned his river-stone eyes to Jude and Mara.

"The council sits at midday," the old man said. "The prisoners will be judged." His gaze lingered a moment on the dark stains on the rock, and his voice, when it came again, was quiet as falling snow. "Come and witness. Justice is owed its witnesses—and you two have carried it farther than anyone."
