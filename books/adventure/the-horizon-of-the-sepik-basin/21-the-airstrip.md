## 21. The Airstrip

The grass came back first.

That was the thing about the Sepik that no one who hadn't lived through a wet season could understand—how fast the land moved to reclaim whatever you'd built on it. Three weeks of rain and neglect had turned the airstrip into a green rumor of itself. Kunai stood waist-high down the middle, taller at the edges where it leaned in over the strip like a crowd pressing toward a stage. Drainage ditches Jude had cut two years ago had silted flat. The windsock pole still stood, but the sock itself had rotted to a gray rag that flapped without conviction, pointing nowhere, which felt honest enough.

Jude stood at the northern end with a machete in one hand and a mug of black coffee going cold in the other, and looked down the length of what had been, until recently, the only fixed point in a wandering life.

"You're not going to cut it all standing there admiring it," Mara said behind him.

"I'm strategizing."

"You're procrastinating." She came up beside him, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth Toma's sister had woven, machete over her shoulder like she'd been born to it. She hadn't been. Six weeks ago she'd been a woman who sorted trade goods in a Wewak storeroom and kept her father's name out of her mouth. Now her hands were blistered in patterns that matched his own, and she swung a blade the way Elias Wren must have swung one, forty years and a mountain range away.

They worked the strip in passes, cutting the kunai low and raking it into windrows to burn when the dry spell came back. It was slow, stupid, glorious work—the kind of work that asked nothing of you except the next armful, the next meter, the next pass. Jude found he loved it in a way that frightened him slightly, because loving a place was how places got their hooks into you, and for eleven years the whole architecture of his life had been built on never letting anything get its hooks in.

The Cessna sat under its tarpaulin shelter at the southern end, looking like something a child had made from memory and spare parts. The port wing carried new skin over the old ribs where the enforcers' second burst had chewed it above the strut. The windshield had a scar across the lower left quadrant, a white comet of impact that no amount of polishing would take out. Jude had decided to keep it. Some damage you repaired so thoroughly it was as if it never happened, and some you left visible, so the aircraft—and the pilot—could remember what it had survived.

Mara stopped mid-swing and wiped her face with her forearm. "Toma said the fuel drums come upriver Thursday."

"Toma says a lot of things."

"He keeps his word now." She said it evenly, but Jude heard the weight under it. They'd argued about Toma twice more since the valley—once in the elder Kombai's longhouse, once on the flight down to Angoram where they'd dropped him with medicine for his brother and a debt neither of them knew how to price. *He sold us,* Mara had said. *And then he bought us back,* Jude had answered, and neither of them had been entirely satisfied with the arithmetic. But the drums were coming Thursday, and Toma would be poling the canoe that brought them, because that was the kind of man he'd turned out to be: a man who betrayed you once, completely, and then spent the rest of his life paying it forward in installments.

Jude went back to cutting. The sun climbed. Sweat ran salt into the corner of his mouth. Somewhere downriver a boat engine droned, and a pair of hornbills crossed the strip at mast height, wingbeats like loose shutters in wind.

It was nearly noon when the mission truck ground up the track from the river landing, and Father Anselm climbed out with his cassock tucked into rubber boots and an envelope in his hand.

"Mail," he said, as if this were remarkable, which, for the Sepik Basin, it was. "And a question I'm instructed to ask in person." He handed over the envelope first—airmail paper, a Wewak postmark, a handwriting Jude didn't know. Then he took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which meant the question was going to be delicate. "There's a man in Wewak. A lawyer. He represents himself as counsel for families of the men who were taken at the pass. He wants to know if you'll give evidence at the government inquiry in Port Moresby."

Jude looked at the envelope. "And what did you tell him?"

"That I'd deliver the question and nothing else." Anselm put his glasses back on. "Though I'll confess my own curiosity, Jude. You've spent years being no one to everyone. The inquiry would make you someone. In print, even."

Mara had come up quietly and was reading over his shoulder. The letter inside was from a journalist—the same one the elders' testimony had traveled downriver to, the woman whose dispatches had already cost Kavera its lease and two ministers their careers. She wanted dates, names, the route through the gorge, everything Jude had sworn to Elias he'd carry and then some.

"They can subpoena me," Jude said. "I'll answer what's asked."

"And the rest?"

"The rest belongs to the valley." He folded the letter away. "Kombai's council decides what leaves that ground. Not me. Not anymore."

Anselm nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that wasn't surprise so much as confirmation—the look of a man watching a river find its level. "Elias is buried up there now?"

"Last week. Under the pandanus above the council house. Kombai spoke over him." Jude paused. "They forgave him, Father. Formally. As law, not sentiment. His name is in their record now, next to the village's. Witness and debtor both."

Mara's hand found his shoulder, brief and hard, and then she went back to the kunai, because grief in her had learned to keep working.

* * *

The map went on the wall of the hut that evening.

Jude had built the frame himself from kwila offcuts, glass salvaged from the Cessna's old cabin door, and now the three of them—Jude, Mara, and the oilskin-wrapped thing that had started everything—stood regarding it. Behind the glass, the map itself had changed in meaning so completely that it hardly seemed the same object. Once it had been a treasure: coordinates, contour lines, the sinuous thread of a creek marked in Elias's pencil with the words ALLUVIAL — RICH. Now those same lines read as a confession. Every mark Elias had made pointed at ground soaked by the massacre at Yalimbun; every annotation was a sentence in the testimony the elders had weighed under the pandanus roof.

Mara hung it on the nail herself, stepped back, and was quiet a long time.

"He drew the creek wrong," she said finally. "Look. It bends north before the junction. He always drew water wrong when he was working from memory instead of surveying. He was too proud to admit it." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied. "I used to think that pride was why he left us. Too proud to come home poor."

"And now?"

"Now I think he couldn't come home because of what he'd seen. You don't walk back into a kitchen and talk about school fees after watching what he watched." She touched the glass, over the penciled creek, over the error. "He ran. But he ran carrying it. That's not nothing."

"No," Jude said. "That's not nothing."

She turned from the wall and looked at him with an expression he was still learning to read—not the anger of Wewak, not the fear of the glacier, but something settled and appraising, the look of a woman taking inventory of a life. "You could have stayed invisible," she said. "At the pass. When the elders asked who would stand witness against the enforcers, you could have let Toma and me speak and kept your name out of it. That was your whole religion, Jude. Neutrality. Belonging to no one."

"It was."

"What changed?"

Jude considered the map on the wall, the comet-scarred windshield thirty meters away, the strip of cut grass lying in windrows under a sky going violet at the edges. What changed. He'd flown eleven years on the theory that a man who belonged nowhere could owe nothing to anyone—that distance was safety, that altitude was innocence. Then a bleeding prospector had fallen out of a dusk sky and put a dead man's promise in his hands, and somewhere between the ambush at the saddle and the whiteout above the tree line, carrying Mara on his back through cold that killed the careless, the theory had died of exposure along with everything else soft in him. What changed was that he'd learned the difference between being safe and being spared. No one gets spared.

"You did," he said. "You changed it. And then the valley changed it. Watching Kombai's court—I'd spent my whole life thinking law was what happened to other people, in rooms I'd never enter. Up there the law was the people themselves. You can't be neutral in front of that. You're either in the circle or you're outside it, and outside it, it turns out, isn't safe. It's just alone."

Mara came to him then, and the kiss was unhurried, and the lamp guttered, and outside the frogs began the evening's argument with the dark.

* * *

Thursday brought the fuel drums, and Toma with them, and Toma brought news the way he brought everything now—openly, hands visible, eyes steady.

"The Consortium's camp at Ambunti is struck," he said, squatting by the fire while Mara cooked rice and tinned fish and the drums gleamed in the firelight like a row of fat brass gods. "The lease is dead. The labor camp at the coast—" he paused, and Jude saw him choose the plain words over the dramatic ones, a discipline learned in the valley, "—my brother walked out Tuesday. There are men from Port Moresby with papers, asking questions. Real ones, this time. The journalist's people."

"And the enforcers?" Mara asked.

"Some will hang. Some will go to prison and come out and be somebody's uncle again." Toma shrugged, and the shrug held the whole weight of the highland court's verdicts—justice real and imperfect, delivered by the wronged to the guilty, with the nation arriving afterward to file paperwork. "Kombai says the record travels. That's the revenge the valley wants. Not blood. Record."

He stayed the night, sleeping on the veranda as was proper, and left at dawn with rice and a promise to bring seedlings for the garden Mara was plotting behind the hut. At the canoe he turned back once. "The strip," he called. "When it flies again—you'll need a man who knows the river landings. Someone the villages trust."

"We'll need exactly that," Jude said.

Toma grinned, poled off into the morning mist, and was gone.

* * *

The first flight after the rebuild was a mail run, nothing more—Angoram, Pagwi, back before the afternoon buildups. But the night before it, Jude walked the strip barefoot in the last light, feeling the graded surface through his soles, checking the windsock Mara had sewn from orange parachute silk, and understood that he was doing something he had never once done in eleven years of flying the basin.

He was checking whether the place would hold him.

Every other field he'd ever kept had been temporary by design. Fuel caches abandoned when rumors got close. Leases signed under names that weren't quite his. Airstrips were things you used and left, like camps, like relationships, like countries. This one he was rebuilding with his own hands beside a woman who'd inherited a war and converted it into a witness, on ground where the grass grew back faster than any secret could stay buried, and when he reached the southern end and turned around, Mara was walking up the middle of the strip toward him with the sunset behind her and the windsock lifting at last into a breeze that smelled of rain on far mountains.

"Well?" she called.

"Holds weight," he said. "Should fly fine."

"Not the strip." She stopped in front of him. "You. Does it hold you? Because I've been thinking, and I want it said plainly, once, out loud, under the sky, so it's on the record the way Kombai would say—on the record. I'm staying. Whatever comes. And if you're staying too, I want to hear you say it, because I have spent my entire life being left by men who loved the horizon more than they loved—"

"Mara." He took her hands. Her knuckles were split from the machete and the grading rake, and he kissed them, each one, which made her laugh wetly despite herself. "Eleven years I flew to disappear. Ask me what I fly for now."

"What do you fly for now?"

"For the hour after dawn, when the mist is still on the river and the whole basin is gold underneath it. For the look on a nurse's face when the vaccine crate lands. For coming back." He said the last two words slowly, tasting them. "For coming back. That's the whole cargo manifest. Everything else is ballast."

She kissed him then, there on the strip, under the windsock, and the frogs started up, and far to the west lightning walked along the front range where the pass was closing under new snow, sealing the valley and its court and Elias's grave beneath the pandanus into another season of silence and law.

* * *

The radio crackled awake at 0540, an hour before first light, while the kettle was still finding its voice.

Jude keyed the handset with coffee-rough patience. "Almeida. Go ahead, Angoram."

Static, then the mission operator's familiar clipped voice—but strained, running fast. "Jude. Strange traffic this morning. Mission boat picked up a passenger at the lower landing overnight. Woman. Foreigner, maybe—hard to say. Half-dead, fever. Wouldn't give a name to anyone." A pause, the hiss of the river between them. "Except she asked for you. By name. Said you'd know her. Said—" the operator's voice dropped, reluctant, "—said to tell you she's carrying the rest of the map."

Jude stood very still. On the wall behind him the framed oilskin caught the lamplight, Elias's crooked creek shining under the glass. Beside the stove Mara had gone motionless, a spoon suspended over the kettle, her eyes finding his across the small warm room.

Outside, the eastern sky was turning the color of new brass, and the Cessna waited at the southern end with full tanks and a scarred windshield, ready—for the first time in eleven years—to fly toward something instead of away.

"Tell her," Jude said into the handset, "we're coming."
