## 22. First Light, First Flight

The mist came off the Sepik the way it always did before dawn—not all at once, but in slow exhalations, as if the river itself were sleeping and breathing. Mara Wren stood at the edge of the rebuilt airstrip with a mug of sweet black tea going cold in her hands and watched the light find the water. The grass was silvered with dew. Somewhere in the treeline a bird she had learned to call the *laughing one*—because that was what its cry sounded like, a joke told over and over to an audience of trees—began its morning performance.

Six weeks since the pass. Six weeks since the valley, the elders, the judgment delivered under an open sky with the whole clan standing witness. Six weeks since Toma came home from the mission hospital at Ambunti with a scar across his ribs and his brother walking beside him, thin as a reed but alive, alive, alive.

She had learned to measure time differently now. Not in the distance between places but in the distance between the person she had been in Wewak—angry, armored, waiting for the world to confirm her worst opinion of her father—and the person standing here, barefoot in wet grass, waiting for a flying lesson.

*You look like him,* the old woman by the river had told her, back when the map was still a secret folded against her ribs. *You stand like him when you're deciding something.*

Mara was deciding something now. She could feel it in her stance, in the set of her shoulders. She was deciding to stay.

The Cessna sat at the downwind end of the strip where Jude had parked it the evening before, its new fabric patch on the port wing catching the first pink of the light—a slightly wrong shade of white, like a scar on a suntanned arm, and Mara had come to love it for exactly that. Nothing about this airplane matched anymore. The propeller was from a wreck at Wewak. The radio was Toma's find, bartered from a missionary who'd given up on the bush. The seats had been re-covered with canvas from a supply drop that had fallen into a swamp two seasons ago. It was an airplane held together by stubbornness and wire, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Jude came out of the hangar—*hangar* was a generous word for the pole-and-thatch shelter they'd raised in a single feverish week with half the valley's young men helping—carrying the preflight checklist on a clipboard that had once belonged to Elias.

That had been Mara's idea. Her father's clipboard, his grease pencil, his cramped handwriting still ghosting the margins: *check fuel caps, check drain, TELL JUDE THE LEFT MAG RUNS ROUGH WHEN COLD.* She had found the last note the first time she opened it and had stood in the hangar for a long time with her thumb over the words, not crying, just letting it be true. Elias Wren had known he was dying and had spent his last hours writing maintenance notes to the man who would carry his map home.

She used the clipboard every day. The left mag still ran rough when cold.

"Morning," Jude said, and handed her the second mug they both knew she wouldn't drink. It was a ritual now. The tea was terrible and always had been.

"Morning." She looked at the sky instead of at him, because if she looked at him this early she would smile, and if she smiled he would know she'd barely slept for excitement, and she had a reputation to maintain as the reasonable one. "Wind's down."

"Wind's down," Jude agreed, in the tone of a person humoring someone they loved. "Fog's burning off the river. We've got maybe two hours of glass before the buildups start over the range."

"Then don't waste my two hours."

Jude laughed—openly, easily, the way they hadn't laughed in the gorge or the cloud forest or the whiteout above the tree line where laughing would have cost calories they didn't have. She still caught herself sometimes, listening for the old Jude underneath this one: the careful, courteous stranger who had landed at Wewak with a dead man's map and an apology in his eyes. That Jude was still in there. But the person who had carried her through the snow on his back, who had stood in the elders' circle with his head bowed while Kombai spoke the law over all of them, who had chosen—out loud, in front of everyone, with everything to lose—to stop being invisible: that person had moved in and redecorated.

They walked the airplane together. It was a liturgy they'd developed, the two of them, and Mara knew the words now. Fuel caps. Fuel drain—clear, no water. Oil—six and a half quarts. Control surfaces—free and correct. Tires, the left one patched with a boot sole Toma had donated solemnly and without explanation. Pitot tube—open. She put her hand flat on the cowling the way Jude had taught her, feeling for the engine's temperature the old bush pilots' way, skin to metal, and found it cool and damp with dew.

"Questions before we start," Jude said, hopping up onto the wing root with the ease of ten thousand hours. "Because once that prop's turning, I want your whole head in the airplane. Nothing left out on the grass."

Mara set down her tea. She looked west, where the range stood up out of the morning haze—the wall of blue-green mountains behind which, in a valley no government map acknowledged, a hundred and forty people were rebuilding a village that a corporation had tried to erase. The pass was closed again, sealed under new snow until next year's calving. It didn't matter. There was a track now, a low route through the foothills that the survivors had used for generations, and every second week Jude flew a load of rice and medicine and school slates to the dirt strip the clan had cut into a river terrace with machetes and patience. The valley had a name again. It was spoken on the radio in the open, without fear, because there was no one left to fear.

"I'm ready," she said.

"Last chance to back out."

"You've said that every morning for two weeks."

"And you've ignored me every morning for two weeks, which is why I keep saying it." Jude slid down off the wing and held out the headset. "Your airplane, Mara Wren. From engine start to shutdown. I'm just the loud passenger."

She took the headset. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Somewhere in the last six weeks her hands had learned what her heart had known all along.

* * *

The engine caught on the third blade of the prop, that familiar cough-and-roar, and the whole airframe shivered like a dog shaking off water. Mara ran the checks with the checklist on her knee and Jude's voice quiet in her ear—not instructing, not yet, just confirming. Throttle friction. Mixture rich. Flaps set. She taxied to the end of the strip with the concentration of a person defusing something, weaving slightly, overcorrecting, and Jude said nothing, because the first lesson of the first lesson was that the rudder pedals were not the steering wheel of a truck and everyone learned it by weaving.

"Wind's down," she said again, mostly to herself, and lined up on the strip.

Grass on both sides, tall and wet, bending away from her in the prop wash. The mountains ahead. The river to the east, running molten gold in the new light. She thought of all the takeoffs she'd watched from the ground—from the trading post at Wewak, from the strip at Ambunti—always from the ground, always the one staying, the one left with the dust settling and the sound fading. Her whole life had been a study in things leaving. Her father first, then her mother's patience, then her own faith that anyone who said *I'll come back* meant it.

"Whenever you're ready," Jude said.

Mara eased the throttle forward.

The engine note deepened. The Cessna gathered itself, the tail coming light, the grass streaming past, and then—the moment she had been waiting for without knowing she'd been waiting for it, maybe her whole life—the wheels left the earth and the ground dropped away and there was nothing between her and the whole enormous morning.

She laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like the bird in the treeline, the laughing one, the joke told to an audience of trees, except her audience was the sky.

"Easy on the climb," Jude said, and she could hear he was grinning. "Nose down a touch. Let her accelerate. There you go. There you go."

The strip fell behind. The river came up to meet them, broad and braided and glittering, and Mara banked gently—her first turn, shallow, careful, the horizon tilting through the windscreen like the world being tipped out of a cup—and followed the water east into the light.

* * *

They flew the basin the way you'd read a favorite book aloud: slowly, savoring, pointing things out to each other.

The oxbow lake where the crocodiles sunned like downed logs. The mission at Ambunti, its tin roof flashing a hello. The tributary where they had come up by dugout, the villages going silent at Kavera's name—except the silence was gone now. Smoke rose from morning cooking fires. A canoe crossed the river below them, two fishermen standing to their paddles, and one of them raised an arm as the shadow of the airplane passed over, and Mara, watching, felt something loosen in her chest that had been knotted since the day a stranger landed at her airstrip bleeding out.

She had hated the flying at first. That was the truth she'd never said aloud. In Wewak, airplanes had been the shape of abandonment—her father leaving in one, her father dying in one, the syndicate's men arriving in one like a disease arriving on a ship. The sky had been the direction everything bad came from.

But from up here, the basin made sense in a way it never could from the ground. The river wasn't a maze of channels and drowned logs; it was a single silver sentence running through the green. The villages weren't isolated; they were beads on a string, and the airplane was the thread. Jude had spent years being the thread, stitching a country together one mail bag and medicine crate at a time, and Mara was only now understanding that this was what her father had been searching for in all his wrong places. Not the gold. The connection. The belonging to something larger than yourself. Elias had looked for it under the mountains, in the glitter of a river bar, and died within sight of it.

"Your turn," Jude said. "Level flight. Small corrections. Fly the horizon, not the nose."

"I am flying the horizon."

"You're chasing the nose. Every time it drops you haul it back up, then it climbs and you push it down. You're sawing. Relax your grip. The airplane wants to fly. Let it."

*The airplane wants to fly.* She loosened her fingers one at a time. And it was true—the Cessna settled, content, riding the morning air like a boat on the river, needing almost nothing from her. Small corrections. Small corrections. She thought: *this is what trust feels like.* Not the absence of fear but the discovery that you don't have to grip everything to death to keep it.

They crossed the foothills where the massacre village had stood, and Mara looked down without flinching. The ruins were green now, the bush reclaiming the burned house posts, but the survivors had built a memorial at the edge of the old ground—a ring of stones, one for each of the dead, and Jude tipped a wing to it as they passed, the way he did every time, and Mara said their names. She knew them all now. The council had taught her, the way you teach a person a psalm, until the names lived in her mouth.

The map hung framed on the wall of the hangar at home. Elias's oilskin, his grease-pencil lines, the coordinates that had started everything. Visitors to the airstrip—missionaries, traders, the occasional government patrol boat officer who pretended not to see what he saw—would look at it and ask, *is that a treasure map?* And Mara would say, no. It's a testimony. It's the only record that a hundred and forty people lived here, and loved this ground, and were murdered for it. The gold was real; some of it had even been panned that first season, enough to buy the valley a generator and the school its slates. But the treasure had never been in the ground. It had been in the bearing of witness, and in the bearing of it together.

"Radio," Jude said suddenly, and reached over to turn up the volume on the set Toma had bartered.

Static, the long hiss of the HF band at dawn. Then a voice, thin and skipping, a missionary at Telefomin reading the morning traffic list the way he did every day: births, deaths, medicine requests, a broken water pump at one station, a wedding at another. Mara had learned to love these lists—the whole basin's life read out in a flat, patient voice, proof that the world went on being ordinary in all the places that had nearly stopped being ordinary at all.

The missionary finished the list. Then he paused, and Mara heard him shuffle paper.

"One more item," the voice said. "This came in late last night from a source downriver, and I'll read it as I received it. A survey party, unaffiliated, asking questions at the coast about—these are their words—*undocumented mineral shows in the upper Sepik drainage.* They're offering money for information. Local guides, river transport, knowledge of the high country." A pause, the static breathing. "They say they're with a company called Meridian Pacific. I don't know the name. I don't like the sound of it. If anyone upriver has thoughts, I'd be glad of them. Out."

The static closed over the voice like water.

Mara flew on. Level flight. Small corrections. She was aware of Jude beside her, very still, and of her own heartbeat, and of the mountains ahead standing blue and enormous in the morning light.

*There's always another map,* her father used to say, and she had thought it was greed talking, and maybe it had been, but maybe it had also been prophecy. The ground didn't stay empty. The hunger didn't stay fed. Somewhere downriver, men with a new name and the same appetite were already asking their questions, offering their money, buying what could be bought and taking what couldn't.

"They'll come upriver," she said. "They always do."

"Probably," Jude said.

"They'll find villages that remember what happened the last time. Toma's already got cousins in every river town between here and Ambunti." She glanced at him. "The council will want to know. Kombai will want to know today."

"Kombai will want to know yesterday." Jude was looking at her with an expression she was learning to read—the look that meant he was waiting to see which way she'd jump, because he had finally, finally understood that they jumped together or not at all. "We could stay out another hour. You've got a whole basin to see. Nobody would blame you for wanting your first morning to be just yours."

Mara looked west. The range. The pass, white and closed. The valley beyond it, where the stones stood in their ring and the names were spoken every morning, and where a hundred and forty survivors had taught her that justice wasn't a thing you received but a thing you kept, the way you kept a fire—fed it, watched it, passed it on.

She keyed the radio herself.

"Ambunti traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo, returning to the strip," she said, her voice steady in her own ears, her hands easy on the wheel. "And tell the council: we heard. We're coming."

She turned the airplane toward home, into the light coming up over the mountains, and beside her Jude smiled—not the careful stranger's smile, not the runner's, but the smile of a man who, for the first time in his life, was flying toward something instead of away from it.

Below them the river ran on, silver in the sun, carrying everything toward the sea.

The horizon was open. They flew into it together.
