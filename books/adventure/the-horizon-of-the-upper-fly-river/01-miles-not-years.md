## 1. Miles, Not Years

The supercell came up out of the Texas line at four in the afternoon, dragging its anvil across the sky like a ship's sail gone slack and then taut again all at once. Rafael Montrose watched it from the shoulder of Highway 54, three miles east of a town whose name she'd already forgotten, and felt the old arithmetic begin: the storm was moving northeast at twenty-two knots, the road ran northeast at sixty miles an hour, and somewhere behind her—two states back, maybe three—a man named Okafor's voicemail was filling up with the sound of her not answering.

She measured her life in odometer readings now. That was the truth of it, though she'd never have said it aloud. Not in years, because years were what other people had—people with mailboxes and leases and dentists who knew their names. Rafael had 214,000 miles on the Tacoma's engine, a number she checked the way a gambler checks his last chip. She had 61,000 more since the transmission rebuild in Amarillo. She had the distance from Guymon to Dalhart, from Dalhart to Tucumcari, from Tucumcari to wherever the next dry bed and cheap motel happened to be. Time passed through her like weather. Miles stayed.

The cell towered over the flatland now, a cathedral of rotating gray, its updraft base so wide it seemed less like a cloud than a place—a country you could enter. The air ahead of it was warm and smelled of cut wheat and dust and something faintly metallic, the smell that always arrived before the first lightning, as if the sky were a battery being charged by sheer will.

"Come on," she murmured to it, to herself, to nobody. "Show me."

Her instruments sat on the passenger seat where a person might sit: the anemometer's readout unit, a battered laptop running radar she could mostly predict without, a paper logbook her father would have insisted on even now, twelve years after anyone needed paper. She kept the logbook anyway. Some habits were load-bearing.

The first hail hit at 4:41. It came down like gravel flung by an angry god, quarter-sized, then golf-ball, then something that cracked against the roof hard enough to make her flinch and swear. She'd parked under the overpass at Keyes—the kind of shelter every chaser knows is a bad idea and uses anyway—and stood watching the white curtain of the precipitation core march past half a mile north, close enough to feel the temperature drop fifteen degrees in as many seconds, close enough to hear the hail roaring on the asphalt like static turned physical.

Inside the storm's circulation, invisible above her, the mesocyclone turned. Somewhere in that turning was the thing she'd driven eleven hours to see, or maybe not see—that was the honest version, the version she didn't put in the reports she sold to the insurance analytics firms for money that never quite covered anything. The honest version was that she chased because when she was under a supercell, her mind went quiet. All the ledgers closed. All the phone calls became irrelevant. There was just the wind doing what wind does, enormous and indifferent, and Rafael Montrose standing small beneath it, owing it nothing.

The tornado warning siren in Keyes began to wail. A funnel touched down northwest of town—brief, ropey, dancing across open range land like a finger testing water. She got forty seconds of usable footage before it roped out, and then she stood in the hail's aftermath among the scattered ice, which lay melting on the blacktop like a field of broken glass, and felt the quiet start to drain away.

By the time the light went gold and green behind the retreating storm, the phone was ringing again.

* * *

She let it ring. She always let it ring twice, because answering on the first ring told them she'd been waiting, and waiting was weakness, and weakness was blood in the water. Then she picked up.

"You're a hard woman to reach, Ms. Montrose." The voice was smooth, unhurried, with the particular patience of someone being paid by the hour to be patient. "This is Mr. Adeyemi's office. We've been trying to update you on your account status."

"My account status," Rafael said. She leaned against the truck, watching the storm's back edge glow orange over the wheat. "Let me guess. It's status is worse."

There was a pause, the sound of papers shuffling or being pretended to shuffle. "Your principal stands at eighty-two thousand. Interest accrues monthly. Your last payment was—" another pause, theatrical, "—eleven months ago."

"I paid six hundred dollars in March."

"A payment of six hundred dollars against a balance of eighty-two thousand is not a payment, Ms. Montrose. It's a gesture." The voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse. "Mr. Adeyemi wants me to remind you that Voss Holdings views default as a failure of imagination, not of means. There are always arrangements available. You have skills. Skills are collateral."

Rafael watched a jackrabbit cross the road, unhurried, absurdly calm, as if the whole sky hadn't just tried to kill everything under it. "I'll have money after chase season. The analytics contracts pay out in August."

"You said that last August."

"And I paid in September."

"Six hundred dollars." The voice let that sit between them like the hailstone that had cracked her roof. "Ms. Montrose, I'm instructed to tell you that this is your final courtesy call. The next contact won't be a call." A beat. "Though I'll say—off the record, purely as one professional to another—that there's been some talk in the office about you. About a job. Something big enough to make all these little conversations unnecessary. If a certain person were to reach out to you, would you take the meeting?"

The question landed strangely. Not a threat, exactly. A door left deliberately ajar, and Rafael had spent too many years around storms not to recognize the pressure drop that comes before something opens.

"Who's asking?"

"That would be telling," the voice said pleasantly. "Drive safe, Ms. Montrose. Weather looks like it's clearing."

It hung up. She stood there a long moment with the phone hot against her ear, listening to the silence it left behind, which was not empty but full—the way the air before a hook echo is full.

Then she got in the truck and drove west, because the rule was the rule.

* * *

The rule was simple, and she'd built it the way her father built river camps: minimal, functional, meant to hold against weather. Never stay anywhere long enough to be found. Three nights maximum in any town. Cash, never cards. Mail sent general delivery, picked up within a day. No lease, no utility bill, no name on any door. When people asked—and they asked, in diners and bait shops and the lobbies of cheap motels from the Panhandle to the Pecos—she said she was between places, and smiled, and the smile was good enough, because most people wanted to believe freedom looked like that: a woman alone with a truck full of weather gear, going wherever the sky told her.

What they didn't see was the arithmetic underneath. What they didn't see was that freedom and flight look identical from the outside, and only the person doing them knows which one it is. Rafael knew. Most mornings, waking in the third motel of the week, disoriented by identical ceilings, she knew precisely which one it was.

She thought about her father tonight, driving west out of the storm's wake with the windows down and the plains unrolling dark and endless on either side. He'd understood staying. He'd spent thirty years on rivers—in Alaska, in Borneo, finally Papua New Guinea—measuring water, mapping current, and he'd always come home. Home had been a small house in Norman with a rain gauge bolted to the fence post and a wall of river charts, and he'd believed, with a sincerity that embarrassed her even now, that a life was something you anchored. *A river always knows where it's going,* he used to say, tracing channels with a blunt pencil. *People are the ones who wander.*

He'd wandered once too far. Twelve years ago this October, his last expedition went up the Fly River into country the local clans didn't want them in, and the radio calls stopped, and the search found a drowned camp and no answers, and the inquest wrote a tidy report that said, in the language officialdom uses for dead men who can't defend themselves, *pilot error, inadequate preparation, disregard of local conditions.* They blamed him. They blamed him so efficiently that the blame became the story, and the story became the stone on the grave, and Rafael had spent twelve years carrying that stone in her chest like ballast.

She'd sworn off the Fly. Sworn it at the funeral, drunk on warm beer and grief, in front of the only other person who'd been close to that expedition—Dario Kell, her father's protégé, her rival, her—whatever he'd been. She'd sworn it and Dario had nodded and said nothing, and then he'd done something in the months after that she still couldn't think about without her hands closing, and after that there'd been no one to swear anything to.

Eighty-two thousand dollars. She did the math again at a stoplight in Boise City, population under a thousand, one blinking yellow light, the kind of town that existed mainly to give the horizon something to lean on. Eighty-two thousand at whatever criminal rate Voss applied. Chase season income, best case, maybe nineteen thousand if the analytics contracts renewed and the footage sold. The math didn't work. The math hadn't worked in two years. She'd been paying Voss the way you bail a boat with a hole in it—faithfully, rhythmically, sinking anyway.

And now this. *Something big enough to make all these conversations unnecessary.* A job, mentioned off the record by a collector whose job was not to mention jobs. Which meant it wasn't off the record at all. Which meant someone wanted her to hear it. Which meant someone had told Adeyemi's office to dangle it, the way you dangle bait in front of a fish you've already hooked.

She should have felt afraid. Instead, pulling into the Dusty Spur Motel outside Boise City—her second night here, which violated the rule by one night, but the storm complex was supposed to redevelop tomorrow and she was tired, God, she was so tired of moving—she felt something closer to relief. Because if someone was dangling a job, it meant the bailing might stop. And if the job was the kind of job that required a courtesy call from a collections office, it was probably a job she'd sworn off.

The Fly River. The words surfaced in her mind unbidden, the way drowned things surface, bloated and unmistakable. She pushed them down. They rose again. They always rose again.

* * *

The Dusty Spur's office smelled of cigarettes and industrial lemon, and the man behind the desk took her cash without looking at her face, which she appreciated. Room 14, end of the row, parking spot visible from the window—old habits. She carried in her bag, the laptop, the logbook, and the single luxury she permitted herself: a jar of instant coffee and a proper ceramic mug, chipped, blue, her father's, with a faded logo from some hydrological conference in 1998. *RIVERS KNOW THE WAY,* the logo said. She drank from it every morning and had never once decided whether it comforted her or accused her.

She showered. She ate gas-station almonds and a sandwich she didn't taste. She opened the logbook and recorded the day's data in her father's format, because his format was better than any she'd ever invented: date, location, odometer, cell structure, hodograph notes, tornado yes/no, duration, personal remarks. Under PERSONAL REMARKS she wrote, after a long moment: *Roped out fast. Wind quiet after. Slept badly lately.*

That was as close as she came, most days, to keeping a diary.

Then she sat on the bed with the lamp on and the TV muted—some rodeo, silent cowboys roping silent steers—and let herself do the thing she only did in motels, in the last hour before sleep, when the day's defenses came down like storm shutters. She let herself remember.

Not the funeral. Not the inquest. Those were the scenes she'd worn smooth from handling, like river stones, and they hurt in the familiar way, the way a scar hurts, which is to say barely, which is to say constantly. Tonight she remembered instead something smaller: her father packing for the Fly expedition, in the garage, surrounded by cases. She'd been twenty-six. She'd helped him inventory the gear—water filters, theodolite, the sealed aluminum survey case he was oddly protective of, wouldn't let her touch, laughed when she joked about what was inside. *Trade secrets,* he'd said. *You'll see it when it's published.* He'd hugged her at the airport, and he'd smelled of sunscreen and coffee, and the last thing he'd said—not the last thing, she corrected herself, the last thing he'd said to her, the radio calls came later—was, *Keep the rain gauge topped up while I'm gone, Rafa. Data doesn't collect itself.*

Data doesn't collect itself. People don't anchor themselves either. She'd learned both lessons the wrong way round.

The phone buzzed on the nightstand. Not a call—a text, from a number with no name attached. She stared at it for a long ten seconds before picking it up.

*Montrose. Word is you're in the Panhandle. Word is usually right. I'm two towns over, and I'm buying breakfast, and you're going to want to hear this before you decide you don't. — DK*

She read it three times. The rodeo flickered silently on the TV. Outside, the plains lay dark and enormous under a sky already rebuilding its storms over the distant Caprock, lightning strobing without sound, a silent argument happening on the edge of the world.

Dario Kell. Twelve years of silence, ended with a text that knew her location, which meant he'd been looking, which meant he'd spent money or favors finding her, which meant whatever he was selling, he needed her bad. Nobody tracked down Rafael Montrose for small reasons. Small reasons let sleeping debts lie.

And yet. Her thumb hovered over the screen. She thought about the collector's voice: *there's been some talk in the office about you.* She thought about eighty-two thousand dollars accruing interest while she slept in room 14 of the Dusty Spur. She thought about a sealed aluminum case in a drowned camp on a river she'd sworn off, and about how a sworn-off thing is just a thing you've agreed to stop wanting, and wanting doesn't sign the agreement.

She typed: *Boise City. Diner on the main drag. Seven a.m. You're buying.*

She set the phone face-down on the nightstand, turned off the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the thunder arrive late from the Caprock, each boom a little softer than the last, the storm spending itself on empty country. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the river. It always brought the river. Brown water, high banks, her father's voice on the radio breaking apart into static, and the sense—half dream, half memory, entirely unresolved—that somewhere upstream, something he'd hidden was still waiting, patient as water, for someone to come and carry it the rest of the way.

Seven a.m. was six hours away. She'd need all of them to decide who she was going to be when she walked into that diner: the woman who owed Voss eighty-two thousand dollars, or the woman who'd sworn off the Fly.

She suspected, falling asleep, that she was going to be both, and that was the trap.
