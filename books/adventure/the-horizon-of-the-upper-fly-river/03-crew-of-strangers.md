## 3. Crew of Strangers

The rain in Port Moresby came down like a verdict—sudden, total, indifferent to who deserved it. Dario Kell stood under the corrugated awning of the Ela Beach guesthouse and watched it hammer the harbor into gray static, and he told himself, for the fourth time that morning, that what he was doing was ordinary. Logistics. Personnel. The unglamorous arithmetic of any expedition.

He did not quite believe it, but he had learned long ago that belief was a luxury you could postpone, the way you postponed a dental appointment or a confession, until the postponement itself became a kind of character.

Inside, on the guesthouse's wobbly table, he had laid out the dossiers like a hand of cards he'd already counted twice. Three folders. Three strangers. And a fourth file he kept in his breast pocket, against his ribs, where it felt less like paper and more like a splinter working its way toward the heart: Rafael Montrose's contract, countersigned by Voss Holdings, with a clause on page nine that she had almost certainly not read closely enough and that he had read so many times the ink had begun to feel personal.

*The recovered materials shall be delivered intact and unexamined to the designated representative.*

Unexamined. He had watched her skim past that word at the diner in Guymon, her eyes already moving to the debt-forgiveness clause, the number that would buy back her life. People always read the numbers first. It was one of the things he liked about her, once—one of the things that had made them good together in the field, before it made them unforgivable to each other. Rafael Montrose trusted arithmetic the way other people trusted God: completely, and only until it failed her.

The rain eased to a grumble. Dario gathered the folders and went to meet his crew of strangers.

* * *

The disgraced geologist was waiting for him at the hotel bar, which told Dario most of what he needed to know about her relationship with disgrace. Dr. Miriam Wren sat with a beer she wasn't drinking and a laptop she wasn't using, both positioned defensively, like a woman who had learned that empty hands invited conversation and conversation invited questions about the journal retraction.

"Dr. Wren."

"Dario Kell." She looked him over with the flat professional appraisal of someone who had spent thirty years being the smartest person in rooms full of men who outranked her. "You're the one who got my email answered. I'd assumed the address was dead."

"It was. I resurrected it." He sat down, ordered nothing. "You know why you're here."

"I know what your email said. Sediment coring, hydrological survey, upper Fly River system." She turned the beer bottle a quarter-turn on the wet coaster. "I also know that nobody pays commercial rates plus hazard bonus for sediment cores. So either you're laundering money, which I'm morally fine with as long as my invoice clears, or there's something in that river valley worth more than minerals."

Dario held her gaze. This was the moment—the small, cheap moment where a decent man might have said something true. *There are documents, Dr. Wren. Land titles. A fraud twelve years old with bodies attached.* Instead he heard himself say, in a voice pitched exactly between candor and theater:

"There's something worth more than minerals. You'll have access to everything relevant once we're upstream. Until then, need-to-know is a condition of employment, and the client insists on it."

Wren studied him for a long three seconds. He watched her decide—not to believe him, but to believe she could handle whatever the truth turned out to be. Pride was such a useful hinge.

"Fine," she said. "But I'll tell you now what I told the ethics board, and they didn't enjoy hearing it either: I don't do work I can't sign my name to. When I find out what this actually is—and I will find out—I'll decide whether I stay. Not you. Not your client."

"Noted," Dario said, and shook her hand, and felt the first small stone of complicity settle into place behind his sternum, smooth and cold as river gravel.

* * *

Ilsa Reiner arrived two days later, and she did not wait to be met; she simply appeared at the edge of Dario's peripheral vision in the guesthouse courtyard, the way large predators appear, by making the light change. She was taller than her file photographs suggested, broader through the shoulders, with the sun-cured leather look of someone who had spent too many years at altitudes and latitudes where sunscreen was considered a personality flaw.

"Kell." Her handshake lasted precisely as long as protocol required. "Your advance cleared. That's the only reason I'm here, so let's skip the part where you tell me how much you respect my reputation."

"I do respect it. That's why you're expensive."

That earned him half a millimeter of something that was not quite a smile. They walked the courtyard while he briefed her—riverine approach, four weeks out, possible overland extension, hostile-adjacent terrain, local clans with restricted zones. She listened the way soldiers listen, cataloguing rather than comprehending, and when he finished she asked the question he'd been bracing for.

"How many shooters on the other side?"

"Unknown. Possibly none."

"Possible none means probable some." She shrugged. "Who's the principal?"

"The guide. Rafael Montrose."

Something moved behind Ilsa's eyes—not recognition exactly, but the flicker of a person checking an internal index. Dario saw it and understood it, because he'd done the same thing himself the first time Voss said her name. *Montrose.* The name still carried weight in certain circles, the way a shipwreck carries weight: everyone knew where it lay, and everyone had a theory about how it went down.

"The daughter," Ilsa said. "Of the Fly River expedition."

"You've heard of it."

"Everyone in this business has heard of it. Four went up, zero came back, and the official story blamed the guide." She looked at him with frank, unhurried curiosity. "And now the guide's daughter is leading us up the same river for the same client class that buried her father's name. That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's neither. It's contractual."

"Sure." Ilsa shouldered her pack. "Contractual. I'll take the room facing the water. I sleep better when I can see approaches."

He watched her go and thought: *she'll switch sides if the price moves.* He filed it away the way he filed everything now, in the growing archive of things he knew and chose not to act on. The archive had gotten heavy lately. Some nights he could feel it pressing on his lungs.

* * *

Tomo he found last, and finding him was not difficult, because Tomo was the only member of the crew who had come early, unpaid, simply because he had said he would.

The young man was sitting cross-legged on the guesthouse veranda with a battered notebook, transcribing something from a cassette player with earphones in one ear—old recordings, Dario realized when he drew closer. Songs. A woman's voice singing in a language that made the humid air feel suddenly thinner, older.

"Tomo."

The boy—man, really, twenty-three maybe twenty-four—pulled out the earphone and stood so quickly he nearly upset the cassette player. "Mr. Kell. I have been studying the route. The middle gorge, here—" he tapped his notebook, "—the maps say Kiunga, but the old names are different. My grandmother's people call the bend after a flood that happened before the government existed. I thought, if we ask the boatmen by the old names, they will trust us faster."

Dario stared at him. In twelve years of assembling expeditions he had never once had a crew member arrive with homework done out of eagerness rather than fear.

"You're from upriver yourself," Dario said. "You told me Western Province. You didn't tell me how far up."

Tomo's face did something complicated—young faces were like that, transparent as water. "My grandmother's village is above the gorge," he admitted. "Not at the source. But the source—" He stopped. Restarted. "Mr. Kell, may I ask a question? Everyone in my grandmother's district says the headwater country is taboo. Not dangerous—taboo. There is a difference. Dangerous means you might die. Taboo means even if you live, you lose something." His dark eyes were steady and terribly sincere. "What is it we are going there to find?"

And there it was again, the open door, the cheap exit, the moment where a man could put down the weight he'd been carrying. Dario felt it all the way through him, a physical ache, like a cramp in the conscience.

"The client believes our principal's father cached survey equipment there before his final expedition," Dario said. Each word selected, weighed, released. "We're going to recover it."

It was even true. It was true the way a photograph is true—a single frame cropped from a much larger scene, with all the context burned off the edges. Survey equipment. Yes. And the land those surveys described, sold three times over to three buyers who each believed they held clean title, backed by a government office whose director took his percentage in a Singapore account, documented in a sealed case that Rafael's father had hidden with the last strength of a dying man who knew no court in the country would ever hear him.

Survey equipment. Dario had become very good at sentences like that.

Tomo nodded slowly, writing something in his notebook. Then he looked up. "And Mr. Kell—if the taboo is real, and the clans refuse us passage, what then?"

"Then we pay them. Everyone has a price."

Tomo didn't argue. He just wrote that down too, and Dario had the sudden, vertiginous sense that the boy was transcribing him the way he transcribed the songs—that somewhere in that notebook, between the flood-names and the grandmother's melodies, Dario Kell was being entered into a record he couldn't read, in a language of obligation he didn't understand, and that someday the entry would be sung back to him at the worst possible moment.

He dismissed the feeling. He was getting very practiced at dismissing feelings. It was, he reflected, the core competency of his entire adult life.

* * *

Rafael landed on a Thursday, off the red-eye from Brisbane, and Dario met her at the Jacksons International terminal because he wanted to watch her face when she saw the crew manifest.

She looked terrible and magnificent, the way she always did after long flights—hollowed out around the eyes, coiled tight everywhere else, a woman running on diesel and denial. She dropped her duffel at his feet without greeting.

"Crew list," she said.

He handed it over. He watched her read it. Wren: no reaction beyond a geologist's nod of professional approval. Ilsa: a fractional narrowing of the eyes, the calculator starting up behind them. Tomo: a pause, a softening—he was the only name on the page that wouldn't cost anyone anything, and Rafael looked at it the way you look at the one clean glass left in the cupboard.

"Good crew," she said. "Expensive crew."

"Worth it."

"Mm." She handed the manifest back and looked at him directly, and Dario felt the old reflex fire, the one from years ago, the flinch he'd never managed to train out of himself whenever she looked at him like that—like she was reading instruments he couldn't see. "One thing, Dario. The boatmen. Have you hired them yet?"

"Local brokers are arranging it. Why?"

"Because the last time a Montrose-led crew went up that river, the boatmen quit at the gorge and my father laughed at them and called it superstition." Her voice was flat, factual, but underneath it Dario heard the hum of a wire pulled taut. "Twelve years later I'm not laughing. If the brokers can't guarantee crews willing to pass Kiunga, we find another way. Promise me you're not hiding problems from me to protect the schedule."

There it was. The third door, the widest one, thrown open right in front of him. *Rafael. The clause on page nine. The client wants the case unopened. Your father's documents prove Voss helped kill him. I've known since before I found you in that diner, and I let you sign anyway, because the directorship they promised me is the first thing I've ever wanted more than I wanted your forgiveness.*

"Problems get surfaced," Dario said. "That's how we work now."

She held his eyes a moment longer, then nodded and picked up her duffel, and the moment passed, the way moments did, the way they always did when you declined them.

* * *

That night he sat alone on the veranda with the rain starting up again, softer now, a patient rain, and he did the accounting he permitted himself once per expedition: the ledger of what he'd told and what he'd withheld.

To Wren: the shape of the truth, minus its teeth. To Ilsa: the outline, minus the body. To Tomo: the frame, minus the picture. To Rafael: everything except the one thing that mattered, delivered in a voice so steady it ought to have won awards.

And the justification rose in him as it always rose, fluent and reasonable and rehearsed: *they're safer not knowing. Knowledge creates liability. If Adeyemi's people intercept anyone, ignorance is armor. I'm protecting the crew. I'm protecting her. When we have the case in hand, when the leverage is real, then there will be time for honesty—honesty from a position of strength, which is the only kind that counts.*

It was a good justification. It had load-bearing walls. It would hold up under questioning, under audit, under almost any weight except the specific weight of Rafael Montrose finding out—which she would, because she always did, because reading terrain was what she was for, and he had known her long enough to know that Dario Kell lying to her was a kind of terrain, and she never missed a contour.

The rain thickened. Down at the harbor, a barge horn sounded once, low and mournful, like something calling across water toward a source it would never reach.

Dario thought about page nine. He thought about the word *unexamined*, sitting there in its clause like a crocodile just under the surface, and he thought about what his father used to say about rivers—that they forgive everything except the man who damns them and drinks downstream of his own wall.

He went inside. He slept badly. In the morning they would brief the crew properly, all four of them around one table, and he would perform the version of the mission he had built, and it would go well, because he was good at this, because he had always been good at this.

And three hundred kilometers upriver, in a country that kept its records in songs, the elders were already singing about strangers coming to take testimony out of the ground—and somewhere in that singing, though Dario Kell would not learn it for many weeks, there was already a verse with his name in it.
