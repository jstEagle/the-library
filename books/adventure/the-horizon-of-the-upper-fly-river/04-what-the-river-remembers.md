## 4. What the River Remembers

Tomo counted the boatmen the way his grandmother had taught him to count anything that mattered: not with numbers, but with names.

There was Kepas, broad as a door, who smoked a pipe even in the wet heat and spoke Kiwai with a lilt from down near the delta. There was his brother Sere, thinner, quieter, with a scar across his forearm that Tomo did not ask about because asking was a door you opened only when invited. There was old Manu, who had run cargo on the Fly for forty years and whose eyes had the color of river water after rain—brown going to gold, gold going to nothing. And there were two younger men whose names Tomo caught only in passing, brothers from a village whose language he half-knew, enough to trade jokes, not enough to dream in.

Five boatmen. Two longboats with outboard motors that coughed like sick men. One river that ran a thousand kilometers from the star mountains to the sea, and Tomo was going up every meter of it that mattered.

He stood on the mud landing at the edge of the port town below the gorge and wrote the names in the small waterproof notebook he carried everywhere, the one his grandmother had given him when he left for Port Moresby to study. *Kepas. Sere. Manu.* Underneath, in his own shorthand, he wrote what each of them loved, because his grandmother said you could not translate for a man until you knew what he would die for and what he would laugh at. Kepas loved his daughter's school fees and hated being hurried. Sere loved the river itself, plainly, the way other men loved wives. Manu loved the old stories and told them only when the motor was off, when the water made its own silence.

The crew came down the landing ramp in the grey light before full morning, and Tomo watched them the way he watched all of them now, with the double attention of a man who lived between languages. There was Rafael Montrose, the guide, tall and weathered, moving like someone perpetually about to leave a room. Dario Kell, the money, in clothes too clean for the mud, smiling the smile of a man who had read that smiles were good for morale. Wren, the geologist, hunched under a pack of instruments, already sweating through her shirt, already muttering coordinates to herself like prayers to a god of numbers. Ilsa, the security woman, who said little and missed less, her eyes doing a slow inventory of the boatmen's hands, the boats' fuel drums, the exits. And the four porters hired in town, who would go as far as the boats went and, Tomo suspected, not one meter further.

"Everything squared away?" Kell asked, and it took Tomo a moment to realize the question was aimed at him.

"The boats are hired. The men are fed. We can launch when you're ready."

"Good man." Kell clapped him on the shoulder, a gesture that was meant to be warm and landed like a receipt. "You've been worth every kina already. These river fellows, they can be touchy about schedules. You make sure they understand we're on a timetable."

Tomo nodded and did not say what he was thinking, which was that the river did not keep a timetable, that the river had never once in the history of the world arrived anywhere on anyone's schedule, and that the men who steered it understood this the way fish understood water.

* * *

They launched into a morning the color of beaten tin.

The Fly spread itself wide and slow, brown-gold, carrying whole islands of floating vegetation on its back like a beast bearing its own fur downstream. On both banks the forest stood in ranks so dense and so green it looked less like vegetation than like a decision the world had made—a decision to be impenetrable. Egrets lifted in white flurries as the boats passed. Somewhere back in the green, a bird cried three notes and waited, and cried them again, patient as a debt.

Tomo rode in the lead boat with Kepas and Sere and Manu, translating as needed, which in the first hours meant almost nothing. The river below the gorge was working water—barges, mission boats, the long canoes of villages that sold fish and fuelwood to the towns. The boatmen talked among themselves about fuel, about a wedding in Sere's wife's village, about the price of a outboard piston ring in Kiunga. Ordinary talk. River talk. Tomo let it wash through him and answered the crew's small questions—*what's that bird, how far to the next fuel stop, is the water always this color*—in his careful, secondhand English.

It was near midday, with the sun pressing down like a flat iron, that Manu turned off the motor for the first time.

He did it without announcement, reaching back and killing the engine so that the boat went sliding on its own momentum into a long, quiet bend, and the silence that came down over the water was so complete that Tomo heard his own pulse. Manu let the boat drift. He was looking at a stretch of the bank where the forest came down to the water in a wall of buttress roots, and at the base of the wall there was a marking—not a carving exactly, not paint exactly, but a place where the trees had been cleared in a half-circle and the exposed earth was patterned with stones laid in a spiral, dark with age, half-swallowed by moss.

"What is that?" Kell asked from the second boat, which had drifted alongside. His voice was too loud for the silence. Everyone heard him flinch at his own volume.

Manu did not answer. He was not being rude; Tomo could see that. He was being careful, the way you are careful walking near a sleeping animal.

"He's asking what that is," Tomo said in Kepas's language. "The boss."

Kepas spat over the side, unhurried. "Tell him it's a story that isn't ours to tell."

"He wants to know what the stones are for."

"Tell him some places on this river are like the old men of a village. You don't question them. You pass quietly and you thank them for letting you pass."

Tomo translated this, softening it, and watched Kell's smile hold while his eyes went flat. Wren was already leaning over the gunwale with a camera, and Tomo saw Kepas's hand come out—gentle, enormous—and press the camera down before the shutter clicked.

"Not for the picture," Kepas said. "Some pictures take more than they keep."

Wren looked at Tomo. Tomo translated, and Wren lowered the camera, and for a moment her face did something complicated—curiosity wrestling with respect and neither winning.

They passed the spiral of stones at idle speed, engines off, and no one spoke, and Tomo found that his grandmother's voice had come up in him unbidden, saying the words she said whenever they passed any place that held its breath: *Walk softly. The ground is listening, and it has a longer memory than you.*

* * *

They made camp that first night on a sandbar the boatmen knew by name, a long pale tongue of an island where the current braided and slowed. The crew pitched tents with the competence of people who had camped before but never here. Ilsa walked the perimeter twice with a flashlight she never switched on. Dario Kell sat on an upturned fuel drum with a satellite phone cradled in his lap like a sleeping child, checking a signal that came and went.

Tomo helped Kepas and Sere cook—rice, tinned fish, the greens Sere had cut from the bank, which the boatmen ate with the confidence of men who knew which plants lied and which told the truth. The crew ate separately at first, and then Wren drifted over, and then Rafael, who crouched by the fire with a tin plate and ate in silence, watching the river go black.

"Your grandmother," Rafael said, in the pause between conversations. "Tomo. You mentioned her village is up this way."

"Upstream. Past the gorge." He said it lightly, and the lightness was a lie, and he suspected she heard it as one, because she had the kind of face that heard lies.

"You're not going to see her?"

"The river decides who visits." He smiled to make it a joke. It did not quite become one. "She's been dead four years."

Rafael looked at him then, really looked, the way she rarely looked at anyone—most of her glances were exits, doors already opening. "I'm sorry."

"It's all right. She prepared me." He turned his plate in his hands. "She used to say that the Fly has two laws. The law of the government, which is written on paper, and the law of the river, which is written in the water. And she said the paper law is for the towns. The water law is for everything the water touches."

"And which law are we under right now?"

Tomo considered the question with the seriousness she had asked it with. "I think," he said, "we are somewhere the paper hasn't reached yet."

Across the fire, Manu had begun, very quietly, to talk. The motor was off. The water made its silence. And the old stories came out the way they always did with Manu—not performed, not announced, just set down between the men like something passed hand to hand. Tomo listened with half his mind and translated nothing, because the story was not for the crew. It was about a time before the towns, when the river ran a different course, when the gorge at Kiunga was not a gorge but a door, and the door had a keeper, and the keeper had been wronged, and everything since—every flood, every drowned canoe, every boatman who went into the upper water and did not come out—was the keeper's long, patient accounting.

"What's he saying?" Kell asked, from the dark beyond the firelight. He had drifted over without anyone noticing, which Tomo suspected was a habit of his.

"A story," Tomo said. "About the river."

"Can you translate?"

Tomo looked at Manu, who had paused, not turning his head, waiting. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark the three-note bird cried and waited and cried again.

"Some stories lose too much in translation," Tomo said. "This is one of them."

Kell held his gaze for a moment, and Tomo watched him decide—watched the calculation move behind his eyes like a fish under ice—and then Kell smiled his receipt of a smile and said, "Of course," and went back to his satellite phone.

But Tomo had seen the flinch. And he had seen Ilsa, at the edge of the light, not pretending to look at the river.

* * *

The second day they entered country where the banks began to rise. The forest climbed away from the water in green terraces, and hills showed their shoulders through the haze to the north, blue and far, the first suggestion of the mountains that fed the river. The current strengthened. The boats labored. The boatmen read the water the way Wren read her instruments—constantly, wordlessly, with small corrections of the tiller that were sentences in a language of eddies and boils and sunken wood.

It was past noon, with the gorge already visible ahead—a dark cleft in the rising country where the whole river narrowed and quickened, the water going from brown-gold to a hard green-grey—that Kepas brought the lead boat alongside a gravel beach and cut the engine, and the other boats came in behind him, and the expedition stopped.

Not for lunch. Tomo knew the difference. Lunch was a noise and a smell and a loosening. This was a stillness. The boatmen got out of the boats and stood on the gravel and did not begin to cook or to smoke or to do anything at all. They stood looking at the gorge the way men stand looking at a house where someone has died.

Kell came up the beach. "Why are we stopping? We're making good time. If we push through the gorge this afternoon we're a full day ahead of—"

"The men won't go past the gorge," Tomo said.

Kell stopped. "I'm sorry. They won't *what*?"

"They won't take the boats past Kiunga." Tomo had known this was coming for two days, had felt it building in the boatmen's silences the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge, and he had hoped, unreasonably, that he was wrong. "I should have told you sooner. I thought—I hoped it was only talk."

"Talk. Tomo. We have a contract." Kell's voice stayed pleasant, which was how Tomo had learned to hear his anger; it went quiet and reasonable, like water rising over a deck. "We paid for the whole route. Up to the headwater country, that was the agreement. There's a schedule."

"There is a paper," Tomo said. "And there is the river."

He turned and translated the situation to Kepas, though translation was hardly needed—the big man had been waiting for the question since the spiral of stones. Kepas answered at length, and Sere added to it, and old Manu said only a few words at the end, and the few words were the ones that mattered.

Tomo took a breath. "Kepas says the upper water is not water to them. It is a grave. Everything above the gorge belongs to the dead and to the ones the dead belong to. He says no payment covers it, because you cannot pay for a thing that was never for sale. He says the men who went up there before—" he paused, because this part he had to get exactly right— "he says the men who went up there before are still there. That the river keeps what it is given."

"The men who went up before," Kell said slowly. "You mean the Montrose expedition."

"I mean everyone the river has kept. There are more than one." Tomo looked at Rafael, who had come to stand at the edge of the conversation without entering it, her arms crossed, her face unreadable in the way of people who have practiced unreadability until it became a face. "Twelve years ago there were others. The boatmen remember. They were young men then, some of them. They remember the search boats. They remember what came back down the river and what didn't."

"And my father's name?" Rafael asked. Her voice was level. "Do they remember that?"

Tomo asked. Kepas answered with something long and low, and Tomo listened all the way through before he translated, because some things you did not hand over in pieces.

"He says they remember him as the man who went up to the door and did not come back. He says there are two kinds of men on this river—the ones who take, and the ones who are taken. He says your father was not a taking man. He says that is all he knows, and that it is not nothing."

Rafael turned away and stood looking at the gorge, at the dark cleft where the water quickened, and Tomo saw her hands, crossed at her arms, tighten until the knuckles paled.

Kell was already recalculating; you could hear the abacus in his breathing. "Fine. Fine. Triple pay. Quadruple. We'll hire porters in Kiunga, we'll find other boats above the gorge—there are other boats, there have to be other boats—"

"There are," Tomo said. "That is not the problem."

"Then what is the problem?"

"The problem," Tomo said, and heard his grandmother's cadence come up through his own voice, uninvited, "is that the boats are not the only thing that won't go past the gorge. Kepas says the river itself has a law, and the law is older than any contract, and the men who wrote your contract have never read it." He paused. "He says we can stand here all afternoon. The answer will be the same at sunset."

Behind them, unnoticed, old Manu had walked to the water's edge. He stood at the margin where the gravel gave way to current, and he did something Tomo had seen his grandmother do and had never once seen anyone do in Port Moresby. He crouched, and he put his hand flat on the surface of the river—not touching the water, but just above it, the way you would rest your hand on the shoulder of someone sleeping—and he spoke to it. Low. Brief. A few sentences, no more.

Then he stood, and came back up the beach, and looked at Tomo with those eyes the color of the water after rain.

"What did you say to it?" Tomo asked, in his own tongue, before he could stop himself.

Manu smiled for the first time in two days. It was not entirely a comforting smile.

"I told it who is coming," he said. "The river already knows. But it is polite to introduce yourself." He nodded upriver, at the gorge, at the dark water running fast and green between the walls. "It knows you too, boy. It knows your grandmother's name. Whether that is good luck or bad—" he shrugged, a small movement, like a leaf deciding—"that is not mine to say. That is yours to find out."

Upstream, the gorge waited, and the river ran through it without pause, without hurry, without the slightest interest in anyone's schedule, carrying toward the sea everything it had ever been given.
