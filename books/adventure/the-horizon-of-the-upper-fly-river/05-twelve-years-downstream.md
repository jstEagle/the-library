## 5. Twelve Years Downstream

The Fly at night did not behave like a river. It behaved like a throat.

Rafael lay awake in the stern of the lead boat, listening to it swallow the dark—water sliding over submerged logs, the wet slap of fish turning beneath the hull, somewhere upstream the cough of a crocodile rolling off a bank into deeper water. The others slept in the tarp shelter amidships: Wren curled around her field notebook like a lover, Tomo sitting upright even in sleep, Ilsa flat on her back with her pistol under her hand, always, as if the river might present an invoice she intended to dispute. Dario was on watch at the bow, a cigarette's coal glowing and dying in the rhythm of his breathing.

Twelve years ago there had been a radio call from this same river, and Rafael had been the one holding the receiver.

She closed her eyes and let the memory come up the way drowned logs come up—slowly, sideways, trailing black water and things that clung.

* * *

The call had come through at 4:47 in the morning, Tulsa time, which made it evening on the Fly. She was nineteen, sleeping on the floor of the storm lab because her apartment's air conditioner had died, and the satellite phone's ring went through her skull like a drill bit. She remembered thinking, before she answered, *no one calls at this hour with good news*, and answering anyway, because it was her father's service number and hope is stupid that way.

*"Rafael."* His voice came compressed and crackling across eleven thousand miles, thin as paper. *"You up?"*

"I am now."

A laugh, static-shredded. In the background, rain on canvas, someone shouting about a generator, the river itself—a vast low hush underneath everything, like the floor of the world breathing. *"We're camped above the gorge. Kiunga's two days behind us and the water's dropping faster than the charts said it would. Your old man may have miscalculated the season."*

"You never miscalculate anything."

*"I miscalculated your mother. Ask her sometime."* Another laugh, but shorter, and then the laugh drained out of his voice entirely, the way water drains out of a channel when the river decides to move. *"Listen. There's something I need to tell you and I don't know how, so I'm going to say it badly. If anything happens down here—"*

"Dad."

*"Let me finish. I've been doing this thirty years and I'm telling you, something about this survey is wrong. Not geologically wrong. Wrong-wrong. There are numbers in these files that shouldn't exist, boundaries drawn where nobody ever walked. I hid copies. That's all you need to know right now—I hid copies, and if I don't come home—"*

The line dissolved into a hiss, came back, dissolved again. She sat on the cold linoleum with the phone pressed so hard against her ear it left a bruise she found three days later.

*"—tell your mother the river knows where it's going. Even when we don't."*

Then the carrier tone, then nothing.

The expedition reported in twice more over the following week—routine position updates, a broken outboard, weather. Then nothing at all. The search found the abandoned camp above the gorge, two swamped boats, no bodies. The inquest took fourteen months to conclude what everyone involved had already agreed upon: Edmund Montrose, senior surveyor, had pushed his team past safe margins in pursuit of a mineral strike he needed to salvage his failing career. Equipment failure, flash flood, pilot error—the report spread the blame generously across the landscape and settled it finally, heavily, on the dead man who couldn't object. Three families received settlements. Rafael's family received the bill for the recovery operation.

Her mother never spoke his name again. Rafael spoke it constantly, silently, like a stone kept under the tongue.

* * *

Now she lay in the stern of a boat on the same river and watched the stars through the gap between tarp and gunwale, and admitted to herself—for the first time, in words rather than shapes—that Dario Kell had not needed to offer her a debt cancellation at all.

He could have offered her a bus ticket to the wrong city and she'd have come.

She turned the admission over carefully, the way Wren turned ore samples. The debt was real. Voss was real; their collectors called from numbers that changed weekly, their interest accrued like mold in the wet season. Saying yes to Dario had erased a hundred and forty thousand dollars, and any sane person would take the job for that reason alone. But sanity hadn't been the operating principle here. When he slid the contract across the diner table in Oklahoma, laminated and initialed, what had moved in her chest wasn't relief. It was recognition. Twelve years of chasing storms across the panhandle—of measuring her life in odometer readings and never signing a lease longer than six months—and every mile of it had been a search pattern. She'd been flying grids over her father's disappearance the whole time without admitting it, looking for the thing he'd hidden, the thing that had killed him, the truth the inquest had buried under its careful bureaucratic dirt.

*I hid copies.* Copies of what? A mineral survey didn't need hiding. Mineral surveys were filed, published, sold to the highest bidder. You hid evidence. And evidence of what crime required a man to seal it in the headwater country of a river whose people considered the source taboo?

The boat knocked against something soft and enormous—a floating island of vegetation, a mat of reeds torn loose upstream—and slid past it. Rafael felt her heart knock too, once, against the inside of her ribs.

Because there was a second question underneath the first, and it was uglier, and she'd been stepping around it for twelve years the way you step around a hole in a rotten floor. The inquest blamed her father. Her mother accepted the verdict because accepting it hurt less than fighting it. But Rafael had read the transcript—read it forty times, in libraries, in truck cabs, in motel rooms across seven states—and the man in that transcript wasn't careless. He triple-checked everything. He once drove four hours back to a site because a benchmark reading was off by two centimeters. The idea that he'd pushed three people into a flooded gorge to chase a bonus was not merely wrong. It was impossible. Which meant the inquest was wrong, which meant it wasn't an inquiry at all, which meant somebody had wanted the record closed in a particular way—

—and the only people with the reach to close records that firmly were the same people now paying her to open them again.

Dario worked for Voss. Dario had been on that original expedition. Dario had come back.

She looked toward the bow, where his cigarette glowed and dimmed. He felt her attention—she saw his shoulders change, the small animal awareness of a man who'd spent years around people calculating whether to trust him—and he didn't turn around. Neither of them slept well enough to pretend.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping, mist standing on the water in columns. They made breakfast of rice and tinned mackerel while the boatmen—six men from the middle-river villages, hired in Port Moresby at wages that had made Tomo wince—worked the poles and the single surviving outboard with the economy of men who had done this since childhood. Rafael ate watching the banks slide by: sago palms leaning over black water, the occasional stilt village with its smoke rising straight in the windless air, children paddling dugouts with strokes older than any nation she carried papers for.

Wren sat beside her, notebook open, chewing thoughtfully.

"Your father's field notes," Wren said, without preamble. "Dario gave me photocopies before we launched. Partial ones."

"Did he."

"There's a page—here—" Wren turned the book around. On it, in her father's small, ruthless handwriting, was a column of figures and, beside them, a notation Rafael knew intimately: *verify against ground truth. Do NOT trust office transcription.* "These coordinates. He flagged them himself. Every other set in the file has a confidence note, a margin, a method. These just have warnings." Wren tapped the page. "In twenty years of reading surveyors' notes, I've never seen anyone warn themselves off their own data like that."

"Maybe the data was bad."

"Maybe." Wren's eyes were pale and steady behind her cracked glasses. "Or maybe the data was true and the map it belonged to was a lie. Those are different problems. One means he was confused." She closed the notebook. "The other means he was scared."

Across the boat, Tomo was talking with the lead boatman, a heavy-shouldered man named Sereva, and Rafael caught fragments—Tomo's grandmother's language flowing between them, easy and warm, and then a hardening, Sereva's face closing like a door, his eyes going upstream toward the haze where the country began to rise. Tomo translated a moment later, quietly, to no one in particular:

"He says after the gorge, the river belongs to the ancestors. He says the company men who went up there twelve years ago—" Tomo stopped. Started again. "He says they're still up there. In the way that matters."

Nobody answered. The outboard droned. Somewhere ahead, though still invisible, the land was gathering itself into the gorge that had taken everything, and Rafael understood suddenly that she was not traveling toward her father's last known position. She was traveling toward her father's last sentence—the one the radio had cut off, the one the inquest had rewritten, the one that waited upstream in whatever form a dead man leaves his testimony.

*I hid copies.*

Well. She was coming to collect.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar, and Rafael took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep. The fire was small; the river was loud. Around midnight Dario came and sat on the opposite side of the coals, and for a long time neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of conversation—the kind they'd been having for twelve years.

"You were on the radio," Dario said finally. "That last night. Weren't you."

It wasn't a question, and he knew the answer, and they both knew he knew. That was Dario. He collected facts the way some men collected debts, patiently, and spent them only when the exchange rate favored him.

"He was going to tell me something," Rafael said. "What he hid. Where. The call dropped."

"Reception up here was always garbage." Dario fed a stick to the fire. His face did what it always did—nothing. "Maybe he told someone else."

"Who? The three people who died with him?"

"The three people who died with him included me, Rafa. And I'm telling you he never said a word to me about hiding anything." A pause, exactly long enough. "Which doesn't mean he didn't."

The fire popped. Downstream, faint but unmistakable, a mobile phone buzzed against metal—once, twice—and stopped. Ilsa's watch rotation. Rafael catalogued it without moving her face: third unauthorized communication in five days, each one shorter than the last, each one followed by Ilsa studying Rafael a half-second too long, the way a person studies a number they're deciding whether to round up or down.

"And you?" Rafael asked Dario. "Why'd you really come back to the Fly?"

"For the same reason everyone does." He stood, brushing gravel from his knees. "Something we lost."

He walked the length of the bar to check the boats, and Rafael watched him go, and grief rose in her again like a drowned log surfacing—slow, massive, displacing everything—because that was the cruelest thing about Dario Kell. Everything he said was true. It was just never all of what was true.

Above the trees, the sky was beginning to organize itself, high cloud streaming in from the west, pressure falling in a way her bones read fluently even here on the wrong side of the world. Somewhere over the Gulf of Papua, weather was being born. She thought of the plains, of supercells stacked like cathedrals over the wheat, of the version of herself that measured life in miles because miles were honest—they accumulated, they went somewhere, they ended at a destination. This river measured differently. This river measured in years.

Twelve of them, downstream.

And tomorrow, if the boatmen held, they would enter the gorge where the ancestors kept what the living had surrendered, and Rafael Montrose would finally learn whether her father had died a failure—

—or a witness.

Behind her, almost lost under the river's voice, Ilsa's phone buzzed once more in the dark.
