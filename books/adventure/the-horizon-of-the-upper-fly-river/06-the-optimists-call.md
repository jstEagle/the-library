## 6. The Optimists Call

The satellite phone sat in the bottom of Ilsa's duffel like a stone she'd been carrying for years, and in a way she had. It was a Thuraya, older model, the casing cracked at one corner where she'd dropped it on a helipad outside Kandahar a lifetime ago, and Voss had issued it to her with the same casual permanence they issued everything—no receipt, no return date, just the assumption that it would end up back in their hands eventually, along with everything else she'd ever been promised.

She kept it wrapped in a shirt that smelled of diesel and river mold, buried under the spare ammunition and the water purification tablets, and every morning when the mist came off the Fly in long gray ribbons she told herself today was not the day he would call.

He called on the ninth day out of Port Moresby, an hour after dark, while the crew lay sweating in their hammocks strung between the stilt posts of a half-abandoned trading station, and the river talked to itself below them in the dark with the patience of something that had been talking for ten million years and intended to keep going long after all of them had stopped listening.

Ilsa heard the phone before she felt it—no, that wasn't right. She felt it first, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm, some animal register in the base of her skull that had never once in her career been wrong about incoming trouble. She was lying awake counting the sounds of the camp: Rafael's breathing, slow and even, three hammocks down. Dario turning over, restless, his mosquito net whispering against itself. Tomo asleep with the unguarded abandon of the young, one arm hanging out into the warm night. Wren muttering coordinates in her sleep, or possibly prayers—hard to tell with geologists.

Then the phone buzzed against the bottom of the duffel, muffled, insistent, and Ilsa was already moving before the second pulse, sliding off her hammock without a sound, because twelve years of this work had taught her that the only thing worse than taking a call from Mr. Adeyemi was letting him wait.

She walked down the bank until the campfire's last coals were a dull orange smudge behind her and the river was a black presence at her feet, wide as a lake here, moving with that heavy silent certainty that big tropical rivers had, and she thumbed the phone open and said nothing, because Adeyemi always spoke first. It was a courtesy, of a kind. He liked to know he'd been waited for.

"Ilsa." His voice came through thin and precise, stripped by the satellite relay down to its essential architecture: calm, intelligent, faintly amused, the voice of a man who found the world's suffering mildly interesting the way an accountant finds an interesting ledger entry. "You're receiving clearly."

"Adeyemi." She watched the river. Somewhere out in the black water a fish broke surface, once, and was gone. "We're nine days up. On schedule."

"Are you." A pause that wasn't really a pause; Adeyemi didn't waste silence, he invested it. "Mr. Kell's daily reports have been somewhat less confident than your assessment. He writes that the boatmen are nervous. That the interpreter is asking questions Kell doesn't like. That Montrose has been standing at the bow every morning staring upstream like a woman trying to remember something."

"Kell should spend less time writing reports and more time steering his boat."

"He does both adequately. Which is more than can be said for the enterprise as a whole." The amusement thinned. What remained underneath was worse. "Ilsa, I'm going to speak plainly, because I know you appreciate plain speech, and because I suspect you've already done the arithmetic yourself and would prefer confirmation over surprise."

Ilsa leaned against a pandanus trunk, its buttress roots rough through her shirt, and looked up at the sky where clouds had shredded the stars into pieces. Here it comes, she thought. The part where the job stops being a job.

"The dry season," Adeyemi said, "ends earlier than your employers originally projected. Hydrological data from Kiunga station suggests the first significant rains in eleven weeks. Possibly ten. After which the upper river becomes what it becomes, and retrieval operations of any kind conclude for the year."

"We'll be well clear by then."

"I'm sure you intend to be. But intentions are not collateral, Ilsa. Collateral is collateral." Something rustled near her boot—a crab, maybe, or one of the enormous river rats—and she didn't look down. "So let me describe the new terms. The syndicate has extended credit to five individuals on the strength of this operation. Montrose's debt is the largest, but Kell accepted financing against his anticipated position, the geologist owes on her own unfortunate history, and the interpreter—" a small sound that might have been a sigh, might have been pleasure— "the interpreter signed something he did not read carefully. Young men rarely do."

Ilsa's stomach went cold and stayed cold. "Tomo doesn't owe Voss anything. He's a translator. He gets wages."

"He received an advance. Against services. With terms. You were present when the papers circulated in Moresby; you countersigned as crew witness. It was all quite legal, which I know matters to you." The line crackled, filled with a faint hiss like sand pouring. "Here is the arithmetic, then. If the cache reaches us before the rains, debts are settled, wages are paid, and everyone walks away richer and wiser. If the cache does not reach us—if the season defeats you, or the river defeats you, or Montrose's sentimentality defeats you—then the debt transfers. Jointly and severally. Every signatory becomes personally liable for the full outstanding sum, plus carrying costs, plus a distress premium the syndicate applies in cases of operational failure."

"That's not how the contract read."

"The contract read exactly how it reads. You may review your copy whenever you like. I'd recommend doing so by lamplight with a clear head, though I suspect you already know what you'll find." He let that sit for a moment. "Five people, Ilsa. Four hundred thousand dollars, distributed across five signatures, compounding monthly. In Port Moresby, that number buys a great many things. Some of them are boats. Some of them are men with boats."

"You're threatening to sell us."

"I'm describing a market. There's a difference, and the difference is that I don't enjoy one and do enjoy the other." And there it was, that thread of genuine delight, brief and chilling as a fish's belly flashing under water. "But I want to be clear that this outcome would disappoint me. I have followed Montrose's father's work since before you were contracted to us. The old man was meticulous. Everything he hid, he hid properly. I believe it's still there, waiting, and I believe your crew is capable of reaching it, provided no one on that river begins to think like an optimist."

"Optimists?"

"Optimists believe things will work out. Pessimists believe things will go wrong. Both of them, you'll notice, are waiting to find out." A pause, and when he resumed, his voice had dropped into the register he saved for the actual point, the one beneath all the other points. "Pragmatists decide what will happen, and then arrange the world accordingly. Be a pragmatist, Ilsa. You always have been. It's why we hired you, and it's why, if the season turns against us, I expect you to make the arrangements necessary to protect the syndicate's position. Whatever those arrangements turn out to require."

The call ended without goodbye. Adeyemi never said goodbye; goodbyes implied endings, and Adeyemi preferred to leave every conversation technically open, every door theoretically unlocked, so that the person on the other end spent the rest of their life wondering whether he was finished with them.

Ilsa stood at the riverbank a long time, the dead phone warm in her hand, watching the water move.

* * *

She did the arithmetic the way she did everything: alone, at night, in precise columns.

Not on paper—never on paper—but in her head, walking the perimeter of the trading station while the others slept, her boots silent on the packed mud, her rifle slung out of habit rather than need because nothing out here had threatened them yet except weather and distance. Four hundred thousand across five signatures meant eighty thousand each on paper, but that fiction collapsed immediately, because the debt wasn't distributed equally and neither were the risks. Rafael carried the principal. Dario carried financing plus whatever private arrangement he'd cut—she'd seen the shape of it in the way he flinched whenever Adeyemi's name surfaced, the guilty eagerness of a man who thought he was going to be rewarded. Wren owed separately, old debts from a disgraced consultancy in Perth that Ilsa had researched in an afternoon. Tomo—

Tomo was the problem.

She stopped at the corner of the station where the stilts rose out of the water and stood looking at the black river, and made herself say it plainly, the way Adeyemi would say it: Tomo was leverage. Not a crew member. Leverage. Twenty-three years old, maybe twenty-four, carrying an advance he hadn't understood against a family village upstream that Voss could reach in forty-eight hours with two men and a boat. If the operation failed, the boy lost everything before he even understood what he'd signed, and the syndicate gained a hostage whose grandmother lived exactly where the documents were headed.

And that was when the other arithmetic started, the one she'd been refusing to do since Moresby, and she hated herself for doing it now, walking in circles in the dark like a caged animal, but she was a professional and professionals did the numbers whether they wanted to or not.

Rafael Montrose: alive, worth four hundred thousand in recovered documents plus the intangible asset of her knowledge of the source country—the river memory, the survey instincts, the father's route locked somewhere in her head. Dead, worth nothing but the example her corpse would set, and examples depreciated fast. Alive was better, provided she stayed compliant. Alive and frightened was best of all.

Dario Kell: alive, useful as an administrator and a traitor-in-residence, the kind of man who would sell the crew's route to anyone who asked if the price included a title. Dead, worth slightly more than Rafael dead, actually, because Dario knew too much about Voss's internal arrangements and men who knew too much had a way of becoming liabilities regardless of outcomes. But killing him would fracture the crew beyond repair, so: alive, watched.

Wren: alive, essential until the coordinates resolved; after that, interchangeable. There were always more geologists.

Tomo: alive, irreplaceable as translator and clan bridge, and therefore—her mind snagged on this like a boot in mud—therefore the most dangerous person on the expedition, because if the clans decided the crew was an enemy, Tomo was the only reason they might not be. And if Tomo turned, if his loyalties flowed upstream instead of downstream, toward his grandmother's village and away from his signature...

She found she had stopped walking. She was standing facing upstream, though she had no memory of turning, and the current pulled past her in the dark toward the source she'd never seen, and somewhere up there, eleven weeks of dry season from now, was a cave with a case in it and an old man's ghost and enough paper to ruin everyone she worked for.

Adeyemi wanted her to be ready to make arrangements. She translated that honestly: he wanted her to be ready to kill Rafael if Rafael tried to keep the documents, kill Dario if Dario panicked, and bring the case downriver at any cost, including the cost of everyone currently sleeping in the hammocks behind her. And she had taken the call, and she had said *on schedule*, and she had not objected to a single word, because objecting to Adeyemi was like arguing with gravity—you did it once, briefly, to establish that you understood the rules, and then you obeyed.

But here was the thing she hadn't said aloud, even inside her own head, until now, standing in the dark with the river talking its endless talk:

She had read Adeyemi correctly for six years. She had never once caught him lying, because he didn't lie—he arranged truths. And the truth he had arranged tonight had a flaw in it, small as a hairline crack in a hull, and she'd almost missed it: if the syndicate truly believed the operation would succeed, they wouldn't need to transfer the debt to the whole crew. Transferring debt wasn't insurance. Transferring debt was preparation for failure. Which meant either Adeyemi expected them to fail—which meant there was a second team already moving, or a plan B she hadn't been shown—or the transfer clause existed not to motivate her but to bind her, to make her complicit in advance, to ensure that whatever happened upriver, she could never walk away clean.

Both possibilities pointed the same direction. Somebody at Voss believed this river was going to eat them, and was arranging matters so that the eating would be profitable.

Ilsa turned back toward the camp, and the fire had died to nothing, and the hammocks hung pale in the starlight like rows of chrysalises. Rafael slept with her face turned toward the water. Dario slept badly, one hand curled around the strap of his document bag even unconscious, even dreaming. Tomo slept like the dead, young and trusting and signed.

She stood over Rafael's hammock longer than she meant to, doing the final column of the arithmetic, the one she'd been avoiding: *what is Rafael Montrose worth to me?* Not to Voss—to her. Six years of contracts, of being the one who made arrangements, and never once had anyone made arrangements for her. Rafael had checked her bandage after the supply run without being asked. Rafael had given Tomo her own insect repellent when his ran out, and lied about having a spare. Small things. Debts of a kind, and Ilsa had survived this long precisely by never owing anyone anything.

Now she owed four hundred thousand dollars to a man who collected in blood, standing guard over a woman who paid her debts in loyalty, and the two ledgers were never going to balance.

The satellite phone sat in her duffel like a stone. Eleven weeks of dry season. Ten, maybe.

She went back to her hammock and did not sleep, and in the morning, when Rafael asked her how the night watch had gone, she heard herself answer, easily, professionally, in the voice of a pragmatist:

"Quiet. Nothing moved."

It was even true. Nothing had moved except her, out there in the dark, shifting her weight from one side of a line she hadn't known she was standing on.

* * *

Three days later, at a bend where the river narrowed and the jungle pressed close enough to drop fruit into the wake, the optimist's answer arrived without calling ahead.

They rounded the bend into the afternoon glare and Ilsa saw it first—she always saw things first, it was her function, her curse—and what she saw was a boat. Not one of theirs. Aluminum, new, twin outboard covers still glossy, riding low in the water with the specific heaviness of a vessel carrying more engine than it admitted to and more men than it needed. Four figures under a tarp rigged against the sun. They wore fishing clothes the way soldiers wore fishing clothes, which is to say convincingly at fifty meters and not at all at ten, and one of them lifted a hand in a lazy wave as the gap closed, friendly as Sunday.

Ilsa's hand found Rafael's shoulder before she'd decided to move it.

"Company," she said quietly, and watched Rafael's face do something complicated and fast—recognition, calculation, a flicker of something that might have been fear or might have been its opposite—and then Rafael raised her own hand and waved back, easy and broad, a woman greeting strangers on a river, while under the waterline of her expression the same arithmetic Ilsa had been running all week visibly began to run itself.

The boats passed. Fifty meters. Thirty. Close enough to see that the lead man's forearms were scarred in the pattern Ilsa associated with a particular training compound outside Lagos, close enough to see the waterproof case lashed flat under the tarp, rifle-shaped, close enough for the lead man's eyes to find Ilsa's face and hold it for one deliberate second—a greeting, an acknowledgment, a message between colleagues: *we see you, we know what you are, carry on.*

Then the bend took them, and the river closed over the place where they'd been, smooth and indifferent as skin over a swallowed thing.

Nobody in the crew spoke for a full minute. The outboards hummed. Water slid past in brown glass. Somewhere in the canopy a bird screamed twice and quit.

"Toll collectors?" Wren offered finally, weakly.

"Fishermen," Dario said, too quickly, and Ilsa watched him not look at anyone.

Rafael said nothing at all. She was looking upstream, at the empty water where the boat had gone, toward the source, and her jaw was set in a way Ilsa recognized from dossiers and stakeouts and six years of reading faces for a living: the set jaw of a woman who has just learned that someone is racing her, and has decided to stop being careful about it.

Ilsa settled back against the gunwale and did the newest column in the ledger, quickly, cleanly, because that was what she was for: whoever those men were, they were ahead of schedule, which meant Adeyemi's timeline was a lie, which meant the deadline wasn't the dry season anymore.

The deadline was the next bend of the river.
