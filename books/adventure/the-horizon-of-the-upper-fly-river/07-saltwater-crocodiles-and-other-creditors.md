## 7. Saltwater Crocodiles and Other Creditors

The river gave them three days of grace and then took them back.

Wren Okonkwo felt the change before she saw it—felt it in the way the *Kuranda*'s hull began to knock against sandbars instead of sliding over them, in the way the boatmen's chatter thinned to a single word repeated like a warning bell: *mangga*. Low water. She had been a geologist long enough to know that every landscape spoke first through its smallest signals, and the Fly River was speaking now in the gravelly rasp of its own bed coming up to meet the sky.

She sat in the stern with the survey case open on her knees, pretending to study it, actually watching Rafael Montrose at the bow. The woman stood like something bolted there, one hand on the rail, scanning the brown water as if she could read the future in its silt. Twelve years ago Wren had been a graduate student at a conference in Brisbane when Elias Montrose presented his Fly Basin work—the magnetic man with the beautiful maps, the one everyone said would redraw the mineral province of western Papua New Guinea. Then he was dead, and disgraced, and his name became a cautionary tale told to young geologists who got ambitious about remote concessions.

And now here was his daughter running his old route with a crew of strangers and a debt collector's deadline, and Wren had signed on because the science was irresistible and because, if she was honest, she wanted to know what had really happened to Elias Montrose. Everyone in the profession wanted to know. Nobody had ever gone looking.

"You're staring at those pages like they owe you money," Dario Kell said, dropping onto the bench beside her. He smelled outboard fuel and insect repellent, the twin perfumes of the expedition.

"They might." Wren turned a sheet toward him. "These are photocopies of field notebooks—Montrose's originals, or copies of them. Half the notation is his personal shorthand. He never published his symbol key."

"And you can read it?"

"Some of it. He taught a seminar on survey methodology my second year. He used fragments of this shorthand on the board." She tapped a column of figures. "Elevations, bearings, sample intervals—that's all standard. But these marginal marks, these little hooked crosses next to certain coordinate pairs? I've been staring at them for two days."

Ilsa Varga passed behind them with a ration crate, moving with that economical predator's walk of hers, and paused just long enough to say, "Whatever you two are whispering about, whisper faster. Adeyemi called. Dry season ends in nineteen days."

Nobody answered her. Nobody needed to. Nineteen days had become the expedition's heartbeat, counted aloud each morning like a pulse they were all afraid would stop.

* * *

The supply run went wrong on the fourth day above the last fuel dump.

They had put in at a stilt village whose name Tomo rendered as something like *the place where the current argues*, to trade for sago, fruit, and diesel from a drum cache the village kept against the government boats' irregular visits. It was supposed to take an hour. Wren stayed aboard to guard the equipment, which meant she was the only one watching when the youngest boatman, a wiry teenager named Sipa who had spent the whole trip teaching Tomo card games, waded into the shallows to push off the trading skiff.

She heard the water move wrong first—a sound like a heavy door drawn through wet cloth—and then the skiff lurched sideways and Sipa went down without screaming, which was somehow worse than screaming. What came up beside him was grey-green and ancient, longer than the skiff itself, its ridged back breaking the surface like a submerged log deciding not to be a log anymore. A saltwater crocodile, four meters if it was an inch, far upstream of any water Wren would have thought saline enough to invite one.

Sipa's leg was still attached when they dragged him onto the bank. That was the extent of the good news. Below the knee the calf was a red ruin, teeth-marks opening the muscle in three places, and the boy was shaking so hard his teeth chattered in a rhythm Wren could feel through the ground where she knelt.

"Ilsa," Rafael said, very quietly, kneeling opposite her. "Your kit."

"My kit is for emergencies."

"This is one."

Something passed between the two women then, some negotiation conducted entirely in the muscles around their eyes, and finally Ilsa crouched and unrolled a medical pack that was frankly better equipped than anything Wren had seen outside a field hospital: antibiotics, sutures, a tourniquet, bottles of iodine, a bag of saline that must have weighed half a kilo and explained a great deal about what else Ilsa carried in that pack.

"Do you know how to use all that?" Wren asked.

"I know how to use enough of it," Ilsa said, and began cleaning the wounds while Sipa's uncle held the boy's shoulders and sang to him—low, rapid, repetitive, a song with the cadence of something meant to hold a soul in its body.

It took two hours. The village elders conferred in low voices, and then a man with a white streak in his hair—the same elder who had spoken the longest during the arrival negotiations—came down to the bank and addressed Tomo, who translated in a voice carefully stripped of inflection.

"He says the crocodile is a creditor," Tomo said. "That's—he says it literally. The river lends and the river collects. Sipa entered the water at a bad hour, in a place where the current argues, and the river has taken its interest. He says we cannot leave until the debt is settled properly. There will be singing tonight. And Sipa cannot travel for two days. His leg could rot otherwise, and—" Tomo hesitated. "He says if the boy loses the leg to our hurry, the debt becomes ours. All of ours. In a way that doesn't wash off."

Rafael looked at the boy, at the grey-brown river, at the nineteen days shrinking to seventeen. Wren watched her do the arithmetic—watched her arrive at the sum and hate it.

"Two days," Rafael said. "We stay two days."

Dario started to object—I saw the breath gather in him—but Ilsa said, "We stay," in a tone that closed the discussion like a door, and Dario, remarkably, let it stay closed.

* * *

That night the village sang, and Wren, unable to sleep in the wet heat, sat at the edge of the firelight with the survey case and a headlamp and worked.

The singing was extraordinary. Dozens of voices braided together in cycles that rose and fell but never quite ended, and beneath the voices someone beat a slit-drum in a pattern that seemed to argue with itself and resolve, argue and resolve. Tomo sat inside the circle of light, rapt, occasionally murmuring along in a dialect slightly different from the one being sung, and Wren understood suddenly that the translator wasn't translating tonight. He was remembering. His grandmother's country lay upstream somewhere in the dark, and every verse of this music came from the same watershed, the same tongue's older sister.

She bent over the photocopied notebook and let the singing fill the part of her brain that usually narrated doubt.

The page she'd been stuck on was dated eleven days before the expedition's final radio call. Coordinates ran down the left margin in Montrose's small, exact hand—bearings and distances from a benchmark near the gorge, marching steadily upstream toward the source region. Standard prospecting geometry. Except for the hooked crosses.

She had assumed the crosses marked samples of interest. That was what a geologist's margin symbols did. But the numbers beside them were wrong for samples. Sample intervals were measured in metres; these figures had too many digits, and they clustered in ways that made no geological sense—one cross beside a coordinate pair, then nothing for two kilometres, then three crosses stacked within fifty metres of each other, then another gap.

Unless they weren't intervals at all. Unless they were—

She pulled out her own notebook, the one with her neat university-trained lettering, and began converting. The hooked-cross coordinates, plotted relative to the benchmark, described a polygon. Not a line—a polygon. Six points, roughly, closing back on themselves, enclosing a shape maybe nine kilometres across, tucked into the fold of country between the gorge and the headwaters.

Mineral claims didn't look like that. Mineral tenements followed geology—fault traces, alteration zones, drainage patterns. They sprawled where the rock told them to sprawl. This shape was too regular. Too deliberate. Its boundaries ran straight for kilometres at a time, ignoring every geological feature Wren could infer from the surrounding notes, cutting across ridgelines and rivers alike.

Boundaries like that weren't drawn by geologists. Boundaries like that were drawn by lawyers.

Her skin prickled despite the heat. She flipped forward through the photocopies, hunting, and found a page near the end of the surviving notes where the hooked crosses appeared alongside a different kind of figure—numbers with decimal points in odd places, formatted like the lot-and-plan identifiers used in cadastral surveys. Land titles. She knew the format because she'd once done six months of contract work mapping tenure boundaries for a mining company, and she had hated every day of it, and she had never forgotten the syntax of ownership.

Elias Montrose hadn't been sampling minerals along that route. He had been walking out property lines.

But whose property? The land around the upper Fly belonged—in whatever sense the government's paper recognized—to the Crown, held in trust, unalienated. That was the phrase. Unalienated. Wren had written it herself on tenure maps, the tidy bureaucratic euphemism for land that had been taken from people without anyone bothering to sign anything. If Montrose's coordinates described titled parcels inside unalienated territory, then somebody had alienated it—quietly, recently, and illegally. Somebody had sold, or leased, or mortgaged ground that no one had ever had the right to sell.

And a geologist who discovered that wouldn't hide his data in a cave because it was commercially sensitive. He'd hide it because it was dangerous.

The singing swelled toward some crescendo that didn't arrive so much as turn a corner, and Wren realized her hands had gone still on the page. She looked up. Across the fire, Rafael sat apart from the villagers, as she always did, arms wrapped around her knees, watching the singers with an expression Wren had spent two weeks failing to classify. Now, with the coordinates burning in her mind, the expression resolved at last. It wasn't hardness. It was vigilance. The look of a woman guarding something she couldn't yet name, in a place where everything, including the river, might be a creditor.

Wren thought about the hooked crosses again. Thought about the fact that Rafael had given her these photocopies without comment, without explanation, days ago—had handed over her father's most private working documents to a stranger with a reputation for asking inconvenient questions. Had she known what was in them? Some of it? Or had she simply been unable to look at them herself?

There was a third possibility, and it settled over Wren with the weight of the river's humidity: Rafael had given her the notes precisely because she couldn't read them. Because reading them required someone who hadn't grown up inside the grief.

The drum changed its argument. Somewhere out on the black water, something large moved with a sound like a door drawn through wet cloth, and Wren flinched before she could stop herself.

* * *

Two days later they loaded Sipa aboard, his leg bandaged and dosed, propped in the shade of the canopy with his uncle beside him, and pushed off into a river that had dropped another visible handspan. Sandbars that had been submerged a week ago now stood exposed like the backs of sleeping animals. The boatmen poled more than they motored, reading the channels the way Wren read stratigraphy—by inference, by habit, by generations of accumulated error corrected.

She waited until the afternoon watch, until Dario was forward arguing with Ilsa about fuel consumption and Tomo was asleep against the supply crates, before she slid onto the bench beside Rafael.

"I cracked your father's shorthand," she said. "Some of it."

Rafael didn't move. "And?"

"The hooked crosses aren't sample markers. They're boundary points." Wren opened her notebook to the plotted polygon and held it out. "Six corners, closed figure, nine kilometres across, sitting inside territory the Crown holds as unalienated. And the associated figures match cadastral formatting. Lot and plan identifiers. Your father wasn't logging a mineral prospect, Rafael. He was surveying land titles that shouldn't exist."

For a long moment Rafael said nothing. The river knocked against a bar; the pole-men called to each other in short phrases. Then she took the notebook, and Wren watched her trace the polygon with one finger, slowly, the way you'd touch a scar to learn its shape.

"Nine kilometres," Rafael said at last. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against weather. "Who owns nine kilometres of the upper Fly?"

"That's the question your father died asking." Wren hesitated, then committed. "There's something else. Whoever drew those boundaries had access to government survey infrastructure—the datum, the plan numbering, the benchmark network. This isn't a company cutting corners. This is the survey office itself, or someone inside it. Your father found a fraud with official fingerprints on it, and he hid the evidence instead of reporting it, because he didn't know how far up it went." She paused. "Or who it went to."

Rafael folded the notebook closed with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when they're afraid of what their hands might otherwise do.

"Nineteen days became fifteen," she said.

"Yes."

"Then we'd better find out fast." She stood, moved toward the bow, and stopped with her back still turned. "Wren. Don't tell Dario. Not yet."

"Why not?"

Rafael didn't answer. She stood at the bow with the brown water sliding past below her, and Wren, watching her, felt the dawning suspicion shift its aim—not at the dead man's notes anymore, but at the living crew, at the contract in Dario's drybag, at the satellite phone Ilsa checked twice a day, at every set of hands on this boat that might belong to a creditor.

Behind them, downstream, the river carried their wake toward the sea. Ahead, the channel narrowed, and the water ran shallower and clearer, and the country on either bank began to rise into hills that no map Wren had ever seen bothered to name.
