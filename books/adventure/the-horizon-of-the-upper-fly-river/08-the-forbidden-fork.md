## 8. The Forbidden Fork

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it, the way grief announces itself before you can name it.

Tomo felt it first as a change in the river's voice. For six days the Fly had spoken in its low, patient monotone—the brown water sliding past the hull with the sound of a conversation held politely at a distance. But now, as the boatmen swung the longboats toward the narrowing channel and the walls of the gorge rose up on either side like the shoulders of something sleeping, the river's voice changed. It deepened. It quickened. Somewhere ahead, water was being forced through stone, and the stone was answering back.

"Kiunga Gorge," said the lead boatman, a broad-shouldered man named Sereva who had barely spoken since Port Moresby except to negotiate fuel and food in the flat tones of someone being paid to endure strangers. He stood at the bow with one hand on the gunwale, reading the water the way Rafael read weather. "We stop here."

"We don't stop here," Dario Kell said from the middle of the boat. He had his contract folder open on his knee, though Tomo had noticed days ago that he never actually looked at it—it was a prop, a talisman, a piece of laminated paper he held the way other men held crosses. "The route goes through the gorge. That's the whole point of hiring boats that can handle it."

Sereva did not turn around. "We stop here."

Tomo translated anyway, though there had been no need; Dario's Tok Pisin was serviceable, and everyone on the river understood the grammar of refusal no matter what language it arrived in. But translating gave Tomo something to do with his mouth while his stomach did something else entirely, because he knew this stretch of water. Not from maps. From his grandmother's kitchen, from her stories told over sago and smoked fish while the rain hammered the iron roof of her house outside Kiunga, stories she had told him in their own language because some things, she said, could not survive translation into English without losing their teeth.

*The river forks above the gorge,* she used to say. *And at the fork, the ancestors keep their records.*

He had been nine years old. He had thought she meant paper.

* * *

They tied up at a shingle beach below the first cliffs, and the argument began the way arguments do when money is involved—quietly, then not quietly at all.

Dario laid it out on the beach with the tide of the river sucking at his boots: triple pay. Then quadruple. He said the word *bonus* twice, and the second time he said it louder, as if volume were a currency the boatmen might accept. Ilsa stood apart with her arms crossed, watching the boatmen the way she watched everything—not their faces but their hands, their weight, the geometry of how many of them there were against how many of the crew. Wren hovered near the supplies, pretending to inventory tins while listening to every word.

Rafael Montrose said nothing. She sat on an overturned fuel drum at the edge of the beach, hat pulled low, looking upriver at the gorge with an expression Tomo couldn't read—and that frightened him more than the argument, because he had spent two weeks learning to read her, and this was the first time her face had gone blank on purpose.

Sereva listened to all of it. Then he spoke, and Tomo translated, and the words came out of him heavier than they went in, because he knew what they would do to the air.

"He says: this water belongs to the clans. The gorge is where the river remembers. Above the fork, the land holds the testimony of the ancestors, and no man carries anything away from it. Those who try—" Tomo paused. His grandmother's voice was in his ears, saying these exact words in another tongue, and for a moment he couldn't tell whose mouth the words were coming out of. "Those who try are erased. Not killed. Erased. Their names are taken back. No one speaks them again. Their mothers' songs forget them."

"That's superstition," Dario said.

"It's law," Tomo said.

"It's *superstition*, Tomo. I'm offering them more money than they'd make in two seasons."

"They're not refusing the money." Tomo heard his own voice go quiet and steady, and was surprised by it. "They're refusing what the money is for. There's a difference. You just can't see it from inside your folder."

Dario looked at him then—a long, cold look, recalibrating, the look of a man discovering that a tool has opinions—and Tomo felt something shift in his chest, some small load-bearing wall cracking. He had been telling himself since Port Moresby that he was only a translator, that words passed through him without sticking, that whatever this expedition was really about, his hands were clean because his hands only carried language. Standing on the shingle with the gorge breathing ahead of them, he understood that translators are never neutral. Every word you carry across leaves mud on your shoes.

* * *

It was Sereva's youngest, a wiry boy of maybe seventeen called Bena, who broke first—but not the way Dario hoped. The boy came to Tomo at dusk, while the others smoked by the fire, and squatted beside him on the stones.

"You speak the old language," Bena said. Not a question. He had heard Tomo on the radio with his grandmother weeks ago, before they left the coast, and had said nothing until now.

"Some. My grandmother's village is upstream. Near the fork."

Bena stared at him. In the firelight his face did something complicated—something between recognition and alarm, the face of a man finding a snake in his own canoe.

"Then you know," Bena said. "You know what's up there, and you came anyway. With these people." He nodded toward the fire, toward Dario's silhouette bent over his maps, toward Ilsa cleaning a blade that had no business on a survey expedition. "Why?"

Tomo opened his mouth and found nothing there. Why? Because Voss paid well. Because the job listing had said *translator, riverine logistics, three months*. Because his grandmother was dead now and the village had scattered and he told himself the work was just work, the way you tell yourself the current isn't strong if you never put a hand in the water.

"I need the money," he said finally, and heard how small it sounded.

Bena shook his head slowly, not with contempt—with something worse. Pity. "Everyone needs the money. That's how the erasure works now. They don't have to take our names anymore. We sell them."

He walked back to the fire, and Tomo sat alone on the stones watching the gorge swallow the last light, feeling the truth of it settle into him like sediment: he had come up this river thinking he was carrying words for hire, and all along the words had been carrying him.

* * *

The boatmen walked at dawn.

All seven of them, taking nothing but their pay already earned, leaving the longboats beached and fueled and useless as furniture. Sereva paused at the tree line and looked back once—not at Dario, not at the money still on offer, but at Tomo. He said something short in the old language, four words, and Tomo translated it for himself alone, because some things should not pass through him into English:

*Your name comes from there.*

Then they were gone into the green, and the crew stood on the beach in the early heat with the sound of the gorge filling the silence, and Dario swore with a fluency that surprised no one, and Ilsa checked her rifle's sling with the calm of a woman revising arithmetic, and Wren said, very quietly, mostly to herself: "Triple pay. They walked off triple pay." As if the fact itself were data, evidence of something larger than fear.

Rafael finally took off her hat. She looked upriver for a long moment, jaw working, and Tomo saw that her eyes were wet—not with fear, he realized, but with something adjacent to relief, which made no sense until later.

"So we walk," she said.

"We *what*?" Dario turned on her. "Montrose, we're talking about headwater country with no boats, no guides, no extraction plan—"

"We're talking about a job you can't finish any other way." She resettled the hat. Her voice was flat, professional, closed. "Unless you want to call Mr. Adeyemi and explain why the dry season is going to end without his documents."

The name landed on the beach like a dropped anchor. Nobody said anything after that.

* * *

It was mid-morning, while Dario and Rafael argued over weight distribution and Wren re-sorted the medical kit, that the old man appeared.

There was no announcement. One moment the tree line at the top of the shingle bank was empty; the next moment a figure stood in it, and Tomo's whole body went cold, because he recognized the posture before the face—the way the man stood with his weight even on both feet, perfectly still, the stance his grandmother had called *standing like a question*. An elder. Not dressed for ceremony, not dressed for anything, just present, the way weather is present.

Yawane. Tomo knew the name the way you know a name from a story—a name spoken by his grandmother with respect, once, years ago, in a list of men whose word still counted for something in the upper country. The old man's eyes found Tomo immediately among the strangers, and there was no surprise in them, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"You are Okafor's grandson," Yawane said. His grandmother's family name, spoken correctly, in the old language, on this beach where nothing of theirs had been spoken aloud in years. "I saw the smoke of your boats two days ago. I came to see whose children were coming to the fork."

Tomo crossed the shingle and greeted him properly, the greeting his grandmother had drilled into him as a boy, the formal words of a junior to an elder of the upper country, and heard his own voice shake on the final syllable. Yawane received it with a small nod, then looked past him—at Dario, at Ilsa, at Rafael standing apart with her hat brim hiding her face, at the stacked supplies and the beached boats.

"These people want to go up," Yawane said. It was not a question either.

"The river fork," Tomo said. "A survey cache. Documents hidden twelve years ago. They believe—"

"I know what they believe." The old man's voice didn't rise, but it seemed to fill the gorge anyway, harmonizing with the water. "Twelve years ago other men came up this river with the same hunger. Four of them did not leave. One of them hid something at the source before the river finished with them." His eyes came back to Tomo, and held. "You know what the source holds, grandson of Okafor?"

"The ancestors' testimony," Tomo said. His grandmother's words, arriving in his mouth like a returning bird. "The land keeps their record. What was done to it, what was promised, what was stolen. The land remembers so the people don't have to carry it alone."

"And those who take from it?"

"Are erased." Tomo swallowed. "Their names are taken back. The songs forget them."

Yawane studied him for a long time. Around them the crew had gone still, listening to a conversation they couldn't understand, watching an old man decide something about them in a language they didn't speak—which, Tomo thought, was probably the most honest introduction any of them had ever received.

"You speak the old words," Yawane said at last, "but you came up the river as a hired mouth for people who think memory is a thing that can be filed and owned." He let that sit. Tomo felt his ears burn. "So I will tell you what I told the last ones, and I will say it in front of them, and you will give them my words exactly, because a translator who softens a warning becomes part of the danger."

He raised his voice slightly—not shouting, never shouting, but projecting the way men project who have spent their lives speaking outdoors to people who needed to hear.

"Tell them: the land above the fork is not empty and it is not abandoned. It is occupied. By the dead, by the living, and by the record of everything done to it. Tell them they may walk there, because the path does not close—but whatever is carried down from the source must first be spoken aloud, in full, before the elders of the clans. Every page. Every word. Nothing leaves that country unspoken. If they refuse this, they should turn around now, because the river will not warn them twice, and neither will I."

Tomo translated. He did it carefully, completely, every clause, and watched the words land: Dario's face tightening around the phrase *spoken aloud, in full* like a fist closing around something fragile; Ilsa's expression not changing at all, which was its own kind of calculation; Wren writing it down, actually writing it down in her field notebook as if it were a condition of tenure; and Rafael—Rafael pulling off her hat again, looking at the old man with an expression Tomo could finally read, and what he read there knocked the breath out of him.

She looked like a woman who had just been given permission to hope about something she'd spent twelve years forbidding herself to hope about.

"And if we agree?" Dario asked, when Tomo finished. "If we accept the condition?"

Yawane turned to Tomo, not Dario. Answering the grandson, not the stranger. "Then you may walk. And the ancestors will watch what you do with what you find." He looked at Tomo one last time, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "Your grandmother sang at my brother's funeral, boy. Her blood is known in this country. Remember that whatever name you carry up that river, you will carry it back down—or it will be kept here for you."

He was gone between one blink and the next, back into the green, and the beach was ordinary again, and the gorge roared on as if nothing had happened.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour. Dario was already talking—route options, elevation gains, water discipline—as if the old man had been a delay rather than a verdict, and maybe that was true for Dario, maybe conditions were just obstacles wearing costumes. Ilsa packed in silence, checking the treeline more often than usual. Wren filled three bottles at the river's edge and said to no one in particular, "Spoken aloud, in full. Before the elders. That's not a superstition, that's a chain of custody."

But Tomo stood a moment longer at the top of the shingle bank, looking upriver toward the fork he had known only as a story, carrying a name that was known in this country whether he wanted it or not.

His grandmother had warned him about this exact moment, he realized. Not the gorge, not the elder, not the erased. She had warned him about the feeling itself—that pull, that terrible gravity of belonging, the way the old country reaches into anyone whose blood remembers it and asks a question no salary can answer. *One day,* she had said, pressing his hand flat against the earth of her garden, *the ground will ask you whose side you are on. Don't lie to it. It always finds out.*

Ahead of them lay three days of walking through country that remembered everything. Behind them lay the boats, the river, the world of contracts and folders and money that forgot names instead of keeping them.

Tomo shouldered his pack and started uphill after the others, and the ground took his weight, and waited.
