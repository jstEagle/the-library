## 9. Upstream on Foot

The boats died at dawn, and nobody mourned them.

Rafael stood on the gravel bar where Yawane's people had left them—four men and a boy who had poled them up from Kiunga against a current that fought like something with opinions—and watched the last of the dugouts slide back downstream, carrying away the last easy miles of her life. The river narrowed ahead into a throat of green. Beyond it, the headwater country rose in folds she could not read yet: ridgelines stacked on ridgelines, cloud snagged in the saddles like wool on barbed wire.

"Triple pay," Ilsa said behind her, to no one. "I offered triple pay and they still walked."

"They didn't walk for money," Tomo said quietly. He was crouched at the waterline, filling bottles, his hands steady in the way of someone who had learned steadiness as a discipline rather than a gift. "They walked because of what's up there. You don't buy your way past that."

"You can buy your way past anything," Ilsa said. "You just haven't found the right currency."

Rafael shouldered her pack before the conversation could curdle further. It weighed thirty-one kilos by her estimate, and every one of them was honest weight—rope, rice, fuel tablets, the medical kit Wren guarded like scripture, and the coil of old survey notes wrapped in plastic that rode against her spine like a second vertebra. Her father's coordinates. The numbers that had brought all of them here, though only three of them knew what they were really chasing.

"Four days if the ground is kind," she said. "Five if it isn't. We move at first light and we stop when the light dies. Nobody wanders."

"And if the ground is cruel?" Dario asked.

She didn't look at him. "Then we learn what kind of people we are."

* * *

The first day taught them humility, which Rafael considered fair tuition.

The trail—if it deserved the word—was a rumor the forest maintained about itself, a thread of beaten earth that appeared and vanished according to some logic older than maps. They climbed through gallery forest so dense that noon arrived as a rumor of light, filtered down through forty meters of canopy into a green gloom where everything dripped. Everything. The air was a wet cloth pressed over the mouth. Within two hours Rafael's shirt was plastered to her back, and within four she had stopped noticing, because there is a threshold past which sweat stops being an event and becomes an atmosphere.

Wren fell twice on the descents, once hard enough that Rafael heard the breath leave him like a tire losing air. He got up both times without complaint, which told her more about his disgrace than his file ever had. Men who complained were men who expected rescue. Wren had stopped expecting anything from anyone some years ago; she recognized the condition because she shared it.

The leeches found them by mid-morning of the first day and never truly left. They came off the wet leaves in blind hunger, thin as brown threads, and attached wherever skin presented itself—ankles, wrists, the soft place behind the knee. Tomo showed Ilsa how to flick them off with the edge of a machete rather than pull, how salt worked better than fire, how you learned to check yourself the way you'd check a watch: automatically, without ceremony.

"You've done this before," Ilsa said, watching him work one off his forearm.

"My grandmother's village is in this country," Tomo said. "I spent summers here until I was nine." He said it lightly, but Rafael caught the pause before *until*, the small door closing behind the sentence. There was a story there she hadn't pried open yet. She filed it with the others.

By evening of the first day they had covered perhaps eleven kilometers. Rafael called the halt at a saddle where wind moved through the trees for the first time since morning, stirring the heat enough to make existence negotiable. They ate rice and tinned fish and drank water measured out in cupfuls, because the streams that should have threaded these slopes had gone quiet—the dry season reaching up into the highlands like a hand withdrawing from a table, taking its silverware with it.

"The springs will be lower tomorrow," Wren said, studying his notes by headlamp. "Your father's log mentions water at the third camp. If his log is right."

"If his log is right," Rafael repeated, and heard how it sounded, and hated Dario a little for making her hear it.

Dario was sitting apart, as he had sat apart for twelve years, cleaning his boots with a stick. He did everything with the methodical patience of a man who believed the world rewarded procedure. She had once found that quality admirable. She had been twenty-three and stupid in the specific way that clever people are stupid: she mistook competence for character.

"Something on your mind, Montrose?" he said, not looking up.

"No," she lied.

* * *

The second day the forest opened onto grassland, and the grassland tried to kill them politely.

Kunai grass stood taller than a man in places, razor-edged, hiding the ground entirely. They walked in single file along paths worn by pigs and people, and the sun, unobstructed now, came down like a verdict. Rafael rationed words along with water. Sweat she lost faster than she could replace it; by midday her lips had cracked and her thoughts had begun to move in circles, returning again and again to the same fixed point, the way a compass needle returns to north no matter how you shake the housing.

The fixed point was a radio call made twelve years ago, on a night of static and rain.

*"Rafael, if you're hearing this—"*

She had been nineteen, in a dorm room in Brisbane, holding a handset she'd begged off a ham operator friend because the expedition's scheduled call hadn't come and hadn't come and hadn't come. And then it had come, broken and drowning in interference, her father's voice stripped down to wire and weather.

*"—if you're hearing this, tell your mother the survey is finished. Whatever they say after, the numbers are real. I hid them where the river starts. Do you understand me? Where the river starts. Don't let anyone—"*

Then nothing. Not even static, afterward. Just the particular silence of a frequency that has stopped being listened to at the other end.

The inquest had taken eleven weeks to decide that Elias Montrose, surveyor, had pushed his team past safe margins in pursuit of a mineral strike, that his judgment had been impaired by debt and ambition, that the deaths of three men were the price of one man's vanity. The report had used the word *reckless* four times. Rafael had read it so many times she could recite the page numbers.

And she had believed it. That was the wound under the wound. For years she had believed it, because believing it was easier than believing the alternative—that someone had helped kill her father, or worse, that he had known he might die and gone anyway, carrying something worth dying for, and left her to inherit only the shame.

"Water break," she called, and the column stopped, grateful, silent.

They had found a seep—a dark stain on a rock face where the mountain wept slowly into a basin of leaves. It yielded barely two liters an hour. Rafael crouched beside it while the others drank in rotation, and watched the water rise with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, and thought: *where the river starts.* She had spent twelve years telling herself those were a dying man's delirium, coordinates invented by hypoxia. But the numbers in the sealed notes matched the phrase exactly. He had hidden the truth at the source, and the source had kept it, and now she was walking toward it on legs that shook, carrying the debt of everyone she'd led into this green cathedral of consequence.

"Montrose." Dario crouched beside her at the seep, close enough that she could smell the salt of him, the machine oil, the faint chemical sweetness of sunscreen failing against this sun. "Your hands are shaking."

"It's called exertion, Kell. You should try it sometime instead of signing things."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. "We need to talk. Properly. Before the ground gets harder."

"We're talking now."

"Not here." His eyes flicked toward Ilsa, fifty meters back, standing watch with the stillness of a woman who missed nothing and reported less. "Tonight."

"There's nothing to say tonight that can't be said now."

"Rafael." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "Twelve years. One conversation. You can hate me during it—I'm asking only that you finish it."

She looked at him then, really looked, and saw what the diner in Oklahoma had obscured: he was afraid. Not of the country, not of Voss, not of Adeyemi's people somewhere behind or ahead of them in this maze. He was afraid of her, of this moment, of the conversation he had flown across the world to have and had been circling for six hundred kilometers like a bird that couldn't land.

"Tonight," she said.

* * *

Night came down fast, the way it does near the equator, dusk lasting twenty minutes before the dark closed like a book. They camped in a hollow below a ridge, fires small and shielded—Yawane's parting instruction, delivered through Tomo in a voice that had carried no anger, only the gravity of a man describing weather: *fires draw eyes, and some eyes belong to no clan.*

Rafael waited until Tomo took first watch and Ilsa rolled into her bag with the efficiency of a soldier, until Wren's breathing went long and even. Then she crossed the hollow and sat on a stone opposite Dario, and the moonless dark between them was maybe two meters wide and twelve years deep.

"Say it," she said.

He was quiet for a long time. In the blackness she tracked him by sound: the shift of his pack, the click of a bottle cap, the exhale of a man assembling sentences he'd rehearsed for a decade and finding none of them load-bearing.

"After the expedition," he began, "when the search was called off—"

"I know when it was called off. Day nineteen. I was there. I read every transcript."

"You read the transcripts. You didn't read what wasn't in them." A pause. "There was a second survey office contact. Not the director. Someone above him. He approached me in Moresby while the search planes were still flying. He had your father's preliminary figures—figures Elias sent by radio before he went dark. Land values. Parcel numbers. Rafael, they weren't mineral surveys. Your father had stumbled onto the land scheme, and the people running it knew he'd stumbled onto it, and they wanted to know what else he had, and where."

The words landed in her chest one at a time, like stones dropped down a well.

"And you told them," she said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She had expected rage. What came instead was something colder and more durable.

"He said it would help the search. That if I confirmed the route, they'd extend the window, commit more aircraft. That lives were at stake and I was wasting time protecting a dead man's secrets." Dario's laugh was short and joyless. "I was twenty-nine and I wanted to be the man who saved the expedition. I gave them the route. The upper fork. Everything."

"And?"

"And the search window closed on schedule. Day nineteen. Nothing changed except that certain documents disappeared from the survey office archive, and the inquest report was drafted by a man who'd had lunch with the deputy director twice that month." She heard him swallow. "I told myself for years that I'd been deceived, not complicit. It took me about eight years to stop believing my own version."

The hollow was very quiet. Somewhere upslope, an animal moved through the kunai with a sound like paper tearing.

"Why now?" she asked. "Why fly to Oklahoma and put a contract in front of me? Why not just tell me this twelve years ago, sober, in daylight, like a human being?"

"Because twelve years ago I still wanted something from Voss. The directorship. The career. The version of my life where the mistake was a rounding error." His voice roughened. "Because telling you would have cost me everything I'd built on top of the lie, and I chose the lie, and I have woken up every morning since inside that choice like a man sleeping in a burning house who keeps deciding the smoke is fog." A breath. "And because Adeyemi showed me the contract terms last month, and I finally understood that Voss intends for no one on this crew to carry the cache back down. Including us. Especially us. And I realized I was going to die out here having never once told you the truth, and that seemed like the actual failure. Not the betrayal. This. The cowardice after it."

Rafael sat with it. The old wound hadn't healed in twelve years—it had simply scabbed over with work and mileage and the particular anesthesia of never staying anywhere long enough to feel anything settle. Now he had torn the scab off on new ground, in the dark, at the top of the world, and underneath the wound was not what she expected. Not fresh blood. Something older. Grief, mostly. Grief for her father, and grief for the twenty-three-year-old who had trusted this man, and a strange, unwelcome grief for Dario himself—for the young surveyor he'd been before ambition taught him arithmetic.

"You let them blame him," she said. "You sat in that inquest room and let them write *reckless* on my father's grave, and you said nothing, because saying something would have cost you."

"Yes."

"That's not a mistake, Dario. Mistakes get made once. That was a policy."

"I know."

She stood. Her knees ached; her throat ached worse. Above the ridge, the stars were ferocious, more numerous than sky, and somewhere among those ridgelines ahead lay the cave where her father had knelt in the dark and sealed the case that proved everything—and named, in his last entry, the man who had sold his route to the people who killed him. She hadn't told Dario about the journal's existence. She suspected, now, that he had guessed.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we climb."

"Rafael." His voice caught her at the edge of the firelight. "Whatever's in that cache—whatever he wrote about me—I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to use it. Use me. I'm the only one on this crew who knows how Voss thinks from the inside, and right now that's worth more than my apology."

She looked back at him, a shape kneeling in the dark, and felt the strange vertigo of hating someone and needing them in the same heartbeat.

"Then pray the cache is where he said," she answered, "because if it isn't, none of us has anything left to trade—not Voss, not the clans, not God."

She slept badly, and woke before dawn to Tomo's hand on her shoulder, and his voice low and changed:

"Rafael. Wake up. Wren's found something down the slope—you need to see it. There's metal in the creek bed. Old metal."
