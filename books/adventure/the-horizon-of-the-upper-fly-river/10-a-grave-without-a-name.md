## 10. A Grave Without a Name

Wren Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning how to read ground, and the ground above the wreck of the original camp was reading her back.

She knelt at the edge of a clearing where the forest had begun its slow work of forgetting. Strangler figs had taken the collapsed mess tent, their aerial roots poured down over the rotting canvas like grey candlewax, thick as her thigh in places. A rusted frame that might once have been a folding table stood upright in the green gloom, held vertical by vines, absurdly domestic. Somewhere beneath the leaf litter were the things that did not rot: aluminium, glass, the zinc coating on steel. She could feel them under her boots the way she sometimes felt water under dry riverbed—a pressure, a presence, an argument the surface was having with what lay below.

"Careful where you step," she said, and heard how her voice came out wrong—too quiet, too careful, the voice of someone walking into a room where something had died and not yet been found.

Rafael Montrose stopped three metres behind her. Wren had learned to read that woman too, these days: the set of her shoulders when she was bracing for bad news, the way she went still instead of fast. Rafael went very still now.

"This is it," Rafael said. Not a question. Her father's camp. Twelve years of not knowing, standing in a clearing full of fig trees.

"It's it," Wren said.

Tomo crouched at the clearing's edge, translating nothing for anyone, because there were no boatmen left to translate for—only the four of them and the hired porters from Yawane's country, who had stopped at the treeline and would come no further. One of them, a broad-faced man named Sereva, made a small motion with his hand, low, sweeping, like a man smoothing a bedsheet. Tomo watched him do it twice before he understood, and when he understood he didn't say anything out loud, but Wren saw his face change. Later, she would ask him. Later, he would tell her: *He was showing me how they covered the dead here. With leaves. Many leaves.*

But that was later. For now there was only the clearing, and the heat pressing down through the canopy like a thumb, and the flies finding the sweat at the back of Wren's neck.

* * *

She had been the one who wanted to stop. That mattered to her, and she knew it would matter to her for the rest of her life, so she made herself notice it even as it happened. Rafael had been driving them upstream on foot for six days with the fixed, joyless intensity of a woman following a coordinate burned into her skull, and when Wren had said, that morning, *the vegetation's wrong, there's a disturbance signature here, something big was cleared and let go*, Rafael had looked at her with an expression Wren couldn't name until later—hope and horror wearing each other's clothes.

Now Wren worked. Work was the only prayer she had ever been any good at. She circled the clearing slowly, hands clasped behind her back so she wouldn't touch anything, cataloguing.

The camp had been abandoned in haste or destroyed in violence; the difference between those two things was written all over the site, if you knew the script. A gas cylinder lay thirty metres from where the kitchen area must have been, hurled, not carried. The mess tent's ridge line had been cut—she found the severed rope ends, frayed white, still knotted to a living tree—and you did not cut your own ridge line to strike camp quickly; you dropped the whole thing and ran. Scattered through the litter were the small archaeology of panic: a boot sole gone hard as horn, a tin of condensed milk swollen and split, a plastic sandal faded to the colour of bone.

And everywhere, the leaves. Decades of leaf fall, ankle-deep, black and sweet-smelling, composting over everything.

"Montrose." Dario Kell's voice came from the far side of the clearing, where he'd been walking the perimeter with Ilsa, both of them moving like professionals, checking sightlines out of habit even though Adeyemi's advance team was supposedly two days behind them. "Over here."

Wren got there first. She told herself afterward that it was because she was closest, but the truth was simpler and worse: some part of her had been walking toward it since the treeline.

It was a mound. That was the first thing. Not a depression, not a scatter of stones—a deliberate mound, maybe two metres long, oriented east-west, raised perhaps forty centimetres above the forest floor and then surrendered to twelve years of leaf fall, so that it read now as a long low swell in the earth, like something breathing very slowly under a blanket. Someone had planted four stakes around it, hardwood, driven deep. Two had rotted to stubs. Two still stood, leaning toward each other like tired men.

There had been a marker. There was no marker now. But at the head of the mound, half-swallowed by root and rot, Wren found the base of one: a flat stone, its face deliberately smoothed, and on it—the breath went out of her—letters, cut deep enough to survive, shallow enough that the lichen had nearly won.

She brushed it clear with the side of her hand, gently, the way you'd brush hair off a sleeping child's face.

No name. Just a date, cut in a surveyor's block capitals, and beneath the date a single word:

*WITNESS.*

Nobody spoke. The forest filled the silence for them—insects, birds, the vast indifferent machinery of the canopy—and Wren knelt there with her fingers on the cold stone and felt the word go through her like a core sample.

Because she had read the official record. Everyone on this expedition had read the official record, or claimed to. Four men had gone up the Fly with Elias Montrose. Three bodies had been recovered downstream, or parts of bodies, over the following months, identified by effects and dental records and finally by the grim arithmetic of subtraction. The fourth member of the party, the junior surveyor, the youngest man on the expedition—

The fourth man had survived. He'd walked out. He'd given testimony at the inquest, pale and halting, blaming navigational error and Elias Montrose's refusal to turn back. His testimony had closed the case. His testimony had put the blame where the survey office needed it to go. And then he had resigned, emigrated, vanished into the ordinary mercy of being nobody, and Wren had always thought—that was the part she'd never said aloud, not to Rafael, not to anyone—that the survivor's guilt must have been unbearable, to be the one who lived.

Except he hadn't lived.

He had died here, in this clearing, within sight of his own camp, and someone who was already dead or about to be had smoothed a stone and cut a single word into it, and the official record said otherwise, and the official record was a lie with a body buried under it.

"Wren." Rafael's voice, very level. "Whose grave is that."

Wren looked up at her. Twelve years of debt and distance sat between them, and none of it mattered, because the answer was going to hurt them both equally.

"The record says Marcus Idowu walked out of this valley," she said. "The record says he testified at the inquest. I've read the transcript. I've quoted it in papers." She heard her own voice go thin. "This is his grave, Rafael. This is the man whose testimony convicted your father."

* * *

They dug because they had to. Wren insisted on it and surprised herself by how much she insisted—her voice cracking, her hands shaking, Dario objecting on grounds of time and Ilsa objecting on grounds of everything, and Rafael ending the argument with four words: *"We dig. Both of us."*

The digging took most of the afternoon. The soil was black and generous and full of roots that fought them, and the heat was a physical weight, and none of them talked. Tomo kept watch at the treeline with the porters, who had begun, without discussion, to sing—low, rhythmic, unhurried, a song with the shape of work in it, and Wren understood without translation that it was a song for this, whatever exactly this was in their law, and that they were singing it from a respectful distance because the dead of other people's stories were not theirs to touch.

Forty centimetres down, the smell changed.

Wren had worked mass-grave forensics for two years after her disgrace, exhuming the victims of a landslide that a mining company's survey had promised could never happen. She knew the smell. She had hoped, stupidly, against every probability, that the mound would hold nothing—that the stakes marked a cache, a store, a sealed drum of documents, another secret but a survivable one.

It held bones.

They laid him out on canvas sheeting with the care owed to any human being and more than he had ever been shown, and Wren did what she could with what she had: eyes, hands, a pocket caliper, a notebook. No skull trauma visible on the fragments present. A healed fracture in the left radius, old, consistent with the broken wrist in Idowu's medical file—confirmation, if confirmation were needed, though the smoothed stone had already settled that. And in the soil at the level of the pelvis, a corroded mass that she freed with her trowel and her fingertips and held up into the bars of late light falling through the canopy.

A field notebook. Waterproof stock, the good kind survey offices issued, wrapped in waxed cloth that had done its job almost perfectly. Inside the wrapping, the pages had darkened to the colour of tea but the ink held, and the handwriting was small and disciplined and getting less disciplined toward the end.

Idowu's handwriting. She recognized it from the margins of the survey maps in the archive copies—junior surveyors initial everything, and Marcus Idowu had initialed everything, obsessively, the way young men do when they are terrified of being blamed.

"He kept records," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Right up to the end. He—" She turned pages with a bamboo splinter, careful as a surgeon. Coordinates. Measurements. Dates. And names—not clan names, not local names, but company designations, parcel numbers, lot references, the sterile vocabulary of land titles appearing again and again in a field notebook that was supposed to contain nothing but mineral readings. "Rafael. These aren't survey notes. These are the same parcel numbers we found in your father's notes. He was documenting it too. From the inside. He was building his own file."

"Why?" Dario asked, and for once there was no calculation in his voice, only the naked question.

"Because he figured out what he was part of," Wren said. "And then somebody figured out that he'd figured it out." She looked down at the bones, at the patient architecture of a man who had trusted paper to protect him. Paper had protected the truth for twelve years. It had done nothing at all for the man.

She kept reading. Toward the final entries the handwriting deteriorated, the letters leaning, the lines sagging downhill, and the last legible page was dated nine days after the date carved on the stone above him—which meant he had lived nine days after the others died, alone or with whoever buried him, writing, always writing, a man converting his remaining hours into evidence because it was the only currency he had ever believed in.

The last entry was two lines. Wren read them aloud because it seemed important that they be said in a living voice, in the open air, under the sky that had watched the whole thing.

*"Kell knows the route. Kell told them the route. If anyone reads this, ask him why he wasn't on the boat."*

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time.

Ilsa broke it, because Ilsa always broke silences, because silence was the one terrain she refused to occupy. "That's not possible," she said. "Kell was in Port Moresby. There are records. Flight manifests, hotel—"

"There are records," Wren agreed quietly. She closed the notebook with both hands, as if it might fly apart. "There are always records, Ilsa. That's rather the point of everything we've found in this valley. Somebody writes the record, and somebody else is in it whether they know it or not." She looked across the clearing to where Dario Kell stood at the edge of the light, and she watched his face do something she had never seen it do in fifteen years of knowing him professionally and eight of knowing him personally.

It went blank. Not calm—blank. The face of a man reading his own obituary in a language he'd studied but never expected to speak.

"Dario," Rafael said.

"I need a minute," Dario said, and walked into the trees, and for the first time since Port Moresby, nobody followed him.

Wren stayed kneeling by the grave. The porters' song had changed key—slowed, deepened, become something with mourning in it—and Tomo came and crouched beside her, and together, without discussing it, they began to refill the grave. They worked until the mound stood again, and then Tomo did something she didn't understand until he explained it: he took four fresh green branches and planted them where the rotten stakes had been, saying quietly, "So he isn't finished. So the place knows someone came back for him."

At the head of the mound, Wren set the smoothed stone upright again, lichen and all, the carved date facing the sky, the carved word facing outward toward whoever came next.

*Witness,* it said. And now, at last, there was a witness. Two of them. One in the ground and one in her pack, ninety pages of a dead man's ink, traveling upstream toward the source of the Fly River in the pack of a disgraced geologist who had once believed that fraud was a crime of spreadsheets, committed at distance, by people who never had to look at what they'd sold.

She had been wrong about that. She had been wrong about so much. The lie didn't live in offices. The lie lived here, under leaves, in a valley that remembered everything, and it had teeth, and it had killed before, and somewhere behind them—two days, maybe less—came men paid to make sure it killed again.

From the trees, faintly, came the sound of Dario Kell being sick.

Wren shouldered her pack. The notebook rode against her spine like a second heartbeat, and she turned upstream, toward the source, carrying a dead man's testimony home.
