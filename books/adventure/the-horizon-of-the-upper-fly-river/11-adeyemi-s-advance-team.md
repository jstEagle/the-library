## 11. Adeyemi's Advance Team

Ilsa Reiner had learned to read men the way other people read weather, and Mr. Adeyemi read like a dry season: no thunder in him at all, just an accumulating pressure that would take something from you before it broke.

He sat across the folding table in the satellite tent with his hands flat on the laminate surface, and he did not look at her while he spoke. He looked at the map. That was his courtesy—Adeyemi never made you watch him decide what you were worth.

"The advance team went in three weeks ago," he said. "Four men. Ex-military, local guides contracted through Kiunga. They're already past the gorge."

Ilsa kept her face still. Three weeks. The crew had spent those weeks fighting current, fighting fever, fighting the river's own stubborn arithmetic, and all that time someone had been walking ahead of them through the same green dark, unhurried, because they'd had a head start and lighter packs and no novelist's daughter slowing them down with questions about her father.

"You didn't mention this in Port Moresby," she said.

"I'm mentioning it now." Adeyemi turned a page of the map with one finger, careful as a man handling evidence. "Mr. Voss believes in redundancy. If Montrose finds the cache, fine. If she doesn't, the advance team will. Either way, the documents come downstream before the dry season ends." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the color of weak tea, nothing in them but patience. "Your deadline hasn't changed. Your odds have improved."

"And if both teams reach it?"

"Then whoever arrives second carries the first team's bags." A small crease at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; Adeyemi's smiles were always not-quite. "You've worked advance before, Reiner. You know how it goes."

She did know. She'd run point for outfits like Voss on three continents, back when the work was asset recovery and the assets were mostly machines. She knew what happened to redundant personnel when contracts closed. It was in the fine print nobody printed anymore: loose ends got trimmed, and the trimmers were usually the people who'd been told they were essential.

"Payment schedule?" she asked.

"Half on retrieval. Half on delivery to Port Moresby." He slid a folded sheet across the table. "Same as discussed."

The paper was warm from the tent's heat. Ilsa took it without reading it, which was itself a kind of reading—she'd learned that the terms never changed, only the ways you could be cheated of them.

"One more thing," Adeyemi said, standing now, gathering his map case. "Montrose. Watch her. Her father cost this company a great deal of money twelve years ago, and grief makes people creative. If she tries to alter the arrangement—if she destroys material, or diverts it, or develops loyalties that conflict with her obligations—you are authorized to act."

"Act how?"

"With judgment," Adeyemi said. "That's why we pay you what we pay you."

He left the tent and the jungle noise came in behind him like water filling a hole, insects and birds and somewhere far off the river working its stones. Ilsa sat alone with the contract and thought about the word *authorized*, and about how authorization was a thing men like Adeyemi handed out the way priests handed out absolution—freely, in advance, and always with the sin attached to someone else's soul.

* * *

That night the camp lay quiet in the way camps do when everyone is pretending to sleep. Rafael had taken first watch upstream. Dario was in his hammock with a headlamp still glowing faintly under the mosquito net, reading survey notes for the hundredth time. Tomo slept like the young sleep, completely, as if the forest were a room he trusted. Wren muttered figures into her notebook even unconscious, or so it seemed.

Ilsa lay in her own net and listened to the camp breathe and did the math she'd been doing since the helicopter lifted out of Port Moresby.

Four people between her and the money. Four variables. The translator was harmless and might be useful later—a local face softened things, and if the clan territory turned hostile, Tomo was the only bridge any of them had. Wren was a coward with a good eye; cowards were predictable, and predictability was worth more than courage in Ilsa's trade. Kell wanted the directorship more than he wanted air, which made him reliable right up until the moment he wasn't. And Montrose—

Montrose was the problem. Montrose had walked into this job carrying something heavier than debt, and Ilsa had watched her carry it upriver for weeks: not greed, not fear, some third thing that didn't fit any ledger Ilsa kept. People motivated by the third thing were dangerous precisely because you couldn't buy the third thing. You could only wait for it to get them killed, or follow it yourself and see where it went.

She thought about Adeyemi's voice saying *you are authorized to act*. She thought about the advance team, four professionals already past the gorge, already drawing Voss money, already—she understood this with professional certainty—instructed that a second team in the field was a complication to be managed.

Hedging wasn't betrayal. Hedging was hygiene. Anyone who entered the bush with only one plan deserved what the bush gave them.

The radio was in the equipment duffel, wrapped in a shirt against the damp. Ilsa waited until Rafael's watch rotation would have her farthest from camp—the long loop along the creek—and then she moved, silent on the wet ground, and carried the set two hundred meters east to a fallen strangler fig whose buttress roots formed a room big enough to sit in. She keyed the set low, dialed in the frequency she'd memorized off Adeyemi's own handset in Moresby—he'd been careless, or he'd wanted her to see it, and with Adeyemi you could never rule out either—and sent the burst.

Position. Strength. Estimated days to source. Requesting instructions regarding primary team disposition after retrieval.

The reply came in eleven minutes, a whisper of static and then a voice she knew. Okafor. Nigerian, like Adeyemi, but nothing else alike—Okafor laughed when he shot people, or so the story went, and Ilsa had once seen him laugh at nothing at all and decided the story was true.

*"Reiner. Good to hear you. We're eight klicks south of your line, ridge country, moving slow. The old man says don't come to us and don't let them come to us until the cave. After the cave—"*

Static ate a sentence. Then: *"—the old man says there won't be an after the cave for anyone but us. Keep them moving. Keep them together. Easier that way."*

Easier that way. Ilsa sat in the root room of the strangler fig with the handset cold against her ear and translated the sentence into every dialect of her profession. *Keep them together.* Not disperse them, not turn them back. Together. Because four people scattered through headwater country were four searches, and four people gathered in one valley were one event, and events could be ended.

Okafor signed off with a joke she didn't answer. She packed the radio back into its shirt, back into the duffel, and walked the two hundred meters to her net, and lay down, and found that sleep did not come with its usual efficiency.

It wasn't conscience. She checked, the way you check a wound for infection, turning the feeling over in the dark. Conscience would have been simpler. This was older than conscience—it was the animal knowledge that comes before a storm drop, the pressure change in the air, the sense that the plan she was inside of had quietly become a different plan, and that she had been promoted from hedge to instrument without being told.

*Keep them together.*

She thought about Tomo sleeping like the young sleep. She thought about Wren muttering numbers. She thought about Rafael on watch, a silhouette against the creek light, motionless in the way of people who grew up in open country and never fully stopped scanning horizons.

And she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like grief: *I have been on both sides of this sentence, and I know exactly which side of it I am on tonight, and I do not know which side I will be on when the sentence finishes.*

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Rafael cooked rice without ceremony and called the day's route from the map, and Ilsa watched her over the fire and saw nothing—no suspicion, no change, nothing in the woman's flat competent morning face to suggest that anything had shifted in the world overnight.

They broke camp. The trail climbed away from the creek into ridgeline forest where the trees stood on stilted roots and the light fell green and dim as aquarium water. Tomo ranged ahead with Yawane's youngest guide, translating bird names and place names, and his laughter drifted back through the trees like something from another expedition, a kinder one.

It was near midday, stopped at a saddle to drink, that Ilsa saw Rafael looking at her.

Not staring. Worse than staring. Rafael was looking at her the way she looked at weather—with that horizon-scanner's attention, cataloguing, filing—and then she looked away and said nothing, and offered the water bottle down the line as if the last ten seconds hadn't happened.

But Ilsa had spent twenty years reading people professionally, and she knew what she'd seen. Not accusation. Inventory.

She ran the tape backward through the night. The radio's burst transmission lasted four seconds; the reply, eleven minutes later, perhaps ninety. Rafael's watch loop took her along the creek, but the wind had been wrong for the creek and right for the ridge—right for carrying a thin signal upslope to anyone standing high on the eastern spur, which was exactly where Rafael positioned her long watches, exactly where Ilsa herself would have posted a sentry.

Had she heard? All of it? The tail end of Okafor's voice saying *keep them together*?

Rafael passed the bottle to Dario and shouldered her pack and started walking, and said nothing, and Ilsa followed her up the trail with the certainty settling into her spine like a splinter working toward the heart:

Montrose had caught something. Maybe only static. Maybe only a voice. But she had caught it, and she was holding it the way she held everything—close, silent, waiting—and that meant the trust in this crew was no longer trust. It was theater. Everyone performing confidence at everyone else while underneath, unseen, the real arrangements went on growing in the dark like fungal threads, connecting everything to everything, waiting for the fruiting body, the sudden revelation, the whole structure flushing visible overnight.

By late afternoon the rain found them, a warm vertical deluge that turned the trail to grease and made speech pointless, and they walked in it single file, blind, each person alone inside their own hood and their own calculations.

Ilsa walked fourth in the line, behind Rafael, and watched the water pour off the woman's shoulders, and thought about the advance team eight kilometers south, closing at their own pace, and about the old man's instruction hanging in the wet air like a smell of smoke from a fire nobody had lit yet.

*After the cave, there won't be an after the cave for anyone but us.*

She flexed her hands inside her sleeves. Somewhere ahead, in the rain, the valley waited with its sealed case and its unmarked grave and its ancestors, and somewhere behind her, eleven minutes of static had already decided what the valley was for.

Rafael stopped suddenly at a bend in the trail, hand raised, and the line halted. Through the rain-hiss, faint, from the ridge to the south—a sound Ilsa identified before her mind finished refusing it.

Metal on stone. Booted feet. Someone else's column, moving parallel, half a kilometer off, keeping pace.

Rafael's hand stayed raised. She didn't turn around. But Ilsa, watching the back of her head, saw the small deliberate tilt of it—listening, confirming—and understood that whatever Rafael had filed away last night, she was filing this beside it.

Two columns now, converging on the same valley. And only one of them knew it was being counted.
