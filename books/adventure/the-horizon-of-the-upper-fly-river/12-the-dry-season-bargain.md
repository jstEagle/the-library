## 12. The Dry Season Bargain

The creek at Wabaga Bend had been a creek once. Dario could see it in the architecture of the place—the clean sweep of stones laid down by water that had run for ten thousand years, the mud-cracked channel cutting through the sago swamp like a scar, the dead fish lying belly-up in the last brown puddle like punctuation marks at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish reading.

He crouched at the edge of the puddle and watched a tadpole struggle in water no deeper than his thumb. It would be gone by morning. Everything here would be gone by morning. The dry season had come down off the mountains like something hunting, and it had found them.

Behind him, the camp was quiet in the wrong way. Not resting-quiet. Calculating-quiet. He'd heard it before, on other expeditions, in other years—the sound of four people and then nine people doing arithmetic with their own survival, each of them keeping private ledgers, each of them wondering what the others were writing down.

Wren sat under the tarp with her boots off, her feet blistered black along the heels, copying figures into her notebook by the flat grey light. She hadn't spoken much in two days. Not since the grave. Dario had watched her change at that unmarked mound of earth, watched something behind her eyes shift from *this is a job* to *this is a crime scene*, and he understood with a professional's clarity that Wren Okafor—no relation to Rafael, though everyone assumed otherwise—was now the most dangerous person on the expedition. Dangerous because she was honest. Dangerous because she wrote everything down.

Tomo was at the edge of camp, sitting where he always sat at dusk, facing upstream. Toward his grandmother's country. The boy held his radio handset in his lap like something that had died and he couldn't bring himself to bury.

And Ilsa stood at the treeline with her rifle slung and her binoculars up, watching the ridge the way she'd watched it every evening since Adeyemi's advance team had crossed their path three days ago—or rather, since they'd crossed *its* path, since the second team had passed them going upstream without so much as slowing down, which meant Adeyemi already knew where the cache was, or believed he did, which meant the only thing left to race for was the documents themselves.

Dario wiped his mouth. His lips were cracked. Everyone's lips were cracked. They had been rationing water for six days, a liter and a half per person per day, and the math said five more days before the reserves were gone, and the math also said the next reliable source was two days' hard walk further upstream, if it existed at all, if Yawane's people hadn't drunk it dry, if the season hadn't taken it too.

He had made a career out of math like this. Twelve years of it. Logistics and leverage, tonnage and time. He had told himself for twelve years that the numbers were neutral, that a man who kept the ledger clean couldn't be blamed for what other people wrote in it.

He didn't believe it anymore. That was the trouble. Somewhere between Port Moresby and this dying creek, he had stopped believing it, and now the numbers just looked like what they were: the price tags on things that shouldn't have been for sale.

* * *

The vote happened after dark, by headlamp, around the cook fire that wasn't a fire anymore—just a ring of stones and the memory of one, because fuel was counted as carefully as water now.

Rafael called it. She stood with her arms folded, backlit by the lamp, and she looked, Dario thought, exactly like her father. That was the cruelty of it. Every year she aged, Elias Montrose came back a little more clearly, until sometimes Dario couldn't look at her directly. The set of the jaw. The way she went still before she spoke, gathering herself like weather.

"We're voting," she said. "Water's got five days in it. The sources ahead are guesses. Behind us is eight days back to the boats, and the boats are another question entirely." She let that sit. "Anyone who wants out gets my share of the argument and half my rations. Nobody gets punished for it. Say your piece."

Silva, the boatman who'd stayed when the others walked at the gorge, spoke first. He was a careful man, Silva, with a face like a closed door. "I go back," he said simply. "My children are downstream. The river has rules and I have already broken enough of them."

"Fair," Rafael said.

One of the porters, a young man named Bex who'd been hired in Kiunga and regretted it daily, raised his hand like he was in school. "I'm done. My feet are done. No offense to anyone."

"None taken."

Wren took longer. She looked at her notebook for a while, and Dario watched her decide how much to say, and he knew—because he'd read over her shoulder once, shamefully, two nights ago—that she had worked out what the coordinates meant. Land titles. Boundaries. Not minerals. She knew, or suspected, and she said none of it.

"I'll go back partway," she said finally. "Far enough to get word out. If anything happens to this crew, someone needs to know where we were." Her eyes came up and found Dario's, and held there a beat too long. "Someone should always know where you were."

"That's the spirit," Ilsa said dryly, and there was laughter in it, but not much.

That left four. Rafael. Tomo. Ilsa. And himself.

"You're sure?" Rafael asked Tomo, and her voice changed when she said the boy's name—softened, the way you speak to someone standing near an edge. "Your grandmother's village is up there. This isn't obligation, Tomo. You can walk away."

Tomo shook his head. He was twenty-three and had lost eleven kilos on this trek and looked carved out of driftwood, but his voice was steady. "Yawane said the ones who take from the source are erased from memory. But he also said—" Tomo paused, translating something in his own head first. "He said the ones who carry truth home are remembered twice. Once by their own people, and once by the ancestors. I want to be remembered twice."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't funny. It was the truest thing anyone had said all week.

Then Ilsa, who never voted early, who always waited to see which way the wind carried the majority before committing her weight to it, surprised him. "I'm in," she said. "Adeyemi's team is ahead of us, not behind. Turning back means walking toward whatever they leave in their wake. Forward's the only direction with information in it." A pause. "Also I want to see what's in the case. Professionally speaking."

"So it's four," Rafael said. "Four go up. Three go down with the satellite phone and the emergency beacon. We rendezvous at the gorge in eighteen days. If we're not there, Wren—" 

"I know what to do," Wren said quietly.

Dario felt the vote settle over the camp like a change in pressure. Two expeditions now, splitting apart in the dark, each carrying half the water and all of the doubt. He helped divide the rations, weighing rice and tablets with hands that wouldn't quite stop trembling—not from fear, he told himself, from fatigue—and he caught Rafael watching him across the fire-ring of cold stones, and he knew, with the certainty of a man who has been waiting twelve years for a bill to arrive, that the moment had come.

She waited until the others turned in. Until the camp was breath and insects and the far-off complaint of some night bird. Then she sat down across from him on a supply crate and said nothing at all, which was worse than any accusation, because silence gave him room to hang himself.

"You've got something to say," she said finally. "You've had it since the gorge. Since before the gorge, maybe. So say it, Dario, because tomorrow we start climbing and I don't lead men uphill with secrets in the pack."

* * *

Here is what Dario Kell knew about confession: it was a currency, same as anything else. Spend it right and it bought trust. Spend it wrong and it bought you a shallow grave. He had watched men confess on three continents—drunk confessions, desperate confessions, deathbed confessions—and he had catalogued them all with the same detachment he brought to manifests and invoices. Confession was a transaction. You paid out shame and received absolution, or pity, or a knife. The exchange rate varied.

But sitting across from Rafael Montrose in the dark, with the dry season pressing down on them like a hand, he found that his carefully stocked inventory of techniques was useless. Because this wasn't a transaction. This was a debt, and debts to the dead compound interest.

"Elias trusted me," he began, and stopped, because even the opening line was a lie by omission, and Rafael's face in the lamplight told him she heard it.

"Try again."

He tried again. "Elias trusted me, and I sold him." The words came out flat, stripped of drama, the way a surgeon describes an incision. "Not his life—I've told myself for twelve years it wasn't his life—but his route. His schedule. Where the expedition would be and when. I sold it to Voss for forty thousand dollars and a promise."

Rafael didn't move. Somewhere in the dark, an insect sawed at the silence.

"The original job," Dario went on, because now that the dam had cracked he couldn't stop it, the words coming faster, uglier, "the survey Elias was running—it wasn't supposed to matter. It was a mineral reconnaissance, boring work, and I was broke, Rafi. I'd mortgaged everything on a copper play in Bougainville that collapsed, and Voss's people found me at the bottom of that hole and offered me a ladder. All I had to do was pass along a few coordinates. Radio schedules. I told myself Elias would never know. I told myself nobody would ever connect it."

"But they did connect it," Rafael said. Her voice was very quiet. "Three men died up there. My father died up there. And the inquest said pilot error, said my father pushed into the wet season against advice, said—" She stopped. Breathed. When she resumed, her voice had gone somewhere below quiet, into a register Dario had only heard once before, the day they buried the empty coffin. "Say the rest."

"The rest is that Voss didn't want minerals either." He made himself look at her. Made himself hold it. "Voss wanted the land. The survey data Elias collected—it mapped clan territories. Boundaries that had never been registered, never been ceded, never signed away by anybody. Prime timber, gold traces, arable valley floor. And if those boundaries could be redrawn on paper—if the clans could be shown to occupy that land only informally—then the government survey office could title it, and Voss could buy it, and everybody downstream got rich except the people who'd lived there for a thousand years."

"And my father found out."

"Your father found out. We don't know how much, or when, or what he planned to do about it. What we know is that his final transmission mentioned discrepancies in the title records. What we know is that the wet-season storm that killed them hit an area no sane pilot enters—which they entered anyway, maybe because they were running, maybe because they were lured. What we know is that the official record shows four survivors walking out of that valley, and Wren found a fifth grave." He spread his hands. Empty hands. He'd been showing people his empty hands for twelve years. "That's the rest."

For a long moment Rafael said nothing. The headlamp between them threw her shadow huge against the tarp behind her, a giant hunched shape that trembled slightly, and Dario realized the trembling was her breathing, controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a woman holding a door shut against a flood.

"Why tell me now?" she asked at last. "Twelve years, Dario. Twelve years of silence, and you pick tonight, above a dried-up creek, the night before we climb?"

"Because of the promise." He swallowed. Here it was. The last of it. "When Voss contracted me to find you—to find *you*, specifically, because you were the only guide alive who knew Elias's route—they sweetened it. Not just debt forgiveness. A position. Director of the National Survey Office. The same office that falsified the records. They promised to make me the man in charge of the lie."

The word *lie* hung in the air between them, and Dario heard how it sounded, finally, outside his own head. Not *discrepancies*. Not *irregularities*. The lie.

"They want the documents unopened," he said. "Unread. Delivered sealed to a contact in Port Moresby, and in exchange I get the directorship and you get your freedom and everyone walks away rich or free or both. That was the deal. That's the deal I've been carrying in my chest since Moresby like a stone." He laughed, and it came out broken. "I told myself it was pragmatic. I told myself a directorship meant power to fix things from inside. I told myself—God help me—I told myself I deserved something after twelve years of being nobody."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the grave." His voice cracked on it. "Now I've watched Tomo translate Yawane's warnings and understood that the people Voss erased on paper are real, are *here*, are watching us from ridgelines we can't even see. Now I've spent six days drinking mud-water alongside people whose lives I gambled for a title and a salary." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and put his head in his hands, and for the first time since he was a boy he let Rafael Montrose see him cry. "Now I know what I am, Rafi. I'm the reason your father's name is dirt. I'm the reason that grave has no marker. And I'm telling you this not because I've earned forgiveness but because if I climb one more step upstream carrying this secret, I'll be the same man who made that phone call twelve years ago, and I can't—I will not—be that man again."

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Long enough for the moon to clear the ridge and lay a white road across the dead creek bed. Long enough for Dario to exhaust every version of the future he could imagine—her walking away, her waking Ilsa, her putting a machete question to him in front of witnesses—and find that he genuinely didn't care which one came. That was the strange gift of confession. Once it was spent, the exchange rate stopped mattering.

When Rafael finally spoke, her voice was rough but level.

"My father used to say you could measure a man by what he did when the map ran out." She stood. Looked down at him, and in the moonlight her face was both hers and her father's, the two generations superimposed, and Dario thought: *there it is. There's the inheritance.* "Tomorrow we climb. You walk in front where I can see you. And when we find that case, Dario Kell, you're going to help me decide what happens to it—with your vote counting exactly as much as mine, and not one bit more. That's the only absolution on offer. Take it or turn around."

"It's more than I deserve."

"Yeah," she said. "It is." She paused at the edge of the lamplight, and he saw her shoulders square, and he heard her take one long breath of the dry, dust-flavored air. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go up into Yawane's country, and I have a feeling the old man's going to want to talk about promises."

She was gone into the dark, and Dario sat alone by the cold fire-ring for a long time, lighter than he had been in twelve years and poorer than he had ever been, listening to the creek that wasn't a creek anymore whisper its last water over the stones—and somewhere upstream, faint on the night wind, he thought he heard drums.
