## 13. Yawane's Path

The rain came at dusk, the first in eleven days, and Tomo felt it on his face like a hand laid there by someone who knew him.

He had been sitting apart from the others, as he had learned to do in the weeks since the gorge—since the boatmen walked away and the elder appeared out of the green like a thought made flesh. The camp was a thin thing now: two tarps, a cold fire ring, four people and their shrinking stores. Rafael sat sharpening her knife with the patience of someone who had decided the blade was the only honest conversation left to her. Dario was inside his tent with the satellite phone he no longer bothered to hide. Ilsa cleaned her rifle the way other people prayed—repetitively, desperately, without expectation of an answer.

Tomo watched the rain come down through the canopy and thought about his grandmother's hands.

She had died when he was nine, in a government apartment block in Port Moresby that smelled of diesel and wet concrete, three hundred kilometers and a whole cosmology away from the river country where she'd been born. But her hands had never left that country. He remembered them working—shelling betel nut, braiding sago, once, memorably, pulling a live eel out of a flooded drainage ditch while the neighbors screamed and she laughed. Hands that knew things. Hands that carried instructions.

*You are the one who goes between,* she had told him, in the last year, when the sickness was already folding her smaller. *That is not a blessing. It is a job.*

He hadn't understood then. He understood it now, sitting in the rain in the headwater country, listening to Dario's low voice through nylon fabric, negotiating with men in Port Moresby about money for a thing that could not be bought.

* * *

Yawane came at first light, which surprised no one who had been paying attention.

Tomo woke because the birds changed their song—a shift in register, a caution note—and found the old man already standing at the edge of camp as though he had grown up out of the ground overnight along with the mushrooms. He wore the same woven cap, the same bone comb thrust through it, and he looked at Tomo first, before anyone else, before even the rifles.

"Your grandmother's people are river people," Yawane said, without greeting. "But your mother married into the hill clans above the gorge. So you carry both waters." He said it in Tok Pisin, but underneath it Tomo heard the older language moving like current under ice. "That is why I can talk to you. And why talking to you is dangerous."

Behind him, Tomo heard the zipper of Dario's tent. Good, some small cynical part of him thought. Let him hear this. Let him hear what we're standing inside of.

"You came back," Tomo said.

"I never left." Yawane's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You moved. I stayed. From where you were going, it looked like I followed."

Rafael emerged from under her tarp, hair plastered flat, eyes already doing their arithmetic—the same arithmetic she did every morning now: water remaining, days remaining, distance remaining, and somewhere off to the side, unspoken, the question of whether any of them would remain. She nodded to Yawane with a formality that Tomo recognized as her particular dialect of respect: she didn't perform deference, but she didn't waste anyone's time either.

"We need passage," she said. "Through clan territory. To the source."

"I know what you need." Yawane turned his gaze on her, and Tomo translated the look even though no translation was needed: *I know what you want. That is a different word.* "For six days you have been walking my people's paths without asking. You drank from streams that have names. You crossed ground where bones sit under the ferns. You did not know. Your ignorance protected you, a little. Ignorance is a kind of blindness, and the ancestors do not trouble themselves much with the blind."

"And if we see now?" Rafael asked.

"Then you are responsible." Yawane said it simply, the way you'd say *then it will rain*. "Seeing is expensive."

Dario came out of his tent with the phone still in his hand, and Tomo watched him do the thing he always did—assess, calculate, reposition. Twelve years ago, Tomo hadn't known Dario Kell. But he had read the expedition reports during the long nights on the river, cross-referenced names, and he knew that Dario had been on the original survey. Knew that Rafael's father had trusted him. Knew, in the way you know a shape by its absence, that something between them had gone wrong in a way neither of them would name aloud.

"Old man," Dario began, in the tone of a man opening negotiations, "we can compensate—"

"No." Yawane didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. The word dropped into the camp like a stone into a well, and everyone heard how deep it went. "You have been offering payment since the gorge. Money. Fuel. Medicine. Rifles." His eyes flicked to Ilsa's weapon with something that was not quite contempt; contempt required more interest than he showed. "You think the land is a storehouse and everything in it has a price tag. That is why your kind get lost here. Not in the bush. In the thinking."

Ilsa, who had been silent at the fire ring, spoke without looking up from her rifle. "Then what do you want?"

It was the right question. Tomo felt it land. Even Yawane paused, and looked at her with new attention, the way you look at a dog that has suddenly spoken.

"What do I want," Yawane repeated. He crouched slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and drew a line in the mud with one finger. Then another, crossing it. Then a circle around the crossing. "This is the source valley. Everything that comes out of it—water, stone, stories, bones—belongs to the law of this place. Not PNG law. Not company law. Older law. The law that says nothing leaves the source without being spoken."

"Spoken," Rafael said.

"Witnessed. Named. Given to the ears of the elders before it touches any other air." Yawane looked up at her. "Whatever your father hid in that place—and do not insult me by pretending you don't know there is something hidden, girl, your face has been shouting it since the gorge—whatever it is, if you carry it out, you carry it first to us. It will be read. Aloud. Translated. Entered into the memory of the clans. Only after that does it belong to you, or to your syndicate, or to whatever government office is waiting downstream with its stamps and its lies."

The silence that followed had weight. Tomo felt it pressing on his shoulders like the humidity.

Because he understood, before the others did, what was being asked. He understood what it meant. The documents—if they existed, if they survived twelve years of rot and flood and the appetites of every scavenger between here and Port Moresby—were leverage. They were the debt-eraser. They were the thing Voss wanted sealed and delivered unopened, the thing Dario's directorship hung upon, the thing that could buy Rafael Montrose her freedom. And Yawane was asking them to pour all of that power out onto open ground, into open ears, where it would stop being a weapon anyone could hold and become simply truth—which is the most powerful and least controllable substance in the world.

"They'll never agree," Dario said quietly, to Rafael, as if Yawane couldn't hear. As if the old man weren't the entire reason they were still alive in this country.

"That is not your decision to make yet," Yawane observed mildly. "But I will tell you the alternative, so you choose with open eyes. You can refuse me. You can try to go around, over the ridge, through the karst country where the sinkholes open without warning and the water is poison and my people have pulled out the bodies of smarter men than you for two hundred years. Some of you might even reach the cave. And then you would come back down carrying what you carry, and you would pass through territory where every man, woman, and child knows your faces now, and knows what you refused. And the law of this place would follow you like a smell." He stood, slowly, unfolding himself joint by joint. "Or you accept, and walk the safe paths, and give the elders first hearing. There is no third road. There is never a third road. That is what your contracts have never taught you."

* * *

They argued for two hours. Tomo sat outside the argument and let it wash over him, the way he'd let the rain.

Rafael wanted to accept—he could see it in her, in the way she stopped pacing whenever Yawane's terms were mentioned, the way her jaw set. She had been moving toward this since the gorge, maybe since Oklahoma, maybe since the radio call twelve years ago that ended her childhood. Dario argued for delay, for ambiguity, for a promise vague enough to break later—I'll consider it, we'll discuss it at the site, nothing binding—and Tomo watched him construct the escape hatches one by one and understood that Dario had already decided to betray this promise too, if it ever became inconvenient. Ilsa said almost nothing, which frightened Tomo more than anything she might have said. She just watched Yawane with those flat gray eyes, running calculations Tomo didn't want to see the output of.

And through all of it, Tomo kept thinking about obligation. About the difference between the obligation written in contracts and the obligation written in blood and ground.

His grandmother had taught him the second kind. When he was seven, she had made him carry a bundle of tobacco to a woman in another settlement, a woman their family owed—not money, nothing so clean—an old debt from before Tomo was born, a favor done to his grandmother's mother in a famine year. The woman took the tobacco and gave him a carved bird in return, and told him the story of the famine while he ate, and sent him home heavier than he'd left. *Now you carry it too,* his grandmother said when he returned. *Not the bird. The story. Debts of story don't shrink when you pay them. They grow.*

Yawane wasn't offering them a deal. He was offering them a debt of story.

In the early afternoon, the argument burned down to embers. Rafael stood at the edge of camp looking up-valley, toward the wall of green beyond which the source waited, and Tomo went to stand beside her. For a while neither of them spoke.

"He's right," she said finally. "You know he's right. Whatever's in that cave—it doesn't belong to Voss. It probably doesn't even belong to me." Her voice was rough. "My father spent his last months learning this country's law better than the survey office ever did. Maybe hiding the evidence here wasn't just strategy. Maybe it was—" She stopped. Started again. "Maybe he was filing it in the only court that couldn't be bought."

"If you say yes," Tomo said carefully, "Dario will find a way around it. Or Adeyemi's people will arrive and make the question moot. You've seen the signs. Someone's been ahead of us for days."

"I know."

"And if you say yes, you should be the one to say it. Not me. I'm the translator. I'm nobody. I'm the kid who carries messages."

Rafael looked at him then, really looked, and something in her expression shifted—recognition, or regret, or both. "You're not nobody, Tomo. You're the only one here whose grandmother isn't spinning in her grave."

He laughed despite himself, a short startled sound, and the laugh decided something in him.

Because that was the thing about being the one who goes between. Nobody asks you. Nobody consults the bridge about which shore it connects. The bridge just stands there and takes the weight, and if it fails, everyone falls, and if it holds, everyone forgets it was ever hard. His grandmother had known. *It is not a blessing. It is a job.* And jobs don't wait for consensus.

Yawane was waiting at the camp's edge, patient as a tree, watching a pair of white cockatoos argue in the crown of a kanda tree.

Tomo walked over to him. Behind him he heard Rafael say his name—once, questioning—and he kept walking.

"In my grandmother's language," Tomo said, "there is a word for a promise made on behalf of people who aren't present. It's used for children and for the dead. Because both of them have to live with what others decide." He swallowed. "I'm making that kind of promise now. On behalf of all of us. Passage through clan territory, by your paths, under your protection. And whatever we carry out of the source gets spoken to the elders first. All of it. Read aloud, translated, entered into the record of the clans. Before it goes anywhere else. Before it belongs to anyone else."

Yawane regarded him for a long moment. Somewhere behind Tomo, Dario had gone very quiet, and that quiet had a temperature to it, cold as a gun barrel.

"You speak for the woman," Yawane said. It wasn't quite a question.

"She'll honor it. I'd stake—" Tomo almost said *my life*, and realized with a strange calm that it was exactly what he was staking, and that this had been true since the gorge. "Yes. I speak for her."

"And the tall one with the telephone?"

"Dario Kell will honor it," Tomo said, "or he'll learn what happens to men who don't. Either way, the promise stands. A promise doesn't break because the person who made it is weak. It breaks because the people who witnessed it let it."

Something moved across Yawane's face then—something old and pleased and faintly sad, the look of a man seeing a shape he'd been waiting for. He reached out and pressed his palm flat against Tomo's chest, over the sternum, and held it there. His hand was dry and warm and heavy as a river stone.

"Your grandmother's people say a promise lives where it is made," Yawane said. "Not in the mouth. Here." He lifted his hand. "It is made now. It cannot be unmade. Go and tell them what you have done, boy-who-goes-between. Tell them gently. One of them will be angry enough to spit."

* * *

He told them gently. Dario spat anyway.

"You had no *right*—" Dario's voice cracked upward, and for a moment Tomo saw the fear underneath the fury, naked and sweating: the directorship, the deal with Voss, twelve years of careful climbing all suddenly standing on ground that could be pulled out from under it by a promise made in mud and Tok Pisin. "We haven't even seen what's in the cache. We don't know what we'd be giving away. You handed these people veto power over—"

"Over the truth," Tomo said. "Yes. That's exactly what I did."

"It's done," Rafael said, and her voice was quiet and absolute, the voice of a woman closing a door on a room she'd been circling for weeks. She looked at Dario, and Tomo saw her looking at him the way you look at a map you've finally read correctly—seeing not the terrain you hoped for but the terrain that was actually there. "It's done, Dario. We walk his path tomorrow at first light."

Ilsa said nothing. She was looking at Tomo with an expression he couldn't parse—something between appraisal and warning—and then she picked up her rifle and walked the perimeter, and Tomo told himself the shiver down his spine was just the evening cool.

That night he dreamed of his grandmother's hands, shelling betel nut by the diesel-smelling window, and in the dream she looked up at him and said, in the old language, *Well done. Now the hard part begins.* And when he woke before dawn with Yawane already standing at the edge of camp, motioning them up-valley into the mist, Tomo understood that the dream had not been a dream at all, but a briefing.

The path Yawane led them onto that morning was invisible until you were on it, and then it was everywhere—worn into the earth by generations of feet, threading between buttress roots, climbing through ravines where the air itself seemed to remember voices. And two hours in, stopping to drink at a stream whose name Yawane made Tomo repeat three times until he got it right, Tomo looked back down the valley they had climbed out of and saw, far below, tiny as insects, figures moving along the riverbank.

Four of them. Moving fast. Carrying something heavy between them.

Heading upstream. Toward the source. Toward them.
