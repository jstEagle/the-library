## 14. The Cache

The valley announced itself before they saw it.

Rafael felt it first in her boots—a change in the ground's give, the packed clay of the gorge giving way to something softer, older, a sponginess that came from centuries of leaf-fall and root-rot and water moving somewhere beneath them the way blood moves beneath skin. Then the sound changed. For nine days the river had been their metronome, that low industrial grind of water over stone, and now it softened, spread out, went quiet the way a crowd goes quiet when something important is about to happen.

Yawane walked ahead of them without breaking stride, his machete swinging in lazy, economical arcs, cutting nothing that didn't need cutting. He hadn't spoken more than twenty words since morning. Tomo translated what little he did say, and Rafael noticed the boy's voice had changed too—lower, more careful, the way people speak inside churches.

"Almost," Tomo said, catching her look. "He says we're almost where the river begins to remember."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one he's giving." Tomo wiped sweat from his brow with the back of his wrist. Nine days of leeches and heat and Dario's silence had worn him down to wire. "Rafael—my grandmother used to say that about places like this. That some country isn't geography. It's testimony. You don't visit it. You *answer* it."

Rafael said nothing. She was saving her breath for the climb, and she was saving something else too—something harder, colder, that she'd been carrying since Wren first put the deciphered coordinates in front of her on that supply run gone wrong. Land titles. Not minerals. Her father hadn't been hunting ore in these hills. He'd been mapping ownership, or the absence of it, or the theft of it, and every step she took upstream now was a step deeper into whatever he'd died trying to hold.

The trees opened.

It happened all at once, the way a door opens—the canopy parting above a bowl-shaped valley maybe half a kilometer across, ringed with limestone walls gone green and dripping, the river here reduced to a clear, shallow ribbon braiding itself through gravel bars so white they hurt to look at. The air smelled different. Cooler. Mineral, like the inside of a cave, like wet stone and something faintly sweet beneath it, orchids maybe, growing where no one had ever picked them.

And at the far end of the valley, where the limestone wall rose sheer out of the gravel, there was a cleft. A seam in the rock, dark as a closed eye.

"There," Yawane said, in his own language, and Tomo repeated it softly: "There."

* * *

They made camp at the valley's edge because Yawane indicated—with two fingers, a small downward gesture—that they were not to approach the cleft until the light was right. Rafael didn't ask what light was right. She'd spent twelve years learning that some knowledge has to be given, not taken, and she was tired of taking.

She sat on a gravel bar while the others filtered water and Ilsa checked the satellite phone for the fourth time in an hour, her thumb hovering over it like a woman deciding whether to scratch a scab. Dario stood apart, as he had stood apart for days, looking up at the limestone walls with an expression Rafael couldn't read—or could read too well, and didn't want to.

"You knew the coordinates," she said quietly, when Tomo had wandered downstream to fill bottles. "Twelve years ago. You knew where my father was going."

Dario didn't turn. "Everyone knew the general area. It was a survey expedition. Surveys have routes."

"He changed the route. The inquest established that. He diverted off the filed plan three days before the radio died, and nobody could explain why." She watched Ilsa's shoulders go still by the water filter. Let her listen. "You were the last person he spoke to on the radio before the final call. You told the inquest it was static."

"It was static."

"Dario."

He turned then, and his face in the flat white light of the gravel bar looked ten years older than it had in Port Moresby, and she understood suddenly that he had been carrying this the whole way up the river—not guilt exactly, but its cheaper cousin, dread, the fear of being found out rather than the wish to undo it.

"Rafael." His voice was gentle, which was worse than anything else he could have done. "Whatever's in that cave, it changes nothing about what happened twelve years ago. Your father made his choices. Voss made theirs. We're here for a case, and then we walk out, and everyone gets paid, and the dead stay dead. That's the deal. That's the only deal there's ever been."

"The dead stay dead," she repeated.

"Yes."

"Then why do you look like a man walking toward his own funeral?"

He had no answer for that. He turned back to the walls, and Rafael sat with the cold thing in her chest and watched the light move down the limestone, waiting for it to be right.

* * *

It was right near dusk, when the sun dropped behind the western rim and the valley filled with that blue, sourceless glow that comes just before full dark. Yawane rose without a word and began walking toward the cleft, and the crew fell in behind him in a line—Rafael first, because Yawane gestured her forward, then Tomo, then Wren, then Dario, then Ilsa with the satellite phone finally, mercifully, left behind in her pack.

The clet was wider than it had looked from across the valley—a mouth, really, four meters high, breathing cool air. Inside, the darkness was total until their eyes adjusted, and then it wasn't darkness at all: phosphorescent lichen traced the walls in pale green rivers, following seams in the stone, so that the cave seemed lit from within, like something alive seen through its own skin.

The floor sloped gently upward. Twenty meters in, Yawane stopped and touched Rafael's shoulder and pointed.

Against the far wall, wedged into a natural alcove above the flood line, sat a hard-case equipment trunk. Orange. Faded almost to pink by twelve years of mineral air, but unmistakably, achingly familiar—she'd grown up with a dozen of them stacked in the garage, her father's field cases, each stenciled with the same block letters: **HORIZON SURVEYS — PROPERTY OF E. MONTROSE**.

Her father's initials. Her father's stencil. Her father's handwriting on the inventory tag, faded but legible, listing contents in his cramped engineer's script: *survey plates (14), field journals (2), seal documentation, personal.*

Nobody spoke. Even the river outside seemed to hold its breath.

Rafael climbed to the alcove. The case was heavier than it should have been, or lighter—her hands were shaking too badly to tell. The latches were crusted with calcite bloom but gave on the second try, and the lid came up with a sigh of cold, sealed air, air that had been waiting in the dark for twelve years for someone to breathe it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth still supple after all this time, lay fourteen survey plates in protective sleeves. Wren knelt beside her with a headlamp and lifted the first one, and Rafael heard the geologist's breath catch.

"Oh," Wren said. "Oh, God."

"What?"

"This isn't a mineral survey." Wren's lamp swept across the plate, and Rafael saw the fine inked lines, the contour work, the parcel boundaries drawn with obsessive precision—and the annotations, hundreds of them, in her father's hand. "These are cadastral overlays. Land titles. Look—" her finger traced a boundary line, then a stamp reproduced in facsimile along the margin, "—this is a government grant instrument. This whole valley. This whole *watershed*. Registered as transferred to a holding company in 1998. Freehold title, Rafael. Except—" she flipped to the next plate, and the next, her voice accelerating, "—except there's no cession anywhere. No clan signature. No negotiation record. These are the clans' territories, and someone sold them *out from under them*, and your father mapped the entire fraud. Every parcel. Every shell company. Every—"

She stopped. Her finger had landed on a letterhead reproduced in the corner margin of plate seven, next to a chain of transfer dates.

**VOSS HOLDINGS LTD.**

And below it, countersigned, the survey office's official seal.

The silence in the cave was absolute. Somewhere in the green-lit dark, water dripped once, twice, counting out the seconds.

"They sold land that was never theirs to sell," Rafael said. Her voice sounded strange to her, very calm, very far away. "Voss and the survey office. Together. And my father found out, and instead of walking away, he re-surveyed the whole watershed in secret and hid the evidence here, where the people it was stolen from could—" She stopped. Where the people it was stolen from could what? Find it? They couldn't find it. They'd never known it existed. Only a survey crew with the coordinates would ever—

Only a crew sent by Voss.

The thought arrived whole and terrible: her father hadn't hidden the evidence for the clans. He'd hidden it *from* Voss, and Voss had spent twelve years knowing it was here, and when the dry season drained the river and the syndicate finally decided the time was right, they hadn't sent lawyers.

They'd sent her.

"Read the journals," Wren whispered. "Rafael. Read the journals."

Two field notebooks lay beneath the plates, rubber-banded, swollen slightly with damp but intact. Rafael untied the second one—the later one—and opened it to the final written page, and her father's handwriting swam up at her out of the lamplight, cramped and slanting, pressed hard into the paper by a man writing fast:

*Day 19. Diverted from filed route. If anyone reads this, know the filed route was compromised—I am certain of it now. Someone passed our position and schedule to Voss's people before we ever left the coast. The grants are fraudulent, all fourteen parcels, and I have the proof, and they will kill for it, so I will not be carrying it home. Whoever comes for this case: it was never about minerals. It was about the land, and who was allowed to keep living on it.*

*Last night Kell radioed ahead from Kiunga. He thinks I don't monitor the relay traffic. He told them where we'd cross the gorge. My own crew chief. Twelve years I've trusted that man.*

*I'm going to split the party tomorrow. The others go downstream with the decoy equipment. I take the real cache up-country with the two men who've agreed to stay. If I don't come back, let the record show the route was sold, not lost.*

*D. Kell. Write it down. D. Kell.*

The cave tilted.

Rafael heard herself make a sound—small, involuntary, animal—and felt Tomo's hand close on her arm, steadying her, though against what she couldn't have said. The lichen-light pulsed green on the page. *D. Kell.* Her father had known. In his last hours, hiding in these hills with two men whose names history hadn't even bothered to record, he had known exactly who sold him, and he had written it down in an engineer's neat block print because engineers believe the record will save you, and the record hadn't saved him, and the record had blamed *him*, and the man who sold him was standing six feet away in the mouth of the cave with lichen-light on his face.

She looked up.

Dario stood at the edge of the lamplight, and he was reading her face the way she'd just read the page, and she watched him understand, in real time, that she understood. Twelve years of friendship and rivalry and silence collapsed between them into a single vertical moment.

"Rafael," he said.

"Don't." Her voice came out level. That surprised her. "Don't say anything. Not one word. Not in here."

"Whatever you think that journal says—"

"*Out.*" She closed the case. The latches clicked like small bones breaking. "We carry it out. All of us. And then, Dario, you and I are going to settle what happened on the filed route, and it isn't going to be settled with money."

Behind him, Ilsa had gone very still, her hand resting on the strap of her pack in a way that Rafael recognized now, finally, after eleven chapters of watching her do it—the way a woman rests her hand when she's calculating distances and angles and whether the person in front of her is worth more alive or dead.

And outside, far downstream, carried up the valley on the evening air, came a sound that didn't belong: the thin, mechanical whine of an outboard motor, running where no boat was supposed to be able to run this late in the dry season.

Tomo translated Yawane's single word before the old man even finished speaking it.

"*Visitors,*" he said.
