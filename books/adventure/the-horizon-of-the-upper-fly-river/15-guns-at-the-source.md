## 15. Guns at the Source

Ilsa Reinhart had heard the valley before she saw it.

That was the first wrong thing. A place like this—sacred, guarded, taboo for twelve years of maps and money—should have been silent. Instead there was water talking to itself over stones somewhere below the trail, and birds doing their idiot morning business in the canopy, and beneath all of it a low hum she couldn't place until she crested the ridge and understood: voices. Many voices. The source region was not empty.

She crouched at the ridgeline with her rifle slung loose and let the valley assemble itself below her. The river here wasn't the brown muscle they'd fought for two hundred kilometers downstream. It came out of the earth pale, almost green, rising from springs that seamed the far wall like veins under skin. Terraces stepped down toward it, old garden beds gone to forest, and at the center, half-swallowed by vines, a limestone overhang sheltered something dark and deliberate—a cave mouth, and before it, small figures moving.

Rafael Montrose stood among them like a woman who had finally stopped running.

Ilsa had been paid to watch people for eleven years, on three continents, for employers who preferred not to be named in any document. She knew what surrender looked like, what resolution looked like, what the particular stillness of a person who has made an irreversible decision looked like. Rafael wore all three at once, and it made Ilsa's stomach do something complicated.

Behind her, downslope, Dario Kell was saying something low to Tomo. Wren was off to the east with the last of the porters, carrying the case wrapped in canvas like a sleeping child. And somewhere below, in the green throat of the valley, Adeyemi's team was already closing.

* * *

She'd made the call two nights ago from the high camp, when the others slept and the rain hammered the tarp into a drum. Eleven minutes on the satphone, coordinates whispered into the dark, positions of four people and one sealed case traded for whatever credit remained in an account she no longer pretended to understand. It hadn't even felt like betrayal. That was the thing she couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't done this work. Betrayal required loyalty underneath. What Ilsa had was arithmetic. Rafael was worth more delivered than defended; the job was worth more finished than abandoned; and the only sin in her profession was being the last one to realize which side of the transaction you were standing on.

So why had she lain awake after the call, listening to Tomo breathe three meters away, feeling like she'd swallowed a stone that was slowly dissolving?

Because of the boy's grandmother's village, maybe. Because he translated the boatmen's refusals without flinching, because he'd walked into the gorge country on his own two feet as if walking into a sentence he'd been born mid-way through. Because when Yawane had appeared out of the mist like a debt come due, Tomo had accepted the elder's condition on behalf of all of them—*whatever is carried from the source will be spoken aloud to the elders first*—and Ilsa had watched a nineteen-year-old bind four adults with nothing but grammar and nerve.

Arithmetic didn't account for Tomo. She kept telling herself that was his problem, not hers.

The radio crackled at her hip at 0940. She checked the frequency twice before answering, though she already knew.

"Reinhart." Adeyemi's voice was exactly as she remembered it: unhurried, courteous, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it. "Confirm your position."

"Ridge above the spring terrace. They're at the cave. Four plus Wren's party coming up the east drainage." She gave the numbers flat and clean. "Montrose has the case open. There are documents. Kell is—" She paused. "Kell is negotiating."

A soft sound down the line that might have been amusement. "Of course he is. Hold your position. We're forty minutes out. Six of us."

"Six," she repeated. "For four tired people and a translator."

"The client wants redundancy." A beat. "And Reinhart—the brief has changed. Listen carefully."

She listened. The rain had stopped sometime in the night and the valley was so quiet she could hear the satellite delay breathing between his sentences, that faint half-second ocean sound, as if the words were crossing water to reach her.

"When the case is secured, the witnesses are to be handled. All of them. Montrose, Kell, the geologist, the boy."

The word *handled* arrived in her ear and lay there, refusing to mean anything else.

"Adeyemi." She kept her voice level the way you keep a boat level in weather. "They're contractors. Locals. The kid is a translator, he's got no—"

"He's got a family name the elders know," Adeyemi said, mild as milk. "He's got eyes that have seen the documents. Everyone down there now has seen or heard something the client prefers unrecorded. You've been in this work long enough to know how records work, Reinhart. Paper can be bought. Memory has to be ended."

There it was. Plain. Not even dressed up.

She looked down the slope at Tomo, who was crouched at the trail's edge sketching something in his notebook—she'd seen him do it a dozen times, writing down words the elders used, whole phrases, as if language itself were cargo he refused to drop. Forty minutes. Six men coming up through country they didn't know, guided by her coordinates, to end a nineteen-year-old's memory.

"I copy," she said.

"You always do." Click.

Ilsa sat back against a tree trunk and did the thing she did instead of praying: she ran the numbers. Six against five, but her six had radios, night optics, and no hesitation. Her five had a rifle (hers), a pistol (Dario's, probably clean), a geologist with a rock hammer, and a translator with a notebook. Rafael had hands I'd seen tie off a boat line in a flood, but hands weren't guns.

The numbers said stand aside. The numbers said forty minutes of silence and then a clean extraction with full payment and a bonus for the coordinates, and by dry season's end she'd be drinking something cold in Darwin with no debts at all.

The numbers said that. But she kept seeing the cave mouth, and Rafael standing in front of it, and she kept hearing Adeyemi say *memory has to be ended* in the same tone he used to order catering.

Eleven years. She had burned villages' worth of other people's secrets, moved men and materials for people whose names she learned and forgot, taken jobs where the collateral was abstract, statistical, someone else's problem printed in someone else's currency. She had one rule, unwritten, never tested: don't look at the faces too long.

She had looked at Tomo's face for six weeks.

* * *

She came down into the valley fast, not bothering with stealth, because stealth was for people with something left to hide. Rafael saw her first and her hand went to the pistol at her hip—Ilsa noted, distantly, that Rafael had gotten better since the Ok Tedi run—and then Dario turned, and then Tomo, notebook still open in his hand like a shield made of paper.

"You made your call," Rafael said. Not a question. Her voice was scraped raw from days of heat and argument, but her eyes were steady, and Ilsa realized with a strange lurch that Rafael had known. Had caught the transmission's tail days ago and said nothing, filed it away, waited to see what Ilsa would do with her rotting trust. Waiting, maybe, for exactly this walk down the hill.

"I made my call," Ilsa said. "And then I heard the new brief." She stopped ten meters out, hands visible, rifle slung backward across her shoulders where it couldn't be mistaken for anything but surrendered. "Adeyemi's forty minutes out. Six men. And the contract changed."

Dario went very still. "Changed how?"

"They're not paying witnesses, Kell. They're erasing them. All four of you. The geologist too." She let that sit. "I was supposed to hold this ridge and watch."

Tomo closed his notebook. Slowly, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room you're leaving. "You called them here," he said. His voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse. "You told them where we were."

"Yes."

"And now?"

Ilsa looked at him. At the sweat-dark collar of his shirt, at the ink smudged on his fingers, at the absolute absence of fear in him—not bravery, she realized, but something older and more frightening: certainty. He had grown up downstream of this valley. He knew what the ancestors' testimony meant. He had never once believed the danger was the men with guns.

"Now," she said, "I'm telling you the rest. There's a game trail along the west wall, under the fig trees. It goes up to a saddle. If you move in the next thirty-five minutes, you're behind them when they arrive instead of in front of them."

"Why?" Rafael asked. One word, stripped bare. *Why would you save us.*

Ilsa found she didn't have a professional answer. So she gave the true one, the one that had been dissolving in her stomach for two days. "Because I've worked for men like Adeyemi my whole life and I always told myself the work was just movement, just logistics, just numbers. And then a kid started writing down dead people's words in a notebook like they mattered, and I couldn't make the numbers work anymore." She shrugged, and it cost her more than any firefight ever had. "I found my line. It was further out than I thought. But it's there."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above them a bird screamed once and quit.

Then Rafael nodded, once, the smallest possible unit of forgiveness, and started giving orders.

* * *

They didn't make the saddle cleanly. Nothing in this country came clean.

Wren's party came up from the east drainage twenty minutes later, breathless, the canvas-wrapped case passed hand to hand like something alive, and by then they could hear the Adeyemi team working up the south approach—machete strokes, spaced and disciplined, men who moved like the billable hours they were. Ilsa took point on the west trail with Rafael behind her and Tomo behind Rafael, and Dario bringing up the rear with the case strapped to his back, and she tried very hard not to think about how much a man's posture changes when he's carrying the only thing in the world anyone wants.

The saddle gave them a view of the whole valley floor, and what Ilsa saw there stopped her with her hand flat against the stone.

The elders were already gathered.

Not dozens—hundreds was closer to it, though counting was impossible in the green shade. Men and women in the faded bright cloth of the river country, some painted, some not, spread along the terraces below the cave in a pattern that was clearly not accidental, clearly not recent, clearly not surprised. Yawane stood at their center, small and upright as a surveyor's stake, and Ilsa understood with a cold slide of comprehension that the clan had known about this day the way the river knows about rain. They had not come to flee. They had come to witness.

"Oh no," Tomo breathed beside her. "Oh—they can't be here. Adeyemi won't—" He stopped. He didn't need to finish. Everyone on the saddle had just done the same arithmetic at the same moment, and the number it produced was unbearable.

Below, the machete strokes ceased. Six figures emerged from the treeline onto the lowest terrace, weapons slung with the casual menace of professionals, and Adeyemi himself walked in front—grey-suited even here, even in the heat, a man who wore civilization like armor. He stopped when he saw the assembled clanspeople. For three full seconds, Ilsa watched him recalculate.

Then he raised a hand—not to his men, but toward the cave, toward Rafael's party frozen on the high trail—and his voice carried up the valley with terrible mildness.

"Dr. Montrose. The case, please. Your debts conclude today, everyone's debts conclude today, and we all go home rich." A pause, pitched perfectly. "Or we conclude things another way. The choice of venue is yours."

Rafael stepped out onto an outcrop where the whole valley could see her. Ilsa watched her friend—because that was what Rafael had become somewhere on the river, God help them both—look down at Adeyemi, at the elders, at the case on Dario's back, and Ilsa saw her arrive at the decision she'd apparently been carrying since the gorge.

But it was Tomo who moved.

The boy stepped past Rafael onto the open rock, into the sightline of six rifles, holding his notebook open above his head like a lantern. His voice went down the valley in both languages, ringing off the limestone, and Ilsa—who had heard him translate threats, refusals, warnings, grief—had never heard him like this.

"These papers are testimony!" he called. "My father's father's mothers are in these pages! You want to buy them? You cannot buy what is already owned! You want to erase the ones who read them?" His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "Look behind me! Look at how many memories are standing on this hill!"

Adeyemi sighed, a small human sound of inconvenience, and lifted his chin a centimeter, and Ilsa saw the nearest two of his men begin to unsling their weapons with the smooth economy of men following an order they'd expected since dawn.

Everything after that happened in the space of a breath.

Ilsa was already moving—down the scree, rifle up, into the open, placing herself on the lower terrace between the barrels and the boy with the purest clarity she had felt in eleven years, no arithmetic in her at all now, nothing left in her but the line itself. She put three rounds into the ground six feet in front of the lead gunman, close enough to sting his shins with stone splinters, and her voice came out of her with a force she didn't know she owned:

"THAT'S FAR ENOUGH."

The valley froze. Six muzzles wavered, tracking her. Behind her she heard Rafael's pistol clear leather, heard Dario's breath go ragged, heard the elders rise—all of them, hundreds, rising together without a word, a slow tide of bodies filling the terraces—and Adeyemi stood in the middle of it with his suit going dark at the armpits and his face finally, finally losing its mildness.

"Reinhart," he said quietly. "You know what happens to people who cross the client."

"Yeah," Ilsa said. Her sights rested on his sternum, steady as a heartbeat. "I've done it to enough of them. I know exactly how it goes."

The muzzle of the second gunman drifted, indecisive, off Tomo and toward her—and in that half-second of drift, everything hung suspended: the case, the cave, the boy, the tide of witnesses, the whole rotten architecture of twelve years balanced on a trigger finger in the green light of a valley that remembered everything.

Then, from the treeline behind Adeyemi's team, a horn sounded—deep, wooden, enormous—and out of the forest came Yawane's people, dozens more, silent, surrounding the six men the way water surrounds a stone, and Adeyemi's radio crackled uselessly in his hand because Ilsa had cut the relay an hour ago and he was only now understanding he'd been talking to no one.

His eyes found hers across the terrace. For the first time since Port Moresby, Mr. Adeyemi looked like a man doing arithmetic he was losing.

"Last chance, Reinhart," he said.

"No," Ilsa said, and discovered she was smiling. "I think it's yours."
