## 16. What Was Sworn Off

The first shot came from the ridge above the cave, and the valley answered it.

Rafael heard it the way you hear thunder in Oklahoma—not as a single event but as a pressure change, a rearrangement of the air's intentions. Then a second shot, flatter, closer, and Tomo was pulling her down behind a shelf of limestone that still held the morning's coolness, and the world broke into pieces: Ilsa shouting in three languages, Yawane's people melting into the treeline like water finding cracks, Adeyemi's men moving down the slope with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this before and been paid well for it.

The case was six meters away.

Six meters of open ground between the limestone shelf and the flat stone where her father's sealed aluminum case sat where they'd opened it an hour ago—where she'd read his last entry and learned that the man walking beside her for twelve years of silence had sold him. The journal was inside it now, tucked back beneath the land-title surveys, the whole weight of two betrayals resting on a slab of rock older than any government that had ever claimed this country.

"Dario!" she shouted, because he wasn't behind cover anymore.

He was moving toward the case. Low, fast, in the awkward crouch of a man who'd spent his life in offices and survey tents and never once under fire. A round sparked off the stone beside his knee and he didn't stop, and Rafael understood with a cold, clarifying horror that she didn't know which direction he was running—for the documents or for Voss—and that both possibilities looked exactly the same from here: a man sprinting through gunfire toward her father's last words.

She went after him.

It was stupid. Every instinct she'd sharpened over twelve years of staying alive by staying gone said stay down, wait, let the geometry resolve itself. But her body had already decided, the way it decided in a truck chasing a wall cloud across the panhandle—that place past calculation where you simply move toward the thing that matters and let the odds sort themselves out afterward.

The ground between was chaos. Bullets chewed the ferns along the stream margin; somewhere to her left one of Adeyemi's men screamed something in Portuguese and another voice answered in English, calm, professional. She registered all of it sideways, the way you register weather, because the only fixed point in the world was the case, and Dario reaching it, and Dario going down.

He didn't fall like a man hit. He fell like a man throwing himself—the full extension of his body over the aluminum box, arms wrapped around it, cheek pressed to the metal as a round struck the stone beside his shoulder and ricocheted singing off into the trees. He took a second in the hip. She saw the impact jolt through him, saw him grunt and hold on anyway, curling tighter around the case like a man sheltering a child, or a secret, or the only thing left of himself worth anything.

Rafael slid the last meter on her stomach through wet leaf litter and grabbed his collar.

"Are you hit?"

"Hip." His face was gray, sweat cutting channels through the dust. "Through-and-through, I think. Don't—" He coughed. "Don't let them take it."

"Which side are you on, Dario?" It came out of her raw, ugly, twelve years overdue. "Right now. Lying there bleeding on my father's grave-ground. Which side."

His eyes found hers, and for once there was no directorship in them, no ambition, no careful arithmetic about unopened documents and promised careers. Just pain and something underneath the pain that might have been shame finally arriving at its own address.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the truth, Rafa. I've been on Voss's side so long I forgot there was another one. But when the shooting started—" he laughed, wet and broken, "—I ran for the case. Not away from it. Toward it. Maybe that means something. Maybe it doesn't. Ask me again if we live."

A burst of fire walked up the streambed toward them. Ilsa's voice cut across the valley—*left flank, MOVE*—and then the specific percussive violence of her rifle answering, disciplined, measured, the shots of a woman buying seconds rather than trying to win anything.

Rafael got her hands under Dario's arms and hauled. He cried out once, bit it off, and dragged his legs, leaving a dark smear on the pale stone. Together they got behind the shelf where Tomo waited, white-faced, gripping Yawane's arm. The old man had not crouched. He stood upright in the falling light with the unhurried posture of someone watching rain come across a valley he'd watched rain cross ten thousand times.

"They will not win here," Yawane said, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather. "But they can kill everyone first. That is also a kind of losing."

* * *

The fight lasted eleven minutes. Rafael counted them against her own heartbeat, which had gone slow and strange, the way it did inside storms.

Ilsa fought like someone settling accounts. She moved between positions with a fluid economy that made Wren's earlier descriptions of her seem almost gentle, and twice Rafael saw her hold fire on targets she could have taken—held fire, shifted, covered Tomo instead. Something was happening inside the mercenary, some ledger being rewritten line by line, but there was no time to ask what.

Adeyemi's team was better armed and worse motivated. They'd expected a retrieval, not a war. When two of their number went down—one dead, one screaming in a way that stripped the professionalism out of the others—their formation loosened like a knot pulled from the wrong end. By the eighth minute they were fighting backward, up the slope, using the same cover their attackers had used minutes before. By the eleventh they were gone, withdrawn beyond the ridgeline, dragging their wounded, and the valley filled back up with birdsong as though nothing had happened, as though the place had simply shrugged off the interruption the way a river shrugs off a thrown stick.

Four rounds had struck the sacred stones. Rafael watched Yawane's people mark each impact with ash, murmuring, repairing something that could not be repaired but must nevertheless be attended to. That was when she understood the shape of the next hour, the way you understand a storm cell's rotation before the hook echo appears on radar: not what would happen, but what had already become inevitable.

They gathered in the mouth of the cave. Dario lay propped against the wall, Wren kneeling beside him with the med kit, her hands steady, her face the color of paper. Ilsa stood watch at the entrance, rifle across her chest, watching the ridge. Tomo sat close to Yawane. And Rafael knelt beside the case with her father's blood-legacy in her hands and did the only math that mattered.

"Adeyemi will regroup," Ilsa said without turning around. "He lost men. That makes it personal now, and personal means he stops negotiating. He has maybe forty minutes while they stabilize their casualty, then he comes back with everything. We cannot hold this position. We cannot outrun them carrying a wounded man. And we cannot—" she finally turned, and her eyes went to the case, "—we cannot let that go downstream. Ever."

"The barge," Wren said quietly. "The fuel. If no one can carry it—"

"No one can carry it," Rafael said, "if no one leaves."

Silence took the cave. Even the birds seemed to lean in.

She laid the case open between them. The surveys caught the thin light—her father's drafting hand unmistakable in every line, the precise, patient lettering she'd fallen asleep watching as a child. Land titles. Clan territories marked in red where lines crossed lines that no clan had ever drawn or agreed to. Signatures. Stamps from the survey office. Voss's holding company threaded through every page like root system through soil.

"This was never a cache," she said. "This was testimony. He knew he was going to die, and he hid the evidence where only the taboo would protect it." Her throat closed and she pushed through it. "He trusted the river more than the courts. Twelve years I thought that meant he'd given up on justice. It didn't. It meant he'd figured out where justice actually lives."

"You're talking about giving it to them." Dario's voice was thin. "To the elders. You understand what that does. The debt dies. The leverage dies. Voss walks free of us forever—we walk away with nothing. No payment, no protection, no copy. They erase the debt and we erase ourselves."

"Yes."

"Rafa—"

"That was always the choice, Dario. From the diner onward. You just never told me it was on the table, because you wanted the other option to be the only one I could see." She looked at him, and found she wasn't angry anymore, which frightened her more than the anger had. "You were going to bring these pages home unopened and trade my father's truth for an office chair. I'm going to do what he actually asked."

"He didn't ask anything. There's no letter. There's no instruction—"

"There's the taboo," Tomo said softly.

Everyone turned. The young translator had barely spoken since the shooting stopped. Now he rose and stood beside Yawane, and something in his posture had changed since the gorge—some load-bearing wall inside him set in place.

"The clans swore off this place. Not because it's dangerous. Because it's *theirs*—because the ancestors' word is kept in the land itself. My grandmother told me: a document locked away is a seed in a drawer. It grows nothing. But a word spoken aloud before witnesses—" he glanced at Yawane, who nodded once, gravely, "—a word spoken aloud enters the living record. It cannot be bought, burned, buried, or subpoenaed. It becomes memory. And memory," he said, looking directly at Dario now, "is the one creditor nobody escapes."

"The condition stands," Yane said. "Whatever is carried from the source is spoken to the elders first. All of it. Before anyone goes anywhere. That was the passage sworn, and the passage holds even now, even with guns on the ridge." He looked at Rafael. "Your father knew our law better than your company did. That is why he came to the source and no further. That is why he is remembered here kindly, and elsewhere as a failure. Choose which remembering you want to be true."

Rafael looked down at the pages. Somewhere upslope, faint but closing, a radio crackled—Adeyemi's people, coordinating. Forty minutes, maybe less.

If she gave the documents to Voss, her debt died and her father's name stayed buried in an inquest report, filed under negligence, under *pilot error*, under the comfortable lies of men with stamps. If she kept copies, kept leverage, she'd spend the rest of her life as a hostage to it—hunted, hedged, owned in new ways, exactly as Ilsa had been owned, exactly as Dario had been owned. Leverage wasn't freedom. Leverage was just debt wearing a different shirt.

There was one way out, and it was the one nobody wanted, and standing in the cave where her father had made the same choice twelve years ago with a radio call dying in his throat, Rafael felt the decision arrive not as a collapse but as a clearing—as the moment after a storm passes and the light comes down clean and hard and shows you exactly what's real.

"We give them everything," she said. "Every page. Read aloud. Translated. Entered into the record. No copies leave this valley in anyone's pocket." She looked at Wren. "No exceptions."

Wren's jaw tightened—Rafael saw the geologist think of her photographs, her insurance, whatever she'd already hidden—and then, slowly, she nodded. Whatever Wren had done, she'd carry alone. Rafael chose not to know. Some mercies were also strategies.

"And us?" Ilsa asked. "We're stranded in the highlands with a hostile team upstream, no boats, no fuel, no leverage, and no payment. You understand what you're trading."

"For the first time in twelve years," Rafael said, "yes. Completely."

She lifted the case and carried it out of the cave, down to the flat stone, and knelt there in the open, deliberately visible to the ridge, and began placing the pages in order for the reading. Behind her she heard Dario groan as Wren helped him stand. Heard Tomo begin the long formal phrases of invitation to the elders. Heard Ilsa chamber a fresh magazine and take up position facing the mountain, guarding not a cache anymore but a ceremony.

Above the treeline, far off, a column of smoke was already rising where the barge lay anchored in the drying river—and Rafael had not yet given the order, had not yet chosen the fire—but she could see it coming, the burning of every road home, and she found she was smiling.

The first elder stepped into the light. Rafael opened her father's pages, and began to read.
