## 17. The Burning Barge

Wren Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning to read rock, and in all that time she had never once mistaken stone for anything else. Stone did not pretend. Stone did not promise and then withdraw the promise. So when she crested the last ridge above the sacred valley with the retreat party—six of them now, hollow-eyed, leech-bitten, carrying two men who could no longer walk unaided—and saw the smoke rising from the river bend below, her first thought was not *fire*. Her first thought was that the landscape itself had changed its mind about them.

Then the wind shifted and brought the smell: diesel and burning rubber and something sweeter underneath, the chemical reek of flares igniting in a fuel drum.

"The barge," said Manu, one of the two boatmen who had turned back at the gorge only to be caught by Adeyemi's team and marched upstream as porters. He said it the way a man says the name of a thing that has just died. "That's the barge."

Wren shaded her eyes. Below, wedged into the shallows where the upper river narrowed to something barely wider than a road, sat the Voss syndicate's fuel barge—a flat-bottomed steel hulk that had been winched, dragged, and bullied up the last navigable kilometers by men who believed that money made rivers negotiable. It had been their guarantee. Their way home. Their floating argument that no matter how deep they went into country that did not want them, there was always a machine waiting to carry them back out.

Rafael Montrose stood on its deck.

Even at this distance Wren recognized the set of her shoulders—the same posture she'd had on the Oklahoma plains, Wren imagined, though she'd never seen Rafael chase a storm. She'd only seen her stand still while weather came for her. That was the thing about Rafael that Wren had catalogued early and never revised: she didn't run toward danger or away from it. She stood in front of it like a woman taking a phone call she'd been expecting for years.

Rafael raised something in her hand. A flare gun. The orange arc of it was almost gentle against the green wall of the valley.

The first drum went up with a sound Wren felt in her sternum rather than heard—a soft, obscene *whump*—and then the fire found the main tank and the world below turned into a column of black smoke and orange light, and the heat of it reached them on the ridge a full three seconds later, pushing against their faces like a hand.

"God," whispered Priya Nair, the medic, beside her. "She's still on it."

"She's getting off," Wren said, because she had learned, over these weeks, to read Rafael Montrose the way she read strata: slowly, and then all at once. And indeed, a figure dropped from the barge's rail into the shallows, waded ashore through water lit gold by the burning fuel-slick, and stood there watching her own work with her hands loose at her sides.

Wren understood then—not fully, not yet, but in the way you understand an earthquake has happened before you understand what it has taken—that everything downstream of this moment had just been erased.

* * *

They came down into the valley in single file, and the fire's noise swallowed their footsteps. The retreat party had separated from the main group two days ago when the vote split the crew—those who would go back down to the boats and those who would follow Rafael to the source—and Wren had chosen to go back, not out of cowardice, though she'd interrogated herself about that long enough to know the answer wasn't clean either. She'd gone back because someone had to get the injured men out, and because she had photographs in a waterproof pouch against her spine that she did not intend to die for but also did not intend to surrender, and because part of her—the geologist part, the part that believed in evidence over sentiment—had thought the smart play was extraction.

Now she watched the smart play burn.

Adeyemi's team was already at the riverbank when they arrived. Four men and a woman, armed, sunburned to the color of old brick, standing in a ragged line watching the barge die. Mr. Adeyemi himself stood slightly apart, the way he always did, a tall man in a field shirt too clean for the country, holding a satellite phone he was no longer bothering to speak into. There was no signal here anyway. Wren had established that days ago, mapping the radio shadows of the valley the way she mapped everything, compulsively, because maps were how she kept fear at a manageable scale.

"You," Adeyemi said, when he saw them. His voice carried flatly over the fire's roar. "Where is the case?"

Wren felt every eye on the ridge behind her, on the path above, on the treeline where Yawane's people moved the way light moves through canopy—present everywhere, visible nowhere. She thought about lying. She discovered, with mild academic interest, that she didn't have to decide; her mouth simply said, "Upstream. With the elders. Being read aloud right now, probably."

Something crossed Adeyemi's face that was not anger. Anger would have been simpler. What crossed it was recalculation—the look of a man whose spreadsheet has just developed a hole in the bottom row.

"Do you understand what you've done?" he said, turning back to the fire. The barge's hull groaned, a long metallic complaint, and settled deeper into the shallows. Burning diesel sheeted across the water surface and burned there too, rings of flame expanding and dying on the current. "That vessel represents—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "There are no roads here. No airstrip within ninety kilometers. The dry season ends in five weeks. After that, this valley floods, and nothing moves until April."

"I can do the arithmetic," Wren said. "I'm a geologist."

"Then do it." He looked at her, and for one unguarded moment Wren saw what lived under the enforcer: a man who had also done the arithmetic, and had gotten a number he didn't like. "Nobody leaves."

Behind him, one of his mercenaries spat into the burning water and said something in Afrikaans that needed no translation.

* * *

Rafael came up the bank while the barge was still burning, and the retreat party gathered around her the way people gather around a well—not sure whether to drink or fall in.

Wren watched her closely. She had expected triumph, or mania, or the flat shock of a person who has done something irreversible and hasn't caught up to it yet. What she saw instead was worse and better: calm. A terrible, laundered calm, the calm of someone who has finally paid a bill she'd been dodging for twelve years and finds the silence afterward almost unbearable in its cleanliness.

"You stranded us," said Manu. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way you'd state the weather.

"I stranded everyone," Rafael said. "Including me. Including them." She nodded at Adeyemi's team. "That was the point. As long as any boat could go downstream, the documents were cargo. Something that could be taken, bought, intercepted, sold. I've watched this crew almost tear itself apart over who carries them out." She looked at Wren, and Wren held her gaze, and neither of them mentioned the photographs. "I decided nobody carries them out. They walk out on their own feet, after the elders have heard them, or they don't walk out at all."

"And if the elders decide the documents should burn too?" Priya asked. "You've seen how they treat things from the source. Erasure goes both ways."

"Then they burn," Rafael said simply. "It was never mine. It was my father's, and before that it belonged to the land it describes. I'm just the courier who finally read the package."

Wren heard it then, under the words: not conviction. Relief. Rafael Montrose had spent the entire expedition—Wren had watched it happen, day by day, the way you watch a river undercut a bank—carrying two cargoes. The case, and the debt. And she had just discovered they weighed the same, and that fire solved for both.

"You realize," Wren said, stepping forward, keeping her voice level, "that Adeyemi's people are going to spend the next five weeks trying to take those documents by force. You haven't ended the fight. You've just removed the exits so the fight has nowhere else to happen."

"Yes," Rafael said.

"That's your plan? *Yes?*"

"My plan," Rafael said, and here something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, "is that we're four days' walk from the source, and they're four days' walk from the source, and between us and both is Yawane's country, and Yawane's country does not sell passage. Not to them. Not for triple pay. Not for anything." She looked past Wren at the burning barge, at the mercenaries' furious faces, at the smoke climbing into the valley air like a black river running upward. "I grew up around storms. You want to know the first thing my father taught me? You don't outrun a storm. You get small, you get quiet, and you let it pass over. We're getting small."

"And when it passes?"

"Then we walk down," Rafael said. "The long way. On foot. Like the river does."

* * *

They made camp that night upstream of the wreck, on a gravel bar beneath a stand of pandanus, because the smell of the burning hull followed the wind downstream and no one wanted to sleep inside it. The fire on the water burned until nearly midnight, a broken orange constellation reflected in the current, and then the river carried the last of the fuel away in flaming ribbons and the dark came back honest.

Wren couldn't sleep. She lay on her groundsheet listening to the expedition breathe around her—Priya checking dressings by penlight, the injured boatmen's uneven breathing, somewhere in the dark the low murmur of Tomo talking with Yawane's scouts in a language that sounded less like speech than like water moving over stones—and she took out the waterproof pouch and, by feel alone, confirmed the photographs were still there.

Forty-one exposures. The land-title surveys. The correspondence between Voss Holdings and the National Survey Office. The page of her father's journal that named Dario Kell. She had shot them in the cave at the source with a camera she'd told no one about, in the thirty minutes Rafael had been reading the original journal with shaking hands, and she had told herself it was archival habit, scientific redundancy, insurance against loss. All true. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth was that Wren Okonkwo had been disgraced once—for a falsified data set she hadn't falsified, a scapegoating she'd never successfully disproven—and she had sworn, in the ash-gray years afterward, that she would never again leave the only copy of the truth in someone else's hands.

So she had made copies. And now the originals were walking upstream to be sung into a clan's living memory, and the copies were pressed against her spine, and the only vessel capable of carrying either of them downstream was a gutted hulk of burning steel settling into the mud of the Fly River.

She should have felt trapped. That was the strange part. By every rational measure she was trapped—no comms, no vehicle, five weeks of dry season left, an armed syndicate team camped two hundred meters downstream, and a body already running a low fever from a cut on her shin that Priya had cleaned twice. Trapped was the correct word. Every map she'd ever drawn said so.

Instead, lying on the gravel with the last glow of the barge fading off the water, Wren felt something she hadn't felt since before the disgrace: weightless. Not free—free was a word for people with options—but *unburdened*, in the specific way of a person who has stopped pretending there's a version of events in which she gets to control the outcome. The photographs would move or they wouldn't. The truth would survive or it wouldn't. What she could do—all any of them could do now—was walk, and testify, and stay alive long enough for the story to matter.

Across the fire, Rafael sat awake, knees drawn up, watching the dark water. Wren got up, quietly, and went and sat beside her.

"For the record," Wren said, "from a purely logistical standpoint, what you did today was insane."

"Noted for the record."

"But from a geological standpoint"—Wren pulled her knees up to match Rafael's, mirroring without meaning to—"it was the only sound decision available. You removed the unstable material before it could fail catastrophically. Controlled demolition." She paused. "I photograph things. Before demolition. Professional habit."

Rafael turned her head. In the ember-light her face was unreadable for a moment, and then it wasn't, and Wren understood that Rafael had known—had probably known since the cave, since some fractional hesitation of Wren's hands, some tell invisible to anyone who hadn't spent weeks learning to read her crew the way she read weather—and had said nothing, and was saying nothing now.

"Habit," Rafael said.

"Habit," Wren agreed.

Neither of them said anything else. Downstream, the barge's superstructure finally gave way with a sound like a sigh, and the sparks went up in a slow spiral, and the river took them.

* * *

Morning brought the fury.

Adeyemi's team came up the gravel bar at first light, weapons slung but hands near them, and the retreat party came awake all along the bank in a rustle of tarps and held breath. But Adeyemi didn't come for violence. He came, Wren realized as he stopped at the cold ring of last night's fire, because he had run out of better options and knew it.

"There's a patrol boat chartered out of Kiunga," he said, to Rafael, to all of them, his voice stripped of everything except logistics. "It checks the lower gorge every ten days during dry season. If we reach the gorge before the rains, we live. If we miss it, we walk to Kiunga, which is nine days through flooded country, assuming the clans let us pass, which they won't." He looked at the blackened hulk in the shallows, at the oil sheen still rainbowing the eddies. "You've killed the easy way out for everyone. I hope whatever you bought with it was worth the price."

"It was," Rafael said.

Adeyemi studied her for a long moment. Then he said, quietly enough that only their circle heard it: "My orders were to recover the documents and eliminate the witnesses. Do you understand what happens to my team when I report that the documents are being sung to a clan council in a valley with no signal coverage? We become the witnesses, Montrose. You didn't strand us. You converted us." He turned away, then stopped. "Your father hid those surveys for twelve years knowing exactly this would happen to whoever found them. I used to think that made him cruel." He walked back down the bar toward his own camp, and over his shoulder: "I'm starting to think it made him the only honest man on the river."

Wren watched him go, and felt the ground shift under the whole expedition—not literally, though she was a geologist and the ground was always shifting somewhere, but in the human tectonics of it. Adeyemi had just told them, in the language of logistics, that his team might need allies before the dry season ended. And allies meant negotiation, and negotiation meant the documents' fate was not yet sealed, and sealed was the only state in which Rafael's gamble was safe.

Above the valley, on the ridgelines, Yawane's people had begun to move. Wren saw them now clearly for the first time in daylight: figures descending the trails in single file, unhurried, carrying nothing that looked like luggage and everything that looked like purpose. Coming down toward the camps. Both camps.

Tomo stood at the edge of the gravel bar, watching them come, and called out softly to Rafael across the morning:

"They're coming for the reading. Everyone's coming for the reading."
