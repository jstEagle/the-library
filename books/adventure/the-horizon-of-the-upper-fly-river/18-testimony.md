## 18. Testimony

*Tomo*

The elders came at first light, as Yawane had promised, and they came the way Tomo's grandmother had once described a thing coming: not walking so much as arriving, as if the valley itself had decided to gather them out of the mist. There were eleven of them. Yawane stood apart from them, slightly downstream, because he had made the promise and therefore could not sit among those who would judge whether it was kept.

They arranged themselves on flat stones above the water where the source broke free of the mountain—a seep of cold, glass-bright water that rose without sound, without ceremony, as though it had always been there and simply tolerated being found. The elders did not look at Rafael or Dario or Ilsa or Wren. They looked at the case.

It sat between Rafael's feet like something wounded.

Tomo translated the first question before it was fully spoken, feeling the words move through him in both languages at once, the way his grandmother's voice used to move through the house in the hour before dawn.

*Who carries this?*

"Rafael Montrose," Tomo said, and then, because the question had not really been about names, "daughter of Elias Montrose, who carried it here first."

Something shifted among the stones—breath, attention, the small rearrangement of bodies that is the oldest form of comment. One of the elders, a woman older than the mission church in Kiunga, older perhaps than the survey office that had signed away her mother's garden, leaned forward and studied Rafael's face with the unhurried thoroughness of someone reading weather.

*She has his jaw,* the elder said. *The one who died. We remember the jaw.*

Tomo felt the sentence go through him like a fishhook. He translated it, and watched Rafael receive it, and saw her hand close on the handle of the case until the knuckles went pale.

"They said—" Rafael began, and stopped, and started again in a voice scraped thin by the trek. "They told Australia my father caused it. Four dead. His fault. They put it in a report."

The eldest elder nodded slowly, not in agreement but in receipt. Then he spoke for a long time, and what he said was this:

*A paper can be burned. A court can be paid. But a song, once it enters the water, travels to every village the river touches, forever, and no one can buy back a song.*

Tomo translated. Around him the crew was very still. Even Ilsa, whose stillness usually meant calculation, was still in some other way entirely.

"The documents," Rafael said. "You want me to read them. All of them."

*Every word,* the elder said. *In your tongue. And the young man will give them ours. That is how a thing becomes true in two worlds at once. That is how it cannot be taken back.*

Rafael looked at Tomo. What was in her face was not fear exactly. It was the expression of a woman standing at the edge of a river she had been circling her whole life.

"Then we'd better start," she said, and opened the case.

* * *

*Rafael*

Her father's handwriting had not changed in twelve years. That was the thing that undid her—not the fraud, not the maps with their careful colored overlays showing parcels of clan land sold twice and three times over to companies that existed only on letterhead, but the handwriting itself. The same backward-leaning script. The same habit of pressing hard enough to dent three pages down. She had grown up under that pressure; she could feel it now through her fingertips, twelve years later, coming up out of the paper like heat off stone.

She read aloud.

The morning sun climbed the eastern wall of the valley while she read, and the light moved across the documents the way light moves across water, and she read until her throat cracked and Wren brought her water from the source itself, cupped in a leaf, and she drank and kept reading.

Parcel 44-Kiunga East. Sold by the Office of Lands to Voss Consolidated Holdings, 11 March, thirteen years ago. Not ceded. Never ceded. No clan signature anywhere on the instrument, only a thumbprint attributed to a man who, the survey notes established with her father's merciless precision, had been dead four months when the ink dried.

Parcel 52. Parcel 60. The upper terraces. The sago stands. The burial grounds—the burial grounds most of all, entered into the record as *vacant marginal land*, eleven hectares, purchased for the price of a used truck.

And beside each parcel, in her father's hand, the annotation that had killed him: *No consent obtained. No compensation paid. Title fraudulent. Originals lodged—*

The line ended there. Lodged where, he had never written. Perhaps he had understood that even a hidden case could be found if the hiding place were written down.

When she reached the journal—the last notebook, the one with the swollen cover and the pages stiff with damp—her voice failed her for the first time. The elders waited. They were very good at waiting. Waiting, Tomo murmured to her during the pause, was half of what an elder was.

She read the final entry.

*"Kell came to me in Moresby. He says the route is compromised, says we should run the western fork instead of the gorge. He was sweating. Dario doesn't sweat unless someone has made him afraid, and nothing in this country makes Dario afraid except a man with more money than him. If anything happens to this expedition, let the record show: Dario Kell knew our route before we chose it. Let the record show who he sold it to. Let the record show."*

The valley held its breath.

Dario sat ten meters away on a fallen trunk, and Rafael read the words about him into the air between them, and did not stop, and did not soften them, and did not look at him until the entry ended. When she finally did look, he was watching the water. His face had gone the color of the clay banks downstream. He did not deny it. Denial, she understood suddenly, was a currency he had already spent.

*Let the record show,* said the eldest elder quietly, when Tomo finished translating, and there was something terrible and gentle in the way he repeated it, the way you might repeat a child's last words. *We have shown. Now we will keep.*

* * *

*Wren*

Wren had spent twenty years believing that data wanted to be free and that people mostly didn't, and she had built a career on the gap between those two facts. Sitting on the cold stone with the source-water running past her boots, she watched a third possibility assemble itself in front of her, clause by clause, song by song, and it rearranged something fundamental in her professional skeleton.

Because they weren't filing it. That was the thing. There was no registry, no stamp, no certified copy, no chain of custody that any auditor would recognize—and yet the documents were becoming more permanent with every hour, not less. An old man with a voice like river gravel took Parcel 52 and turned it into a chant, four lines long, and four younger men repeated it back until it was seamless, and Wren realized with a chill that moved up her spine vertebra by vertebra that the chant contained the coordinates. Not the numbers—the substance. Boundaries named by trees and stones and bends in the water, the way her own discipline had worked for ten thousand years before anyone invented the theodolite.

The clan had been running its own survey office the entire time. It simply recorded in memory instead of ink.

*Memory doesn't burn,* Yawane said, catching her stare. He had drifted near during the reading, and he answered questions nobody asked him, which Wren was learning was his way of being kind. *Paper burns. Ink fades. A grandmother forgets nothing she has decided to keep.*

"You could have taught the whole profession something," Wren said.

Yawane smiled with half his mouth. *Your profession taught us something too,* he said. *It taught us what a lie looks like when it wears a signature.*

By midday the reading had become something else. The elders had begun weaving the documents into longer forms—call-and-response, verses that rhymed in ways Tomo said were untranslatable, a woman's high keening part that held the name of the dead man with the thumbprint, the one who had been dead four months when his consent was manufactured. Each time his name came around, the keening bent downward, and Wren, who had attended exactly two funerals in her life and left both early, found she could not have left this one if the valley caught fire.

That was when Rafael closed the journal and stood, and said, in English, in the flat voice of a person spending their last reserve:

"There's one more thing. The grave."

* * *

*Dario*

He had been waiting for it since the journal. Some part of him had been waiting for it for twelve years, since the night in Port Moresby when Adeyemi's predecessor slid an envelope across a bar table and Dario, thirty-one years old and hungry in a way he mistook for ambition, told a stranger which fork of the river Elias Montrose would take.

He had told himself a hundred stories since. That the information wouldn't be used. That Voss only wanted to scare the old man off the claim. That Montrose Sr. was reckless anyway and would have died in that country regardless of what Dario whispered to whom. He had told the stories so often they had worn smooth as river stones, and now, sitting in the sacred valley with the truth chanted around him in a language he didn't speak, every one of those stones turned over and showed its pale underside.

The grave lay two hundred meters below the cave, under a screen of pandanus, marked by nothing. Rafael led them to it. She had found it days ago, on the trek in—Wren's chapter of the story, though everyone present had been there—but she had waited, Dario understood now, waited until the documents were in the keeping of the living record, because a grave belonged to testimony, not to evidence.

"The official record says five men went in," Rafael said, standing at the head of it. "Four died. One walked out—Peter Olembe, the surveyor's assistant. He gave the inquest its central testimony. He blamed my father. His statement closed the case." She looked down. "This grave is too short for my father. Too narrow. And the camp we found had six sets of eating things."

Nobody spoke. The elders had risen and come down silently, and now stood in a crescent behind the crew, and Dario felt their presence the way you feel a change in barometric pressure.

"Olembe never walked out," Rafael said. "Olembe saw everything. Olembe was the witness who could have broken the fraud wide open twelve years ago, and somebody silenced him here, and wrote his mouth into the record instead." Her voice finally fractured. "He's been lying in this country for twelve years while his testimony hung my father."

The eldest elder stepped forward. He looked at the grave for a long time, then at Tomo, and spoke, and Tomo's translation came out slow, weighted, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*The man who lies under foreign earth without a name is the loneliest of all the dead. Today he is lonely no longer. Peter Olembe. Son of a mother in the Sepik country, whom we will send word to, by drum and by traveler, whatever the season. Today he has witnesses. Today his testimony is kept in the water.*

They sang him into the ground. That was the only way Dario could describe it afterward, though he described it to no one. They sang for hours, and the singing had structure the way a bridge has structure, and somewhere in the middle of it Rafael knelt at the head of the grave and pressed her palm flat against the earth and stayed there through four full cycles of the verse, and Dario, who had spent his life calculating exits, discovered there was no exit from a thing like this, and did not want one.

When the singing ended, the eldest elder turned—not to Rafael, but to him.

*And now,* the old man said. *The one the paper calls Kell.*

Dario stood up. His legs felt borrowed.

*The record shows what you did,* the elder said. *The record also shows that you took a bullet at the source, standing between guns and the case.* He paused, and the pause had weight, deliberation, mercy and judgment balanced in it like scales. *Among us, these things do not cancel. They are carried together, like two loads on one pole. You will walk out through our territory, and you will carry both loads, and when we reach the place of judgment, the elders of the lower villages will weigh them. Not you. Not the papers of your government. Us.*

"I understand," Dario said, and found, to his astonishment, that he meant it—that some sealed chamber inside him had opened for the first time in twelve years, and the air coming out of it tasted like relief.

Rafael was looking at him. Not with forgiveness—he hadn't earned that and knew the arithmetic of it better than anyone alive. With acknowledgment. Two loads on one pole.

* * *

*Tomo*

It ended at dusk, the way his grandmother said all true ceremonies end—not with a conclusion but with a dispersal, the way a flock decides all at once to become individual birds again.

The last verse was given to the youngest elder, a man barely older than Tomo, who sang the name *Montrose* awkwardly at first and then cleanly, and then the whole circle took it up, and Tomo heard his father's name enter the song of a country his father had never seen, and had to turn his face toward the water so no one would see what his face was doing.

Elias Montrose, who measured rivers. Who was wrong about many things, the elders noted without rancor—a foreigner's errors, errors of map and assumption—but who had written the truth down at the cost of his life, and truth written at cost was the only kind worth keeping.

*It is done,* Yawane said, coming to stand beside Tomo as the fire was lit below, the first fire of the mourning-feast, the fire that would burn three days. *The water has them now. Every village downstream will know within a season. There are women on the far tributaries who make it their work to carry songs, and they are faster than your motorboats.*

"Is it really over?" Tomo asked. "For them—for us?"

Yawane considered the fire, the mountains going blue above it, the strangers sitting among the elders drinking smoked tea with the awkwardness of guests who had arrived as trespassers and would leave as something else.

*Over?* Yawane said. *Nothing with the river is over. But tonight it flows the right direction.*

Below them, Rafael sat alone at the edge of the firelight with her father's journal open on her knees, no longer reading it, just holding it, one hand resting on the cover the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal. Tomo watched her for a moment, and understood that she was listening—to the songs still rising from the elders' circle, the ones that would now travel the length of the Fly River and out past the delta into the salt water and the world.

Then Rafael lifted her head, and looked upstream, into the dark where the river came down out of the country of its beginning, and Tomo saw her shoulders change—saw twelve years leave them all at once, like a load set down on stone.

She stood. She picked up the journal. And she walked toward the fire to ask Yawane the question Tomo had been dreading and hoping for all day:

"How far is it, on foot, to the coast?"
