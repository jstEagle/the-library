## 19. The Long Way Down

The first morning out of the valley, Rafael woke to the smell of woodsmoke and taro and realized she could not remember the last time she had slept without one hand near her pack.

It was a small thing. It should have been a small thing. But for twelve years her body had kept a ledger of its own—every motel room door double-locked, every truck cab scanned for tails, every stranger's approach measured in escape routes—and now here she was, lying on a woven mat in a clan longhouse in country no map acknowledged, and her hands were empty and open at her sides like a child's.

She lay still a while, listening to the rain on the sago-thatch roof, and let the strangeness settle over her.

Yawane's people moved around the fire already. A woman whose name Rafael had learned as Serei was grinding something aromatic against a flat stone; two boys were arguing in whispers about a fishing net; an old man sat by the doorway sharpening a bush knife with the patience of someone who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. Nobody looked at her the way people in the lowlands had looked at her—no calculation, no appraisal of what she carried or owed. She was a guest under Yawane's word. That was all. That was everything.

Dario was not there.

He slept—or didn't—in the men's house at the far end of the compound, apart, the way you keep a man who is waiting to be judged. The bullet had gone through the meat below his shoulder blade, missing everything that mattered by a finger's width, and he'd walked the first three days on willpower and Tomo's boiled analgesics before Yawane's people made him a litter of poles and vine and carried him without complaint or conversation. He had said almost nothing since the cave. Once, crossing a ridge above a river the color of strong tea, he had tried: "Rafael." Just her name, weighted with everything he wanted to say after it. She had kept walking. There would be time enough for whatever he had to say when the elders decided whether any of it was worth hearing.

Ilsa had lasted four days.

They had come down off the high shoulder of the divide into a valley of pandanus and mist, and at the fork where the trail split—one branch dropping toward the coast road, the other climbing back into country that belonged to no government Rafael had ever heard of—Ilsa had simply stopped, set down her rifle, and looked at it for a long moment the way a woman looks at a tool she has finally finished using.

"You're not coming?" Tomo had asked.

"I'm not going where you're going," Ilsa said. "And I'm not going where Adeyemi went." She had unclipped the radio handset from her belt—the one Rafael knew she'd used to call in their position, the betrayal they had never spoken aloud but both remembered—and dropped it into the mud. Then she'd crushed it under her heel, twice, until the casing cracked and the green light died. "Nobody's tracking anybody anymore."

"Where will you go?" Tomo asked.

Ilsa had looked up into the misted ridgelines with an expression Rafael had never seen on her face—not the flat professional calm, not the cold arithmetic, but something almost young. "People live out here," she said. "Somebody always needs a strong back who can shoot. Or maybe nobody does, and I learn to fish." She'd picked up her pack, adjusted the straps once, and added, without turning around: "Montrose. You burned the barge. I've been trying to work out if that was the stupidest thing I ever saw or the smartest." A pause. "Still working on it."

Then she walked into the bush, and the bush closed behind her, and that was the whole of it. No debt settled, none outstanding. Owing nothing to anyone. Rafael understood, watching the place where she'd vanished, that Ilsa had chosen the only exit she'd ever believed in—one that ran through wilderness instead of paperwork.

* * *

They walked for nineteen days.

That was how Yawane's nephew, a wiry, laughing guide named Kombu, counted it—not in kilometers, which meant nothing on trails that switchbacked through cloud forest and cane grass, but in nights. Nineteen nights of sleeping in longhouses and hunting shelters and, twice, in caves whose walls were scored with handprints older than any nation. Nineteen mornings of waking to rain or birdsong or, once, to the deep resonant boom of a slit-gong sounding from across a valley—a message traveling village to village faster than any radio, announcing them ahead, smoothing their passage through territories that had been at war with each other within living memory.

"They are saying who you are," Tomo explained that morning, translating Kombu's rapid Tok Ples with a wonder he wasn't bothering to hide. "Not your names. They don't know your names. They're saying: these are the ones who carried the ancestors' words out of the source and gave them to the elders. These are the ones who burned their own boat so the words couldn't be stolen back." He glanced at Rafael, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—something like awe, carefully contained. "You're famous here. In a way that matters."

"I destroyed eighty thousand kina of equipment and stranded nine people," Rafael said. "Including myself."

"Yes," Tomo said, grinning now. "That's the part they like."

She laughed. It startled her—the sound coming out of her chest unbidden, rusty as an engine turned over after years in a barn. Kombu laughed too, though he couldn't have understood more than a quarter of the exchange, and the old man with the bush knife looked up from his sharpening and smiled at nothing in particular.

The country itself did its slow work on her. She had spent her life in landscapes built for speed—prairies where a storm you could see forty miles off would be on you in an hour, roads straight as arguments, horizons that offered themselves completely and held nothing back. This land was the opposite of legible. Every ridge concealed another ridge. Rivers appeared and vanished under limestone. The trails followed logic older than sightlines: here a path bent around a stand of trees because the trees were named ancestors; there it climbed unnecessarily because the low route belonged to a clan whose permission had not been asked. You could not conquer this landscape or even fully know it. You could only be vouched for in it, step by step, person by person.

And slowly, without deciding to, she stopped measuring.

It happened so gradually she only caught it in retrospect, somewhere around the twelfth night, sitting on a ridge in the blue hour with her boots off and her feet blistered beyond dignity, when she realized she had not once thought about the odometer. For twelve years her mind had run like a taxi meter—miles to the next town, miles to the next job, dollars per mile, interest compounding quietly in the background of every thought. Voss had owned that meter. Debt had owned it. Even before Voss, some restlessness had owned it, the conviction that wherever she was, the thing she needed was somewhere else, down the road, over the horizon.

Now she sat on a ridge in the middle of nowhere, owing money to no one, required by no schedule, escorted through country where her name meant nothing and her actions meant everything, and the horizon could do whatever it liked. She was not going anywhere except down, at walking pace, in good time.

Her father, she thought, would have understood this instantly. All those years she'd resented the expeditions, the absences, the man who loved rivers more than parent-teacher conferences—and the whole time he had been trying to teach her the one lesson she'd refused to learn: that a river knows where it's going, and the trick of a life is finding out whether you do.

* * *

On the sixteenth night they reached the village of Tomo's grandmother.

Rafael saw it hit him from fifty yards out—an old woman standing at the edge of the sweet-potato gardens, small and straight-backed, and Tomo making a sound that wasn't quite a word and breaking into a run. What followed was loud and tearful and conducted half in Tok Ples and half in an embrace that needed no translation. Rafael stood back with Kombu and watched, and found her eyes burning and could not entirely blame the woodsmoke.

Later, by the fire, the grandmother took Rafael's hands in hers—hands like tree bark, strong as cable—and studied her face for a long time. Then she spoke, and Tomo translated, his voice not quite steady.

"She says she remembers the surveyors. Twelve years ago. Before your father's expedition. Men with instruments who measured the ground and told the clans the papers they signed were about a road, when really they were about selling the land itself." Her grip tightened. "She says when your father came upstream after that, asking questions, the elders argued about whether to warn him or turn him back. Some wanted to warn him. The ones who wanted to turn him back won the argument." Her voice dropped. "She says they have carried the weight of losing that argument for twelve years. And she says—" Tomo stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She says your father's name is spoken now in the songs, and the argument is over, and the weight is lifted. And she says to tell you: it was never yours to carry."

Rafael nodded, because speech wasn't available.

Twelve years she had hauled that grief like ballast—her father dead, three others dead, the inquest's cold verdict that Edmund Montrose had pushed his crew past every safe limit chasing glory, the name dragged through hearings while the family name curdled into cautionary tale. She had built a whole life as a fugitive from that verdict, running roads, running debts, running anything that might catch her and make her sit still long enough to feel it.

And it had never been hers. It had been theirs—the liars', the signers', the men in the Port Moresby offices who had sold ground that was never ceded and buried the one honest witness in an unmarked grave. Her father had died carrying the truth upstream, and the truth had waited in a cave like a seed, and all she had done—all anyone could do—was carry it the last few kilometers and give it to people whose whole civilization was built for keeping things alive.

* * *

They came down to the coast road country on the twenty-third day, where the bush thinned and the first power line sagged across a clearing like a stray thread from another world, and Kombu's people began the formalities of handing them over—with speeches, with gifts, with a ceremony at the boundary stream where Yawane himself had traveled down to meet them.

The old man looked thinner than in the mountains, Rafael thought, but his eyes were the same: unhurried, bottomless, seeing considerably more than he said. He addressed each of them in turn. To Tomo, warm words and a carved hook for his grandmother. To Wren—who had come down with the retreat party and met them on the trail, still apologizing for having voted to turn back—to Rafael's surprise, a blessing of safe travel delivered in careful English.

To Dario, carried on the litter now mostly as formality, he said little. What he said, Tomo translated in a flat voice: *The elders will hear you when your wound is closed. You will speak, and they will decide what is remembered.* Dario received it the way a man receives a sentencing date—pale, upright, and, Rafael thought with something that surprised her, not entirely unwilling. Whatever ambition had driven him to sell her father's route to Voss twelve years ago and take Voss's directorship bribe this year, the cave had stripped it down to the studs. She had watched him read her father's journal entry naming him, and she had watched the exact moment the future he'd purchased evaporated. What was left was a man who would stand before elders whose justice operated outside every court he'd ever gamed, and answer for himself with nothing between him and their judgment but the truth. Maybe that was the only punishment that had ever scared him. Maybe it was the only kind that worked.

Then Yawane came to Rafael last.

He took her hand and held it in both of his and spoke for a long time in Tok Ples, and Tomo translated in pieces, struggling slightly:

"He says: your father came to the source carrying questions, and the questions killed him, and for twelve years we thought the land had swallowed him whole." Pause. "He says: you came carrying his answers, and you gave them away freely, and you burned your own boat so no one could buy them back. He says the clans have a saying—that a debt paid in money is forgotten by morning, but a debt paid in truth is remembered by grandchildren." His voice wavered. "He says you owe us nothing. He says we owe you. And he says—" Tomo faltered, then steadied. "He says go home, daughter of the river-measurer. Go home and stay somewhere."

* * *

They camped that night at the boundary, on the last ground the clans controlled, and tomorrow the world of roads and courts and newspapers would take them back.

Rafael sat apart at the fire, turning it all over, and found that the ledger in her head—the odometer, the meter, the running total she'd carried since she was twenty-six years old—was quiet. Voss would come for her, surely; syndicates did not forgive burned barges and vanished documents, and Mr. Adeyemi's employers had long memories and longer reach. The thought surfaced, tested its weight, and found nothing to hold on to. Let them come. There was nothing left to take that she couldn't survive losing. The documents were in the elders' keeping, sung into memory in a hundred heads that no subpoena could serve and no fire could burn. Her father's name was restored. The grave on the mountain had its name now, and mourning rites, and grandchildren who would speak it. The debt was paid—not discharged, *paid*, in the only currency that had ever really mattered.

For the first time in twelve years, Rafael Montrose owed nothing to anyone.

She was still sitting with the enormity of that—still learning how to breathe inside it—when Wren came around the fire and crouched beside her, glancing back toward the longhouse to make sure no one else was listening. The geologist looked different out here. Steadier. Like a woman who had spent three weeks doing arithmetic she couldn't undo and had finally made peace with the sum.

"Rafael," she said quietly. "Before we cross that stream tomorrow, there's something you need to know. About the cache." She hesitated, and Rafael felt the old dread stir, the reflex of a woman who has learned that good news is usually the load-bearing lie in a bad structure.

"The night before the burning, at the barge," Wren said. "While everyone was arguing about fuel rations—I was alone with the case for about four minutes." She reached into her shirt pocket and drew out a slim black rectangle, holding it cupped in her palm like a coal that might still be alive. "The case was sealed, but the journal wasn't. Your father's journal. I photographed pages. Key pages—the title surveys, the correspondence, the signatures." Her eyes came up, steady and unapologetic and afraid all at once. "The elders have the truth, and no court will ever touch it. But Voss has lawyers, and lawyers have shredders, and songs don't cross-examine. If the story dies in the highlands, it dies forever." She turned the camera over so the screen faced Rafael, dark and waiting. "So the question is—what do we do with the only copy in the world that speaks English?"
