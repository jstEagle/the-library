## 20. Copies

The coast arrived the way truth does—gradually, then all at once.

Wren Okonkwo smelled it before she saw it: salt and rot and diesel, the brackish breath of a delta giving itself up to the sea. For nineteen days they had walked down out of the highlands, following Yawane's people along paths that did not appear on any map she'd ever digitized, and now the trees opened like a curtain and there it was—the Gulf of Papua, flat and pewter-colored under a sky that couldn't decide between rain and mercy.

Her boots had given up somewhere around day eleven. Her feet had not. She stood at the tree line with her pack straps cutting grooves into her shoulders and let herself feel, for one full minute, the astonishing animal fact of being alive. Then she took out her notebook and wrote the coordinates anyway, because she was who she was.

"You're doing it again," Tomo said beside her.

"Doing what?"

"Measuring paradise."

Wren closed the notebook. "Habit. The land doesn't exist until you've measured it. That's what I believed for twenty years." She looked out at the water, at the fishing canoes stitched across the shallows like punctuation marks. "Turns out the land existed just fine without me."

Behind them, the escort party waited with the patient stillness of people who had never once been in a hurry in their lives. Yawane's nephew—a boy of maybe sixteen named Sereva who had appointed himself Wren's shadow for the entire descent—tugged her sleeve and pointed downriver, where a mission boat was moored at a stilted jetty, its hull painted the faded blue of a sky remembered rather than seen.

"Kerema," Sereva said. "Two days by water. There is a radio. There is a road, sometimes."

There is a road, sometimes. Wren thought that would make an excellent epitaph for the entire country's infrastructure budget, but she only nodded and shouldered her pack again.

* * *

Kerema was a town built on stilts over mud, all corrugated iron and rain barrels and the perpetual amber light of a place where the sun came up over water and went down over water and never quite committed to either. They arrived on a Thursday—or Wren believed it was a Thursday; the days had gone soft and interchangeable somewhere in the headwater country—and the mission station gave them beds with actual mattresses, which felt decadent enough to be slightly obscene.

Rafael Montrose slept for fourteen hours. Dario Kell, whose shoulder wound had been sewn shut in the field with fishing line and whose fever had broken two days out from Kerema, slept longer. Ilsa was gone—had simply walked into the bush on the second morning of the descent without farewell, leaving her rifle wrapped in banana leaves as a parting gift to Sereva, which told Wren everything about how Ilsa processed gratitude: as a debt to be discharged quietly and immediately.

Tomo sat on the mission veranda translating documents.

That was the thing Wren kept coming back to, watching him through the window while she pretended to inventory their remaining supplies. The boy—he was twenty-three, barely older than her graduate students had been, back when she'd had graduate students and a career and a name that appeared on things—sat cross-legged with the photocopied pages Rafael had carried out of the cave, reading them aloud in Rotokas and then again in Tok Pisin, his lips moving, his brow furrowed, occasionally stopping to ask Yawane's people a question about a word that had no equivalent.

He wasn't translating for the crew anymore. There was no crew. He was translating for the record—for the elders' living archive, for whatever ceremony had been performed at the source, for the songs into which the clan's memory-keepers were folding her father's surveys line by line. Wren had heard fragments of it on the walk down: old men chanting coordinates. It had sounded, at first, like nonsense. Then she'd understood. The numbers were becoming geography. The fraud was becoming story. And a story, unlike a filing cabinet, could not be subpoenaed, purchased, or burned.

Which raised the question that had been sitting in Wren's chest since the night of the fire, heavy as a swallowed stone.

She had photographed the pages.

* * *

It had happened at the source, in the forty minutes of chaos between finding the cache and hearing Adeyemi's engines echo up the valley—and Wren had told no one. Not Rafael, whose decision to burn everything had been made with the clean ferocity of someone amputating her own escape route. Not Dario, who lay feverish and untrusted. Not even Tomo, whose faith in her was the first uncomplicated thing Wren had been given in years, and whom she therefore could not bear to complicate.

Here was what she knew about herself, and had known since a Tuesday in Brisbane nine years ago when a mining company's lawyers had slid a settlement agreement across a table and she had signed it because she was tired and broke and afraid: Wren Okonkwo was a woman who kept copies. It was her pathology, her religion, her single unbreakable habit. She kept duplicates of everything—field notes, correspondence, the emails she should have sent and the ones she shouldn't have. When the survey office had stripped her registration, it was her own archived data that convicted her; when Voss's people had blacklisted her from every consulting contract in the Pacific, it was her meticulous records that let them do it. Copies had ruined her life.

Copies had also, she now understood, saved it. Because a disgraced geologist with nothing left to lose is the most dangerous kind of witness, and because on the night Rafael Montrose poured diesel over the fuel barge and struck the match, Wren had already done the arithmetic: the originals belonged to the clan now, spoken into songs, untouchable. But songs don't cross oceans. Songs don't arrive on the desk of a journalist in Port Moresby or a land-rights lawyer in Geneva. Songs require you to trust that memory will hold—which the clans did, magnificently, at the cost of generations—but Wren had spent her career learning exactly how far institutions would go to make memory inconvenient.

So she had knelt in the cave's dim light with her phone—the battered satellite-capable handset she'd carried up the Fly River against everyone's advice—and she had photographed thirty-one pages. Every title survey. Every transfer deed bearing the forged signatures of men who claimed the clans had ceded land no clan had ever agreed to sell. Every internal memo linking the payments to a Voss subsidiary and countersigned by the deputy director of the government survey office. And the last page of Elias Montrose's journal, the one naming Dario Kell, which Wren had photographed and then sat looking at for a long moment, feeling the weight of it, deciding—not yet—that some truths needed to travel in order.

Thirty-one photographs. She checked them nightly, the way other people check a sleeping child. Still there. Still legible. Still waiting.

* * *

On the third morning in Kerema, the mission's shortwave radio coughed to life with news from the outside world, and the outside world had noticed them.

"—expedition members believed dead after supplies barge destroyed in upper Fly River region, sources say. Voss Resources Group has declined comment on reports its contracted survey team failed to return. In related developments, questions are being raised in Parliament regarding land title registrations in Western Province dating to—"

Wren turned the dial until the voice dissolved into static, then sat very still on the veranda steps, listening to the rain begin.

They think we're dead. Of course they did. A burned barge, a vanished party, a syndicate with every incentive to let the assumption stand. Somewhere in Port Moresby, Mr. Adeyemi's superiors were writing obituaries for four people who were eating fried sago on a mission veranda, and the gap between those two facts was the most valuable real estate Wren had ever occupied.

Dead women can't be sued. Dead women can't be discredited, threatened, or bought. Dead women can send letters.

She went inside, found paper and the mission's ancient typewriter, and began to write.

* * *

The first letter went to a journalist. Not the famous ones—the ones with bylines in the national papers, whose editors owned nothing Voss couldn't reach. Wren wrote instead to a woman named Margaret Kaupa, who published a newsletter out of Lae called *The Ground Beneath*, circulation four hundred, funded by church groups and stubbornness, dedicated entirely to documenting land disputes in the highlands and Sepik. Wren had cited Kaupa's work in her own abandoned doctoral thesis a lifetime ago. Kaupa had once spent six weeks in a Wabag jail for refusing to name a source. People like that didn't sell.

*Dear Ms. Kaupa,* the letter began. *You do not know me. My name will mean something to anyone who followed the 2019 inquiry into falsified geological assessments—I am the geologist who was struck off for it, though the assessment was not mine. I am writing to tell you that the pattern you have documented in three provinces is one pattern, and I can prove it extends to the upper Fly River, and I can prove who signed the orders. I cannot send you the proof electronically; the channels are watched. If you can arrange to meet me in Port Moresby within the month, I will put thirty-one pages in your hands, and you will understand why I am writing this letter by hand.*

The second letter went to the Legal Rights Institute, care of a property lawyer named Emmanuel Tovue, whose name Rafael had produced from somewhere in her father's papers—a man Elias Montrose had corresponded with in the expedition's final months, a man who had written back twice asking pointed questions and received, presumably, silence. The third letter went to a church land-mediation office in Goroka. The fourth went, after long deliberation, to the Ombudsman Commission—not because Wren trusted the Commission, but because a complaint filed there created a timestamp, a paper existence, a fact that would be awkward to disappear.

Each letter said substantially the same thing. Each letter was posted separately, in different towns, through different post offices, because Wren Okonkwo had learned paranoia the hard way and saw no reason to waste the tuition.

"What are you doing?"

Tomo stood in the doorway, holding two cups of tea, watching her seal the fourth envelope. His eyes went to the stack of finished letters, then to her face, and Wren watched him arrive at the answer before she said it—watched the understanding move through him like weather.

"You copied them," he said quietly. "Before the burning."

"I did."

"Rafael doesn't know."

"No one knows."

Tomo set the cups down and lowered himself onto the step beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. Rain drummed on the iron roof. Down at the jetty, someone was arguing cheerfully about fish prices in three languages simultaneously.

"She'll be angry," Tomo said finally.

"She'll be furious. She burned everything we had so that no one—not us, not Adeyemi, not Voss—could carry those documents downstream and trade them. She traded her freedom for the principle that the truth belongs to the people it was stolen from. And I agree with her completely." Wren smoothed the envelope flat. "But Tomo, listen to me. The elders' justice is real. What happened at the source was more real than anything I've witnessed in my professional life, and I include the time I watched a volcano recalibrate an entire valley. But the clans' memory holds the truth for the clans. It doesn't hold it for Parliament. It doesn't hold it for a judge in a courtroom in Moresby, if this ever gets that far. The songs protect the land. These—" she tapped the envelopes "—protect the singers. Because the moment Voss understands what happened at that source, they will not come for the documents. The documents are ash. They will come for the witnesses. For Yawane. For the elders. For your grandmother's village, if it comes to that. Memory is strong, but memory has been burned before, and the people who burn it have helicopters."

Tomo was quiet for a long moment. "My grandmother says a thing," he said at last. "She says: a song sung alone can be silenced. A song sung in two places is already flying." He looked at her sideways, and there was something in his face that was not quite a smile but lived next door to one. "You are the second place."

"I'm the second place," Wren agreed. "And Margaret Kaupa is the third, and a lawyer in Geneva is the fourth, and by the time Voss counts them all, the song will be everywhere and nowhere they can reach."

"And Rafael?"

Wren looked out at the rain, at the gray water beyond the jetty, at the horizon where the river's delta dissolved into the sea and the sea dissolved into sky.

"Rafael will understand eventually," she said. "She's spent twelve years believing that the only safe thing is to leave nothing behind—no address, no trace, no copy. But she learned something at the source. We all did. You can't outrun what's owed. You can only decide who you owe it to." She picked up the pen. "I owe it to the truth. All of it, including the parts that incriminate me. Especially those."

"The parts that incriminate you," Tomo repeated. "You're putting your confession in the letters."

"I'm putting the context in the letters. Kaupa will find the rest—it's what she does. And a witness who confesses her own crimes first is a witness no one can discredit. I learned that from watching them try to discredit me for nine years and fail to explain why I kept records that hurt me." She signed the last envelope with a flourish that surprised her with its steadiness. "Voss taught me everything I know about self-protection, Tomo. I'm just using the syllabus in reverse."

* * *

They posted the letters over the following week, as passage arranged itself piece by piece—a mission supply boat to Daru, from Daru a coastal trader to Port Moresby, seats bartered for labor and for the story, which Yawane's people had already begun telling in the river settlements they passed, so that by the time Wren stepped aboard the trader, half the crew had heard of the fire at the source and the documents that had become songs, and looked at the four gaunt strangers with an expression Wren had never once received from a stranger before: recognition.

On the last evening in Kerema, Rafael found her on the veranda.

Wren knew she'd been found out before Rafael said a word—knew it from the particular quality of the silence, the way Rafael Montrose stood at the top of the steps with her hands in her pockets and her weight on her heels, a woman assembling a sentence she'd been drafting for hours.

"Tomo talks in his sleep," Rafael said.

"He talks in three languages in his sleep. It's very educational."

"Wren." Rafael came down the steps and sat, heavily, the way she did everything now—like a person relearning the weight of her own body. "The photographs. Tell me."

So Wren told her. Thirty-one pages, photographed in the cave, checked nightly, copied nowhere else, addressed now to four recipients in four directions. She laid it all out plainly, without apology and without defense, because she had decided during the walk down that she owed Rafael exactly what she owed Kaupa: the whole truth, offered before it was demanded.

Rafael listened without interrupting, which Wren would learn was the most frightening thing she did. When it was done, she stared out at the darkening water for a long time.

"I burned the barge," she said finally, "so that no one could ever hold those pages hostage again. Including me. Including you." Her voice was level, but Wren heard the fault line running under it. "And you kept them."

"I kept them."

"If Adeyemi had searched us—if any of his people had gotten their hands on your phone—"

"They didn't."

"That's not a plan, Wren. That's luck wearing a plan's clothes."

"Yes," Wren said. "It was. I've built a career on knowing the difference, and I did it anyway, and I'd do it again tonight." She turned to face Rafael directly. "You gave the truth to the elders. That was right. It was the bravest thing I've ever seen anyone do, and I watched a man take a bullet for a cardboard case, so the competition is stiff. But Rafael—the elders' justice and the world's justice are different rivers. Yours flows through the valley. Mine has to flow through newspapers and courtrooms and the slow, ugly machinery of a country deciding whether its own laws mean anything. The songs keep the land. The copies keep the singers. We need both rivers, or Voss dams one and poisons the other."

Silence. Rain starting again, soft on the iron. Somewhere below, Tomo was singing—one of the transposed documents, the boundary survey rendered as melody, his voice thin and clear in the wet air.

Rafael listened to it for a while. Then she laughed—a short, exhausted, genuine sound, the first laugh Wren had heard from her since the panhandle, since another lifetime.

"My father kept receipts too," she said. "Boxes of them. I used to think it was a sickness." She shook her head slowly. "Okay, Wren. Okay. But we do it my way on one point—nothing moves until we're off this coast and the letters are past recall. And you tell me the names of every person you've contacted, and Tomo vets them through his grandmother's network, because if Voss has a cousin in the Lae postal system, I want to know before the envelope does, not after."

"Agreed."

"And Wren?" Rafael stood, and in the lamplight spilling from the mission window her face was unreadable and then, briefly, entirely readable. "Thank you. For being the kind of coward who plans ahead. It turns out the brave ones need you desperately."

She went inside. Wren remained on the veranda a while longer, listening to the rain and the singing and the sea, and allowed herself, cautiously, deliberately, the unfamiliar sensation of hope—not the bright brittle optimism she'd once mistaken for professionalism, but something heavier and better-built. Hope with a foundation. Hope with duplicates.

Four letters, moving toward four horizons. Thirty-one pages, flying.

Three days later, at the Daru post office, as she handed the final envelope across the counter, the clerk glanced at the return address and then at her, and said, in the mild tone of a man remarking on weather:

"You're the second person this week sending mail to that lawyer in Moresby. Funny thing—" he weighed the envelope in his hand, frowning faintly at some private calculation, "—the other one was posted from Port Moresby itself. Business envelope. Voss Resources, printed in the corner." He shrugged. "Probably nothing."

Wren smiled, thanked him, and walked out into the heat with her pulse hammering and her mind already working—because Voss didn't write letters to land-rights lawyers, and whatever game the syndicate had just entered, it had entered it first.

The race, she understood, had already started. And they were behind.
