## 21. Settled Accounts

The courthouse in Port Moresby had no air conditioning that worked, and by ten in the morning the room smelled of sweat and mildew and the particular sweetness of old paper baking in the tropics. Rafael Montrose sat in the witness box with her hands folded in her lap and watched a fly walk the length of the railing beside her, unhurried, as if it too had been subpoenaed.

She had testified before. Not like this. Once, twelve years ago, she had sat in a room in Oklahoma City and answered questions about her father's expedition with the flat voice of someone reading from a script she hadn't written—the inquest's script, Voss's script, the script everyone had agreed on because it was easier than the truth. *Pilot error. Weather. A man who pushed too far.* She had been twenty-six and drowning and they had handed her a rope made of lies and she had grabbed it.

This time nobody had offered her anything. That was the difference, and she held onto it the way a climber holds a good hold: not because it was comfortable, but because she knew exactly what it was worth.

"State your name for the record," said the lawyer for the landowners' consortium—a thin woman named Alu Kepas who wore her exhaustion like a uniform.

"Rafael Elena Montrose."

"And your role in the expedition of last year, up the Fly River?"

"I was the guide. I led a crew contracted by Voss Holdings to recover a survey cache my father hid in the headwater country before he died."

Kepas let the silence sit. In courtrooms, Rafael had learned, silence was a tool like any other—some people used it to intimidate witnesses, but Kepas used it to give the words room to land. "And what did that cache contain?"

Rafael looked at the gallery. Tomo sat in the third row, pressed shirt, hair cut short since the highlands, hands quiet on his knees. Wren was beside him with a folder of photographs she'd already surrendered twice over. Dario was not there. Dario was where he had been for eight months now, in the highlands, under the judgment of people whose courts didn't have walls—and some mornings Rafael woke up wondering if that was mercy or sentence, and most mornings she decided it was both, which was probably what justice actually looked like when you stripped away the furniture.

"It contained land-title surveys," she said. "Original field surveys of territory belonging to the clans of the upper Fly. Territory that was never ceded, never sold, never signed away. And documentation showing that Voss Holdings, working through officers of the national survey office, registered those same parcels as state land and sold them—concessions, timber rights, mineral exploration licenses—to buyers who had no idea the titles were built on paper that didn't exist. Or rather," she corrected herself, "paper that existed only because men made it exist. The land itself never agreed to any of it."

She heard it in the gallery: the small sound a room makes when something true is said aloud in a place built for something else. Not gasps. Just a settling, like a house at night.

* * *

It had taken months for the unraveling to begin, and it had not begun the way anyone expected—not with the documents themselves, which moved slowly and carefully through lawyers' hands, but with a photograph. Wren's photograph, one of forty-three she'd taken in the cave before Rafael burned the barge: a survey sheet with two signatures on it, one belonging to a survey office director named Bram Ondassa, the other to a man whose name meant nothing to the public until the newspapers printed it large—Marcus Voss himself, third generation of the syndicate, signing away country he'd never seen to buyers he'd never meet.

The syndicate had survived wars, coups, three royal commissions, and one genuine attempt on Marcus Voss's life, but it had never survived being *boring*. That was what Rafael came to understand watching it happen from the outside, from newspaper archives and late-night radio and Tomo's letters, which arrived irregularly and always contained more news than his own. Fraud was dramatic; fraud was survivable, because drama could be bought down, litigated, starved out. But the documents had made Voss boring. Every parcel, every concession, every shell company had to be traced, and each trace produced another signature, another date, another small administrative sin, until the story stopped being about villains and became about paperwork—which was the one thing money couldn't make interesting enough to defend.

The lawsuits came first, then the license suspensions, then the banks. By the time Rafael flew back to Port Moresby to testify, Voss Holdings existed mostly as a name on buildings being quietly rebranded, and its creditors had begun eating each other with the efficiency of the crocodiles Wren had once catalogued, deadpan, in her field notes.

Adeyemi they got on a Tuesday in March, and not for any of it—not for the threats, not for the advance team, not for standing in a sacred valley with a gun while Tomo stepped between the barrels. They got him on cargo fraud, of all things, containers of illegally harvested rosewood moving through a port he'd once controlled, a paper trail so mundane it was almost insulting. Rafael read the article three times. She kept waiting to feel something larger than she felt, which turned out to be a tired, dry satisfaction, like drinking water after a long walk. Justice, it turned out, rarely announced itself. It just showed up in an unmarked car outside a warehouse in Lae and put handcuffs on a man who had once made her calculate whether her own guide was worth more alive or dead.

Ilsa, as far as anyone knew, was still nowhere. Rafael thought about her sometimes—not with anger anymore, though the anger had been real, had been necessary. She thought about the moment in the valley when Ilsa's rifle had come off Adeyemi's team and onto no team at all, the moment a mercenary discovered a line she couldn't cross. People assumed mercenaries had no lines. Rafael knew better now. Everyone had a line. The tragedy was only ever in how far you had to travel to find yours.

* * *

The cross-examination took the better part of the afternoon, and it was conducted by a barrister retained—everyone assumed—by what remained of Voss's interests, a heavy man named Quillan who sweated through his robe and asked questions like a man laying sandbags against a flood.

"Ms. Montrose. You were, at the time of this expedition, indebted to Voss Holdings. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"To the amount of—" he consulted a page "—one hundred and ninety-two thousand dollars, US."

"That sounds right. I stopped counting at a hundred thousand. After that it's just weather."

Someone in the gallery laughed and was shushed. Quillan pressed on. "And this debt was to be extinguished upon recovery of the cache. So you had a financial interest in recovering these documents and returning them to your employer."

"I did."

"You did not return them to your employer."

"No."

"You destroyed the crew's fuel barge. You stranded your own team. You carried the documents upstream and gave them to"—he paused, and Rafael watched him decide how to say it—"to persons with no legal standing in any jurisdiction."

"They had standing," Rafael said. "Just not in yours."

Quillan blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The clans of the upper Fly have been the standing authority on that land for longer than there has been a jurisdiction to be outside of. The question isn't whether they had legal standing, Mr. Quillan. It's whether your clients ever bothered to find out whose standing they were standing on." She heard Kepas shift slightly at her table—not disapproval, exactly. Restraint. Letting Rafael spend her own capital where she chose.

Quillan tried again. "You've described yourself as a debtor. You burned the assets securing your employer's claim. Isn't it possible that your testimony here is simply the continuation of a personal vendetta? That you destroyed evidence and are now destroying reputations to settle a private score?"

Rafael had thought about this question for months. She'd rehearsed answers in motel rooms and airport lounges, and all of them had sounded defensive, and so in the end she'd thrown them out and decided to do the thing her father had taught her without ever meaning to: tell the truth plainly and let it stand in the weather.

"My father spent his last year documenting what was done to that land," she said. "He died for it, along with three other people, including a man whose name isn't even in your client's records because erasing him was cheaper than admitting him. For twelve years I believed the official version, because the official version was written by the people your firm represents, and I helped bury my own father's name under it because I was young and broke and they handed me a rope." She paused. "So yes, Mr. Quillan. There's a private score. But the score isn't mine alone, and it isn't private, and it isn't settled by me testifying. It'll be settled when every parcel is returned and every name is read back into the record—including the ones your clients deleted. I'm just the part of it that can talk."

The courtroom was very still. Even the fly had gone somewhere else.

Judge Mendi, an old woman with reading glasses on a chain of woven beads, looked at Rafael over the top of her glasses for a long moment, and then said, in the tone of someone closing a door gently, "The witness will confine her remarks to questions." But her eyes, before she dropped them to her notes, said something else entirely.

* * *

That night, in a guesthouse near the harbor where the ceiling fan clicked like a slow clock, Rafael opened the envelope she'd been carrying for two weeks and hadn't yet found the nerve—or the right hour—for.

It was from Yawane. Not a letter, exactly; Yawane did not write letters. It was a single sheet of paper on which Tomo had transcribed, in careful English beneath the original, a message dictated over a radio handset from a station in the highlands that the clan ran on solar power and stubbornness. The message concerned Dario.

*The man Kell has walked the boundaries,* it read. *All of them. He has carried water and dug the graves we asked him to dig, including the grave of the one who had no name, and he has learned the songs badly but he has learned them. The elders will speak their judgment at the end of the wet season. He asks whether you will come. He does not ask whether you will speak for him. He says he knows better than to ask that.*

Rafael read it four times. Outside, the harbor lights smeared across the black water, and a ship's horn sounded somewhere far off, low and patient, like the river remembering the sea.

Dario Kell. The man who had tipped Voss off about her father's route—her father's journal had named him, in his own handwriting, in the last entry he ever wrote—and who had then spent twelve years building a career on the bones of that expedition, and who had finally, in a firefight at the source of everything, taken a bullet shielding the case. Or appearing to. Rafael had turned that phrase over so many times the words had worn smooth. *Or appearing to.* Because that was Dario. Even his courage was ambiguous. Even his redemption would need footnotes.

But he had stayed. When the barge burned and the way home dissolved, he had stayed, and he had walked the boundaries, and he had dug the graves—including the grave of Emeric Saul, the surveyor the records said had survived, the witness who hadn't made it home, whose name the elders had spoken aloud for the first time in twelve years while Wren wept openly and Rafael stood with her jaw locked so hard it ached for a week.

Would she go? Would she stand in the highlands at the end of the wet season and watch men she loved and hated in equal measure pass judgment on the man who had betrayed them all?

She set the letter on the nightstand, next to the small potted basil she'd bought at the market—she was keeping plants alive now; it had become, without her deciding it, a kind of discipline, a daily vote in favor of staying places—and she lay in the dark and listened to the fan click.

Yes, she thought. She would go. Not to speak for him. To witness. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point.

* * *

The last payment cleared on a Thursday.

It wasn't dramatic. It was a text message from a bank she'd opened an account with specifically because it had no connection to anything Voss had ever touched, and it said: *Your balance has been updated.* One hundred and ninety-two thousand dollars, paid out across eleven months from wages earned guiding scientific surveys up rivers in three countries—honest work, hard work, work where the only thing anyone wanted from her was her knowledge of water and weather and how to keep people alive between the two. Kepas's consortium had arranged the expedition contracts as part of the settlement framework; Rafael had insisted on that part. She would not take settlement money. Settlement money was Voss's money, laundered through lawyers, and she had spent enough years carrying Voss's weight. She wanted wages. She wanted to know, down to the last digit, that every dollar that freed her had passed through her own two hands.

She sat on the guesthouse steps with her phone in her palm and read the balance and waited for the feeling.

It didn't come the way she expected. No flood, no lightness, no cinematic exhale. What came was quieter and stranger: the absence of a sound she'd lived with so long she'd forgotten it was there. Like stepping off a boat after weeks on the river and noticing, only then, the constant hum of the engine. Twelve years of debt—not just the money, but the older debt, the one to her father's memory, to the truth she'd helped bury—and for the first time since she was twenty-six years old, nobody owned a piece of her morning.

She thought about calling someone. Tomo. Wren. Her mother, who had died believing the inquest, God rest her, and whom Rafael visited sometimes in the cemetery in Tulsa and told things she should have told her alive. Instead she called no one. She went inside, watered the basil, and wrote a reply to Yawane's letter in longhand, because some messages deserved the slowness of ink:

*I'll come at the end of the wet season. Tell him I'll stand where I can see him. Tell him I won't speak for him—but I'll speak, and what I say will be true, and he can live with that or not. Tell the elders I'm bringing nothing this time except myself, and ask if that's acceptable. Tell Tomo his letters are the best part of my mail and his English is better than my Tok Pisin and I intend to fix that.*

She sealed it, addressed it, and set it by the door.

Then she went back out to the steps, because the evening was coming on gold over the harbor, and the ships were coming home, and somewhere upstream a river she loved was running high with the wet season, carrying everything toward the sea and remembering everything it had ever been given.

For the first time in twelve years, Rafael Montrose owed nothing to anyone.

What she had instead was a promise to keep, a judgment to witness, and a long walk into the highlands ahead of her—and she found, sitting there in the cooling air, that she was looking forward to it the way you look forward to a storm on the plains: not because it would be easy, but because it would be true, and she had finally learned the difference.
