## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

There are three things a storm chaser learns before she learns anything else. One: the sea does not care about your plans. Two: the weather service lies by omission, which is a polite way of saying they round down. Three: never answer the phone when the caller ID reads VANE MARITIME, because the Vanes do not call to chat.

I answered it anyway. That should tell you everything about my week.

"Oscar!" The voice was warm as bathwater and twice as slippery. Desmond Vane. The elder brother, or possibly the younger — they were identical in every way that mattered, including their smiles, which arrived like weather fronts of their own. "Desmond. What a surprise," I said, wedging the phone between ear and shoulder while I lashed down the last fuel drum on *Peregrine*'s deck. "Don't you people sleep?"

"We sleep beautifully," he said. "It's one of the perks of not owing anyone money. You should try it sometime."

"I'll put it on my list. Right after 'breathe underwater' and 'win the lottery.'"

He laughed. That was the thing about the Vanes — they laughed easily, generously, like men who had never once been punched in the mouth. "Oscar, Oscar. We're not monsters. We're calling with good news."

"The last time you had good news for me, I spent four days in a hospital in Port Calloway with a collapsed lung."

"And didn't we cover the bill? Every cent." A pause, delicate as a fishhook. "Well. Covered it the way we cover everything. On account."

I cinched the knot, tested it, and let myself look out over the harbor at Cape Reckoning's southern arm, where the water lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky that couldn't decide what color to be. Forty miles offshore, according to three separate models and one very nervous meteorologist I'd bribed with coffee, a monsoon cell was organizing itself into something historic. Barometric pressure falling like a man off a cliff. Winds that would rewrite the record books or erase them entirely.

To anyone else, that was a reason to board up windows.

To me, it was a payday.

*Peregrine* was my rig — thirty-one feet of salvaged fishing hull, reinforced bow, sensor mast bolted where the mast used to be, and an engine that ran on prayer and diesel in roughly equal measure. I'd chased eleven named storms in her. She'd brought me home from nine. The other two were why I owed the Vanes more money than I would earn in six years of honest work.

"Here's the good news," Desmond said, and I could hear him smiling through the wire, could practically see the office behind him — mahogany, brass, photographs of ships his family had no business owning. "The storm. We've heard you're going out after it."

News travels fast in harbors. Faster than decency.

"Am I?"

"Everyone says so. And we thought — well, brothers that we are — what an opportunity. If something were to happen to you out there, Oscar, in this historic weather, our investment would be... unrecoverable. So we're prepared to be flexible. Come see us after the storm passes. Bring whatever you've got. We'll make it right. One way or another."

"One way or another," I repeated. "That's almost poetry."

"We're a literary family," he said cheerfully. "The other way involves Corvo. Do give our regards to the cape. Grandfather always said there was gold in those cliffs."

He hung up before I could decide whether that was a joke. With the Vanes it never quite was.

* * *

I want to be clear about something: I am good at this. Not lucky, not reckless-for-its-own-sake — though my ex-wife would dispute the distinction from her very sensible house in a very sensible town — but *good*. I read pressure gradients the way some people read faces. I can smell a squall line forty minutes before the radar paints it. When the barometer drops past twenty-nine and holds, something in my chest unclenches instead of tightening, and I have stopped apologizing for that, mostly.

So when I took *Peregrine* out of the harbor at 0400, ahead of the fleet of television trucks already crawling up the coast road, I felt — briefly, honestly — like myself again. The debt, the calls, the slow acid dread of the Vanes' cheerful voices: all of it fell away the moment the harbor light dropped astern and the open water took the hull.

The plan was simple, which is to say it was the kind of plan that only seems simple until you're inside it. Run south along the shelf, drop the sensor buoys ahead of the landfall, ride the edge of the circulation where the data is richest and the waves are merely murderous rather than apocalyptic, then cut back into the lee of Cape Reckoning before the eye wall arrives. Sell the data to the institute, sell the footage to whoever paid fastest, and use the money to buy myself — not freedom. I'd stopped believing in freedom somewhere around the second hospital stay. But time. Another season. Another chance to outrun arithmetic.

The first buoy went down at 0630 in a following swell. The second at 0745, just as the horizon began to organize itself into something with intent. By 0900 the sky to the southeast had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, and the wind had found its voice — not roaring yet, but muttering, the way a big dog mutters before it commits.

I stood at the helm with salt drying on my forearms and thought: *this one is different.* Not fear exactly. Recognition. Some storms you chase. This one had turned around and was looking back.

My phone buzzed. I looked at it the way you look at a spider on the ceiling.

Merrick Vane this time. The other twin, or the same twin; theology on this point remained unsettled.

"Oscar Coldharbour," he said, without preamble, in a voice identical to his brother's down to the warmth. "You're underway. Wonderful. Desmond said you would be. He won the pool."

"There's a *pool*?"

"There's always a pool. Currently the smart money says you make it back. The dumb money says otherwise, but the dumb money also bet on your last two outings, so." He sighed happily. "I only called to say: deadline stands. Storm's end. After that, we stop being patient, and patience is genuinely the only nice thing we own."

"Merrick—"

"Also, small thing, purely professional interest: you're heading toward the cape. Reckoning Point. Any particular reason? Family history, is it?"

The wind gusted. I let three seconds pass, which with the Vanes is a confession wearing a trench coat.

"No reason," I said. "Good water there."

"Mm," said Merrick Vane, who did not believe me even slightly. "Our grandfather used to say the same thing. Well. Fair winds, Oscar. See you after the storm. One way or another."

They both said that. It was their signature, their cologne, their little family crest: *one way or another.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about debt: it doesn't feel like numbers. It feels like weather. It sits on the horizon of every morning. It changes how you read the sky. There were years — before the loans, before the engine rebuilds and the hospital and the season the fishery died and took half the coast with it — when I chased storms because I loved them. Now I chased them because the alternative was standing still long enough for the Vanes to catch up, and I had seen what happened to people who stood still. Old Tomas Ferro sold his boat, then his house, then, when those ran out, his hands — he trawls for them now, on their boats, in waters he used to own. He smiled at me last spring. That was the worst part. He still smiled.

So yes: recklessness. Call it that if it helps you sleep. But understand that when I pointed *Peregrine*'s bow at a bruising sky with sixty thousand owed against her hull, I wasn't gambling. Gambling implies you might walk away with nothing. I was already walking away with nothing. I was gambling with the only currency I had left, which was nerve.

By noon the sea had changed its mind about being a sea. The swells came in sets now, long and organized, eight feet, ten, the wind steady out of the northeast at thirty-five and building. I'd deployed four buoys, all reporting, all screaming — pressure down to 28.6 inches and falling faster than any model predicted, which meant either the instruments were lying or I was riding the leading edge of the storm of the century, and my instruments had never once lied to me. People had. Instruments hadn't.

I ate half a tin of sardines standing up, one hand on the rail, and allowed myself ninety seconds of pure, uncomplicated joy. Because that's the truth nobody puts in the documentaries: there is joy in it. Out here, with the storm assembling itself on the horizon like something being built by an angry god with a deadline of its own, there is no debt, no Vanes, no ledger with my name at the bottom growing longer by the month. There is only the next wave and the one after it, and me, competent, necessary, exactly where I belong.

Then the joy passed, as joy does, and I went below to check the charts, and that's when I saw it.

Cape Reckoning. My grandfather's cape.

I hadn't planned to go there. I want that on the record too, addressed to whichever court eventually hears about all this: I had *not planned* to go there. My track line bent south for perfectly respectable meteorological reasons — the lee of the cape was the safest shelter within fifty miles once the eye wall made landfall, any fool could see it, I was being *responsible* —

But the chart was out on the table, and the chart has that annotation, faded brown, in handwriting I would know anywhere because I learned my letters copying it: *E.C.'s mark*, a little anchor symbol scratched beside the point itself, dated the summer before he vanished. Elias Coldharbour. Forty years gone. No wreck found, no body, no distress call. Just a boat that sailed out of Port Calloway on a rising tide and never came home, and a seven-year-old girl — me — who waited on the breakwater for a father figure the sea had decided to keep.

The legends say the tide around Reckoning Point goes out farther than it has any right to, once in a generation, once in a lifetime, and shows you things. The fishermen say it like a warning. My grandfather used to say it like a promise.

I stood there with the chart under my hand and the storm coming up my wake like a debt collector, and I thought about deadlines. The Vanes'. The storm's. They were converging, I realized — the same clock, the same final hour. Whatever I was going to do about my life, I had until the monsoon made landfall to do it.

Outside, the wind found the rigging and began, softly, to sing.

I altered course for the cape.

* * *

The last call came at dusk, when the light had gone strange and greenish and the first outer bands of rain came across the water in gray veils, hissing on the swells. I almost didn't answer. But by then the pattern was established, and patterns matter to me — it's how I read storms — and a pattern of two calls means a third, and a third unanswered would only bring Corvo, and Corvo does not use telephones.

"Before you say anything," Desmond began, "I want you to know how much we admire you."

"That's new."

"Genuine admiration. You're out there, right now, sailing *toward* that." I could picture him at his window, wherever the Vanes kept their windows, watching the same bruised horizon from the safe side of the glass. "Most people run from weather like that. You run at it. It's magnificent. It's also, frankly, terrifying, from a portfolio standpoint."

"What do you want, Desmond?"

"A detail. A tiny detail. Merrick says you turned toward the cape. Our sources say the same. And our grandfather—" the warmth flickered, just once, like a lamp in a draft "—our grandfather left certain papers, when he passed. Family history. Shipping records. The Reckoning comes up in them, oddly enough. Your grandfather's ship, your grandfather's loss. Small world, the sea."

My hands were steady on the wheel. I remember noticing that, noticing how steady, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a quiet room.

"I don't know anything about shipping records," I said.

"Of course not," he agreed pleasantly. "And if you ever did come across anything — papers, charts, family curios — well. Debts have been forgiven for less. Much less. Consider it a standing offer, Oscar. Storm's end. One way or another."

The line went dead. I stood in the cockpit with the rain starting in earnest and the cape's dark shape rising off my port bow like a sleeping animal, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since dawn: I owed the Vanes sixty-one thousand, four hundred dollars. I had maybe nine hundred in the account and a data contract worth perhaps six. The storm would pay for fuel and repairs and leave me exactly nowhere, unless—

Unless the legends were true. Unless the tide went out farther than it had any right to. Unless the sea that took Elias Coldharbour had kept him somewhere, and kept with him whatever he'd been carrying, and was willing — once in a lifetime, once in a generation — to show me.

That's not science. I know it's not science. But I've watched the ocean my whole life, and I'll tell you what I believe: it keeps accounts too. It just runs longer ledgers than the Vanes.

Behind me the monsoon filled half the world, black and vast and coming on like fate. Ahead the cape waited under the last green light, and beneath it, somewhere under all that held-back water, whatever the tide had taken was waiting too.

I trimmed the sails and ran for the point, straight into the teeth of the rising wind, and if my hands shook a little at the tiller — well. The sea was rough. Anyone would shake.

Even me. Especially me.

One way or another.
