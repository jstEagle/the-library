## 2. Cape Reckoning

The barometer had been falling for eleven hours, and I'd been falling with it.

Not literally, of course. Literally I was standing at the helm of the *Hazardous Optimist*, my twenty-six-foot storm rig, watching the needle on the marine barometer slide down the dial like a man easing himself off a cliff one fingerhold at a time. 984 millibars. 979. 974. Numbers that made most sailors turn around and numbers that made me lean into the throttle.

"Once in a century," I said out loud to no one, because saying things out loud is a habit you pick up when you spend enough time alone on open water. "Maybe once in two centuries."

The phone buzzed in its cradle for the fourth time since dawn. I didn't have to look at the screen. The Vanes called in rotation—Desmond first, then Ambrose, then Desmond again, like a metronome keeping time to the rhythm of my ruin. Each call was cheerful. That was the thing about the Vanes; they were never once unpleasant. Desmond had told me last week, in a voice warm as fresh bread, that he admired my work ethic, that a woman who chased storms for a living clearly wasn't afraid of weather, and that it would be a shame if something happened to her boat in bad conditions. Uninsured boats sink all the time, he'd said. Terrible luck, some families have.

I let it ring out. Then I turned the phone face-down on the chart table, which is the nautical equivalent of putting your fingers in your ears, and went back to watching the sky do its slow, theatrical darkening over the water ahead.

The cape came up out of the haze around noon, and I felt it before I saw it—that particular pressure behind the sternum that isn't quite grief and isn't quite dread but has the same weight as both. Cape Reckoning. Forty years ago, give or take a season, my grandfather Elias Coldharbour had sailed his trawler around that headland with a hold full of cargo and a crew of four, and neither the trawler nor the cargo nor any of the five men had ever been seen again. The official story was a squall. The unofficial story, depending on which barstool philosopher you asked, was pirates, insurance fraud, a sea monster, or—in the version my grandmother had believed until the day she died—a curse laid down by a partner he'd cheated.

I was nine when she died, old enough to remember her telling me that the cape took what it was owed, and young enough to believe her. I'd spent thirty years unlearning that belief and replacing it with science, which is just superstition with better instruments. Mostly.

The headland rose black and wet out of the sea, cliffs stacked in layers like the pages of a book someone had dropped, the topmost ones crowned with wind-bent pines that all leaned inland as if flinching from the water. A lighthouse stood at the tip, decommissioned, its paint gone the grey of old bone. Below it, the famous reef—the one that had chewed up the *Reckoning* according to legend, though no wreck had ever been found on it, which had always struck me as the legend's weakest seam.

I brought the *Optimist* in close along the northern shore, looking for the little harbor town of Salt Hollow, which per the charts consisted of a breakwater, a fuel dock, and a population that probably hadn't doubled since my grandfather's day. The swell was already picking up, long rolling shoulders of green water coming out of the southeast at intervals that were getting shorter by the hour. My instruments loved it. My stomach did not, though I'd never admit that to anyone, including myself.

* * *

Salt Hollow's fuel dock was a single wooden pier with a hand-painted sign reading FUEL — ASK FOR PETE, and Pete turned out to be a man in his seventies built like a fence post, all knuckle and sinew, mending a crab pot on the dock with hands that moved without his eyes' participation. He looked up when I tied off, gave the *Optimist* a long appraisal, then gave me an even longer one.

"You're Coldharbour's granddaughter," he said.

Not a question. I blinked. "That's a hell of an opener, Pete."

"Elias had that same look. Like the ocean owed him money." He went back to his pot. "Fuel's under the tarp. Pump takes coins. Storm's coming—you can feel it in the pilings, if you know how to listen."

"I know how to listen," I said, and started hauling the hose. "That's why I'm here. Pressure's dropping faster than anything in the records for this coast. If it keeps this rate, we're looking at a sub-950 low making landfall inside forty-eight hours. Possibly a record. Definitely a career-maker."

Pete snorted, which I chose to interpret as respect.

"You'll want to anchor north of the point," he said. "Reef south side gets ugly fast when the wind backs around. And—" He paused, and I heard the pause do work, the way old men's pauses do. "You'll want to be well clear of the cape itself when the tide goes out."

I looked up from the fuel gauge. "When the tide goes *out*?"

"Tide legends." He shrugged with the elaborate carelessness of a man about to say something he considered very important. "My grandfather worked these waters. His father before him. There's a tide comes every so often—big storms, big lows—and it doesn't come back right. Goes out further than it should, stays out longer than it should. Strips the bottom bare past the point. Old folks used to say the sea was showing you what it kept. Showing you your dead."

"That's a lovely image," I said. "Biologically impossible, but lovely. Tides are harmonic. They don't 'go out wrong,' they respond to lunar and atmospheric forcing, and yeah, a deep low plus storm surge timing can produce anomalously low water—but it's predictable physics, not the sea holding a séance."

"Mm," said Pete.

"Pete. I've chased forty-one cyclones. I've stood inside a tornado's inflow channel and taken core samples of a hail shaft. I promise you the ocean is not personally invested in my family history."

"Didn't say it was." He finished a knot, tested it, started another. "Said it shows you things. Whether it means to or not. Low water's low water. Things get seen that got put away." His eyes came up, pale as fish scales. "Your grandfather's been put away forty years. You sure you want to be standing here when the water decides to give him back?"

The fuel hose suddenly seemed very loud. I laughed—it came out more easily than I expected, which annoyed me—and shook my head.

"You've been reading the tourist brochures. Look, I appreciate the local color, genuinely. It's great material. But I'm here for the pressure gradient, not ghost stories." I screwed the cap back on the tank. "Besides, if the tide does something unprecedented, that's data. That's the whole reason I'm anchored here instead of running south like a sensible person."

"Sensible people live longer," Pete said.

"Not as interestingly." I flipped him a coin for the pump, then, because some atavistic instinct made me, asked: "You knew him? My grandfather?"

"Everyone knew Elias." For the first time his hands stopped moving. "He'd come through every season, buy fuel, tell lies about fish. Good man. Bad judgment about partners." Something crossed his face, there and gone. "His partners are still around, you know. Big house up the coast now. Vane, Vane. They own half the harbor licenses these days."

The name landed in my chest like a hook finding flesh. "I'm aware," I said, and kept my voice level, because the last thing I needed was a stranger seeing what the Vanes cost me. "Small world."

"Small cape," Pete corrected. "Everything that happens here happens twice. Once in front of everyone, and once later, when nobody's watching." He gathered his crab pot under one arm and stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "Watch your tide tables, girl. And watch the barometer. When it stops falling—when it goes *flat*, dead flat, like the world holding its breath—that's when the water does what it shouldn't. That's when the cape opens."

"Opens," I repeated.

"Like a mouth," Pete said, and walked away down the pier, leaving me with a full tank, a rising swell, and the distinct impression that I'd just been handed either folklore or a warning, and no way to tell which.

* * *

I anchored north of the point, because even reckless people aren't stupid, and spent the afternoon doing science as a form of self-medication.

The readings were extraordinary. I ran them three times, calibrated against two independent sources, and got the same answer each time: central pressure in the approaching system was down to 961 millibars and accelerating, with the storm's forward track bent almost due west—straight at the cape. Cross-referenced against historical records going back a hundred and forty years, nothing on this coast had come close. The lowest previously recorded was 972, in a cyclone that had flattened the harbor town two generations back. This thing was going to be a once-in-a-lifetime event, and I intended to have instruments in the middle of it, because that was the deal I'd made with my life somewhere along the way: if I couldn't stop being afraid, I could at least be afraid productively.

And underneath the data, quiet as silt, the other reason sat where it always sat.

Forty years ago, Elias Coldharbour had rounded that cape in weather far less remarkable than this. The official inquiry had blamed a squall—*sudden localized deterioration*, the report said, in the tone of men closing a file. No wreckage. No debris field. No distress call, though the *Reckoning* carried a radio. Just gone, along with a cargo manifest worth more than the rest of the fleet combined, insured by a company whose principal shareholders included—my grandmother had found this out late and bitter—one A. Vane.

I'd grown up on that injustice the way other kids grew up on religion. While other children learned prayers, I learned the manifest line items. I could recite the cargo list at ten years old: baled rubber, tin ingots, sealed crates marked only as *machinery*. Enough value to build the Vane fortune twice over, vanished into the sea along with my grandfather, while the men who'd loaded his hold bought houses on the hill.

Every storm chaser has a storm they're really chasing. Mine had a name and a date and a coastline, and I'd been circling it for thirty years telling myself it was about the barometry.

So when the satellite feed showed the low's center tracking directly across the cape, I didn't run south. I set a second anchor, checked my drogue, and told myself it was for the data.

The evening came down thick and greenish, that bruised light that precedes serious weather, and the swell had organized itself into long disciplined ranks marching out of the southeast. On the radio, the forecasters had begun using the word *historic* in the careful voices people use for things they don't want to jinx. I ate cold noodles standing up, watched the light die behind the lighthouse, and tried not to think about a nine-year-old girl being told the sea keeps what it's owed.

Around midnight, the barometer did the thing Pete had described.

I was half-asleep in the pilot berth when I noticed the silence—not outside, the wind was still building, but inside the boat, in the instruments, in the quality of the air. I hauled myself up and looked at the dial and felt the hair rise on my arms. The needle had stopped falling. Not slowed. Stopped. Dead vertical, dead flat, 953 millibars and frozen, as if the entire atmosphere had paused mid-sentence.

Outside, the sea had gone strange. The swell still marched, but beneath it the water lay flat and oily, and the moonlight—breaking through a rift in the cloud deck like something spilled—lay on it in a way that made the whole surface look less like water than like glass laid over something else. Something underneath.

I stood in the cockpit with my breath loud in my ears and thought about Pete's voice. *That's when the cape opens.*

"Don't be absurd," I told myself, and meant it about sixty percent.

Then I saw the water falling.

Not waves—falling. The sea beyond the point was dropping, visibly, steadily, like water draining out of a basin, the reef bars surfacing one by one, gleaming wet in the moonlight, and beyond them the seabed emerging in long dark shelves that no chart showed and no tide table predicted. The ebb roared faintly across the distance, a sound like the world exhaling.

I grabbed my binoculars and pressed them to my eyes, and that was when I saw it, there where the cliffs met the retreating water: a black opening in the rock below the lighthouse, wide as a barn door and growing wider as the sea fell away from it, a cave mouth that appeared on no chart, in no survey, that had been drowned and hidden for—

For how long?

For forty years?

My hands were shaking. I told myself it was the cold. I told myself it was the pressure drop, the adrenaline, the scientific significance of an anomalous tidal event. I told myself all the true things, the defensible things, the things I could write up in a paper someday.

And then I went below, checked my dive gear, and started weighing the anchor, because apparently I was about to find out whether Pete's legends came with a refund policy.

The *Optimist* swung her bow toward the cape, toward the opening in the cliff where the sea was giving back what it had kept, and somewhere behind me my phone lit up silently in the dark cabin—Desmond Vane, calling with cheerful news I would never hear.
