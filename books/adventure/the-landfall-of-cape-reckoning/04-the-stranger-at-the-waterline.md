## 4. The Stranger at the Waterline

I'd been hunting that cave for eleven years, and I want it on the record that I found the tide chart first. Found it, bought it, laminated it, and slept with it under my pillow like a love letter from a dead man—which, in a sense, it was, since my father had drawn half of it before the Reckoning's coast took his lungs and left me a boat, a debt, and a theory.

The theory was simple: the wreck never washed out to sea. Everyone said it did. The insurers said it, the historians said it, the old men in the Portside Tavern said it with the certainty of men who'd never been further out than the second sandbar. But my father's last dive log had a bearing penciled on the final page—*the cliff drinks deep when the moon pulls thin*—and a man doesn't pencil a bearing for nothing.

So when the barometer started falling like a stone off a wharf, and the tide tables showed something the printer probably thought was an error—a low so low it would strip the seabed bare under Cape Reckoning itself—I didn't hesitate. I loaded the *Magpie* with salvage gear, charged cutting line, and enough coffee to kill a horse, and I ran south ahead of the weather like a woman being chased by God.

Which is why, when I finally stood on the wet sand beneath those cliffs and saw the cave mouth gaping open like a wound in the world, I expected to be alone.

Instead there were footprints. Fresh ones. Small boots, long stride, heading straight into the dark with the confidence of someone who had no idea they were trespassing on the only thing I'd ever wanted.

I remember standing there with my knife still sheathed and thinking, very clearly: *well, isn't that just my luck.*

* * *

I went in angry, which I'll admit was a tactical error. Angry people make noise. I made plenty—boots splashing through the shallow pools, breath echoing off the limestone, my headlamp carving white slashes across walls furred with forty years of darkness. The cave ran back maybe sixty meters, the floor stepping down in terraces, tide pools sitting in them like coins left in a collection plate. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell of salt and old wood and something older underneath—stone, cold stone, the kind of cold that hasn't felt sun since before anyone alive was born.

I caught her at the third terrace.

She was crouched over a curve of black timber jutting from the silt, her lamp angled low, one hand flat against the grain like she was taking its pulse. She had a camera around her neck and a crowbar lying beside her knee—the crowbar was what decided me, honestly. Photographs are documentation. Crowbars are looting.

"That's my claim," I said.

She didn't jump. I'll give her this—she turned slow, lamp swinging up to blind me first, professional habit or just good manners for someone about to be rude. When my eyes adjusted I got a look at her: mid-thirties maybe, storm-chaser lean, salt-stained jacket with some meteorological service logo half peeled off it, and a face that was doing a bad job of hiding how rattled she already was.

"You'll have to point me to the claim office," she said. "I don't recall signing anything."

"The claim is older than your paperwork." I came down the terrace, keeping the water between us. "Eleven years of diving these waters. My father spent twenty before that. That wreck—" I nodded at the timber under her hand "—is the reason he's dead. So you can step away from it."

Something moved across her face when I said the name—I hadn't even said the name yet—and she looked down at the timber like she was seeing it for the first time, though I doubted it. People who carry crowbars into caves know what they're looking for.

"Reckoning," she said quietly. Not a question.

"Forty years missing," I said. "And you're standing on her spine."

For a moment neither of us moved. The cave breathed around us—that slow tidal sigh through channels we couldn't see, the sound my father used to say was the coast swallowing. Then she straightened up and put her hand deliberately behind her back, away from the crowbar, which was either surrender or strategy.

"Ines Marlowe," I said, because apparently I introduce myself to thieves now.

"Oscar Coldharbour."

The name hit me sideways. Coldharbour. Even out here, even living half my life on the water, you knew that name if you knew anything about the Reckoning—the captain who sailed into a monsoon chasing a fortune and never came home, whose granddaughter used to show up every few years asking questions nobody wanted answered. I'd read the newspaper clippings. I'd read all of them.

"You're kidding me," I said.

"About which part?"

"Your grandfather captained this ship."

"And?" Her chin came up. There it was—the bravado, the thing people wear when they're terrified and hoping armor reads as confidence. I know the outfit well; I own several.

"So you're not looting. You're grave-robbing your own family." I picked my way closer along the terrace edge. "That's almost poetic. It's also still my claim."

"Your claim." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ines, look around. Look at—" she gestured at the dark, at the timbers ghosting up out of the silt in ranks, ribs of a beast that had lain down here to die "—this. Do you really think a piece of paper makes this yours? My grandfather drowned for this ship. Maybe literally drowned, maybe worse—we never found him, we never found the cargo, we never found anything except questions. And you want to talk about claims?"

"Yes," I said. "Because grief doesn't hold legal weight, and neither does yours."

She came at me then. I'd like to say I was surprised, but I'd been waiting for it since I walked in—people like Oscar Coldharbour, people running from creditors or ghosts or both, they always swing eventually. She grabbed for the crowbar; I kicked it skidding across the terrace into a tide pool. She shoved me; I caught her wrist and we went down together into ankle-deep water, all elbows and fury and lamps rolling wild across the ceiling, two shadows fighting over a graveyard.

It was pathetic, honestly. Two grown women brawling on top of a dead ship while the ocean held its breath outside. She got a handful of my jacket. I got a knee somewhere effective. We rolled apart, soaked, breathing hard, and she scrambled up first with a rock in her hand and me reaching for the knife I suddenly remembered leaving on the boat—

And that's when the sound started.

At first I thought it was thunder, but thunder comes from above, and this came from behind us—from the entrance, from the sea. A low, gathering roar, the kind of sound water makes when it stops being patient. My father's voice in my memory, calm as a weather report: *when you hear the sea come back, you count, girl. You count and you run.*

I turned. My lamp found the cave mouth.

It was already shrinking.

"Get up," I said. All the fight gone out of my voice, which scared her more than the rock did. "Get up *now*. The tide's turning."

"It's supposed to be low for another hour—" she started, and then the water hit.

Not gradually. The sea came back the way debts do—all at once, with interest. It poured across the outer terrace in a brown sheet, swallowed the first pool, the second, climbing the steps we'd walked down like it was reading our names off a list. Cold spray reached us where we stood and I watched Oscar Coldharbour's face do the arithmetic faster than mine did: the entrance was thirty meters away, the water was moving faster than any person could swim through a collapsing surge channel, and between us and daylight there was now a wall of ocean doing forty knots.

We ran anyway. Of course we ran. I led, because it was my cave and my father's charts and if anyone was going to die here it was going to be me knowing exactly where. We got twenty meters. The water got the rest. I felt it take my feet out from under me, heard her shout, and then we weren't running anymore—we were being carried, tumbled, slammed against stone, my lamp gone dark, her scream torn sideways by the roar, and the whole world was brown violence and the taste of salt and blood.

Then, as fast as it had come, the surge set us down.

I came up coughing in water to my chest, wedged against a rock rib I couldn't see, and for a long moment there was only the sound—the sea filling the cave mouth behind us, thick and total, a door closing. When my eyes cleared enough to make out shapes, I saw the last light from outside go green, then gray, then nothing.

Sealed. We were sealed in.

Beside me, gasping, drenched, her headlamp cracked but still throwing a weak yellow cone, Oscar Coldharbour stared at the wall of water where the entrance used to be. Her hands were shaking. So were mine, though I kept them underwater where she couldn't see.

"Tell me there's another way out," she said.

"There's a channel system through the back," I said. "My father mapped part of it. Before he died mapping it."

"Before he—" She stopped. Recalculated. "How far?"

"Nobody knows. He didn't finish."

The sea boomed against the entrance, patient as interest accruing. Somewhere deeper in the dark, water was rising through channels I could hear but not see, and I thought about air, and hours, and my father's last log entry, and I hated her—for the footprints, the crowbar, the name, all of it—and I hated myself more for the thought that arrived next, calm and clear:

*Well. At least I won't die alone in here.*

Oscar turned her lamp toward the interior, toward the wreck sleeping in the rising dark, and I watched her make whatever decision people like her make.

"We should keep moving," she said. "While there's still floor."

Against every instinct I owned, I followed her.
