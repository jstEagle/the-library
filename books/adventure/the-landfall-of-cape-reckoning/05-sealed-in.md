## 5. Sealed In

### Oscar

The sound of the sea coming back was not a sound. It was a verdict.

I'd heard it before, in smaller ways—the click of a cell door, the pause before a doctor says *unfortunately*, the cheerful lilt of Silas Vane's voice on the phone saying *no rush at all, Oscar, we're family*. But never this loud, and never with this much salt water behind it. The cave mouth that had gaped open like a miracle forty minutes ago was now half-swallowed by a wall of gray-green water, and through it came a roar like the whole Atlantic clearing its throat to say something rude.

"Move!" The stranger—Marlowe, she'd said, before we got down to the business of trying to drown each other—grabbed my arm with fingers like mooring cleats and hauled me deeper into the cave.

"I'm not leaving the ledger—" I started.

"It's not going anywhere. You are, if you stand there admiring the view." She shoved me past a shelf of rock slick with weed, and I went, because my legs had apparently decided she was in charge without consulting me.

Behind us the water hit the entrance with a boom I felt in my back teeth. Spray sheeted across the gap where daylight had been, and then the daylight itself was gone—not dimmed, *gone*, replaced by the white churn of foam and then nothing at all. Blackness. Total, absolute, the kind of dark you get only underground and underwater and, apparently, in caves that eat storm chasers.

My headlamp clicked on under my hand. Hers did too, a second away. Two cones of light swinging over wet stone, and between them, breathing hard, two women who hated each other slightly less than they feared the ocean.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something and my mouth has never once waited for permission. "That could have gone better."

Marlowe didn't answer. She was looking at the waterline below us—the ledge we stood on rose maybe eight feet above a channel of black water that hadn't been there when I'd walked in dry-shod. Even in the last few minutes I could see it climbing. Slow, but steady. The tide didn't hurry. It had never once needed to hurry in four billion years.

"How high does it go?" I asked.

She turned her light toward the ceiling, which was lower than I liked. "Depends which channel's feeding it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Great. A woman of few words, all of them bad news. I looked back the way we'd come, at the wall of stone where the cave mouth used to be, and felt something cold settle into my chest that had nothing to do with the spray. Six hours. That was the slack window the old man at the fuel dock had laughed about—*the sea gives it back, girl, but she always takes it home again*. Six hours of exposed cave, and I'd spent forty minutes of it finding the wreck and twenty of those fighting a salvager who thought I was robbing her.

Which left, by my math, five hours to find a way out that didn't exist yet.

"The timbers," Marlowe said suddenly. Her light swung inland, deeper into the cave, away from the drowned entrance. "The Reckoning's hull. If her stern section drove this far in during the breakup, the ship herself might have punched a channel through. Old wrecks do that—they act like a wedge."

"You've done this before."

"I've been looking for this cave for nine years." There was something raw under the flat delivery, quickly buried. "I know what ships do when they die."

We picked our way inward, over rock that had never seen sun and grew things I didn't want to name. My boots were soaked, my jacket was soaked, and somewhere in the last ten minutes my phone had given up its signal and most of its dignity. I checked it anyway. No bars. Of course no bars. The universe has a sense of humor; it just isn't a kind one.

The water followed us. Not fast—but following. I kept checking the black channel beside our ledge and telling myself it wasn't rising, and then checking again and admitting it was. An inch every few minutes. Maybe more. Behind us, the sealed entrance boomed once, twice, like something enormous knocking politely and getting no answer.

Then Marlowe stopped so abruptly I walked into her.

"Listen."

I listened. Over the boom and hiss of the tide, there was another sound—a low, steady rushing, like wind through a keyhole, coming from ahead and above.

"Air," she said. "Moving air means a second opening. Or a pocket big enough to breathe off."

"Or it means the cave is exhaling right before it swallows us."

"Do you always narrate your own doom?"

"Only when nobody else will do it justice."

Something happened to her face in the lamplight—I want to say it was almost a smile, but it might have been indigestion. She climbed. I climbed after her, because the alternative was standing still and becoming a statistic, and I have enough debts already.

### Ines

The Coldharbour woman climbed like she argued—loudly, gracelessly, and somehow arriving anyway. I gave her thirty seconds of silent contempt and then, when she slipped on a shelf of barnacle-crusted rock and nearly took my lamp out with her flailing elbow, revised it upward out of pure respect for her refusal to fall.

"Left hand," I said. "There's a crack. Use it."

She used it. We gained the upper gallery and the air changed—drier, warmer, moving. The sound of the sea fell away beneath us, muffled now, a heartbeat instead of a roar. My light found the reason: a chamber, wide as a chapel, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor a slope of shingle and broken timber. And along the far wall, stacked against the stone like driftwood arranged by a careful giant—

Hull planking. Dozens of lengths of it. Copper-sheathed. Stamped.

I knew the stamp before my light fully resolved it. I'd drawn it from memory a hundred times, in margins, on napkins, in the dust of a hundred decks. *RECKONING — PORTHOLLOW YARD — 1887.*

Nine years. Four salvage licenses, two busted dive boats, one father at the bottom of the sea, and here it was, and the person standing next to me was a debt-ridden tourist who'd stumbled onto it chasing weather like a kid chasing ice cream trucks.

"This is my claim," I said. It came out quieter than I meant it to. Everything about this moment was quieter than I'd rehearsed it, back when I'd imagined confronting trespassers as a stern and victorious professional rather than a trapped one.

"Your claim." She swept her light across the chamber, generous with other people's property. "Filed where? With the Mermaid Court? Because last I checked, a cave that exists twice a century doesn't come with paperwork."

"With the maritime registry. Contested claims pend adjudication, and until then possession—" I stopped myself. Arguing law with her while the tide climbed was like arguing menu options on a sinking ferry.

"Possession is nine-tenths," she finished. "Yeah, my grandfather's partners said something similar, I imagine."

That landed strangely. I let it go, because the water was letting neither of us go anywhere, and because the chamber floor was doing something I needed to see.

The shingle sloped down toward a darker hollow at the chamber's heart—and the hollow was dry. Bone-dry. Above it, the ceiling dipped into a limestone dome, smooth and pale, and the air moving through it smelled of stone and old wood and nothing else. An air pocket, fed by some fissure, sitting over the lowest point of the chamber like a held breath.

And in the middle of that dry hollow, half-buried in shingle, sat a strongbox.

Not gold. Not coin. Iron-strapped oak, small, the kind of box a captain keeps papers in. It had been dry for forty years, or near enough, and my hands were on it before my pride caught up.

Coldharbour crouched opposite me. For once she didn't say anything. Her lamp held steady on the box while I worked the lock—it was rusted shut, and I broke it with the pry bar I keep on my belt for exactly the occasions when the world refuses to open—and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were books.

Ledgers. Two of them, plus a folio of letters tied with wire. And on the top ledger's cover, in faded iron-gall ink, a name written in a hand I recognized from a photograph I'd carried since I was nineteen:

*E. Coldharbour.*

The woman across from me made a sound I'd only ever heard from people at gravesides.

### Oscar

His handwriting. Forty years, and I'd forgotten the specific architecture of it—the tall ascenders, the way he crossed his t's like he was slashing them, the little hook he put on his e's. I used to forge it for school notes. I would have known it blindfolded, by smell.

Elias Coldharbour. My grandfather. Missing, presumed drowned, presumed ruined, presumed *careless*—that was the word the family history settled on, the polite word. Careless with his cargo, careless with his crew, careless with the fortune that evaporated and left my mother to grow up poor and proud about it. He sailed the Reckoning out of Porthollow in a storm in 1984 and neither he nor the ship nor the cargo was ever seen again, and the insurance paid his partners, and the partners built warehouses, and then banks, and then lending offices with cheerful signs in the windows, and one of those offices lent money to his granddaughter at rates that would make a loan shark blush.

I opened the ledger with hands that shook, and I am not a person whose hands shake. Ask anyone. Ask the Vanes, who've never once seen me flinch, because flinching costs interest.

The entries were cargo manifests. Timber, tin, specie—coin, actual gold coin, listed lot by lot. Dated three weeks before the storm. And against each entry, in the margin, in that slashing hand, annotations:

*Received short 40 crates — T.V. says "shrinkage," says I'll be paid for silence or buried for noise.*

*T.V.* Thomas Vane. The patriarch. The grandfather of the men who currently owned my life.

I turned pages. The handwriting deteriorated as the dates advanced, tighter, harder, pressing through the paper. The last full entry was dated the day before the storm:

*They mean to take her out heavy and scuttle her insured. I have proof of everything. If I don't come back, it's because they decided the sea is cheaper than a courtroom. E.C.*

Below that, one line, different ink, hurried:

*If anyone finds this — I did not lose the Reckoning. She was taken from me. Tell my daughter I was careful after all.*

Tell my daughter. My mother, who died believing her father had been a fool.

I don't remember deciding to laugh. It came out of me anyway, ugly and echoing off the limestone dome, and Ines Marlowe watched me do it with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"They didn't lose the cargo," I said. "They stole it. They stole the *ship*. They let him drown and collected the insurance and built—" I gestured at nothing, at everything, at a financial empire forty years deep. "The Vanes. The money the Vanes lend out, the money I owe them, it's *his*. It's his blood money, and they've been charging me interest on my own inheritance."

The echo died. The water below us made its small patient sounds.

"He was careful," Marlowe said at last. Quietly. Like she was reporting a fact she'd verified personally.

"Yes."

"My father dove for this wreck for eleven summers. The Vanes' lawyers told him the site was worthless and dangerous and that a man of his background should know his limits." She paused. "He didn't know his limits. That was the problem. He knew them fine. He just didn't respect them."

It was the longest speech she'd produced, and it cost her something—I could see the cost sitting on her like ballast. Two griefs, forty years apart, sitting in a dry pocket of a drowning cave, and between them a box of paper worth more than both our lives and worth absolutely nothing if the water reached it.

"We need to move these," I said. "Higher. Dry."

"We need to move *us* higher. Water's at the base of the slope."

She was right. The shingle's lower edge had gone dark and gleaming while we read. An hour, maybe less, before the pocket flooded. I looked at the dome overhead, at the fissure the moving air whispered through—too narrow to climb, probably, but probably wasn't good enough anymore.

"Your charges," I said. "You're a salvager. You carry cutting charges."

"For controlled demolition. Under supervision. With permits."

"Ines." First time I'd used her name. It hung there between us. "The tide doesn't care about permits."

She looked at the water. At the ledger in my hands. At the fissure. And I watched nine years of solitary obsession collide with the arithmetic of survival, and I watched the obsession lose, narrowly, the way it loses in all the best people.

"There's a charge rated for breving hull plate," she said slowly. "Set at the thinnest point of that wall—" her light touched the eastern side of the chamber, where the rock was fractured, veined with quartz, *thin*— "it might open us to the cliff face above the high-water line. Might."

"Might is my favorite word," I said. "I've built a whole life on it."

"That explains a great deal about you."

"And you'll notice I'm still alive."

"So far." She was already unpacking the charge, hands moving with a competence that made my own look decorative. "Detonator's wet. Give me ten minutes to dry it and rig the wire."

"Ten minutes." I tucked the ledgers inside my jacket, against my chest, where they sat warm as a heartbeat. "I'll hold the tide to its schedule."

For the first time since the water sealed us in, Ines Marlowe laughed—one short, disbelieving note, gone as fast as it came. But it came. And in a cave filling with the sea, with a dead man's testimony pressed against my ribs and a stranger wiring explosives ten feet away, it was the strangest sound of all, and the best.

Above us, through the fissure, the moving air changed pitch—cooler, faster, tasting faintly of rain.

The monsoon had arrived early.
