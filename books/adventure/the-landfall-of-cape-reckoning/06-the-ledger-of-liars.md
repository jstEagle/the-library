## 6. The Ledger of Liars

The lamp Ines had salvaged from her ketch's stores threw a circle of light no wider than a dinner table, and inside that circle sat the ledger, bound in leather gone the color and texture of driftwood, its spine cracked like an old man's knuckles.

I didn't want to open it.

That sounds absurd, I know. I'd crawled through a flooded sea cave, nearly drowned twice, and traded blows with a stranger over salvage rights to get my hands on it. But there's a particular kind of fear that lives on the far side of wanting something. You spend so long chasing the thing that the chase becomes the point, and when the thing finally sits still in front of you—when it stops being a quest and becomes a fact—you realize you have no idea who you'll be once you've read it.

Forty years, my family had called Elias Coldharbour's disappearance an accident. A storm, a miscalculation, a man too proud to turn back. My mother had built her whole grief around that story the way you build a house around a load-bearing wall. Pull it out and everything comes down.

"Open it or don't," Ines said. She was sitting across the air pocket from me with her back against the stone, arms crossed, radiating the patience of a woman who had waited seven years for this cave and could evidently wait seven more. "The tide's coming back through the inner channels either way. Your grandfather's been dead four decades. He can wait for your feelings."

"You're a real comfort, you know that?"

"I've been told." She nodded at the book. "But she's right about one thing—the paper won't survive another soaking. If you're going to read it, read it now."

*She.* Right. The wreck. The Reckoning. Somewhere behind us, down a passage we'd marked with a spare dive light, her hold still held cargo that had made three families rich and one family dead. And somewhere in that hold—or so Ines claimed, with the flat certainty of someone who'd spent years reconstructing a rumor—was the ship's manifest and correspondence chest.

The ledger had been in the chest. Along with a brass sextant I recognized from photographs on my grandmother's mantel, which is the kind of thing that hits you sideways when you're not braced for it.

I opened the cover.

The first pages were boring, and I've never been so grateful for boredom in my life. Shipping entries. Dates. Tonnage figures in a cramped, slanting hand I recognized instantly because I'd grown up seeing it on the flyleaf of every book my grandfather ever owned—*E.C., Cape Reckoning Station*—and there was something obscene about how ordinary it looked. Just numbers. Just work. A man doing his job four days before the sea took him.

Or before men took him. I didn't know yet which. I turned the page anyway.

* * *

Here is what I knew going in, the version I'd been raised on: Elias Coldharbour ran a salvage cooperative out of Cape Reckoning, three partners, one ship. In the autumn of his last year, the Reckoning went out after a wreck rumored to carry bullion—insurance gold off a merchantman that had foundered on the outer shoals. The Reckoning came back light. Elias went out again alone, against weather, and never came back. The official finding was recklessness. The family finding was worse: that he'd found something on that second trip and meant to keep it, and the sea had punished his greed. Either way, the Coldharbour name came out of it stained. We'd spent forty years living quietly under the weight of a story everyone in the district half-believed.

The ledger told a different story. It told it in the most damning way possible, which is to say: in his own handwriting, dated, initialed, and cross-referenced.

The first anomaly was small. An entry where the recovered tonnage didn't match the insurance claim filed by the cooperative—a discrepancy of eleven crates, logged as *damaged beyond recovery, jettisoned*. Except two pages later, in what was clearly a private memorandum set apart from the official record, Elias had written:

*R. insists the crates went down with the longboat. V. backs him. H. says nothing, which from H. is an answer. I counted the longboat's draft at the dock. It rode six inches low. Six inches of draft is eleven crates of specie, and every man on that dock knows it and none will say it.*

R. V. H.

I sat with those initials for a long time. The lamp hissed. Water moved somewhere in the dark behind us—that slow, muscular sound the tide makes when it's filling channels it carved for exactly this purpose—and I remember thinking, with a strange detached clarity: *six inches of draft.* Forty years of family shame, and it had come down to six inches of measured draft, written in pencil, in a book nobody was supposed to find.

"Problem?" Ines asked.

"I don't know yet." I kept reading.

The memoranda got worse. Over the following weeks Elias documented, with the meticulousness of a man building a case he hoped he'd never have to argue, a pattern of shorted shares, falsified manifests, and cargo that appeared in market towns he'd never delivered to. He wrote names now, not initials. Rourke. Halloran. And Vane.

Vane.

I read the name four times before it landed, and when it landed it landed like a dropped anchor—sudden, total, dragging everything down with it. Because I knew that name. Everyone on this coast knew that name. The Vanes owned half the waterfront from here to the capital. They owned the chandleries and the insurance houses and, as of eight months ago, they owned me—my boat, my rig, my next five years of earnings, all of it signed over in cheerful pink ink to two smiling brothers who'd bought up their grandfather's loan book along with everything else their family had ever touched.

My debt. My cheerful, patient, blood-priced debt.

I did the arithmetic without wanting to. The Vanes' founding fortune—the one their patriarch had parlayed into docks and banks and political favors—dated to precisely this era. Precisely this coast. There were portraits in the Vane brothers' office, oil paintings of a heavyset man with side whiskers and a smile like a closed door, captioned *Founder*. I'd sat under that portrait while signing my life away, and I'd thought, *self-made man,* and felt the peculiar envy people like me are trained to feel toward people like that.

Self-made. Made out of what?

Out of eleven crates of insurance gold that rode six inches low in a longboat. Out of a partnership bled dry by lies. And finally—because a man who notices things, a man who counts drafts at the dock and writes it all down in pencil—is a dangerous man, and dangerous men get handled.

The last memorandum was dated nine days before Elias vanished. The handwriting had changed. Not degraded—changed. Tighter. Angrier. Each letter pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

*I have the proof entire. Manifests, drafts, witness statements from the deckhand Marlowe saw fit to dismiss. Tomorrow I take the Reckoning out to move the evidence somewhere safe, and then I go to the magistrate. If anything happens to me, let it be known: I was not reckless. I was certain. There is a difference, and the difference is worth more than everything they stole.*

Marlowe.

I looked up. Ines was watching me with an expression I couldn't parse in the lamplight—something between wariness and a kind of grim recognition, like a woman watching a fire start in a house she used to live in.

"The deckhand," I said. "Marlowe."

"My great-grandfather." Her voice was flat. "Dismissed for asking questions. He spent the rest of his life telling anyone who'd listen that Elias Coldharbour was murdered, and getting laughed out of every room on this coast. My family's been trying to clear both their names ever since. Seven years I've been looking for this cave." She nodded at the ledger. "You're holding the reason my father died diving for a wreck the charts say doesn't exist."

I should have said something. Some bridge-building sentence, some acknowledgment that our families' griefs had been moored to the same anchor for four generations. What I actually said was nothing, because the rage had arrived.

* * *

I need to describe the rage properly, because it wasn't what I expected.

I'd imagined, over the years, that if I ever learned my grandfather's death was something other than accident, I'd feel vindication. Closure. Some clean emotion with a name and a use. Instead what I felt was heat with no outlet—a furnace stoked and sealed, burning everything inside itself. Because here was the truth, sitting in my lap in fading pencil: my grandfather hadn't been reckless. He'd been *right*. He'd been right, and careful, and brave, and he'd taken the proof of his own innocence out onto the water to protect it, and the men he'd trusted had followed him out there—or arranged for the weather to do their work for them—and the coast had spent forty years telling his granddaughter's family a story about greed.

And the men who told that story. The men whose grandfathers got rich on the telling.

I owed them money.

That was the fact that broke something open in my chest. Not the murder—murder is old news, four decades old, past grieving, past avenging in any way that matters. But the debt. Every cheerful phone call. Every *no rush, Oscar, we're friends here*, delivered in that warm brogue while the interest compounded like floodwater. Every month of my life bent around payments to the descendants of the men who killed my grandfather and buried his name under a lie. I hadn't borrowed money. I'd inherited a crime, and been billed for it.

I stood up too fast in the low air pocket and cracked my head on a shelf of rock, which is the sort of detail I'd normally leave out of a story except that it's true, and the pain was clarifying. I stood there bleeding a little, holding a lamp, wanting to hit something, and the only things in reach were stone, seawater, and a woman who'd just lost her own ancestor to the same lie.

"Say it," Ines said quietly.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're chewing on. Say it out loud. It keeps better in the open."

So I said it. All of it. The Vanes, the loans, the pink-ink signatures, the portrait in the office with its founder's smile. How I'd spent eight months hating myself for falling into their net—reckless Oscar Coldharbour, storm-chaser with more courage than sense, another casualty of her own bad judgment—and how it turned out the net had been woven before I was born, by the same hands, for the same family, and I'd simply swum into it on schedule.

"They didn't lend me money," I heard myself say. "They collected rent on a grave."

Ines was silent for a moment. Then: "My father said something once, before his last dive. He said the Vanes don't remember being thieves. That's the worst part. Three generations of spending stolen money until the stealing dries out of the story entirely, and what's left is just—wealth. Respectability. Men who shake your hand and mean it kindly." She picked up the lamp and held it between us. "Corvo works for men like that. Which means Corvo will come looking for this book eventually, whether he knows why or not."

"Let him come."

"That's the rage talking."

"Obviously it's the rage talking. I'm entitled to some rage. I'm standing in my grandfather's grave, Ines. This whole cave is a grave. The water outside is a grave. And somewhere above us the tide is turning, and in a day or two I'm supposed to sail back into a world where those men own my boat and my future, and I'm supposed to *negotiate*—" My voice cracked, which humiliated me, so I stopped talking.

Ines let the silence sit. She was good at that; I was learning it about her. She didn't fill quiet spaces with comfort. She let them be rooms you had to walk across yourself.

Eventually she said, "There's a page you haven't read. The last one. Under the back cover."

I looked down at the ledger. I hadn't noticed anything loose. But when I worked my fingers under the rotted back board, a folded sheet slid out—thinner paper, different stock, folded twice and brown with age. I opened it by lamplight.

It was a letter. Unsent. Addressed to *My daughter, if I do not come home.*

My mother had been six years old.

I read the first line only. I read it maybe ten times. *If you are reading this, then the sea has done what the Vanes could not do honestly, and I am sorry to leave you a name that liars will try to keep dirty.*

Then I folded it closed again, carefully, along its original creases, and I held it against my sternum like ballast, and I did not cry, because the water rising in the inner channels had already claimed enough of my family's tears and I refused to add to the collection.

"What now?" Ines asked.

"Now?" I tucked the letter inside my jacket, next to my skin, where the storm couldn't get it. "Now we get out of here alive. Then I make sure every soul on this coast reads that ledger out loud. Then—" and here the furnace finally found its flue, and the rage went somewhere useful, cold and directional and steady— "then the Vanes can explain to a magistrate why their founder's fortune matches, crate for crate, eleven boxes of insurance gold that never sank."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. "The tide turns in four hours. We'll need the slack to blast the entrance."

"Then we'd better hope your charges are as good as your timing."

Outside, the monsoon that had chased me down this coast was still two days out, gathering itself over open water like a debt accruing interest. Inside, the sea was already climbing back through the channels toward the wreck, patient as any creditor, ready to reclaim what the tide had lent.

We had four hours, one leaking cache of explosives, and the truth.

For the first time in eight months, I intended to use all three.
