## 7. Escape at Slack Water

The tide came back at four in the morning, and I heard it before I saw it.

That's the thing about water under pressure—it doesn't announce itself politely. It seeps. It whispers through cracks you didn't know existed, and by the time it's talking, it's already shouting. I'd spent eleven years listening to the sea for a living, and I knew that sound the way other people know their mother's voice on the phone.

I was sitting with my back against the Reckoning's hull when the whisper started, somewhere down in the flooded channel that ran along the cave's eastern wall. Oscar was asleep—or pretending to be—curled up on a shelf of dry rock near the entrance we no longer had, wrapped in her storm jacket like it could argue with physics.

"Hey." My voice echoed wrong in here. Too flat. "Coldharbour. Wake up."

She surfaced fast, which I'll give her credit for. No groggy blinking, no where-am-I. Her eyes went straight to my face, then to the dark water below us, reading me the way she probably read barometers.

"How long?"

"Hours. Maybe three. The tide turns around seven, but the cave fills long before slack." I clicked on my headlamp and swept it across the chamber—the wreck lying on its side like a beached whale, ribs green with forty years of growth, the air pocket above us maybe eight meters across and shrinking. "We're not waiting for the sea to change its mind."

"So what's your plan? Swim?" She said it lightly, but I heard the fear underneath, doing its best impression of a joke.

"I brought charges."

She stared at me. "You brought *explosives* into a sea cave."

"I'm a salvager. I bring explosives everywhere. It's how you say hello to a shipwreck." I hauled my kit bag over and started laying out what remained: two cutting charges of C-4 equivalent, det cord, a firing system I'd built myself from parts that had no business working together. "There's a chimney in the northwest corner. I found it while you were sleeping off your heroics. It goes up—air shaft, old blowhole, something. It's choked with rockfall, but rock is just an argument, and I have the winning rebuttal."

"You're going to blast our way out through a blowhole."

"I'm going to blast our way out through a blowhole," I confirmed, "at slack water, when the pressure differential is smallest, and we are going to do it fast, because the moment that hole opens, this whole cave becomes a drain. Everything in here goes out with the tide—including us, if we're stupid about timing."

Oscar looked at the water again. Then at the ledger, wrapped in plastic inside her jacket, pressed against her chest like a second heartbeat. She'd barely let go of it since we found it. I understood that more than she knew.

"And if you're wrong about the chimney?"

"Then we drown in a room full of gold-adjacent history, and honestly? There are worse epitaphs." I tossed her a spool of det cord. "Make yourself useful. Weave this through the crack line—loose pattern, don't pack it tight. We want the rock to shrug, not sneeze."

She caught it one-handed. "You've done this before."

"Eleven years. My father did it for twenty before that." I didn't elaborate, and she didn't ask, and that was the first thing either of us had given each other for free.

* * *

We worked through the small hours, and I'll say this for Oscar Coldharbour: she was useless with a boat hook and worse with knots, but she followed instructions like her life depended on it, because it did. She threaded det cord through the chimney's choke of fallen stone while I set the main charge at the base, tamping it with wet sand so the force would go up instead of out. The chimney breathed cold air down at us, faintly, like something alive on the other side.

At six-forty, the water below us had climbed two meters. The air pocket had gone from a cathedral to a closet. My ears were popping.

"Slack's in twenty minutes," I said. "After that, the tide starts running back out, and if we haven't opened that hole by then, the outflow will suck everything—including our air—down and out. So we fire the charge at slack exactly, and we climb, and we do not stop to admire the view."

"Any chance," Oscar said, checking her watch against mine, "that you mention the odds?"

"The odds are that I'm very good at my job and moderately good at math, and both of those say we live."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give." I ran the wire one last time, checked the detonator, and put the firing box in her hands. "You do it."

Her head came up sharply. "Why me?"

"Because if it misfires, I need my hands free to fix it, and because—" I stopped myself. Because my father always said the person who fires the charge owns the outcome, and I wasn't ready to explain what that had cost him. "Because you wanted to be here. You anchored up and walked into a cave against every instinct you own. Finish it."

She looked at the firing box for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and held it against her chest next to the ledger, and I thought: there's a woman collecting things that weigh nothing and everything.

"Tide's turning," I said. "On my mark. Three. Two. One."

* * *

The explosion in an enclosed space is not a sound. It's a decision the world makes about you.

The charge went off and the cave decided we were leaving. Rock came down and rock went up, and the chimney above us opened like a throat clearing forty years of phlegm, and then daylight—not much, a gray coin of it, rain-hazed—but daylight, real daylight, pouring down through ten meters of blown-out blowhole.

And the water started to move.

"Go!" I shoved her toward the rubble slope. "Climb! Don't look at the water!"

She climbed. I climbed behind her, and the sea came up beneath us like a hand closing, and I felt the pull of the outflow grab my ankle as I reached the lip of the chimney—felt it try to keep me, the way the sea keeps everything eventually—and then Oscar's hand locked around my wrist and hauled, and I came out of the cave mouth gasping onto wet rock under an open gray sky, with the monsoon wind hitting my face like a slap from an old friend.

We lay there on the cliff top, side by side, breathing like bellows, while the storm built itself around us.

* * *

Oscar's boat was dead.

We saw it from the cliff edge, or what was left of it—her rig had dragged its anchor in the surge and gone onto the rocks below the cape, hull stove in, mast snapped, being taken apart by the surf with the patience only the sea has. Forty thousand dollars of storm-chasing equipment, becoming flotsam in real time.

Oscar didn't say anything for a long time. The rain came down harder. Somewhere behind us, the blowhole exhaled spray like the cave was smoking a cigarette.

"Well," she finally said. "That's the debt settled, anyway. Now I owe the Vanes money *and* a boat."

"You owe them nothing," I said, and then realized how that sounded, and didn't take it back.

She turned and looked at me. Rain ran off her chin. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing. Forget it." I jerked my head south along the cliff path. "My ketch is in the cove past the point. The Marlowe. She leaks, but she leaks slowly, and I have a bilge pump with opinions. Storm's making landfall tonight—you can't be on this coast without shelter, and you can't afford a harbor. So you're coming with me. Temporary. Until the weather breaks."

"Why?" she asked. Just like that. No armor on it. "You fought me in that cave. You called me a looter. Why haul me out now?"

I watched the surf dismantle her boat and thought about my father going down off this same cape with a tank of bad air and a head full of the Reckoning's legend, and I thought about the ledger pressed against her ribs, and I said:

"Because whatever's in that book, it killed people I care about too. And because nobody should face the Vane brothers alone. Believe me. I've read about them."

She studied me for a long five seconds, the way she'd study a pressure chart—looking for the lie in the data. Then she stuck out her hand, rain hammering between us.

"Temporary," she said.

"Temporary," I agreed, and shook it, and her grip was colder than the rain and twice as steady.

We walked south along the cliffs, two drowned rats with one secret between us, while behind us the tide rose to swallow the cave mouth whole—and ahead, far out on the gray water, I thought I saw a boat running lights it had no business running, in weather no sane skipper would sail.

I told myself it was a fishing boat.

I told myself a lot of things that morning.
