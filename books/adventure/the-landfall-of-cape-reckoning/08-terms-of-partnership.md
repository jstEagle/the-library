## 8. Terms of Partnership

### INES

The cove had no name on any chart I owned, which was exactly why I chose it. A horseshoe of black rock, a fringe of mangroves thick enough to hide a church, and water the color of strong tea where the river bled into the sea. I brought the *Magpie* in on the last of the flood tide and dropped both anchors in a V, because if we were going to be sitting ducks, we were going to be well-anchored sitting ducks.

Oscar Coldharbour's boat was dying behind us, taking water through a hull wound the size of my forearm, and she watched it settle on its mooring line with the expression of a woman watching a dog she'd never liked get put down.

"She'll float till morning," she said.

"She'll float till morning," I agreed. "Then she'll be a reef."

"Good. Reefs are useful."

I decided then that I could probably tolerate her for another day, which was more than I'd have bet on twelve hours earlier, back when we were trying to drown each other in a collapsing cave.

I made coffee on the single burner because it was the only civilized thing left to do. She took the mug without thanks but held it with both hands like it was warm and mattered, and I filed that away under *data*.

"So," she said. "Terms."

"Straight to business."

"I owe two men money who collect in blood, and you've got a claim staked on a cave full of dead men's cargo. I don't think either of us can afford small talk."

Fair. I sat across from her in the cramped saloon, the lantern swinging on its gimbal, and laid out what I wanted before she could lay out what she wanted, because whoever speaks second is always negotiating from behind.

"The wreck is mine," I said. "Salvage rights, whatever's down there that isn't paper. My father spent eleven years looking for that cave. He died in the water off Cape Reckoning with his lungs full of silt and the Reckoning still a rumor. I finished his search. The claim follows the search."

"And the ledger?"

"That's your grandfather's ghost. I don't traffic in ghosts."

She looked at me over the rim of her mug for a long moment. There's a particular look people get when they're deciding whether to believe you, and hers said she'd been lied to by professionals recently and had gotten good at spotting amateurs.

"You expect me to believe you dove into a flooded cave system, nearly died twice, fought me over timbers stamped with a name — all for rusted fittings and copper sheeting?"

"Copper is money. Fittings are money. And yes, I fought you, because you came at me swinging a dive light like a club." I leaned forward. "Here's what you keep missing, Coldharbour: I've been out here nine years. Nine years of chart work, tide tables, dead ends, and one very expensive sonar unit I'm still paying off. You stumbled onto that cave in an afternoon because the ocean hiccupped. If salvage law means anything, it means something to people like me, not people like you."

"People like me."

"Storm chasers. Tourists with barometers."

That landed. I saw it land. Her jaw did a thing, and her eyes went flat and bright at once, the way water does right before it freezes.

"My grandfather died in that cave," she said quietly. "Or near it. Or because of it. Forty years, nobody knew. I found him yesterday, Marlowe. I found his handwriting. I found proof he was murdered by business partners and fed to the tide. So no, I'm not going to hand you the site and walk away whistling. What I want is simple: the ledger goes public. Every page. Every name. Broadcast, printed, nailed to the courthouse door if it comes to that. The Vanes' fortune gets dragged into daylight and their empire rots in front of witnesses."

"And your debt?"

"Gone or not gone, depending on how fast justice moves versus how fast Corvo moves." She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "I'm aware of the flaw in the plan."

It was the first honest thing she'd said since I'd met her, and I hated how much it moved me. Because I knew about debts. Not money ones — mine were paid in other currencies. But I knew what it was to owe people who smiled while they sharpened the knife.

"Your plan has a second flaw," I said. "You make the ledger public, the Vanes go down, and every lawyer, historian, and treasure hunter on this coast descends on that cave within the month. Your grandfather's ghost becomes a tourist attraction. My father's dive site becomes a parking lot for dive boats. That's what public means, Coldharbour. It means everyone."

"Better than it meaning the Vanes."

"Marginally."

We sat there while the anchor light swung and the mangroves ticked against the hull, and I'll admit I was doing arithmetic I didn't like. Because she was right about one thing: the moment word spread — and word always spreads; it spreads faster than fire in a dry season — the Vanes would come. Not the brothers themselves, maybe. Corvo. Corvo and however many quiet men he needed. And they wouldn't come for the ledger alone. They'd come for everything: the ledger, the gold if there was gold, and the two women who knew where it all slept.

Neither of us would survive that alone. That wasn't sentiment. That was tonnage.

"Here's my counter," I said. "The ledger goes public — eventually. After we've documented the site properly. Photographs, survey, a formal salvage declaration filed with the maritime authority, with my name as salvor-in-command and yours as claimant of record for the documentary material. We do it legal, we do it first, and we do it before anyone else even knows there's something to steal. Then the truth comes out, and when it does, it comes out armored. With paperwork. With precedent. With my name on the wreck so no court in this country can hand it to the Vanes' estate when it all shakes loose."

Oscar turned her mug slowly on the table, thinking. I could practically hear the gears grinding against salt corrosion.

"You want to use the law," she said. "Against the family that owns half the judges."

"I want to use paper. Paper is patient. Paper outlives men like Corvo."

"And until then? Until your filing is done and the story breaks — we just sit on the biggest secret on this coast with a target painted on the mast?"

"Now you're learning."

She laughed — one short, sharp syllable, surprised out of her. And God help me, I almost smiled back.

### OSCAR

Here's the thing nobody tells you about negotiating with a salvager: they negotiate like the sea. No wasted motion, no drama, just pressure applied patiently in whichever direction finds the weakness. Ines Marlowe had been chipping at me for an hour and hadn't raised her voice once, and I was starting to understand why she'd survived nine years out here and why my boat hadn't survived one afternoon.

But I'd grown up around negotiators too. Mine just wore nicer suits and smiled more.

"Say I agree to your sequence," I said. "Documentation first, publication after. What stops you from sitting on the ledger forever? From deciding, six months from now, that the world doesn't need my grandfather's testimony — that it's leverage, and leverage is better sold than spent?"

"What stops you," she said, "from publishing tomorrow and getting us both killed before the ink dries?"

"Nothing. Nothing stops me except judgment, and you've seen my judgment. I anchored next to a vanishing tide because a barometer told me a story."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind that lives in the corners. "Yes. I've been meaning to ask about that. What kind of reading makes a person do something that stupid?"

"A once-in-a-century one. Pressure drop like nothing in forty years of records. The same forty years, as it happens, since—" I stopped. Since the last time the sea pulled that trick, the night my grandfather's partners swore up and down he'd sailed into the storm drunk and careless. "Since he disappeared."

Ines was quiet for a moment. The lantern swung. Somewhere out in the dark, a fish jumped, the slap of it loud as a door closing.

"My father had a theory about the tides," she said finally. "He used to say Cape Reckoning kept a schedule nobody had written down. That every few decades it opened its mouth." She turned her mug the way I'd turned mine, and I realized we were both holding our coffee like talismans, and that annoyed me, because it meant we had something in common, and I hadn't budgeted for that.

"Is that why you searched?" I asked. "The theory?"

"No. I searched because he died searching, and somebody had to finish it or his whole life was a joke with a bad punchline." She shrugged, a small tight motion. "The theory was just — ballast. Something to keep the search steady when the money ran out."

Two griefs, sitting across a table from each other, pretending to be a business meeting. I recognized the shape of it. I'd been wearing the same disguise for forty years myself, just tailored differently — grief dressed up as meteorology, as data, as a career chasing storms along the exact stretch of coast where Elias Coldharbour went into the water and didn't come out.

"Fine," I said. "Your sequence. Documentation, filing, then publication. But I want it in writing. Actual paper, actual signatures, witnessed by—" I gestured at the empty saloon. "Well. By the coffee pot."

"The coffee pot has excellent character references."

"And one more term." I leaned forward. "If the Vanes come — when they come — we don't split. Whatever happens, whatever they offer each of us separately, we decide together or we refuse together. Because Corvo's whole method is the fork. He offers one of us a way out and watches the other one get abandoned. I've read about men like him my whole life. I've owed men like him for three years. They don't break doors. They let you open them yourself."

Ines studied me for a long time. Outside, the wind had begun to find the cove, moving through the mangroves with a sound like slow applause, and the *Magpie* tugged gently at her anchors, restless, wanting the open water the way some people always do.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"I've had three years to think about it. Mostly at three in the morning."

"And your conclusion?"

"That the only thing worse than drowning in debt is drowning alone." I said it lightly, the way you say true things when you don't want them to weigh anything. "So. Partners? Provisional, suspicious, mutually armed partners?"

She put out her hand across the table. Hers was rough as rope, scarred across the knuckles, and she shook like she meant it — two pumps, firm, done.

"Provisional," she agreed. "Suspicious. Armed." A pause. "Though I should tell you, Coldharbour, I cheat at cards and I lie about weather."

"Noted. I should tell you I once bet a year's income on a storm cell that dissipated over open water."

"I heard about that. It's famous in certain bars."

"Wonderful. My reputation precedes me into the one partnership I actually need."

"It's not a liability." She gathered the mugs, and for a moment her back was to me, and her voice came out lower, less armored. "A gambler who bets on storms and loses is a fool. A gambler who bets on storms and keeps betting is someone who believes the sea owes her an answer. I can work with that. I've been asking the same question for nine years."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I said nothing, which between the two of us was apparently becoming a whole language.

### INES

We shook on it, and then, because neither of us trusted the other further than a thrown line, we wrote it out. Actual ink, on the back of one of Oscar's barometric charts — fitting, I thought, that our contract would be drafted on a document predicting catastrophe. Salvage rights to me, publication rights to her, joint refusal clause, mutual defense. Two signatures. The coffee pot presided.

She signed "O. Coldharbour" in a slanting, impatient hand. I signed "I. Marlowe" the way my father taught me, block letters, legible from a distance, because on the water your mark has to be readable by strangers in bad light.

"There," she said, blowing on the ink. "Legally binding, probably, in the jurisdiction of this cabin."

"In that jurisdiction I also declare myself admiral."

"Admiral Marlowe. It suits you."

"Don't push it."

I stowed the chart in the dry box under the berth — the one place on the *Magpie* that had never taken a drop of water, through two groundings and a hurricane — and that was when I noticed Oscar had gone very still, staring out the saloon window toward the cove's mouth.

"Ines."

"What."

"How many boats are supposed to be in this cove?"

I turned. The anchorage was black water and blacker rock, our two vessels and the mangrove shadows, and beyond the headland the sky was beginning to bruise with the monsoon's outer bands. One light. Far out, past the cove's throat, low on the water where a running light shouldn't be — white, steady, patient. Not fishing. Fishing boats work in patterns. This light sat still, the way a man sits still when he's watching a house.

"That's not a fisherman," I said.

"No," Oscar said. "It isn't."

I killed the lantern without being asked, and we stood together in the dark, two women who'd signed a contract ninety seconds ago, watching a single white light hold its position at the mouth of the cove like a held breath.

The storm was still a day out. Whatever that was, it had gotten here first.

"Get the ledger," I said quietly. "And Coldharbour — whatever you packed that's waterproof, pack it again."
