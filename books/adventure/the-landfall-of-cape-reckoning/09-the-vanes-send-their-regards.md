## 9. The Vanes Send Their Regards

The satellite phone sat in its cradle like a loaded thing.

I'd been staring at it for the better part of an hour, which is a stupid way to spend an hour, but there it was. Ines's ketch, the *Maren*, rode at anchor in a cove so small it didn't have a name, just a notch in the chart that some hydrographer a hundred years dead had bothered to ink in. Mangroves crowded both arms of it like gossips at a funeral wall. The monsoon had moved offshore to sulk and regroup, and what was left of the sky hung low and gray and wet, breathing on us in long slow drizzles.

Ines was aft, splicing line with the concentration of a surgeon and the charm of one too. I'd learned over the past two days that she could go four hours without speaking if the work was good enough, and that her silences weren't hostile so much as structural — load-bearing silences. Take them out and something would collapse.

The phone rang anyway.

It doesn't ring, exactly. It chirps, this polite little three-note chime that the Vane brothers had programmed into it themselves, back when they'd handed it to me along with the money and the smile. Three ascending notes. A doorbell for a door you never wanted to open.

I let it chirp twice more before I picked it up.

"Coldharbour," I said.

"Oscar!" The voice came through bright and warm as a heat lamp. Denny Vane. The younger brother, or possibly the older — nobody had ever been able to tell me, and asking felt like a question you'd regret having asked. "There she is. There's my favorite debtor. You sound wonderful. Salt air agrees with you."

"I'm hanging up now."

"Don't." Still cheerful. He could make *don't* sound like a hug. "I've got maybe ten minutes of satellite window and a lot of good news to deliver, so let's be professionals about this, huh?"

Ines had stopped splicing. She hadn't looked up, hadn't turned around, but her hands had gone still on the rope, and I understood that she was listening with every part of her except her eyes.

"What do you want, Denny."

"What do I— Oscar. Oscar, Oscar, Oscar." I could hear him settling in somewhere, a creak of leather, the clink of ice against glass. Somewhere dry and expensive, while I sat in a drizzle in a stolen hour of calm. "You went dark. That's all. Your beacon dropped off the grid during the worst weather this coast has seen in thirty years, and my brother and I were worried sick. Genuinely. Marty lost sleep. Well — Marty naps aggressively, so we'll call it concern."

"My beacon died with my boat," I said carefully.

"Mm." A pause, just long enough to be a knife laid flat on a table rather than raised. "See, here's the thing about modern electronics, Oscar. They're redundant. They're stubborn. They ping home from the bottom of swimming pools. Yours didn't die. Yours went quiet, and then yesterday—" he let the word sit there like a fly on the rim of a glass "—it came back on. For eleven minutes. Long enough to get a fix off a passing constellation before somebody thought better of it and pulled the battery."

Eleven minutes. Two days ago, when Ines and I had jury-rigged the emergency transponder off my swamped rig onto her rail mount to test it, and then I'd yanked the power in a fit of paranoia that had apparently been forty minutes too late.

Beside me, Ines said nothing, but the silence changed shape. Structural after all.

"That's a coincidence," I said. "Storm damage. Intermittent fault."

"Sure," Denny agreed warmly. "And the tide going out past the point at Cape Reckoning last week was a coincidence, and my grandfather's ledger books being full of entries about Cape Reckoning is a coincidence, and the fact that your family name shows up in them is—" he clicked his tongue, disappointed, almost tender "—Oscar. Come on. We've known each other five years. We've done lunch. You cried at my birthday dinner, which, by the way, we never talk about, and I appreciate that."

My mouth had gone dry. "You have no idea what you're talking about."

"Probably not! Probably not. That's why I'm not the one coming out there." Ice again. Somewhere behind him, faintly, his brother Marty's laugh, the big rolling one he used in boardrooms. "Here's the good news I mentioned. We're sending someone. His name's Corvo. Lovely man. Terrible at small talk, wonderful at everything else. He's got a settlement offer for you — a real one, not one of those things we say to loosen people up. Debt forgiven. Clean slate. Maybe even a finder's fee, depending on what you've found."

"And what do I give him?"

"Oh, you know." Light as a napkin folded over a fork. "Paperwork. Family documents. Anything old and interesting you might've stumbled across in a cave that officially does not exist. Corvo will explain the details. He's much better at explaining than I am."

"Denny." I kept my voice level, and I want credit for that, because my pulse was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. "What happens if I don't take the deal?"

Another pause. When he spoke again the warmth was still there, every degree of it, which was somehow the worst thing yet.

"Then Corvo explains that too. He's very thorough. Look — Oscar. Sweetheart. Champion. Nobody wants this to be unpleasant. My brother and I are businessmen. We'd rather write off a debt than a friend. But you've got to understand how this looks from where we're sitting. You vanish into a storm, you pop up on a coastline our family's been... fond of... for generations, carrying whatever you're carrying, and you won't return calls. What are we supposed to think? We think: poor Oscar's in over her head. We think: somebody should help her. That's all this is. Help."

"Where is Corvo now?"

"He left this morning." A sip, a swallow. "Should make landfall by tomorrow night, day after at the outside, weather permitting. And Oscar? Weather's permitting. Barometer's falling again. Big second act coming ashore. So you've got a window. Use it to think. Use it to be smart. Be the smart girl who walked out of a Category Four with nothing worse than a wet boat."

"How do you know what shape my boat's in?"

Silence. Then, gently, like a hand on the shoulder from behind: "Corvo will see you soon."

The line clicked dead.

* * *

For a while I just stood there holding the phone, watching the mangroves drip.

The rain had thinned to almost nothing. Out past the cove's mouth the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, deceptively calm, the kind of calm that meteorologists describe with words like *eye* and *false*. Somewhere beyond that horizon, a second landfall was assembling itself out of warm water and bad luck, and somewhere behind that horizon, a man named Corvo was pointed at me like a drawn arrow.

"You told me the beacon was dead," Ines said.

She still hadn't turned around. Her voice was flat as the sea.

"It was supposed to be. I killed the battery." I set the phone back in its cradle and looked at it with loathing. "We tested it on the rail mount Tuesday morning. For eleven minutes. I powered it down and stripped it after."

"Eleven minutes." She turned then, and her eyes had the color and temperature of deep water. "That's enough for three satellites and a position fix accurate to fifty meters."

"Fifty meters in open ocean is a haystack."

"Fifty meters in a cove with one anchorage," she said, "is a pin on a map."

I opened my mouth to argue and closed it again, because she was right, and because arguing with Ines Marlowe about seamanship, navigation, or anything else involving the ocean was like arguing with the ocean. It just made you tired and wet.

"So they know where we are," I said. "Or close enough."

"They know where we were. Two days ago." She came forward and took the chart tube from under the dodger, spreading the local sheet across the cockpit table and weighting the corners with winch handles. Her finger came down on our notch of a cove. "If Corvo's coming from the north — and the Vanes' money comes from the north — he makes landfall at Port Sable or he pushes down to the fishing harbor at Kestrel Point. From either one, this coast is a hundred miles of reef, shoal, and weather. He can't search it fast. But he doesn't have to search it fast, because he doesn't have to search at all." Her finger tapped the cove. "He starts here."

"Then we leave."

"We leave," she agreed, "in the next six hours, before the wind backs southeast and turns this whole coast into a lee shore. Which means you need to tell me what's actually going on, Coldharbour, because I've been sharing my boat, my fuel, and my salvage charges with a woman who owes money to people who send men named Corvo places, and I did that on the understanding that your trouble was ordinary. Ordinary trouble drowns. This kind—" she tapped the chart again "—this kind gets people disappeared."

So I told her.

Not everything — I'm not a fool, and some of it I barely understood myself yet — but the shape of it. The debt. Five years of it, built one friendly loan at a time, each one easier to sign than the last, until the interest alone was more than I made in a season of storm chasing and the Vanes' smiles had started to feel like weather of their own. The deadline: the end of the monsoon season, pay or else, and the else never once stated out loud, which was the whole artistry of it. And then the cave, and the timbers stamped RECKONING, and the hold, and the ledger in my grandfather's handwriting that read less like accounting than like testimony — pages and pages naming names, including, on the third-to-last page, a name that had made me sit down hard on a flooded crate:

**V. VANE — received his share in full. Denies all knowledge to the widow.**

The fortune the Vanes had built their empire on wasn't earned. It was stolen, off the wreck of a ship they'd helped strand, from a man they'd let drown with the truth in his pocket. And I'd been borrowing money from his murderers' grandsons to keep my boat in the air.

Ines listened without moving. When I finished she looked at the chart for a long moment, and then she said, quietly, "Vane Salvage & Marine. They bought out half the coastal operators in the eighties. My father refused to sell to them. Six months later he was diving a site he shouldn't have been diving, alone, in weather he shouldn't have trusted." Her jaw worked. "I always wondered who'd sold him the contract."

The cove dripped. The mangroves breathed.

"So," I said. "Still glad you blew the cave instead of taking the gold?"

"I'm glad I know who I'm blowing caves with," she said. "Ask me again when Corvo's standing on my deck."

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing to run, and I spent most of it losing an argument with my own reflection in the brass of the compass binnacle.

Because here's the thing I couldn't say out loud, the thing that sat in my chest like swallowed ballast: for one bright, stupid day, the storm had been on my side. The same monster that had sealed me in a flooding cave with a stranger and my grandfather's bones-in-ink had also sealed everyone *else* out. No calls. No collectors. No Corvo. For seventy-two hours the world had narrowed to bilge pumps and barometers and a taciturn salvager who shared her coffee like it cost her blood, and I had — God help me — rested. The hunted feeling had gone quiet. I'd actually slept, curled in the *Maren*'s narrow berth while the hull groaned around me, dreaming about nothing worse than water.

And I'd gotten comfortable. Complacent enough to test a beacon on an open rail and take forty minutes remembering fear existed. The storm that hid me had also, in the end, betrayed me — not by lifting, but by letting me believe, for a few hours, that I wasn't prey. That was the trick of it. That was the lesson the sea keeps trying to teach me and I keep failing to audit: the calm isn't mercy. The calm is the eye. Something is always circling.

Denny's voice was still in my ear, too. *Be the smart girl.* He'd meant it as a compliment. With the Vanes, every compliment was a measurement.

I found Ines bent over her charge locker, counting demolition charges the way other people count rosary beads.

"If we run south," she said without looking up, "we put the reef systems between us and him and burn fuel we need for salvage ops. If we run north, we run toward his landfall. If we stay, we anchor here, ride out the second landfall in the mangroves — which this boat will do beautifully, thank you for asking — and we wait for a man whose job description I can guess, in a cove he already knows about."

"You're really selling all three options," I said.

"I'm a salvager. I present the wreck; you pick what to do with it." She snapped the locker shut. "But since you asked me to have an opinion: we stay. Not because staying is safe. Because running burns the fuel, the food, and the luck we'll need later, and because a Category Five doesn't care whose errand you're on. Corvo has to survive the same storm we do. Let the weather sort the field." A ghost of something crossed her face that might, in a more expressive woman, have been a smile. "Besides. I want to see what he's made of before I decide what he's worth."

"And after the storm?"

"After the storm," she said, "we're going to stop reacting. We've been reacting since the cave. Time we made somebody else do it." She looked at me straight on. "You've got a ledger full of names and dates and numbers that could hang an empire. That's not evidence, Coldharbour. That's ordnance. Question is whether we fire it or trade it — and we'd better decide before a man with a settlement offer puts the question to us in person."

I thought about my grandfather's handwriting. *Denies all knowledge to the widow.* Forty years of a lie, told to my grandmother on her deathbed by men who shook her hand at the funeral. I thought about Denny's ice clinking in a glass somewhere dry and high above the waterline. I thought about the word *settlement*, which is what they call it when they mean surrender, and *offer*, which is what they call it when they mean the last chance you'll get.

The light was failing. Out past the mangroves, the sea had begun to breathe differently — longer swells, deeper-set, arriving from a long way off with news. The barometer on the bulkhead had dropped four points since noon and was dropping still, the needle easing down like a man leaving a party early because he knows something.

Ines followed my eyes to it.

"Window closes tonight," she said. "Whatever we're going to decide, we decide it before that needle stops moving."

And that was when the satellite phone chirped again — those three ascending notes, bright as a doorbell — though this time, before I could touch it, it stopped. One chirp only. Then silence, and the soft blue blink of a message waiting.

I opened it with hands that were steadier than I deserved.

No text. Just a photograph, sent from a number with no country code, taken from far enough away that the compression had chewed the detail to rags but near enough that there was no mistaking it: a boat at anchor in a green notch of coastline. White hull. Red boot stripe. A ketch, riding quietly among the mangroves.

The *Maren*.

Below it, a single line of type:

**Lovely spot. See you after the blow. — C**

Out in the channel, right on schedule, the rain began.
