## 10. Monsoon Season

The monsoon announced itself the way my father used to announce dinner: a long, low grumble from far away, followed by everything you loved being swept off the table.

I felt it in my teeth first. Thirty years of salvage diving will do that—barometric pressure gets into your fillings, your old fractures, the places where bone met rock and lost. By dawn the sky over the cove had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had that particular hungry note in it, like something breathing through its teeth.

"Landfall by afternoon," I said, spitting over the side for luck. Old habit. My mother's habit, actually, but I'd inherited it along with her hands and her temper.

Oscar stood at the rail of my ketch—the *Sable Gull*, forty feet of stubborn oak and worse decisions—and watched the horizon with the expression she probably thought looked scientific. It looked, to me, like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You're sure about the mangroves?" she asked. Third time she'd asked. I was starting to keep count.

"I'm sure the mangroves are the only thing between us and being rolled across the reef like a beer bottle," I said. "The roots knit together. Whole forests of them hold the seabed down when the surge comes through. Boats have survived category fives anchored in mangrove country. Boats have *not* survived category fives anchored in open water pretending to be brave."

"And you'd know."

"My father taught me to anchor before he taught me to swim." I said it lightly, which is how I say most things that aren't light at all. "Come on. We've got maybe six hours to make this boat boring enough to survive."

Making a boat boring is most of seamanship, if you ask me. Strip everything that can catch wind or tear loose. Double-lash the dinghy. Take off the sails, bag them, wedge them below. Rig chafe gear on every line that touches anything, because in a storm it isn't the wind that kills your boat—it's the rope sawing itself to death against the rail while you sleep. Oscar worked without complaint, which surprised me. She had the hands of someone who'd spent more time with instruments than halyards, but she learned fast and she didn't do things twice, and in my experience that puts a person ahead of half the sailors on this coast.

By noon we were wedged into the mangrove shallows behind Serpent Cay, three anchors out in a fan—one to the north, one to the east, one straight astern—all buried deep in the mud with chain enough to make them mean it. The mangroves stood around us in their millions, gray-trunked and patient, holding the land together with their toes. The water between them had gone strangely still, the way it does before weather, like the whole sea was holding its breath.

That's when I got out the drill.

Oscar looked at it the way you'd look at a snake in a breadbox. "Why do you have a drill?"

"Because in about four hours, this boat is going to be under more stress than it has ever been under in its life," I said. "And if the sea decides to come aboard, I would rather it came aboard through holes I chose than through the hull splitting like an envelope."

"That sounds insane."

"It sounds insane because you're thinking of holes as failure. Holes are plumbing. You put them in low, near the keel, where any water that comes in pools anyway, and you plug them with soft wooden plugs lashed to a lanyard so they're always there when you need them. If we take a knockdown and ship a ton of water, I pull two plugs, she drains in minutes, I hammer them back in. If we don't have the plugs—" I shrugged. "Then we find out what's in the bilge the hard way, which is usually everything."

She watched me drill the first hole with the expression of a woman watching someone defuse a bomb in reverse. But she's not stupid—that's the thing I'd noticed about her even back when I wanted to throw her off my wreck. Stupid people argue. Smart people watch, and then they pick up the second drill.

"Where do you want this one?" she said.

* * *

We drilled four holes in all, two each side, low down where the garboard strakes curved toward the keel. We plugged them, tested the plugs, pulled them, let the sea gurgle up through the openings and then stopped it again. It's a strange feeling, deliberately opening your home to the ocean. Like inviting the wolf in to show him where the door is, so he doesn't have to break anything looking for it.

The first squall hit around two o'clock—a messenger, just the vanguard, throwing rain hard enough to sting. Then it passed, and the light went strange and greenish, and the air sat on us like a wet wool blanket, and I knew the real thing was coming behind it.

So we ate. That's another thing people get wrong about storms. You don't wait until you're in it to eat; you eat beforehand, big, because afterward nobody wants to and you'll need the fuel. Rice and tinned fish and the last of the good coffee, brewed on the gimbaled stove while the boat still rocked gently enough to pour.

Oscar sat cradling her mug and watching the mangroves bend.

"Ines," she said. "Can I ask you something, or are you going to bite me?"

"That depends entirely on the question."

"The cave. The Reckoning. You said you'd been searching for years. Why? Salvage rights pay well, but there are easier wrecks. Shallower ones. Ones that don't come with a monsoon attached."

I turned my mug in my hands. Outside, the first real gusts were starting to comb the tops of the mangroves flat, and the sound was building—that vast white-noise roar, like the world being erased and redrawn.

"My father died diving for the Reckoning," I said.

She didn't say anything. Good. People who say *oh, I'm sorry* at moments like that should be made to walk the plank.

"Nineteen eighty-one. I was nine. He was a diver, a good one, one of the best on this coast, and he'd spent eleven years chasing the story of the Reckoning the way other men chase women or drink. He had charts. He had interviews with fishermen who'd been boys when she went down. He had a theory about the tide legends—you remember the local warned you about those? He believed them. Not as superstition. As data. He thought the cape did something strange with its tides, some resonance, some trick of the shelf geometry, and that once or twice a century the sea would pull back far enough and long enough to expose the cave mouth. He called it 'the sea giving back.' He was going to be standing there when it happened."

"What happened instead?"

"He went out early. Before his window. A charter captain swore he'd seen timber in the shallows after a storm, and my father couldn't wait—he mortgaged our house for dive gear and boat time and went out with a partner whose name I won't dignify by remembering. They found something. We never knew what. My father went down on the last dive of the trip and didn't come up. His partner said his regulator failed. Said there was nothing anyone could do." I looked at her. "Two years ago I found the partner's logbook in an estate sale. His regulator hadn't failed. His air had been shut off at the valve. Somebody turned it off while he was down, and the partner rowed home and told a nine-year-old girl her daddy wasn't coming back, and then he lived another thirty years on whatever they paid him."

Oscar's face had gone very still. "The Vanes."

"The Vanes' grandfather sat on the salvage commission that denied my father's permit application three times. And the partner bought a new truck the month after the funeral. I'm not saying I have proof. I'm saying I have a shape, and the shape fits." I drank my coffee. It had gone cold. I drank it anyway. "So when I found you crawling around my cave with your little camera, taking pictures of my grandfather's—your grandfather's ledger, understand that what I felt wasn't greed. It was rage. Twenty years of rage with nowhere to put it, and suddenly there's a woman standing on the deck of history with a flashlight."

The wind took the masthead then, a long shriek like something being flayed, and the *Gull* leaned her head into it and shrugged it off. The mangroves roared—a sound I can't describe to anyone who hasn't heard a forest fight, ten thousand trees bending nearly double and springing back, bending and springing, the whole world flexing.

"We should check the anchors," I said, and stood up, and that was the end of the conversation for a while, because the monsoon arrived properly at dusk and did not stop for anything so small as grief.

* * *

It came in waves of violence, if that makes sense—not one storm but a hundred, stacked and queued. The eye was still hours south of us, but the outer bands hit like freight trains: forty minutes of screaming wind and horizontal rain, then five minutes of unnatural quiet where you could hear your own heart, then the next band, from a slightly different direction, so the anchors that held for one wouldn't necessarily hold for the next. I ran the deck in harness, checking chafe, checking the snubbers, checking the mangroves weren't trying to board us. Oscar ran below, bailing nothing, because nothing needed bailing yet, and keeping the log, because she's the kind of person who keeps a log in a hurricane, and I decided somewhere around midnight that I could live with that.

At some point in the black hours the third band caught us broadside and laid the *Gull* over hard enough to put the rail under. Water came aboard in a solid sheet—I heard it hit the cabin sole below like a dropped mattress—and I yelled down to Oscar, "Plugs!"

And here is the thing I'll remember until I die: she didn't panic. There was a moment—I felt the boat hanging on her beam ends, felt the whole night balanced on whether that hull came back up—and through the sole above my head I heard her voice, steady as a radio operator's: "Which ones?"

"Both! Pull both, let her drain, I'll re-plug when she settles!"

The sea came in through the holes I'd drilled, exactly as invited, found the lowest places, and began to leave again through the same doors. When the *Gull* righted herself and shook herself like a dog, Oscar was already on the sole with the flashlight, watching the water level drop, calling numbers to me like a surgeon's nurse. "Six inches. Four. Two. Coming up on dry."

I hammered the plugs home in the next lull, soaked to the skin, laughing. I couldn't help it. Somewhere in the roaring dark, with a woman I'd known for a week and tried to rob twice, I laughed like my father used to laugh at the top of a good dive.

"You drilled holes in your own boat," Oscar shouted at me over the wind, when I came below dripping.

"I *invited* the ocean in on my terms!" I shouted back. "There's a difference! It's the difference between a guest and a burglar!"

"That is the most deranged sentence ever spoken aboard a floating vessel!"

"Thank you! My father said it first!"

* * *

Near dawn the eye found us—or rather, we found the edge of it, the northern fringe of the calm, and for ninety minutes the world went still as a held breath. Stars came out overhead through a ragged chimney of clearing sky. The mangroves stood up straight again, dripping, and the water lay flat as poured metal, and everywhere around us, beyond the circle of calm, the storm walls stood like cathedral buttresses, lightning flickering inside them without a sound reaching us.

We sat in the cockpit and didn't talk. Some silences are company; others are just two people avoiding a room. This one was company.

"He taught you the drilling thing," Oscar said finally. "Your father."

"He taught me everything worth knowing. The rest I learned from mistakes, mostly mine." I wrapped my hands around the thermos. "He used to say the sea doesn't hate you. It doesn't love you either. It just doesn't know you're there. And the trick—the whole trick—is to become known. Make yourself legible to the water. Give it doors instead of letting it make windows."

"That's why you gave me the stern berth," she said. "Lowest point in the boat. Doors, not windows."

"You catch on fast for a landlubber with a barometer."

She smiled, and then it faded into something else. "My grandfather's letter came forty years late. I only read it last week—in the cave, by lamplight, with you threatening me. He knew he was going to die. He wrote that his partners would call it bad weather, and everyone would believe them, because everyone always believes the sea did it." Her jaw tightened. "Everyone always believes the sea did it. That's what they said about your father too."

"Yes."

"So we're both—" She stopped. Started again. "We're both daughters of stories somebody else wrote."

I looked at her then, really looked, this debt-ridden, reckless, brilliant woman who'd walked into my cave and out of my storm, and I understood that whatever bargain we'd struck in that cove, it had quietly stopped being a bargain somewhere in the last twelve hours. Bargains are transactions. What was sitting in my cockpit drinking cold coffee with me was something older and less convenient.

"Here's what I know about stories," I said. "You can't unwrite them. But you can refuse to let the liars keep the pen."

The eastern wall of the eye began to lean toward us, gray and total, and the first gusts of the back side of the monsoon came moaning through the mangroves. I stood up, joints complaining, and started checking harnesses.

But Oscar stayed seated a moment longer, staring east, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman reading instruments.

"Ines. Look at the water outside the eye wall."

I looked. Beyond the mangroves, past the flat silver of the eye's calm, out where the storm walls stood, there was a light. Small, white, steady, moving against the wind—moving *with* purpose, the way no drifting thing moves in a monsoon.

Nobody runs a searchlight through the eye of a category-five cyclone unless they're looking for something specific.

"Somebody's out there," Oscar said quietly. "Somebody's coming through the storm. And I'd bet my grandfather's ledger it's Corvo."
