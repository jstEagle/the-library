## 11. Corvo Comes Ashore

The mangrove shallows had a smell I was learning to love—rot and salt and green things fighting for sunlight—and I was elbow-deep in the ketch's bilge when Ines said, from the cockpit, in the flat tone she used for weather warnings: "Boat coming."

"Fisherman?"

"Fishing boats don't run at nine knots with their running lights off."

I climbed out of the hold with bilge water to my elbows and a wrench still in my fist, which felt about as useful as a spoon in a knife fight. Through the screen of mangrove branches I could see it: a white-hulled sport cruiser sliding up the channel like it owned the tide, all polished fiberglass and expensive arrogance. It came off the anchor with a practiced ease that said money had bought the hands on the wheel.

"That's not Corvo's boat," I said.

"It's a charter out of Port Meridian," Ines said. "Rented. He doesn't want his own hull on anyone's charts."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell that because nobody rents a boat like that unless they're trying very hard to look like they didn't plan this." She was already moving below decks, and when she came back up she had two things: a shotgun wrapped in an oilcloth, and a second expression underneath her usual one, which I'd have sworn was fear if I hadn't known better. "Get the ledger off the boat."

"It's not on the boat. It's—" I stopped. We'd been through this. The ledger lived in three places now: her head, my head, and a waterproof tube buried under a specific crooked tree on the island's north spit, because we were neither of us fools, whatever else we were.

"Good," she said. "Then all he can take is us."

* * *

Corvo came ashore the way a good salesman comes ashore: smiling, empty-handed, and slightly early.

He stepped off the dinghy onto our little crescent of shell beach with the ease of a man arriving at a resort, and I'll give him this—he looked exactly like what he was supposed to look like, which was harmless. Mid-fifties, silver at the temples, linen shirt unbuttoned one button past businesslike, a man whose tan said *golf* rather than *work*. He had the kind of face you'd trust with your luggage. Your car keys. Your life, if you weren't paying attention.

I'd been paying attention since I was nineteen years old and a Vane brother smiled at me across a desk while explaining, in the warmest possible terms, what happened to people who missed payments.

"Miss Coldharbour." He spread his hands like a man presenting a sunset. "Oscar. May I? You don't mind if I use your name. I feel like we're practically family already—you've spent so much time with ours." His eyes went past me to the ketch, cataloguing her with a professional's speed: the patched hull, the storm damage, the shotgun-shaped bulge under Ines's oilcloth. None of it changed his smile. "And you must be Marlowe. The salvager. My employers speak of you. Well—" a small, elegant correction, "—they spoke of your father, mostly. Terrible business, that dive. All those years ago."

Ines said nothing. Ines could out-silence a lighthouse.

"I come unarmed," Corvo said, turning slowly so we could see it, arms wide, linen fluttering. "You can search the boat. You won't find so much as a pocketknife. I'm here to make an offer, nothing more. A settlement. The Vanes are sentimental men, despite their reputation. Family history does that to a person."

"Family history," I repeated.

"The Cape Reckoning coast is soaked in it," he said pleasantly. "May I sit? Standing negotiations give me a crick."

He sat himself, on a piece of driftwood worn smooth as furniture, crossing his ankles like a man at a garden party, and I stood over him with the wrench still in my hand because putting it down felt like a concession and I wasn't giving this man a single inch for free.

"So," I said. "The offer."

"Straight to business. I admire that. Your grandfather was the same way, by all accounts." He let that sit in the air between us, watching my face the way a fisherman watches a line. "Here it is, plainly. The Vanes will forgive your debt. Every cent. Principal, interest, penalties—the whole ugly arithmetic, gone. Clean slate, clean credit, clean name. In exchange, you hand over the ledger from the Reckoning. The original. And you forget where you found it."

"And that's it."

"That's entirely it." He beamed. "You walk away owing nothing. Isn't that what you've wanted since the day you signed with them? Freedom?"

It was. God help me, it was. I did the math without wanting to—the number I owed, the number of years it would take to earn it chasing storms for data brokers who paid by the reading, the number of mornings I'd woken up doing that math before my feet hit the floor. Forty-one months of payments made. A lifetime left. And here was a man on a driftwood throne offering to erase it all for one old book.

"You're smiling," I said.

"I'm a cheerful man."

"No," I said. "You're smiling because you think I'm going to say yes, and you're enjoying how easy it is. That's a different smile."

His smile didn't falter, but something behind it shifted—a shutter closing over a window. "Miss Coldharbour. Oscar. Let me be frank, since you appreciate frankness. The Vanes sent me because they prefer me to the alternative, and the alternative is a great many large men with poor impulse control. This offer expires when I leave this beach. What happens after that isn't personal. It's just tidying."

"Tidying," Ines said. Her first word in ten minutes, and it landed like a cleat dropped on a deck plate.

"The Vanes are thorough," Corvo agreed, as if she'd complimented him. "They've been thorough for four generations. It's why there's still a fortune to inherit." He turned back to me, and here he did something clever, something I almost admired: he softened. Shoulders down, voice down, the salesman stepping aside for a moment to let a human being stand there. "Oscar. I've read the file on you. All of it. Your mother's medical bills. The boat loan. The way you took the Vane contract knowing exactly what the terms meant because your grandfather's name was already ruined and you figured you had nothing left to lose but interest. You're not a criminal. You're a woman who got handed a bill for someone else's dinner. I understand the temptation to burn the restaurant down. But the ledger doesn't burn anything. It just gets you drowned in a cave forty years too late, next to a man who's already dead. Take the deal. Live."

For a moment—one long, treacherous moment—I let myself stand inside that sentence. *Take the deal. Live.* The debt gone. The Vanes satisfied. The monsoon season spent somewhere warm with money in the account and no one's name on my neck. My grandfather had been dead for forty years; the ledger wouldn't raise him. The gold was sunk beyond any reach, sacrificed to the flood, and all that remained of the Reckoning's truth was paper and memory, and paper could buy a life.

Then I looked at Ines.

She was standing at the waterline with the oilcloth bundle held loose against her thigh, watching Corvo with an expression I'd learned to read in the cave, in the storm, in the long nights repairing the ketch: the look of a woman counting divers. Her father was down there somewhere off this coast, in the cold dark where the Vanes' ancestors had put him, along with however many others whose names never made a file Corvo could read aloud. The ledger wasn't paper to her. It was a headstone that hadn't been carved yet.

And Corvo watched me watch her, and I saw—just for a flicker, just for a frame or two—the real face behind the linen shirt. Not anger. Patience. The patience of a man checking his watch while a tide comes in. He wasn't worried whether I said yes. He was only deciding how long to wait before he stopped needing me to.

That was the thing about the Vanes' charm, I realized, standing there with salt drying on my arms and a wrench going soft in my grip: it was never an offer. It was a countdown with good manners.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said.

Corvo sighed with genuine pleasure, like a sommelier hearing a customer order the expensive bottle. "Oh, I hoped you'd negotiate. Go on."

"You tell the Vanes the ledger's destroyed. Water damage, cave collapse, tragic. Tell them I'm a broken woman with a leaking boat and no proof of anything. In return, I keep breathing, and you tell them whatever makes that story true."

"And why would I do that?" he asked, curious, almost kind.

"Because if you go back and tell them I refused, they send the large men with poor impulse control, and those men come out here, and maybe they find the ledger and maybe they don't, but either way they find *me*, and I promise you, Mr. Corvo, I am a much cheaper problem solved than I am unsolved." I crouched down to his level, driftwood and all, and gave him back his own smile. "You know that. It's why they sent you instead. You're the cheap version. So let's both be cheap."

He studied me for a long moment. Somewhere behind the mangroves a bird started up its idiot racket, and the tide whispered against the shells, and Corvo sat in the middle of it looking like a man who had all the time in the world and knew exactly how much of mine was left.

"You sound like your grandfather," he said finally, rising, brushing sand from his trousers. "According to the family stories. Stubborn right up until the water closed over him." He straightened his collar, unhurried, and turned toward his dinghy, then paused, half-turned, the picture of a man remembering a courtesy. "I'll tell them you're considering it. That buys you—oh, let's say until this storm clears. Weather like this keeps even thorough men indoors. After that, they'll want an answer, and they'll want it in person, and I'm afraid I won't be able to protect you from their disappointment." He smiled his luggage-trusting smile. "Do consider the offer, Oscar. It really is generous. Everything about my employers is generous, right up until the moment it isn't."

He walked to the dinghy. He pushed it off the shell beach. He rowed the fifty yards to the rented cruiser with the unhurried strokes of a man who had never once been followed, and the cruiser lifted anchor and slid back down the channel with its lights still off, polite as a burglar.

* * *

We didn't talk until the channel was empty and the bird had given up.

"He's coming back," Ines said.

"He's not leaving," I corrected. "There's a difference. That boat didn't head for open water—it took up station past the headland. He's going to sit out there and watch us until the storm breaks, and then either I hand over a ledger that doesn't exist anymore, or he comes ashore with friends and we find out how thorough the Vanes really are."

"Then we move tonight."

"In this?" I gestured at the sky, which had spent the afternoon assembling a bruise-colored wall out past the reef. The barometer on the ketch's bulkhead had been falling all day like it had somewhere better to be. "Ines, that's the second landfall coming. The real one. We'd be running into the teeth of a cell that eats boats like yours for breakfast."

"My boat has survived worse than breakfast."

"Your boat has a hole in it I patched with epoxy and prayer."

"Prayer holds," she said, "if you apply enough layers." But she was already looking at the sky too, doing her own arithmetic, and I could see the answer forming before she said it. "We can't stay. He knows where we are. Tonight, while the wind's still building—they won't expect anyone to move. We run south ahead of it, into the lee of the outer islands, and we let the storm cover our wake."

"And if the storm catches us instead?"

She looked at me then, and for the first time since Corvo's dinghy touched the beach, something moved behind her eyes that wasn't calculation. It took me a second to name it, because I'd seen it so rarely on that weathered face: it was apology. Or the closest thing Ines Marlowe kept in stock.

"Then we find out together," she said. "Get your gear aboard. We've got maybe two hours of light, and I want the reef passage memorized before dark."

I went to strike the camp, and halfway through folding the tarp I stopped, because my hands were shaking—not from fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that sloshing around, but from the other thing, the thing Corvo's smile had shown me the shape of. He hadn't come to bargain. Bargains end. He'd come to measure the distance between me and the water, the same distance his employers' grandfathers once measured between my grandfather and the sea, and he'd found it short, and he'd smiled about it.

Well. They'd measured wrong once before, forty years ago, in a cave full of stolen gold. A man had died—but not before writing everything down.

I folded the tarp, shouldered my bag, and waded out toward the ketch where Ines was already raising the anchor, silhouetted against a sky going the color of a bruise, and I told myself the shaking would stop once we were moving.

Out past the headland, a white hull sat motionless on the darkening water, watching us go, and I had the distinct, skin-crawling certainty that letting us run was exactly what it had come here to do.
