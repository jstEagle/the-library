## 12. The First Betrayal

There are two kinds of liars in this world: the ones who lie to you and the ones who lie to themselves. I've always prided myself on being neither. I'm a third kind—the kind who tells herself that what she's doing isn't lying at all, it's *due diligence*, it's *asset protection*, it's the sort of sensible contingency planning any professional salvager would be a fool to skip.

I was photographing the ledger by the light of a headlamp clamped between my teeth, which should tell you something about how much dignity was involved in the whole enterprise.

The ketch rocked gently at anchor in the cove, mangroves breathing on the shore like a sleeping animal, and Oscar Coldharbour was up top checking the storm lashings because she couldn't sit still for more than twenty minutes at a stretch. That was my window. Twenty minutes. I'd been rationing her absences all day like a woman hoarding water in a drought, and every time she came below I'd snap the ledger shut and slide it back under the floorboard where we'd found the chart, and I'd smile at her with a face I genuinely believed was innocent until I caught my reflection in the brass lamp housing and saw a woman who looked exactly like what she was.

The camera was small—a diver's documentation unit, waterproof, shockproof, built for photographing wreck artifacts in silt. I'd told Oscar about it weeks ago, back when honesty was still cheap. *For cataloguing finds,* I'd said. She'd nodded. She hadn't asked to see it since.

I turned another page of the ledger and pressed the shutter.

*Received of H. Vane, per agreement, forty cases specie, condition good.* My grandfather's contemporary—no, not mine, hers, Elias Coldharbour's handwriting, looping and confident, the script of a man who trusted his partners because he'd known them since boyhood. Forty cases of gold coin, received from Hiram Vane, logged as cargo transferred aboard the Reckoning for the run south. And then, three pages later, in a different hand entirely, the entry that had kept me awake for two nights running: *Cargo manifest amended. No specie aboard.* Signed by the same H. Vane who had received it.

They'd taken the gold off the ship before she sailed, filed papers saying she carried nothing worth stealing, and then let the storm—or whatever actually happened out there—do the rest. Elias Coldharbour had gone down with a hold full of ballast stones and a paper trail that made him look either incompetent or complicit. His partners split the fortune and built banks on it. Banks. Plural. Generations of them.

And one of those generations now held a debt against his granddaughter that they intended to collect in blood, cheerful as Sunday morning.

Click. Another page. The flash diffuser softened everything to a warm amber glow, and in that glow the ledger looked almost holy, which was ironic, because what I was doing with it was about as far from holy as a person can get while still technically wearing clothes.

Here's what I told myself, and I want it on the record that I told myself this with tremendous conviction: the bargain was that the ledger goes public. That was Oscar's half. My half was salvage rights to the wreck itself—the timbers, the fittings, the historical material, the things that don't testify but do pay. Fair enough. Clean division. But salvage rights are only worth anything if you can finance the recovery operation, and financing requires capital, and capital requires leverage, and leverage is exactly what a set of high-resolution photographs of an authenticated primary document represents. I wasn't going to sell the ledger. I wasn't even going to sell copies of the ledger. I was going to sell *proof of the ledger's existence* to Aurelio Vance—no relation to the Vanes, thank God, though the coincidence had caused me a full day of confusion when we first met—who ran salvage ops out of Port Meridian and had been hunting the Reckoning's true story longer than I'd been alive. He'd front me the money for the claim registration and the dive season. Oscar gets her truth broadcast to the world. Everybody wins.

Except that Aurelio Vance doesn't lend money. He buys options. And an option on the Reckoning's story, in the hands of a man like that, meant the story would come out on his schedule, shaped to his purposes, with my name attached as the source and Oscar's grandfather's testimony reduced to a press release with a price tag. I knew that. I knew it the way you know a current is running under your boat—you can't see it, but you can feel it pulling.

I turned another page anyway.

*E.C. suspects nothing. H. says let him sail. Weather will handle the rest.*

My hand shook. The photograph came out blurred. I deleted it and took it again, steadier, because if I was going to betray this woman I was at least going to do it competently.

That's the thing nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't arrive all at once like a storm front. It arrives in installments. First you take one photograph, just to have the information, purely informational, no intent attached. Then the photographs accumulate into a set, and a set implies a purpose, and a purpose implies a buyer, and somewhere in there you've crossed a line so gradually that you can stand on the far side of it and honestly believe you're still standing where you started. The sea does the same thing. You anchor in six feet of water over sand, go below for an hour, come up aground on a falling tide, and swear the seabed moved. It didn't. You did.

"Ines?"

I snapped the ledger shut so fast I pinched my thumb in it, which served me right, and got it under the floorboard in maybe four seconds flat, which was a personal best, and had the nerve to say, "Yeah?" in a voice pitched at exactly the wrong register—too high, too quick, the vocal equivalent of a guilty dog.

Oscar came down the companionway ladder with salt drying white in her hair and a coil of spare line over one shoulder, and she stopped halfway down because she was tall and the ketch was old and the builder had clearly never met anyone over five-foot-eight. Her eyes went around the cabin the way they did—she read rooms like charts, noting hazards—and they passed over the floorboard, over the headlamp still clamped to my collar where I'd forgotten to take it off, over my face.

"Storm lashings are done," she said. "Forestay's chafing where it runs through the fairlead. I put a rag on it, but we'll want proper leather."

"I'll cut some tomorrow."

"Good." She stowed the line. She didn't move toward the galley or the chart table or any of the places a person goes when a conversation is over. She stood there in the middle of her own boat, dripping a little, looking at me with those pale eyes that missed less than I'd budgeted for.

"You're wearing a headlamp," she said.

"Am I." I reached up and took it off, casual as anything, a woman who simply enjoyed headlamps as a lifestyle choice. "Habit. Diver's neck."

"Mm." She sat down across from me, folding her long frame onto the settee, and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the boat talked instead—the tick of the hull, the halyard tapping the mast, the mangrove shallows lapping at us like a tide with opinions. "You know what I keep thinking about," she said. "Back in the cave. When we found the air pocket and you went through the flooded passage first. You didn't hesitate."

"Somebody had to check it."

"You could've gone out. The entrance was still open a crack. You could've swum out, left me, claimed the wreck solo. Nobody would ever have known."

"I don't leave people trapped in caves."

"No," she agreed. "You don't. That's the problem." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion under her eyes—the debt, the storm, the dead grandfather, all of it stacked behind her face like cargo. "Because I've been sitting here trying to figure out why a stranger would do that, and the only answer I keep coming back to is that Ines Marlowe is honest the way the sea is honest. It'll drown you, but it won't pretend it won't."

Every word of that landed like a weight belt. I want it noted that I considered, briefly, telling her right then. There was a version of the next thirty seconds where I pulled the camera out and said, *I've been photographing the ledger, I was going to sell proof of it to Aurelio Vance, and I need you to hear me explain why before you decide what I am.* That version existed. I felt it there, available, like a hatch you could open onto clear weather.

Instead I said, "That's very poetic for someone who names her boats after engine parts."

"My last boat was named after my mother."

"That tracks too."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't. "The floorboard," she said quietly. "Under the chart table. Where we found the map."

"What about it."

"It's open."

I looked. Of course it was open. In my four-second personal-best stowage job, I had failed to seat the board flush, and it sat proud of its frame by perhaps the width of a fingernail, which might as well have been a searchlight. The ledger's corner showed beneath it, brown leather against dark wood, visible only to someone who knew to look—which she did, because she'd found the map there, because it was her grandfather's hiding place, because she had spent forty years of inherited grief learning to look at exactly that kind of gap.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for me to count my remaining options, and the count came up short.

"How many photographs?" she asked. Her voice didn't rise. That was somehow worse than shouting. Shouting would have given me something to push against.

"Ines." She said my name again, harder. "*How many?*"

"All of them." I made myself meet her eyes. If I was going to be caught, I was going to be caught standing. "Every page. Both hands' entries. The amendments. The part where Hiram Vane writes 'weather will handle the rest.'"

She went very still. People think stillness means calm; it usually means the opposite—it's the moment before the explosion, the barometer falling, the pressure dropping before the cell makes landfall. But Oscar Coldharbour's storms didn't blow outward. They blew inward. I watched her take it, all of it, fold it down small, and store it somewhere deep, and when she spoke again her voice was level as a spirit gauge.

"To sell."

"Proof of existence. Not the ledger itself. Not copies of the pages. To Aurelio Vance, in Meridian—he fronts the capital for my salvage claim, the recovery operation, the—"

"The operation on *my grandfather's grave.*"

"Your grandfather's grave is currently the property of whoever registers the claim first, and right now that's a race between me, a corporation, and the tides. Capital decides who gets there. I was getting capital."

"At what cost?"

"None to you! That's the—" I heard my own voice climb and dragged it back down. "The ledger still goes public. Your truth, broadcast, exactly like we agreed. Nothing changes for you."

"Nothing changes." She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in weeks, worse than Corvo's charm, worse than the storm hammering the cave mouth. "You photographed my grandfather's murder confession—their confession, his death warrant—in secret, by headlamp, in the dark, while I was on deck fixing *your rigging*, and you're telling me nothing changes for me." She stood up. The cabin was too low for it; she hunched under the deck beams like the boat itself was pressing down on her. "What changes, Ines, is that I find out whether the one person I started to trust on this coast is another person who looks at my family's worst night and sees inventory."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. That's what makes it accurate."

I stood too, because sitting felt like surrender, and God knows pride has never once saved a drowning woman but we reach for it anyway. "You want to talk about accurate? Let's be accurate. You're Oscar Coldharbour, heiress to a stolen fortune you didn't know about, and the only reason you know anything is because I spent nine years and my father's life savings finding that cave. Nine years, Oscar. I dove that reef until my ears bled. I lost a boat to it. I lost—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "I lost my father to it. So yes, I took photographs in the dark, because the dark is where I've lived for nine years, and I am *tired* of watching other people decide what my father died for is worth."

The words hung there between us, and I realized, with the particular horror of a person who has just said the truest thing possible in the least useful way, that I'd handed her a knife and she was holding it correctly.

"So the memorial," she said softly. "The divers' memorial you told me about. That was real."

"That was real."

"And this was real too. Both at once."

"Both at once," I said. "Welcome to being a person."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the mangroves rustled, and somewhere across the cove a fish jumped, indifferent to us, and I understood suddenly how much I'd come to need this—not her forgiveness, I hadn't earned the right to want that—but her *regard*. Somewhere between the cave and the storm and the confessions in the hold, her opinion of me had become ballast, and I'd just jettisoned it, and the boat of myself was listing badly.

"Give me the camera," she said.

I gave her the camera. What else was there? I unclipped it from my harness and set it in her palm, and she turned it over once, examining it like a recovered artifact, and then—this is the part I still don't fully understand—she didn't throw it overboard. She slid open the media tray, popped the memory card, and held it up between two fingers.

"We're even on secrets now," she said. "You have mine—I nearly took Corvo's deal. I have yours." She tucked the card into her shirt pocket, over her heart, which felt pointed. "But understand something, Ines. If Vance's name comes out of your mouth again—if I so much as smell Meridian salt on your plans—I won't get angry. I'll just stop trusting you. And you'll find out what this boat feels like without a second pair of hands."

"That's not a threat, that's just arithmetic."

"Everything important is arithmetic." She moved past me toward the galley, and paused, close enough that I could see the tremor she was holding still in her jaw. "The ledger stays on deck with me tonight. In my bag. You'll get it back in the morning, because I believe you'll still be here in the morning. Prove me right."

She climbed out into the cockpit, and I sat alone in the cabin with my open floorboard and my empty hands, listening to her settle the ledger into her bag above me, zip it, and sit down beside it where she could feel it against her hip.

I lay awake most of the night doing the arithmetic she'd mentioned. Trust divided by betrayal, multiplied by nine years, minus one memory card. The numbers didn't work. They never do, after. That's the whole cruelty of it—the math only balances before you cross the line, never after.

Near dawn, footsteps crossed the deck above me, light and quick, and stopped. A pause. Then Oscar's voice came down the companionway, quiet, stripped of everything but fact:

"Ines. Get up. There's a boat running dark along the mangrove line, and it's flying no lights but I'd know that hull anywhere." A beat. "Corvo's here."
