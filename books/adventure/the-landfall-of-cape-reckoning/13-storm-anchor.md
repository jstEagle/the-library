## 13. Storm Anchor

**Ines**

The barometer started lying to me at dawn.

Not lying exactly—more like confessing in a voice so quiet I almost missed it. I'd been reading glass for thirty years, since I was small enough to stand on my father's feet while he pointed at the needle and said, *The sea tells you everything twice, girl. Once in the sky and once in the water. Your job is to listen the first time.* The needle had been falling all night, slow as a secret, and by first light it had dropped twelve points and kept going like it had somewhere better to be.

"Category five," I said, tapping the glass. "Maybe worse. The cell's tightened overnight."

Oscar looked up from the chart table where she'd been sleeping sitting up, which told me everything about how well she trusted our anchorage. Her hair was doing something architectural. "Worse than the thing that already tried to kill us?"

"The monsoon has layers. Like an onion. Like grief." I handed her coffee because it was easier than explaining that the storm we'd been riding out was only the opening act, and the main event was now a black wall on the satellite feed, rotating toward us with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. "We can't stay in this cove. It opens north. When the wind backs around, we'll be on a lee shore with no room to run."

"So we run now."

"So we run now."

She was moving before I finished saying it, and I'll admit—that surprised me. Two days ago she'd caught me photographing the ledger behind her back, and the silence between us since had been the kind you could moor a boat in. Cold, deep, roomy enough for resentment. But when I said *run*, she ran. No argument, no negotiation, none of the lawyer-brain cross-examination she usually applied to my seamanship like she was billing me by the question.

Small mercies. Or maybe just the weather. Fear simplifies people. It had certainly simplified me—I hadn't thought about salvage rights or rival buyers or money at all since I'd seen that cell on the radar. Just the boat. Just the sea. Just the next hour.

We weighed anchor in gray light and ugly water, the *Vigil* complaining in every joint the way old boats do when they know something you don't. And there, at the mouth of the cove, exactly where I knew he'd be, sat Corvo's trawler.

He wasn't hiding anymore. That was the message. He sat broadside to the entrance, lights on, unhurried, a man watching a mouse decide which side of the trap to exit from. Through the binoculars I could see him on his aft deck with a mug in his hand, and I swear to you the man waved.

"He's going to follow us," Oscar said.

"He's going to try."

"And if the reef channels—"

"If the reef channels do what reef channels do," I said, "then Corvo has a choice between backing off and becoming a very expensive artificial reef himself. His boat draws three meters. Mine draws two-point-one. That half-meter is the only advantage God has given me in this entire affair, and I intend to spend it."

I put the helm over and took us out past him, close enough to see his smile, far enough to keep it theoretical. He didn't move to block us. He didn't need to. A cat doesn't chase the mouse out of the house; it just follows, comfortable in the knowledge that every door eventually closes.

The wall of cloud to the northwest had stopped looking like weather and started looking like geography—a second horizon, stacked and boiling, lit from inside by lightning that flickered the way a signal lamp does when someone far away is trying to tell you something urgent in a language made entirely of warnings.

I turned south. Toward the reefs.

Behind us, right on schedule, Corvo turned too.

* * *

**Oscar**

Here is what nobody tells you about being chased through a hurricane approach by the enforcer of the men who own your debt: after the first ten minutes of terror, it becomes almost boringly technical. There's the wind, which wants one thing. The current, which wants another. The boat, which wants to survive. And you, standing in the middle of these disagreements, holding a wheel, trying to be the referee.

Ines was the referee. I was mostly ballast with opinions.

She drove the *Vigil* the way I imagine she does everything—without wasted motion, without apology, without once looking back to check whether I was scared. She stood braced against the cockpit coaming with one hand on the wheel and the other on the chart she'd memorized an hour ago, and she steered us into the reef fields off Cape Reckoning's southern shoulder like a woman walking into her own kitchen in the dark.

"Sandbar shoal at two o'clock," she said. "Then the channel narrows. When I say jump, don't ask questions."

"I never ask questions. It's why my life is such a success."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, maybe—the way trust looks when it's still deciding whether to come back from the dead.

The rain found us first, fat warm drops that went from scattered to solid in the space of a breath, and then the world contracted to a circle about forty meters wide with the *Vigil* at the center of it. The wind arrived a minute later and it did not arrive politely. It hit us abeam and laid the ketch over until the rail kissed the sea, and I heard myself make a sound I'd deny under oath.

"Sheet in!" Ines shouted. "Hard!"

I hauled. The sail came across with a crack like a rifle shot and the boat stood up underneath us, shaking herself like a dog, and then we were running—actually running—down a corridor of green water between coral walls that I could see glowing pale beneath the surface on either side, close enough to touch if I'd been stupid enough to lean out.

Corvo's trawler was behind us somewhere, a smudge in the rain. I heard him before I saw him—a different engine note, heavier, laboring. Every minute we gained in the shallows was a minute his deeper hull couldn't buy back.

"He's turning," I shouted.

"He's smart. For now." Ines's eyes never left the water ahead, where the color changed from green to brown to white in bands that meant nothing to me and everything to her. "There's a cut through the outer reef. Half a mile of channel with breaking water on both sides. If we make it, we're in open water ahead of the storm's eye track and he's got to go around the whole bank—two hours, minimum, in conditions that will eat him alive."

"And if we don't make it?"

"Then we'll be a story fishermen tell each other," she said. "Hold on."

What followed, I have tried several times to describe to myself since, and the honest version is this: it was like being threaded through the eye of a needle by someone who loved the needle more than she feared the thread. The channel bent twice. The waves came at us quartering, steep and confused, stacking up where the outgoing tide fought the rising wind, and each one lifted the *Vigil*, aimed her briefly at destruction, and then—inexplicably, obediently—set her down again on the line Ines had chosen. Coral scraped somewhere beneath us once, a long grinding shudder I felt in my teeth, and Ines didn't even flinch. She just eased the wheel a hand's width and read the water the way other people read handwriting.

Then the last bend, and the white water fell away on both sides, and we were through.

I let out a breath I felt I'd been holding since Chapter One of my life.

"Ines Marlowe," I said, "if we survive the next six hours, I'm going to say something generous to you, and I want you to know in advance how much it will cost me."

"Noted," she said. "Save it. Look astern."

I looked. Through the rain curtain, Corvo's trawler had reappeared at the edge of the reef field—not following, just watching, holding position where the water was safe, a dark shape with its running lights burning patient and orange. Waiting out the storm on our flank. Keeping us in view.

"He knows where we're going," I said.

"He suspects. There's a difference." She glanced at the barometer, which had given up pretending and was falling like a stone down a well. "But yes. After the eye passes, he'll come. So we'd better be gone."

That was when the wave hit us sideways.

* * *

**Ines**

I never saw it properly. That's the truth of it, and the truth is all I've got left that's worth anything. A rogue cell inside the squall line, a wave out of sequence with the others, coming from the northeast while everything else came from the east—and it caught us beam-on at the exact moment I had both hands full of wheel keeping us off a bommie I'd spotted late.

It picked the *Vigil* up and threw her.

Not capsized. Thrown. Thirty degrees past anything a keel forgives, and Oscar went across the deck like cargo that hadn't been lashed, and I heard the lifeline snap—snapped clean, rotted core, a thing I'd meant to replace in harbor and hadn't—and then she was gone. Over the leeward rail into water the color of wet slate.

People talk about time slowing down in moments like that. It doesn't. Time speeds up. Everything happens at once and all your years of training compress into a single instruction, and mine was: *the danbuoy, the helm, the engine, in that order, and do not look at the water where she isn't.*

I threw the buoy. I lashed the helm. I put the engine in gear and came around in a full circle with the wind trying to lay us flat the entire way, and I will not describe what those ninety seconds were like, because I don't remember them as experience. I remember them as arithmetic. Wind angle. Drift rate. Search arc. My father's voice in my head, calm as tide tables: *Man overboard is not a tragedy, girl. It's a math problem. Solve it fast or it becomes the other thing.*

And then—there. Off the port bow, a hand, an arm, a flash of yellow foul-weather shell, and Oscar Coldharbour, who owed the Vane brothers more money than most people see in a lifetime, reached up out of the ocean and grabbed a floating buoy with both hands and held on like holding on was the one skill she'd mastered completely.

Getting her aboard nearly killed us both. The swim platform was surfing; the ladder was awash; every swell tried to slam her body into the hull like a battering ram. I got a line around my waist, lay flat on the deck, and reached down into that chaos, and she looked up at me through the rain with her hair plastered across her face and shouted something I couldn't hear over the wind.

"What?"

"I SAID—" another wave shoved her under, and she came up spitting, furious, magnificent, "—I'M NOT LETTING GO OF THE BUOY."

"You don't have to! Grab my wrist!"

"I'M KEEPING THE BUOY."

"OSCAR. MY WRIST."

She grabbed my wrist. I hauled. Between the lift of a swell and whatever gods look after fools and debtors, she came over the platform and onto the deck in a rush of seawater and profanity, and we lay there together on the boards, gasping, while the *Vigil* staggered on southward with the storm howling behind us like something cheated of a meal.

After a while she said, into the deck planking, "You came back for me."

"You were a math problem," I said. "I solve those."

She laughed. It was a wrecked, salt-burned laugh, half sob, and it did something to my chest I'm not going to write down here. Because the last time trust had passed between us, I'd broken it—me, with my camera and my secret copies and my private arithmetic about salvage shares—and she'd caught me at it, and neither of us had said a word since. Words were finished between us. We'd used them all up.

But she'd kept hold of the buoy. And I'd come around in a circle in a building category five to pick her up. And some things, it turns out, are said better with a danbuoy and a line around your waist than they ever were with words.

"Your lifeline's snapped," I said finally. "Below. Now. Harness on. I mean it."

"Aye, Captain," she said, and for once the irony in it was thin enough to see daylight through.

* * *

**Oscar**

Below decks in the *Vigil*'s cabin was like being inside a drum played by an angry giant, but it was dry, and dry was a currency I'd learned to value. I clipped my harness to the jackline bolted through the cabin sole—Ines had installed it years ago, for a father who never got to use it, though she didn't say that and I didn't ask—and I sat on the floor with my back against the hull and listened to the storm try to take the boat apart above my head.

Ines came down an hour later, soaked through her oilskins, gray-faced, hands steady. Always steady. She checked the bilge, checked the engine, checked the chart, and only then allowed herself to sit across from me, wedged in the corner where the motion was kindest.

"We're through the worst of the leading edge," she said. "Eye passes over us around midnight. Then the back side. Then—" she tapped the barometer, which had bottomed out and begun, grudgingly, to climb, "—then it's over, and we find out what's left."

I nodded. My hands were still shaking. I watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, the way you'd watch someone else's hands.

"Ines." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "On deck. Before. You turned the boat around."

"It was a math problem."

"You turned a thirty-ton ketch through a following sea in a building category five, alone, for a woman who caught you selling her grandfather's grave out from under her." I looked at her. She looked at the bulkhead. "Why?"

Long silence. The wind screamed through the rigging overhead like it wanted an answer too.

"Because my father drowned in that cave," she said at last, quietly, "chasing the same wreck you inherited. And in thirty years I have never once turned my back on a person in the water. Not for a claim. Not for gold. Not for anything." She finally met my eyes. "The photographs were wrong. I knew it when I took them. I told myself it was for the memorial—for the divers, for Dad—but it was for me, same as it always is. Same as it was for the Vanes' grandfather, probably, when he signed that ledger. Everyone steals for good reasons. That's how you spot a thief: they've always got a reason."

"That's bleak."

"That's true." She pulled off her boots and dumped out a surprising amount of seawater. "You kept the ledger pages safe even when Corvo offered to erase your debt. I watched you do it. So we're both idiots. Idiots recognize each other."

Outside, the world ended and began again in strokes of lightning. The *Vigil* rode it, groaning but game, carrying us south ahead of the eye. And I sat there in the dark with a woman I had every reason to distrust, feeling the strange lightness of a debt I hadn't known I carried quietly canceling itself—not the Vane debt, that beast was still alive and circling out there beyond the rain—but the other one. The one between us.

"Ines," I said. "When this is over. The photographs. Show me them. All of them. And we'll decide together who sees what."

Her head came up. For a long moment she studied me, looking for the trap, the lawyer, the angle.

"Together," she repeated.

"Together. I'm tired of arithmetic done alone. It's how people drown."

Something in her shoulders let go—an inch, no more, but in Ines Marlowe an inch is a mile. She reached into the locker, pulled out the waterproof pouch with the camera and the memory cards, and set it on the chart table between us. Not handing it over. Just—putting it where both of us could reach it. Which, I was beginning to understand, was the only kind of trust that survived at sea.

Then the radio crackled. Static, static, static—and through it, faint and cheerful as ever, a voice I knew.

*"Coldharbour. Can you hear me, Coldharbour? Corvo sends his regards. He says to tell you the eye gives you six hours' head start. He says the Vanes are reasonable men, but the sea isn't—and he knows where you're going."*

A pause. Rain hammering the deck above.

*"Oh—and he says to check your chart tube. See if anything's missing."*

I looked at the chart table. At the locker where we'd stowed my grandfather's hidden chart—the one showing the second chamber.

The tube was there. The tube was empty.
