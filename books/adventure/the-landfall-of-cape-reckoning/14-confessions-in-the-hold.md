## 14. Confessions in the Hold

The storm found us at two in the morning, which felt about right. Storms and creditors keep the same hours.

We'd run for the lee of a drowned volcanic plug the charts called the Old Man's Teeth—a cluster of black rock islets rising out of nowhere like the knuckles of something that had once tried to climb out of the sea and thought better of it. Ines anchored bow and stern in the mangrove shallows behind them, double-bitted, crossed, the way you do when you expect the night to try to take the boat away from you. Then she came below, sealed the hatches, and sat down across from me with a hurricane lamp between us like a referee.

Outside, the world ended on schedule.

I don't know how else to describe a Category-five landfall except that it sounds like the ocean has decided, personally, to come collect you. The wind didn't howl so much as *insist*—a sustained, muscular roar that made the hull flex and the rigging sing notes I'd never heard a boat sing before, none of them comforting. Rain hit the deck above like gravel thrown by an angry mob. Every few minutes something heavy struck the topsides—driftwood, maybe, or part of somebody's roof—and Ines would glance up at the overhead without expression, cataloguing it, the way other people check their phones.

"So," she said, over the din. "While we wait."

"While we wait," I agreed.

Neither of us said anything for a while. The lamp swung on its gimbal. Somewhere aft, water sloshed in the bilge where it was supposed to be, which after the last week counted as good news.

Here's what nobody tells you about being trapped in a steel box with another human being while the sky tries to kill you both: eventually, one of you is going to talk. It's not courage. It's physics. Silence takes up room, and the room was shrinking.

"I nearly took his deal," I said.

Ines looked at me. She has a way of looking at people that makes you feel like a specimen she hasn't decided whether to pin yet.

"Corvo," I said. "Before he came ashore. On the satellite call—he offered to forgive the debt. All of it. Forty-one thousand, plus the interest they calculate in a way that should be illegal in every jurisdiction including maritime law, plus—" I waved a hand. "Everything. Gone. For the ledger."

"And?"

"And I said I'd think about it." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, the laugh of someone standing on a ledge explaining that they're just admiring the view. "That's the honest answer, Ines. He asked me for the ledger and I told him I'd think about it. I didn't say no. I want you to understand that when I tell you the rest of this, because the rest of it is going to sound better than it deserves."

The lamp swung. The wind took a fresh grip on the boat and shook it, and we rode it out, shoulder to shoulder against the bulkhead because sitting upright had stopped being an option around midnight.

"The rest of it," Ines said.

"My grandfather died owing money." I'd never actually said it out loud before. Forty years of family silence, and it turns out all it takes to break it is a hundred-and-fifty-knot wind and a woman who looks at you like a pinned specimen. "Not much. A few hundred. Enough that my mother grew up hearing about it, enough that it became this—this family story. The Coldharbour curse. Grandfather Elias went to sea with debts and never came home, and the moral of the story, as it was told at every dinner table of my childhood, was: *don't be like him.*"

"And you were exactly like him."

"I am *exactly* like him." I spread my hands. "Storm chaser. Debtor. Out here chasing a legend instead of a paycheck. When Corvo offered to wipe the slate, do you know what went through my head? Not *the Vanes killed him.* Not *that ledger is forty years of truth.* What went through my head was: *Mom would finally be able to sleep.*"

"That's not nothing."

"It's not nothing, but it's not the whole thing either. The whole thing is that for about four seconds—four, maybe five—I calculated whether my grandfather's name was worth forty-one thousand dollars, and the calculation did not immediately come back in his favor." I pressed my head back against the hull and let the vibration of the storm rattle through my skull. "He let me live, you know. Corvo. He could have taken the ledger off my body and reported that I ran afoul of the weather, and who would ever have questioned it? A storm chaser dying in a monsoon? That's not murder, that's *genre.* And I stood there on your deck doing arithmetic with his offer, and the only reason I'm here now is that I got lucky with my conscience, not strong with it. There's a difference. I need you to know there's a difference."

Ines was quiet long enough that I started composing my own eulogy in my head. Then she reached down beside her seat, pried up a floorboard—I would learn later that everything on her ketch comes up if you know where to push—and took out a flask. She unscrewed it, drank, handed it to me. Rum, cheap, warm, perfect.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because you saved my life twice this week and lied to me once, and I've decided the saving outweighs the lying, and I can't build anything on top of a lie I haven't admitted I almost committed myself." I took another pull and passed it back. "Also because the storm is going to kill us and I refuse to die having been the only coward in the room."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. On Ines Marlowe's face, that qualifies as laughter.

* * *

She held the flask for a long time without drinking, turning it in her fingers, watching the lamp through the glass. Outside, a gust came through that lifted the whole boat—I felt the keel leave the water, briefly, weightlessly, like being picked up by something enormous and curious—and set us back down with a slam that rattled the dishes in the galley rack. Neither of us flinched. We were past flinching. Flinching is for the first six hours of a storm; after that you achieve a kind of Buddhist acceptance punctuated by bilge checks.

"My father dove for the Reckoning," she said.

I knew some of this. Chapter ten of our acquaintance, the mangrove anchorage, the night she'd talked with her hands for the only time I'd seen. But she hadn't finished the story. I hadn't understood why until now.

"He wasn't a salvager originally. He was a fisherman. Good ones, my family—good boats, bad luck, the usual Cape Reckoning inheritance. And then in 'ninety-one the Vane family bought up half the fishing licenses on this coast, and the half they didn't buy, they priced out. My father had a boat and three daughters and no license, and a man named Aldous Vane—the grandfather of the two cheerful gentlemen currently trying to bankrupt you—came to our house and made him an offer."

"Diving work."

"Salvage survey. The Vanes had spent thirty years looking for the Reckoning's cargo. Family obsession. Aldous used to say it was the family's birthright, which tells you everything, because you don't call stolen goods a birthright unless you know they're stolen." She drank. "My father knew the water better than any man on the coast. Knew the currents, knew the caves, knew every reef from here to the border. Aldous Vane paid him to find a wreck that Aldous's own father had helped sink."

The lamp swung. I did the math I'd been avoiding since the ledger. "Your father found it."

"He found the cave." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone carrying something heavy across ice. "Nineteen ninety-four. He came back with a fix on it—coordinates, depth, tide tables worked out to the minute. He wouldn't tell Aldous where it was until the contract was rewritten. He wanted a partnership. A share. He had three daughters and he'd spent his whole life watching other men get rich off water he fished for free, and he thought—for once—that knowing something was worth something."

"And Aldous Vane didn't renegotiate."

"Aldous Vane sent him back down with a new dive partner and instructions to mark the entrance." She turned the flask over. "There was a storm coming in. My father said the window was wrong, the surge would funnel through the cave system, the vis would go to zero. Aldous said the window was fine. My father went anyway, because refusing Aldous Vane meant losing the contract, and losing the contract meant losing the boat, and the boat was the last thing standing between my family and the road." She stopped. Started again. "They found his tank on the reef three days later. Never found him. The dive partner came up alone and told a story about a cave-in, and Aldous Vane paid him off, and the partner moved to the mainland and lived another thirty years, which is its own kind of testimony, if you ask me. Nobody asked me."

"Ines—"

"Fourteen divers have died on that coast looking for the Reckoning. Fourteen that I've documented. Fishermen, salvagers, two kids in a rubber raft in '03 who got curious about the caves. Every one of them poor. Every one of them chasing a fortune that the Vanes' grandfather stole in the first place, because the legend of the lost treasure is worth more to the Vanes than the treasure itself—it keeps everyone looking seaward while the family counts its money inland." She looked up at me, and her eyes in the lamplight were absolutely steady, which somehow made it worse. "I don't want the gold, Oscar. I've had eleven years to want the gold and I find I still don't. I want a wall. Granite, on the point at Cape Reckoning, where the tide legends come from. With fourteen names on it. Starting with Tomas Marlowe. And I want the Vanes' family name on it too—not as mourners. As the reason."

The storm hit us broadside then, a full-body blow that put the lamp out for three seconds. In the dark I heard her breathing, even and deliberate, and mine, less so. The lamp caught again. The world reassembled itself: hull, bilge, woman, flask, rain.

"That's why you photographed the ledger," I said.

"That's why I photographed the ledger." She didn't look away. "A rival salvager's money builds a faster wall than a lawsuit does. I told myself that. I also told myself you'd sell out for forty-one thousand, so we were even in our arithmetic." She held out the flask. "I'm not asking you to forgive it tonight. I'm telling you the number I was doing it for isn't a number. It's fourteen names."

I took the flask and drank, and the rum burned all the way down, and I thought about my grandfather's handwriting in that ledger—*E.C.,* neat, patient, the hand of a man keeping accounts against the day they'd matter—and I thought about how debt works, really works, how it's never money. Money is just the receipt. The debt is always something else. The Vanes had been collecting on my grandfather for forty years, and on her father for thirty, and neither of us had known we were paying.

"Okay," I said. "New terms."

"You're in no position to dictate terms. You're a passenger on my boat in a Category-five."

"I'm always in no position. It's my best negotiating posture." I leaned forward. "The photographs stay on this boat. Not sold—not yet. When this is over, they go into the public record alongside the ledger, all of it, together, in the open, where it can't be bought or buried or bargained away. Your wall gets built on testimony, not on a rival's hush money. Because the day you sell those photos, you become a person with a secret worth buying, and the Vanes buy people, Ines. It's the only thing they're good at."

"And if the lawsuit dies and the wall never gets built?"

"Then I'll stand on the point with a chisel and start carving myself." I handed the flask back. "But it won't die. You saw the ledger. Names, dates, cargo manifests, my grandfather's own hand itemizing what they took and who took it. That's not evidence, that's a confession with a binding. No lawyer on earth loses that case. No newspaper on earth ignores it."

She studied me for a long moment, the way she studies weather, the way she studies reefs—looking for the rip current underneath.

"All right," she said finally. "The photos stay aboard. Joint custody." She screwed the cap onto the flask. "And Coldharbour—if you ever do arithmetic with my father's grave the way you did with your grandfather's name, I'll know. I always know. I grew up reading water."

"Noted." I managed something that was mostly a smile. "For what it's worth, I'm done with arithmetic. I've seen where it lands. It lands in a flooding cave with the tide coming in."

"Poetic."

"It's the monsoon. Everything's poetic. Ask me again in a week and I'll be back to sarcasm."

She almost smiled. Almost. On Ines Marlowe that's a parade with confetti.

* * *

We kept the watch together through the worst of it, which is to say we sat on the floor of the cabin with our backs against the same bulkhead, passing the flask, shouting observations to each other over the wind like scientists at the end of the world. Around four, the eye came through—we'd tracked it all night on the barometer, the needle falling and falling until it seemed physically impossible for there to be any air left to lose—and for twenty minutes the wind stopped entirely.

The silence was enormous. It pressed on the ears. Stars stood over the mangroves, obscene and calm, and the water lay flat as poured metal, and somewhere out in that flatness Corvo's boat was riding it out too, waiting, patient as arithmetic.

"We should move," Ines murmured. "While the eye's over us. Check the anchors, pump the bilge."

"In a minute."

She glanced at me.

"I just want to stand on deck for one minute," I said, "in a world that isn't trying to kill us. Is that allowed on this vessel?"

"Sixty seconds. Then we work."

We went up. The air was warm and utterly still and smelled of salt and crushed leaves. The Old Man's Teeth stood black against the stars. For sixty seconds neither of us said anything, and it was the best conversation I'd had in years.

Then the far edge of the eye arrived like a freight train, and we went below and worked, and the second half of the storm was worse than the first, because the second half always is.

It was near dawn, wedged in the cabin while Ines re-stowed gear that the storm had redistributed with violent editorial intent, that I noticed the floorboard. Not her hiding place—another one, forward, under the berth where I'd been sleeping, sprung loose by the hull-slamming. I pried it up expecting bilge junk.

What I found was oilcloth. Wrapped, tied, waxed at the seams—the wrapping of a man who expected his package to get wet.

Inside was a chart. Hand-drawn, hand-annotated, the paper soft with age. The coastline of Cape Reckoning, rendered in a careful draftsman's hand I recognized instantly, because I'd spent forty years recognizing it in old letters: *E.C.*

And in the corner, in the same hand, a note. Small, precise, addressed—to my astonishment—to no one, to whoever, to the future:

*"If you found the hold, you did not find everything. The cargo sleeps deeper. Follow the second channel at slack water, past the pillar, where the water breathes."*

Below it, a sketch. A chamber. And a depth figure that made my stomach drop through the hull.

"Ines," I said. "You're going to want to see this."

She came over. She read it twice. Outside, the storm was finally dying, the wind dropping to a snarl, and through the leaking gray light of dawn I watched her face do something I'd never seen it do.

Hope. Arriving exactly like a trap.
