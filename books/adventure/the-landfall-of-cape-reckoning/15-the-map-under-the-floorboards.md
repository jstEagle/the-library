## 15. The Map Under the Floorboards

The storm left us a bill, and like all bills in my experience, it was itemized.

I stood in the hold of the *Magpie's Rest* the morning after landfall and took inventory of what the sea had decided to repossess. The starboard rail: gone. The mizzen shroud: frayed to a handful of strands that had been holding on out of politeness rather than strength. Two hatches sprung, a dinghy punctured, and—this was the one that made me sit down on an upturned crate and breathe through my nose for a while—a seam above the waterline aft that had opened like a bad idea and let in enough of the Indian Ocean to float half my tools.

"Your boat," Oscar said from the companionway, "is a sieve with opinions."

"My boat is a ketch of distinguished age," I said, "and she's earned every leak."

"She's earned a shipyard."

"She's earned respect." I tossed her a bilge sponge. "Start there. You wanted partnership; this is what it looks like. It looks like sponging."

She caught the sponge against her chest and gave me the look she'd been perfecting since the cave—the one that said she was cataloguing my flaws for future publication. But she knelt at the bilge and started working, and that was the thing about Oscar Coldharbour I was learning to stop resenting: she complained like a rich woman and worked like a wreck diver. Her hands were blistered from three days of repairs already, and she hadn't once asked whose fault the storm was, which put her ahead of most people I've shared a deck with.

We worked through the morning in the cove where we'd anchored out the worst of it—a green pocket of water behind a headland, mangroves leaning over us like gossips, the barometer still sulking low but steady. The monsoon wasn't finished with the coast. We both knew it. The second landfall was coming, the way a second wave comes when you've barely surfaced from the first. But for now the sky was that bruised, exhausted grey that follows a beating, and the sea lay flat as spilled paint, and there was work to do, and work is the only prayer I've ever trusted.

The floorboards came up because they had to. The seam damage ran under the aft cabin sole, which meant pulling planks that my father had laid down before I was born—or that someone had laid down before him; the *Rest* had changed hands more times than a coin in a port town. I'd owned her eleven years and never pulled those boards. There'd never been reason. Boats keep their histories under their floors the way houses do, and most of them are boring: old caulking, a rusted tin of screws, the skeleton of some previous owner's ambitions.

I pried the first board at nine o'clock. By ten I had four of them stacked by the companionway and a view of stringers and bilge stringers and forty years of salt-crusted history, and Oscar was lying flat on her stomach with a headlamp, peering into the dark between frames.

"There's something wedged aft," she said. "Starboard side. Against the hull."

"A dead rat?"

"It's too flat for a rat."

"Everything's too flat for a rat until you get close." But I got close. I handed her the pry bar and she worked it into the gap with the delicacy of a surgeon and the vocabulary of a dockworker, and then it came free—a bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with tarred twine that had gone hard as wire with age. It was longer than my forearm and heavier than it looked, and when I lifted it, something inside shifted with the dry whisper of paper.

We both went very quiet. You learn to go quiet around things like that. The sea gives you gifts sometimes, and every salvage man and woman I've ever known will tell you the same thing: the gifts are never free. They're bait.

"That's not mine," I said.

"It's not mine either." Oscar turned the bundle over. "Unless—" She stopped. I watched her do the arithmetic. My grandfather's boat, her grandfather's disappearance, a cave full of Reckoning timbers, a ledger proving his partners drowned him for his cargo. "The *Rest* is old enough. Where did you get her?"

"Bought her off an estate sale in Port Meridian eleven years ago. The family didn't know what she was worth. Neither did I, apparently." I worked at the twine with my knife. The blade was sharp; the twine was harder. Forty years of tar does that. "Whoever wrapped this meant it to last."

"Or meant it to be found by the right person," Oscar said quietly. "And no one ever came."

That thought sat between us while I sawed. Because there was only one person who could have hidden a chart in the bilge of a working ketch and then vanished off Cape Reckoning, and his granddaughter was kneeling six inches away from me with salt water on her knees and her whole inheritance about to come up out of my floor.

The oilcloth fell open.

It was a chart—not a published one, not a hydrographic office product with its tidy soundings and its polite margins. This was hand-drawn on drafting linen, the kind salvagers used because it didn't rot. The ink had faded to sepia but the lines were confident, drawn by someone who knew coasts the way priests know scripture. And I knew the coast it showed, because I had spent eleven years and most of my savings learning it line by line: Cape Reckoning, the cliffs, the reef shelf, the deep channel—and there, tucked under the cliff face where no chartmaker had ever bothered to put anything, a cave mouth rendered in fine, patient strokes.

With annotations.

Oscar read them aloud, and her voice did something strange halfway through, dropped half an octave, went rough. I understood why. I've heard men read their fathers' handwriting at funerals. It sounds like that.

"*Inner channel runs dry below two fathoms at spring lows,*" she read. "*Second chamber lies beyond the squeeze. Cargo secured here.*" A small cross, inked careful as a signature. "*They think it all went down with her. Let them.*"

"Let them," I repeated.

"He hid it." Oscar's finger traced the passage on the chart, the little dashed line running past the cave mouth into the rock. "He found the cave before he died. He moved the cargo—whatever was left of it, whatever they didn't take—into a second chamber. And then he wrote it down and hid the map in a boat, and went back for—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Went back for something else. Or someone made sure he didn't leave."

"The ledger said his partners took the cargo," I said. "Most of it."

"Some of it." She tapped the annotation. "*Cargo secured here.* Present tense. He's telling whoever finds this that it's still there. Gold, Ines. Documents. Whatever the Reckoning carried that was worth killing four men over." She looked up at me, and her eyes were doing the thing I'd seen them do in the cave when the tide first sealed us in—that bright, terrible light, wonder and dread sharing a socket. "Worth enough to erase everything."

I should have said something sensible then. I'm told I'm capable of it. What I actually said was nothing, because I was looking at the chart and doing my own arithmetic, and my arithmetic had a name on it: my father, who died thirty meters down off Cape Reckoning chasing exactly this, except he'd been chasing a rumor, a drunk fisherman's story about a cave that breathed with the tide. He'd had no chart. He'd had faith and a bad regulator and twenty-two years of believing his own father's stories about the Reckoning's treasure. The sea took him the way it takes all believers—politely, permanently.

And here was the proof, in ink, that he'd been right. That the cave existed. That the chamber existed. That everything he died short of finding had been sitting under the cliffs the whole time, waiting for a woman who didn't believe in anything to pull up her own floorboards.

Hope arrived like a trap. That's the only way I can describe it. It walked in wearing the face of everything I'd ever wanted and it closed around my ankle before I could decide whether I wanted to be caught.

* * *

We spread the chart out on the cockpit table and weighted the corners with winch handles, and neither of us touched it for a long moment, the way you don't touch a contract until you've read the fine print. Except there was no fine print. There was just the coast, and the cave, and the cross.

"The squeeze," Oscar said. "Beyond the squeeze. Look at the depth markings on the inner channel." She traced them. "Two fathoms at spring lows. That's not a dive, Ines. That's a crawl. Flooded passage, maybe four meters, maybe less clearance. You'd do it on breath or hookah, in the dark, in water that's been sitting undisturbed for forty years."

"I've done worse."

"You've done worse alone. This time you'd have me topside, which statistically makes it worse." She sat back. "And we'd be doing it during the second landfall window, because that's the only spring low we'll get before Corvo stops pretending he isn't coming."

There it was. The other name in the room, the one we'd been stepping around since the satellite call. Corvo. The Vanes' settlement offer, delivered with a smile that didn't reach the parts of the face where intentions live. Forgive the debt for the ledger alone—which meant the ledger, and then, if Corvo was the man I thought he was, the witnesses. Oscar had stalled him. We both knew stalling was a currency that depreciated fast.

"If we run," I said, "we run now, north, and we sell the story to the first newspaper with a fax machine, and we spend the rest of our lives looking over our shoulders while lawyers eat the truth alive. The Vanes own judges the way other families own horses."

"And if we stay—"

"We dive the chamber, we take what's ours, and we settle this the way it started. With weight and water." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize the hunger in it. Eleven years I'd told myself I was a salvager, not a treasure hunter. Salvage is honest work: you raise what's lost, you split fair, you sleep. Treasure hunting is grief with a boat. And here I was, planning a dive in a monsoon, and the plan felt like coming home.

Oscar was watching me with that look again. Not judgment. Recognition. That was somehow worse.

"My father dove for this," I said, before I could stop myself. "Cape Reckoning. Everyone said he was chasing a myth. Maybe he was. Maybe the myth was true and the timing killed him. Either way—" I put my hand flat on the chart, over the little cross. "Either way I'm finishing the dive. If the gold's there, it pays for the memorial. Every diver the Vanes' ancestors sent down and never sent for. Names on stone, finally. And if it's not there—"

"If it's not there, we'll know," Oscar said. "And knowing has been expensive so far, but it's the only thing I've ever bought that held its value."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out cracked, the first real laugh since the storm, and Oscar almost smiled, which for her is a seismic event.

Then she went still. I watched her look at the chart again, really look, and I saw the trap close on her too.

"Ines. The annotation. Read it again."

"*They think it all went down with her. Let them.*"

"No—the other part. *Let them.*" Her finger hovered over the ink. "My grandfather wrote *let them*. He knew who they were. He knew they'd come looking eventually. He hid the cargo and he hid the map and he let them believe it was lost." She looked up. "Why would a man hide a fortune and never come back for it? Why would he leave it in a hole in the rock for forty years?"

"Because he was dead."

"Because he was dead," she agreed. "But before that. He drew this chart the way a man draws a chart when he expects someone specific to find it. Someone who'd know his hand. Someone who'd know the boat." She swallowed. "This was addressed, Ines. To whoever inherited his search. And it waited eleven years in your bilge because I wasn't looking, and you didn't know what you were carrying."

The mangroves ticked against the hull. Somewhere out past the headland, the sea was already starting to move, the long slow swell that comes ahead of a big system, the ocean clearing its throat.

"So we're the addressees," I said.

"We're the addressees." Oscar rolled the chart carefully, oilcloth and all, and held it against her chest the way you hold a letter you haven't finished reading. "Which means the gold is ours to take. And it means the Vanes will figure that out the moment word moves, and Corvo is already one headland closer than we'd like, and the next spring low is in—" she checked the tide table pinned by the nav station, and I watched her do the math and watch the math come back ugly— "thirty-one hours. Right in the teeth of the second landfall."

"Of course it is," I said. "The sea doesn't give gifts. It gives terms."

Oscar looked at me across the chart, across the ruined cabin and the leaking seam and the whole beautiful disaster of the last week, and she nodded slowly.

"Then we accept the terms," she said. "Get your dive gear ready, Marlowe. We're going back to Cape Reckoning."

Outside, far off to the south, the horizon had begun to go the color of a bruise, and the swell was rising, patient as a creditor, and somewhere behind that grey wall of weather a man named Corvo was doing his own arithmetic, and I would have bet the *Magpie's Rest*—what was left of her—that his numbers were better than ours.
