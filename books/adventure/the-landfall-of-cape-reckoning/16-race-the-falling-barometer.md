## 16. Race the Falling Barometer

*Oscar*

The barometer fell like a confession—fast, then all at once.

I stood on Ines's ketch at dawn with the instrument cupped in my hand, watching the needle slide through twenty-nine inches and keep going, and I thought about how my grandfather must have felt reading numbers like these. Except he'd been reading them from a deck that was about to become his grave, and I was reading them from a deck that belonged to a woman who still hadn't decided whether she trusted me. The needle didn't care. Needles never do. They just tell you what's coming and let you decide how stupid to be about it.

"We have maybe fourteen hours," I said. "Maybe twelve."

Ines was already at the winch, hauling the anchor in hand over hand because the motorized capstan had drowned in the last blow along with most of my optimism. She didn't look up. "Then stop talking and start coiling."

That was the thing about Ines Marlowe. Other people gave you speeches. Ines gave you rope.

We'd spent two days in the mangrove shallows patching her ketch—the *Salt Mercy*, which sounded less like a boat name and more like something you'd whisper over a body—and drilling holes in the hull. Not out of madness. Out of rehearsal. If Corvo came for us again, if he sealed us in anywhere the way he'd tried to seal us in the cave, we wanted to know exactly how fast this old girl could take on water and exactly where we'd cut her loose to swim free. There is a particular kind of intimacy in planning your own sinking. It either breaks a partnership or cures it. Ours had come out the other side mostly cured, which by our standards was practically a marriage.

Now we were going back. Back toward Cape Reckoning, toward the second chamber, toward the gold and the ledger pages and my grandfather's final accusations sitting in the dark where forty years of tide hadn't managed to erase them. And one headland behind us—or soon to be—Corvo, with his smile like a dock cleat and his men and whatever orders the Vane brothers had given him when they realized their family fortune was built on a drowning.

The weather window was real but narrow. The monsoon's first landfall had spent itself against the coast; the second, the big one, the one the forecasters were calling *historic* with the flat calm of men who get paid whether anyone believes them or not, was still gathering over open water. Between the two systems sat a seam of almost-quiet. Twelve hours, maybe fourteen, of wind that would be brutal instead of murderous. A door opening and closing. We intended to be on the far side of it when it shut.

"Course?" I asked, taking the wheel while Ines set the main.

"One-eighty-five to clear the shoal, then straight down the trough until the cape." She squinted south, where the horizon wore a bruise-colored collar. "Corvo will take the outside line. Deeper water, faster, but longer."

"You're hoping he takes the outside line."

"I'm counting on him being rich enough to think deep water belongs to him." She hauled the last of the main home and finally looked at me. Her face did what it always did, which was give away nothing except the exact amount she chose to give away. "He'll be behind us by an hour. Maybe two. That's not a lead, Coldharbour. That's a rumor."

"A rumor I intend to make true."

"Make it true quietly. Screaming engines carry over water."

So we went. The *Salt Mercy* leaned into the first miles with the grudging obedience of an animal that has decided, provisionally, not to bite its rider. I stood at the helm and watched Cape Reckoning's direction grow a storm out of nothing, cloud stacking on cloud in towers so tall they bent with the height, and I felt the thing I always felt before a chase: that bright, terrible clarity where fear stops being a weight and becomes fuel. My grandfather had sailed into this coast chasing cargo. I was sailing into it chasing truth. The sea, as far as I could tell, didn't distinguish between the two. It just kept the receipts.

Behind us, right on schedule, a white hull appeared at the edge of the world.

* * *

*Ines*

Corvo's boat was a sixty-foot motor yacht painted the color of money changing hands, and he ran her like a man who had never once considered the possibility of a reef. I watched him come down the outside line with binoculars and felt the old salvager's contempt rise in me like bilge water. Rich men's boats all drove the same way—all throttle and no listening, as if the ocean were a road someone else was responsible for maintaining.

"He's gaining," Oscar said.

"He's burning fuel he can't replace until the storm passes." I passed her the glasses. "Look at his wake. He's running flat out. That's not pursuit, that's panic wearing a nice jacket."

Oscar studied the horizon for a long moment. When she handed the glasses back, there was something new in her face—not fear exactly. Recognition. "His bosses told him what happens if he comes back without it," she said. "The Vanes don't fire people, Ines. They *retire* them."

I knew about the Vanes. Everyone on this coast knew about the Vanes, the way everyone knows about undertow—you don't need to see it to respect it. Cheerful men, the brothers. Laughing lenders. My father had taken Vane money once, thirty years ago, to fund his diving seasons. He'd spent the rest of his life paying it back at rates that made arithmetic feel like violence. When he died off Cape Reckoning looking for the *Reckoning*'s cargo, the Vanes sent flowers. I was eleven. I remember thinking the flowers were the most expensive thing that had ever entered our house.

So yes. I understood what was chasing us. I'd been chased by it my whole life in slower boats.

The wind built through the morning the way the forecasts promised, fifteen knots becoming twenty becoming twenty-five, and the *Salt Mercy* took it with the stubborn grace of a vessel built by someone who expected the sea to misbehave. I'd refitted her myself over nine years, plank by plank, fitting out salvage runs around the edges of other people's claims. Every scar in her hull was mine. Every repair. When she groaned in the gusts it sounded less like complaint and more like conversation, and I answered her the only way I knew: trim here, ease there, keep her bow into the steep stuff and let the long swells pass underneath like rumors of worse weather to come.

Oscar, to her credit, was learning the boat. Two days ago she'd been a passenger with opinions. Now she read the sails' shadows on the water, anticipated my commands half a beat early, and steered through the building chop with the same reckless precision she brought to everything—which was to say, precisely enough to work and recklessly enough to frighten anyone watching.

"You're enjoying this," I said, as a wave broke green over the foredeck and she laughed—a short, sharp sound torn sideways by the wind.

"I'm *focused*. Enjoying comes later, if we live."

"That's the correct order."

But I watched her, between sail changes, and I saw what focus was costing her. Every mile south brought her closer to the cave where her grandfather's handwriting waited in the dark, naming names. Closer to proof that the debt strangling her—the debt the Vanes held over her like a hook through the gill—was itself stolen goods. She wasn't racing Corvo. She was racing toward an answer she'd been carrying since she was a child: *what happened to the man who vanished?* Some questions you spend decades avoiding because you already know the shape of the answer and dread its weight.

The cape grew out of the haze by early afternoon, black cliffs wearing white crowns of breaking sea. And Corvo's yacht, damn him, had shaved an hour off our lead by ignoring every rule of prudent seamanship between here and there.

"He'll beat us to the anchorage," Oscar said.

"No." I checked the chart, then the water color ahead, then the wind, doing the arithmetic that had kept me alive on this coast for twenty years. "He'll beat us to the *easy* route. But there's another way in."

Oscar looked at the reefs off our port bow, where the swell was detonating against coral in columns of spray fifty feet high, and her expression performed a small, controlled collapse. "Through that?"

"Through the channel behind the outer reef. Six feet at low water, ten at mid. It saves four miles and puts us inside before him." I met her eyes. "It also requires steering that doesn't forgive mistakes."

A grin spread across her face, slow and slightly unhinged, and I recognized it because I'd seen it in mirrors my whole life. "Ines Marlowe," she said, "are you offering to teach me your reef?"

"I'm offering to drive. You're going to stand next to me and call the water color, and if you say 'white' I'm going to believe you, and if you're wrong we're both going to die, so try to be right."

She took the helm beside me and we went in.

* * *

*Oscar*

There is a moment in every storm chase—I've had hundreds—when the data stops mattering and the world becomes pure present tense. No debt. No deadline. No dead grandfather, no cheerful creditors, no ledger full of liars. Just the next three seconds, repeated indefinitely, forever.

The reef channel was three seconds at a time for eleven minutes.

Ines drove like a surgeon who hated the patient. Her hands moved on the wheel in small corrections, constant, almost invisible, and the *Salt Mercy* threaded the coral with barely a fathom under her keel while the reef roared on both sides like an audience that hadn't decided whether to applaud or eat us. My job was the water. Green meant go. Brown meant coral shallow enough to scrape paint and worse. White meant the reef was breathing through that spot, and white meant turn.

"Green. Green—brown, bear left—" 

She bore left without comment. A coral head slid past to starboard close enough that I could have touched it, could have counted the polyps, and then we were through the pinch and the channel opened and the swell rolled under us, huge and smooth, lifting the ketch like a hand.

"Ten feet," Ines said, exhaling for what I realized was the first time in eleven minutes. "Told you."

"You told me nothing. You said *try to be right.*"

"I was right about you being right. Different skill."

We came out the far side into the lee of the cape, and I looked back the way we'd come, and there—rounding the headland a mile astern, white hull heeling hard, running flat out through water that would have shredded a careless keel—came Corvo. He hadn't taken the reef. He'd taken the long way and pushed his boat like a whip, and now he was coming down on us with the patience removed.

"He sees us," I said.

"He was always going to see us." Ines began coiling the reef line, quick and economical. "Question was never whether he'd catch sight of us. Question is whether he catches *us*, and the answer to that is the tide."

The plan was simple, which is to say it was insane, which is to say Ines had designed it. The cave mouth beneath the cliffs—the one the freak low tide had exposed, the one that had nearly eaten us once already—opened only near slack low water. Inside, the passage to the second chamber ran flooded but navigable by dive, and the chamber itself held air, gold, and my grandfather's last testament. Corvo couldn't follow us into the cave in his yacht; the entrance was too tight, too shallow, too treacherous unless you knew it. But he could wait outside. He could anchor across the mouth like a spider on a web and let the sea do his work.

Unless the sea was busy.

"The second landfall makes landfall tonight," Ines said, reading my thoughts the way she read water color. "Barometer's at twenty-eight-six and falling. When it hits, no boat stays anchored off that cape. Not his, not ours, not the Queen's. He has to choose: come in after us on foot and by dive, in a rising storm, or run for shelter and come back when the coast is a graveyard."

"And if he chooses wrong?"

"Rich men usually do. They're used to the ocean making exceptions."

We rounded into the cove below the cliffs with an hour of ebb left, and I killed the engine and let the ketch glide, silent, into water so sheltered it seemed embarrassed by the chaos offshore. Above us the cliffs rose two hundred feet, black and dripping, and somewhere behind that rock face lay the flooded passage, the second chamber, the gold, and the words my grandfather had written with whatever light and time remained to him.

I thought about the letter I carried in my jacket—the last one he'd ever mailed, postmarked three days before he vanished, that I still hadn't finished reading. Twenty-two years I'd owned it. I'd read the first paragraph perhaps a hundred times. *My dear Oscie, the sea here is generous and the partners are not—* and then I'd always stopped, some internal tide pulling me back, because finishing it meant knowing what he'd suspected, and suspecting alongside him meant grieving him twice.

Not today. Today the grief could wait in line behind the living.

"Ines." I pointed astern, where the headland hid Corvo's approach. "How long do we have?"

She looked at the sky, at the water, at the barometer in the cabin whose needle was now performing a slow, deliberate suicide. "Until the tide turns, we own the cave. After that"—she shrugged, and the gesture contained multitudes, none of them comforting—"we own whatever we can hold."

We dropped anchor in the shadow of the cliffs, and I got the dive gear ready, and the wind began to sing against the rock above us in a note I remembered from the worst night of my life, the night the tide went out and showed me a door I should have walked past.

One headland behind us, I knew, Corvo was doing the same math. Choosing. The barometer fell through twenty-eight inches, and the first outer bands of the monsoon came ashore like scouts reporting back to an army, and the window—the beautiful, narrow, fatal window—began to close around us all.

"Slack water in forty minutes," Ines said, checking her watch against the almanac. "Dive's ninety minutes round trip if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

She pulled her fins on and looked at me with the closest thing to warmth her face permitted, which was roughly the temperature of a fish knife. "Then we find out underwater. Try to be right, Coldharbour."

And together we went over the side, into the green dark, while behind the headland the lights of Corvo's yacht swung into view—too close, far too close, and getting closer.
