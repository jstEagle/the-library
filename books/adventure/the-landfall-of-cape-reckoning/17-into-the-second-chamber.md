## 17. Into the Second Chamber

The chart under the floorboards had been drawn by a man who knew he was going to die.

That was the thing I couldn't stop thinking about as Ines and I kitted up on the pitching deck of the *Marlowe's Luck*, forty years of salt and silence between my grandfather's last handwriting and my own shaking hands. He hadn't scrawled it in panic. The lines were ruled, the depths annotated, the passage into the second chamber marked with a neat little arrow and a note in his careful draftsman's script: *Air lasts one diver, perhaps two, if they do not waste it screaming.*

"He had a sense of humor," Ines said, reading over my shoulder.

"He had a death sentence and a fountain pen."

"Same thing, some days."

The weather window was exactly what the radio promised: four hours of grudging calm between monsoon cells, a held breath in the atmosphere, the barometer falling like a stone with somewhere to be. Behind us, one headland back, Corvo's boat was a smudge of white hull and bad intentions. We'd lost visual twenty minutes ago, which meant nothing. Men like Corvo didn't lose you. They let you think you'd lost them, because hope made people predictable.

"Last chance to be sensible," Ines said, checking her regulator for the third time. She wasn't looking at me when she said it. She was looking at the cave mouth below us—a black seam in the cliff where the sea poured in and out with each swell, breathing.

"Sensible got my grandfather drowned," I said. "Sensible is what his partners counted on. Nobody sensible goes after them."

"That's not the argument for sensible I would have made."

"It's the one that works on me."

She grunted, which from Ines Marlowe was a standing ovation, and went over the side.

* * *

The flooded passage was everything the chart promised and nothing I wanted.

Forty feet down, the tunnel narrowed to a throat of rock, our dive lights carving cones out of water so thick with suspended silt it felt like swimming through smoke. My grandfather's arrow pointed slightly left of where instinct said to go—of course it did—and there, just where he'd marked it, the ceiling opened upward into blackness. An air bell. A pocket of trapped breath that had been waiting, patient as a debt, since before I was born.

We surfaced into it, and I pulled my regulator out and breathed air that tasted of stone and old rope and time.

"Ines." My voice came out strange in the enclosed dark, flattened, close. "Look up."

Her light found the ceiling, and the ceiling was not rock.

It was planking. Ship's timbers, close-fitted, still bearing the ghost of tar, curving overhead like the inside of a rib cage. We were floating inside the Reckoning herself—or what the sea had made of her—the hull inverted and wedged into the chamber, preserved by cold and dark and the particular mercy of places nobody looks.

"The whole stern section," Ines breathed. "It must have washed in during the storm that took her. Forty years sealed in here." Her light swept down, and we both went quiet, because below us, resting on a shelf of rock that the low tides had exposed, lay the cargo.

I want to tell you I thought of the money first. I'm told that's what honest people admit to. But what I saw was chests—iron-bound, latched, furred with mineral bloom—and among them, unmistakable even crusted in forty years of calcification, oilcloth bundles tied with waxed cord, and a strongbox with a name stamped into its lid that I knew the way you know your own face in a mirror.

*COLDHARBOUR & VANE SHIPPING.*

My grandfather's company. His name first. Theirs second, like an afterthought, like a knife slipped in between the letters.

"There it is," Ines said softly. "Enough gold to buy your life twice over. Enough documents to hang them with it."

"Let's not get sentimental about hanging," I said, but my hands weren't steady, and she saw, and she didn't comment, which was its own kind of kindness.

We worked fast, because the chart had been clear about the arithmetic: this air was borrowed, not owned. We hauled two of the oilcloth bundles and the strongbox to the air bell's rocky lip, stashing them above the waterline where we could reach them, and then I saw it.

A tin. A simple sailor's biscuit tin, wedged into a crack in the rock at chest height, deliberately placed, deliberately visible, a triangle scratched into the mineral crust beside it—the same triangle that marked every waypoint on his hidden chart.

My grandfather had left mail.

"Ines," I said, and couldn't say anything else.

She looked at the tin, then at me, and stepped back to give me the space, holding her light steady so I could work with both hands.

The lid came away with a sound like a sigh. Inside, wrapped in oilskin gone stiff as bark: a ledger. Not the ship's ledger we'd found in the hold—that had been business, cargo tallied in a fair hand. This was different. This was smaller, personal, the pages soft with handling, written in the same careful draftsman's script as the chart, and dated across the last three weeks of my grandfather's life.

He'd known. That was the first thing, the thing that hit me like cold water. Elias Coldharbour had stood in this chamber or one like it, or maybe aboard his own ship while his partners smiled at him across a chart table, and he had understood exactly what was being done to him, and instead of running he had stayed to write it all down.

*March 3rd,* the first entry began. *Discovered the discrepancy in the manifest today. Fourteen crates logged as ballast. I did not load fourteen crates of ballast.*

I read by Ines's light, my lips moving, my breath fogging in air that was getting older by the minute, and my grandfather told me everything.

The Reckoning had never been lost. She'd been scuttled—her cargo offloaded over three nights into this cave by men who then put her back out into the storm with her seacocks opened and her captain aboard. The fortune everyone mourned hadn't drowned. It had been carried here, piece by piece, by lantern light, by men who laughed about it afterward over brandy in the harbor tavern. And the names—God, the names, written in a dying man's hand with the precision of a surveyor, dates and amounts and initials—

*Aldous Vane directed the third night's work personally. He wore my coat while doing it, saying it would fit his sons better than mine ever fit me again. I let him keep the joke. It costs nothing to let a thief laugh.*

"Aldous Vane," I said aloud. "The patriarch. The founder. The beloved grandfather of the men who own my life."

Ines was quiet a moment. "So the debt they're collecting," she said slowly, "was built on this."

"On him. On this cave. On fourteen crates of ballast and a man who died writing their names." I turned pages with fingers that wanted to tear them and forced them to be gentle, because these pages were the last living piece of him and I would not be the second person to destroy Elias Coldharbour's work. "They've spent forty years collecting interest on his murder, Ines. And they sent me a cheerful letter every month reminding me I owed them."

The last entry was short. The hand had begun to fail by then; the letters leaned like tired men.

*If anyone finds this, know that I forgave none of them, and I do not ask you to. But take the documents and leave the gold. Gold is how they bought the world. Documents are how we take it back.*

*Tell my granddaughter the sea keeps better books than any bank.*

*E.C.*

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the air tasted used. Long enough that Ines finally put a hand on my shoulder—not gentle, Ines didn't do gentle, but firm, real, an anchor line thrown to a drifting hull.

"We need the documents and the strongbox lids," she said. "Not the gold. You heard him."

"I heard him."

"And you're going to listen to a dead man?"

"He's the only man who's never lied to me." I wiped my face with the back of my glove, which accomplished nothing underwater-adjacent except making me feel briefly human. "We take the ledger, the manifests, the correspondence bundle. We mark the chamber. And we leave the rest for whoever comes with warrants instead of guns."

"That's going to be a hard sell to your survival instincts."

"My survival instincts are why I owe the Vanes money. They don't get a vote anymore."

We loaded ourselves until we were heavy as ballast ourselves—documents in waterproof sleeves against our chests, the strongbox lid pried loose for its stamped proof, Ines insisting on one small chest of coins because, as she put it with terrible logic, "warrants cost filing fees." Then we dropped back beneath the surface, through the throat of rock, into the open gray-green of the sea beyond.

And that was when I saw the lights.

* * *

They came around the headland the way weather does—no hurry, no doubt, total certainty of arrival.

Two boats. Corvo's white hull in the lead, and behind it something longer, darker, riding low with the weight of men and equipment. The monsoon's leading edge was building behind them, a wall of bruised cloud stacked to the stratosphere, and the light had gone that particular green-gray that makes the ocean look like it's holding its breath.

We were still fifty yards from the *Luck*, gear heavy, lungs burning from the long swim, when Corvo's boat slowed and turned toward us with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has already won.

"Get us behind the point," Ines said through clenched teeth, hauling herself over the gunwale. "There's a channel through the reef. Shallow. His draft's too deep to follow."

"You know this coast that well?"

"I know every wreck on this coast personally. Some of them I introduced myself." She was already at the wheel, and the *Marlowe's Luck* came alive under her hands in a way that made me understand, suddenly and completely, why her father had died diving and why she'd never stopped diving either. This boat wasn't her possession. It was her continuation.

Corvo's voice reached us across the water, amplified, cheerful, horrible.

"Miss Coldharbour! Wonderful! I see you've been shopping!"

I stood at the stern, the ledger dry against my chest inside its sleeve, and looked at the man who had come to collect forty years of interest on my grandfather's murder, and I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the dive, harder than the cave.

I waved back.

"Come to admire the view!" I called. "It's about to get dramatic!"

Behind him, the storm wall swallowed the horizon whole.

Ines opened the throttle, and the *Luck* ran for the reef like a fox for cover, and I pressed my hand flat against the oilskin sleeve and felt the shape of the pages inside and thought: *You told me to leave the gold, Grandfather. You didn't say anything about outrunning them.*

The first rain hit like gravel, and Corvo's engines rose behind us, patient as tide.

*End of Chapter 17.*
