## 18. The Cave Becomes a Trap

The first thing I noticed was the light going out of the water.

I'd been in the second chamber maybe twenty minutes, my dive light playing over the gold—actual gold, stacked in corroded strongboxes like some pirate's fever dream—when the blue glow filtering through the flooded passage dimmed to a bruise. Then to nothing.

"Oscar." My voice came out flat and wrong in the enclosed air. "The entrance."

She surfaced beside me a second later, pulling off her regulator, and I watched her do the same math I'd just done. The passage behind us was sealed. Not by rock. By something that moved with the deliberate patience of a man who'd had all day to think about it.

"The anchor line," she said. "They've run it across the mouth and weighted it. We're corked."

Above us, through the shaft of open water where the cave's chimney rose toward its blowhole, came a sound I knew from salvage yards and debt collectors' offices alike: an engine, idling, unhurried. Corvo's boat, sitting on our grave like a gull on a clam.

* * *

We climbed out onto the shelf of rock where we'd staged our gear, and that's when the smoke started.

It came down the chimney in a slow grey tongue, curling along the ceiling the way smoke does when it's in no hurry because it knows exactly where you are. Corvo's voice followed it, amplified and cheerful, bouncing around the limestone until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

"Ladies! Wonderful work down there, truly. The Vanes have been looking for this chamber since before either of you was born." A pause, warm as a handshake. "Now. Here's what happens next. You send up the ledger and the cargo manifests—all of them—and then you swim out, and everyone has a lovely story about a storm and a miracle. Or—" the engine revved once, companionably, "—we wait. The tide comes back in four hours. The smoke does most of the work before then anyway. Your call. No rush."

There was always a rush. There was always a clock. That was how men like Corvo worked—they made you feel the minutes bleeding out so you'd grab the first deal offered, even a bad one, especially a bad one.

Oscar was already moving, dragging our gear bags behind a spur of rock, away from the falling smoke. Her face in the lamplight had gone very still, the way it did when the fear got big enough to stop being visible.

"He'll take the ledger and kill us anyway," she said quietly. "You know that."

"I know that," I said. "He knows we know that. It's part of his charm."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being trapped: it isn't the trap that breaks you. It's the arithmetic.

I sat with my back against cold stone and ran numbers, because numbers were the only thing I'd ever trusted. Air: two tanks half-spent, plus the surface supply we'd rigged, plus whatever the pocket above the second chamber held—which, judging by the way the smoke was already layering into it, wasn't much and shrinking. Water: the tide tables lived in my head, and they said slack at 21:40, low water after midnight, which meant roughly five hours until the sea pulled back far enough that the entrance might—if the line came off, if the collapse hadn't shifted anything, if the moon cooperated—stand open again.

Five hours. We had maybe ninety minutes of breathable air if we conserved, less with smoke filling the upper reaches.

Corvo didn't need to storm us. He needed only to be patient, and patience was free.

"He's not going to leave," Oscar said. She'd found her grandfather's ledger—the real one, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp—and she held it against her chest like something that could bleed. "Not with the gold down here. He can't report failure. The Vanes don't forgive failure."

"No," I agreed. "They collect it."

That landed between us and neither of us picked it up. Some truths you leave lying where they fall.

* * *

It was Oscar who broke first—not into panic, but into something worse. Planning.

"The charges," she said. "How many left?"

"Two. Maybe two and a half if the damp hasn't gotten into the third." I'd been avoiding that number since we dove in. "Enough to bring the chimney down on our heads, or enough to crack the seaward wall if I place them perfectly, which requires me to swim through a passage currently blocked by a man with a rifle and excellent insurance."

"So we don't go through the passage."

I looked at her.

"There's a second way out," she said. "My grandfather's chart. The one under the floorboards—it showed the second chamber connected to something on the landward side. A fissure. He drew it as an escape route, back when he still trusted his partners enough to plan for the day he couldn't." Her thumb moved across the ledger's cover. "He drew himself a door out of his own tomb, Ines. He just never got to use it."

"Because his partners sealed him in," I said. "Same play, forty years apart. These people only ever have one idea."

But I was already unrolling the chart copy, my hands steadier than they had any right to be, tracing the ink lines by lamplight while the smoke thickened overhead like weather. There it was: a dotted line running northeast from the chamber, up through broken ground, annotated in a cramped antique hand—*air for three, stone for forty fathoms, God for the rest.*

"He was funny," Oscar said, and her voice cracked on it.

"He was optimistic," I said. "Worse."

* * *

The fissure, when we found it, was everything the chart promised and nothing anyone would call a door. A crack in the limestone wide enough for a determined person and too narrow for a person carrying anything, rising at an angle no honest engineer would sign off on, breathing cold air from somewhere above like the mountain itself was sighing.

We could fit through. Our gear mostly couldn't. And the gold—that beautiful, cursed, life-erasing gold—couldn't at all.

That was the plan neither of us liked, sitting there in the lamplight while Corvo's smoke curled down and his voice called out new, friendlier deadlines. To go out through the fissure meant leaving everything. The ledger's originals. The strongboxes. Forty years of proof and a fortune that could buy back every debt on this coast. We'd carry out copies and memories, and Corvo would own the rest, and the Vanes' lawyers would spend a decade explaining why a dead salvager's chart didn't constitute evidence.

Unless.

I looked at the remaining charges, and then at the flooded passage, and then at the ceiling of the second chamber, where the old tectonic scar ran fat and fractured above the strongboxes—a fault line older than the wreck, waiting for permission.

"You're making a face," Oscar said. "I know that face. I've seen it on demolition crews."

"If we can't take the gold out," I said slowly, "and he can't be allowed to take it out either..."

"Then nobody takes it out." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage, and then stood there looking slightly appalled at herself. "Ines. That's my grandfather's fortune. That's the proof. That's everything."

"It's bait," I said. "It's been bait since 1987. Every person who's died on this reef died reaching for those boxes. Your grandfather. My father. Half the divers on this coast. That gold isn't a treasure, Oscar. It's a headstone that pays out." I tapped the fault line with my knuckle. "We sink it. Properly. Bring the chamber roof down across the passage and drop half the cliff on top, and let the sea keep its dead. We walk out the fissure with the ledger pages sewn into our jackets, and Corvo gets to explain to the Vanes why their inheritance is now geology."

Silence. Smoke drifted past the lamp. Somewhere above, the tide had begun its long turn, and I could hear it in the walls—a deep, patient knocking, like something asking to be let in.

"And if the charge cracks the fissure too?" Oscar asked. "If your perfect placement is off by ten feet and we seal ourselves in with it?"

"Then we die in a room full of gold," I said, "and honestly, of all the ways the sea has planned to kill me, that's the most flattering."

She laughed. It was a terrible laugh, wet and sharp, the kind you hear from people standing on ledges—but it was a laugh, and it broke something loose in both of us, because terror is just resolve that hasn't picked a direction yet, and in that moment ours picked.

"Place them perfectly," she said.

* * *

We worked the way I imagine my father worked, in his last hour, in dark water, doing the careful thing one motion at a time. I swam the passage as far as the anchor line's shadow—close enough to see Corvo's floodlights turning the entrance into a stage set, close enough to hear his men talking, bored already—and placed the first charge in the throat of the channel where the rock narrowed. Oscar wired it from the chamber side, her hands sure on the contacts, her face a mask of concentration that I recognized as my own.

The second charge went high, into the fault scar above the strongboxes. The third—the damp one, the maybe—we wedged into the fissure's mouth itself, not to close it but to shake loose the rubble that would hide our exit and bury any pursuit behind us.

Then we climbed back to the shelf, and Oscar took the detonator, and I put my hand over hers.

"On three," I said. "And Oscar—whatever happens, whatever we lose down here—your grandfather gets out. Understand? Him, and my father, and every diver they drowned. They all walk out of this cave tonight. We're just the ones carrying them."

Her eyes were shining and furious and absolutely certain.

"One," she said.

Above us, Corvo's voice, sing-song and reasonable, offering terms one final time.

"Two."

The tide knocked at the walls, patient as a creditor.

"Three."
