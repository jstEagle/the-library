## 19. What Survives the Flood

**OSCAR**

The smoke came in slow, like a guest who knows he's unwelcome but owns the house.

We'd sealed the inner passage with tarps and salvage foam—Ines's idea, a stopgap bought with minutes, not hours—and now we crouched in the second chamber with our headlamps off, listening to Corvo's pump throb somewhere beyond the collapsed mouth of the cave. The air tasted of diesel and old salt and, beneath it, the faint sweet rot of forty years of drowned timber.

The gold was still there. That was the problem.

It lay heaped along the chamber floor exactly as my grandfather had left it: coin chests burst open, bullion bars furred green and black, document cases stacked with a care that broke my heart—he'd known someone might come someday, and he'd wanted them to *understand*. The final messages were tucked in oilskin above it all, every thief named in his own careful hand, including a Vane patriarch who had signed his name twice: once to the theft, once to the lie that my grandfather drowned drunk.

"He's going to wait us out," Ines whispered beside me. "Smoke us down, then come in breathing his own air when the tide drops. He thinks we're rats."

"Are we?"

"No." She tapped her dive tank. "Rats drown. We have options."

I looked at the gold again. Two days ago I'd have said those options included carrying some of it out. An hour ago, with Corvo's voice drifting through rock—*Miss Coldharbour, let's be reasonable, you can't spend dead men's money*—I'd have said the options included giving him half to make him go away.

But Corvo doesn't go away. Corvo is what the Vanes send when going away isn't the plan.

"How much charge do you have left?" I asked.

Ines went very still. "You know what you're asking."

"I'm asking how much."

"Two cutting charges. Enough to drop the shelf rock over the passage mouth. Enough to bring half that ceiling down on the chamber if I set them wrong by three meters." She turned her lamp on me, low, so it just caught her face. "And enough water coming through the breach to fill this room in maybe four minutes. We'd need to be through the flooded channel and into the first cave before the surge hits. On one shared regulator each if the tanks flood. In zero visibility."

"And Corvo?"

"Corvo's men are sitting right outside the passage mouth waiting for us to surrender or suffocate. When the roof comes down—" She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "Geology doesn't take sides."

*Yes it does,* I thought. *Tonight it takes ours.*

I thought about my grandfather's handwriting. About the Vanes' cheerful voices on my phone for eight months, always warm, always reasonable, always with the unspoken arithmetic underneath: your life against your signature. I thought about Corvo's smile at the anchorage, and how charm is just violence wearing cologne.

If we swam out and left the gold, Corvo would dig for it. He had boats, men, time, and the patience of a man who'd never once been told no by anyone who mattered. The ledger pages hidden in Ines's ketch would name him eventually—but trials are slow, and men with money are fast.

The only vault nobody can crack is the sea itself.

"Do it," I said.

Ines studied me for a long moment. Then she smiled—not her usual expression, which was somewhere between suspicion and indigestion, but something older and sadder. "My father spent eleven years looking for this room," she said. "He died eighty meters from it. And you're asking me to sink it."

"I'm asking you to make sure it kills the right people."

She laughed once, sharp and wet, and started unpacking the charges.

* * *

**INES**

There is an art to demolition underwater, and most of it is grief management.

You spend years learning where things break, and then the day comes when breaking them is the job, and your hands have to pretend your heart isn't in the room. My father taught me charges on wrecks he loved. He used to say the sea keeps better books than any bank—you just have to be willing to read them in ink that runs.

I placed the first charge high on the shelf rock, where the strata dipped toward the passage mouth. The second went low, against the fault line I'd mapped by feel two days ago, back when the plan was to blast *in*, not bury everything behind me forever. Four minutes of fuse cord. Delay caps so they fired in sequence—high first, dropping the bulk, low second, finishing the seal.

Oscar worked beside me without speaking, hauling the document cases up into the highest crevice she could find, wedging them behind a shelf of coral limestone above any plausible flood line. The testimony would survive even if the treasure didn't. That was the whole architecture of the thing: let the Vanes' gold buy the sea, and let the sea keep the receipts.

"The coins," she said suddenly, standing over an open chest. Even in the bad light I could see her jaw working. "Some of these were minted the year he was born."

"I know."

"He carried them aboard himself. It says so. Page nine."

"Oscar." I put my hand over the chest lid, not closing it, just resting it there. "Page nine doesn't float."

For a second I thought she'd argue. She was built for arguing; you could see the machinery of it in her face, all that clever fury looking for a target. But then she closed her eyes, and when she opened them the fury had gone somewhere deeper and colder, which is where fury belongs when there's work to do.

She closed the lid herself. She pushed the chest gently, almost kindly, back into the dark.

"Fuse cord," she said. Her voice was steady. Good. Steady voices keep you alive; shaking hands just announce you've noticed you're mortal.

I ran the cord back along the wall to the flooded channel mouth, checked the splices twice, checked the caps a third time. Through the rock I could feel the pump's vibration change rhythm—Corvo's men shifting position, restless, maybe hearing something, maybe just bored men being paid too well to be bored.

"Regulators," I said. "On my mark, we flood the channel and swim. You stay on my fin. Do not pass me, do not stop for anything, and if I signal fist"—I showed her—"that means the surge has caught us and we wedge into the first side pocket and hold. Clear?"

"What happens to the air pocket in here?"

"Gone. This whole chamber becomes ocean in about ninety seconds after firing." I met her eyes. "No version of this where we watch it happen. We'll hear it. That's enough."

"It isn't."

"No," I agreed. "But it's what's on offer."

I struck the fuse igniter. The cord caught with a soft hiss, a spark crawling away into the dark like something small escaping a larger fire, and we rolled into the black water side by side, tanks bumping, lamps off, swimming blind through a channel we knew by count and touch alone.

Twelve kicks to the bend. Eight more to the first cave.

At kick eleven the world detonated.

* * *

**OSCAR**

Sound underwater isn't sound. It's your skeleton being played by an instrument the size of a mountain.

The first charge hit and the pressure wave shoved us forward like a hand between the shoulder blades—I lost Ines's fin, found it, lost the wall, found it. Silt exploded past us in boiling clouds. Then the second charge, closer somehow, deeper, a *whump* I felt in my teeth, and the sea itself began to move, a current dragging backward toward the chamber as the void we'd made drank the ocean in.

Ines's fist punched out of the dark. The signal. The surge had us.

We wedged into the side pocket—I cracked my shoulder on rock, wrapped both arms around a formation that felt like a drowned tree root, and held on while the world tried to pour itself through a hole the size of a church door. The roar was total. My mask pressed against my face. Somewhere above us, tons of shelf rock settled into the passage mouth with a grinding groan I felt more than heard, the door closing on the second chamber for good.

And through it all, faint under the water-noise, the sound I'll carry longer than any other from that night: the smaller crashes, the splintering, the slow deep percussion of coin chests splitting open and their contents settling into the mud. A fortune becoming sediment. My grandfather's stolen cargo going home to the only heir who couldn't be bribed.

Then the current slackened. The roar became a rumble, the rumble a murmur, the murmur the ordinary breathing of the sea through stone.

Ines's lamp clicked on. The beam showed nothing but brown murk, and her eyes above the mask, watching me. Asking a question without words.

I gave her the diver's OK sign. My hand shook doing it. She saw, and didn't pretend she hadn't, and nodded anyway, which is the truest kindness I've ever been shown.

We swam for the first cave, where the old air pocket waited, foul and precious.

* * *

**INES**

We surfaced into air that tasted like a penny left in a drain, and Oscar started laughing before her head was fully above water—a ragged, disbelieving sound that echoed around the first cave and came back at us changed.

"We're alive," she said, stating it like a scientific finding she intended to publish.

"Barely counts as a finding. Sample size of two." But I was grinning too, and my hands were shaking, and I let them.

Then the shouting started.

Voices. From outside—from the cave mouth, from the water beyond it, thin and panicked across the storm-lifted surface. Corvo's men. Two of them had been positioned close when the charges fired, close enough that the collapse had taken the rocks down around them, and now they were screaming for help in the kind of voices men only use when they understand, completely, that they are going to die.

Oscar looked at me. I looked at Oscar.

"Don't say it," she warned.

"You were about to say it."

"I was *not* about to—"

"You were composing it. Your face composes."

She swore and started kicking for the entrance, and I followed, because the awful truth is I'd already decided the same thing and hated myself for the speed of it. We broke out of the cave mouth into driving rain and gray chaos—the monsoon had arrived properly while we were underground, wind flattening the sea into moving hills—and found the first man clinging to a boulder awash at the cliff base, blood sheeting down his face, one arm wrong in a way arms shouldn't be.

His partner was gone. We found that out later, from the report, from the inquiry, from all the machinery that turns tragedy into paperwork. The sea took him between one wave and the next.

Getting the survivor onto Ines's ketch nearly killed all three of us. The swell was picking the boat up and setting her down like a dog shaking a rat, and the man screamed every time we moved his arm, and Oscar—small, debt-ridden, storm-drunk Oscar—anchored herself against the rail and hauled with a strength I still don't fully believe, roaring at me over the wind with instructions that were mostly profanity but structurally sound.

We got him aboard. We got lines rigged. And then we got our answer to the question we hadn't asked yet, because Corvo's launch came around the headland through the rain, running lights swinging, and Corvo himself stood at the bow.

* * *

**OSCAR**

I want to tell you I felt triumphant when I saw Corvo's face. I want to report a clean feeling, justice-shaped, warm.

What I actually felt was cold, and tired, and watched him do the thing that told me everything.

The launch came alongside, and Corvo wasn't looking at us. He wasn't looking at his injured man bleeding on our deck, or at Ines with the winch handle still in her grip, or at me. He was looking past us, at the cliffs—at the place where the cave mouth had been, where now there was only a scar of fresh rock and falling white water, a wound already healing over with sea.

"Where is it?" he called. His voice carried strangely across the wind, calm under all that weather, which was somehow worse than screaming. "The cargo, Miss Coldharbour. Where did you put it?"

"In the bank," I shouted back. "The oldest one. Ask the tide."

I watched him understand it. It moved across his face like weather of its own—disbelief first, then calculation, then a flicker of genuine horror as the arithmetic finished: the gold, the leverage, the thing he'd crossed a monsoon to hold, gone. Not stolen. Not hidden. *Retired.* Beyond reach, beyond ransom, beyond even the Vanes' lawyers.

Corvo was many things—patient, ruthless, immaculately charming—but he was, at bottom, a man who believed everything could be bought, and we had just shown him a commodity that couldn't. I think it genuinely broke something in him. I think that matters, morally, though I've decided not to examine it too closely.

He ordered his launch around. I'd like to say he did it with dignity.

Here's what happened instead: he came about too fast in a following sea, and the man we'd rescued—his own crewman, hurt because Corvo had parked him too close to a cliff during a demolition—saw the launch turning away and understood, in one visible heartbeat, that he'd just been written off. Written off as evidence, as expense, as loose end. The man started shouting Corvo's name, and Corvo didn't even turn his head.

That image will do more damage to the Vane family than any ledger page. I made sure of it later. Cameras love a moment; courts love witnesses; and a wounded man abandoned by the charming enforcer is both.

The launch vanished into the rain toward the open sea, and Ines put the ketch's bow into the worst of it and started for harbor, and the monsoon came down on Cape Reckoning like a verdict.

* * *

**INES**

We ran north all night with the storm on our heels and a broken man in the cabin drinking rainwater through gritted teeth and telling us, unprompted, piece by piece, everything Corvo had ever said within his hearing. Men abandon loyalty quickly when loyalty abandons them first. By dawn we had names, dates, account locations, and a confession about the night they'd tracked Oscar's beacon—all of it volunteered, all of it repeated twice for emphasis, the way guilty men testify.

Oscar sat across from him writing it all down, and I steered, and neither of us spoke about the chamber behind us. There was nothing to say that the sea hadn't already filed.

Near morning the wind eased just enough, and Oscar came up beside me at the wheel, salt-crusted, hollow-eyed, holding a scrap of oilskin she'd fished from her pocket. Inside it, one gold coin. One. She must have palmed it in the last seconds before the fuse caught—I never saw it, and I'm the one who taught myself never to look away from another salvager's hands.

"Minted the year he was born," she said. "Page nine. He carried it aboard himself."

I looked at the coin, then at her, then at the gray horizon where Cape Reckoning had disappeared behind us.

"That's theft," I said.

"From whom?" She turned the coin over. "Everyone with a claim on it is either dead, drowning, or about to be arrested."

"Put it in the memorial case," I said finally. "Center of the display. With the ledger pages."

She smiled—tired, real—and closed her hand around it.

That's when the radio crackled. The harbor station, breaking into the emergency band, voice tight with excitement: a Vane family lawyer arrested at the airport trying to leave the country, the firm's accounts frozen, and—here the announcer slowed down—authorities requesting contact with one Oscar Coldharbour, whose grandfather's testimony was, quote, *"the key that unlocked the entire case."*

They were asking for her by name. The whole coast was asking for her by name.

Oscar stared at the radio like it might bite. Then she looked at me, and at the leaking, battered, beloved wreck of a ketch that had carried us through all of it, and she said:

"So. How do you feel about being famous by association?"

I put the wheel over toward harbor and let the wind answer for me.
