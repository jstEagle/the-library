## 20. Landfall

The sea spat us out like a bad oyster.

I remember it in fragments: the ketch's keel kissing sand where no sand should be, Ines shouting something that was probably profanity and possibly a prayer, and me clutching the waterproof satchel against my chest like a newborn I'd gone to terrible lengths to acquire. The second chamber had given up its gold to the flood and nearly given us up too. What we had left fit in a bag the size of a loaf of bread — copied pages, photographed plates, and one original ledger page I'd torn free in the dark because some stubborn animal part of me needed to hold paper my grandfather's hand had actually touched.

Everything else was at the bottom of Cape Reckoning, along with Corvo, his men, and roughly four million dollars of Vanes family inheritance.

Fair trade. Ask anyone who knew me.

* * *

We came ashore eleven miles south of Port Calloway on a beach that consisted mostly of broken shell and old regret. The ketch — Ines's ketch, which she'd named *Patience* with what I now understood was bitter irony — was holed below the waterline, her rudder bent, her dignity in tatters. We ran her aground deliberately, which is a polite way of saying we drove her into the surf at speed and let physics handle the paperwork.

Ines stood in the shallows surveying the wreck of her home with the expression of a woman doing long division.

"That's the third boat," she said.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. Apologizing implies you'll stop." She picked a strand of weed off her shoulder and flicked it into the foam. "Port Calloway's a day's walk. Less if you don't complain, which we both know you will."

"Complaining is load-bearing for me," I said. "Remove it and the whole structure collapses."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth — the closest thing to a smile the storm had left either of us energy for. Then she shouldered her pack, checked the flare gun out of habit rather than hope, and started up the beach.

I followed, one hand flat against the satchel, feeling the pages shift inside like a sleeping thing.

* * *

Port Calloway announced itself an hour before we reached it: rusted cranes over tin roofs, the smell of diesel and drying fish, a radio mast leaning against the sky like a drunk against a lamppost. It was the kind of harbor town that existed because the mapmakers needed somewhere to put a dot. Forty years ago my grandfather had sailed out of a place very much like it and never come back.

The harbormaster's office was a shed with a window. The harbormaster himself was a broad woman named Okonkwo who looked at two salt-crusted women dragging themselves off the beach and asked exactly zero questions, which told me Port Calloway had seen everything worth seeing and most things twice.

"Radio's dead," she said, when Ines explained what we needed. "Storm took the antenna Tuesday. Satellite phone rental's fifty a call, cash."

"We have no cash."

Okonkwo looked at us. She looked at the satchel. She looked back at us.

"You've got a story, though," she said slowly. "I can see it from here. You've got that look people get when they're carrying something heavier than it looks." She jerked her chin toward the end of the street, where a building with peeling blue paint bore a faded sign: *CALLOWAY COASTAL BROADCAST — 88.1*. "Meriem still runs the station. Storm knocked her off air too, but the transmitter's fine. She owes me a favor, and I owe her a favor, and between the three of us somebody's getting paid in gossip."

"What kind of gossip?"

"The kind that gets people arrested, if the way you keep touching that bag means anything."

* * *

Meriem Vasquez was seventy if she was a day, chain-smoked even indoors, and received us in a studio that smelled like burnt coffee and forty years of news. She listened to our story — or the parts of it we could tell in ten minutes — with the unblinking patience of someone who had interviewed politicians and pirates and saw little difference.

She stubbed out a cigarette when I finished. That was how I knew it was serious. She lit another one immediately after, but the gesture had been made.

"You understand," she said, "that the moment this goes out, it's out. No take-backs. The Vanes have lawyers in every port from here to the capital. They'll have a statement drafted before your voice stops echoing."

"I'm counting on it," I said.

"Are you?" Her eyes were sharp as fishhooks. "Because a girl walks in here soaked to the bone, carrying papers she says prove the oldest crime on this coast, asking to broadcast to a transmitter that reaches maybe sixty miles of fishing boats. That's not exposure. That's a flare. You want them to hear it. Why?"

Because a flare draws eyes. Because once enough people are looking, you can't quietly drown us in a cave and call it weather. Because Corvo had died reaching for gold he thought would buy silence, and his employers needed to know the silence was already spent.

But what I said was simpler.

"Because my grandfather waited forty years for someone to listen," I said. "He drowned holding proof that his partners stole everything and let him die for it, and the whole coast believed he'd lost the cargo through incompetence, and his name rotted while theirs got marble plaques. He doesn't get to wait another day. Not for lawyers. Not for timing. Not for safety." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. Meriem didn't look away, and neither did Ines, standing by the door with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a gate.

Meriem nodded once.

"Studio's live in ten minutes," she said. "Try not to cry until the second segment. Crying reads well on radio, but not first thing."

* * *

Ten minutes later I sat behind a microphone older than I was, and Ines sat beside me because when I glanced at her she said, "You're not doing this alone," in a tone that closed discussion.

Meriem counted us down with fingers, and then a small red light came on, and I was talking to the sea.

"This is Oscar Coldharbour," I began. "Forty years ago, my grandfather, Tomas Coldharbour, master of the salvage brig *Reckoning*, was reported lost with all hands and all cargo off Cape Reckoning. His partners reported him negligent. His insurers paid them. His family inherited his debts instead of his name, and I am the last of that family, and for most of my life I believed the story everyone was sold."

The words came easier than I expected. Maybe because they weren't mine. They were his, transcribed in a flooded cave by lamplight, in handwriting that grew shakier toward the end — written, the dates suggested, during his final hours, while the water rose and his partners' boats sailed away heavy with his cargo.

"I'm holding his ledger," I said. "His own testimony, written as he drowned. It names every man who took what was his. It names the arrangement they made. And it names the price they arranged for him." I paused. Meriem gestured: keep going, second segment, save the crying. "Three of those names belong to the Vane family. Aurelio Vane signed the manifest that moved the cargo south. His brother Silas took the insurance settlement. Their descendants built a lending empire on that money, and this year their collectors were closing on me the same way their ancestors closed on him. I know how that sounds. I know what it costs me to say. But I have photographs, copies, and witnesses — Ines Marlowe, salvager, whose father died diving these waters looking for the truth I'm holding — and as of tonight, so does every authority and every newsroom I could reach before this broadcast ends."

Meriem, off-mic, slid me a list. Frequencies. Names. She'd been patching relay calls while I talked — coastal stations, a national desk, a maritime tribunal liaison who owed her from some scandal I'd ask about someday when I had leisure, which might be never.

"To the Vanes," I said, and here I did let something into my voice that wasn't grief. "You sent a man named Corvo to offer me forgiveness for the debt in exchange for the evidence. He's dead. The gold is sunk beyond any salvage ever mounted, and I say that with joy. Your fortune has forty years of compound interest on a drowning, and the principal just came due. Come collect."

Meriem cut the mic. Silence rang in the little studio like a struck bell.

"Well," she said, lighting another cigarette with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "That'll do."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way landfall does — all that pressure finally meeting ground.

Within the hour, the harbor had woken. Fishermen who'd listened on deck radios came by the studio to look at us like relics. A young constable named Adeyemi arrived with a laptop and a solemn face and spent two hours photographing the ledger pages under Meriem's desk lamp while Ines dictated coordinates with the precision of a woman reading scripture. The maritime tribunal liaison called back twice. By midnight there were journalists on the ferry from the mainland — actual journalists, cameras and all — and Meriem charged them all a landing fee she invented on the spot and split with Okonkwo, and honestly, the economy of this coast deserved its own chapter.

I sat on the seawall around two in the morning with the torn original page in my lap, watching the water. Somewhere out there was the cape. Somewhere under it was the cave, sealed now, its chambers flooded, its gold sleeping in the dark where no Vanes hand would ever reach it. Somewhere down there, presumably, was what remained of Corvo, and I found I couldn't work up a single gram of sorrow about it, and decided that was information about myself I'd examine later, or never.

Ines dropped onto the seawall beside me and handed me half of a sandwich she'd extracted from somewhere. Harbor food. It tasted like salt and victory, which are nearly the same flavor.

"You did it," she said.

"We did it."

"Don't get sentimental, I'm tired." But she bumped her shoulder against mine, gently, the way you'd nudge a boat alongside a dock. "My father dove for twenty years trying to find what we found in a week. I used to think that meant he failed. Now I think it means the truth keeps. It waits. It lets the liars build their houses on it, and then one day the tide goes out further than it's ever gone, and there it all is."

"Freak low tide," I said. "Once in a century."

"My father used to say the sea settles its accounts eventually. Everyone laughed at him." She looked out at the black water. "I'm going to put his name on the memorial. All their names. Every diver the Vanes' money killed or ruined. Right at the cape, facing the water."

"He'd like that."

"He'd hate the attention," she said, and smiled, and this time it stayed.

* * *

Dawn brought the hook.

Adeyemi found us at Meriem's — we'd claimed the studio couches as sovereign territory — with his phone in his hand and his solemn face gone carefully blank, which on a young constable is the face of a man holding alarming news at arm's length.

"There's a statement," he said. "From the Vane family counsel. Released an hour ago, to every outlet." He turned the phone toward us.

I read it twice. Ines read it once, then took the phone and read it again slower, the way she read charts.

"They're denying everything," I said. "Of course they are. Fabrication, grievance-driven fraud, the emotional instability of debtors—"

"No," Ines said quietly. "Read the last paragraph."

I read it. My stomach performed a slow, cold roll.

The last paragraph thanked me. Warmly. Publicly. For bringing closure to a painful historical episode. It announced that the Vane family welcomed a full independent investigation and would cooperate completely — and that, pending such investigation, the family's legal representatives wished Ms. Coldharbour no ill will whatsoever, and hoped she understood that certain outstanding financial obligations remained, regrettably, in force.

"They're not running," Ines said. "They're inviting us in. Tribunal hearings, depositions, months of process — all of it inside rooms they own, in front of judges they've lent money to." She set the phone down. "Corvo was the blunt instrument. This is the scalpel."

Outside the studio window, the first ferry of the morning slid into the harbor, and standing at its rail, unmistakable even at distance, was a tall figure in an immaculate coat, raising one hand toward the broadcast station in a small, courteous wave — a greeting from men who had just learned, publicly and irreversibly, that the tide had gone out on them too, and were already deciding what to do about the woman who'd pulled it.

"They know exactly where we are," I said.

"Yeah," said Ines, reaching for her boots. "Time we stopped being anywhere they expect."
