## 21. Settlements

The news reached us the way most important things do in Harrow Bay: badly, and by radio.

I was below decks in the *Pelican's Grace*, re-caulking a seam that had been leaking since before I owned her, when Oscar's voice came down the companionway, carefully flat in the way she got when she was trying not to hope too hard.

"Ines. You'll want to hear this."

The harbor frequency was full of static and gossip. The gossip was better than usual. The Vane brothers—both of them, Marcus and Aldous, the cheerful twins of Cape Reckoning's financial underworld—had been taken into custody that morning at their offices on the quay, in front of clerks and clients and at least one journalist who'd been tipped off an hour early. Fraud, conspiracy, obstruction, and—this was the part the anchors kept repeating like they couldn't believe their luck—conspiracy to commit murder, historical, arising from evidence presented to the maritime tribunal regarding the wreck of the barque *Reckoning*.

Evidence. That was us. That was Oscar standing on the customs house steps three weeks ago with photocopied pages flapping in the wind, reading her grandfather's handwriting out loud to a crowd of fishermen, reporters, and one very uncomfortable harbormaster, while Corvo's body was still being pulled out of the flood channel behind Cape Reckoning and nobody had yet worked up the courage to ask her how.

I put down my caulking iron. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, so I made fists until they stopped.

"Well," I said.

"Yeah," said Oscar.

"Both of them?"

"Both of them. And four of the old directors. The ones named in the ledger." She leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman waiting for the other shoe—and possibly the whole boot rack—to drop. "There are lawyers everywhere. Actual swarms of them. I counted six different firms' names in one article."

"Vultures," I said, "or cleaners."

"Is there a difference?"

"In fees, mostly."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten she could still do that. Three weeks of tribunals and testimony and journalists camped outside the marina had wrung most of the laughter out of both of us. It was good to hear it come back, even briefly, even at the expense of lawyers.

Outside, through the porthole, Harrow Bay went about its morning as if nothing had happened. Gulls arguing over fish heads. The fuel dock opening late, as always. A kid hosing down his father's trawler. The Vanes had cast a long shadow over this coast for two generations, but shadows are funny things—remove the object casting them and the light doesn't celebrate. It just arrives, ordinary and total, and everyone squints and carries on.

That, I decided, was exactly right.

* * *

The unraveling took longer than the arrest, and it was stranger to watch.

Empires don't collapse like masts in a storm—one dramatic crack and over the side. They come apart the way rigging does, strand by strand, quietly, until one day you notice the whole thing is just rope on the deck. The Vanes' shipping contracts were voided within the month. Their salvage brokerage—the same firm that had spent forty years "certifying" wrecks as unrecoverable, conveniently including the *Reckoning*—was frozen by the tribunal pending audit. Two banks distanced themselves with statements so carefully worded they qualified as origami.

And then the descendants started coming forward.

That was the part neither of us had predicted. We'd thought we were handing the authorities one crime from 1943. What we'd actually done was knock over the first domino in a very long row.

It began with a woman named Petra Okafor, seventy-eight years old, who arrived in Harrow Bay on the ferry with a tin box under her arm and asked at the chandlery where she could find "the Coldharbour girl." Her grandfather had been the *Reckoning*'s third mate, one of the six crew listed in the ledger as paid off and silenced—paid off, in the Vane family's accounting, meaning paid once, in lead, at the bottom of the sound. Petra had grown up on her grandmother's bitterness like it was porridge, every meal, every day, and she had the correspondence to prove it. Letters. Insurance denials. A widow's pension application refused on the grounds that the man had "abandoned ship," when the truth was he'd been abandoned into it.

Then came the Ferreiras, whose great-uncle had owned a share of the cargo and drowned asking questions. Then a family from up the coast named Lindqvist, three generations of whom had been told their ancestor died a debtor and a coward, and who arrived at the tribunal with their ancestor's name written on a banner because they'd never been able to afford a stone.

Oscar sat through all of it. Every session. She said afterward that it was the hardest thing she'd ever done, harder than the cave, harder than the storm, and I believed her, because I watched her do it. She sat in the public gallery in her one good jacket with her hands folded and listened to strangers describe griefs that rhymed with hers, and when the tribunal called her to testify she told the truth plainly, without ornament, which for a woman raised on her grandfather's sea stories and seasoned by her own is a kind of discipline bordering on sainthood.

I went to two sessions. I'm not built for courtrooms. Too many people saying important things in voices designed to make you stop listening. But I went twice, and both times what struck me wasn't the drama—it was the tedium. Justice, it turns out, is mostly paperwork performed by tired people in bad lighting. The fireworks, if any, happened somewhere else entirely, inside the people in the gallery, silently, where no one could see them.

Petra Okafor found me on the harbor wall after the second session. She looked at my hands—salvager's hands, scarred and salt-cured—and said, "You're the diver. Marlowe. Your father was Thomas Marlowe."

"He was."

"My grandmother knew him. Said he was the only one who ever came back from looking for the *Reckoning*, and he never stopped going back." She squeezed my arm with a grip that belied her age. "You finish something today, girl. Whatever else happens. Remember that."

Then she walked off toward the ferry with her tin box, upright as a mast, and I stood on the wall like an idiot until Oscar came to find me and pretended not to notice my face.

* * *

My own business with the tribunal came later, and it fit in an envelope.

Official recognition of dive site HR-7, formerly known among the diving fraternity as "the Vane Hole" for reasons that will now be obvious. Effective immediately, the site of the wreck of the barque *Reckoning*, and the surrounding seafloor designated as the final resting place of eleven souls, was to be entered into the coastal register under a new name: the Marlowe Grounds.

Thomas Marlowe. My father. Who spent nineteen years and every shilling he ever earned diving the cold water off Cape Reckoning looking for a shipwreck everyone official swore wasn't there, and who died doing it—not dramatically, not in some storm worthy of a ballad, but stupidly and slowly, of the bends, on a day with good visibility and a bad decision, because he'd found a timber stamped with a name and couldn't bear to leave it unmarked.

They'd laughed at him, you know. The whole coast had. Old Tom Marlowe and his ghost ship. The Vanes had been especially generous with their mockery, which I understood now, having read the ledger, was the mockery guilty men use—loud enough to drown out their own pulse. He'd gotten close, closer than anyone knew. Close enough that Aldous Vane himself had once bought my father a drink and told him, warmly, to let it go, some things belong to the sea.

Some things did. Just not the things the Vanes meant.

When the letter came, Oscar insisted on reading it aloud on the foredeck, formal as a herald, while I tried to look like a woman unmoved by ceremony and failed within the first sentence. Eleven souls. Final resting place. The Marlowe Grounds. My father's name in government type, on government paper, permanent, filed, real.

"They should've done it forty years ago," I said.

"Yes."

"He'd have hated the attention."

"Absolutely."

"He'd have liked the name, though." I turned the letter over in my hands. "Marlowe Grounds. Sounds like somewhere you'd grow things."

"You are," Oscar said. "Give it time."

Sentimental nonsense. I let her get away with it because the wind was in the right direction for it and because, if I'm honest, my eyes needed somewhere else to be for a minute.

* * *

Oscar's settlement was less tidy, because debts rarely die clean.

The formal debt—the one the Vane brothers had held over her, the loan against her boat and her future with interest rates that should have required a priest rather than a notary—was extinguished by the tribunal along with everything else the Vanes had touched. Contracts procured under a criminal enterprise were void. All of them. A clerk handed her a piece of paper saying so, and I watched her hold it like it might detonate.

But owing money was never really what the Vanes had held over her. They'd held fear, dressed as friendship, and fear doesn't show up on any ledger. For weeks after the arrest, Oscar kept catching herself flinching at cheerful voices. A smiling man on the dock asking the time nearly put her through a stack of crab pots. We didn't talk about it much. There's a kind of damage you can't salvage; you just keep bailing until the weather changes.

What she had left was the truth and the name, which was what she'd chosen, back in the cave, when the choice cost a fortune in gold sitting at the bottom of a flooded chamber where no honest dredge would ever reach it and no dishonest one deserved to.

"People keep asking me if I regret it," she said one evening, watching the sun go down over the Marlowe Grounds, because of course we'd anchored there; of course we had. "The gold. It was right there. Enough to buy a fleet."

"Do you?"

"I keep checking," she said. "Like testing a bruise. And it keeps coming back the same. No." She shrugged. "Corvo drowned reaching for it. The Vanes are in a cell because of what it proved. If I'd taken it, I'd just be them with better manners."

"Worse manners, probably. You snore."

"That's slander."

"It's observation. I sleep ten feet away."

We let the argument run its course, which is to say we let it dissolve, which is what our arguments did more often now. Something had gotten rebuilt between us out on that reef passage, and again in the hold during the worst of the storm, and it held the way good work holds—not pretty, but sound.

* * *

The last loose end tied itself almost apologetically.

Ines's photographs of the ledger—the ones I'd taken planning to sell to Halloran the salvager, the betrayal Oscar caught me committing in the hold of my own ketch—came home to roost in the strangest way. Halloran, it emerged during the tribunal's wider inquiry, had been one of the Vanes' quiet contractors for years, the man who made inconvenient wrecks officially disappear. My photographs hadn't gone to him—I'd never gotten the chance, thank God, or whatever passes for God on this coast—but my *attempt* had surfaced, and instead of burying me it buried him. His records, subpoenaed, corroborated half the ledger's claims from the other direction.

Justice has a sense of humor. It's a cruel one, and it works slow, but it's there.

Oscar brought it up once, gently, the way you check whether a repaired seam is holding. "You never told me why. Really why. Not the money-for-the-memorial version. The underneath version."

So I told her. On my own deck, with my own hands busy on rope that didn't need working, because that's how I say things. That my father had died a joke, and I'd grown up determined to prove him right instead of proven wrong, and when the cave finally opened after all those years, I couldn't stand that it opened for someone else—that his life's obsession had delivered its answer to a stranger with a debt and a death wish. The photographs weren't greed. They were grief wearing work gloves. I wanted the claim to be mine because the loss had been mine, and I'd have burned a friendship to feel like the sea owed me something back.

Oscar listened all the way through, which she's good at, being a novelist—listening is half the trade. Then she said, "For what it's worth, it wasn't your father's obsession that opened the cave. It was a freak tide and my terrible judgment."

"Terrible judgment runs in my family too."

"I know. It's why we're friends."

Friends. She said it easily, like it had always been true. I let it stand.

* * *

On the morning the verdicts came down—guilty, guilty, guilty, read out in a monotone that made forty years of villainy sound like a grocery list—we didn't go to the courthouse.

We went out to the Grounds.

I dove first, alone, on a line I'd rigged myself, down through water green as bottle glass to the seabed where the *Reckoning*'s ribs rose out of the sand like something growing rather than something dead. The tribunal had ruled it protected: no salvage, no disturbance, a grave with boundaries at last. I'd helped place the boundary buoys myself, and there was a plaque going on the largest timber next week—eleven names, my father's among the seekers honored on the reverse side, which was apparently my doing, though I genuinely don't remember requesting it and several people swear I did.

I stayed down until my air said otherwise. I didn't say anything down there. My father wouldn't have wanted speeches, and neither did I. But I touched the timber, the way he must have touched his, and I came up slower than I needed to, letting the water take its time with me.

Oscar was waiting at the rail with coffee that tasted like punishment and two of the worst pastries on the coast.

"So," she said. "It's finished."

I looked at the bay, at the town getting on with its squinting morning, at the buoy marking the edge of the Grounds rising and falling on the swell like a patient heartbeat.

"No," I said. "It's settled. Different thing."

"How so?"

"Finished means there's nothing left to do." I took the coffee. It was terrible. I drank it anyway. "Settled means you know exactly what you owe, and to whom, and you pay it forward instead."

Oscar smiled at that—her grandfather's smile, actually, though she doesn't know I've noticed the inheritance—and looked out at the water like she was reading something in it.

Which, knowing her, she probably was. She'd been quiet for days in a particular way, the way she gets before a story grabs her by the throat. And that evening, spread across my chart table, she laid out a coastal survey with a dozen little marks inked along it—unidentified wrecks, drowned sites, places the register calls *position approximate*—and said, "Ines. How do you feel about building something that lasts?"
