## 22. The Next Horizon

*Oscar*

The ketch had a name once, but the storm took it along with most of her starboard rail, so we called her *the Ketch* for three weeks while we argued about what to call her next. Ines wanted something practical. I wanted something sentimental. We compromised on nothing, which is how most of our compromises worked.

"Hand me the caulking iron," Ines said from inside the hull, her voice bouncing around the bilge like a coin dropped in a well. "And stop staring at that letter."

"I'm not staring at it."

"You've been staring at it for twenty minutes. The varnish is going to set with your fingerprints in it."

She was right, which was becoming an uncomfortable habit of hers. The letter sat on the workbench where it had sat for weeks, folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling, its envelope addressed in my grandfather's hand: *For Oscar, if she ever stops running long enough to read it.*

He'd known me forty years before I was born. That's the thing about grandfathers who plan ahead—they're insufferable about it afterward.

I picked up the caulking iron instead and passed it through the hatch. Below decks smelled of pine tar and salt and the particular sweetness of new wood, and I stood there breathing it in like it was an answer to a question I hadn't asked yet.

The harbor at Port Callis was loud with other people's ordinary lives—gulls arguing over fish heads, a ferry horn, someone's radio playing a song about a woman who left. Three weeks ago the Vanes' empire had come apart on every screen in the country, and my face had been on half of them, holding up photocopied pages of a dead man's ledger. Now nobody looked at me twice. It's amazing how fast the world moves on from your worst year when it has worse years of its own to chew on.

"You going to read it today?" Ines asked, emerging from the hatch with sawdust in her hair and a smudge of tar across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a distracted artist.

"Maybe."

"You said maybe last Tuesday."

"I say a lot of things."

"You do." She took the iron back anyway, out of some instinct I was starting to recognize as affection. "Chart table's warped. We'll need a new one before we can plot anything."

"We're not plotting anything yet."

"We're plotting everything," she said. "That's the whole point of the boat."

* * *

*Ines*

My father used to say a boat is never finished, only abandoned at various stages of denial. He said it the way he said everything—flat, certain, usually while doing something with his hands that proved it true. Refitting the ketch felt like a conversation with him I hadn't gotten to have: here's where you check for rot, mija; here's where the sea gets in if you let it; here's where you decide what the vessel is *for*, because a vessel without purpose is just a hole you pour money into.

We were three weeks into pouring money into the hole, and I had never been happier.

Oscar Coldharbour was sanding the toe rail with the grim focus of a woman punishing the wood personally. She'd been like that since the hearings ended—restless, over-caffeinated, allergic to stillness. The lawyers and journalists had gone back to their own harbors. The Vanes' assets were frozen in neat bureaucratic rows. And Oscar had discovered that being right didn't fill a day the way being hunted did.

"The memorial commission approved the survey permit," I told her, dropping the letter on the workbench next to the one she wasn't reading. "Official charting of the drowned sites. Reckoning first, then the others. They want our soundings by spring."

"They want *your* soundings. My name's a liability on government paperwork."

"Your name's the reason there's paperwork at all." I pulled the tarp off the chart table we'd dragged aboard—a secondhand thing with a cracked glass top—and started marking out the coast in pencil. Cape Reckoning to the Serrat shallows, every wreck site my father had logged in his notebooks, every dive he'd planned and never made. "Besides, they listed us jointly. 'Coldharbour-Marlowe Expedition.' You can't resign from your own surname."

"That's exactly what surnames are for."

I laughed despite myself. This was the other thing about the three weeks: somewhere between the hull scraping and the argument over anchor chain gauge, the wary bargain had quietly become friendship, the way water becomes wine only in stories but becomes ballast in real life—something heavy you're glad to carry.

She drifted over and looked at the penciled coast. Her finger stopped at a small cross north of the cape.

"What's this one?"

"Serrat. Trawler, 1961. Four men." I kept my voice level. "One of them was the diver who taught my father. His body was never recovered. The company log says engine failure. My father's notes say the cargo hold was full of Vane freight that never got reported lost."

Oscar was quiet a moment. Then she picked up the pencil and wrote *SERRAT — VERIFY* beside the cross in handwriting far steadier than mine.

"All of them?" she asked. "All the crosses?"

"As many as we can reach."

"Then we'd better fix the chart table properly." She glanced at the letter on the bench, and this time she didn't look away from it so quickly. "After."

* * *

*Oscar*

There is no good time to read a letter from a dead man who loved you. I'd been telling myself this for weeks, and it was true, and it was also a coward's arithmetic—the kind of math you do when you want the sum to come out to *later*.

But it was evening, and the harbor was going gold and quiet, and Ines had gone ashore to argue with a chandler about zincs, and the ketch rocked in that patient way boats rock, like they're waiting for you to catch up.

I unfolded the letter.

*Oscar—*

*If you're reading this, then either the truth came out or it didn't, and either way I'm sorry I'm not there to see your face. Your grandmother always said I planned things too far ahead. She was right. I planned this wrong too—I thought naming them would be enough. Names are not enough. Paper burns, memories fade, and men like the Vanes hire better lawyers than the dead ever get.*

*So if it did come out: don't let them make you a monument. Monuments are where causes go to be forgotten politely. Build something instead. Chart the water. Put names on charts where companies put numbers. Make it so anyone who sails this coast has to look at what's down there.*

*And if it didn't come out—if you found this alone, with no proof—then I'm sorry twice. But listen: the sea keeps better records than any bank. What sinks stays. What's true doesn't rot. Find people who know the water and trust them more than you trust your own stubbornness, because God knows yours will need the help.*

*I chased that fortune my whole life and it cost me everything except the part that matters, which is you. Don't chase it. Chart it. There's a difference, and the difference is who you become.*

*All my love, longer than the tide,*
*Grandpa*

I read it twice. The second time my eyes blurred, and I let them, because there was no one below decks to see and because Ines would have said something dry and kind and I needed the unkind silence first—the silence where grief finally got to finish its sentence.

Forty years I'd thought he was lost. Then I'd learned he was murdered, and rage had carried me further than grief ever had. Now, sitting in a half-refitted ketch with tar under my fingernails and a survey permit in the drawer, I understood what he'd actually left me. Not the fortune—we'd sunk that beyond any salvage, gold and all, and I'd sign off on that choice again every morning for the rest of my life. Not even the ledger, though the ledger had burned the Vanes down to the waterline.

He'd left me the work. The unglamorous, unpayable, endless work of putting true things on maps.

I folded the letter along its old creases and put it in the chart drawer, under the glass, where I'd see it every time we plotted a course. Not a shrine. A heading.

* * *

*Ines*

I came back aboard at dusk carrying four zincs, a bag of oranges, and the news that the chandler had given us a discount "on account of the newspaper business," which meant our reputation had become currency in Port Callis, spendable in small denominations.

Oscar was at the chart table. The letter was open under the glass.

I set the oranges down and said nothing, because there are moments you don't walk into, you just stand at the edge of and keep watch.

"He wanted a chart," she said finally. "Not a monument. A chart."

"Then he'd have liked the permit." I laid it beside the letter. "Section four's interesting. They gave us authority to designate protected sites. Legal standing. If a Vane shell company—or any company—tries to raise anything inside our surveyed boundaries, it's interference with a memorial. Federal offense."

Oscar read section four twice. Something moved through her face, slow as weather—a front clearing.

"So the gold we sank," she said, "isn't just gone. It's *guarded*."

"It's guarded. By us. On paper, at least, which is where the Vanes used to live."

She laughed—short, surprised, real. Then she picked up the pencil and began ruling lines on the chart, cape to cape, cross to cross, and I watched my father's notebook and her grandfather's letter start to become the same document.

"We'll need a better depth sounder," she said.

"We'll need a crew eventually."

"We'll need to name this boat."

"We agreed not to discuss the name until the hull was done."

"The hull's done," I said. "I finished it this afternoon while you were having your feelings."

She glared. I ate an orange. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and beyond the breakwater the sea did what it always does—kept its records, held its dead, waited to be read.

* * *

*Oscar*

We named her two days later, at slack water, which Ines insisted was the only honest time to christen anything since it was the only time the sea wasn't lying about itself.

She broke a bottle of cheap sparkling wine across the bow—my grandfather's brand, chosen deliberately—and said the name out loud once, and that was it. No ceremony. No speeches. The name went onto the transom in white paint that afternoon, both of us kneeling on the dock, taking turns with the brush because neither of us trusted the other's letters.

*RECKONING'S LEDGER.*

"Not subtle," I said, sitting back to admire it.

"Subtlety is for people with something to hide." Ines capped the paint. "We're in the business of the opposite."

The tide turned. I felt it before I saw it—the dock lines taking up their small tension, the water sliding past the pilings, the whole harbor exhaling and beginning again. Somewhere north, past the headlands, wrecks waited with our names penciled beside them: *SERRAT — VERIFY.* And beyond those, more crosses in my partner's flat sure hand, each one a man or woman the companies had written off as weather.

Ines stood and offered me a hand up. I took it.

"First sounding run tomorrow?" she asked.

"First sounding run tomorrow. Weather window looks clean."

"For now."

"For now," I agreed. "It always does. That's the deal."

We walked the dock toward the chandlery to settle our account, two women with tar on their hands and a debt to no one, and behind us the ketch rode the turning tide under her new name—carrying nothing worth stealing, bound for everything worth finding.
