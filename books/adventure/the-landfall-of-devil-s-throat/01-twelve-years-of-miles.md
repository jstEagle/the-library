## 1. Twelve Years of Miles

The thief's name was Pellit Osk, and by the second day of tracking him across the salt flats, Ines Coldharbour had learned three things about him that Pellit Osk would have been mortified to know.

First, that he favored his left boot, which had a loose heel and made him walk like a man perpetually stepping over something invisible. Second, that he stopped every few miles to look back, which meant he was afraid, which meant he was smart, because the flats were exactly the sort of place where fear kept a man alive. Third, that he drank his water too fast. His camps were marked by the small, tidy circles where he'd set his skin, and every circle was pressed deeper on one side, the way a skin sits when it's been drained and set down empty.

Ines crouched over the fourth such circle and allowed himself a small, private smile. He liked a thief with a routine. Routines were just confessions written on the ground, and Ines had spent twelve years learning to read them.

*You'll stop at the flat rock before dark,* he thought, standing, rolling the ache out of his knees. *You always stop at the flat rock. Men like you believe in flat rocks the way other men believe in saints.*

The salt flats stretched in every direction, white as a scoured bone, cracked into hexagons big as dinner plates. The sun was doing its patient, murderous work overhead, and the horizon shimmered like the flats were dreaming of being a lake again. There was nothing out here. That was the whole point of the flats, and the whole reason Ines loved them, insofar as he permitted himself to love anything, which he generally didn't, on principle.

Twelve years ago, he had made himself a list. It wasn't written anywhere—writing things down was against the entire spirit of the exercise—but it lived in his head, polished by use like a river stone:

*No debts. No names. No history.*

No debts, because a debt was a thread, and a thread could be pulled. No names, because a name was a hook, and hooks got set. No history, because history was the longest thread of all, and the people who held the other end of *his* history were the kind of people who never, ever let go.

He had been very good at it. He was, if he was honest with himself—and honesty with himself was the one debt he allowed, paid nightly, in full—perhaps the best in the world at it. He paid cash. He gave the name "Ines" and nothing else, and "Ines" was common enough in the border towns to be no name at all, like calling yourself "Sand" or "Tuesday." He moved before he was missed. He was polite to everyone and known by no one. He had become, over twelve years, a kind of professional absence, a man-shaped hole in the world that closed up behind him as he passed.

It had felt, for the first ten years, like freedom.

Lately—say, the last two years, though he'd only admit to the last six months—it had felt like something else. Like standing in a room with no doors and congratulating yourself on never having to leave.

He shook the thought off the way you shake off a fly. There was a thief to catch, and a thief was a simple problem, and Ines had learned to treasure simple problems the way a starving man treasures plain bread.

The job had come to him in the town of Corro's Landing, which wasn't much of a landing since the river had changed its mind twenty years ago and left the town high and dry, sulking. A salt merchant named Dree had hired him—not for his name, which was nothing, but for his reputation among the small, careful people who needed things found and did not want to explain things to constables. Pellit Osk had stolen a strongbox. Not gold, not jewels. Papers. And Dree had gone gray as his own merchandise describing them, and had paid Ines triple to get them back and say nothing about it anywhere, ever.

Papers. Ines had turned that over on the ride out of town. A thief who steals papers is either a fool or a tool, and fools don't cross the salt flats alone. Which meant someone had sent Pellit Osk, and someone wanted whatever was in that strongbox badly enough to spend a fool on it.

That was the part Ines didn't like. He told himself he didn't like it the way a locksmith doesn't like a lock that's been picked before—professionally, cleanly, without any personal stake whatsoever. He was getting better at telling himself things. Twelve years of practice.

* * *

He caught up with the flat rock at dusk, and Pellit Osk wasn't there.

Ines stood in the thief's cold camp—the flat rock still holding a faint warmth, a fire scraped together and scattered with the haste of a man who'd heard something—and read the ground like an apology. Boot prints, left-heel-heavy, running north. Not walking. Running. And beside them, pressed into the salt crust, another set of prints that made Ines's stomach do something slow and cold.

Prospector's prints. The wide, splayed kind, the kind a man gets from twenty years of walking canyon bottoms in nailed boots, and they were *dragging*. One foot landing flat, the other scuffing. A man walking hurt.

Ines looked north, at the last violet light dying over the nothing, and felt the particular unease of a man whose tidy problem has just grown a second problem. He was a ranger—had been, once, in another life with another name, before the list—and the old training rose in him like water finding its level. *Hurt man in the flats at night is a dead man by morning. Thief running from something that isn't you. Ask yourself what a dying man and a fleeing man are both running from, and ask yourself why the answer is the same direction.*

He followed the prospector's prints instead of the thief's. He told himself this was because a dying man might still be saved, and saving people was the one entry on the old list he'd never managed to erase. He told himself it had nothing to do with the fact that the thief's trail led north, and the prospector's trail led north, and the strongbox full of papers had come from somewhere north, and the flats were supposed to be empty in every direction, and yet here were three men all pointed at the same nothing like compass needles.

Three men, all pointed at the same nothing. Ines did not like arithmetic like that. Arithmetic like that always summed to his own name, and he'd spent twelve years making sure his name summed to nothing at all.

* * *

He found the camp an hour past full dark, by its fire.

That was the thing that told him everything before he saw anything: the fire was *built*. Not a scrappy thief's fire, huddled and shielded, but a proper prospector's fire, laid in a ring of stones with a reflector wall, the kind of fire a man builds when he has stopped caring whether anyone finds him, or has decided he wants to be found. It threw a warm, wobbling light across a camp that had been lived in for days—a lean tarp, a cold camp stove, a mule that looked at Ines with the profound indifference of an animal who has already made its peace with whatever happens next.

The man by the fire was sixty, maybe older, the way desert men get old—weathered down to the essential, like a stick of driftwood. He had a bedroll propped behind him and a hand resting on his belly in a way that was not resting at all, and there was a dark stain on his shirt that the firelight was very interested in.

"Evening," the prospector said, without surprise. "Took you long enough."

Ines stopped at the edge of the light, an old habit, hands loose and visible. "I'm not who you're expecting."

"Son, I wasn't expecting anybody. That's rather the point of where I am." The man smiled, and it cost him something; Ines saw the smile get paid for in a tightening around the eyes. "But you've got ranger's feet. I'd know them anywhere. You walk like the ground owes you money and you're collecting."

"I'm just a tracker."

"Mm." The prospector looked at him for a long moment, firelight moving in the deep creases of his face, and Ines had the sudden, crawling feeling of being *read*—of standing in a market with his pockets open. "Just a tracker. Out here. Alone. Following a dying man's blood across sixty miles of salt for no pay and no name." He chuckled, and the chuckle turned into a cough that went on too long. "You're just like your grandmother. She also lied badly when she was being careful."

The flats were silent. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, the salt crust ticked and settled, the sound the flats make at night as the heat leaves them, like the whole world quietly cracking its knuckles.

Ines found that his heart was going hard and slow, a drum in an empty hall. "You've mistaken me for someone."

"Have I." The prospector's eyes were pale, nearly colorless in the firelight, and utterly unfooled. "Maybe I have. Maybe I've been bleeding out here for two days and mistook a stranger for the one man in the world who'd walk this far to stay nobody." He shifted, and the pain moved across his face like weather. "Or maybe I've spent the last three years looking for you, Ines Coldharbour, and the flats finally did me the kindness of sending you by."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Ines heard it fall and fall and never heard it hit bottom.

Twelve years. Twelve years of *no debts, no names, no history*. Twelve years of being Tuesday, of being Sand, of being a man-shaped hole that closed up behind him. He had been so careful. He had been so *good* at it. And here was a stranger with a hole in his gut saying the buried word out loud, easy as passing the salt, and the terrible thing—the thing that made Ines's hands go cold despite the fire—was not that the man knew.

The terrible thing was that some sealed, starved room inside him had *recognized* the name. Had stood up. Had come to the door.

"Sit down," the prospector said gently. "Or don't, and bleed with me standing up, it's all one to me now. But you'll want to hear this before your friend Pellit sells my map to the salvage fleet, and believe me, boy, you will want to hear it. Everyone does, in the end. It's got your family's name on the cover."

The fire cracked. The mule sighed. Out on the flats, in the dark, the world went on being empty in every direction, and Ines Coldharbour—no debts, no names, no history—understood with perfect clarity that the cage door he'd spent twelve years pretending was a horizon had just been opened by a dying man, and that he was going to walk through it.

"Start talking," he said, and sat down in the light.
