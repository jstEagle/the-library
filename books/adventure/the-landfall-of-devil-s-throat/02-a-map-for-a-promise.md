## 2. A Map for a Promise

The thief's trail ended at a camp that wasn't the thief's.

Ines knew this before he crested the last rise of salt-crusted gravel, because the smoke was wrong. A petty thief traveling alone builds a fire small enough to hide behind a kneeling camel. This fire was generous, cheerful even, throwing light against a canvas lean-to and a hand-cart stacked with sample sacks. Somebody out here had been planning to live a while.

Somebody out here was also bleeding to death, if the dark spread across the ground in front of the lean-to meant what it usually meant.

Ines crouched at the edge of the firelight and did what he always did first: he counted his exits. Two, on foot, plus one bad idea involving the cart. Then he listened. The desert at night has a vocabulary—wind through wire, sand ticking off stone—and underneath it came a sound he recognized from twelve years of finding people who didn't want to be found. Wet breathing. The kind with a rattle in it.

"Whoever you are," said a voice from inside the lean-to, "you walk like a man reading a ledger."

Ines stayed low. "That's not a compliment where I come from."

"I don't know where you come from." A pause, then a wet laugh. "Neither do you, I'd wager. Come into the light. I'm past robbing anyone, and you're past pretending you weren't going to look anyway."

There was no arguing with that kind of arithmetic, so Ines stood up slowly and let the fire see him: weathered coat, no insignia, rifle slung muzzle-down the way rangers carry when they want to look like herders. The prospector propped against the cart wheel was sixty if he was forty-five, sun-cured to the color of old rope, one hand pressed flat and useless against his belly where someone had opened him up with more enthusiasm than skill.

"You're not Hollis," Ines said. "The man I'm tracking. Skinny. Stole a horse with sentimental value and no other value whatsoever."

"Hollis." The prospector tried the name like a bad date wine. "Skinny fellow, talked too much, asked me twice about water and once about whether I'd seen any 'old wrecks around.' That your thief?"

Ines felt something cold settle behind his ribs. "He asked you about wrecks."

"He asked everybody about wrecks. Then he asked somebody the wrong question, or maybe I did." The prospector lifted his hand from his belly, looked at it with mild academic interest, and put it back. "Shot me at dusk. Took my good boots, left these." He wiggled a foot clad in cracked galoshes. "Left everything else, which tells you he was in a hurry, which tells you somebody else is coming, which brings us to the part of the evening where I make you an offer you'll pretend to refuse."

"I haven't refused anything yet."

"You will. Everyone does. It's the refusing that makes them take it." He fumbled under the sample sacks and produced a wax-cloth packet, oilskin-wrapped, tied with waxed cord—the kind of wrapping desert people used when they wanted a thing to survive being buried, flooded, or inherited by idiots. He held it out. His arm shook, but his eyes didn't.

"I'm not taking anything from a dying man," Ines said. "Except maybe advice, and only if it's short."

"It's short. Don't die near your own camp." The prospector grinned, which cost him something. "The map's not for you to take. It's for you to *carry*. There's a difference. Taking is theft. Carrying is a promise. I've done the arithmetic on you already—you count exits, you keep your hands still, you asked about the horse before you asked about the gold. You're a man who keeps promises because it's cheaper than keeping secrets."

"That's cynical."

"That's *desert*." He patted the packet against his knee. "Devil's Throat. Drowned canyon, west of the Serevane flats, opens to the sea twice a day like a throat clearing itself. There's a wreck down in it. Old wreck. Sealed strongroom, Coldharbour workmanship, which means it opens on tide-tables and nothing else—not force, not patience, not prayer. I have the tables." He tapped his own forearm, and Ines saw then the ink there, fine lines of numbers running from wrist to elbow like a second skeleton drawn under the skin. "I have them right here. And here is about to stop working."

Ines should have walked away. Every discipline he owned told him to walk away: no debts, no names, no history, no dying strangers handing him packages with family names stitched into them. Twelve years he had practiced the art of being nobody, and the first rule of being nobody was that nobody gets handed maps.

"What's in the strongroom?" he asked, which was the moment he lost, and he knew it even as he said it.

"Ledgers." The prospector watched him with terrible attention. "Sealed ledgers of the Coldharbour family. You flinched."

"I squinted. Wind."

"There's no wind."

"There's never no wind. That's how you know it's suspicious when there isn't."

The prospector laughed again, and this time the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Ines knelt beside him without deciding to. Up close the man smelled of iodine and salt and the particular sweetness of a body negotiating its final terms. He pressed the packet into Ines's hands with the authority of a man who had been waiting all day to do exactly that.

"Salvage fleet's coming," he said, when he could talk. "Veyra Coldharbour's fleet. Six tides behind me, maybe five now. She's been buying up every rumor of that wreck for two years. When she gets there and can't open the strongroom, she'll start cutting answers out of people, and I'd rather the answer be gone than cut. So. Promise me."

"Promise you what? Exactly? I want the terms in writing, and you're clearly not in a position to write."

"Reach the wreck before she does. Open it if you can. If you can't—" his grip tightened, surprising strength in it, "—then make sure whatever's in there ends up in hands that won't use it as a weapon. Or no hands at all. I leave the philosophy to you. I was always better at maps than morals."

"This is a terrible bargain. I don't know you. I don't know what's in those ledgers. For all I know they're recipes."

"They're not recipes."

"They could be very good recipes."

"Ines." 

The name landed like a dropped glass.

Ines went absolutely still. Not ranger-still, which is chosen; this was the stillness of a man caught somewhere he had spent twelve years making sure he could never be caught. "You have the wrong person."

"Do I?" The prospector's eyes were going glassy at the edges but his smile held. "Hollis sold your description to the fleet's outriders three days ago. Desert ranger, gray coat, counts his exits, asks about horses before gold. They paid him in coin and bad whiskey. But I bought the rest of it off a station clerk in Marrow's Rest, two years back, when I started looking for someone exactly like you. A ranger who walked out of nowhere with a ranger's warrant and no history. No debts. No names. No record anywhere before the age of—" he paused, savoring it, "—nineteen. Do you know how hard it is to be invisible, boy? It's expensive. Somebody paid for it. Somebody loved you enough to spend a fortune making you into no one."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the wind finally remembered its job and came through the camp, lifting ash and the smell of blood.

"My name," Ines said carefully, "is Ash. Just Ash. It's on my warrant."

"Mm. And my name is Odo Fenn, and yesterday I was alive." The prospector—Odo—closed his eyes for a moment, and Ines thought he'd lost him, but he came back up like a swimmer. "Coldharbour," he whispered. Not to Ines, exactly. To the fire, to the night, to whoever collects last words. "The ledgers are Coldharbour. The wreck is Coldharbour. The fleet hunting them is Coldharbour. And the man I'm giving them to—" his fingers found Ines's wrist, closed around it with the last of his strength, "—smells like a Coldharbour trying very hard not to."

"I don't know what you think you—"

"Promise me the tides, Ash-of-nowhere. Reach it first. That's all. One promise. Cheapest thing you'll ever give away and the most expensive thing I'll ever buy."

And here was the truth Ines would examine for many nights afterward, turning it over like a stone looking for the worm underneath: he wanted to say no. Every instinct, every rule, every careful year of his erasure said *no, drop the packet, walk out the two exits, become nobody again.* But underneath the dread—underneath it, wearing dread's coat, speaking in dread's voice—was curiosity, and curiosity is just hunger that's learned manners.

He was an orphan. He had always understood himself as an accident: parents unnamed, unrecorded, unaccounted for, a boy who appeared at a station house one winter with no story attached. If there were records—if there was a *reason*—

"One promise," Ines heard himself say. "I reach the wreck before the fleet. After that, I owe you nothing."

"After that," Odo agreed, "you owe yourself everything. Which is worse." He sagged back against the cart wheel, spent, and his breathing changed gear into something slower and more rattling. "Tide-tables are on my arm. Memorize what you can. The map shows the channels; the arm shows the doors. Devil's Throat doesn't forgive improvisation."

Ines sat with him while the fire burned down, memorizing. Numbers in columns, tide heights, moon phases, a sequence that repeated with variations like a song with verses. Odo drifted and surfaced, drifting longer each time, and near the end he surfaced once more and looked at Ines with sudden clarity, almost gentle.

"They hid you well," he said. "But nothing stays hidden forever. That's not a curse, boy. Sometimes it's a rescue."

Then he said the name again—*Coldharbour*, soft as a tide going out—and didn't say anything else, ever.

* * *

Ines buried him at dawn, which took longer than expected because the ground was salt pan and the shovel bounced. He marked the grave with the hand-cart, wheels removed and planted upright like a headstone, and he took nothing except the map, the galoshes (his boots were better, but leaving a dead man barefoot seemed rude), and a debt he hadn't agreed to and could not put down.

He stood over the grave and told himself the sensible things. *Walk away. Burn the map. The fleet will find a locked box and waste years on it. You are nobody. Nobody owes anybody.*

Then he looked east, where the horizon held a smudge of smoke that hadn't been there yesterday, and did the arithmetic he was so good at, and hated the answer.

Six tides.

Against every instinct he owned—every rule, every year, every careful mile of his invisible life—Ines Coldharbour folded the map into his coat, turned west, and started walking toward the one place in the world where his name was waiting for him.
