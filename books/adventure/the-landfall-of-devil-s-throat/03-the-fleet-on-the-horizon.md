## 3. The Fleet on the Horizon

The thief's name was Oskit Brall, and he had never in his life sold anything as honestly as he was about to sell this.

He sat in the stern of the salvage barge *Mercy of Numbers*, third vessel from the left in a fleet of nine, with his wrists bound in front of him and a cup of something hot pressed into his hands by a woman who had not yet told him her name. She didn't need to. Everyone on the flats knew the silhouette: Veyra Coldharbour, captain of the landfall fleet, the woman who had once bought an entire season's salvage rights to a dead port just so she could be the one to burn it down for the insurance of knowing what was inside.

Oskit had been running for two days when they caught him, which he considered a professional compliment. Now he sat drinking their tea and doing the arithmetic that had kept him alive through eleven years of petty crime: what do I have that they want, and how much of it can I afford to give away?

"You were seen," the woman said. She had a voice like a ledger being balanced—no wasted entries. "Two days ago, at the salt-gatherer's station near the Cinder Reach. You came out of a prospector's camp moving fast, and you weren't carrying anything."

"I wasn't carrying anything because there wasn't anything to carry," Oskit said.

"Then you ran fast from nothing."

"I run fast from everything. It's a discipline."

Someone behind her laughed and then stopped laughing very quickly. Veyra did not smile. She crouched down to his level, forearms on her knees, and studied him the way he imagined jewelers studied gravel looking for the one stone worth keeping.

"A man died in that camp," she said. "Gut-shot. And you, Oskit Brall, who has never in your life been within a mile of honest work, came out of there at a dead sprint. So either you shot him—and if so, where is his purse—or you saw something worth running from. Or running toward." She held out her hand, palm up. "I'm offering you a boat, provisions, and my personal guarantee that nobody on this fleet will break anything of yours that can't be set. In exchange for whatever it is you're pretending you don't have."

Oskit looked at the tea. He looked at her hand. He thought about the wax-cloth map, which he had indeed taken from the dying man's kit before fleeing—the whole map, folded into eighths, currently sweating against his ribs—and he thought about the fact that he could not read a single mark on it. It was full of numbers and channels and depth-soundings written in a hand too fine for a prospector, and every time he'd tried to make sense of it, the numbers had looked back at him like a locked door.

Here was the thing about Oskit Brall: he was a coward, but he was an honest coward, which is rarer than people think. He knew exactly what he was.

"It's a map," he said. "To a wreck. The Devil's Throat."

The name went around the deck like a dropped coin. Veyra's face did not change at all, which told Oskit two things: she already knew about the wreck, and she had not known anyone else alive knew where it was.

"Show me."

He produced the map from inside his shirt with the care of a man surrendering a hostage. She unfolded it on her knee. For a long moment she said nothing, and the only sound was the wind off the salt and the creak of nine ships riding at anchor.

"This isn't the prospector's work," she said finally. "This is Coldharbour surveying. Third-generation drafting style. Where did he get it?"

"How would I know? He was dying. He gave me—" Oskit stopped. Recalculated. "He asked me to carry word. That's all. Word."

"Word of what?"

"That's the part I'm still deciding whether to sell."

Veyra looked up at him then, and Oskit Brall, who had stared down customs men, debt collectors, and one genuinely memorable crocodile, felt his stomach drop like a stone down a well.

"He wanted me to reach the wreck before your fleet did," Oskit said. "That's the word. That's all of it. There was something else he said, some nonsense about tables, tide-tables, but he was bleeding out and half of it was gibberish—"

"Tide-tables," Veyra repeated softly.

She stood up. She handed the map to a tall man with rope-burned hands without looking at him, and the tall man took it the way priests take relics.

"Give Mr. Brall a bunk and a hot meal," Veyra said. "And put him somewhere he can't reach any small objects. Not out of cruelty. Out of respect for his limitations."

They led Oskit away, and he went, and later—much later—he would tell himself that selling the dying man's errand for a bunk and a meal was simply good business. But that night, lying in a hammock between two harpoon racks, he found himself thinking about the prospector's face, and the way the man had gripped his sleeve with the last of his strength, and the words he'd whispered: *Tell them nothing. Tell no one. Tell—*

Well. Oskit had told someone. But in his defense, and he offered this defense to nobody, the tea really had been excellent.

* * *

Ines Coldharbour—who had been Ines Marrow for twelve years, and Ines Vetch before that, and a dozen other names worn smooth as river stones—crouched on the rim of a salt pan forty miles east of the Cinder Reach and watched the smoke.

Nine columns of it, thin and dark, rising off the horizon in a neat row like organ pipes. Woodsmoke and coal-smoke together, which meant galleys burning, which meant a fleet under way and in no hurry. A hurried fleet burned hot and dirty. This smoke was patient.

He counted the columns twice. Nine ships. The salvage fleets worked the drowned ports along the coast, dredging the sunken towns for copper and coin, and they moved like weather—slowly, inevitably, and with total indifference to anything in their path.

He did the arithmetic the way he did everything else: carefully, and alone.

The thief had reached the Cinder Reach two days ahead of him. If the fleet had picked him up—and nine ships didn't gather in one place by accident; they gathered because someone had bought information—then they had the map, or word of the map, and they were heading for the Devil's Throat. The canyon lay six days' hard travel northeast across the flats, but the tides governed everything there. The wreck sat in a drowned channel that opened to the sea only at dead low water, and the spring tides—the ones deep enough to expose the strongroom level—came in a rhythm as fixed as a heartbeat. Six tides. The next usable low water fell six tides from now, and the one after that fell twelve, and after that the season turned and the channel stayed drowned until autumn.

Six tides. The fleet would arrive for the sixth. So would he, if he pushed. They would arrive rested, provisioned, nine ships strong, with divers and cranes and winches and probably a small army of men whose job description was *problems*.

He would arrive with a stolen map, a dead man's promise, and whatever he could carry.

Ines sat back on his heels and allowed himself one long breath—the kind he permitted himself perhaps twice a year—and admitted the truth he'd been circling since the prospector died whispering his own buried surname into the salt wind.

He wasn't running anymore. He hadn't been since the camp. Running was what you did when you wanted to stay invisible, and somewhere between the dying man's grip and the empty horizon, invisibility had stopped being the point. The map was in his shirt, against his skin, and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything, and every mile he'd walked since taking it had been a mile toward a name he'd spent twelve years burying.

*Coldharbour.*

He said it once, silently, just to feel the shape of it. Like putting his tongue against a broken tooth.

Then he got up, because self-pity was a luxury item and he'd given up luxuries along with names, and went to find a skiff.

* * *

The fishing village of Tarnholt consisted of eleven buildings, four boats, and a general air of having been forgotten by everyone including its own residents. Ines walked in at dusk with his hood up and his ranger's stride deliberately broken into the shuffling gait of a laborer, and he learned three things in the first hour from the taproom of the only establishment that served both beer and bait.

First: the salvage fleet had sent a tender through two days ago, buying charts, asking questions about the eastern channels, and paying in fresh coin that made the locals helpful and incurious in equal measure.

Second: the best shallow-draft boat in the village belonged to a man named Doss Halloway, who was currently drunk enough to be describing his boat's virtues to anyone who would listen and several who wouldn't.

Third: the village kept its tide-almanac chained to a post outside the harbormaster's hut, because the harbormaster was also the priest, the tax assessor, and possibly a fictional character maintained for bureaucratic purposes.

Ines drank one beer slowly, listened to everything, and made his plans with the mild regret of a man who had genuinely liked being nobody.

Stealing from Doss Halloway proved almost embarrassingly easy. The skiff was moored at the end of the rotting pier, shallow-keeled, tarred canvas over her stores, built exactly for the kind of water the Devil's Throat demanded. Ines boarded her quietly, found her provisioned for a week—which told him Doss had plans of his own, plans that were about to develop a significant delay—and cast off on the ebb.

The almanac was harder. It was chained, yes, but the chain's padlock was older than the harbormaster-priest-assessor and had rusted into a state of ceremonial rather than functional security. Ines broke it with a pry bar in the time it takes to feel bad about something, tucked the almanac under his arm, and left on the post a coin purse containing every piece of silver he owned, which was enough to buy Doss Halloway a better boat and the village a new lock, with change left over.

It wasn't honesty, exactly. It was more like tipping.

Then the shore fell away behind him and there was nothing but the channel, the stars, and the almanac open on his knees, its tide-tables marching down the page in columns of numbers that suddenly mattered enormously.

He read them by lamplight as the skiff slid north. Dead low water, day one: the outer bars exposed, passable to a shallow draft at dawn. Day two: lower still. Days three and four: neap tides, useless, the channel drowned past wading. Then the turn, and the springs building toward the fifth and sixth days—the great low waters, the ones that would strip the Devil's Throat bare to its bones and hold it open just long enough.

Just long enough for what, the almanac didn't say. Almanacs are honest that way. They tell you when the door opens, never what's behind it.

Ines steered by the stars and let the skiff run with the ebb, and somewhere past the second hour, with the village lights gone and the desert a black breathing presence on either side of the channel, he caught himself talking aloud—to the prospector, maybe, or to the map, or to nobody, which was the closest thing he'd had to a conversation in months.

"Six tides," he said. "You could have mentioned the fleet. You could have mentioned a lot of things."

The map, folded against his chest, said nothing. Maps are honest that way too.

But as the night deepened and the channel narrowed and the first pale suggestion of the drowned country rose ahead of him—dead trees standing in black water, the masts of a town that had been a town once—he felt something shift in his chest that he hadn't felt in twelve years. Not fear, though fear was there, keeping its respectful distance. Not quite hope, either. Something older than both.

It was the feeling of a door opening on a schedule, and a man deciding, against every instinct he'd ever cultivated, to be standing in front of it when it did.

Behind him, far to the south, the nine smokes of the salvage fleet burned steady and patient against the stars.

Ahead, the Throat waited with its mouth full of water and its belly full of paper.

Ines shipped the oars, checked the almanac one last time, and rowed into the dark.
