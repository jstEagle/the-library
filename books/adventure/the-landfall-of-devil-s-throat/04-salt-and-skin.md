## 4. Salt and Skin

The storm announced itself the way bad news always does: quietly, from a long way off, and then all at once.

Ines had been riding the skiff's runner across the hardpan for two days when he saw the horizon turn the color of old milk. He'd weathered sandstorms before—twelve years in the desert teaches you to read a sky like a ledger—but this one had the particular appetite of a storm that meant to take its time with him. It would bury the trail behind him, which was good. It would also bury him, which was less good.

He turned north toward the salt pans, where the gatherers kept their huts low and their doors heavy.

The first hut he found was half-swallowed by a dune, its roof bristling with iron stakes and strung glass that chimed in the rising wind like a nervous orchestra. The door was scarred oak banded with copper—good work, old work—and when Ines hammered on it, a voice inside said, "If you're the wind, go away. If you're the tax man, I'm dead. If you're neither, wipe your feet."

He wiped his feet.

The woman who opened the door was sixty-odd and built like the terrain she worked: flat, wide, and not to be moved by anything short of geological force. Her forearms were white as chalk to the elbow, stained permanently by her trade, and her eyes did a full inventory of Ines in the time it took him to blink twice.

"Ranger," she said. "Or ex-something. You've got the look of a man traveling light on purpose." She stepped aside. "Maren Ostrow. Come in before the sky comes in after you."

* * *

The hut was one room, dense with the smell of brine and lamp oil and something baking that made Ines's stomach file a formal complaint. Maren fed him without being asked—flatbread, salted fish, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in—and then settled across from him with the unhurried air of someone who intended to find out exactly who was eating her bread.

"Where are you bound?" she asked.

"South," Ines said.

"That's a direction, not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

She snorted, but let it lie, and for a while there was only the wind worrying at the walls and the glass chimes singing their anxious song overhead. Ines ate. Outside, the light went from milk to rust to a brown so total it seemed the world had been folded up and put away.

It was when he unrolled his gear to check his stores against the storm's length that the map came out of its oilcloth—the wax-cloth map, the dying man's map, the map Ines had told himself for two days he was carrying merely to destroy it or sell it or any reason other than the true one.

Maren's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Where," she said, "did you get that?"

Ines looked at the map as if seeing it fresh. The wax-cloth, stiff and pale. The ink drawn in that fine, patient hand. The canyon rendered in cross-section like a surgeon's diagram, the wreck marked with a symbol he now knew too well: a stylized throat, open.

"A prospector," he said. "Dying. He traded it to me."

"Dying how?"

"Gut-shot. He didn't say by whom."

"Mm." Maren set down her cup and held out one chalk-white hand. "May I?"

He hesitated. Everything in him said *no*, the same instinct that had burned his name out of twelve years of records. But the storm had him boxed in, and refusing would tell her more than handing it over.

She turned the map toward the lamp and ran her thumb along the border, where a line of tiny characters repeated like a hem.

"Coldharbour survey mark," she said. "See? The little interlocked C-H. My father gathered salt for their factor at Greywater Landing. I've seen that stamp on crates, on deeds, on the side of a dredge barge they scuttled rather than pay the duty on." She looked up. "This is Coldharbour workmanship. Nobody's drawn a map like this in thirty years, because nobody's had the money or the reason."

"They're gone," Ines said. "The Coldharbours. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what everyone knows." Maren smoothed the map flat on the table between them, careful as a woman handling a wound dressing. "You know what this is a map *of*?"

"The Devil's Throat. A drowned canyon. A wreck."

"And do you know why there's a wreck in a drowned canyon, ranger? Ships don't sail canyons. That wreck was put there, or it fell there, and either way—" she tapped the cross-section, the deep chamber marked in the patient hand, "—the story my father told, and his father before him, is that the Coldharbours drowned a town."

Ines kept his face still. Twelve years of practice. "Towns flood. Rivers shift."

"Not this town. Greywater Landing sat in that canyon, a salt port, four hundred souls. And the story goes that the family owed debts—enormous ones, the kind that end with creditors arriving with fire—and rather than pay, they bought the water rights upstream, broke the weirs in a wet spring, and let the canyon fill. Insurance paid for the ships lost. The ledgers went down with the town. Dead men don't collect debts, and drowned paper doesn't testify."

"That's a story," Ines said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "Salt camps breed stories the way standing water breeds mosquitoes."

"It does." Maren poured more tea, unhurried. "And yet here you are, crossing the nothing with a Coldharbour map to the exact spot, chasing a tide-table, if I know prospectors, because the strongroom under that wreck opens on a tide-window narrow as a keyhole. So either you believe the story, ranger, or you're betting your life that it's false. Which is it?"

"I'm betting nothing," Ines said. "I'm delivering a promise. A dying man asked me to get there before the salvage fleet does. What's in the wreck is his business, not mine."

"And what will you do when you're standing in it?"

"Leave."

Maren laughed—a real laugh, warm and rough, entirely without mockery, which somehow made it worse. "Oh, you're a cold one. Good. Cold men live out here." She leaned forward. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you ate my bread and you'll be stuck under my roof till the sky gives back. Whatever's in that strongroom, it isn't gold. Gold doesn't need a tide-key and a sealed room. Paper does. Paper that somebody wanted underwater very badly and very permanently."

"You sound certain."

"I sound like a woman whose grandfather watched the water come up the main street of Greywater Landing while the Coldharbour factor's family rode out of the canyon on the high road, dry as sermons." She said it simply, without heat, the way you'd report the weather. "Four hundred people, ranger. Some got out. Some didn't. And every record of who owed what to whom went into the drink, and the family packed up their name and scattered like startled birds, and the desert forgot them because the desert forgets everything. Except us. Salt keeps things. That's the whole trade."

Ines looked at his hands. They were steady. He made them pick up the tea.

"I'm not a Coldharbour," he said. "I don't know anything about them beyond what children say around fires."

"No," Maren agreed mildly. "Of course not."

The denial sat between them on the table next to the map, and it rang hollow the way a struck pot rings hollow—you could hear the emptiness in it, feel the thinness of the metal. Ines heard it himself. He wondered if she could see it in his face, and decided from her expression that she could, and worse, that she wasn't going to press. That was somehow more unnerving.

Because here was the thing he had not said aloud in twelve years, the thing he had buried deeper than any ledger: he remembered the name. Not from fireside stories. From before. From a house with a blue door and a mother who hummed while she copied figures, and a father who smelled of ink and river water, and a night when both of them went still as rabbits at the sound of hooves, and after that—after that, an orphanage under another name, and then no name at all, and miles, so many miles.

He had spent twelve years telling himself he'd fled a name that meant nothing to him. That he erased himself out of principle, out of a ranger's discipline, out of simple preference for clean horizons over dirty histories.

Sitting in a salt-gatherer's hut while a brown world howled outside, holding a dead man's map stamped with his own buried surname, Ines admitted the thing he'd been outrunning since he was nine years old:

You cannot flee a name that means nothing. Only a name that means everything takes that much running.

"You've gone somewhere," Maren observed. She refilled his cup unasked. "That's all right. Storms make people go places. Eat your bread."

* * *

The storm held them for a day and a half. Maren talked, because she was the sort who talked, and Ines learned more about salt than he'd ever wanted to know, and some things about the Coldharbours he'd never wanted to know either: the factor's kindness to the camp children, the family's private chapel with its strange open-throat sigil, the last barge that left Greywater Landing loaded with strongboxes and none of the crew ever seen again.

"They were careful people," Maren said, banking the lamp as the wind finally began to lose its temper. "Careful people keep records. Records are how careful people stay powerful after they're dead. If those ledgers exist, whoever holds them holds every debt, every crime, every name the family ever ruined—including, I'd wager, the names of anyone who tried to stop them." She glanced at him sidelong. "Funny thing, though. My father always said the factor's own clerk vanished the same season the town drowned. Man, wife, and a boy. Never found. Never written about anywhere, neither—which for careful people is saying something. As if somebody scrubbed them out special."

Ines stood very still in the lamplight.

"Why scrubbed out?" he managed.

"Protection, maybe. Or shame. Or maybe the clerk's kin paid someone to make them invisible." She shrugged her broad shoulders. "Desert's full of people who became nobody on purpose. Present company included, I'd guess."

Outside, the last gusts dragged themselves over the hut and died. Through the little window, stars were reappearing, sharp and indifferent, and the horizon lay clean and blank as unwritten paper.

Ines rolled the map back into its oilcloth with hands that were almost steady.

"I should go," he said. "There's a fleet six tides behind me. Maybe five now."

"At night? Over the hardpan?"

"The tide won't wait for daylight."

Maren studied him for a long moment, then went to a shelf and pressed a wrapped bundle into his hands: bread, dried fish, a skin of water. "For the road. And ranger—one thing more." She tapped the oilcloth bundle, right over the hidden interlocked letters. "Whatever you find down there, whoever you find written in it—remember that paper only has the power you give it. Burn it, keep it, drown it again. But decide *before* you're standing in the water. Decisions made waist-deep belong to the tide."

He nodded, and it was a lie of a nod, because he already knew he hadn't decided anything, and wouldn't, couldn't, until he saw what his family had drowned to hide—and whether, in those drowned pages, there was a line of ink with his mother's humming handwriting in it.

At the door, Maren caught his sleeve.

"The clerk's boy," she said quietly. "My father said the family name was Coldharbour too. Distant branch. Disowned early, lucky ones, they called them." Her chalk-white eyes held his, gentle and merciless. "Safe travels, *nobody*. Mind the fifth tide."

Ines walked out under the washed stars, and did not look back, and thought about nothing at all, very loudly, the whole way to the skiff.
