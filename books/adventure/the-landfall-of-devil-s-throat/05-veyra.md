## 5. Veyra

The thief's name was Oskit Brall, and he weighed less than the chair he was tied to.

Veyra Salt-For-Coldharbour — she had taken the second name herself, at fourteen, in a fit of spite that had since hardened into policy — sat across from him on the deck of the *Ledger's Due* and ate her breakfast while he sweated. Eggs, hard-boiled. Bread with real butter, which cost more out here than the boat he'd sold them. She ate slowly, because watching a captain eat calmly while you waited to learn your fate was worth three hours of shouting, and shouting gave her nothing but a sore throat and liars who felt brave.

"Oskit," she said. "That's a made-up name."

"It's my mother's name."

"It's a name someone invented for a man they expected to cheat." She wiped her fingers on a cloth and set it down neatly beside her plate. "You told my quartermaster there was a map. You told my pilot it led to gold. You told my bosun — and this is my favorite — that the fleet would never find it without you. Three stories from one small mouth. I admire the industry. I don't believe any of them."

"They're all true," Oskit said, in the voice of a man discovering that his own voice could crack in two directions at once.

"Then this will be quick." She nodded, and her bosun — Pell, enormous, gentle as a landslide — tipped the thief's chair back until his head hung over the rail and the green water of the shallows looked up at him. Not deep water. Shallow water, the kind with teeth in it. "Here is what I know already, so you can spend your breath on something useful. A prospector came through Sallow Reach six weeks ago asking after tide-tables for the Vane coast. He paid in salt-silver, which means he'd found something worth minting. Two weeks ago he was seen heading east with a wax-cloth map case. Yesterday you come running into our camp with sand in your boots and a story about a map you saw but didn't take, because — why didn't you take it?"

"I tried," Oskit said, with dignity, upside down. "There was a ranger."

Veyra raised an eyebrow. Pell brought the chair back up.

"A ranger," she repeated.

"Desert type. Grey coat. Came out of nowhere. Took the map off the prospector before I got there. I only saw it a minute — wax-cloth, red stitching, a canyon drawn like a throat." He swallowed. "He moved like nobody I've ever seen. No tracks. I tracked him for a day and lost him on bare stone."

Veyra stood and walked to the rail. Below, the salvage fleet lay at anchor in the roadstead of Little Corrow: nine hulls, four cranes, two hundred and forty souls who ate because she fed them and followed because she was right more often than anyone else they'd ever met. Smoke from the village chimneys drifted across the water. It smelled of fish and tar and, faintly, of burning — but that last part was memory, or appetite, or something she preferred not to name.

A ranger with no tracks. A map with red stitching.

"You're Coldharbour," Oskit said suddenly, from the floor, having done the same arithmetic. "Everyone says it. The way you look at old wrecks. The way you asked about the Vane coast twice."

Pell shifted his weight, ready to tip the chair again.

"No, leave him," Veyra said. "He's finally being useful." She crouched so their eyes were level. The thief flinched; she had been told her eyes did something at close range, some inheritance of attention that people read as threat. She had never decided whether to correct it. "Yes. My grandmother was a Coldharbour before the name became a thing you buried your children under. My mother spat when she said it. My father left before I could ask him why. So understand me, Oskit Brall: I have spent thirty years collecting pieces of a family that disowned mine first, and I am very, very good at it. Now. The ranger. What else?"

"He had grey eyes," Oskit said. "And he — when he took the map, he read it. Upside down, fast, one pass. And then he went still, like a man hearing his own name called from a house he burned down." The thief licked his lips. "And he headed east. Toward the Throat."

Veyra rose. Her knees cracked; she was thirty-eight and the sea collected its fees early. "Pell, give him bread and put him somewhere he can't invent anything else. If he behaves, he eats tomorrow too."

"And if he runs?"

"Then the shallow water gets its teeth back." She smiled at Oskit, warmly, and watched him decide not to run. That was the whole art, really. Nobody ever had to be thrown overboard. They just had to believe she'd enjoy it.

* * *

Little Corrow's dock was built on pilings older than anyone living, and the harbormaster, a woman named Dree with forearms like mooring rope, had spent forty years charging every vessel that touched it and remembering every debt. Veyra had met her kind before. They were the historians of the coast, and historians could be bought, but never cheaply, and never twice.

She brought Dree aboard at dusk, poured her a cup of plum wine — the good bottle, the one she'd been saving for a victory — and laid a chart of the Vane coast between them.

"The Devil's Throat," Veyra said. "What do you know that isn't in the almanacs?"

Dree drank, considered, and said, "Nothing I'd sell for wine."

"Name a price."

"You couldn't pay it."

"Try me."

Dree set down the cup with great care. "I'll tell you what everyone knows for free, Captain, since you've been generous. Everyone knows the Throat floods wrong. Six tides a year it drains past reason, and six tides a year it fills like something's breathing. Everyone knows there's a wreck down there big enough that the fisherfolk call it the Cathedral. And everyone knows the Coldharbours drowned a town to hide what they owed, and the town was right about where the Throat opens, and no one has ever proved it because proving it would mean diving into a place that eats divers." She folded her arms. "That's the free portion."

"And the priced portion?"

"The priced portion is names. Dates. Which Coldharbour signed what, and who rowed the boats, and where the survivors were resettled so they'd stop talking." Dree's eyes were flat and patient as ballast stones. "My grandmother was one of the survivors, Captain. So the price is this: you tell me, when you find it, whether it's true. Not the fleet's version. Yours. Face to face, before you sell it to anyone."

Veyra looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, genuine sound that surprised even her. "You want the truth delivered personally. To a dockmaster."

"To a witness," Dree said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

"Done." Veyra shook her hand. It felt like shaking hands with a cliff. "But understand — if the truth turns out to be worth more than your promise, I'll still keep the promise. I just want you to know what you're dealing with."

"Oh, I knew when you boarded," Dree said. "You've got the Coldharbour chin. My gran described it. Said the whole family looked like they were waiting for the world to apologize."

* * *

It was near midnight, going over the fleet's stores with her purser, that the answer arrived — not with drama, but the way real answers always came, sideways, while she was counting rope.

The ranger. Grey coat, no tracks, read the map in one pass and went still. Headed east alone, ahead of nine ships and two hundred forty hands.

She had grown up on stories of the family that wasn't hers. Her mother's sister had married a Coldharbour — the youngest son, the soft one, the one who'd argued against the drowning and been erased for it along with his wife. There had been a child. Veyra remembered, distantly, being seven and hearing the adults whisper that the boy hadn't died with his parents, that someone had carried him off into the interior, that the family's records had been scrubbed so clean even the guilt had no name attached anymore.

*Erased from every record.* That was the phrase her mother used, spitting. *They erased him.*

And here was a man who had erased himself. Twelve years, if Oskit's tracker-talk meant anything — a professional absence, a life sanded down to no debts, no names, no history. Who learned to do that? Orphans learned to survive it. Only someone taught it, deliberately, by someone who loved him enough to make him unfindable —

— only a Coldharbour, hidden by the one Coldharbour with a conscience.

Veyra set down the inventory list and stood in the lamplight of her cabin, feeling the strange vertigo of a hunt turning into a reunion. Her cousin. Somewhere east, moving fast, carrying a map stitched in red toward the proof of everything. He didn't know she existed. He'd been made not to know.

"Poor bastard," she said aloud, and meant it, and then began giving orders anyway.

* * *

At dawn she took the fleet's fastest hull, the *Tithe*, up the coast to a fishing village called Brackenstep, because Dree's free information had included one more item: a Coldharbour agent had passed through Brackenstep twenty years ago asking the same questions, and the villagers had answered him badly.

The village had one dock, one shrine, and one collective memory, and the memory was locked behind a headman named Sorrel who greeted Veyra's longboat with the smile of a man who intended to know nothing at a very profitable rate.

"Captain," he said. "Welcome. Terrible weather for salvage."

"It's clear," Veyra said.

"Terribly clear." He spread his hands. "Ask your questions. We're simple folk. We'll answer simply."

She asked. He answered, simply, with nothing in any of it, and his people stood behind him in a silent wall of knowing nothing, and Veyra understood that this had been rehearsed for twenty years. The Coldharbour agent had come; the village had closed like a fist; the fist had never opened.

She could have stayed a week. She could have offered coin until the coin lost meaning. Instead she walked to the end of their dock, looked at the pilings — old, tarred, the village's whole livelihood tied up along them — and said, quietly, to Sorrel:

"My family drowned a town once to hide a debt. I'm trying to find out if it's true. You've known that for twenty years and chosen silence, and silence was your right. But now a man is racing me to the proof, and every hour he's ahead is an hour I might lose him, and if I lose him, the truth dies in the dark with everything else." She struck a match off her boot heel. "So I'm going to burn your dock. Not the boats. Not the homes. The dock. Because you'll rebuild it in a season, and you'll remember rebuilding it, and the next time someone asks you a question about the Coldharbours, you'll remember what your silence costs and decide whether it's still worth the price."

Sorrel's face went through several weathers. "You're mad."

"I'm thorough," Veyra said, and lit the tar.

The fire caught the way old tar always dreams of catching — fast, bright, roaring up the pilings like a choir finding its note. The villagers shouted and formed a bucket line that was mostly theater; the dock was gone inside an hour, black ribs steaming in the shallows, and no one had drowned, and no one had swung an axe at anyone, and Veyra stood on the beach and watched them hate her with a clarity she found almost restful.

Sorrel came to her at the waterline, soot-faced, shaking. "You'll burn in hell for this."

"Probably," she agreed. "But I'll burn there knowing things. Now — the agent, twenty years ago. What did he find out before you closed your mouths? And think carefully, because I'll be back in a season to check the new dock, and I burn slower the second time."

And Sorrel, broken open exactly as precisely as she'd calculated, told her: the agent had traced the hidden child as far as the interior garrisons. The child had been renamed. The trail had ended at a desert ranger station and a boy of five listed under a name that meant nothing — a name the agent had written down and carried home, and the writing of it was recorded, Veyra knew, in the Coldharbour correspondence held in a bank vault in Sallow Reach.

A name that meant nothing. Except to her, standing on a burning beach, doing the arithmetic at last.

Ines. The soft uncle's son. Her cousin, twelve years invisible, six tides ahead, and — she checked the horizon, where the heat shimmered over the salt like a held breath — running straight toward the only thing in the world that belonged to both of them.

"Signal the fleet," Veyra said, turning back to her longboat. "All nine hulls. We're going to the Throat."

She paused, and almost smiled.

"And send a rider ahead. Tell whoever's tracking him this, word for word: *Your cousin says hello.*"
