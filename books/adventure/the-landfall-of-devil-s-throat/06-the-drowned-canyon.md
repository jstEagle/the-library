## 6. The Drowned Canyon

The Devil's Throat did not announce itself. That was the first thing Ines learned about it, standing on the rim at dawn with the almanac open against his knee and nothing in front of him but a crack in the world so narrow he might have stepped across it if he were drunk enough to try.

Twelve years of reading country had taught him that the land kept its secrets the way he kept his: not by hiding, but by being unremarkable. A canyon that swallowed a town did not need walls of black glass or spires like teeth. It needed only to be ordinary—a seam in the salt, a shadow with a lip of stone, the kind of place a man on a fast horse would pass without turning his head, because his head was turned toward the horizon where interesting things were supposed to be.

Ines turned his head.

He had ridden the last two days on almost nothing—water rationed to a cup at noon, the skiff abandoned at the salt-gatherers' causeway when the flats gave out, Maren's mule borrowed with a promise he intended to keep if the world allowed him to keep anything. The mule, whose name was apparently Dog despite being no kind of dog, stood behind him cropping something from stone that no living thing should have found edible, and regarded the canyon with the flat contempt of her kind.

"Dead low tide," Ines said to her, because talking to animals was one of the few habits he'd never managed to break, and because the almanac agreed with him. "You can wait up here and judge me."

Dog judged him.

He went down into the Throat on foot, and the world changed around him the way weather changes—not all at once, but decisively. The heat fell away. The light went from white to a deep underwater green even though there was no water yet, only stone polished smooth as river rock, and the air smelled of brine and old rope and something colder underneath, something mineral and patient. His boots found channels cut into the floor, worn by centuries of tides funneling through the throat twice a day like breath through a sleeping man's lips.

The map said *the wreck lies where the second channel meets the third, forty paces past the drowned bell.* The prospector had drawn the channels with a sure hand, and had labeled them in a cramped script that got shakier near the bottom of the page, as though he'd known even then how much of himself he'd have to spend to get here.

Ines counted paces. At thirty-eight he found the bell.

It hung from an iron bracket bolted to the canyon wall, half its mouth filled with salt crust, and when the wind moved through the Throat it made a sound—not a ring, exactly. More of a hum. A held note, low enough that he felt it in his back teeth rather than heard it. He put his palm against the bronze and the vibration traveled up his arm like a word trying to be remembered.

*Forty paces past the drowned bell,* the map said, and Ines looked down at the pool below the bracket, dark and still, holding the sky upside down in its surface like a coin someone had dropped and decided not to retrieve.

He checked the almanac again, though he'd checked it four times since dawn. Dead low tide gave him perhaps three hours before the sea came back through the Throat with the indifference of all large things. Three hours to find a wreck that had waited sixty years, in water cold enough to stop a man's heart if he stayed too long, alone, with no one above who knew his name.

Which was, he supposed, the way he liked it. He'd spent twelve years making sure no one would come looking. Now the discipline paid its dividend: if he drowned, the desert would simply close over the space where he'd been, neat as water over a stone.

The thought should have been comforting. Standing at the edge of the pool, working the straps of his gear loose, he found it was not.

* * *

The cold hit him like a door.

He'd dived in mountain lakes, in cisterns, once in a flooded quarry outside a town whose name he'd let go of on purpose. Nothing had prepared him for this—the Throat's water was not merely cold but *old*, cold the way stone is cold, cold that had been waiting down here in the dark between tides, keeping the temperature of another century. It closed over his head and the world went green-black and silent, and his body did what bodies do, which is panic politely for three seconds and then remember its training.

He swam down the second channel.

The map had promised ribs, and the map told the truth. They rose out of the gloom like the skeleton of something that had once been enormous and had died on its side—a ship, or what the prospector's generation had called a ship, though Ines could see even through the murk that this thing had been built for cargo and profit and very little else. Barnacles had colonized the timbers in gray continents. A school of small silver fish poured through the frame like spilled coins, reforming, pouring again, indifferent to him.

And there, wedged in the silt where the hull met the canyon wall, a shape that stopped his heart harder than the cold had.

A strongroom door.

Iron, square, banded, with a wheel-lock in its center gone to rust-bloom—and around the wheel, set into the metal in a circle, a ring of numbers. Tides. High water marks, low water marks, the arithmetic of a specific year, and unless you knew which number answered which hour of which day, the wheel would turn forever and open nothing.

Ines hung in the green water before it, lungs beginning their slow complaint, and read the numbers, and understood.

The map was real. The wreck was real. The ledgers of the Coldharbour family—every debt, every drowning, every name erased and every name recorded—lay six feet of iron and sixty years of salt behind that wheel. And the key to the wheel was tattooed on the skin of a dead man buried a hundred miles back under a cairn Ines himself had built, with his own hands, because no one else would.

He laughed underwater, which is a strange thing to do. A burst of bubbles, a sound like a sob played backward.

The prospector had known. Of course he'd known. A man doesn't tattoo tide-tables on his own arm unless he means them to be the only copy—unless he wants whoever comes after him to carry him, literally, back to the door. *Bring me with you,* the ink had said. *I'll open it when we arrive.*

Except the man who arrived was Ines, and Ines had left him under stones in the company of a prayer he didn't believe in, and now the promise stood between him and the door like a toll.

His chest was burning. He kicked for the surface, broke into air and daylight, and hung onto a rib of the dead ship breathing hard while the silver fish scattered and regrouped below him.

"All right," he said aloud, to the canyon, to the bell, to nobody. "Very funny."

Because here was the joke, and it was a good one, the kind the desert told best: the map was worthless without the tables, the tables were a hundred miles east, and between here and there lay the salvage fleet. Six tides behind him when he'd done the arithmetic at the causeway. Five, maybe, now. Veyra's smoke would be crawling along the coast road, and every mile Ines rode back toward the tables was a mile he rode *through* them.

He could stay. Dive again, study the wheel, memorize the numbers, and hope that somewhere in the almanac's dense printed columns the answer hid—that some published table from some harbor master matched the private arithmetic of a family that had drowned a town to hide its debts. He gave that hope about as long as the silver fish gave their formations.

Or he could go back. Ride east, through the fleet's territory, dig up a dead man's arm, and ride west again—with Veyra's ships behind him and the tide-tables burning a hole in his shirt like a coal.

Ines climbed out of the channel and sat on the warm stone with the water streaming off him, and Dog, far above on the rim, was too distant to judge him properly, so he judged himself instead.

Here was the other thing, the thing he didn't want to look at directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at your own motives: some part of him had known about the tables since the night the prospector died. Had known, and had built the cairn anyway, and had ridden west anyway, telling himself he'd figure it out at the door. Because riding west felt like flight, and flight was the only direction he trusted. Going back meant going *toward*—toward the fleet, toward the name, toward the cousin he hadn't seen since they were children standing at different ends of the same funeral.

The bell hummed behind him. The tide would turn in two hours, and the Throat would fill, and the door would keep its secret one more day whether he liked it or not.

"You could walk away," he told himself. It was an old conversation, well-worn as the channels underfoot. "No one would know. That's rather the point of you."

But the prospector had died whispering *Coldharbour* like a man passing a lit match, and Ines had caught it, and you don't catch a fire without carrying it somewhere.

He thought of Maren saying, *They drowned a whole town, they say, just to hide what they owed,* and of his own voice answering *not my family* in a tone that had fooled neither of them.

He thought—briefly, unwillingly—of his parents. Twelve years of not thinking about them, and they still lived in him like tenants who paid no rent.

"Damn it," Ines said, with feeling, and began to climb.

* * *

By the time he reached the rim the sun was high and the Throat below had begun its slow inhale, green water rising through the channels with a sound like a long-held breath finally released. He repacked the mule with hands that were steadier than he deserved, ate half a strip of dried fish, and opened the almanac to the coast road.

East. Through them.

He worked it out the way he worked out everything: distances, water, the fleet's likely speed, the tides. If he pushed hard he could reach the cairn in four days. The fleet, if it hugged the coast, would reach the same stretch of country in five—which meant he'd cross their path somewhere in the middle, somewhere flat and open, somewhere a lone rider on a borrowed mule would stand out like a flame in a cellar.

Unless he wasn't a lone rider anymore. Unless he traded away the ranger's coat, the ranger's badge, the last twelve years of careful invisibility, piece by piece, until he was just a man on a mule, forgettable, unrecorded—which was, he realized with a grim twist of humor, the one skill he'd actually mastered. He'd spent a decade becoming no one. Time to be professional about it.

He swung up onto Dog's back. Far below, the bell hummed its held note through the rising water, keeping time for a door that would wait.

"One more trip," he told her. "Then I swear I'll take up something quiet."

Dog flicked an ear, which from her was a signed contract, and they turned east—back across the nothing, toward the smoke on the horizon, toward the cousin who was already reading his trail like scripture, toward a dead man's arm and the truth buried under a hundred miles of salt.

Behind him, the Throat filled with the sea, patient as a ledger, and waited to be balanced.
