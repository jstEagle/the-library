## 7. Six Tides

The first thing Ines sold was the long rifle.

It hurt. A ranger's rifle is not a tool, it is a biography — every scratch a season, every worn patch of stock a place you slept against it. The trader at Sorrow Wells turned it over twice, whistled at the bore, and offered a price that was an insult to both of them.

"I know what it's worth," Ines said.

"Course you do. That's why I'm offering you this." He held up a bag of coins that jingled like a coward's conscience.

Ines took it. Pride was heavy, and he was traveling light. That was the whole discipline of it — twelve years of learning that everything you carry, you can lose, and everything you can lose, you eventually will. The rifle was just the first thing he could afford to stop being.

The skiff ran the dry canal road south, sail cracking in a wind that smelled of hot iron and old salt. Behind him, on the horizon, the salvage fleet's smoke stood up straight as a held breath. Six tides. He'd done the arithmetic a dozen times and it never came out kinder. The fleet was heavy and slow, but it didn't have to be fast. It only had to be pointed at him.

He stopped at a way-station that was mostly a roof and a rumor, and traded his bedroll for a fresh water skin and a plate of something the cook called stew with a straight face. The cook was a round woman with flour on her forearms and the unbothered air of someone who had outlived three governments.

"You look like a man selling his past," she said, ladling seconds without being asked.

"Piece by piece."

"Smart. Cheaper than carrying it." She squinted at him. "You running from something or toward it?"

"Neither. I'm running *ahead* of something. It's different. You get to feel superior while you do it."

She laughed, and for a moment the road was almost kind. Then, through the way-station's one window, he saw the smoke again — closer, or the wind had shifted, or the world was simply tired of giving him slack. He ate the stew in four bites, thanked her, and went.

* * *

Veyra stood at the rail of the *Kindly Interest* and watched the way-station dwindle behind her lead skiff's report like a woman watching a card game she'd already won.

"Say it again," she told the thief.

The thief — a narrow, damp-eyed man named Pell who had sold the same map twice in one week and would have sold it a third time given the opportunity — said it again. "Ranger type. Grey cloak. Traded for a skiff at the salt landing, headed for the canal road. Paid for nothing, argued about everything."

"Argued about everything," Veyra repeated, and smiled in a way that made Pell take a half-step back and discover a sudden fascination with the rail.

Because that was the detail. Thieves didn't argue. Thieves paid fast and left faster. But a man who argued about everything, who knew exactly what his gear was worth down to the last scratch — that was a man who had counted every possession of his life the way a miser counts coins, because each one was a decision to keep existing somewhere.

She had grown up on that discipline. She'd learned it at her mother's knee, in a rented room in a port town, from a woman who never said the family name above a whisper and flinched when merchants said it too loud. *We are Coldharbours,* her mother had told her, *and that means we are owed, and it means no one will ever pay.* Veyra had spent thirty years deciding which half of that sentence to believe. The ledgers in the Devil's Throat would settle it. The ledgers would settle everything.

"He's good," said her second, a broad woman named Osk who navigated by instinct and insult in equal measure. "Canal road to the Throat in — what, four tides if the wind's mean?"

"Three and a half. He'll cut the salt pans at night." Veyra turned the thief's secondhand description over in her mind like a stone with something underneath it. Grey cloak. Ranger's boots, resoled twice. A man erasing himself in pieces.

"Then we run him down," Osk said. "We've got nine skiffs."

"We've got nine skiffs and one wreck at the bottom of a drowned canyon with a strongroom that opens on a tide-table we don't have." She let that sit. Osk was slow but she was not stupid, and Veyra watched the understanding arrive on her face like weather.

"You think he knows how to open it."

"I think the man who sold him the map died with the tide-tables written on his skin, and I think our ranger friend knows that, and I think he's already done the arithmetic about going back for them." She looked south, toward the Throat, toward the water that rose and fell over her family's sins twice a day like the world breathing over a grave. "Which means he has to go back through us. Or around us. Either way, he does the diving."

"And if he doesn't come back up?"

Veyra considered the question honestly, which was her one extravagance. "Then we've lost a cousin I never met, and we're no worse off than we were."

Osk blinked. "Cousin?"

"Slow the fleet," Veyra said. "Full sail, but lazy about it. Let the wind decide. And put a man on the salt pans — not to catch him. To watch where he goes."

* * *

Ines traded the grey cloak at a canal toll-house, and felt the loss more than he'd expected. Not for the wool — for the color. Grey was the color of not being looked at. What replaced it, for a few coins and a promise he didn't intend to keep, was a faded brown boatman's coat that smelled of fish and made him, he was fairly sure, look like a man who had never once been interesting.

The toll-keeper, a one-eyed old man with a parrot that swore in two dialects, studied him a beat too long.

"Boatman," the toll-keeper said, in the tone of a man reading a menu.

"That's me."

"Mm." He stamped the passage chit. "Boatmen don't walk like they're counting the exits."

Ines paid the toll and walked out counting the exits.

That night, camped in the lee of a salt dike with the fleet's smoke a fat grey finger against the stars, he did the arithmetic again and got a different answer, and the different answer was worse. They weren't gaining. That was the thing he kept circling back to as he lay watching the smoke stand still on the horizon. Nine skiffs, a heavy fleet, a captain who — if she was any good, and the smoke's discipline suggested she was — should have run him down two tides ago.

They were keeping pace with him. Like a hand on the back of his neck, not pushing. Just *resting* there.

He thought about Maren's kitchen, the storm rattling the salt-gatherer's shutters, and the way she'd looked at the map's wax-cloth seal and said, *I know that workmanship,* and how the word Coldharbour had sat in the room between them like an uninvited guest nobody had the nerve to evict. He had denied it. *I'm nobody. I'm a ranger with a stolen name and a rented past.* And she had poured his tea anyway and said, gently, the way you tell a child the dog has died: *Nobody's don't flinch, love.*

He flinched now, alone in the dark, at the thought of who might be behind that smoke. Because there was only one kind of person who would slow a fleet to let a stranger do the dangerous work. Family. Family, or someone who had read his trail the way you read a letter from home — recognizing the handwriting before you've read a word.

He got up, checked the map by feel, and made himself a promise in the old grammar of his invisible years: *no debts, no names, no history.* It didn't take. It hadn't really taken in years. The words had stopped being a discipline somewhere around the dying prospector's camp and started being a cage, and he was beginning to suspect he'd been rattling the bars for a long time without noticing.

"Three tides," he said out loud, to the salt, to the smoke, to whoever was reading him like scripture on the other side of the dark. "Then you can have what's left of me."

The wind changed. The smoke leaned south. So did he.

* * *

Veyra read the morning's report twice and laughed — a short, genuine sound that startled her crew, who had collectively concluded she didn't know how.

"He bought a boatman's coat," she said.

Osk looked at the watcher's scrawled note. "So?"

"So he's stopped being a ranger. He's shedding it. Rifle, cloak, next it'll be the boots." She tapped the note against her palm. "You know what that means, Osk?"

"That he's desperate."

"That he's *Coldharbour*." She said the name aloud for the first time in twenty years, quietly, tasting it like water she'd forgotten she was thirsty for. "We hoard. We count. We sell a piece of ourselves only when the arithmetic demands it, and we hate every coin we get for it, because the coin is proof we needed something." She looked south. "My mother did it. Her brother did it — that's the one I'm following, I'd bet the fleet on it. And I do it, every time I make one of you sign something." She smiled again, and this time the crew looked away. "He'll make the Throat by the fourth tide. We'll make it by the sixth."

"Then he opens it and we take it."

"Then he opens it," Veyra agreed, "and I meet my cousin at last." She turned the stolen map's corner over in her fingers, the wax seal cracked, the drowned canyon drawn in a dead man's careful hand. "Family reunion. I've waited my whole life for one. I intend to enjoy it."

Ahead, the horizon had begun, very faintly, to smell of deep water — and beneath the water, if you knew the stories, waited a strongroom, a set of ledgers, and a tide that would rise whether anyone was ready for it or not.
