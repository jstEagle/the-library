## 8. What the Ledgers Hold

The tide-tables were written on a dead man's arm, and Ines had carried them folded in oilcloth for four days like the world's most terrible letter.

He had cut them free at the grave he'd dug a hundred miles back—two neat rectangles of skin from the prospector's forearm, because the man had asked him to, because *the map's no good without them and I'd rather they went to someone than to worms*. Ines had thought, at the time, that this was the strangest request he would ever honor. He was no longer sure. The week had been generous with strange requests.

Now he sat on the wreck's broken sterncastle with the skin laid flat on his knee, waiting for the fifth tide, and tried not to think about how gently he'd handled it. Like something living. Like something that might still feel.

*Low water, fifth day: two hours past moonrise. The Throat breathes out for ninety minutes. No more.*

Below him, the drowned canyon did its slow breathing. Devil's Throat wasn't a canyon anymore—it was a lung. Twice a day the sea poured in through channels no map fully agreed on, and twice a day it drained back out, leaving the wreck exposed to the waist like a drunk sleeping off a night in a ditch. The locals called the tide cycle breathing. Maren had called it something less polite.

Ines checked the almanac against the tables a third time. They agreed. He checked the sky. It agreed too, which felt like showing off.

"Two hours past moonrise," he told the wreck. "Don't start without me."

The wreck didn't answer, but then, neither had anyone else in twelve years, and he'd managed.

* * *

He went down at slack water with a lantern in a leather sack and the tide-tables strapped inside his shirt, against his own skin, which felt like a conversation he wasn't ready to have.

The first channel was exactly where the prospector's ink said it would be, which unsettled Ines more than if it hadn't been. He had spent twelve years learning that nothing could be trusted—not names, not roads, not the kindness of strangers or the arithmetic of tides—and here was a dead man's arm proving reliable across a hundred miles of nothing. There was a lesson in that. He declined to learn it.

The channel opened into the hull through a gash amidships, and inside, the Devil's Throat stopped being a story.

She had been a barque once, a big one, three-masted, and she had gone down whole. The ribs rose around him like the skeleton of something that had died mid-gallop, draped in weed and crusted white with salt, and the lantern light moved along her bones and made shadows that breathed when he breathed. His own breath echoed back at him off surfaces that hadn't heard a voice in sixty years.

Ines was not a superstitious man. He was a careful one, which in practice looked similar and paid better. But he found himself saying, quietly, "Just me. Nobody you know," and then feeling foolish, and then feeling worse than foolish, because there was every chance somebody down here knew him after all.

The strongroom was aft, below the waterline even at low tide—which was the point of it, the prospector had wheezed, *they built it so only the lowest tides would ever reach the door, and only a man who knew the tables would know which ones*. A door of black iron, banded, uncorroded in a way that iron had no business being. Coldharbour workmanship, Maren had said, running her thumb along the map's edge like she was greeting an old enemy. *You can tell by the hinges. Nobody else ever bothered to make things that lasted this well for reasons this bad.*

Ines set the lantern down. Unstrapped the tide-tables. Read them by touch as much as sight, because the light was bad and his hands were shaking and reading shaking hands is a cooperative effort.

There was a lock, and beside the lock, a row of small brass dials—five of them, each numbered around its rim, each stiff with sixty years of salt. Not a keyhole. A combination. And the numbers, he understood with a lurch, were not written anywhere on the map or the tables. They were the tables. The dials wanted the fifth tide itself—the hour, the minute, the height of the water in hands and fingers, the moon's age. The lock didn't hold a secret. The lock *was* the tide, frozen into brass, and only a man standing here at the right moment, with the right dead man's arm, could tell it what time it believed it was.

"He really did love you people," Ines murmured to the door. "In the way of a man documenting a crime."

The water around his knees was cold and rising by fractions. Ninety minutes. He worked the dials with numb fingers, cursing softly, inventing two new curses and retiring one old favorite, and on the last dial—moon's age, fifth day, waxing gibbous, nineteen—the mechanism gave a click he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

Then a sound like a held breath being released after sixty years, and the door swung inward on hinges that had waited precisely long enough to make their point.

* * *

The air pocket beyond was dry.

That was the first impossible thing. The second was the smell—paper, dust, tallow, and under it something faintly sweet, like a room that had been closed on a summer afternoon and never reopened. The third thing was the shelves.

Ledgers. Dozens of them. Bound in grey leather, spines stamped in gold that had not tarnished, arranged in date order with a thoroughness that could only be described as damning. On a desk beneath the lantern hook lay a single sheet of paper, weighted with a brass seal, addressed in a hand Ines did not recognize and yet somehow did:

*For whoever the water sends.*

He stood in the doorway a long moment, water to his thighs, letting the room be empty of him for one more minute. Then he climbed in, wrung out his sleeves, and lit the lantern properly.

He took the letter first. Cowardice, probably. A letter is shorter than a ledger.

*If you are reading this, then the tables survived, and someone loved us enough to carry a dead man's arm across the salt,* it began, and Ines made a sound that in a better-lit room might have been mistaken for a laugh. *We are the Coldharbours. You will have heard the name, and everything you heard will be true, and none of it will be all of it.*

He read it twice. Then he put it down very carefully, the way you put down something that has just told you your own name, and turned to the ledgers.

* * *

He started at the beginning, because that is what the books demanded and because starting anywhere else felt like cheating.

The early volumes were almost boring. Cargo manifests. Wages. Grain shipments, salt contracts, the patient bookkeeping of a family that owned half a coastline and counted everything twice. Ines read fast—he had taught himself speed-reading out of spite, on the theory that a man who reads quickly leaves fewer traces in any one place—and the years turned under his fingers like water.

Then came the year of the drowning, and the handwriting changed.

Not the hand—the same clerk, the same ink—but the pressure. The letters got smaller. Careful. The kind of careful a man writes when he knows someone else may read this someday, and that someone may be a magistrate, or may be God.

The town had been called Lowmark. Four hundred souls, a harbor, a debt. The Coldharbour debts were in other books—Ines found them, page after page of sums that grew like tumors—and the town of Lowmark sat downstream of those debts in a way the entries never quite said aloud. A dam project, funded. A reservoir survey, commissioned. An engineer's report, *misfiled*. A final entry, dated eleven days before the flood, in that small careful hand:

*The valley will be flooded Tuesday next. The insurance is arranged. The families are to be told of the "accident" after. G. insists the children's school records be among the losses. Paper burns; stone remembers; we must ensure there is very little stone.*

Ines read it four times. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above him, the sea shifted its weight, patient as a creditor.

He kept going, because stopping was not survivable. The flood itself occupied half a page—a line item, *dam failure, total loss, claims pending*—and then the books moved on to salvage rights, to land acquired cheaply from the drowned town's "heirs," to a decade of quiet profit wearing mourning clothes. And there, wedged between a grain contract and a shipping schedule, in a different hand entirely—sharper, slanting left, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page—

His mother's hand.

He knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you've been avoiding for twelve years. She had written him letters, before. Three of them, hidden in a floorboard, burned the night he ran. He had spent twelve years telling himself he remembered them wrong.

*They mean to drown Lowmark and call it weather,* the entry read. *I have copied the true accounts and hidden them where Gregory will never look—in the one place he trusts absolutely, because he trusts it to keep his sins safe. If these pages survive me, then someone must have come looking, and I pray it is you. We did not send you away because we were ashamed of you. We sent you away because everyone whose name appears in these books will eventually need somewhere to hide, and we could not protect you and stay to fight. Being invisible was the inheritance we could actually give you. Forgive us for making it sound like abandonment. It was the opposite.*

*It was the only armor we had left.*

Ines sat down. Not gracefully. His legs simply resigned, and the deck caught him, and he stayed there with the open ledger in his lap while the grief arrived—not gradually, not politely, but all at once, the way a wall arrives when you walk into it—and right behind it, close enough to share a coat, the fury.

Twelve years. Twelve years of no names, no debts, no history. Twelve years of believing he had erased himself, when in fact he had been *erased*—lovingly, deliberately, by two people who had then walked into a flood's aftermath and apparently into silence, leaving him armored in absence and calling it a gift.

And the worst of it—the part that made him laugh, alone, in a dry room under the sea, in a laugh with no humor anywhere in it—was that it had *worked*. He was excellent at invisible. He was the best invisible man on the coast. He had honed their last gift into a discipline and called it freedom.

He wiped his face with a sleeve that was already wet and stood up.

The ledgers went back on the shelf. All of them. He was not taking them—not tonight, not soaked and shaking, not with ninety minutes of air and a fleet six tides behind him. He took his mother's letter, and the clerk's letter, and one volume only—the year of the drowning, because some things you do not leave twice—and he wrapped them in oilcloth and strapped them inside his shirt, against his skin, where the tide-tables had ridden.

At the door he paused, lantern raised, and looked back at the little dry room full of proof.

"I'm coming back," he told it. "Probably. Don't start without me."

Above, faint through sixty feet of stone and water, the sea began to turn.

The fifth tide was ending early.
