## 9. The Throat Closes

*Veyra*

The sixth tide came early, and Veyra knew it before her almanac did.

She had learned to read water the way other people read faces—her grandmother's doing, back when the family name was still spoken in their house like a curse and a prayer at once. The sea pulled back from the Devil's Throat's rim rocks a hand's width sooner than it should have, and the wind swung around out of the southwest with that particular wet weight it carried when it meant business. Somewhere below them, in the drowned throat of the canyon, the ocean was already turning over in its sleep.

"He's been down there since the fifth tide," said Osk, her bosun, squinting through his glass at the canyon mouth. "Skiff's still tied off on the spur rock. No movement."

"Of course there's no movement." Veyra folded her almanac closed. "He found what he came for. Men don't move when they've found what they came for. They sit down in it."

The thief—Dree, he called himself, though she'd heard four other names fall out of him in three days of gentle questioning—shifted his weight at the rail beside her, wrists bound in front of him with salvage line. He had sold her the prospector's secret for a purse and passage, and she had paid both without haggling, because Dree was worth exactly what he'd cost and not a copper more. Still, he had one use left.

"You said he traded gear as he rode," Veyra said. "What did he look like by the end?"

Dree shrugged elaborately for a man whose hands were tied. "Ranger coat gone. Boots gone. By the salt pans he was wearing half a stranger's wardrobe and looking pleased about it. Man trades like he's shedding a skin."

"He is." She turned back to the water. "Twelve years of skin."

Osk glanced at her. "You're sure it's him? Your cousin?"

"I'm sure it's an Ines," she said. "Whether he answers to that name anymore is between him and the desert."

Below, the tide lipped at the first channel rocks. The Devil's Throat opened twice a day and kept its secrets the rest of the time, and Veyra had spent six tides and one burned dock getting here six hours after her cousin instead of six days. That was close enough to feel like fate and far enough to feel like an insult.

"Prepare the dive lines," she said. "And bring me the lamp oil. All of it."

Osk frowned. "All of it, Captain? That's—"

"All of it. If I'm going down into my family's grave, I intend to arrive lit properly."

* * *

*Ines*

The strongroom smelled of iron and old paper and, faintly, of the sea's long patience.

Ines sat cross-legged on the raised deck plate—the only dry ground in a room the size of a modest chapel—with the ledgers stacked around him in their wax-sealed cases, and he did not touch them.

That was the discipline. Twelve years of practice: don't touch, don't keep, don't sign. The ledgers were right there, close enough that he could hear the wax creak as the hull flexed around him, and every instinct he owned told him to open them, read them, *know*. His parents were in those pages. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming—not a fact yet, just a pressure behind the ears.

"Not yet," he told the room, because talking to himself was one habit twelve years hadn't cured.

The air pocket held. The strongroom sat high enough in the wreck's stern that even at low tide its upper third stayed dry, breathing through a cracked vent pipe that whistled softly when the swell ran past—a thin, mournful note, like the wreck clearing its throat. Ines had decided to think of it as company.

He had the tide-tables now, inked in faded blue across a strip of prospector's skin he'd dug up outside Maren's station, wrapped in oilcloth where the old man had sworn he'd hidden them. Maren had wept a little, handing it over. Ines had felt nothing he could name, which he'd learned to treat as a warning sign.

The tables had opened the strongroom's double lock at the fifth tide exactly as promised. The map had not lied. The dead man had not lied. Only the living lied, in Ines's experience, and usually about inheritance.

He was reaching for the first case anyway—discipline was a muscle, but muscles tire—when the vent pipe stopped whistling.

He went still. Listened. The note came back, thinner than before, then stopped again, longer this time.

The tide was turning. Early.

He checked the tables against his memory of the sky and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since dawn: the sixth tide would flood the channel in under two hours, and the strongroom's air pocket would drown with it. Whatever he chose to do with the ledgers, he would have to choose soon, and carry the choice up through forty feet of darkening green water.

Somewhere above, faintly, through the hull, came the sound he'd been listening for all day: anchor chain, running out.

"She's here," he said to the room.

The room, being honest, said nothing.

* * *

*Veyra*

She dove the first channel herself, because sending anyone else into your own family's tomb seemed rude.

The water was cold and going green, visibility shrinking by the minute as the flood tide dragged silt off the flats. She swam the ribs of the wreck with a diver's lamp in one hand and a knife in the other, past cargo hooks furred with barnacle, past a doorway that had once been somebody's parlor, and thought, absurdly, of her grandmother's stories: *the Coldharbours built ships like promises, girl. Big, beautiful, and built to be broken.*

The strongroom hatch stood open. Of course it did. Her cousin had beaten her to the door of everything she'd ever wanted, and he'd done it barefoot, probably, trading his boots along the way like some ascetic saint of the salt flats.

She surfaced inside the air pocket, spat her regulator, and found him sitting among the sealed cases with a lamp turned low, looking—there was no other word for it—*comfortable*. Like a man in his own kitchen.

"Ines Coldharbour," she said.

He didn't startle. She'd known he wouldn't. "That's a heavy name to carry down a dive line," he said. "I mostly go by nothing."

"I know. I've been reading your nothing for six tides. It's very well written." She hooked an arm over the deck plate and hauled herself up beside him, water sheeting off her suit. Up close he looked older than thirty-four ought to look and younger than twelve years alone should allow, which she supposed was the whole trick of him. "Hello, cousin."

"We're not that."

"My grandmother was Halda Coldharbour. Your father's sister. Which makes us—" she counted on wet fingers, enjoying herself, "—first cousins, once removed from politeness."

"That's not how the phrase works."

"It's how it works in this family. Nothing about us works the way it should. You've noticed."

He almost smiled. She saw him catch it and put it away, the way you'd put away something breakable. Twelve years of practice, she thought. And underneath it, plain as a brand under paint: *he's glad I'm here.* God help us both.

"The ledgers," she said.

His eyes went to the cases. Something moved behind them—grief, fury, both arriving together like bad relatives—and then the shutters came down, smooth as a hatch closing.

"You haven't opened them," she said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because once I do, I can't un-know it." He looked at her then, fully, and she felt the strange vertigo of meeting your own eyes in someone else's face. "And neither can you. That's the trap, Veyra. It's not the reading. It's the owning."

"Owning is the point." She reached for the nearest case, and he didn't stop her, which frightened her more than a fight would have. "Listen to me. Our family drowned a town to hide their debts. A town, Ines. With people in it. Every record says it never happened—every record except these. Whoever holds these holds the truth. Not gold. Truth. Do you understand what that's worth? We could make them *say* it. Judges, magistrates, the houses that sneered at the name—we could walk into any hall on the coast and be untouchable, because we'd know what everyone did, and everyone would know we know."

"And then what?" he asked quietly.

"Then we'd be safe."

"For how long? Until someone else finds a ledger about us?" He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't end, Veyra. That's the inheritance. It's not a fortune or a weapon. It's a debt that pays itself forward. Our parents' parents bought silence with a town, and the interest has been compounding ever since, and if we take these pages we're just the next signature on the loan."

"They hid *you*," she said, and watched him flinch. "I read enough of the trail to guess. You weren't abandoned, cousin. Someone loved you enough to erase you. Don't you want to know why? Don't you want it *in writing*, so no one can ever take it back?"

For a moment—one long, swelling moment, while the vent pipe stayed silent and the water below them rose by inches—he wanted it so badly she could see it happen, the whole future unrolling: the two of them armed with truth, feared, safe, *together*, the orphaned halves of a broken name made whole and terrible.

Then he exhaled, and it was over.

"Yes," he said. "I want it more than I've ever wanted anything. That's why I can't keep it."

The vent pipe gave a single, dying whistle.

* * *

*Ines*

The water came into the strongroom the way bad news does—all at once, from below, no negotiation.

It surged over the lower deck plates and found the lamp oil Veyra had ordered brought down, sloshing it across the deck in a spreading rainbow film, and Ines understood in one cold flash that she had planned for fire the way he had planned for escape, and that they were, appallingly, the same person.

"Veyra," he said. "The lamp."

She looked at the oil. Looked at the ledgers. Looked at him.

"You'll kill us both," she said. "The pocket's flooding. We have minutes, and you want to spend them on arson."

"On *renunciation*," he said. "There's a difference. Ask any monk."

"There are no monks in this family!"

"There were," he said. "They got erased too."

The water was at their knees now, black and rising fast, and the air pocket shrank overhead like a held breath running out. Ines pulled the first case free of the stack, waded two steps to the lamp, and broke the wax seal with his thumb. Inside, the ledger lay fat and patient, its pages foxed brown, its columns ruled in a dead accountant's careful hand. He did not read it. He had read enough of the world to know what it said.

"I'm sorry," he told it. Sincerely. It wasn't the pages' fault they were true.

"Ines—" Veyra's voice cracked on the second syllable, and for the first time since the dive she sounded exactly her age, exactly his cousin, exactly a woman watching her inheritance burn. "There has to be another way. We could seal it back up. Let the sea keep it. Nobody holds it, nobody uses it—"

"And the next prospector with a map digs it out in twenty years, and the next fleet, and the next us." He struck the flint. "The sea keeps nothing, Veyra. It just stores things until we're ready to hurt ourselves with them again."

The flame caught the oil with a soft, greedy *whump*, and fire walked across the deck toward the cases, orange and absolutely indifferent, and Ines stood in the rising water and watched twelve years of not-looking come to an end in light.

Veyra grabbed his arm. "The air," she said, and he saw it—the pocket collapsing, the flame eating the last good breath, the water chest-high and climbing. "We go *now* or we stay forever."

He took one last look at the burning ledgers—his parents in there somewhere, hidden, loved, erased, and now, finally, *free*—and let her pull him under.

The channel was chaos, green-black and roaring with the flood tide, and they swam it side by side, cousins, strangers, the only two people alive who knew what had burned in the Devil's Throat and would never be able to prove it. His lungs burned. His eyes burned. Somewhere behind them the strongroom filled with fire and sea at once, and the last of the Coldharbour truth went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

They broke the surface in the open channel, gasping, and the sky was enormous and indifferent and going gold at the edges, and Ines laughed—actually laughed, ragged and disbelieving, treading water beside a woman who had tried to recruit him into empire an hour ago.

"What," Veyra choked, spitting seawater, "is *funny*."

"We're orphans of the same crime," he said, "and we just drowned our inheritance together. If that's not family, I don't know what is."

She stared at him. Then, to his lasting astonishment, she laughed too—short, unwilling, real—and the tide carried them both, side by side, out of the throat of the canyon and toward a shore where, for the first time in twelve years, Ines Coldharbour had nowhere to run and, for the first time in his life, no reason to.
