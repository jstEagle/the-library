## 10. The Memory He Kept

The sea gave them back grudgingly.

Ines crawled out of the surf at the mouth of the Devil's Throat with salt in his teeth and his boots full of the canyon, and behind him Veyra came ashore the way a drowned thing comes ashore—on her hands and knees, coughing half the Throat out of her lungs, her captain's coat gone to the water along with whatever dignity she'd carried into it.

They lay on the shingle for a long time without speaking. The tide was coming back in behind them, patient as a debt collector, filling the channel they'd nearly died in. Somewhere up the coast, Ines could see the salvage fleet's masts, and he watched them one by one turn away from the shore like birds deciding against landing.

"They're leaving," Veyra rasped.

"Nothing left to salvage." He rolled onto his back. The sky was doing its evening trick, going gold at the edges, and it occurred to him that he had almost drowned twice in two days and had still noticed the sky. That was new. That was probably important.

"You burned them." She said it flatly, not a question, not yet an accusation. "Forty years of ledgers. Proof of everything. You burned them in an air pocket while the water came up our necks."

"I did."

"I want to be furious about that."

"Take your time," Ines said. "I've found fury keeps well. Like salt fish."

She laughed—actually laughed, a short ugly bark with seawater still in it—and then she was quiet again, and they both listened to the tide fill the canyon behind them, sealing the strongroom, sealing the ashes, sealing the whole drowned argument forever.

* * *

They walked north along the coast for three days because there was nothing else to do and because walking is what their family had always done when the truth got too heavy to sit under. Veyra's hands were cut from the rocks. Ines's left ear had stopped working somewhere around the fifth tide and never fully apologized. They shared water without discussing it, which is more than most cousins manage.

On the third evening she finally said it.

"When I was eight," she said, "they taught us in the village school that Coldharbour was a curse word. Not even a name—a curse. *May the Coldharbours drown you.* Children said it like a game. And I'd go home and my mother would close all the shutters, and I knew—I *knew*—that we were the curse. That I was made out of it." She turned a stone over in her fingers. "I wanted the ledgers so I could put them down. So I could say: here, look, this is what we are, this is the whole of it, weigh it and be done."

"And now?"

"Now they're at the bottom of a canyon, burned, and I'm still made out of it." She looked at him. Her eyes were her grandfather's eyes, and Ines hated that he knew that. "You could have given them to me. You'd have been rid of it clean."

"No," Ines said. "I'd have just been rid of it *loudly*. There's a difference."

"That's not wisdom. That's cowardice with better posture."

"It can be both," he said. "Most things are."

Veyra threw the stone into the sea with surprising violence for a woman who could barely lift her arms, and then she sat down next to him on the cold sand, shoulder almost touching his, and after a while she said, quieter: "Tell me about them. Your parents. What you found."

So he told her. Not everything—the ledgers were ash and belonged to the eels now—but the shape of it. His mother's handwriting in the margins, arguing the numbers down. His father's name signed to a protest that had gotten them both erased, struck from every record but the one nobody was ever meant to read. *He was not abandoned; he was hidden,* and saying it out loud to the one person alive who shared the blood felt less like confession and more like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing.

Veyra listened all the way through without interrupting, which for her was practically sainthood.

"They hid you," she said at last. "And nobody hid me. I got the name instead." She stood, brushed sand off, and looked down the darkening beach toward the scattered lights of the fleet's shore camp. "I'm going to go tell them it's over. Some of them will want to kill me. Most of them will just want their wages."

"What will you tell them about the wreck?"

"The truth," she said, and smiled with all the warmth of a drawn knife. "That it's flooded, sealed, and cursed. *May the Coldharbours drown you.* Let the children have it back. It makes a better story than a ledger anyway." She paused. "Ledgers lie by being complete. Stories get to choose."

She walked away down the beach toward the fires, straight-backed, coatless, dripping, and Ines watched her go and understood that he would probably never see her again, and that this was somehow the least lonely ending available to either of them.

* * *

Maren's station sat where the salt flats began, a low stone house with a drying yard and a wind-bent fence and a guest book.

The guest book was Maren's single concession to civilization, and she defended it like a fortress. Travelers passing through signed it or they didn't drink her tea. In twelve years of careful living, Ines had passed through twice and signed *J. Marrow, drover*, and *T. Fell, surveyor*, and each time Maren had looked at him over the pot with the expression of a woman filing something away for later.

Now he stood at her table with the pen in his hand and the book open, and Maren sat across from him darning a sock and pretending very hard not to watch.

"You don't have to," she said. "It's only tea."

"I know."

"Half these names are lies anyway. Half my own are." She pulled the thread tight. "A book full of false names is still a book. It remembers that somebody came."

Ines looked at the page. Twelve years of miles. No debts, no names, no history—he had built that life plank by plank and called it freedom right up until the day he'd discovered it was a cage with excellent ventilation. The thief he'd tracked had stolen nothing worth chasing. The map had cost a man his last breath. The ledgers were ash. All that remained, all that had ever truly remained, was the story—and a story needs somewhere to stand.

He wrote:

*I. Coldharbour.*

Then, because it seemed dishonest to stop there, he added: *ranger. Orphan. Grandson of arsonists and heroes, depending on the page.* Then, after a moment: *cousin to a pirate, who says hello.*

Maren read it upside down, which took her a while, and when she finished she didn't say anything dramatic. She just got up, refilled his cup without being asked, and said, "Room's yours through the wet season if you want it. The roof leaks over the bed but nowhere else, so sleep accordingly."

He stayed the season.

It was, by any honest measure, the strangest thing he had done in twelve years—stranger than diving a drowned canyon, stranger than burning forty years of his family's sins in an air pocket while the sea closed its throat. Staying. Learning where Maren kept the good tea versus the tea she gave to salesmen. Fixing the wind-bent fence and discovering the wind always won, which Maren said was the fence's way of staying humble. Watching the salt gatherers come and go, and signing each of them in himself once Maren delegated the duty, and noticing how they'd hesitate at the pen—the same hesitation he'd felt—and how some of them lied and some of them didn't, and how the book held them all the same.

One evening a young salt-gatherer with a sunburnt nose asked him why he'd stayed.

Ines considered the question seriously, because the boy deserved that much.

"I spent twelve years making sure no record anywhere said I existed," he said. "Turns out that's not the same as being free. It's just being unread." He tapped the guest book. "This is the only ledger I keep now. One entry per person, no debts recorded, no crimes. Just: *you were here.*"

"That's not a ledger," the boy said. "That's just remembering people."

"Yes," Ines said. "I've found that's the version worth keeping."

The wet season ended the way seasons do, all at once and without announcement. One morning the wind changed, the air went dry and bright, and Ines woke knowing it was time—not running-from time, the old kind, but moving-toward time, which he'd almost forgotten existed. He packed light. He'd gotten good at packing light; it was the one skill from the invisible years worth keeping.

Maren walked him to the edge of the flats, where the world went white and shimmering and the road dissolved into suggestion.

"You'll come back through?" she said. Not *will you come back*. Through. As if he were a current she expected, a tide with a table of his own.

"Through," he agreed. "Sign me in ahead of time. Save me the pen."

She snorted and turned back toward her house, and Ines shouldered his pack and stepped out onto the salt.

He carried no proof. The ledgers were coral and ash; the tide-tables had gone into the sea with the prospector's skin; even the wax-cloth map had finally come apart in his pocket sometime during the second week at Maren's, its lines fading to ghosts. What he carried instead was heavier and weighed nothing: his mother's marginal hand arguing the numbers down. His father's signature on a protest that mattered precisely because it failed. A cousin walking away down a beach, promising to remember differently. A name in a guest book, in his own writing, that no one had forced him to give.

Twelve years of miles, and it had taken one season in a leaky-roofed stone house to learn the difference between erasing yourself and choosing yourself.

The salt flats stretched out ahead, white as an unwritten page. Ines Coldharbour walked out onto them, unhurried, visible to anyone who cared to look, and began—to his own mild surprise, and with something that was almost a song under his breath—to hum.

Behind him, in a stone house with a wind-bent fence, a woman opened a guest book to its newest entry and read it once more before turning the page:

*I. Coldharbour. He was here. He'll be back through.*

### Coda

Far south, in a harbor town that smelled of tar and bad decisions, Veyra Coldharbour sat in the back room of a chandlery with her fleet's remaining officers arrayed before her, waiting on her word.

The word was: disperse. Wages paid, grudges noted, the Devil's Throat declared dead water by order of the captain. Half of them thought she'd lost her mind. The other half thought she'd found it, and those were the ones who shook her hand on the way out.

When the room emptied, she took out a cheap notebook—the kind sold to schoolchildren—and on the first page wrote, in a captain's blunt hand:

*What I know of my family, being the true account, kept by choice:*

Underneath, she wrote the first entry. Not a debt. Not a crime. Just a name, and beside it three words:

*Ines. He chose.*

She capped the ink, looked out the window at the sea that had tried to drown them both, and allowed herself—one time only, never to be repeated—the smallest smile.

There were, after all, other wrecks. And now she knew exactly how her cousin liked his stories told.
