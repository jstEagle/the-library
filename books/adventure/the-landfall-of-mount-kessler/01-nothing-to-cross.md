## 1. Nothing to Cross

The thief made his mistake at dawn, same as they all did.

Silas Pemberton watched him from the crest of a dune that wasn't really a dune — just a long shoulder of hardpan the wind had dressed in sand — and marked how he'd stopped to water his horse in the open. No cover for a quarter mile in any direction. He'd unsaddled her, too, which meant he thought he'd outrun pursuit, and that was the second mistake. The first had been stealing a bay mare with a white sock on her near hind from a man in Corrin's Ferry who paid Silas half up front and would pay the other half never, because Silas would take this one in trade instead of coin, the way she took everything now. Trade kept things simple. Coin invited conversation.

The flats ran flat to the horizon in every direction, cracked clay gone silver with alkali, and the sun came up over them like something being born angry. Heat shimmered off the ground by midmorning and turned the world to water that wasn't there. A greenhorn looked out at all that nothing and saw nothing. Silas saw a road. She saw every place a man could put his feet and every place he couldn't, and she read the thief's line of travel the way Odile used to read survey maps — quickly, without seeming to think about it, the knowledge sitting somewhere below thought.

*Odile.* She put the name down before it could pick anything up. That was the trick of it. You learned to set things down fast, like hot iron.

She gave the thief an hour. Let him saddle up and ride on, let him build his confidence back on the flat white miles where confidence went to die. Then she swung down into the basin and followed at a walk, leading her own horse through the broken ground where the crust would hold a walking horse but not a trotting one, saving the mare's legs and her own, conserving what the day would ask of her. The thief rode the high line, straight as a fence wire toward the pass at Redwater, because Redwater was the nearest town and towns were where thieves went to become men who had always owned horses. He didn't know about the low route through the saltbush. Nobody knew about it unless they'd crossed these flats with a dying man on a litter and no time to grieve, which was a specific education, and Silas had received it.

She didn't think about that either.

By noon the heat stood up off the ground in visible columns and the thief's lead had shrunk from an hour to forty minutes, because he was pushing too hard and his stolen mare was pushing back. Good horse, that bay. Smart. She'd have dumped him in a ditch by now if horses were allowed opinions. Silas ate in the saddle — jerky, a handful of meal stirred into water — and drank small, and watched the shimmer ahead resolve by degrees into a figure and then into a man, and felt the old machinery engage, smooth and cold and reliable. Track. Close. Take. It was work that asked nothing of her except the part of herself she was willing to spend.

That was why she never stopped moving. She'd worked it out years ago, in the bad first winter, lying awake in a line shack with wind worrying the roof: stillness invites memory. Motion starves it. A body in motion has no time to sit in a chair by a window and watch the light change and feel the afternoon open up under it like a wound. There was always another track, another contract, another stretch of empty country wanting crossing, and empty country was honest company. It didn't know her name. It didn't say *you should have*, or *if only*, or the worst one, the one that wore a dead man's voice: *you could have stayed.*

The flats ended at a dry wash choked with tamarisk, and the thief had done what thieves do — cut into the cover to rest his horse in the shade, thinking shade was safety. Silas left her mare ground-tied in the open where the thief would see her coming, because half of taking a man was letting him understand there was nowhere to go, and walked the last two hundred yards on foot, quiet as weather.

He heard her at fifty. Saw her at thirty. Went for his gun at twenty and found her looking down the barrel of hers at him, which is a very educational distance.

"Easy," she said.

The thief froze with his hand hovering. He was younger than she'd guessed from the tracks — twenty-two, maybe, sunburned to the color of brick dust, with the fine-boned hands of somebody raised on a farm and starving off it. His eyes went from her face to her gun to the badge-shaped absence on her chest. Rangers didn't wear badges out here. Out here the authority was the rifle and the certainty behind it.

"You're the one been following me." Not a question. His voice cracked on it anyway.

"Since Corrin's Ferry."

"I needed the horse." He said it like it was a defense. Like need was a door out.

"They mostly do," Silas said. "Hands where I can see them. Slow."

He complied, and she took his pistol from his belt without touching him, stepped back, and considered him. Up close he smelled of fear sweat and cheap soap and horse. The bay mare stood hipshot in the tamarisk shade, watching Silas with one dark eye, and Silas felt the animal recognize her the way animals recognized people who had spent their lives being recognized by animals.

"Name?" she said.

"Why?"

"So I can write it down when I turn you in. Or don't. Makes no difference to me."

"Hollis," he said. "Tom Hollis." And then, because fear fills silence the way water fills a hole: "My sister's sick. Back past the Ferry. Fever three weeks running and the doctor wants eight dollars a visit and I had nothing, so I—" He stopped himself, hearing how it sounded, and his jaw set. "I was going to sell her in Redwater. The mare. She's worth thirty if she's worth a dollar."

"She's worth sixty," Silas said, "and you're asking me to believe a story I've heard forty times, most of it lies."

"Is some of it true?"

The question caught her sideways. Most men doubled down. This one stood in the shade with his hands shaking and asked, honestly, whether any piece of him might be worth believing, and Silas looked at him for a long moment and thought about the difference between a thief and a desperate man, which was mostly luck and mostly paperwork.

"Sit down," she said. "Don't touch anything."

She watered both horses from her own skins, which was against every rule she had, and shared her food, which was worse, and while the boy ate like a starving thing — confirming the story better than words could — she sat on a tamarisk root with her rifle across her knees and let the heat press down and tried not to notice that she was being still. Stillness invites memory. She could feel it circling already, patient as a buzzard.

Three years ago, almost to the month, she had sat exactly like this on exactly this kind of root, in a camp above a river she hadn't been able to look at since, sharing food with people she'd have died for and one of whom she should have watched closer. The fire had been small because fuel was scarce up there. Tam had been arguing with Odile about load weights. Bo — no. Not Bo. Bo didn't exist yet; that was the strange arithmetic of grief, that a man could be alive in the world and dead in your ledger at once. Her mentor had been there, though. Her mentor was always there. He'd been mending a pack strap by the fire, humming, and he'd looked up at her and said something easy and ordinary — *you're getting good at this, girl* — and she'd carried those six words for three years like a stone in the boot, because they were the last ones he ever said to her, and because he'd been wrong. She wasn't good at this. She was good at moving, and she'd mistaken the one for the other, right up until the river took him and the expedition scattered like a flushed covey and Maren Kessler stood on the bank with ash on her face and a story in her mouth that Silas had been too gut-shot to question.

She set it down. Set it down. Hot iron.

The boy was watching her. "You went somewhere," he said.

"Nowhere. Eat."

They sat while the shadows shortened, and Silas weighed him the way she weighed crossings — depth, current, bottom. Tom Hollis, twenty-two, maybe a liar, maybe not, certainly a fool, possibly a brother. The law said take him in. The law in Corrin's Ferry was a justice of the peace who'd hire out his docket to whoever paid the fee, and a horse thief with no money would get a rope and a short trial, and somewhere past the Ferry a fevered sister would hear about it and grieve twice.

Silas had buried enough people. She had no appetite for arranging more burials, even at a distance, even for strangers. Especially for strangers. Strangers were safe to be kind to. Kindness spent on strangers cost nothing that mattered, and it bought a few hours' relief from the thing she wouldn't name, which was that she had stopped being able to tell whether she kept moving because she was looking for something or because she was making sure she'd never find it.

"Here's how it goes," she said, standing. "You ride back to Corrin's Ferry today. You return the mare to the man whose name will be in the saddlebag — don't look surprised, I read the brand registry, the horse is flagged. You apologize. You offer to work off whatever he says you owe, and if he's the man I think he is, he'll take labor over law, because he's got fences down and no sons. You do that, and I lose your description. Can't recall a thing about you. Terrible memory."

The boy stared at her. "You're letting me go."

"I'm failing to catch you. There's a difference. Ask anybody."

"Why?"

And there it was, the question she wouldn't answer, arriving right on schedule. Silas looked east, where the flats ran out against the blue wall of the far ranges, and beyond them, though no map she owned admitted it, the highlands where the Kessler River came down out of country no surveyor returned from. Somewhere up there was a source. Somewhere up there was a grave. The two facts had been braided together so long she couldn't pull them apart anymore, and she had built three years of motion on top of the braid like planking over a hole in a floor.

"Because somebody let me go once," she said, "and I've been trying to earn it since. Didn't work. But the debt's real." She swung up onto her mare. "Ride. And Hollis — next time somebody's sick, steal from a rich man. They can afford the loss and they never chase."

He was still staring when she wheeled away, and she didn't look back, because looking back was a habit that grew.

* * *

She crossed the flats south through the afternoon, following the low route out of pure habit, and the land did what it always did, which was nothing, magnificently. Alkali glitter. Saltbush going gray-gold. A hawk riding a thermal with its wings fixed, spending nothing, going nowhere, and Silas envied it with an intensity she found embarrassing.

Toward evening the country began to lift into low mesas, and she made camp in the lee of one, a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, coffee bitter enough to strip paint. She was on her second cup when she heard the horse.

One horse, coming on at a pace that said money rather than urgency, from the northeast — from Vell's Landing, which was the nearest thing to civilization and the last place she wanted visitors. She banked the fire and waited in the dark with her rifle loose in her hands, and after a while a rider resolved against the last of the light: a courier, government-saddle, mail case strapped behind, the whole rig gleaming with the particular shine of equipment maintained by someone else's budget.

"Silas Pemberton?" the courier called, before he was even fully stopped. "Looking for a Silas Pemberton, ranger, out of Corrin's Ferry district?"

"You found her ghost. What do you want?"

"Got a letter for you. Signed delivery." He fumbled the mail case open and held out an envelope heavy as a brick, cream-colored, sealed with wax, absurd out here among the saltbush like a piano in a sheep camp. "Been chasing you two weeks. Man at the Ferry said you'd be working the flats."

Silas took it without dismounting. The wax was unbroken, deep green, stamped with a K inside a circle of water. She knew the seal. Everyone on the river knew the seal. It had been on crates and manifests and, once, memorably, on a coffin shipped downstream at government expense.

Kessler.

She turned the envelope over twice, as if the back might explain the front. The courier shifted in his saddle, waiting, wanting a receipt, wanting to be gone before full dark. The hawk was long gone. The stars were coming out in their cold millions, indifferent as ledgers.

"You want me to sign," she said.

"Please."

She signed. He touched his hat and was swallowed by the dark, and then it was just her and the fire's red eye and the letter sitting in her hand like something live.

She should burn it. That was the correct move, the move the version of herself she presented to the world would make — feed it to the coals unread, ride north at first light, stay moving. The name on the seal was a wound dressed in wax. Whatever Maren Kessler wanted, it would cost more than triple whatever she was offering, because Maren didn't make offers; she made investments, and investments expected returns measured in other people.

Silas fed the corner of the envelope to the fire.

The wax bubbled. The cream paper browned, curled, caught — and she watched it burn with a satisfaction that lasted four seconds and then curdled, because underneath the burning she could already read the first line through the char, black letters blooming out of the flame like fish rising:

*Mrs. Pemberton — Forgive the intrusion. I find myself in need of a ranger who knows the upper Kessler, and there is exactly one—*

Her hand moved before her mind voted. She snatched the letter out of the fire, beat it against her knee, slapped the burning corner into the dirt and ground it, and sat there in the sudden dark holding a half-charred document and hating herself with a thoroughness that felt almost like rest.

Stillness invites memory. So did firelight. So, apparently, did that name, that handwriting, that phrase — *the upper Kessler* — which hit her like cold water and left her sitting very still indeed, listening to her own pulse, reading the surviving lines by ember-light with the ashes of her good judgment still warm on her boot.

The letter began again, further down, where the flames hadn't reached:

*—who knows the upper Kessler. Triple your standard rate, paid half in advance. Monsoon comes early this year, the almanacs all agree. We would depart within the fortnight.*

*I will be at Vell's Landing until then. I will be waiting.*

It was signed simply, *M.K.*, in ink gone slightly smudged, as if the writer's hand had hesitated at the end — though Silas doubted it. Maren Kessler didn't hesitate. Maren Kessler calculated, and a smudge was probably strategy.

Silas sat by the ruined fire a long time, turning the letter over, the way she'd turned the envelope, hoping the back would explain the front. It didn't. It never did.

Triple rate. The upper Kessler. *I will be waiting.*

She told herself she'd sleep on it, and knew, lying back with her hat over her eyes and the letter weighted under a stone beside her — not burned, not thrown away, kept — that she had already decided. She just hadn't admitted it yet, and admitting things had never been her strong suit. That was how you ended up carrying stones in your boots for three years.

North, then. Downriver to Vell's Landing, where ghosts kept a town the way rust keeps iron.

She'd stop moving just long enough to regret it.
