## 2. The Rival's Letter

The courier came at dusk, which told Silas everything before he opened his mouth. Nobody honest traveled the flats after sundown, and nobody with good news paid a rider to cross forty miles of nothing to find a woman who made her living not being found.

She watched him come from the shade of the waystation's lean-to, a speck of dust trailing a lathered horse across the hardpan. She'd been mending a saddle strap that didn't need mending, because her hands wanted work and her mind wanted silence, and neither was getting what it wanted.

The rider reined in, swung down, and stood a moment working feeling back into his legs. Young. Maybe twenty. Dust to the eyebrows, water skin long empty by the way he eyed her canteen hanging on its peg.

"Silas Pemberton?"

"Depends who's asking."

He fumbled a leather dispatch case off his shoulder and held it out like it might bite him. "Got a letter for you. Signed receipt required."

"Who pays couriers to chase rangers across the flats?"

"The lady did." He swallowed. "Paid double, actually. Said you'd try to refuse it."

That earned a short laugh out of her, dry as the ground underfoot. *Said you'd try to refuse it.* Maren Kessler had always known her moves three steps ahead. It had been the thing Silas liked least about her, back when there were things she'd liked about her at all.

"I don't take letters."

"It's not really optional, ma'am. I get the signature or I ride back and tell her I failed, and then she pays me triple to try again tomorrow."

Silas looked at him a long moment. He shifted his weight, too tired to be scared of her, which she found almost charming. The sun was going down behind him, dragging its heat off the land, and somewhere out on the flats a jackrabbit started its evening business among the rocks.

"Water first," she said, and unhooked the canteen, and that was how Maren Kessler got her foot in the door — through a thirsty boy and Silas's own soft habits.

While he drank she took the case, turned it over. Good leather. Wax seal pressed with a crest she recognized and wished she didn't: a river bar bent into a K, stamped deep enough to feel with a thumb. The paper inside was heavy stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than the courier would make in a week.

"You know what's in it?" she asked.

"No, ma'am. Don't want to, either. Lady scares me."

"Good instinct," Silas said. "Keep it."

She signed his book against the lean-to post, watched him remount, and sat back down with the letter in her lap while his hoofbeats faded east toward Vell's Landing. Then she just looked at it. For a long time. Long enough that the light went from gold to copper to that flat gray that isn't quite dark.

*Kessler.*

Even thinking the name was like pressing a thumb into a bruise she'd spent six years pretending wasn't there.

* * *

There was a river named after her family, and that should have been the first warning anyone needed about Maren Kessler — a woman so hungry to own things she'd put her name on moving water, as if water could be owned, as if anything that moved could.

Silas had learned that lesson the hard way, up that same river, six years ago.

Eleven had gone up. Four came down. And the one who mattered most hadn't come down at all — Aldous Wren, her mentor, the man who taught her to read country the way other people read scripture, who'd pulled her out of a gutter town at fifteen and given her a trade instead of a temper. His body was never recovered. The river kept him. The river, Silas sometimes thought, kept whatever Maren fed it.

The official report said flash flood. Unseasonal rain, a narrow gorge, bad luck. Tam had signed it. Odile had signed it. Silas had been half-dead with fever by then, carried downriver on a litter, and by the time she could stand upright and ask questions, everyone had already agreed on the story, and agreeing on a story is a kind of cement. You can break it, but it takes the whole wall down with it.

So she'd let it stand. That was the coward's arithmetic she'd lived with ever since: four graves bought with one lie, and her silence as the mortar.

She struck a match off her boot heel and lit the letter without reading it.

It caught fast, heavy paper burning slow and bright, and she held it by one corner over an old tin plate and watched Maren Kessler's handwriting curl and blacken. Whatever was in there — money, flattery, the word *please* dressed up in expensive ink — it turned to ash along with the seal, the crest, the name. She stirred the ashes with a stick until they were uniform gray, then set the plate on the ground and went to see about dinner.

She got as far as the coffee pot.

Because here was the thing about burning a letter unread: it doesn't burn the words. The words are already in you, waiting. *Triple pay,* the courier had said, and *before monsoon season*, and *up the Kessler River*, and every one of those phrases was sitting in her chest now like swallowed stones. Triple pay meant desperation. Before monsoon meant a deadline, and deadlines meant risk, and risk meant Maren was doing something she didn't want examined. And the river —

The river meant Aldous.

She stood at the fire with the pot in her hand and hated herself with a clean, familiar hatred, because she already knew how this ended. She'd known since the courier crested the rise. Six years of motion, of taking jobs that kept her horizon empty and her nights full of miles, all of it built on one rule: *never go back up the Kessler.* And now the only person alive with the power to make her break that rule had written her a letter, and Silas had burned it, and burning it changed nothing, because the hook was set the moment she heard the word *Kessler*, and hooks don't care whether you read the line.

She put the pot down. Went back to the plate of ash.

Kneeling in the dirt like a woman praying to something she didn't believe in, she tipped the ash gently onto a flattened piece of bark, tilting and tapping, letting the lighter fragments blow away on the evening wind while the heavier char stayed put. It was slow work. Her hands shook, and she let them, because no one was watching and shaking hands are honest hands.

Fragments emerged. Half a sentence in Maren's slanted hand:

*...no one else alive who knows that country...*

And lower, a scrap with the wax still clinging:

*...triple your rate, and I will pay it without haggling...*

And one more, small, almost lost:

*...before the monsoon makes landfall. Come to Vell's Landing. — M.K.*

No threat. No mention of the past. No apology, either — she wouldn't have apologized if the sky fell on her. Just business, laid out plain, the way Maren did everything: as if the intervening years were a ledger already balanced.

Silas sat back on her heels with the charred scraps arranged on the bark and felt the trap close around her with a click she could almost hear. Because that was the genius of it. No one else alive who knew that country. True, damn her. Every survey crew in the territory would balk at the upper Kessler in monsoon season, and the ones who wouldn't balk would die, and Maren knew exactly one person reckless enough and haunted enough to say yes.

*Come to Vell's Landing.*

Vell's Landing. Where the old crew had scattered from, six years ago, carrying their separate shames in separate directions. Where Odile had gone back to engineering bridges she'd never finish. Where Tam had taken the magistrate's clerk job nobody believed he wanted. Where Bo —

Well. She didn't know where Bo was. She didn't even know who Bo was yet. But the name Vell's Landing pulled at her like current, and she understood, sitting there in the dark with a dead man's ghost standing at her shoulder and a burned letter's bones in her hands, that Maren hadn't sent her an offer at all.

She'd sent her a map home.

Silas fed the scraps back into the fire, one at a time, watching each one flare and die. This time she let them go. The decision was already made; the paper was irrelevant. It had always been irrelevant. Maren Kessler could have scratched the offer in sand and it would have worked just the same, because the offer was never the bait.

The wound was the bait. And wounds, unlike letters, don't burn.

She banked the fire, lay down under the lean-to with her boots on, and stared up at the stars doing their indifferent wheeling. Somewhere south, the highlands were stacking up clouds she couldn't see yet. Somewhere north, a barge was loading gear for a run up a river that had already killed four people and gotten away with it.

And Silas Pemberton, who had spent six years outrunning the memory of that river, lay perfectly still in the dark and understood, with the calm of a woman watching her own house catch fire, that in the morning she would saddle up and ride to Vell's Landing — and that whatever waited for her there, she was riding toward it not for Maren's money, and not for the truth, but because some part of her had never stopped believing that the river owed her a body, and she meant to collect.

Sleep took her eventually, dreamless and thin as the last of the ash.

At first light she was on the trail north, and the flats gave way to scrub, and the scrub to the first green shoulders of the hill country, and by the third day she could smell the river before she saw it — mud and willow and something mineral underneath, cold stone breathing through the water. Vell's Landing rose out of the willows at dusk on the fourth day: a straggle of rooftops, a magistrate's flag, barge smoke standing straight in the still air.

And tied at the main dock, riding low with cargo, flew a pennant she knew better than her own heartbeat.

A river bar bent into a K.

Silas reined in at the top of the landing road and sat her horse, looking down at the barge, at the figures moving on its deck, and felt six years collapse into the space of one long breath.

One of the figures looked up. Even at this distance, even across four days of dust and six years of silence, she knew the shape of that smile.

Maren raised one hand — not a wave, Silas thought. A summons.
