## 3. Crew of Ghosts

Vell's Landing smelled of tar and river rot and the particular desperation of a town that existed only because two governments couldn't agree on whose problem it was. Silas came down off the flats at dusk with the letter's ash still under her fingernails — she'd scrubbed twice at the waystation outside Corren's Ford and it hadn't mattered, as if the words had soaked through skin into blood.

She told herself she was here for supplies. Grain for the horse, cartridges, salt pork. That was all.

The Landing didn't believe her either. Towns like this had a way of looking at a person and seeing the truth before the person did. The ferryman took her coin and studied her face too long. "You'll be wanting the Salt House," he said. Not a question. "Kessler's people been asking after rangers all week."

"I'm not for hire."

"Didn't say you were." He poled off across the brown water, leaving her on the near bank with a horse that needed shoeing and a lie that was already coming apart in her hands.

* * *

The Salt House was the kind of tavern that served as courthouse, chapel, and morgue depending on the hour. Silas pushed through its door expecting noise and got it: dice, argument, a fiddler losing a war against the crowd. What she didn't expect was the voice cutting through all of it, precise and dry as a surveyor's line.

"—the load-bearing calculation is not negotiable, Tam, because gravity is not negotiable, and if you'd ever once read past the first page of my figures—"

Silas stopped so hard the door swung back and hit her shoulder.

Odile Marchetti stood at the corner table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, one boot up on a bench, arguing over a set of drawings weighted down with beer glasses. Her hair was going grey at the temples now, and there was a new scar along her forearm, but everything else was exactly as Silas remembered: the impatience, the certainty, the way she held a pencil like a weapon she'd already decided to use.

And beside her, hunched over the drawings with a compass in his hand and ink stains to both elbows, sat Tam.

Older. Thinner. A beard he hadn't earned trying to cover a jaw she'd have known blindfolded. But when he looked up to defend himself against Odile's arithmetic, his eyes found hers over the crowd, and the compass slipped out of his fingers and rolled off the table, and the whole room seemed to go quiet around the small sound of it hitting the boards.

"Silas," Tam said.

The name carried. Odile turned. For three full seconds nobody at that table breathed, and Silas stood in the doorway of the Salt House feeling every mile she'd put between herself and these people collapse at once, five years folding up like a map.

"You're supposed to be dead," Odile said finally. "Or worse. You're supposed to be *content* somewhere."

"I could say the same."

"I'm never content. It's a professional requirement." But her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and she covered it by sweeping the drawings off the table and into her satchel with more force than paper deserved.

Tam came around the table slowly, like approaching a spooked animal. Up close Silas could see what the years had done to him — the tremor in his left hand he kept curling into a fist, the way he'd stopped meeting anyone's eyes above chin height. "We heard you were tracking out of the southern flats," he said. "Horse thieves and claim jumpers."

"It pays."

"So did we." He said it gently, which was worse. "Silas—"

"Don't." She stepped back. The room was watching now; she could feel it, forty sets of eyes pretending not to. "I came for grain and cartridges. I'll be gone by morning."

"The Kessler job," Odile said behind her.

Silas closed her eyes. Of course they knew. Maren Kessler would have made sure they knew, would have sent the same courier to each of them with the same triple-pay offer, because Maren never did anything without understanding exactly how it would land. "There is no Kessler job."

"There's a barge loading at the north dock," Odile said. "Provisions for twenty. Survey instruments, blasting permits, enough chain and cable to outfit a bridge crew. I've seen the manifest myself, because I was asked to sign on as engineer, and I told her no, and then I came here anyway to see who else she'd asked." She planted both fists on the table. "She asked everyone, Silas. Everyone who walked away from that river. Which means she wants us specifically, which means whatever's up that gorge needs people who've been there, which means—"

"Which means it's a trap or an apology," Silas said, "and Maren Kessler has never apologized in her life."

"Then it's a trap." Odile shrugged, furious and unbothered by her own fury. "So let's walk into it awake instead of asleep. Because I'll tell you what I know, ranger: I've spent five years building locks and levees for men who think water is a commodity, and every night I dream about that gorge, and I am *tired* of dreaming about it."

The fiddler gave up. In the sudden quiet, Silas heard the river itself, low and constant under the town's foundations, running north toward the highlands. Toward the source. Toward the place where Halven Oakes had died with the expedition's maps in his pack and Silas half a canyon away, close enough to hear the rockfall start and too far to do anything but listen.

"I burned the letter," she said.

Tam blinked. "What?"

"Maren's offer. I burned it at Corren's Ford." She held up her hand, showed them the fingernails. "And then I dug it out of the fire pit and read it twice, and rode here, and told myself it was about grain." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "So whatever you think of me, understand I think less."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then a voice from the shadowed end of the bar, young and unhurried:

"She's telling the truth about the burning. There's ash in the creases of her knuckles. Paper ash goes grey; wood ash goes white." A man unfolded himself from a stool — big through the shoulders, sun-darkened, maybe thirty, with Halven Oakes's exact heavy brow sitting above eyes that were nothing like Halven's at all. Halven's eyes had been quick and amused. This man's were careful, watchful, the eyes of someone who'd learned early that being noticed was dangerous. "You scrubbed twice. River sand doesn't get paper ash out of skin. Only time does."

Silas stared at him. "Who are you?"

"Bo." He crossed the room without hurry and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that she could see the family resemblance wasn't just the brow — it was the set of the shoulders, the way he stood square to the world like a man braced to carry something. "Bo Oakes. Halven was my mother's brother."

The name went through her like a spear through wet canvas. Around her the tavern kept moving — dice, laughter, the ferryman's bell on the crossing — but Silas heard none of it. "Halven didn't have family. He said so. Every year on the trail, he said so."

"He said that to the crew." Bo's voice stayed level. "He said it to me too, once, the year before your expedition. He wrote to my mother every month from the field. I've got eleven years of his letters in a oilskin packet in my bunk. He talked about you in them." Something moved behind the careful eyes. "He talked about all of you. Like family."

"Letters can be forged."

"They can." Bo didn't flinch. "You want to see them anyway?"

Yes, she wanted to say. God help her, yes. Instead she looked at Tam, at Odile, at the drawings and the compass and the beer glasses holding down five years of unfinished business, and felt the trap close around her with a click so gentle she might almost have missed it.

Maren hadn't sent couriers. Maren had sent a ghost.

* * *

They ended up on the north dock after midnight, because Odile refused to talk business indoors ("walls have ears and tavern walls have mouths") and because Tam, once he started walking, seemed unable to stop until he reached water. The barge sat low and heavy against its moorings, lanterns hooded, a gangway watched by a broad-shouldered man with a rifle slung casual as a bedroll. Dray, someone would later tell her the man's name was. At the time Silas just marked him: weight forward, eyes doing sweeps, hired muscle with a professional's patience.

Beyond the barge, the Kessler River ran black and silver under a half moon, sliding past the pilings with that patient, patient sound. Five years since she'd stood beside it. It sounded exactly the same. That was the unforgivable thing about rivers — they never changed, no matter what they took.

"She offered me triple pay," Tam said. He sat on a mooring bollard with his knees drawn up, the tremor in his hand quieted by the night cold, or by exhaustion, or by the relief of saying it out loud at last. "Triple, plus a pardon provision. I've got debts, Silas. Debts with teeth."

"What kind of debts?"

"The kind you get when you drink to forget a thing and the drinking costs more than the forgetting." He smiled without any humor in it. "I signed on yesterday. Then I sat in the Salt House all evening hoping you'd walk in, because I don't trust myself to do this alone, and I figured if anyone could look at that woman and see straight through her, it's you."

"And what do you see?"

Tam was quiet a long moment. "I see someone who used to be our friend," he said. "That's the part that scares me."

Odile stood at the dock rail with her arms folded, staring at the barge the way she stared at a flawed design — cataloguing failures. "Here's my position," she announced, to the river as much as to them. "I will not work for Maren Kessler. I will not take her money, I will not eat her food, and I will not pretend the first expedition didn't happen because she finds it inconvenient. If she wants me on that boat, she can ride in the cargo hold with the blasting powder."

"She won't accept terms," Silas said.

"Then she doesn't get an engineer, and her barge tears its hull out on the second riffle above the narrows, and she learns why you pay for competence." Odile turned. The moonlight caught the grey at her temples and the old burn scar on her jaw, and for a second Silas saw her as she must have been at twenty-two, drawing bridge plans in the dirt while Halven laughed at her tolerances. "Unless," Odile said, quieter, "you're not actually here to hire us. Unless you're here to tell us to stay home. Is that it? Did you come all this way to save us from ourselves?"

"Yes," Silas said. "No. I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." She looked down at the water. "Every instinct I have says turn around. Every year of training says a river that killed nine people hasn't gotten safer. And I burned that letter because I knew if I read it twice I'd come here, and I came here, and I hate that I came here, and I need—" The word stuck. She pried it loose. "I need you. Both of you. Not for Maren. For the river. Because if somebody's going up that gorge, I would rather it be people who loved Halven than people who love money, and I don't know anyone left who loved him except the three of us." She glanced at Bo, standing apart at the end of the dock, silent as furniture. "Four."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, patient, patient.

"Say it properly," Odile said at last.

"Odile—"

"No. Five years ago you walked off without goodbye because grief is easier alone, fine, I've done it too, we've all done it. But if you're asking me to board that woman's barge and run her gauntlet, then you ask me like a person and not like a ranger deputizing a posse." Her voice roughened. "Say you need us. Out loud. To our faces. I've waited five years to hear it and I'll wait five more."

The old Silas — the one who'd ridden out of this country with a dead mentor's compass and a vow of permanent motion — would have mounted her horse right then. Stillness invites memory. Memory invites grief. Grief invites the question of whether any of it was survivable, and Silas Pemberton had built a whole life on never letting that question finish forming.

But Bo was watching her with Halven's brow, and Tam was watching her with Halven's hope, and Odile was watching her with something harder than either — the look of a woman who'd been left once and had decided, somewhere in those five years, that she would rather break than be broken again.

"I need you," Silas said. It came out rough, unsanded, true. "I need your engineering and your navigation and your stubbornness, and I need you to tell me when I'm wrong, because I've been wrong for five years and there's been no one to say so. I need you like I needed you before, and I never said it then either, and Halven died without hearing it, and I will not make that mistake twice." She swallowed. "Please."

The word hung over the black water.

Odile exhaled, long and slow, all the fight going out of her at once like air from a punctured hull. "Well," she said. "Finally." She pushed off the rail and stuck out her hand, and Silas took it, and Odile's grip was as strong and unforgiving as ever. "But I keep my terms. No working for Maren — we work for you, and Maren rides along as cargo. And the first time she lies to us—"

"She won't get a second lie," Silas said.

"See, that's the job description." Odile almost smiled. Almost.

Tam slid off the bollard and hugged her before she could brace for it, quick and fierce, his trembling hand pressed flat between her shoulder blades. "I've got conditions too," he murmured. "When this is over — whatever's at the source, whatever Maren wants — we talk about it. All of it. On the record. No more rivers full of secrets."

"Agreed."

"I mean it, Silas. Secrets are how the first one killed us."

"I know," she said, and meant it, and did not yet know how much she would regret agreeing.

Bo approached last. He didn't offer a handshake; he just stood there in the moonlight, big and careful, and produced from inside his coat a single letter, soft at the folds from years of handling. "This one's from the spring before your expedition," he said. "Last page mentions you. I figure you should read it before you decide whether I'm real."

Silas took it. She didn't read it there — some things you take with you, like ammunition, like water. She folded it into her coat over her heart, where the burned letter's ghost still lived.

"We board at dawn," she said. "All of us. Nobody signs anything Maren puts in front of them, nobody drinks anything she pours, and nobody walks anywhere on that barge alone." She looked downriver, toward the darkening highlands where the Kessler narrowed into gorge country and the water ran cold and fast over whatever it was Maren Kessler would dynamite a mountain to own. "If we're going back to that river, we go back as a crew."

"A crew of ghosts," Tam said quietly.

"No." Silas turned for the stairs, the letter warm against her chest. "Ghosts haunt. We're going back as family — the kind that forms against its will." She paused at the top of the dock steps. "Dawn. Don't be late. And Bo?"

He looked up.

"If your uncle's letters say what I think they say, you and I are going to have a long conversation before Maren's barge clears the narrows."

Bo held her gaze, and for the first time the careful mask slipped, and underneath it Silas saw something that looked terribly like grief — and terribly like resolve.

"I was counting on it," he said.
