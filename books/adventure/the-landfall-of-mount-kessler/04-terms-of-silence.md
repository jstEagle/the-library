## 4. Terms of Silence

Maren Kessler had learned long ago that the most dangerous room in the world was a small one with four people in it who all remembered the same night differently.

She stood at the rail of the *Vera K.* and watched them come down the plank from Vell's Landing, one by one, like a confession assembling itself. Odile Fenn first, of course — Odile was always first, punctuality being the last virtue of the guilt-ridden. Then Tam Wray, thin as a heron's leg, carrying his instruments in a canvas roll he'd owned since before the first expedition, before the river, before everything. Then a stranger Maren hadn't expected: broad-shouldered, sunburned, with a jaw she recognized the way you recognize a house you grew up in. Bo Calloway. The nephew. She'd done her reading. She'd known he was coming, and she'd hated him for it three weeks before he ever set foot on her deck.

And last, Silas Pemberton, moving like a woman who had never once in her life arrived anywhere on purpose.

Maren smiled. She'd practiced the smile against the cabin mirror that morning, and the one before that, until it sat on her face the way a saddle sits on a horse — fitted, familiar, bearing weight without complaint.

"Welcome aboard the *Vera K*," she said. "She's named for my mother, who drowned in a river far less interesting than the one we're about to climb. I consider it a family joke."

Nobody laughed. Good. Laughter would have meant they'd already decided to like her, and Maren needed them undecided a while longer. Undecided people listened.

"Below decks," she said. "There's brandy and there's coffee, and I know which one Odile will take."

"Brandy," said Odile. "It's not even nine."

"It's nine somewhere I've been," Maren said, and led them down.

* * *

The main cabin of the *Vera K.* was walnut and brass, maps under glass, a table long enough to lay out a body. Maren had designed it that way deliberately, years ago, though she'd never had to lay out a body on it. Yet. She poured the brandy herself — four glasses, one coffee, the coffee black and bitter enough to strip varnish, because Odile took it that way and always had, and remembering that was a kind of currency Maren had spent a decade accumulating.

They sat. They arranged themselves the way old grief arranges furniture: Odile with her back to the wall, Tam with his hands around the coffee cup like it was warm blood, Silas standing, because Silas would stand, Silas would always stand, at the edge of every room she'd ever been paid to leave.

"And you're the nephew," Maren said to Bo.

"Bo Calloway."

"I know who your uncle was."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why *you think* you're here." Maren set down the brandy decanter with a click she'd timed in her head. "You're here because a dead man's name opens doors, and you've been carrying it around like a crowbar. I don't blame you. I carried it longer." She let that land. She watched it land — saw it hit Bo in the chest and Silas in the spine, both of them straightening slightly, and thought: *there, that's the seam, that's where they're joined, the dead man runs through both of them like a vein of ore.* "But on my boat, the name stays ashore. All of it."

Silas's eyes came up. Gray-green, river-colored, the eyes of a woman who'd spent fifteen years looking at water from the wrong end of a rifle. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning terms." Maren took her seat at the head of the table, the seat with the map of the Kessler gorge under glass in front of her, the river running silver and blue from the highlands down to Vell's Landing, its source a blank space the cartographers had filled with a hatched pattern that meant, in the language of maps, *nobody who drew this ever went there.* "I'll give them to you plainly, because I know how this crew likes its terms. Plain, signed, and paid. One condition, and it's the only one I'll ever put on you."

She let the silence do a lap of the table. She'd learned silence from her mother, who'd learned it from the river, which had learned it from the mountains: the thing that wins is the thing that waits.

"No one speaks of the first expedition," Maren said. "Not the names. Not the night. Not the river, not the camp, not what happened to any of it. Not in the cabin, not on deck, not in your sleep where the others can hear. We are a survey crew going up an unmapped river before monsoon season to chart a source that will make every one of you wealthy enough to never work for anyone again. That is the expedition. There was no other one. There is no other one. If the past comes up this river, it goes overboard at the first rapids, and I won't dive after it."

The brandy sat untouched in front of Odile. Tam looked at his coffee. Bo looked at Silas.

And Silas looked at Maren, and Maren felt the old sensation, the one she'd felt across a card table in Ketterdam and across a negotiating table in the capital and once, memorably, across the sights of a rifle held by a man she'd later have to pay to forget: the sensation of being *read*. Silas Pemberton didn't talk much. That was the trouble with her. She listened the way a weir listens to a river — patiently, with a structure underneath, and everything that came through her came through changed.

"You're paying triple," Silas said. "Triple buys silence. It doesn't buy forgetting."

"I'm not asking you to forget." Maren spread her hands, and let the hands be honest even though nothing above them was. "I'm asking you to be professionals. There's a difference, and the difference is what I'm paying for." She turned to the map, tapped the blank space at the river's head. "There is water up there. Enough water to make the Kessler navigable to the highlands, enough to turn Vell's Landing from a town that smells like fish into a city that smells like money. I have the charter. I have the capital. What I don't have is the only crew that's ever seen this river's teeth and lived. That's you. That's the whole pitch, Silas. I'm not buying your forgiveness. I couldn't afford it. I'm buying your competence, and I'm paying triple because I know what it costs you to sell."

Silence again. Maren counted it — three seconds, four. Odile's jaw worked. Tam's thumb traced the rim of his cup. Bo sat very still, the way his uncle used to sit, and Maren had to look away from that, had to make herself look away, because there was a version of this room where she told the truth, where she said *I know what happened to your uncle, I know better than any of you, I was there, I did it* — and that version of the room was a version where she ended up in front of a magistrate or in front of a gun, and Maren Kessler had not survived to forty-four by choosing the rooms where she ended up in front of things.

"Well," said Odile at last, dry as a riverbed in August. "You've gotten better at speeches. You used to just buy people."

"I still do," Maren said pleasantly. "The speeches are free."

* * *

They signed in the end. Of course they signed. Maren had counted the debts before she ever sent the letter — Odile's engineering practice drowning in a lawsuit she'd lose, Tam's mapmaker's license suspended for a survey he hadn't actually falsified, though the court hadn't believed him, and Silas, Silas with nothing but a name and a reputation and the particular poverty of people who own only what they can carry. Triple pay wasn't a wage. It was a hook, and Maren had tied the fly herself, feather by feather, and she watched it go into their mouths one by one and felt nothing about it that she would have called guilt. Guilt was for people with alternatives.

She watched them go up on deck afterward, the four of them, and she stayed below with the brandy and the maps, and she listened to the sound of the boat — the creak of the hull, the slap of water against the *Vera K.*'s sides, the river already talking, already telling them things. Rivers always talked. That was the problem with rivers. They had no discretion whatsoever.

The documents were in the cabin safe, behind the false back, behind the real back, in the compartment that had been built by a carpenter in the capital who was now, as far as Maren knew, comfortably retired in a country with no extradition. She didn't open the safe. She didn't need to. She knew what was in it the way you know what's in a wound: the first expedition's true survey, the one Tam had written and Maren had ordered changed; the assay reports, the ones with the ore figures, the figures that would have started a stampede if they'd ever seen daylight; the letters between Maren and the mining consortium, dated *before* the first expedition, dated before the disaster, dated in an order that told any reader with eyes exactly what she'd known and when she'd known it and what she'd done to keep it.

And the last document. The one with her uncle's handwriting on it. The guide's. The one she'd taken off his body herself, because a dead man's map was worth more than a dead man's grave, and because leaving it would have meant leaving proof.

Maren poured a second brandy and didn't drink it. She stood at the little porthole and watched the four of them on deck — Odile already down on her knees at the stern wheel housing, checking the linkage, hands moving with the blind fluency of long marriage to machines; Tam up at the bow with his instruments, shooting the sun like the sun owed him money; Bo hauling line, big and easy and wrong, so wrong, because he had Calloway's hands and Calloway's laugh and he didn't know — couldn't know — what his uncle had died of, and Maren was going to keep it that way if it cost her the whole river.

And Silas at the rail, looking upstream. Always upstream. Maren had hired her because she was the best, but she'd known she could hire her because Silas Pemberton was a woman running from a fire who could be convinced the road out led through it. All Maren had to do was keep the road looking like a road.

The condition would hold. It had to hold. People were sentimental about the dead but they were *practical* about money — she'd built a career on the gap between those two things, and the gap was wide, and the Kessler was long, and by the time the crew understood what was actually at the river's source, they'd be too deep in the gorge, too deep in the pay, too deep in each other's debts to turn around.

That was the theory. Maren had built her life on theories, and most of them had held, and the ones that hadn't she'd paid for in ways that were now locked in a safe behind a false back, in documents she could not let anyone read, in a cabin on a boat that was, as of this morning, crewed by the only four people alive with both the skill and the reason to open it.

She heard footsteps on the companionway ladder. Light ones. Tam's.

Maren closed the porthole cover, put the smile back on like a tool she was picking up off a bench, and turned to greet him with the decanter already in her hand — and saw, in the last second before the door opened, that Tam's canvas roll of instruments was not the shape it had been this morning, and that one corner of it, down near the bottom, was square, and dry, and did not belong to any instrument she'd ever paid for.

"Mr. Wray," she said warmly. "More coffee?"

"No, ma'am." Tam stood in the doorway, heron-thin, eyes down, and Maren watched him decide — watched the decision move through him like a fish under ice. "Just came to say the current's right for an early start. If we leave on the morning tide, we make the gorge narrows by the second day."

"Then we leave on the morning tide," Maren said.

He nodded and went, and Maren stood alone in her walnut-and-brass cabin with the safe behind the false wall and the river talking under the hull, and she made herself a promise she'd made before, on worse nights, in worse rooms: whatever Tam Wray was carrying in that canvas roll, it would be at the bottom of the Kessler before it was ever in anyone else's hands.

She finished the brandy. Upstream, the mountains were already waiting, patient as documents, and the monsoon was three hundred miles out at sea, coming for all of them like a debt.
