## 5. Upstream

The gorge country announced itself the way grief does — not all at once, but in a narrowing.

For two days the Kessler had run wide and brown and lazy, and the crew had found its rhythm on Maren's barge: Odile at the engine with her sleeves rolled and her hands black to the wrist, Tam up on the pilothouse roof calling depths off his charts like a man reading scripture he half-believed, Bo wherever work was heaviest without ever being asked. Even Dray and Maren's other hired muscle kept to themselves at the stern, playing cards with the flat-eyed patience of men paid by the day. The barge was old but sound, a flat-bottomed freighter Maren had named *Prospect*, which told Silas everything about why it existed.

Silas stood at the bow on the third morning and watched the walls come down around them.

The flats ended where the river met the first spine of the highlands, and the water went from brown to green to a hard, glassy teal in the space of an hour. Redstone cliffs rose on either side, close enough that she could have thrown a line across to the far bank, and the river between them quickened like something waking. The sound changed too. Out on the flats the Kessler had been silent, a broad animal breathing. In here it began to talk — low at first, then insistent, then loud.

"Half a mile to the narrows," Tam called down, not looking up from his chart. "Then the first real water."

"You've run it?" Odile asked from below, voice pitched over the engine's clatter.

"I've read about it." A beat. "By a man who died doing it."

"Wonderful," said Odile.

Silas didn't smile. She was watching the water ahead, reading it the way she'd taught herself to read tracks on hardpan — every ripple a sentence, every standing wave a warning. The narrows were visible now as a bright seam of white between the cliffs, and above the roar she could hear the deeper note underneath it, the one that meant the river dropped away somewhere out of sight.

She'd run this stretch once before. Eleven years ago, with a different crew, on a different boat, with Hal Joubert laughing at the wheel because he laughed at everything that should have frightened him. She remembered the narrows as a long scream and then a pool of impossible quiet afterward, everyone soaked and shouting and alive, and Hal saying, *See? The river only wants you to pay attention.*

She hadn't paid attention when it mattered later. That was the arithmetic she carried.

"Silas." Maren's voice behind her, silk over gravel. She came to stand at the rail, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and Silas caught the smell of her — tobacco and some expensive soap that had no business on a river. "You remember this part."

"Some of it."

"The narrows haven't gotten gentler." Maren's eyes were on the white water, and for a moment — just a moment — the charm dropped and something older showed underneath. Something tired. "I want you to take the wheel through here. I'll pay the crew to stand ready, but I want your hands on it."

"It's your boat."

"It's my money. It's your river." Maren turned and looked at her, and there it was again, that strange intimacy she wielded like a blade held by the flat. "Nobody reads water like you, Pemberton. Not even Hal did."

The name landed exactly where Maren knew it would land. Silas looked back at the narrows rather than let her see what crossed her face.

"Aft," she called. "Everyone aft and hold on. Odile — engine steady, no more, no less. Tam, call me the drops."

* * *

The narrows took four minutes and cost them nothing, which was a kind of miracle, and miracles on rivers are always bought with attention.

Silas drove the *Prospect* through standing waves tall enough to hide the cliffs, through a chute no wider than the barge itself where the redstone walls scraped close enough to leave orange paint on both sides, through a drop that lifted her stomach into her throat and set it down again like a careful hand. Tam's voice never wavered — *drop, hard left, hold, hold, NOW right* — and Odile kept the engine singing exactly the note Silas needed, and the crew held the rails and shouted wordless things at the water, and for four minutes they were not six strangers and three hired guns but a single animal moving through the throat of the mountain.

Then the pool. The impossible quiet. The river spreading wide and green between the cliffs, mist hanging in the air, everyone drenched and gasping and — she could hear it building behind her — starting to laugh.

"That," Odile said, climbing up beside her, wringing out her shirt with two efficient twists, "was the most fun I've had since the last time it nearly killed me."

"You're welcome," Silas said.

Tam came down from the roof grinning, and Bo came aft from the bow where he'd ridden the whole thing crouched like a catamount, watching the water ahead of her instead of behind, calling rocks before Tam could find them on the chart. He was soaked through, lean and sunburnt, and there was a look on his face Silas recognized from mirrors — the look of a man who has just discovered the exact size of his own hunger.

"You've run whitewater before," she said.

"Lakes." He pushed wet hair out of his eyes. "Rivers are just lakes with opinions."

Tam snorted. Odile almost smiled. And Silas felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel since Vell's Landing, which was the slow, dangerous warmth of a crew becoming a crew — the way separate bodies start to move like one body, the way trust arrives uninvited and sets up housekeeping in your chest.

She looked away from Bo and checked the lines on the cargo instead. Trust was how people died out here. She knew that better than anyone aboard.

They made camp that night on a gravel bar in the lee of a cliff, a hundred yards of pale stone between the water and the wall, and Odile built a fire out of driftwood that burned green and sputtering, and Tam produced a bottle of plum brandy from somewhere inside his coat with the shameless magic of a lifelong smuggler. Even Dray and the muscle drifted closer to the firelight, drawn by warmth the way moths are drawn by anything brighter than themselves.

Bo sat across the fire from Silas, drying his boots, and told a story about Hal Joubert teaching him to swim in an irrigation canal when he was nine — how Hal had simply picked him up and thrown him in and stood there laughing while he thrashed, then waded in after him when the thrashing turned real.

"He said fear is just respect that hasn't learned manners yet," Bo said. "He talked like that. Drove my aunt crazy."

Silas said nothing. She listened to the river talking in the dark and let the ache sit where it sat.

"You hate hearing about him," Bo said quietly. Not accusing. Just seeing.

"No," Silas said. "I hate that I need to hear about him. There's a difference."

Bo nodded slowly, and she saw him decide not to push, and she was grateful, and she hated being grateful, and she drank Tam's terrible brandy and let the fire do its work.

* * *

The second day of the gorge country was worse, and the third worse than that.

The river steepened. The pools between rapids shortened until the water ran white for miles at a stretch, and the *Prospect* groaned through chutes that Odile swore were narrower than the drawings suggested, and twice they grounded on gravel shoals that hadn't been on any chart and had to be winched off by main strength, everyone hip-deep in freezing current, shoulders against the hull, Bo's voice counting the rhythm the way Hal used to count it — *and pull, and pull* — until Silas made him stop, or wanted to, and couldn't bring herself to say it aloud.

On the fourth afternoon they came to the place the maps called the Scissors, where the river split around a fang of black rock and rejoined itself in a chaos of crosscurrents below.

"Left channel," Tam said, flat on his belly on the roof, chart weighted with stones. "Right channel's a trap — recirculating hydraulics, whole lower third's a keeper hole. Left is fast but clean."

"Left it is," Silas said, and took the wheel.

What the chart hadn't shown — what no chart could have shown — was the sweeper. A cottonwood, dead and enormous, wedged across the left channel at the waterline, its trunk just under the surface, its branches combing the current downstream like the fingers of a drowned giant. Silas saw it a heartbeat too late to do anything but choose which kind of disaster to have.

"Hard right!" she screamed, and threw the wheel over, and the *Prospect* swung its nose toward the rock fang instead, and for one suspended moment they were aimed at stone on one side and wood on the other and the river underneath them was neither, just chaos, just the water deciding.

The hull kissed the fang. A grinding shudder ran the length of the barge, paint and planks screaming, and then the current took the stern and slewed them sideways and Tam — who had come down off the roof and was standing at the rail with a boathook for exactly this reason — went over the side.

No splash big enough. No shout at all. One moment he was there, and the next the river had him, and Silas saw his head come up once in the foam below the fang, arms windmilling, mouth open on a cry the water ate.

Everything after that happened in pieces.

She remembers slamming the wheel hard over to swing the stern toward him. She remembers Odile killing the engine without being told, because Odile understood that a propeller turning near a man in the water is a decision you can't take back. She remembers Dray, of all people, lunging for a coil of rope. And she remembers Bo — already moving, already kicking off his boots, already going over the rail with a line tied around his waist before anyone had finished understanding what was happening.

He swam the way he did everything else, like a man who'd decided fear was just respect learning its manners. The current dragged Tam toward the keeper hole below the fang, the recirculating water that would hold a body and turn it and turn it, and Bo angled across the current instead of fighting it, letting the river carry him to the intercept point the way Silas would have cut a trail across a slope to head off a rider below. He caught Tam's collar just as the swirl took hold. For three seconds — Silas counted them, she'd count them the rest of her life — both of them vanished under the foam.

Then the line went taut, and Dray and the muscle hauled, and Bo surfaced with one arm locked around Tam's chest, and together they came out of the water like something being born, gasping and streaming and alive, onto the gravel below the fang.

Tam lay on his back and coughed half the Kessler River out of his lungs. Bo knelt beside him, dripping, shaking now that it was over, the shaking of a man whose body had spent its courage and was presenting the bill.

"You," Tam wheezed, "are insane."

"Hal's nephew," Bo managed. "It's genetic."

That night nobody asked Bo to prove anything. When the fire was lit and the brandy came out, someone — Odile, Silas thought, though she'd have denied it — simply handed him the cup first. Tam sat pressed close to the flames with a blanket around his shoulders, and every few minutes he looked at Bo and shook his head slightly, the way a man looks at a debt he doesn't know how to carry.

Silas stood apart, at the edge of the firelight, and watched her crew become a family in real time, and felt the two truths she carried grind against each other like the barge against the fang: that these people were beginning to trust each other, and that one person aboard had already lied to all of them about why they were here.

* * *

She woke past midnight to the smell of smoke that wasn't the fire.

The camp slept in the shape of habit — Odile curled by the embers, Tam still wrapped in his blanket, Bo on watch rotation in an hour, Dray and the muscle off at the stern where Maren's money kept them. Maren's bedroll lay empty.

Silas rose without sound and followed the smell along the base of the cliff, past a shoulder of fallen rock, to a hollow where the firelight couldn't reach. Moonlight came down the crack in the canyon wall in a thin silver bar, and in it she saw Maren kneeling over a small flame of her own — a candle, nothing more, cupped against the breeze — feeding papers into it one at a time.

Not burning them carelessly. Burning them *carefully*. Holding each page until the corners caught, watching the fire climb the ink, stirring the ash with a stick so nothing legible survived. There was a leather document case open beside her, and it was less than half full.

Silas stood in the dark and watched.

She should have spoken. Every ranger instinct she owned said speak — a captain who burns documents at night is a captain whose expedition is built on something other than surveying. But she watched Maren's face in the candleflame instead, and what she saw stopped her tongue. Not guilt. Guilt she'd have known how to use. What she saw was grief — real grief, raw and unperformed, the face of a woman feeding letters to a fire because keeping them had become unbearable and destroying them was its own kind of wound.

Maren fed another page to the flame. In the light Silas caught a fragment of handwriting on it, a line of ink curling as the paper browned — and the handwriting was Hal's. She knew it the way she knew her own. She'd have known it in the dark.

The page curled, blackened, and was ash.

Maren stirred the embers, closed the case, and sat for a moment with her hand flat on the leather, head bowed, like a woman at a grave. Then she rose, brushed off her knees, and walked back toward camp with her chin up and her stride easy, and by the time she reached the firelight she was Maren Kessler again, seamless, charming, whole.

Silas stayed in the hollow until the candle was out and the cold came up from the river.

She could ask. Tomorrow, at breakfast, casual as weather: *Couldn't sleep? Smelled smoke.* And watch Maren lie, and know the shape of the lie, and let it curdle in her through all the miles upstream. Or she could say nothing, and carry it, and let it grow heavy the way unsaid things grow — and either way they were committed now, all of them, committed to the water and the narrowing walls and the source waiting somewhere above with whatever truth Maren was willing to burn rather than share.

She thought of Tam coughing riverwater onto the gravel. Of Bo's arm locked around his chest. Of Odile's hands, steady on a dead engine, making the one choice that mattered without being asked.

*Say nothing,* she told herself. *Not yet. Get them through the gorges first. There'll be a time.*

But the river talked all night below the cliff, low and insistent, and it sounded less like water and more like a question she was going to have to answer soon.

In the morning, when they cast off, the document case rode in Maren's cabin, lighter than it had been. And forty miles upstream, where the gorge bent east and the charts went blank, the first gray smudge of monsoon cloud stood on the horizon like a man waiting at a door.
