## 6. The Drowned Camp

Odile Voss had built her first weir at nineteen, under a father who believed water was a debt you paid the land before it paid you back. She knew stone. She knew how it sat in a current, how it told on itself, the way a liar's hands do. So when the Kessler River began to slow beneath the barge — not the lazy slowing of a wide channel but the *held* slowing of water backing up against something — she put down her coffee and went to the rail.

"Tam," she said. "Check your soundings."

Tam was already checking them. He stood at the bow with the lead line coiled in his fist like something he'd rather throw away, and when he swung it out and brought it back, his face did a thing Odile had seen only once before, years ago, on a man reading a depth that shouldn't exist.

"Twelve feet," he called back. "Was six yesterday."

The gorge walls here leaned in close enough that their shadows lay across the water all day, so the light never quite touched the surface. The river ran green-black and glassy, moving with the slow muscular patience of something deep. Odile watched the banks instead. She watched the waterline on the rock — a pale mineral stain running along both sides of the gorge, four feet above the current, dead straight as a chalk line drawn by a careful hand.

Water didn't do straight lines. People did.

"Silas," she said quietly.

Silas Pemberton came up from the cargo hold where she'd spent the morning re-tying lashings nobody had asked her to re-tie, because Silas Pemberton could not stand still even on a boat, especially on this boat, going up this river. Odile had known her eleven years and had never once seen her idle. Stillness invited memory; memory invited grief; grief invited the kind of honesty that ended friendships. So Silas worked. Everyone aboard had learned to let her.

She came to the rail and followed Odile's pointing finger to the stain line.

"That's old high water," she said.

"That's *held* water." Odile kept her voice level. "Old high water leaves a ragged mark. Floods are sloppy. This is a pool line. Something upstream is backing this river up, and it's been doing it long enough to leave a tide mark."

Silas looked at her. In the flat grey light of the gorge, her eyes were the color of the water itself, and just as hard to read. "A beaver dam?"

"On a river this size? Beavers would need a treaty."

Behind them, Maren Kessler's voice carried from the stern deck, bright and easy as always, telling Bo some story about a port town and a magistrate's wife. Maren's voice was the most reliable instrument on the barge. It never varied. It ran warm and confident through every hour of the day, and Odile had begun, in the last two days, to think of it the way she thought of a bearing that never wavered: either perfectly true, or perfectly compensated for.

Maren appeared at the rail beside them as if summoned by suspicion, which Odile supposed was her actual talent. She wore a surveyor's coat cut for her in some city Odile couldn't name, and she looked at the waterline stain with an expression of mild professional interest.

"Flash flood debris jam, most likely," Maren said. "The gorges up here funnel everything. You'll see stranger things before the source."

"Probably," Odile agreed, because agreeing cost nothing and watching cost everything. But Maren had answered too fast, and she'd used the word *stranger*, which was a word people used when they wanted you braced for the wrong surprise.

* * *

They found the drowned camp on the third morning past the narrows.

Tam spotted it first — or rather, he stopped spotting anything else. He was taking his noon bearings off a side canyon mouth when he went quiet in a way that made Odile look up from her engine notes. Tam was a navigator who narrated. He talked to his instruments, to the river, to himself, a low running commentary that Odile found less annoying than reassuring, the way you'd trust a clock that ticked aloud. When Tam went silent, something had gone wrong with the world.

"What is it?" Silas said, coming forward.

"There." Tam pointed with the brass sight vane, and his hand wasn't steady. "That shelf. Above the waterline. On the east bank."

At first Odile saw only geology: a gravel terrace tucked into the canyon wall, forty feet above the current, backed by a fold of rock. Then the light shifted through a break in the clouds and the terrace resolved into shapes. Squares. Angles. The ghost-outline of tent platforms bolted to the stone, their timber gone silver-grey with age. A collapsed cook shelter. An iron stove pipe standing out of rubble like a grave marker. And below the terrace, half-drowned, the black ribs of a boat cradle rising out of the water at an angle, holding nothing.

Nobody spoke. The barge drifted in the held-back current, engine idling, and the silence had a texture to it, a weight, like the air in a room where someone has died.

"I know this place," Silas said finally. Her voice came out stripped of everything except fact. "This is Camp Three. We laid over here nine days waiting on the survey gear. There was a fig tree—" She stopped. Her eyes went to the base of the terrace, where a dead fig tree stood in the flood zone, its trunk black and leafless, killed by drowning. "There was a fig tree," she said again, uselessly, the way people repeat themselves when the world has contradicted them.

"The maps show no camp here," Tam said. His voice had gone thin. "I've checked every chart of this reach twice since Vell's Landing. The Kessler above the narrows is marked *unsurveyed*. This whole stretch is blank paper."

"It isn't unsurveyed," Odile said. "It was surveyed. Someone un-surveyed it."

She was already moving to the rail with her field glasses, and what she saw through the lenses made her stomach drop in the particular way it dropped when she recognized her own craft done badly, or worse, done well for a bad reason.

Because the camp hadn't been destroyed. It had been *drowned*. Deliberately. Systematically. By design.

"Do you see it?" she said. "Look at the east bank. Look at the masonry."

They looked. Even Bo, who claimed no engineering and whose uncle had died somewhere on this river, leaned over the rail and squinted.

"Low across the gorge, maybe eight hundred yards upstream," Odile said, tracing it with one finger though they couldn't see her finger's target. "You can just make the crest of it breaking the surface in the middle channel. That's a weir. A proper one — coursed stone, keyed into the bedrock, probably with a timber core. That's why the river's backed up twelve feet. That's why the waterline stain runs straight. Somebody dammed this gorge."

"To flood a camp?" Bo said. "Why would anyone—"

"Not to flood a camp." Odile lowered the glasses. Her hands were steady, which surprised her, because inside she was eleven years younger and standing in a flooded cutting, learning that water could be made to lie. "Flooding the camp is a *consequence*. You build a weir like that to raise the water level, and whatever sits behind it drowns. The camp was in the way. Or—" she paused, choosing the harder truth because these people deserved it, "—or the camp was evidence, and water erases evidence better than fire. Fire leaves ash. Ash can be read. Drowned timber rots. Drowned paper becomes pulp. Drowned men stay found, if they're found at all, and then only as bones nobody can question."

Silas turned from the rail very slowly.

"You're saying someone built a dam," she said, "to kill a camp."

"I'm saying someone built a dam, and a camp died behind it, and I'm saying those are not necessarily the same intention but they are definitely the same result." Odile met her eyes. "And I'm saying whoever built it knew hydraulics. That weir is keyed into bedrock — I can see the abutment scars from here. That's not bandit work. Bandits burn. This was engineered."

The word landed on the deck like dropped iron. *Engineered.* Odile heard it land and understood, with a sick lurch, what it implied about her own profession — that the same knowledge that let her keep this barge alive in the rapids could be turned around, used to rewrite a river, to drown a history. Any tool worth having cuts both ways. She had told herself that for years. Standing here, looking at the proof, she stopped finding it comforting.

"Camp Three," Silas said again. She had gone very still, the stillness of a woman counting. Odile watched her count. "Nine days we were here. Eleven people. Two boats. A full survey outfit — theodolite, chain, notebooks, the ore assays." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied itself by force. "The assay results were in the strongbox. They went downstream with the retreat party. With—" She stopped. Everyone on the barge knew what she'd stopped short of saying. With the people who didn't come back. With her mentor, whose name none of them used, because using it opened something none of them could close.

"And now the camp is underwater," Tam said quietly, "and the maps say this river was never surveyed, and the man who led the survey is dead, and the woman who commissioned the second survey is standing right behind us."

Odile didn't turn around. She didn't need to. She felt Maren Kessler's presence the way you feel weather change — a pressure, a shift in the air, warmth arriving where warmth had no business being.

"A tragedy," Maren said softly. "I forget sometimes how much this river costs the people who love it."

Odile turned then. Maren stood at the rail with her hands folded, gazing at the drowned terrace with an expression of such perfect, composed sorrow that Odile thought: *no one grieves that neatly unless they've rehearsed.*

"You commissioned this survey," Odile said. "Your name is on the charter. Your money bought the gear. So tell us, Maren — did you know there was a weir on your river?"

Something flickered behind Maren's eyes. It lasted less than a heartbeat, and Odile might have missed it if she hadn't spent her whole life reading water, and water and women who controlled water were, it turned out, the same subject.

"I knew the reach was difficult," Maren said. "I knew the first expedition met disaster. Beyond that—" she spread her hands, an elegant gesture of helplessness, "—I hired rangers and engineers precisely because I don't know rivers. That's the arrangement. You navigate. I pay."

It was a clean answer. It was a complete answer. And Odile had been around machinery long enough to know that the most dangerous answers were the ones with no gaps in them, because real answers, like real rivers, always had gaps. Somewhere under every honest account there was a snag, a hidden rock, a place where the numbers didn't come out even. Maren's numbers came out even.

"Then we'll want to look closer at that weir," Odile said pleasantly. "Professional curiosity. If it's structurally sound, it changes our draft calculations for the upper reaches. If it's failing—" she shrugged, "—we should know before monsoon puts ten thousand tons of water behind it."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — Maren Kessler looked at Odile Voss the way a card player looks at an opponent who has just counted the deck. Then the warmth returned, seamless as ever.

"Of course," Maren said. "Measure whatever you like. Science belongs to everyone." She smiled at Silas, at Tam, at Bo, gathering them all into the smile like a net. "We make camp tonight on the north bank. There's a good shingle bar past the bend. Rest while you can, my friends. The hard water is ahead."

She went aft to give orders to Dray and the deckhands, and her voice resumed its bright, unvarying music, and Odile stood at the rail and felt cold in a way the gorge's shade couldn't explain.

* * *

They moored at the shingle bar as instructed, but Odile had no intention of resting, and neither, it developed, did Silas.

They took the skiff at dusk, when the light went copper and long, and rowed the half mile up to the drowned terrace with Tam running the tiller and Bo pulling stroke, because Silas had said only four words — *"I need to see it"* — and nobody had suggested leaving anyone behind. That was the thing about this crew, Odile thought, shipping her oar as the skiff ground onto the gravel below the terrace. Nobody had suggested it. Whatever they were becoming, they were becoming it together, and the speed of it frightened her more than the weir did.

The camp smelled of wet rot and old iron. They climbed the terrace in the failing light and walked among the bones of Camp Three, and Odile watched Silas move through it like a woman walking through her own house after a fire — touching things, naming things under her breath. Tent platform three. The mess shelter. The cache pit, dug into the gravel and lined with stone, empty now, its lid rotted through. At the edge of the flood line, the dead fig tree stood with its arms out, and Silas put her hand on the trunk the way you'd touch a coffin, briefly, and then took it away.

"Here." Tam's voice came from the mess shelter, strange and tight. "Odile. You should see this."

He was crouched at the shelter's foundation, where the floodwater had scoured away years of silt in a single season — the recent high water, the water the weir was holding back, had done in one spring what nature would have taken a decade to do. Exposed in the clean-washed gravel was a length of iron pipe, galvanized, jointed, running from the foundation into the bank.

"That's not drainage," Odile said, kneeling. She brushed the gravel away with her fingers. The pipe ran back toward the canyon wall, angling down. "That's a supply line. Pressurized feed. Someone piped water *into* this camp from higher up." She followed its course with her eye and her breath went shallow. "From a spring. A captured spring, upslope, tapped and plumbed down here. Do you understand what that means? You don't run pipe like that for a survey camp. Survey camps drink from the river. You run pipe like that when you're staying. When you're *working* something. Long-term. Industrial."

"Working what?" Bo said.

Odile looked at him. Behind him the dusk was settling into the gorge, and upstream, invisible beyond the bend, the source of the Kessler waited under its held-back water, and the answer assembled itself in her mind with the terrible click of parts fitting.

"Ore," she said. "Or a claim. Or a mine. Something worth piping water to. Something worth building a weir for." She stood, wiping her hands on her thighs. "Silas. The river was rewritten. Whoever built that dam didn't just drown a camp — they drowned the *survey*. Every benchmark, every datum, every note tied to this elevation is wrong now. The maps don't just omit this place. They *can't* show it. The water changed the ground truth. Anyone who comes up here after, reading the old charts, reads a lie."

Silas had gone to the edge of the terrace. Below her, the drowned boat cradle reached its black ribs out of the water, and beyond it the green-black river slid past, deep and patient, holding its secrets at twelve feet and rising.

"My mentor surveyed this reach," Silas said. Her voice was very quiet. "He sounded it, chained it, fixed every point to the ridge cairns. His charts were the best work of his life. And somebody sat down afterward and decided that his work was worth less than a secret." She turned around, and in the last light her face was terrible and calm. "The river didn't do this. A person did this. A person with engineers, and money, and time."

"Money," Tam repeated. His voice had a hollow ring. "Enough money to build a weir. Enough money to buy silence. Enough money to—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and Odile saw him glance upstream, toward the source, and then away, quickly, as if the looking itself were dangerous.

Bo crouched at the foundation, turning over a scrap of canvas in his big careful hands. "My uncle wrote that the first expedition found something up here worth kingdoms," he said. "I thought he meant the water. A source this reliable, in country this dry — that's wealth enough." He looked up. "But you don't build a dam to hide water. Water wants to be found. You build a dam to hide what the water runs *over*."

Nobody answered him. The words sat in the twilight among the drowned camp's bones, undeniable, and Odile felt the last comfortable assumption of the voyage go out like a lamp.

She had signed onto this expedition for wages and for the chance to do honest work on a hard river. She had told herself the past was the past, that the first expedition's failure was tragedy, plain and simple — bad water, bad luck, bad weather. Engineers believed in causes. Causes could be corrected. But standing in the ruins of Camp Three, with pipe in her hands and a weir upstream and a patron whose answers came out too even, Odile understood that the cause here was not bad luck. It was intention. Someone had wanted this valley to forget, and had spent a fortune making it forget, and had then — the elegance of it turned her stomach — hired the survivors' children to guide her back to what she'd buried, trusting their grief to keep them from looking too closely at the water.

"Well," Silas said at last. She straightened, and Odile saw the ranger come up in her like armor being buckled on, piece by piece. "Tomorrow we sound the weir. All of it. Every course of stone, every abutment, every scar in the bedrock. If there's a date cut into that masonry, or a maker's mark, or a survey pin, I want it found." She looked at each of them in turn — Tam, Bo, Odile — and something passed between the four of them in the dark that had no name yet but had weight, like a keel being laid. "And Odile — when we're done sounding it, I want your honest read. Can it fail?"

Odile considered the question properly, because Silas deserved a proper answer, and because she already knew the shape of the reply and wanted, selfishly, one more night of not saying it.

"Yes," she said. "Anything built can fail. And that one is holding back a monsoon."

They rowed back to the barge in the dark, past the dead fig tree, past the drowned cradle, past the terrace where eleven people had once lived and worked and trusted, and the river slid beneath them, deep and silent and patient, keeping its rewritten story at twelve feet — and rising.
