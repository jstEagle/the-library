## 7. What Tam Read

The drowned camp surfaced on the third morning after the weir, and by then the river had already given back most of what it meant to keep.

Tam saw it first from the bow of the barge — not the camp itself but its geometry, the way a rectangle of rotting pilings broke the current in a pattern no floodwater made. He'd been a navigator long enough to read water like print, and this was print. This was a floor plan. Bunkhouse there, cook shack there, the survey tent staked on high ground that wasn't high ground anymore.

"First expedition," Odile said behind him, flat as a sounding line hitting bottom.

Silas said nothing at all. Tam watched her watch the ruins slide past, her jaw set in that way he remembered from before — from the years ago he'd spent six years pretending had happened to other people. The barge drifted through the drowned camp at walking speed, Maren's pilot easing off the throttle without being asked, and everyone aboard looked down into the green water as if it were a mirror they weren't sure they wanted to use.

That was when Tam saw the box.

It sat wedged in the fork of a dead alder leaning out of the floodline, maybe eight feet above the current — which meant it had floated there and lodged, or someone had put it there to be found. Tin, army-green, dented along one corner. He knew that kind of tin. Survey crews packed their logbooks in them against rain and river both, because paper was the only thing you came home with that mattered more than your skin.

Nobody else saw it. Silas was staring at the collapsed mess tent; Odile was arguing quietly with one of Maren's deckhands about the towline; Bo stood at the stern with his arms crossed, wearing his uncle's frown like an inherited coat. Maren herself was below decks with her charts and her hired muscle and her locked strongbox, and the less Tam thought about any of those things, the better his hands worked.

"Bring us alongside that tree," he said to the pilot.

"Why?"

"Line snag. I'll clear it."

The lie came out smooth. That was the thing about lies — six years of practice made them cheaper than truth, and truth had gotten expensive around this crew. The pilot nosed the barge in, Tam hooked the alder with the boathook, and the tin came free into his hands lighter than it should have been, because half of it was water and the other half was guilt, and guilt weighed nothing until you opened it.

He carried it aft and did not open it. Not yet. He wrapped it in his oilskin and stowed it under his bunk between his spare chart rolls, where a rectangular lump would read as equipment to anyone who looked, and he went forward and took his turn at the leads and called depths in a steady voice while the river ran dark and cold beneath him, carrying him upstream toward the thing he'd helped bury.

* * *

He opened it that night in the lee of the cargo deck, by candlestub, after the fire had burned down and Bo had gone to sleep mid-sentence for the fourth time — the boy talked in his sleep too, whole arguments with an uncle six years dead, and Tam lay listening to those murmurs and feeling like a thief in a house full of honest people.

The logbook was swollen to twice its thickness. The cover had gone soft as leather left out in weather, and the pages inside had fused into a brick that he had to tease apart with a knife tip, one leaf at a time, each one releasing a smell of silt and rot and, underneath it, ink. Good ink. The waterproof kind Odile used to mix herself from lampblack and tallow because she never trusted store-bought anything.

He knew whose hand it was before he turned past the title page. Of course he did. It was his own.

*Log of the Kessler Source Expedition. T. Vann, navigator & recorder.*

The date was seven years gone. The first entry was in his voice, if a voice could be handwriting: crisp figures, tidy margins, the little shorthand symbols he still used today without thinking about why. River mile 112, depth soundings, barometric readings. A note about the cook burning the bread again. A joke in the margin that he could hear himself making, hear the exact laugh that would have followed it, and none of it belonged to a man he recognized. It belonged to a younger Tam, a Tam who hadn't yet learned that a record was a weapon depending on who held it.

He turned pages by candlelight with wet fingers, careful as a surgeon.

The expedition moved upriver. The entries got shorter as the country got harder. And then, at river mile 190 — three miles short of the source, he realized, close enough to spit at it — the handwriting changed.

Not the letters. The letters stayed the same. But the sentences changed. The margins emptied of jokes. The numbers stopped matching the sky, because Tam had been checking them against the entries around them, running the old cross-checks out of pure habit, and the barometric readings didn't agree with themselves. Someone had fed him numbers. Someone had told him what to write, and he had written it, and the worst part — the part that made him set the candle down and press the heels of his hands against his eyes — was that he couldn't remember whether he'd known.

*Ore samples promising beyond estimate,* said the entry for the ninth of the month. *Recommend immediate claim registration under Kessler interests.*

He remembered the samples. God help him, he remembered the ore — the vein of it running through the limestone like a vein of lightning frozen in stone, so bright the guides had called it the river's backbone. What he did not remember was writing that sentence. What he did not remember was deciding anything at all. There was just a blank where the decision should have been, a white space in his own history, and the sentence sitting in it in his handwriting like a stranger wearing his face.

Maren had been there. He could feel her presence in the entries now that he knew to look for it — the way the tone shifted after any day she'd spent with the crew, the way the recommendations always bent toward her interests, the way his own objections, which he must have raised, because he had been twenty-four and principled and insufferable about it, appeared nowhere on the page. Erased. Or never written. Or written and cut out — and he checked, he went through the whole book with his thumbnail feeling for stubs of torn leaves, and near the end he found one. A single page razored out clean at the spine, and the ghost of ink transferred onto the leaf facing it, backwards, unreadable except as evidence that something had been here and was gone.

The last intact entry was dated two days before the disaster.

*Mr. Kessler has ordered the boats cached and the retreat postponed pending final survey. Crew morale poor. R. argues we are past safe season. I have recorded the order.*

R. was Rosha. Rosha the guide, whose bones they'd pulled out of the mud above the weir four days ago, and Tam had stood there in the drizzle watching Silas hold the man's rusted compass and say nothing, and Tam had said nothing too, and his silence had felt like a second skeleton laid over the first.

He had recorded the order. That was all he'd done, all any of them had done — recorded, obeyed, deferred. And then the river had come down early and fat with storm water, exactly as Rosha said it would, and the boats were cached where Maren wanted them instead of where sense said to keep them, and the retreat became a rout and the rout became a grave, and Aldous — Bo's uncle, Silas's mentor, the best man Tam ever worked under — had died in the water holding the line rope for people who should never have needed holding.

And the report they'd carried back downstream, the official survey, the document that had made Maren Kessler rich enough to buy a barge and hire muscle and rewrite a river — Tam had carried that too. In his own pack. In his own handwriting.

He closed the book.

The candle had burned to a puddle. Across the cargo deck, Bo murmured something in his sleep — *"you have to tell her, Uncle"* — and rolled over, and Tam sat in the dark with the swollen logbook in his lap and understood that he was holding the one object on this river capable of tearing the crew apart or saving it, depending on nothing he trusted himself to judge.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept him awake: if he gave it to Silas tonight, she would read it and know that the navigator of her lost family had falsified the record that got her mentor killed. She would know that Tam — quiet, competent Tam, the one who'd kept the maps straight and the crew laughing on the bad days — had signed his name to the lie. She might forgive him. She was the kind of person who might. But she would look at him differently for the rest of the trip, and the crew would fracture at the exact moment they needed to be a single animal, and Maren would win by default the way she always won: by letting other people's honesty do her work for her.

And if he hid it — well. He was already hiding it. Hiding was the one skill he'd mastered since the river, and look what it had bought him: a berth on a barge sailing back up the wound, working for the woman who'd carved it, keeping her charts and calling her *ma'am*, six years of comfortable compounding interest on a single act of cowardice.

The tin went back under his bunk. His hands shook doing it, and he hated them for shaking, because a navigator's hands were supposed to be the steadiest thing on the boat.

* * *

Two days later, Maren found him at the chart table.

She did it the way she did everything — pleasantly, with wine, standing close enough that the conversation could be denied as casual by anyone watching. "Tam. Still my best mapmaker?"

"Still your only mapmaker."

"Mm." She set down two cups though he hadn't asked, poured, pushed one across the table. Her eyes went over his charts the way they went over everything: appraising, weighing, pricing. "You know what I've always valued about you? Discretion. A man who records what needs recording and lets the rest go downriver." She smiled. "Water under the bridge, eh?"

"That's not usually how bridges work."

"No," she agreed, and drank, and set the cup down with a small precise click. "We make landfall at Kettle Falls in four days. After that, everything changes. I'll want your charts finalized before we portage." She paused, and the pleasantness thinned just enough to show the wire underneath. "Finalized, Tam. Not annotated. There's a difference, and I'm given to understand you appreciate the difference."

She left him the wine he didn't drink.

That night he took out the logbook again and read it cover to cover a third time, faster, hunting for anything he'd missed, and found it in the last quarter — a line he'd skipped past twice because it was so ordinary. An entry noting a side canyon at mile 203, unmarked on any published chart, with a note in his own hand: *A. says the source feeds through here in flood season. Says the river can be made to speak.*

Aldous had known. Had mapped it, had told Tam about it, had probably planned to use it — to prove where the water truly came from, to make the source impossible to privatize, to drown the claim in fact. And then Aldous was dead, and the knowledge had drowned with him, sealed in a tin box in a tree for seven years.

Until the river gave it back.

Tam repacked the book and sat very still in the dark, listening to the water slap the hull, doing the thing navigators do: plotting a course through water he couldn't see the bottom of. Every route ended at the same bend. Either he handed Silas the truth and let it break whatever it broke — or he kept carrying it alone, upriver, into the narrowing gorge, where Maren's patience was visibly running out and Dray's eyes followed everyone including him, and where a secret heavy as this one would eventually sink whatever boat tried to hold it.

He thought about Silas at the drowned camp, staring down into the green water, saying nothing. He thought about how she'd looked away first.

He decided he'd tell her. Tomorrow. When they stopped to wood the boiler, somewhere with solid ground under them, privately, before Maren's four days ran out.

He fell asleep believing it, which was the last easy sleep he'd get — because in the morning the fog came down thick off the gorge walls, and out of the fog, dead ahead, came the sound every soul on that river knew by heart and feared: Kettle Falls, roaring louder than it had any right to roar, and the monsoon still a hundred miles out to sea.
