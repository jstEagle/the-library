## 8. Portage of Bones

The weir announced itself as a sound before a sight — a long, flat roar rolling up the gorge like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Silas heard it for a full mile before the canyon bent and showed her the truth of it: the river simply stopped. Ahead, where the Kessler should have run white and furious through the narrows, there was instead a wall of black timber and dressed stone, water spilling over its lip in a curtain so even it looked manufactured, because it was.

"Nobody built a dam up here," Odile said. She had come forward to stand at Silas's shoulder at the barge rail, and her voice had the flatness she got when her engineer's mind was working a problem it didn't like. "Not on any survey. Not on any map I've ever seen, and I've seen the ones that were never published."

"Maren has," Tam said from the tiller. He didn't look up from the water. "You can tell by how calm she isn't."

Silas looked aft. Maren Kessler stood at the stern beneath the cargo awning the way she had stood at the head of every table Silas had ever shared with her — hands folded, weight even, watching the crew with the patient attention of a woman counting her assets. She caught Silas looking and smiled, and the smile was warm the way a lamp in a window is warm when the house behind it is on fire.

"Portage at the weir," Maren called. "There's a track on the north bank. We go around on foot, haul the barge light, and run the spillway channel below. Odile, you'll want to see the stonework."

"I already see it," Odile said, too quietly for Maren to hear.

They put in at a gravel shelf below the dam, and the crew began the work portage always was — breaking the load down, slinging crates, coiling rope, Bo and Dray hauling the barge's mast down and lashing it flat. Silas worked in silence and watched the north bank track emerge from the willow scrub as they climbed: a cut path, graded, switchbacked, old enough that the willows had grown back over its edges but new enough that the cuts were still clean.

A path like that took a crew. A crew like that took a reason.

"Silas." Maren had come up beside her, boots sure on the stone, and gestured ahead with her chin. "You're frowning. It's a portage, not a funeral."

"Everything up this river is a funeral," Silas said. "We just haven't found the graves yet."

Maren laughed — a real laugh, brief and bright, and for a moment it was nine years ago and they were twenty-two and laughing exactly like that on the deck of the *Merry Adela*, before everything. Then the moment closed over, the way moments did, and Maren walked on ahead, and Silas stood in the willow shade hating how much she had wanted the laugh to be true.

* * *

The portage was three miles of the worst ground Silas had walked in years. The track climbed away from the river and then dropped into a fold of the canyon where the sun never reached, and the path there had gone soft with seep water, and the crates went from shoulder to shoulder to shoulder until Silas's hands burned and the strap of her pack had worn a groove she could feel through her shirt. Tam counted cadence without being asked, the way he always had, his voice thin and steady over the sound of the weir. Odile stopped twice to look at the retaining walls holding the track above the river, and the second time she crouched and put her palm flat against the stone and stayed there long enough that Dray, hauling the rear, growled at her to move.

"Someone dressed this stone," Odile said, standing, wiping her hand on her thigh. "Dressed and mortared. That's not a survey crew's work. That's a construction crew's. A funded one."

"Move," Dray said again.

They came down out of the fold in late afternoon, the light going copper, and the path bent along the base of the weir itself, close enough now that spray drifted across them and beaded cold on Silas's forearms. The willows thickened. The ground flattened. And the dogs — the two rangy camp dogs Maren had brought aboard at Vell's Landing — went stiff-legged and silent at the same instant, hackles up, staring into the willow brake ahead, and would not be moved another step.

Silas knew what dead smelled like. Every ranger did. She had hoped, walking this river again, that she'd forgotten.

"Wait here," she said.

"Silas—" Tam started.

"Wait here."

She went in alone, one hand on the revolver at her hip out of habit rather than expectation, pushing the willow whips aside. The brake opened into a hollow at the weir's base, a pocket of gravel and standing seep where the dam's builders had staged their stone. The smell was stronger here, sweet and terrible, and the dogs had been right, and the dogs were always right.

He was under a cairn.

Not a proper grave — a cairn, stones stacked in haste by someone with no shovel or no time, and the years and the seep water had done the rest. What was left of him lay partly tumbled, one arm bone spilled out from the pile like something reaching. His boots were still on his feet, rotted to lace-holders, and the boots were the thing that did it, because Silas knew those boots. She had cuffed their owner for the state of them a hundred times. She had resoled them herself by a fire in the Salt Range while he complained that she stitched too tight.

"Kestrel," she said. The name came out of her like a stone dropping down a well.

Joren Kestrel. Guide of the first Kessler expedition. The man who had walked Silas's mentor — who had walked all of them — through country nobody else could read. The man listed on the expedition's final report, in Tam's careful handwriting, as *lost in the rapids below the gorge, body unrecovered.*

Not lost in the rapids. Not below the gorge. Here, at the weir, at the place where the river had been rewritten, under a cairn somebody had stacked in a hurry and run from.

Silas stood over him for a long time. The spray drifted. Somewhere behind her the crew's voices had gone quiet, and she knew they'd followed her in, and she found she didn't care.

"Silas." Maren's voice, at her shoulder. Careful. "Step back. Let me look."

Silas stepped back. Maren crouched at the cairn, and Silas watched her face do something complicated and fast — grief, or the shape of grief, worn expertly, like a coat — and then Maren reached out and turned one of the spilled bones with one finger, clinical, and looked at the skull, and looked at the boots, and stood.

"Bandits," Maren said.

Silas said nothing.

"Years ago. This country was full of them before the river trade was tamed." Maren brushed gravel from her knee, unhurried. "He probably surprised a claim-jumping party. They killed him and stacked the stones and moved on. It happens. It happened everywhere, out here."

"That's not what happened here."

"It's what the record will say." Maren's voice stayed level, pleasant, and that was the thing about Maren that had always frightened Silas most, even back when they were friends: the pleasantness never moved. It was a wall she hung things on. "Silas. I know what you're thinking. I know what this looks like. But we are three days' walk from the source with a monsoon coming, and I will not have this crew spending its nerve on a nine-year-old grave. We mark it. We say words if you want words. And we walk on."

"His name was Joren Kestrel."

"I know what his name was."

"Then say it."

Maren looked at her. The pleasantness held, but behind it, for the first time since Vell's Landing, Silas saw something move — quick and cold, a door opening a crack on a room Silas had never been allowed into. "I have said his name more times than you have, Silas. I carried that expedition's dead longer than anyone. Do not stand there in your ranger's coat and tell me how to grieve."

"You don't grieve." Silas heard her own voice go low, and didn't stop it. "You file. You've been filing him for nine years under *lost in the rapids*, because *lost in the rapids* doesn't raise questions and a cairn at your private dam does. Who built the weir, Maren? Who paid for the track? Who dressed this stone?"

"Bandits," Maren said again, softer now, and smiled, and the smile was the wall. "Grief makes people see conspiracies. I'd hoped better of you."

She turned and walked out of the willow brake, calling orders to the crew in her bright carrying voice — camp here, first light, the spillway channel below — and the crew parted for her and closed behind her, and Silas stood alone with the cairn and the spray and the smell of the years.

* * *

They camped on the gravel shelf in the shadow of the weir, and the sound of the spill filled the whole canyon, so that every conversation had to lean close and every silence felt louder. Odile built the cook fire low. Tam sat apart with his hands around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. Dray and the two deckhands Maren had hired took the far side of the fire and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of message.

Bo found Silas at the edge of the light, where she'd taken the first watch out of a need to be doing something with her hands.

She heard him coming — he moved better than a man his size should — and didn't turn until he settled onto the rock beside her, close enough to talk, far enough to give her the choice of it. He had his uncle's letters, she realized. The oilskin packet was in his hand, and he turned it over once, twice, and didn't open it.

"You knew him," Silas said. "Kestrel."

"By letter only. But my uncle wrote about him like a brother." Bo looked at the fire, at the weir's white shoulder looming above them. "You want to know what happened to him."

"I want to know why Maren lied about it in front of all of us."

"Because the truth has a shape," Bo said, "and the shape points upstream."

He opened the packet. His hands were big and careful, a stonemason's hands, and he drew out a single letter, soft at the folds, and didn't hand it to her — just angled it so the firelight caught the ink, and read.

"*J. Kestrel found the vein today. He came into camp at dusk with ore in his hat and wouldn't say where. He said the location goes to the grave with him until the crew votes on what's right. Maren argued with him half the night. I have never heard her like that.*"

Bo stopped. The fire cracked. Up on the weir, the spill roared on, indifferent, drowning everything.

"Two nights later," Bo said, "my uncle's letter changes. Shorter. Careful. Like a man writing for a record instead of a friend. And the one after that is the last one — the one that says the expedition is retreating, the boats are lost, Kestrel is lost, and the survey found nothing worth the paper." He folded the letter away. "Kestrel died protecting the source's location, Silas. From somebody on his own crew. And the weir — the weir that drowned your old camp, the weir that put this river on no map — was built after, to make sure nobody ever walked up this canyon and found what he died keeping."

The spray drifted down. Silas found she was gripping her own knee hard enough to ache, and made herself let go.

"Say it plainly," she said. "I've spent nine years not saying things plainly."

Bo looked at her then, and his eyes in the firelight were his uncle's eyes, and it hurt to look at them, and she didn't look away.

"Maren Kessler killed Joren Kestrel," he said, "or had him killed, and built a river over the grave of it. And she has hired you — she has hired all of us — to walk her back up to the source before the monsoon seals it, so she can own what he died protecting. That's the plainly."

Silas sat with it. Below them, the barge lay on its mooring lines, riding the backwater, and beyond it the canyon ran black toward the source, and somewhere up that blackness was a vein of ore worth kingdoms and a grave that had been filed under a lie for nine years.

She thought about her mentor's face. She thought about the *Merry Adela*, and the crew scattered like spilled seed, and every year since that she had kept moving because stillness invited memory, and memory invited this exact moment, standing at a grave she could have prevented if she had ever once stopped running and asked the question.

"Tomorrow," Silas said, "we run the spillway and we keep moving upstream. We smile at her. We haul her crates. We do nothing that tells her we know."

Bo frowned. "And then?"

"And then we get ahead of her." She turned and looked up the canyon, into the dark where the water came from. "Kestrel wouldn't say where the vein was. Fine. But he was a guide, and guides leave marks, and I've spent nine years learning to read marks like his. Somewhere between here and the source, he left the location written in the only language Maren can't burn."

Bo was quiet a moment. "You're going to find his trail."

"I'm going to finish his walk," Silas said.

She stood, and settled her pack straps, and looked once more at the willow brake where the cairn stood in the dark. The spray drifted cold across the gravel, and the weir held back the river the way Maren held back the truth — completely, and only for now.

Above the gorge, far upstream, the sky had begun to stack clouds, and the first far thunder rolled down the canyon like a bell being rung for them.
