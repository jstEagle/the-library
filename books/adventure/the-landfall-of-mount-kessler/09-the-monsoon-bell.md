## 9. The Monsoon Bell

The bell woke Maren before the storm did.

It hung at the barge's stern, a dented brass thing she'd bought in Vell's Landing from a man who swore it had come off a temple roof somewhere east of the mountains. She'd bought it for luck, which was a lie she told herself the way she told most lies — fluently, and with a straight face. It wasn't luck. It was an alarm. A bell only rings when something is coming.

She lay in her cabin listening to it swing on its bracket as the barge rocked against its mooring lines, and she thought: *that's not wind.*

Then the wind arrived to prove her right.

It came down the gorge like something poured, a cold gray wall of air that hit the canvas awnings and made them crack like whips. The river below the mooring changed color in the space of a minute — went from green-glass morning to a churning brown, thick with silt flushed down from country they hadn't reached yet. Country that was already drowning.

Maren dressed in the dark. She'd gotten good at dressing in the dark over the years; there were whole seasons of her life that had happened without light. Boots, coat, the small ledger she kept inside her coat where no one would think to look for it. She paused at the little mirror above the washbasin and gave herself thirty seconds of honesty — gray coming in at her temples now, eyes still steady, mouth set in the line that Odile had once, drunk, called *the seam of a purse that never opens.*

Odile could be cruel when she drank. Maren had learned to use cruelty like that, back when they were all younger and poorer and still pretending to be friends.

On deck, the sky was wrong.

Not storm-wrong — she knew storms, had priced them into every contract she'd ever signed. This was worse. The clouds weren't building over the peaks the way monsoon clouds were supposed to build, stacking themselves up all afternoon like furniture before the fall. These were already here. They came down the canyon ahead of schedule, low and fast and the color of wet slate, dragging their shadows across the water toward the barge like fingers closing.

Tam was already at the rail with his instruments out, and he didn't look at her when she came up beside him, which meant he already knew, which meant she was the last to know, which she hated more than the news itself.

"Say it," she said.

"Pressure's dropped half an inch since midnight." Tam's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man reading numbers he wished said something else. "Barometer hasn't done that outside of landfall. Not this early. Not for another three weeks."

"The highlands seal by mid-monsoon."

"They'll seal early." He finally looked at her, and there was something in his face she didn't like — pity, maybe, or the beginning of it. "Maren. If we're not through Kettle Falls before—"

"We'll be through Kettle Falls." She heard her own voice go smooth and pleasant, the register she used for closing deals. "Thank you, Tam. Wake the crew."

* * *

She worked them through breakfast.

That was how she thought of it — *worked* — because that's what it was. She moved along the deck while they ate, laying it out in the warm reasonable voice that had built her fortune: the weather turning, the window narrowing, the source still four days upstream if they pushed hard, two if they pushed harder. And money. Money was the hinge of every argument she made, so she made it early and often.

"Triple pay stands," she said, pacing the deck with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing troops. "But I'll add a bonus. Five hundred crowns apiece if we stand at the source within ten days. Five hundred more if we're back downriver before the highlands close."

Silas sat apart from the others, cleaning her rifle with the methodical patience of someone who found the ritual calming, and she didn't look up. That was its own kind of answer.

"That's a thousand crowns," Bo said slowly. He was the newest, the nephew, the one with his uncle's jaw and his uncle's habit of asking questions nobody wanted answered. "For comparison, my uncle died on this river for less than that."

The deck went quiet. Maren felt the silence settle on her shoulders like a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd stopped noticing it, except in moments like this, when someone else picked it up.

"Your uncle died because the season closed on him," she said. "Because he wouldn't turn back when turning back was still possible. I'm offering you the opposite bargain. Turn nothing back. Take everything forward. And get paid like kings for it."

"And what's at the source that's worth king's prices?" Silas asked, still not looking up. The rifle's bolt slid home with a click that sounded, in the quiet, very final.

"Water," Maren said. "The most valuable water in the territory, and the right to say you found where it lives. There are men in the capital who'd pay a fortune just to put their name on that map."

It was a good answer. It had been a good answer for twenty years. But she watched Silas's hands pause on the rifle — just a hitch, just a breath — and she understood that the answer was wearing thin, the way a coin wears thin, until one day someone holds it up to the light and sees the base metal underneath.

"Ten days," Silas said, and went back to her rifle.

Maren let the silence hold for exactly three seconds, then smiled and clapped her hands once, sharp as a starting gun. "Then we move. Odile — I want steam up within the hour. Tam, plot us the fastest line through the narrows, and I mean fastest, not safest. Dray—" she turned to the big man leaning against the cargo crane, arms folded, watching everything the way he always watched everything, "—with me."

* * *

Her cabin smelled of lamp oil and old paper.

Dray filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway, and waited. That was the thing about Dray that made him worth every crown she paid him: he never asked why. Other men needed reasons. Dray needed instructions.

"The ranger," Maren said. "I want her watched."

"She watches herself pretty good."

"I'm sure she does. That's what worries me." Maren crossed to the porthole, where the brown water ran past, fast and getting faster. "She's been asking questions. About the first expedition. About the weir. About the guide." She kept her voice level, but she could hear the hairline fracture running under it, and she knew Dray could hear it too. "There are things on this barge that stay on this barge. Papers. Records. If she comes looking—"

"You want me to stop her?"

"I want you to *know* about it." She turned from the porthole. "Stopping her is the last resort. Do you understand? The moment anyone lays a hand on Silas Pemberton, this crew stops being a crew and starts being a jury. We do this clean. We do this quiet. You watch. You report. Nothing else unless I say."

Dray nodded once, slow, like a door closing. "And if she finds something anyway?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the bell swung on its bracket, tapping the brass in a rhythm too irregular to be wind alone.

"Then we're past clean," she said. "And into fast."

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, so she did what she always did instead: she counted.

She counted the days to the source — six, if the river behaved, four if it didn't dare misbehave. She counted the crowns left in the strongbox, and the dynamite crated under the false floor of the cargo hold, and the years since the first expedition, each one a debt compounding quietly in the dark. She counted the people on this barge who trusted her, and got to a number so small it fit in one hand, and even those fingers were borrowed.

The rain started after midnight — not the monsoon itself, not yet, but the outriders of it, fat drops that hit the deck like thrown stones. She stood at the porthole watching them strike the brown water, and she thought about the first expedition again, the way she thought about it every night whether she wanted to or not: the camp in the dark, the guide's face when he understood what she'd decided, the sound the river makes when it takes something it intends to keep.

Everyone believed she'd sent that expedition upriver to find water. Even Silas believed it, or had, once. But water was common. Water fell out of the sky for free. What Maren had found in those hills — what the guide had died keeping quiet, what the forged report had buried, what the weir drowned — was rarer than water and heavier than gold, and it was sitting in the dark at the head of the Kessler River waiting for her, patient as ore, patient as sin.

All she had to do was get there before the sky did.

She took out the ledger from inside her coat and opened it to the last written page, and beneath the columns of figures she wrote a single line, small and neat:

*Early. Everything is early. Move faster than the truth.*

Then she blew out the lamp and stood in the dark listening to the rain thicken, and the bell tap-tap-tapping its warning at the stern, and beneath both — faint, far upstream, almost imagined — the sound of the river rising.

* * *

Morning brought fog, and with the fog, trouble.

It came up the gorge in banks so thick the bow of the barge vanished ten feet out, and the crew moved through it like ghosts, voices arriving from nowhere, footsteps on wet decking belonging to no one visible. Maren stood at the wheel with Odile beside her, both of them peering into the white.

"Sandbar should be off the port bow," Odile said. "If the channel's shifted—"

The barge shuddered. Grounded, hard enough to knock Maren against the wheel, hard enough that somewhere below decks a crate broke loose and slammed the bulkhead. Odile was already shouting orders, already moving, and Maren watched her do it — watch the competence pour off the woman like water off waxed canvas — and felt the old familiar envy rise in her throat, bitter as bile.

Odile had always been like this. Even broke, even disgraced, even after everything, she could put her hands on a machine and make it love her. Maren had spent twenty years buying loyalty and calling it friendship, and Odile had spent twenty years earning it without ever once charging for it, and there was no price Maren could pay that would make the ledger between them come out even.

"Off in ten minutes!" Odile shouted up. "Everybody push!"

They pushed. All of them — even Silas, shoulder to the capstan bar beside Bo, muscles working in the fog, saying nothing. Maren stood at the wheel and watched her crew strain together, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the thing she could never buy: not the muscle, not the skill, but the way they leaned into the same bar without being ordered to. The way family leans.

The barge groaned, scraped, and slid free.

"Course holding!" Tam called from the bow, sounding surprised, and for one moment the fog seemed to lift around them, showing brown water and black cliffs and the narrow thread of channel winding upstream toward everything Maren had buried.

Then the fog closed again, and the bell at the stern began to ring — really ring this time, swung wild by the strengthening wind — and Maren gripped the wheel and steered into the white, telling herself the sound was only brass and weather.

Telling herself the sound was not a countdown.
