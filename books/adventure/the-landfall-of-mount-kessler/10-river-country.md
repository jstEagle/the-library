## 10. River Country

Bo had carried the letters through three hundred miles of river country, and he'd carried them the way a man carries a thing he's afraid to set down and more afraid to open.

They rode in an oilcloth packet inside his coat, against his chest, over his heart, which was where his uncle would have wanted them. Uncle Aldous Kessler — no relation to Maren, whatever the name suggested; he'd married into it and worn it like borrowed clothes — had been a letter-writer. A bad one, by family account. Too honest for letters, Bo's mother used to say. Too honest for anything but graves.

Bo had never met him. He'd been born two years after the first expedition went up the Kessler River and didn't come back whole. But he'd grown up on the man's handwriting, because Aldous had written ahead of himself the way other men prayed — constantly, uselessly, beautifully. Letters from every river town between here and the coast. Letters with mud on them. Letters that ended mid-sentence when the money ran out or the mood did.

And then one last letter, dated nine days before the expedition launched, that Bo's mother had kept in a biscuit tin for twenty years and never once spoken of.

Bo had read it a hundred times since Vell's Landing. He'd read it on the barge deck while Tam plotted their course and Odile cursed her engines. He'd read it by lantern while Silas stood watch and pretended not to see him reading it. He'd read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

He knew what it said. He knew what it meant. What he didn't know was how to hand it to a woman who had buried her mentor above this same river and spent every year since running from the sound of its name.

Tonight, though, the river made the decision for him.

* * *

They'd made camp on a gravel bar below a bend the maps called the Elbows, where the Kessler doubled back on itself twice like a man checking over his shoulder. Maren's barge was tied off upstream, lanterns burning at her rail, Dray's silhouette motionless at the gangplank. The survey crew kept their own fire fifty yards down, close enough to be summoned, far enough to breathe.

It was the last easy night any of them would get. Bo could feel it in the air the way you feel weather coming — the gorge narrowing ahead, the water already quickening under the hull, the mountains stacking themselves against the stars. Tomorrow they'd enter country where the river ran faster than regret.

Silas sat apart from the fire, as she always did, working a whetstone along a knife she never seemed to need sharpened. Bo watched her for a long time. Then he got up, crossed the gravel, and sat down beside her without asking, which was either courage or stupidity, and he'd stopped being able to tell the difference since he'd joined this crew.

"You're staring," Silas said.

"I brought something."

"I figured. You've been carrying it like a stone in your boot for a week."

He took out the packet. His hands weren't steady, and he let them be unsteady, because hiding it would have been worse. "These are my uncle's letters. Aldous Kessler. He was the guide on the first expedition."

Silas went very still. Not the stillness of surprise — the stillness of a woman deciding whether to draw a weapon. "Your uncle was Aldous?"

"My mother's brother. I told you I was his nephew. I didn't tell you I've never seen his face except in a photograph, and that I know him better than I know most living men." He turned the packet over in his hands. "She kept them. My mother. Twenty years in a biscuit tin, and she never told me why until the night before I left to find you. She said, *Somebody has to give them to somebody who'll hate what they say.*"

"Then don't give them to me."

"They're yours more than mine." He held them out. "You were there."

"I was there," Silas said, "and I've spent twenty years not reading things."

Bo almost laughed, and it came out wrong, half a breath. "So have I. Whole tin of them. You know what I did instead? I learned his handwriting. I can pick it out of a crowd now. That slanted *K*. The way he crossed his sevens. I know a dead man better than I ever knew my own father, and I did it because I was afraid to find out what he actually said." He pressed the packet into her hands. "Read one. Just one. If I'm wrong about what they mean, throw them in the fire and I'll row us all back to Vell's Landing myself."

Silas looked at the packet a long moment. The firelight moved on her face and gave nothing away. Then she untied the oilcloth with the care of someone handling something that might still go off.

* * *

The first letter was dated three weeks before the launch. Bo watched her read it by the light of the coals, and he recited it from memory, low, because he couldn't help himself:

*Aldous to his sister:*

*M. has the financing settled and won't say from whom. That alone should frighten me more than the gorge does. She's bought survey instruments finer than anything the magistrate's office owns, and she counts our powder twice a day and logs the count herself. I've guided this river eleven years and I've never seen a patron count the powder.*

*The girl Pemberton is good. Better than good — she reads water like scripture. Maren praises her to her face and mocks her to me, and I don't think Maren knows I hear it. I'm writing this down because if anything happens up there, I want it known that I saw it early.*

Silas read it twice. Her jaw worked once, like a woman swallowing something that wouldn't go down. "He wrote about me."

"He wrote about everyone. That was the disease he had." Bo passed her the next one. "This is the one from the source country. Dated after they found it."

The second letter was shorter. The handwriting had changed — hurried, the slant steeper, as if the man had written standing up.

*Sister — we found it. God forgive us, we found it. There's a cavern above the spring where the rock runs gold and green, veins thick as your wrist, ore enough to buy the territory twice over. Tam wept. Odile laughed until she cried. Even Maren — even Maren looked afraid, and I mistook it for awe.*

*I know now it wasn't awe. It was arithmetic.*

*She asked me today, casual as asking the time, whether the source could be sealed. Whether a charge in the right place might bring the cavern mouth down behind us. I told her the truth: yes, easily. I shouldn't have answered. I saw her write it down.*

*Pemberton's girl trusts her completely. That's the worst of it. Whatever Maren does, she'll do it knowing the girl will take the blame or take the grave, and she's decided the girl is worth both.*

Bo heard Silas's breathing change. It was a small thing, a hitch no louder than a fish rising, but on a quiet gravel bar at midnight it was thunder.

"The retreat," Silas said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "The retreat failed because the monsoon came early. That's what the report said. That's what *everyone* said."

"The report was written by Maren," Bo said gently. "There's one more. My mother called it the last one. It arrived after the news did. She said the postmark was the only reason anyone believed it."

The third letter was stained. Water, maybe, or something else. The handwriting had come apart entirely, letters leaning like fence posts in a flood.

*Sister. If you're reading this I didn't come home, and you must give these pages to whoever asks the right questions.*

*Maren cut the lines. I watched her do it. Night before the retreat, she cut the mooring lines on the supply boats and blamed the current, and by morning we were walking out with what we could carry and the monsoon on our backs. She wanted us scattered. She wanted witnesses thinned. A dead guide tells no tales and a drowned engineer signs nothing.*

*I got the others to the high ground. I stayed behind at the camp to burn the survey plates — the real ones, the ones showing the vein — because she ordered them destroyed and I obeyed, God help me, I obeyed, and then I understood that destroying them was exactly what she wanted, because with the plates burned the claim dies with us and lives again whenever she returns with capital and no witnesses.*

*So I kept one plate. I hid it where she'll never look, and I'm going back for the rest, and if I die doing it, then let the record show: the disaster on the Kessler was not weather. It was arithmetic. It was a woman counting powder.*

*Tell Pemberton's girl —*

The letter stopped there. It stopped there the way Aldous Kessler had apparently stopped everywhere, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-life.

*Tell Pemberton's girl* — what? Bo had wondered for twenty years. An apology. A warning. A blessing. The sentence had died with the man, and no amount of reading could raise it.

Silas held the page in both hands. The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and her face above them was the face of a woman watching a building collapse in slow motion — everything she'd stood on for twenty years settling, cracking, finding new ground that wasn't ground at all.

"She cut the lines," Silas said. "I pulled bodies out of that river for six days. Six days. And she stood on the bank with me. She *mourned* with me." 

"I know."

"She named the river route after him. The Kessler Survey. She put his name on her letterhead." Silas laughed, and it was the worst sound Bo had ever heard, dry and cracked as the flats outside Vell's Landing. "I thought hiring me was guilt. I thought maybe, underneath everything, she wanted to make it right."

"Or," Bo said, and hated himself for saying it, "she wanted the one witness who loved her close enough to control."

Silas closed her eyes. For a moment she looked younger, and infinitely tired, the way people look when armor they've worn so long they'd forgotten it was heavy finally comes off and shows them the weight they've been carrying. When she opened her eyes again, the grief was still there, but it had been forged into something with an edge.

"Why now?" she asked. "Why give me this tonight?"

"Because tomorrow we pass the drowned camp," Bo said. "Odile spotted the weir on the depth soundings yesterday — a structure where no structure should be. And because I've watched you follow that woman's orders for a week with your teeth shut, and I couldn't watch another day of it without putting the truth in your hands. You deserved to choose. We all do." He nodded at the letters. "That's the choice. Written by a dead man who liked you."

Silas looked at the letters. Then at the barge upstream, at the lanterns, at the shape of Dray at the rail, patient as a fence post. Then at the fire, and Bo saw her weigh the packet in her hands like a woman deciding whether to feed it to the flames or keep it as a weapon.

When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that only he could hear it over the river.

"Aldous stayed behind to protect the source," she said. "From her."

"And died for it."

"And she hired his nephew." Silas shook her head slowly. "No. She didn't hire you. She hired *me*, and you attached yourself like a burr, and she let you stay because a burr is easier to pluck than a witness." She tucked the letters inside her coat, over her own heart, mirroring where Bo had carried them. "Thank you. I mean it. This is the cruelest gift anyone's ever given me, and I needed it."

"That's family for you," Bo said. "We deal in cruel gifts."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first he'd seen since Vell's Landing. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we float past a graveyard, and I want you sharp."

Bo rose, brushed gravel from his knees, and took three steps toward his bedroll before her voice caught him.

"Bo."

He turned.

"When we reach the source," Silas said, and the firelight made her eyes hard and bright as river stones, "if what these letters say is true — I'm going to need to know whose side you're on. Not hers. Not your uncle's ghost's. Yours."

Bo considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty. "Mine," he said. "Same side as yours, if you'll have it. Same side as Tam and Odile. The side that goes home together or doesn't go home."

Silas held his gaze a long moment. Then she nodded once, slow, like a door closing on one room and opening on another.

"Sleep," she said again. "Tomorrow we find out what she built to hide."

Bo lay down in his bedroll and listened to the river run in the dark, faster now, impatient, pulling them all upstream toward whatever waited at the head of it. Above the fire, Silas sat awake with a dead man's letters against her chest, and the lanterns on Maren's barge burned on, steady and white, like eyes that never blink.

In the morning, they would reach the drowned camp.
