## 11. The Turn at Kettle Falls

You heard Kettle Falls before you saw it — a sound like the whole river clearing its throat, a low grinding roar that came up through the soles of your boots and settled somewhere behind your sternum. Silas had been hearing it for half a day, getting louder by the mile, and she'd been dreading it for exactly that long.

She stood at the bow of Maren's barge with one hand on the rail and watched the gorge open ahead of them like a book to a page nobody wanted to read. The walls narrowed. The water went from brown to white in long ragged seams. And there, where the canyon pinched to less than forty yards across, the river simply ended — dropped thirty feet into a churning cauldron of spray and broken stone that no boat ever built was coming back up from.

"Kettle Falls," Tam said beside her. He said it the way a man names a grave.

"I know what it is."

"I know you know." He shifted his weight. "I just like saying it out loud sometimes. Makes it real. Real things you can plan around."

Silas didn't answer. She'd been doing a lot of not answering lately. Three days since Bo had put his uncle's letters in her hands around the fire, three days since the words *she sabotaged the retreat* had been sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, and still she hadn't confronted Maren, hadn't turned the crew around, hadn't done anything but walk the deck at night and watch the dark water slide past and ask herself the same question over and over: *what are you waiting for?*

She knew the answer. She hated the answer. She was waiting because turning back meant admitting the letters were true, and admitting they were true meant everything since Vell's Landing — the grudging handshakes, Odile's engine work, Bo's terrible campfire coffee, Tam humming off-key while he plotted their course — all of it had been built on top of a lie she'd agreed to carry.

The barge swung wide around a bend and the falls filled the world.

* * *

They tied off at a shelf of flat rock a quarter mile below the drop, where an old landing jutted from the canyon wall — timber pilings black with age, iron rings rusted to stains in the stone. Someone had portaged here before. A long time ago. The pilings were cut in a style Silas recognized, deep-sunk and braced, the kind of work her mentor Harlan Voss used to do with his own hands when he didn't trust anyone else's.

Bo touched one of the rings and said nothing. He'd been saying nothing about Harlan a lot lately, which was its own kind of loud.

Maren came down the gangplank in her surveyor's coat, crisp as if she'd pressed it that morning, which knowing Maren she probably had. Dray followed with two of the hired hands, and behind them came the rest — six souls and a barge full of chain, rope, and dynamite that Silas tried very hard not to look at.

"Well," Maren said, spreading her arms at the roar. "Here we say goodbye to the river's help."

"Here we say goodbye to the barge," Odile corrected, coming up last, wiping her hands on a rag. She'd been below deck all morning, and she came up now with the particular set of her jaw that Silas had learned, years ago, preceded bad news delivered without apology. "And I want everyone to hear this before anybody starts pretending otherwise. That hull took damage in the rapids above the drowned camp. I patched it twice. There won't be a third patch. Even if there were a channel down from here — and there isn't — this boat is done carrying us anywhere."

"So we go on foot," Maren said lightly. "As planned."

"As planned," Odile repeated, and let the word sit there like something rotting. She walked to the edge of the shelf, looked up the gorge — the walls rising sheer and gray, the river above the falls running fast and narrow through a slot no wider than a wagon road — and then she turned around and looked at all of them, one face at a time, and finished it. "Or we don't."

The roar of the falls filled the silence after that. It was a good silence-filler. Silas suspected the falls had been filling silences for men in trouble for a thousand years.

"Odile," Maren began, warm and reasonable, "nobody's asking you to—"

"You're asking us to walk four days up a slot canyon into monsoon country," Odile said, "behind a woman who burned papers on her own deck, past a weir that isn't on any map, over bones that nobody's explained, carrying explosives that nobody's explained either. So yes, Maren. Somebody is asking. And I'm answering." She slung the rag over her shoulder. "I'm a good engineer. You know why I'm a good engineer? Because I know when a structure is compromised. This expedition is compromised. I want to turn back."

"There's no turning back," Dray said. It was the first time Silas had heard him volunteer a sentence, and his voice matched the rest of him — big, flat, unhurried. "Barge is done, you said so yourself. Trail downriver's flooded past the weir. Only way out is through."

"That's not true," Odile said, "and you know it's not true, because I've seen the maps. There's a stock trail over the rim, two days west. Hard walking, but walking."

"The stock trail doesn't exist anymore," Maren said. "Rockfall, years ago. I have the survey."

"You have a survey you wrote yourself," Odile said, "in a report nobody else has seen. Forgive me if I've stopped taking your paperwork on faith."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. Silas caught it because she'd spent twenty years learning to read that exact flicker on the faces of people about to do something unforgivable. But Maren only smiled, spread her hands, and said, "Then put it to a vote. We're all equals here, aren't we? Partners on the river."

It was a beautiful performance. Silas almost applauded.

Because Maren knew — she had to know — how the vote would fall. The hired hands would follow Dray, and Dray followed Maren's money. Tam wouldn't vote against Maren; Silas had watched him flinch every time the first expedition came up, watched him carry something heavy and unspoken, and whatever he was carrying made him obedient. Bo would follow Silas. Which left it all, as Maren had surely calculated, coming down to her.

Everyone looked at Silas.

The falls roared. The spray drifted fine and cold across the shelf. Somewhere upstream, four days hard climbing away, there was a cavern laced with ore worth kingdoms, and a woman standing next to her who had killed to keep it secret once already.

Say it, Silas told herself. Say *we turn around.* Say it now, in front of everyone, and let the chips land where they land.

What came out instead was: "Before anyone votes, there's something you should all hear."

Every head turned. Tam went very still.

* * *

He'd been sleeping with the logbook under his bedroll. Silas had known it for days — known the shape of it through the canvas, known the way he checked for it each morning with a touch too casual to be casual. She'd told herself it wasn't her business. She'd told herself a lot of things weren't her business. That was practically her profession.

Now she looked at him across the wet rock and said, "Tam. The logbook. Read it to them."

"Don't," he said. Just that. One word, cracked down the middle.

"You were going to keep carrying it until it crushed you," Silas said. "I've done that. It doesn't work. Trust me — I'm the best in the territory at it, and it doesn't work."

"Silas—" Bo started.

"Let him speak," Maren said softly, and there it was again, that charm laid over control like frost over a window. "Whatever it is, Tam, we're your friends. Nothing you say changes that."

Silas wanted to laugh. Nothing you say changes that — as if friendship were the thing at stake. As if the thing at stake weren't the truth of a dead man and a drowned camp and a report that had sent Silas running from stillness for the better part of a decade.

Tam knelt. His hands shook getting the oilskin wrap loose. When he lifted the logbook free, water-stained and swollen, pages furred at the edges, he held it against his chest for a moment like a man holding a wound closed.

"I found this at the drowned camp," he said. "In the strongbox under the old mess floor. It's mine. My handwriting. I wrote it — the summer of the first expedition. And I—" His voice snagged. He cleared his throat and started over, quieter, the way men talk when they've decided the falls will cover them if it comes to that. "I'm going to read the last entry. All of it. Because I've read it alone maybe fifty times and every time I found a reason not to show anyone, and I'm done finding reasons."

He opened to the page. The spray drifted. The falls ground on.

"'Ninth day out from the drowned camp,'" Tam read. "'Miss Kessler has instructed me to revise the survey figures before we return downstream. The assay samples from the source cavern came back richer than anything recorded in the territory — she says kingdoms, and she's right, I've held the rock in my hand. But she does not want the extent of it reported. She wants a modest find. Enough to secure her the claim quietly. Not enough to bring competitors, or inspectors, or partners.'"

Someone behind Silas breathed in sharply. One of the hired hands. Good, Silas thought. Let them hear it too.

"'I asked her why,'" Tam went on, and his voice was steadier now, hollowed out, a man reading his own confession from evidence. "'She said: because a secret this size is only safe if it stays small. I told her Harlan would never agree to falsify a government report. She smiled at me — I can see the smile right now, ten years gone — and she said, Harlan won't be agreeing to anything.'"

The words landed on the shelf of rock and lay there.

"'I did it,'" Tam read. "'I rewrote the figures. I told myself I'd correct the record later, when we were safely downstream, when there was distance. Then the retreat went wrong. Then Harlan was dead, and the guide was missing, and Miss Kessler told us all that bandits hit the camp, and I looked at her and I knew — I knew the way you know a thing in your spine — that there had never been any bandits. And I said nothing. I signed the final report. I took my pay. I have been paid every year since, quietly, to stay quiet. This is the ninth day out from the drowned camp, and I am a coward, and I am writing it down so that someday, someone, knows.'"

Tam closed the book.

Nobody moved. The spray drifted through the light like slow snow. Silas became aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat, and of Bo's hand finding her sleeve — not gripping, just touching, an anchor offered rather than imposed.

"You signed it," Odile said at last. Her voice was terrible — not loud, terribly quiet. "Tam. The report. The official one. The one that said the Kessler source was a seep. A nothing. The one that got the valley closed to survey and kept it closed for ten years."

"Yes."

"You let them close the valley. You let Harlan's name get attached to a failed expedition — a *botched* expedition, his fault, sloppy navigation, that's what the record says—"

"I know what it says." Tam's voice broke clean through. "I've read it. I helped write it. Do you think I sleep?"

"No," Odile said. "I think you got paid. Every year. Quietly." She turned away from him, turned toward the river, and Silas watched her shoulders rise and fall twice, three times, an engineer counting her breaths the way she counted loads on a bridge. "God. God, Tam. I grieved for that man. We all grieved. And the whole time the record was a forgery and you were on the payroll."

"I was scared."

"We were all scared! That's what a crew is! Everybody scared together!" Odile rounded on him, and now her voice did crack. "You were supposed to be scared *with* us."

* * *

It came apart after that, the way Silas had always privately known crews come apart — not in one dramatic break but in a hundred small ones, fast, everywhere at once, like ice going out on a spring river.

One of the hired hands — Reyes, the young one, the one who still laughed at his own jokes — stepped back from the group and said, "I want no part of dynamite and forged reports. I'll take the stock trail."

"There is no stock trail," Dray said.

"There damn well might be," Reyes said, "and I'd rather find rockfall than find out what happens to people who know too much on this boat. No offense meant, ma'am," he added, to Maren, which somehow made it worse.

"None taken," Maren said pleasantly. "You'll forfeit your wages."

"My wages." Reyes laughed, high and unhappy. "Ma'am, I've been doing sums since the weir. Wages is the least of what I might forfeit."

Two more of the hands shifted toward him. Dray didn't move to stop them; he just watched, flat and patient, a man who already knew how this arithmetic resolved and was content to wait for it. Maren's money held three. Maybe four. The rest were suddenly, visibly, calculating exits.

And through all of it, Tam stood holding the logbook against his chest, and Bo stood with his hand still on Silas's sleeve, and Odile stood facing the river with her back to all of them, and Maren stood in the middle of the fracture she'd spent ten years engineering, smiling her frost-window smile, saying, "Gently, gently. Everyone breathe. One man's confession, however moving, doesn't change our position. The barge is dead, the trail is closed, and the monsoon is coming whether we argue or not. Whatever Tam did or didn't write ten years ago, today we are still nine people on a rock, and the only road is up."

"It's eight people," Reyes said, "and falling."

"Reyes—"

"And I'll tell you the other sum I've been doing, ma'am." The young man's voice shook, but he planted his feet. "That dynamite. Nobody's said what it's for. Surveyors don't need forty sticks of dynamite. Blasters need it. And blasters blast *after* they've found what they're looking for. So either you already know what's at the source—" he swallowed— "or you mean to make sure nobody else ever sees it. Either way, I'm done."

Silence again. The falls ate it.

Silas looked at Maren. Maren looked back, and for one unguarded instant — half a heartbeat, no more — the charm dropped and Silas saw the thing underneath, the thing that had been underneath all along: not guilt, not fear. *Assessment.* Maren was weighing her, the way she'd weighed Harlan Voss, the way she'd weighed a river and decided which bank to blow out.

Then the smile came back up like a drawbridge.

"Well," Maren said briskly. "Eight, then. Fewer mouths, faster marching. We break camp in an hour. Those staying, see Dray for your loads. Those leaving—" a gracious tilt of her head toward Reyes and the two hands— "the rim trail is that way, and my sincere wishes travel with you."

* * *

They didn't break camp in an hour. They broke it in three, because Odile refused to touch another of Maren's crates until Silas came to her — came alone, in the lee of the old pilings, where the spray couldn't reach — and said the thing she'd been not saying since the letters.

"I read Bo's uncle's letters," Silas said. "All of them. I've known since the fire three nights ago."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Water dripped off the piling between them, keeping small time.

"I know," Odile said. "Bo told me this morning. I've been waiting to see if you'd tell me yourself."

"I'm telling you now."

"Why?" And it wasn't cruel, the question. It was honest, which was worse. "Why now, Silas? Why not at the weir, when we found the bones? Why not the night you watched her burn papers and stood there and said *nothing* to me, to any of us? What finally tipped it?"

Silas looked up the gorge. The slot canyon waited, narrow and gray, running away north into monsoon country. Four days' climb. Somewhere at the end of it, a cavern full of ore and a fuse with Maren's name on it.

"Because Tam read it out loud," she said. "Because he stood up in front of everybody and handed them the worst thing about himself, and the world didn't end, and I thought — if he can do that, maybe I can stop being the last one keeping secrets." She exhaled. It sounded, even to her, like something structural giving way. "I'm sorry, Odile. I should have turned us around at the weir. I should have turned us around at Vell's Landing. I kept telling myself I was protecting the crew, and the truth is I was protecting the version of things where I didn't have to admit I'd led us back to her."

Odile studied her. Then, slowly, she nodded — once, the way she accepted a load rating.

"All right," she said. "Then here's my terms, and they're not negotiable. We go on — because you're right, the barge is dead and the rim trail may be a fairy tale, and I'd rather face Maren in the open than starve on a rockfall. But we go as *our* crew. Not hers. Tam carries his own pack and his own shame, and nobody kicks him off the rope team, because a man who confesses like that is worth more to me than a man who never sinned. Bo navigates. And you—" she jabbed a finger at Silas's chest— "you tell us everything you know, the moment you know it, from here to the source. No more armor. Deal?"

"Deal," Silas said.

They shook on it, spray drifting past, the falls roaring their thousand-year indifference. Behind them, the camp was breaking apart into loads and rope coils and raised voices, and Maren's voice rose over all of it, bright and commanding, arranging her diminished kingdom.

And it was Bo who brought the news that changed the shape of the morning — Bo, jogging down from the high shelf where he'd been scouting the portage route, moving fast, face drained gray under his tan.

"Silas." He caught his breath. "The portage trail. The one Harlan cut, above the falls. Someone's been through recently — fresh cuts on the switchbacks, boot prints, mule sign. Days old, not years." He swallowed. "And there's a cairn at the top of the first pitch that wasn't there on any of the old surveys. Fresh-built. Marking a fork."

"A fork to where?"

Bo looked at her, and Silas saw it in his face before he said it, saw the whole ugly architecture of it assemble itself in his eyes.

"To the source," he said. "Somebody's already racing us. And they're ahead."
