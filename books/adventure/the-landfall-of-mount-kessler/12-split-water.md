## 12. Split Water

Tam had spent eleven years learning how to be invisible, and the first thing invisibility taught him was that nobody watches a man doing paperwork.

Maren Kessler's barge was beached below Kettle Falls now, half-loaded for the overland push, and her documents lived in a locked strongbox in the aft cabin — the same strongbox she'd carried since Vell's Landing, the one she'd fed page by page to the fire on that first night upstream until only a thin residue remained. Tam knew because he'd seen it. He'd said nothing. He'd been saying nothing for so long it had become a kind of profession.

But last night at Kettle Falls, when Odile wanted to turn back and Silas stood there with the wind off the falls flattening her voice into something terrible, Tam had read his own handwriting aloud. *I helped falsify the report. Maren ordered it. I did it.* The words were still hanging in the air of this camp like smoke that wouldn't clear. Silas hadn't spoken to him since. Bo had looked at him once — just once — with an expression Tam couldn't file anywhere, and that was worse than anger.

So: amends. Not the kind you say. The kind you do.

He waited through the evening meal, through Dray's rounds, through the moment Maren's lamp went dark in her tent. Then he moved.

The barge rocked gently against its mooring lines. Inside the aft cabin the air was close and smelled of lamp oil and river damp. The strongbox sat under the chart table where it always sat, and beside it — this was new, this was what made his heart start climbing before he'd even touched anything — a canvas satchel, buckled shut, heavy in the way that paper is heavy when there's a great deal of it.

He knelt. His hands were steady. Eleven years of forging survey reports under a woman's eye will teach your hands steadiness if nothing else does.

The strongbox lock was old brass, older than Maren's money, and Tam had picked locks since he was nine years old in the tideflats of Vell's Landing, back when picking locks was the difference between eating and not. Two minutes. Maybe three. He worked by feel, eyes half-closed, listening to the river and to the camp above — Odile's low voice, the clink of tools being staged for morning, the falls muttering their one long syllable downstream.

The lock gave. He opened the lid.

Bills of lading. A mineral assay stamped with a seal Tam recognized from the first expedition — the seal they'd been ordered to scrub from the official record. Correspondence in Maren's hand, years of it, negotiating with men whose names appeared nowhere in any registry. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, a single dynamite requisition. Forty sticks. Detonators. Fuse. Signed for at a supply post three weeks downriver, drawn against Maren's personal account.

Forty sticks was not survey equipment. Forty sticks was a door, sealed forever.

Tam's mouth went dry. He'd imagined many things about Maren's secrets — fraud, bribery, the ordinary rot of ambitious men and women. This was different. This was her plan to reach the source and blow the cavern mouth behind her, sealing the claim against every soul who might follow, drowning the evidence of what she'd done along with the evidence of what she'd found. The river would keep her secret the way it kept the drowned camp. Forever, or until someone else died finding out.

He heard the plank creak.

Not Odile — Odile walked heel-first, deliberate, a surveyor's walk. This was soft, weight on the balls of the feet, and it was coming up the gangplank.

Dray.

There was no time to close the box, no time to relock it, no time for anything but the satchel. Tam grabbed it, buckled it wrong, and looked around the cabin like a man looking for a door in a room he already knew had one. The stern window. Small, meant for light, not for men. But Tam had been small and quick his whole life, quick enough to survive Maren Kessler's employ for eleven years, which was its own kind of apprenticeship in getting out of rooms.

He got one leg through the window when the cabin door opened.

* * *

Later — much later, lying in the rocks above the camp with blood drying stiff on his ribs — Tam would try to reconstruct those seconds and find them mostly gone. What remained was sensation. The satchel strap catching on the window frame. Dray's hand closing on his ankle with the unhurried grip of a man who had done this many times and never once lost. The world tilting as Tam went backward off the sill, the river swinging up, and then the deck, hard, and Dray's knee in his chest, and Dray's face above him lit by the lamp spilling through the cabin door — calm, faintly regretful, the face of a foreman watching a machine break.

"Maren said you might," Dray said.

"Then she knows I know," Tam managed, "and she'll know you let me see the—"

Dray hit him once, short and professional, somewhere below the ribs where it wouldn't show. The pain went through Tam like a struck bell. He heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize as his own voice.

"Box," Dray said, glancing past him into the cabin. "Open. Satchel gone." He looked back down, and for a moment something almost like sympathy crossed his face, the way weather crosses a flat plain. "You should've stayed a coward, Tam. Cowards live long."

"I've tried," Tam wheezed. "Doesn't take."

He brought his knee up into whatever he could reach and rolled. It wasn't skill; it was panic wearing skill's clothes. They went across the deck together, over a coiled line, into the gunwale. Dray was stronger — Dray was stronger the way a capstan is stronger — but Tam had grown up on decks and Dray had not, and when Tam threw them both sideways into the mooring lines the barge lurched, and the lurch bought him half a second, and half a second was the whole currency of his life.

He went over the side.

The river took him the way it takes everything, without opinion. Cold closed over his head. He heard, muffled and enormous, Dray shouting behind him, and then nothing but water and the satchel dragging at his shoulder like an anchor made of paper. He surfaced thirty yards down, gasping, and clawed for the bank, and the bank gave him mud and root and no footing at all, and so he let the river carry him further, around the bend, out of the lamplight, until his feet found gravel shallows and he crawled out among the stones like something the water had decided not to keep.

* * *

He lay there a while. That was honest. He lay in the shallows with the satchel hugged to his chest and shook, and the shaking wasn't cold, or wasn't only cold, and somewhere in it he understood that he had just become a man Maren Kessler would hunt, and that there was exactly one direction left to walk: toward the people he had betrayed, carrying the proof of it.

The camp was a quarter mile upstream. He could see its fires through the trees, small and orange and impossibly far away. Every step was going to cost him. He counted the costs the way Odile had taught the crew to count rapids — look first, commit second, don't stop in the middle.

Ribs: bad. Probably cracked, maybe worse. Each breath came with a fine white wire of pain through his side.

Blood: his own, from where the deck or the rocks had opened his scalp, sticky down his neck, cooling fast in the night air.

Satchel: intact. He checked it three times in the dark, by touch, like a man checking a child.

And the fourth cost, the one he couldn't count until he paid it: Silas Pemberton's face when he walked into her firelight bleeding, carrying the truth she'd spent eleven years trying not to carry.

He stood up. The river pushed past him toward the falls, patient, going where it went. Tam put his shoulder to the pain and started walking.

* * *

They saw him before he reached the firelight — of course they did; Silas had posted watches the moment the crew split from Maren's party, and Bo was on first watch, and Bo came down through the trees with a lantern held high and a coil of rope over one shoulder like a man expecting trouble and disappointed to find only Tam.

"God," Bo said, stopping dead. "Tam."

"Is she awake?" Tam's voice came out shredded. "Silas. Is she awake?"

Bo looked at him for a long moment — the same unreadable look from Kettle Falls, and now Tam thought he could read it after all, and what it said was: *you're finally worth looking at.* Then Bo turned and shouted up the slope, "Silas! Odile! Get up!"

They came fast. Silas first, coat thrown over her shirt, hair loose, a knife already in her hand out of pure habit — and when she saw what he was, the knife didn't lower so much as change purpose, angling slightly, as if the danger had relocated. Odile came behind her with a blanket and the particular frown she wore when assessing damage to structures.

"He caught me at the strongbox," Tam said. Words were spilling out of him now, all of them, eleven years' worth backing up behind tonight's. "The documents. There's an assay from the first expedition — the real one, the one we buried — and correspondence, years of it, names that aren't in any registry. And a requisition." He fumbled the satchel open with blood-slick fingers and pulled the oilcloth bundle free and held it out to Silas like a man surrendering a weapon. "Dynamite. Forty sticks. Detonators. She signed for it three weeks ago under her own account."

Silas took the bundle. She didn't open it. She looked at it the way she'd looked at the drowned camp, and then she looked at Tam, and her face did something he had never seen it do in all the years he'd known her: it softened, and hardened, both at once, grief and fury arriving in the same instant and refusing to choose between themselves.

"She means to seal it," Tam said. "The source. Blow the cavern mouth behind her and drown the claim — drown the whole truth of it — and let the river keep her secret. Same as the weir. Same as the camp." His legs chose that moment to inform him they were done, and he sat down hard on a rock, and Odile was suddenly there with the blanket around his shoulders, her hands brisk and impersonal and gentler than her face. "She'll move at first light. Before the monsoon. She has to — the flood will close the canyon, and if she's inside it when the charge goes—"

"Then she doesn't care if her own crew comes out," Odile said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd done the arithmetic already; Tam could see it running behind her eyes, the numbers of men and hours and water.

"No," Tam said. "She never has."

Silence, except the falls, except the river. Bo crouched by the fire and fed it a branch and the flames climbed and threw everyone's shadows tall against the trees.

"Dray saw my face," Tam said. "He knows I took the satchel. By morning Maren knows exactly what I have and exactly where I am." He laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway, because the alternative was something he refused to do in front of these people. "So I've made myself useful at last. Bait."

"You're not bait," Silas said.

It was the first thing she'd said to him directly since Kettle Falls, and it landed in his chest harder than Dray's fist had. He looked up at her. Rain-smell was coming into the air now, faint but unmistakable, the high country sending its warning down ahead of the storm itself, and Silas stood in it with the oilcloth bundle under her arm like a woman holding a verdict.

"They're ahead of us," she said, to all of them, to the fire, to the mountain. "Half a day, maybe less, and they're not slowing. If she seals that source—" Her jaw worked. "Everything those men died protecting gets buried twice. Once by her, once by the water."

"So we go up," Bo said. "Tonight. Now."

"In the dark? On those switchbacks?" Odile's voice was flat with disbelief. "Silas, the trail above Kettle Falls is barely a trail in daylight. One slip in the dark and we lose someone to the canyon before Maren spends a single stick of dynamite."

"And if we wait for dawn," Silas said, "we lose it anyway." She turned. Her eyes found Tam's, and held, and what was in them wasn't forgiveness — he hadn't earned forgiveness, and some part of him understood she knew that better than he did — but it was something adjacent to it, something that had decided to stand next to him in the dark regardless. "How badly are you hurt?"

"Cracked rib. Maybe two. Scalp." He shrugged, and immediately regretted the shrug. "Nothing that stops me walking."

"Can you navigate in the dark?"

That was the question, and they both knew it. Navigation was his trade, his guilt, his whole biography compressed into a single skill. He thought about the switchbacks above the falls, the slot canyons beyond, the route he'd mapped on the first expedition and re-mapped in his nightmares ever since.

"Yes," he said. "If I lead, and nobody argues with me about the pace."

"Nobody argues," Silas said. She looked around the fire — Odile with her arms crossed, Bo with the rope still on his shoulder, the firelight making her face into something carved and certain. "We go up tonight. We beat her to the source or we die trying, and either way, when the monsoon makes landfall, we won't be standing in her shadow wondering."

Odile exhaled through her nose — the sound she made instead of arguing. Bo was already kicking dirt over the fire.

Tam stayed sitting a moment longer, the blanket around his shoulders, his ribs ringing, the river talking its endless talk below. Eleven years he'd carried the forged report like a stone sewn inside him, and tonight he'd finally set it down, and setting it down had cost him blood and a beating and the last bridge back to the life he'd been living, which had never really been a life, only a long way of not stopping.

He got up. The pain came with him. He let it.

"Lead on, navigator," Silas said.

And Tam, bleeding, forgiven-adjacent, pointed up into the black throat of the canyon where the rain was beginning to fall, and led.
