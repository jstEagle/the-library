## 13. Storm Signs

Odile felt the weather change in her teeth first. She'd grown up in the delta country where her father read storms off the water like a ledger, and thirty years later the old arithmetic still ran: pressure dropping, air gone thick and sweet, the river's pitch climbing a half-tone overnight. She stood on the shingle at dawn with a tin mug of chicory going cold in her hand and watched the clouds stack themselves against the western rim of the canyon — not the scattered white of fair weather but a solid, bruised wall, building altitude by the hour.

Three days. Maybe less if the wall moved faster than it looked.

She did what she always did when the world threatened to become arithmetic she didn't like: she checked the numbers twice. The barometer she'd salvaged from the barge — *her* barge, once, before Maren Kessler bought the debt out from under her — had fallen a full inch since yesterday evening. The river had come up four inches overnight, muddy now, carrying twigs and torn leaves from country they hadn't reached yet. Upstream water meant upstream rain, already falling somewhere above them in the high country where the Kessler was born.

Four inches in one night. In monsoon country that number didn't stay small. It doubled.

"Engineer."

Dray's voice behind her. She didn't turn around. Of all of Maren's hired muscle, Dray was the only one who'd learned to say her title without sneering, which made him more dangerous, not less.

"Maren wants the portage crews double-timed today," he said. "She wants the falls camp struck and everyone moving by noon."

"The falls camp sits on the only flat ground within six miles," Odile said. "You strike it, you're sleeping on scree."

"She knows."

"Then she's not thinking about the ground. She's thinking about the clock." Odile finally turned. Dray stood with his thumbs hooked in his belt, watching the sky with an expression she recognized — the look of a man who'd signed on for money and was beginning to suspect the currency might be devaluing. Good. Let him do the same math she was doing. "Tell her the gear goes slower wet. Tell her the rope lines go slack in rain. Tell her I said three days."

"You tell her."

"I don't talk to my employer through her bodyguard."

Something flickered across Dray's face — not anger, something wearier. He looked at the river a long moment. "It's early," he said. Not a question. A man checking his own work.

"It's early," Odile confirmed. "Two weeks, maybe three. Which means somebody upstream is about to make very bad decisions very fast, and we're standing in the drain."

Dray left without another word. Odile drank her cold chicory and watched the wall of cloud build, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the drowned camp: *we should turn back.*

Every competent instinct she owned said it plainly. Retreat downriver while the gorge still allowed it. Abandon the survey, abandon Maren's money, abandon the whole rotten enterprise, and let the flood sort out who deserved what. Tam would live. Bo's shoulder would heal at sea level. Silas —

Silas would never forgive any of them, and worse, she'd be right.

Odile turned the problem over the way she turned everything: as load-bearing structure. What held the retreat up? The barge was wrecked at Kettle Falls; they'd need to walk out along the canyon rim, four days at best pace with the gear they couldn't leave, and the first day's walk was exposed benchland with no shelter. If the storm caught them there, they died tired instead of drowned. What held staying up? Nothing, except that turning back meant Maren won, and Odile had spent eleven years telling herself she'd paid for Maren's win already, in full, and she'd be damned if she'd pay interest too.

That was the honest arithmetic. She wrote it down anyway, because writing made lies visible: *Retreat: survivable, dishonorable. Advance: possibly fatal, possibly the only thing worth doing.*

* * *

The crew gathered at midday under the overhang where they'd cached the lightened gear — Silas, Tam, Bo with his arm strapped tight, and Odile herself, while twenty yards downstream Dray and two of Maren's other men pretended to repack crates they'd already packed. Maren was upriver with the vanguard party, pushing toward the source with the dynamite and the best of the provisions. She'd left them behind like ballast she no longer needed, and everyone at the fire knew it.

Odile laid it out flat, because they deserved flat.

"Barometer's dropped an inch. River rose four inches overnight and it'll double tonight. Monsoon's making landfall two weeks early." She held up three fingers. "Three days until this canyon starts flooding in earnest. Four days to walk out along the rim, if nothing goes wrong, and something always goes wrong. So the retreat window closed sometime yesterday while we were sleeping."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, louder than it had been a week ago.

"So we're here," Odile went on. "Which means the real question isn't whether we can leave. It's what we do with the time we've got." She pulled out the map she'd been drafting since dawn — the canyon above them, drawn from memory and Tam's notes and the drowned guide's route marks they'd recovered at the weir. "Maren's party is eight hours ahead of us with the explosives. Her plan, whatever she tells her men, is to reach the source, take her samples, set her charges, and seal the approach behind her so nobody can follow or contest the claim. She doesn't need to get out ahead of the flood. She just needs the source sealed before it comes."

"And us?" Tam asked quietly.

"We can't beat her to the source. Eight hours is eight hours." Odile tapped the canyon walls on either side of the route. "But look at the ground. From here up to the source, the canyon narrows three times. Three slots, each one barely wider than a wagon. When the flood comes down, those slots don't pass water — they *fire* it. Thirty-foot bore, moving faster than a horse can gallop. Anything in the narrows when it hits is gone."

Bo leaned forward, wincing as his strap pulled. "You're saying we let her get into the narrows."

"I'm saying she's already going in. We don't have to push her." Odie met Silas's eyes across the fire. This was the part she'd rehearsed all night, the part she knew would cost her. "We follow. High on the rim, fast, above the flood line. We let the monsoon do the fighting. By the time Maren sets her charges, the water will already be coming down the canyon behind her. She seals the source, and then the seal seals *her*. No way out but up and over, in a storm, with supplies sized for eight hours of margin she no longer has. And the claim —" Odile spread her hands. "A claim needs access to be worth anything. A source sealed inside a flooded canyon, reachable one month a year at risk of death, with a documented history of sabotage and fraud attached to it? Nobody finances that. The river takes the claim away from her better than any magistrate could. Permanently."

Silas hadn't spoken. She sat with her forearms on her knees, looking into the fire, and Odile watched her run the same calculation she had — except Silas's version had a dead mentor standing in the middle of it.

"There's a flaw," Silas said finally.

"One."

"If we're on the rim when the bore comes through, we're fine. If we're anywhere else, we're dead. And the rim above the second narrows is a scramble even in dry weather. You're asking five people, one of them one-armed, to move fast through storm country on terrain that kills people in sunshine."

"Yes," Odile said. "I am."

"And the alternative is walking out on exposed benchland in front of a storm front that moves faster than we do, and dying tired instead of drowned." Silas looked up. There was something in her face Odile hadn't seen since the first expedition — not the armored flatness, not the ranger's blank calm, but an actual decision being born. "I've done the retreat. Eleven years ago, I did the retreat, and it worked, and everybody I loved was still dead at the end of it." She stood. "We go up."

Tam exhaled — Odile couldn't tell if it was relief or terror. Probably both. "I'll want to recheck your flood timings," he said. "If the second slot fires early—"

"It'll fire early," Odile agreed. "Everything about this monsoon will be early. That's why we leave within the hour."

Bo laughed, short and disbelieving, and then winced and pressed his good hand against his strapped arm. "My uncle used to say the Kessler gives you two choices every time, and both of them are the river."

"Your uncle also died choosing wrong," Odile said, and immediately regretted it — grief wasn't a thing you spent to win arguments. But Bo didn't flinch. He looked at her steadily, and there was something in his face that was older than his years, some inheritance passed down in letters and silence.

"No," Bo said. "He died choosing right. He just chose it alone." He looked around the fire. "We're not alone. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Downstream, one of Dray's men had stopped pretending to pack crates. He was watching them, and Odile made a note of his face — young, uncertain, doing his own arithmetic — because in three days' time that face might matter. Men followed Maren for money. Money was about to stop meaning anything in this canyon. What replaced it was an open question, and questions like that could be answered from either direction.

* * *

They broke camp in forty minutes, which Odile noted with professional pride — eleven years ago this same crew had needed two hours and a shouting match. They climbed the first rim trail in single file, Silas leading, Tam navigating off his recovered logbook and Odile's sketch map, Bo carrying the rope coils despite his arm because he refused to be carried like cargo. The air was hot and close, pressing down like a lid. Ahead of them the canyon walls rose sheer and red, and above the walls the cloud bank had begun, at last, to move.

Not drifting. Advancing.

By midafternoon the light had gone strange — yellow-brown, shadowless, the color of old brass. The wind came in gusts that smelled of wet stone and ozone, then died to nothing, then gusted again from a different quarter entirely, which was the worst sign of all. Odile had lived through two landfalls in the delta. She remembered the way the world seemed to hold its breath before it, the way sensible animals vanished, the way even the insects went quiet. The canyon had begun to go quiet now. The birds had stopped. Somewhere below them the river kept talking to itself, lower and hoarser than it should have been, gathering its argument.

They made the first overlook at dusk and Odile called the halt, though every hour counted, because marching the rim in the dark was how people fell off things. From the overlook they could see downcanyon — and what Odile saw stopped her breath.

Far below, at the limit of sight, the river had changed character. It wasn't rising anymore. It was *arriving.* A brown seam had appeared in the channel, wide as a road, moving with a purposefulness that no natural current possessed — the leading edge of the first flush, days of high-country rain arriving all at once, and behind it, invisible past the bend, everything else.

"That's not tomorrow," Tam said softly. He had his logbook open, though the light was nearly gone. "That's tonight. Or the small hours. The main bore follows the first flush — hours, not days."

Odile checked her watch, then the sky, then recalculated everything she'd told them at the fire. The numbers shifted under her hands like wet rock. Three days became something smaller. She wouldn't say the new number aloud yet, not until she was sure, not until she'd watched another hour of that seam crawling upcanyon. But she knew, the way she knew load tolerances and frost dates, that the margin had just been spent.

Behind them, thunder spoke for the first time — a long, rolling statement from the west, less a sound than a change in the atmosphere's mind.

Silas stood at the edge of the overlook with the last light on her face, looking upcanyon toward the source she'd sworn off for eleven years. The wind pulled at her coat. When she spoke, she didn't raise her voice; she didn't need to. The canyon had gone too quiet.

"How far to the second narrows?" she asked.

"Half a night's climb," Odile said. "Above it, the rim levels out. We can move fast."

"Then we don't wait for full dark to fall. We move now." Silas turned, and Odile saw that the decision born at the fire had finished itself. "Maren thinks the flood is her ally — something that seals her claim behind her. She hasn't understood yet that the flood doesn't take sides." A ghost of something crossed her face, not quite a smile. "Time we introduced them properly."

They shouldered their packs. Below them the brown seam widened, patient as arithmetic, and overhead the first drops hit the stone — fat, warm, spaced far apart, each one landing with a sound like a footstep.

Odile took one last reading of the barometer, wrote the figure in her log without comment, and closed the case on it. Some numbers you recorded so that afterward, whoever came looking would know exactly when the choice was made.

The rain found its rhythm. The crew began to climb.
