## 14. The Ascent

The canyon took them at dawn, and it did not take them kindly.

Silas went first, as she always did, testing each foothold with her boot before trusting it. The slot canyons above Kettle Falls were a maze of red sandstone cut thin as a knife wound — places where the Kessler's ancestors had torn through the plateau on their way down from the highlands, leaving corridors barely wide enough for a man's shoulders. In dry weather they were a shortcut. In rain they were coffins.

The rain had started an hour ago. Not the monsoon yet — not the full weight of it — but a fine, needling drizzle that slicked the sandstone and turned every handhold into a question.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Tam said behind her, breath ragged. He'd tied his recovered logbook inside his shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, and Silas could see him touching it through the fabric every few minutes like a man checking a wound.

"Because Maren's party left camp four hours ahead of us," Silas said, "and because if she reaches the source before we do, there won't be a source to reach. Just a crater and a sealed door and thirty years of lies set in stone."

"And because turning back means climbing down that," Bo added from the rear.

They all looked back. Below them the canyon floor dropped away in terraces of broken rock, and beyond it the barge sat abandoned at Kettle Falls, looking small and foolish as a toy boat left in a bath. The river roared over the falls with a voice that was already changing — deeper than yesterday, hungrier. Odile had heard it first, standing on the deck with her head cocked like a bird's, and said, *The river's swallowing its own sound. That's flood water coming down from the highlands.*

Three days, she'd calculated. Maybe less now, with the rain.

Silis climbed.

The route Odile had charted threaded between two slot canyons — the wide one Maren's party would take, hauling their dynamite crates up the gentler grade, and this one: narrow, vertical in places, brutal, but fast for anyone willing to trust it. Silas had been willing. She'd spent years being willing to trust nothing but her own hands and the ground directly beneath them.

"Chimney section ahead," she called back. "Keep your pack tight. If you slip, push off the wall — don't grab at the rock."

"I love how calm you make that sound," Tam muttered.

She found the first handhold and pulled herself into the crack. The walls pressed close enough that she could brace her back against one side and walk her feet up the other, the way she'd learned as a girl on the mesa country south of nowhere in particular, back when climbing was a game and falling was something that happened to other people. The drizzle ran down the stone in threads. Somewhere above, water was already gathering, pooling on the plateau, looking for ways down.

*Don't think about flash flood,* she told herself. *Think about Maren.*

That was easier. Thinking about Maren came naturally these days, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. She thought about Dray's face when Tam had stumbled bleeding into camp two nights ago, the hired muscle standing too casually by Maren's tent. She thought about the dynamite — twelve cases, Odile estimated, enough to bring half the cavern down and bury the ore veins under a hundred tons of rock. She thought about Maren's voice across the fire, warm as brandy: *You were always the best of us, Silas. That's why I need you to understand what I'm doing.*

She understood perfectly. That was the problem.

The chimney opened onto a ledge no wider than a coffin lid, and Silas hauled herself out onto it and lay flat, breathing, while the others came up behind her. Bo emerged last, moving with an ease that still surprised her — the big man folding himself through the crack like he'd been born in one. He crouched beside her on the ledge, water dripping off his jaw, and looked back the way they'd come.

"Storm's building," he said quietly. "West."

She followed his gaze. Beyond the canyon rims, the sky over the highlands had gone the color of wet slate, and lightning flickered inside the cloud without sound, like a lamp being tested behind a curtain.

"Odile gave us three days," Silas said.

"Odile gave us three days yesterday." Bo wiped his face with his sleeve. "My uncle used to say the mountains don't count days. They count reasons."

"Your uncle said a lot of things."

"Yeah." Bo smiled, and it was a strange, sad thing, that smile — grief and inheritance tangled together. "I keep finding out which ones were true."

* * *

They made the second mile in silence, because the canyon allowed nothing else. The walls narrowed until they walked sideways, packs scraping stone, and the light dimmed to a bruised purple thread overhead. Silas counted turns the way Odile had taught her — left, right, right again, marking each junction with a scratch of her knife where the route bent toward the upper drainage. Behind her she could hear Tam's breathing and Bo's steady footfalls, and beneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sound of water running along the canyon floor.

It hadn't been there an hour ago.

She didn't say anything. Saying it would make it real, and they couldn't afford real yet. They needed another hour, maybe two, to reach the saddle where the slots opened onto the highland shelf — and from there, a hard climb to the source cavern before Maren finished whatever ritual of ownership she was performing with her dynamite and her documents and her terrible, patient certainty.

The water sound grew anyway. Water doesn't care what you can afford.

At the third junction the canyon split, and Silas stopped, hand raised. The left fork climbed; the right fork dropped toward a lower gallery where the walls pinched to almost nothing. And in the dim light, on the smooth sandstone at eye level, someone had scratched a mark.

An arrow. Fresh. Pointing left.

"Maren's scouts?" Tam whispered.

"No." Silas touched the groove. The stone dust still clung to her fingertip. "Scouts mark their own route. This is meant for us." She looked at Bo. "Your uncle's letters. Did he ever write about signals? Marks the crew used?"

Bo went very still. Then he reached past her and traced the arrow's tail with one thick finger — a small double-scratch at its base, almost invisible unless you knew to look.

"That's his," Bo said. His voice had gone rough. "He always doubled the tail. Said a single line was an opinion, but a double line was a promise." He swallowed. "He marked this for us. Before he died, or—"

"Or someone who learned his marks," Silas finished.

Nobody wanted to finish that sentence either. But Silas was already thinking it: *the guide.* The dead guide in the portage of bones, who had died protecting the source's location. Men who died protecting things sometimes left messages first. Or — and this thought she kept in a locked room at the back of her mind — maybe the guide wasn't the only ghost walking this canyon.

They took the left fork.

It climbed steeply, switchbacking up a crack in the wall where iron rungs had been driven into the stone decades ago — old expedition hardware, rusted orange, weeping rust-streaks down the rock like old blood. Silas tested each rung with her full weight before letting the others follow. Halfway up, the drizzle thickened. Drops began striking the stone around them with individual, deliberate sounds, like fingers tapping.

Then the thunder came. Not overhead — upstream, deep in the highlands, a long rolling boom that she felt through the rungs in her hands more than heard.

Tam stopped climbing. "That's not weather," he said. "That's a door closing."

"Keep moving."

* * *

They topped out onto the highland shelf as the rain arrived in earnest.

Not monsoon — not yet — but a hard, slanting rain with weight behind it, drumming on the sandstone, filling the potholes, turning the shelf into a shallow braided stream. Ahead of them the canyon country fell away into gray murk, and somewhere in that murk, higher, the source waited. Silas could feel it the way she felt weather in her bad knee: a pull, a pressure, a wrongness in the shape of the land.

And then the ambush came, because of course it did. Because Maren Kessler had never in her life left a door unguarded.

Dray stepped out from behind a fin of rock twenty yards ahead, and even at that distance Silas saw the rifle, and even through the rain she saw the smile.

"Took you long enough," he called. "Boss said you'd come the hard way. Boss is usually right about people."

Silas put herself between Dray and the others, low, weight forward, rain streaming off her hat brim. "Three against one, Dray."

"Two against one." He jerked his chin past them, and Silas twisted — too late — to see the second man coming up the shelf behind them, out of a slot they'd passed without checking, a big silent shape with a pry bar in his fist. Maren hadn't left a door unguarded. She'd left two.

"Walk away," Dray said. "Boss's offer stands. Triple pay, same as her. She figures you're worth it. Frankly I figure you're worth more trouble than gold, but I don't set wages."

"You set bones," Silas said. "You did it to Tam."

"He signed the first report. He can sign a bandage." Dray shifted the rifle, casual, terrible. "Last chance, ranger. Walk down the hill. Storm's coming, and the boss wants witnesses gone before it does."

The world narrowed to the space between heartbeats. Silas measured the distance — forty feet of slick rock, a rifle that outranged anything she carried, rain degrading everyone's aim equally. Behind her, Tam was easing backward toward cover. Bo was—

Bo was already moving.

He didn't announce it. He just broke left, huge and fast, cutting across the open shelf toward the flank of rocks where the second man stood, and the whole geometry of the standoff shattered. The second man swung the pry bar; Bo caught it on his shoulder with a grunt that Silas felt in her own chest, wrapped his arms around the man, and drove him backward —

— backward off the shelf edge, onto the sloped apron of stone below, and the rain-slick rock did what rain-slick rock does. Both men went down in a tangle. The second man rolled free and scrambled away into the murk. Bo caught himself on the slope, caught the edge of the shelf, held —

— and the ledge under his hands let go.

It came away whole, a slab the size of a door, undermined by years of water working through the joint, and Bo went with it. Silas was moving before she decided to move, dropping to her belly at the shelf edge, arm plunging down into the rain and the roar. Her hand closed on his wrist — his huge wrist, barely half her grip around it — and for one moment she held him, his weight swinging free over the drop, his boots finding no purchase on the wet stone.

Their eyes met. Rain hammered between them.

"Silas." His voice was calm. That was the worst part, how calm it was. "The climb. Don't stop for me."

"The hell I won't—"

"The slab's going. It's going *now*, Silas—" And it was; she felt it shift under her knees, the whole apron groaning, and below her Tam was shouting and grabbing her belt, and Bo's weight was pulling her shoulder out of its socket, and the arithmetic was simple and obscene: one man's life weighed against three, weighed against the source, weighed against everything Maren would bury if they failed.

Bo solved the arithmetic himself. He opened his hand.

He fell the way big men fall — straight, quiet, almost dignified — and the darkness below took him, and the slab came down after him with a boom that the thunder swallowed whole.

* * *

Silas didn't remember deciding to climb down. She remembered Tam's arms around her, dragging her back from the edge, both of them screaming things she couldn't hear over the rain. She remembered the drop — fifteen feet, no more, onto a rubble slope where the slab had shattered — and she remembered finding Bo on his side in the wreckage, alive, conscious, his left leg bent where legs don't bend.

"Stop," he said, before she could speak. "Listen. It's a break, not a crush. I've had worse hauling timber." His face was gray as the sky, sweat cutting channels through the rain on his forehead. "Odile can splint it. Odile can fix anything. You get her down here, and you two stay with me, and Tam goes on alone—"

"Nobody goes anywhere alone," Silas said. "That's the whole point. That's the entire lesson of my entire stupid life."

"Then nobody reaches the source," Bo said, "and Maren wins, and my uncle's grave stays a lie forever." He gripped her forearm, his grip still strong, infuriatingly strong. "Silas. Listen to me. My uncle wrote that a crew isn't people who never leave each other. It's people who know *which* leaving is the right one." His breath hitched. "This is the right one. Go."

She got Odile down the slope — Odile, who climbed through the rain with her jaw set and her eyes bright and terrifying, who knelt in the wreckage and splinted Bo's leg with a strut from her pack and strips of shirt and never once let her hands shake. Tam stayed above, anchoring ropes, watching the murk where the second man had fled, watching everywhere at once.

"It's a clean fracture," Odile said finally. She sat back on her heels, soaked through, and looked up at Silas, and Silas saw the calculation finish behind her eyes — the same arithmetic, done by a different mind, arriving at the same obscene answer. "He can't climb. Carrying him costs us a day. A day is the monsoon." She exhaled. "I'll stay. I built the first expedition's medical kit; I can build a shelter out of this rubble that'll hold. When the flood comes, we go downriver together — there's a route through the lower slots, I mapped it years ago. We'll be at Vell's Landing before you're back."

"You don't know I'm coming back."

Odile smiled, and it was Bo's smile, grief and inheritance tangled. "No," she said. "But I know you."

They rigged a shelter in the lee of the fallen slab — a lean-to of rubble and oilcloth, a fire in a hollow of stone that the rain couldn't reach. Bo insisted on giving Silas his uncle's letters, the oilcloth packet, pressing it into her hands with both of his.

"You'll need the true parts," he said. "For Maren. For whoever asks after."

Silas took the packet. It weighed nothing and everything.

At the base of the final pitch — a crack in the canyon wall climbing two hundred feet toward the highland plateau, streaming with rainwater now, a waterfall in miniature — Silas stopped and looked back once. Bo lay propped against the stone, leg splinted, firelight catching his face. He raised one hand. Not a wave. A salute.

Above her, Tam was already climbing, small and determined against the gray.

Silas spat on her palms, took the first wet hold, and went up.

The rain followed her, harder now, and far upstream — so far it might have been imagination — she heard the monsoon clear its throat.
