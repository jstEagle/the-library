## 15. Source of the Kessler

The rain found her an hour before the source did.

It came first as a whisper on the stone above — a sound like sand poured over a drumhead — and then as fat, cold drops threading down through the slot canyon, striking the back of Silas's neck, the backs of her hands, the iron head of the climbing hammer slung at her hip. She didn't stop. Stopping was a habit she'd spent ten years unlearning, and she wasn't going to relearn it now, four hundred feet of wet sandstone above the river, with Tam somewhere below her on the switchbacks and Bo lying broken on a ledge two miles back with Odile kneeling over him.

*Keep moving. Grieve later.* That had been Odile's instruction, delivered in the flat voice she used for load calculations and verdicts. *If you stop, we all stop. If we all stop, we die here.*

So Silas climbed.

The canyon narrowed as it rose, walls closing in until she could press her palms to both sides at once, the rock slick and breathing cold. Water ran in threads down every face now, and the threads were thickening. Somewhere above, the sky was opening its throat. She had maybe a day. Maybe less. Odile had said three, but Odile had said that before the storm front came down the valley a night early like a debt collector.

She thought about Bo's fall. She thought about it the way you worry a cracked tooth — constantly, uselessly, with a dull sick pleasure in the hurt. The ledge had given way under him the way ledges do, all at once, no negotiation. He'd had time for exactly one look at her, one shove that put her back from the edge and himself over it, and then forty feet of bouncing, screaming descent into the scree. He'd been conscious when they reached him. He'd been laughing, actually — a thin, shocked laugh, blood in his teeth — and he'd said, *Uncle Aldous would've done the same thing and then written a very boring letter about it,* and Odile had told him to shut up and save his breath, and he had, because he was Bo, and Bo did what Odile told him when it mattered.

*If he dies,* Silas thought, gripping a hold that crumbled and finding another, *it's because Maren Kessler wanted a hole in the ground.*

She climbed faster.

The canyon kinked left, then right, then opened without warning into a bowl of stone where the rain fell freely and the air changed — thickened, sweetened, carrying a smell she hadn't smelled in eleven years and had never forgotten. Wet iron. Green water. The particular mineral breath of the high country, the smell of the place where the Kessler River was born.

She'd told herself she didn't remember it. She'd been lying.

The bowl narrowed again to a throat of rock, and through the throat she could hear it: water. Not falling, not rushing. *Rising.* A deep, patient upwelling sound, like breath drawn through sleep, and beneath it a faint ringing she couldn't place until she was through the throat and standing in the cavern mouth and the ringing resolved into the sound of water striking stone in the dark, drop by drop by drop, a clock that had been running since before there were people to count it.

Silas stood at the edge of the light and looked at the source of the Kessler.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word her mind would accept. The cavern opened upward into a chimney a hundred feet high, and the rain was finding its way down that chimney in silver ropes, striking a pool that filled the floor of the place — a pool the color of bottle glass, so clear she could see the pale bones of the rock beneath it, and so deep that the bones faded into blue-black mystery ten feet down. The water rose from somewhere under that darkness, fed by snowmelt and deep rain and whatever slow underground patience had carved this room out of the mountain's heart. Where the water overflowed, it spilled across a lip of stone in a wide, silent sheet — the first water of the Kessler River, beginning its three-hundred-mile run to the flats where Silas had spent her life chasing horse thieves and outrunning her own name.

And the walls — God, the walls.

The lantern-light she'd brought caught them and they answered with color. Veins of ore ran through the sandstone in branching rivers of their own: copper gone green as river-moss, silver threads bright as fish, and through it all, everywhere, the dull gold gleam of something rarer, something that made her breath go shallow even though she'd known — she'd known since the letters, since the drowned camp, since the bones on the portage — what was up here. The ore didn't look like wealth. It looked like a wound that the mountain had spent a million years trying to heal over and never quite managed, and men had looked at it and seen money, and men had died for it, and the mountain had kept it all the same.

*Beautiful and damning.* That was what Bo had called it, reading his uncle's letters by the fire, and Bo had been right, and Silas hated that he'd been right, and she stood in the mouth of the source and let it be beautiful anyway, because refusing to see it wouldn't bring Bo's legs back or raise the drowned camp or put breath into the guide's bones on the portage.

"You're late."

The voice came from the dark at the far side of the pool, and Silas's hand went to her sidearm before her mind caught up — and stopped, because she already knew. There was only one person alive who could make three words sound like a dinner-party rebuke while standing in a flooded cave at the top of the world.

Maren Kessler stepped out of the shadows with a lantern held high.

She looked, Silas thought, terrible. Not weak — Maren would never be weak, not if you flayed her — but *spent*, the way a river looks spent when it's been running hard over rock for days. Her clothes were torn at one knee, crusted with mud to the hip. Her hair had come loose from its braid and hung in wet ropes. There was a cut above her eyebrow, crusted and ignored. But her eyes were the same eyes that had looked at Silas across a card table eleven years ago and across a contract three weeks ago: bright, amused, and utterly without mercy, including for herself.

"Dray?" Silas said.

"Downstream. He had an argument with a scree slope and lost." Maren smiled thinly. "He'll live. He's tougher than he looks and stupider than he is tough. I'd have brought him, but I wanted to talk to you alone, and Dray's idea of alone is standing six feet away with a rifle."

"Where's your crew?"

"I sent them back at Kettle Falls with the mules and a story." Maren set the lantern on a dry shelf of rock. The light climbed the far wall, and the ore veins caught it and burned. "I don't like witnesses to my better decisions."

Silas came forward along the pool's edge, boots finding the stone automatically, her eyes doing the thing ranger's eyes do — sweeping, cataloguing. And that was when she saw them, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Crates. Three of them, stacked against the wall, lids off. Sticks of dynamite in neat, terrible rows. Wire running from the crates along the base of the wall, coiled and waiting, and — her eyes followed it — up, into the chimney, to a cluster of charges already placed in the rock thirty feet above the pool, taped and wedged and ready, directly beneath the throat of stone where the rain came down.

Directly beneath the overhang that held the cavern mouth open.

"You're going to bring it down," Silas said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "The mouth. The whole throat. You're going to bury it."

"I'm going to *seal* it," Maren corrected gently. "Burying is what happens by accident. This is engineering." She gestured at the crates with something like pride. "Odile would appreciate the charge placement, if she were here. I did the math myself. The mouth collapses, the chimney fills, the pool rises, and in fifty years this is a spring in a cliff face with no way in. No way in, Silas. The claim becomes worthless because the ore becomes *unreachable* — except by me, because I have the survey coordinates, the assay records, and eleven years of patience. When the technology exists to get at it profitably — and it will, it always does — I'll be the only one holding the key."

"You'd drown it." Silas looked at the pool, at the clean water rising out of the dark, at the first water of the river that gave life to everything from the highlands to the flats. "You'd drown the source of the Kessler."

"The Kessler will survive." Maren shrugged. "Rivers always do. They reroute. They find another way down. It's what I've always admired about them." She smiled. "It's what I always admired about you."

Silas's hand was still near her gun. Maren saw it and laughed — a real laugh, short and sharp, echoing off the wet stone.

"Shoot me, then. Go on. You'd be doing the world a favor and yourself a life sentence, and the charges are on a timer anyway, so you'd die in here with me and the river would seal itself for free." She stepped closer, boots splashing at the pool's rim, and her voice dropped, and the amusement went out of it, and what was left underneath was worse — it was *earnest*. "But you're not going to shoot me. You never were. Because you're not a killer, Silas Pemberton. You're a *runner*. And I'm here to offer you the one thing a runner can't refuse."

"Which is?"

"Somewhere to stop."

The word landed in Silas's chest like a bullet in wet wood.

Maren saw it land. Of course she did. Eleven years of reading Silas across card tables and contract negotiations and one catastrophic night on this very river had made Maren Kessler the best reader of Silas Pemberton alive, and she pressed the advantage the way she pressed everything — immediately, completely, without apology.

"I know what you are," Maren said softly. "I've always known. You think you run because of what happened here. You don't. You run because of what you *felt* when it happened — because for one night, one night, you wanted to be the kind of person who could do what I did, and you found out you weren't, and you've been running from that relief ever since. Aldous died and you didn't avenge him. You didn't even *report* me. You just left. And every year since, the leaving gets harder, because the thing you're running from isn't behind you, Silas. It's ahead. It's the day you finally stop and have to sit with what you are."

"Say it," Silas said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "Say what you did."

"I sabotaged the boats." Maren said it plainly, the way she might have reported the weather. "I loosened the lashings at the Drowned Camp and I took the spare oars and I let the river do the rest. Aldous found the ore and wanted to file the claim honestly — a full claim, a *public* claim, with the company and the magistrate and every prospector from here to Vell's Landing holding a piece. Eleven years of work split eleven ways. And I had done the work, Silas. I had found the vein. I had *paid* for the expedition. So I made a decision, and the river made the rest, and I have slept every night since like a well-fed child." She spread her hands. "Because here is the truth nobody wants to say out loud: the world rewarded me. Every single time. Ruthlessness bought me the barge, the crew, the reach, the *this*—" she gestured at the cathedral of stone and water around them, "—and honesty bought Aldous a grave and you a decade of empty flats and Tam a decade of guilt and Odile a decade of shame. I did the math, Silas. I did it eleven years ago and I've never once been wrong."

"And now you want me to do the math with you."

"I want you to *stop*." Maren came closer still, close enough that Silas could see the exhaustion under the paint of her composure, the sleepless nights, the eleven years of it. "Half the claim. Half of everything. You, me, and a river of gold that no one else will ever touch. You'd never chase a horse thief again. Never sleep in a hollow with your boots on. Never have to decide whether the next town is far enough away from the last one." Her voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the cruelest thing she'd done all day. "You could build a house, Silas. You could have a *bed*. You could wake up in the morning and know exactly where you were. All you have to do is stand here, and say nothing, and let me light the fuse, and walk out with me before the monsoon seals the highlands — and seal this place behind us forever. No witnesses. No testimony. No more running."

The rain thickened in the chimney above them. The silver ropes of water came down harder, striking the pool, and the pool rose — Silas could see it, a finger's width up the pale stone of the rim, another finger — and somewhere far below, in the gorge country, the Kessler River was beginning to feel the first push of the flood that would come down like the fist of God and remake every mile of it.

And Silas stood in the mouth of the source of the Kessler, and let herself, for one long, honest moment, *want it*.

That was the thing nobody understood about temptation. It wasn't a devil with a forked tongue. It was a bed. It was a door that locked. It was the end of the arithmetic — no more counting the miles behind her, no more calculating whether the next stop was far enough, no more waking at three in the morning in some waystation with her hand on her gun and her heart going like a trapped bird because in the dream the boats were breaking apart again and Aldous was calling her name in a voice that got younger every year. Maren was offering her the one currency she'd never had and never been able to buy: *stillness.* And she was offering it at the exact moment stillness seemed least possible — with Bo broken on a ledge, with the storm coming down the canyon, with the truth in her pocket like a stone.

*Say nothing. Walk out. Stop running.*

She thought of Bo's laugh, blood in his teeth, on the ledge. *Uncle Aldous would've done the same thing.*

She thought of Tam, reading his own forged handwriting aloud at Kettle Falls, voice shaking, choosing truth with the whole crew watching. She thought of Odile, who had carried the first expedition's shame in her spine for eleven years and set it down somewhere on the portage and stood up straighter. She thought of the waystation they hadn't built yet, the house above the weir that didn't exist, the thing that wasn't a thing yet but had become, over these three weeks, the only future Silas had ever actually wanted — and it wasn't a future you could buy with a dead river's gold. It was a future you could only *build*, with timber and stone and the people who'd chosen you, and every plank of it was mortared with truth.

"You're right about one thing," Silas said.

Maren's eyes sharpened. "Which thing?"

"I'm not a killer." Silas looked at the dynamite, at the wires, at the charges gleaming wet in the chimney above the pool. "And I'm not a runner anymore either. Turns out you can only quit one thing at a time, and I already quit."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — fast, quickly buried, but Silas saw it, and it wasn't anger. It was *loneliness*. Eleven years of sleeping like a well-fed child, and here at the top of the world, with the flood coming, Maren Kessler had wanted — needed — one person to walk out of this cave with her and make the math true.

"You'll die here, then," Maren said quietly. "Or worse. You'll go back down and testify, and they'll take it all, and in twenty years some company with better explosives will take the mountain anyway, and none of this —" she gestured at everything, the ore, the dead, the decade, "— will have *meant* anything."

"It meant Aldous," Silas said. "It meant the guide on the portage. It means Bo. That's what it means, Maren. It was never going to mean money."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The rain came down the chimney in earnest now, drumming the pool, and the pool rose another finger, and the light from the two lanterns wavered on the wet gold veins of the walls like something alive.

Then Maren Kessler smiled — the smile Silas had first seen across a card table eleven years ago, the smile that had preceded every disaster of her adult life — and reached into her coat, and came out with a small brass timer, and turned it over in her fingers like a lady examining a pocket watch.

"Well," she said. "Then I suppose we'd both better hurry."

Her thumb found the plunger on the detonator box at her feet, and Silas lunged for her across the rising water as the first charge, somewhere in the chimney above, began to *sing*.
