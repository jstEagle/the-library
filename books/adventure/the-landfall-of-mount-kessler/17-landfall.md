## 17. Landfall

The water came for us like a debt called in.

I heard it before I saw it — a sound I'd only known from flash floods in the box canyons south of the Salt Line, but scaled up past anything my ears had a right to understand. Not a roar. A pressure. The whole gorge inhaling.

"Get up." I hauled Maren by the collar of her ruined coat. She was still coughing cavern dust and river spray, one arm hanging wrong where the blast had thrown her against the spring's stone lip, and she looked at me with an expression I'd never seen on her face in twenty years of knowing her. It looked almost like surrender. Almost like relief.

"Silas," she said, and then the first wall of water hit the lower chamber and whatever she said after that was swallowed.

The source had been a cathedral. Now it was a throat.

The monsoon didn't arrive — it *landed*, the way a ship lands, hull-first, all at once, with everything aboard. Rain came down the slot canyon in ropes thick as my wrist. The spring that had fed the Kessler for ten thousand years was suddenly a tributary of the sky itself, and every gallon of it wanted downriver, and we were standing in its way.

I got Maren moving. That was the whole of my ambition for the next hour: get Maren moving, get myself moving, get above the flood line or die trying. Her boots slipped on wet ore — the raw veins glittering under my lantern light even now, gold and copper threaded through black stone like something holy, like something that had gotten people killed, like something that would be underwater within the day. She stumbled and I caught her good arm and half-carried her through the side passage Tam and I had climbed, the one Dray hadn't known about because Dray was dead or fled or somewhere behind us in the dark, and I found I didn't care which.

The passage went up in switchbacks cut by nothing but water and time. Behind us the cavern filled. I heard the dynamite's damage give way — the mouth Maren's charge had blown open widening, stone cracking loose, the river claiming the architecture of its own house. The sound was enormous and strangely calm. A door opening, not closing.

Maren looked back once. I didn't.

* * *

We came out onto the shelf of rock above the source as the sky opened completely. No more rain in ropes — rain in sheets, rain as weather, rain as a fact of the world you either built your life around or drowned inside. Lightning walked the ridgeline across the canyon, patient as a surveyor, taking measurements.

I dragged Maren to the highest ground I could find, a granite shoulder maybe forty feet above the new waterline and rising, and put her against a boulder, and checked her arm. Broken below the elbow. Clean break, probably, though clean wasn't a word that applied to much anymore.

"Splint," I said, and tore a strip off the hem of her coat without asking, because Maren Kessler had never once asked my permission for anything and I figured the ledger could balance a little.

She watched me work with those gray eyes that had run crews and ruined lives and signed reports with other men's names. "You could have let the water take me," she said. Her voice was scraped hollow. "Back there. One less thing to carry downriver."

"I've carried worse."

"You haven't." She laughed, and it turned into coughing. "That's the tragedy of you, Silas Pemberton. You carry everything. You think it's strength. It's just a different way of drowning."

"Tie this," I said, and put the splint ends in her teeth, and she bit down, and for a moment there was something almost like the old days — two women in bad country, fixing what was broken, trusting each other with the small things because the large things were beyond saving. Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and she was Maren again, and I was the woman who'd just tackled her into a flood to stop her from blowing up a mountain.

Below us, the source disappeared.

It happened fast, the way everything happens fast when water decides. One minute the cavern mouth was a black arch vomiting white water; the next the arch was gone, submerged, and the river stood over it thirty feet deep and rising, brown and muscular, carrying away the last of Maren's dynamite smoke like it was carrying away a rumor. Somewhere under all that water lay the ore veins. The claim stakes. The documents in their oilskin wrap that Maren had guarded across four hundred miles of river country. The whole secret worth kingdoms.

The river took it the way it took everything. Without malice. Without ceremony. Without caring who had bled for it.

"You understand what you've done," Maren said quietly.

"I do."

"Not the claim. The claim is—" she waved her splinted arm at the flood, a gesture that cost her visibly, "—gone. I understood that the moment the fuse caught and you were still coming at me instead of running. No, Silas. You've done something worse. You've made it so no one owns it. Do you know what that means? Anyone can come. Everyone will come. There'll be no company, no order, no—"

"There'll be a river," I said. "There'll be fish in it. There'll be travelers drinking from it who never have to know what it cost." I sat back on my heels. "You wanted to own the source of the Kessler. Congratulations. You're the last person who ever tried."

Lightning again, closer. The thunder rolled down the canyon walls and came back doubled.

Maren looked at the water for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had changed — smaller, stripped of the boardroom polish, the charm-as-control I'd watched her wield since Vell's Landing. What was left underneath sounded young. Sounded like a girl from a mining town who'd learned early that the world rewarded ruthlessness and buried honesty in unmarked graves.

"My father worked a claim like that," she said. "Smaller. Poorer. He died owning it — died *of* owning it, coughing his lungs out in a tent while the company lawyers picked it off his estate for pennies. I was eleven. I decided that day I would never be the man with the claim. I'd be the woman who owned the company." She shook her head slowly. "And here I am. Drowned by a river. Beaten by a ranger who lets horse thieves go."

"You knew about that?"

"I know everything about you, Silas. That was always my problem. I mistook knowing for having."

I didn't answer that, because there was nothing in it I wanted to agree with and nothing I could honestly deny.

* * *

They came for us out of the rain an hour later — Tam first, a whistle cutting through the storm, then Odile behind him with a coil of rope over her shoulder and Bo's weight leaning hard against her side. My heart did something complicated at the sight of them. Bo was limping badly, one leg bound with a strip of shirt, his face gray with pain and rain, but he was upright, he was *here*, and Odile had gotten him down off the mountain through a slot canyon in a rising flood, which meant Odile had performed engineering miracles with nothing but rope and stubbornness, which meant Odile was Odile.

"Report," I said, because someone had to be a ranger about it.

"Canyon's flooding faster than my worst model," Odile shouted over the rain. She crouched beside me, rain streaming off her jaw, and pointed downstream with her chin. "The weir — the one that drowned the old camp? It'll overtop within six hours. Maybe four. Everything below the source goes into surge. We need to be off this shelf and onto the east ridge before that happens, or we're part of the geology."

"Can you get Maren down with that arm?"

"I can get anyone anywhere," Odile said. "That's why you hired me."

"I didn't hire you. I begged you."

"And I charged you double for the begging." She was already uncoiling rope, hands moving with the calm of a woman who had finally stopped punishing herself for the first expedition and started using what it taught her. Watching her work, I felt something in my chest loosen that had been tight for years. This was what she was for. This was what all of us were for, maybe — not the running, not the hiding, but this: skilled people in bad weather choosing each other's survival over their own comfort.

Tam reached Maren and stopped, and I watched him look at her — really look, the man who'd falsified her report, who'd carried that guilt up the whole river like ballast. She looked back at him. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing left to say that the river wasn't already saying better.

Bo lowered himself onto the rock beside me with a grunt. Up close he looked terrible and alive, which was my favorite combination in a person.

"Dray?" I asked.

"Gone. Took the fall wrong when the charge blew — I don't know if he walked out or the water has him." Bo spat rainwater. "Either way he's not our problem tonight."

"The letters made it?"

He patted his chest, where his uncle's correspondence rode in oilskin against his skin. "Made it. All of it. Every word about the ore, and every word about what she did to cover it." He glanced at Maren, hunched against her boulder with her broken arm cradled like something newborn. "You know what happens to her now."

"I know."

"And you're still sitting here looking sorry for her."

I was quiet a moment. Below us the river climbed another foot, smooth and inexorable, erasing the last visible trace of the source. "I spent seven years wanting her to pay for Halvard," I said. Bo flinched at his uncle's name; I pressed on, gentle. "For the guide in the portage. For the camp. For all of it. And now she's paying, and it doesn't feel like I thought it would. It feels like watching a flood take a house nobody lived in anymore."

"That's not sorrow for her," Bo said. "That's just the weight coming off. Feels the same at first."

I looked at him. Rain ran down his face and he grinned through it, Halvard's grin, the family grin, and I thought: *this one stayed*. He fell off a mountain for me yesterday and he's grinning today. That was the difference between Maren's crew and mine, between the first expedition and this one. Not skill. Not luck. Just this — people who, given every chance to leave, kept choosing to stay.

"All right," I said, and stood. "Odile. Get us off this rock."

* * *

The descent to the ridge line took three hours and cost us everything we had.

Odile rigged lines down the flooded chute with Tam anchoring, and we went down in stages — Bo first, hopping on his good leg with Tam's shoulder under his arm; then Maren, lowered on a rope seat, biting down on every curse; then me, last, because rangers go last, because somebody has to see everyone else safe before they'll trust the rock beneath their own feet.

Halfway down, the shelf we'd slept on vanished. I heard it go — a deep structural groan, then a crash, then the river closing over forty feet of granite like it had never existed. Maren hung in her rope seat below me and stared up at the empty air where the boulder had been, and I saw her understand, finally and completely, how close the arithmetic had been.

"Keep moving," I told her. "Grief later. Gravity now."

The east ridge took us at midnight, if midnight meant anything in that deluge — a basalt spine above the treeline where the wind hit sideways and the rain finally slackened to something merely biblical. We got Maren under an overhang. Odile built a fire out of wet wood through sheer force of professional insult. Tam counted heads twice, then a third time, like he couldn't quite believe the number.

Five. All five. Broken in various places, various ways, but five.

I stood at the edge of the overhang and looked down at the valley, and that was when I saw it happen.

The weir overtopped.

Even from this distance, even through rain and dark, it was unmistakable — the old dam that had drowned the first expedition's camp, the structure Odile had identified as someone's lie built in concrete, gave way all at once. Not a collapse. A release. The impounded water behind it, years of it, the water that had hidden the bones and the logbook and the truth, came down the canyon in a single moving hill, and where it met the flood from the source the two waters merged into one voice, one current, one Kessler River running full and free and furious from the mountains to the flats.

Every stake, every marker, every legal description of Maren's mineral claim dissolved into that current. By morning the survey points would be silt. By summer the maps would be wrong. The claim wasn't destroyed — it was *unmade*, returned to the category of things no paper could hold, which is the only true ownership a wild river has ever recognized.

Nobody owned the source of the Kessler now.

Nobody ever would again.

I felt tears on my face and realized I was crying, and realized I didn't mind, and realized that was new. Seven years I'd kept my grief dry, stored it, rationed it, carried it like water through a desert. And here was more water than the world knew what to do with, washing the whole canyon clean, and it seemed suddenly absurd — obscene, even — to keep hoarding my small share.

Halvard, I thought. Uncle Halvard, who wrote letters his nephew would carry through fire. Guide-of-the-portage, name unknown, who died keeping a secret that was never worth his life. Old camp, drowned. Old lies, drowned. Old Silas, drowned too, maybe, the one who never stopped moving because stopping meant remembering.

The river took all of it downriver, toward the sea, toward the salt where everything dissolves eventually.

Behind me, Bo said my name softly. I wiped my face with the back of my hand and turned around. The fire had caught properly. Odile was re-splinting Maren's arm with actual competence and Maren was letting her, head bowed, and Tam was spreading their last dry blanket over all of them without being asked, dividing warmth the way he divided rations — evenly, automatically, like breathing.

"We make Vell's Landing in four days if the flood roads hold," Odile said, looking up at me. "Two weeks if they don't. Either way—" she nodded at Maren, at the letters, at all of it, "—there's a magistrate in that town, and a story that needs telling."

"I know," I said.

"It won't be an easy story."

"No," I agreed. "But it's ours now. All of it. We tell the true parts."

Tam looked up sharply at that — *the true parts*, words that had cost him blood to earn — and then he smiled, tired and crooked, and pulled the blanket higher over Bo's shoulders.

Outside the overhang, the rain kept falling on a river that remembered everything and forgave most of it, and for the first time in seven years, Silas Pemberton sat down in the middle of a journey instead of running through it, and let the people she loved build the fire.
