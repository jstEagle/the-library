## 18. Downriver

The river had doubled in a single night.

Odile stood at the lip of the high shelf where they'd dragged Maren and watched the Kessler eat its own banks, brown water climbing the canyon walls in ropes of foam, whole trees cartwheeling past like matchsticks. Somewhere above them — hours behind them now, sealed behind a wall of rock that no longer existed — the source was emptying itself into the gorge with the sound of a mountain clearing its throat.

"Can we go down in this?" Bo asked beside her. His arm was splinted and strapped across his chest, his face gray with pain he was refusing to acknowledge, and Odile thought: *he's her nephew all right. Every one of them refuses to admit when they're hurt.*

"Not today," she said. "Today we watch."

She'd spent the morning doing what she did best: measuring. The flood crest would come within six hours, maybe eight, driven by the burst at the source plus whatever the storm was dumping into the headwaters. After the crest, the water would drop — not to safe, but to survivable. A barge could run it. Not their barge; Kettle Falls had taken care of that, and Maren's barge with it, both of them somewhere downstream now as kindling. But the old weir below the drowned camp had a service channel, and the service channel had a lock gate, and if the lock mechanism hadn't rusted through in fifteen years —

If. Her whole life lived inside that word. If the bearing held, if the welds were true, if the man she loved came home, if the report she signed was honest. Odile Voss had built a career on engineering certainty out of ifs, and it had never once occurred to her until this season how much of her life she'd spent pretending the ifs weren't prayers.

Below the shelf, Silas crouched over Maren Kessler, who lay on a bedroll with her eyes open and fixed on nothing.

Maren hadn't spoken since the cavern. She'd been conscious for most of the haul down from the source — Silas dragging her by the collar through slot canyons while the first rain turned to deluge around them, Tam ahead with the lamp, Odile behind carrying rope and half their food because someone had to be the mule and she'd long since stopped being too proud for it. Twice Maren had tried to stand on her own. Twice her leg had folded under her like wet cardboard. The third time she'd simply stopped trying, and something in her face had closed like a shop door, and she hadn't said a word since.

Odile climbed down to them. Rain hammered the shale, ran off her hat brim in sheets.

"How is she?" she asked Silas.

"Alive." Silas wrung out a bandana without looking up. "Leg's broken. Simple fracture, I think — mid-shin, clean break. I splinted it." She paused. "She didn't fight me."

That last part sat between them like a third person. Silas Pemberton did not do tenderness, but Odile had watched her carry a woman she hated down four miles of flooding canyon with a gentleness she probably didn't know she possessed, and Odile had decided years ago — back before the first expedition, before everything — that some mercies were better left unremarked.

"I'll check the splint," Odile said, and knelt.

Maren's eyes tracked her. Up close, the famous charm was gone — stripped away like paint off a hull, leaving bare wood, weathered and split. She looked older than Odile remembered. She looked, in fact, like exactly what she was: a woman whose entire architecture of control had collapsed in one afternoon, one lit fuse, one choice she hadn't been able to buy or bully or seduce her way around.

"The water," Maren said.

Her voice startled all of them. It was the first thing she'd said in nine hours.

"What about it?" Silas said flatly.

"It's loud." Maren turned her head toward the gorge, toward the brown roar filling the canyon floor. "It's so loud."

Then she laughed — a small, cracked sound, nothing like the laugh Odile remembered from Vell's Landing, the one deployed like currency — and began to talk.

* * *

They couldn't stop her. Later, Odile would wonder whether any of them had really tried.

Maren talked to the river. That was the only way Odile could describe it — she lay on the bedroll with rain running down her temples and spoke in the direction of the flood as though the water had summoned her, as though it had called her name up the canyon for thirty years and she had finally answered. Maybe it had. Rivers have long memories, and the Kessler had every reason to remember her.

"You want to know why the weir," Maren said, and it wasn't quite a question. "You've all been wondering. Odile figured it out at the drowned camp — I saw her face. You always were the clever one, Odile. Cleverer than me, everyone said. That's why I made sure you were never in the room where decisions got made."

Odile kept working the splint. Her hands were steady. Fifteen years of practice at keeping hands steady.

"There was ore in those hills before my father ever put a boat on this river. He knew. Grandfather knew. We grew up on stories about the blue streak in the limestone — Kessler silver, they called it, before the name meant anything else. And then the surveyors came from the capital and mapped the watershed, and my father understood that whoever controlled the source controlled the claim, and the claim was worth more than the town, more than the river, more than all of us put together."

"So you drowned your own camp," Silas said.

Maren smiled at the sky. "I drowned a lie. The camp was real enough — tents, gear, your dear Halvorsen's cook fire. But the expedition was already dead by then. They'd found the vein. They'd taken samples. And Hal—" Her voice snagged, just once, like a nail catching cloth. "Hal wrote to the magistrate. Said the claim should be public domain. Said no family should own a river."

The rain filled the silence.

"He was going to give it away," Maren whispered. "Everything. My grandfather's life's work. My father's. Mine. He was going to hand it to strangers because it was *right*." She said the word the way other people said a curse. "So I bought the guide. Paid him to lead the retreat party through the wrong cut of the gorge, and I went ahead and blew the weir myself, and the water did the rest. Bandits, we said. Tragic. Unmapped country. And the logbook—" her eyes flicked to Tam, huddled under the tarp with his arms around his knees, looking like a man watching his own funeral from outside his body— "the logbook needed correcting. And Tam corrected it. Because I asked. Because I paid. Because he was twenty-three years old and terrified, and I have never in my life met a fear I couldn't purchase."

"I know," Tam said hoarsely. "I told them. At Kettle Falls. I told them everything."

"I know you did, sweetheart." Maren's mouth curved. "That's what broke it open, isn't it? All these years I only ever needed one person per secret. One guide to mislead. One engineer to falsify. one navigator to keep quiet. One ranger to blame herself." Her gaze slid to Silas. "You never even wondered why you survived, did you? Why the retreat route you took was the safe one? I drew you the map myself, Silas. I put you on dry ground and let the water take everyone who might have talked. You've spent fifteen years punishing yourself for surviving a massacre that was, for you, a mercy. My mercy. And you wore it like penance, and penance keeps a person quiet, and quiet people don't ask questions."

Odile heard Silas breathe. Once, slow, deliberate — the breath of a woman choosing not to strike. She had seen Silas Pemberton angry in ways that rearranged landscapes. This was different. This was anger that had nowhere left to go because its target had already destroyed herself more thoroughly than any fist could.

"You're confessing," Odile said quietly. "Why?"

Maren turned her head. For a moment — one strange, unguarded moment — she looked almost young.

"Because the river's taking everything else," she said. "The claim. The barge. The money. Dray is either dead upstream or gone over the ridge with whatever gold he could carry, and either way he's finished with me. There's nothing left to protect. Do you understand? There has never once in my adult life been a moment when there wasn't something to protect. And now the water has it all, and it turns out that when there's nothing left to hide, the truth is the only thing I still own."

She looked back at the flood.

"So I'm spending it," she said. "While I'm the one holding it. Before the river takes this too."

Nobody answered. The rain came down. Down in the gorge, the Kessler roared on, indifferent as arithmetic, carrying away trees and silt and fifteen years of lies, asking nothing, forgiving nothing, remembering everything.

Odile finished checking the splint and sat back on her heels, and found that her eyes were burning, and blamed the rain.

* * *

They descended at dawn two days later.

Not dawn exactly — the monsoon had eaten the sun, and dawn was a gray brightening of gray, rain easing to a steady soak, the air thick enough to drink. Odile stood at the top of the service channel above the old weir with her notebook soaked soft as bread and every calculation she owned committed to memory, and gave the order she'd been dreading for forty-eight hours.

"We run it."

The crew looked at her. Silas, hollow-eyed, steady. Tam, pale, gripping the recovered logbook against his chest like a shield he'd finally earned. Bo, one-armed, jaw set, refusing for the fourth time to stay behind — and losing the argument for the fourth time, because Odile had pulled rank she didn't technically possess and Silas had backed her without a word.

And Maren, lashed into an improvised litter between two pack frames, watching Odile with an expression Odile couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Explain it again," Silas said. Not doubting — wanting to hear it. Wanting the numbers said aloud, the way sailors want the depth sounded twice in fog.

"The flood crest passed yesterday midnight. We're on the falling limb — dropping about a foot an hour. By noon the main channel runs maybe three meters over normal. Too fast to row, too deep to portage the whole way down, and our food doesn't allow for portaging." Odile tapped the map drawn in her head. "But the weir's service channel is stone-lined, engineered flow, and the lock gate at the bottom is iron. If the gate mechanism still moves, we can ride the channel down instead of the rapids. Controlled water instead of chaos. Twenty minutes of controlled terror instead of two days of uncontrolled."

"And if the gate doesn't move?"

"Then we hit the trash rack at speed and find out together how well I tied these lashings." Odie allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Which is why Bo stays here with Maren and the radio flares. If we're not back by tomorrow night, he signals the mining post at Vell's Landing, and they send a recovery crew, and he gets to spend a week alone with Maren Kessler, which I'm told is its own punishment."

"I hate this plan," Tam said.

"You're welcome to swim," Odile said.

The channel was exactly as she remembered it from the survey maps — except that memory was a liar and maps were worse, and the reality was a stone throat choked with fifteen years of debris, driftwood wedged in the upper gates, silt banks slumped across the incline. They worked three hours clearing it in the rain, hauling logs with rope and leverage and language Odile wouldn't repeat in front of her mother, and every hour the light got grayer and Odile recalculated and recalculated and made herself stop calculating, because the numbers said go and the fear said wait and she had spent fifteen years letting fear cast the deciding vote.

The first expedition. There it was, the way it always arrived — not as memory but as weather, a pressure change, a weight behind the eyes. The lock gate at the drowned camp, the one she'd designed, the one that failed. The bearing she'd specified, cheap, because the budget was tight and Maren had smiled at her and said *we'll replace it next season, Odile, next season for sure.* The gate that seized. The water that rose. Halvorsen on the far bank with the rope, waving them across, waving them to safety, going back for the gear, going back a second time, not coming back the third.

Fifteen years Odile had carried that bearing. She had rebuilt locks and dams and sluices across three territories, obsessively, perfectly, over-engineered beyond all sense, as though she could weld shut the gap in the world where Halvorsen used to be. And it had never once occurred to her — not truly, not in the marrow — that forgiveness wasn't a structure you built. It was a load you finally agreed to set down.

She set it down now, standing in the rain at the top of a stone chute with a broken woman's litter lashed beside her and a found family soaked to the bone around her, and it weighed nothing at all.

"All right," she said. "Boats in."

* * *

The channel run took eleven minutes and lasted approximately nine years.

They rode it in the salvaged skiff and the overturned barge tender, roped in convoy, Odile steering the lead boat by feel and faith. The stone walls blurred past. Water hit them in shoulders, in sheets, in whole cold continents. Tam screamed something joyful or terrified — indistinguishable, possibly identical — and Bo's flare pistol stayed holstered and Maren lay in the stern of the second boat with her eyes closed and her face turned up to the rain like a woman receiving communion.

The gate moved.

It moved stiffly, shrieking like a murdered cat, shedding rust in orange ribbons, but it moved — Odile's hands on the wheel, Tam hauling the counterweight chain, Silas bracing the tiller, all three of them leaning on iron that some forgotten engineer had installed sixty years before any of them were born, trusting that someday, in flood, strangers would need it to work.

It worked.

They shot out of the channel into the lower river, into wide brown water running fast but sane, and the crew of the Kessler expedition — the second one, the true one — drifted in the rain and laughed like lunatics, and Odile steered with hands that shook only afterward, when no one needed them steady anymore.

* * *

Vell's Landing appeared on the fourth day, smelling of woodsmoke and mud, and the whole town came down to the water because word had outrun them somehow — the mining post's telegraph, the fisherman who'd seen five figures in two boats hauling a prisoner, rumor moving faster than floodwater as rumor does.

They brought Maren ashore last. She went quietly, splinted leg, wrists bound with the same rope that had hauled her out of a canyon. On the dock she stopped, swaying between the constables, and looked back at the river one time.

"It's higher than I've ever seen it," she said. Almost conversationally. To no one. To the water. "My father would have hated that. He always wanted it lower. Contained. Useful." A pause. "I think I understand him less every year."

Then they led her away up the plank road toward the magistrate's office, and the crowd swallowed her, and Odile found she felt nothing she had predicted. No triumph. No satisfaction. Just a vast tiredness, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, something like peace — the specific peace of an engineer seeing a structure stand under load, seeing the thing she built hold, seeing the people she loves walk off a boat alive.

Silas appeared at her shoulder. Said nothing. That was Silas's whole vocabulary sometimes, and Odile had learned to read it fluently.

"She confessed to the river," Odile said. "Did you hear all of it?"

"Most of it." Silas watched the water slide past the pilings, brown and heavy and, Odile noticed, beginning already to clear at the edges, the silt settling, the mountain giving the river back its color. "Tam goes to the magistrate tomorrow. Hands over the forged report. Takes his share." She exhaled. "He asked me to tell you thank you. For the channel. For getting them down."

"Tell him himself. He's right there."

Silas almost smiled. Then: "What will you do? When this is done. Back to the contracts? The dam work in the eastern ranges?"

Odile looked downriver — past the docks, past the landing, down the long brown curve toward the drowned camp and the old weir and the place where Halvorsen had last stood on a bank and waved. Fifteen years she had not let herself look directly at it. Now she looked, and the looking cost nothing.

"No," she said. "I think I'm done building things to get away from here. I was thinking..." She turned the idea over, testing its joints, and found it sound. "The drowned camp. The site. It's good ground — stone foundations, fresh water, defensible against flood if the engineering's redone properly. Someone should build a waystation there. For travelers on the river. Guides, surveyors, anyone running the Kessler."

Silas was quiet a moment. "A house above the weir," she said.

"Something like that." Odile shouldered her tool roll. "Come on. Tam's surrendering tomorrow, and I intend to be standing next to him when he does. Nobody testifies alone. Not on this crew."

Behind them the Kessler ran on toward the sea, high and muddy and slowly clearing, carrying away the last of the mountains, keeping what mattered.
