## 19. The Testimony

The magistrate's office in Vell's Landing smelled of ink and river damp, and Tam had been sitting outside its door since dawn with the forged report in a canvas satchel against his chest.

He'd rehearsed this. On the barge coming downriver he'd rehearsed it, hauling Maren's prisoner when the ropes needed hauling, bailing water when the flood demanded it, and through all of it the words turning over in his head like stones in a current: *I did it. I drew the maps. I changed the numbers. I knew what she was and I helped her anyway.* He'd said them to himself so many times they'd worn smooth, lost their edges, become almost easy.

They were not easy now.

The door opened at the eighth bell. A clerk looked him up and down — mud-stained coat, hands still scabbed from rope burns, eyes that hadn't slept properly in eleven days — and said, "You're the survey crew come down off the Kessler."

"Some of us." Tam stood. His knees cracked like kindling. "I need to file testimony. And I need to surrender myself."

The clerk's eyebrows went up, but he was a river-town man, and river-town men had seen stranger things than a broken navigator asking to be arrested. "Magistrate Corren's at his breakfast. You can wait or you can talk to me first and I'll tell you if it's worth his time."

Tam set the satchel on the clerk's desk and untied the drawstring. The logbook inside was swollen and warped, its pages stiff as dried fish, but legible. Legible enough. He'd made sure of that years ago, when he'd written every damning number in his own careful hand.

"This is worth anyone's time," he said.

* * *

Magistrate Corren turned out to be a small woman with grey hair cropped short and reading glasses pushed up into it, who listened without interrupting for the better part of an hour. She'd cleared her desk of breakfast and spread the documents out in chronological order as Tam laid them down: the original survey report of the first expedition, six years old; the second copy, the one filed with the territorial office, where three pages of ore assays had been replaced with clean paper showing nothing worth a claim; his own field notes from both versions; and finally the letters — Bo's uncle's letters, rescued from Dray's ambush, water-spotted and creased, naming Maren Kessler's hand in all of it.

"You're telling me," Corren said, taking off her glasses, "that you falsified a mineral report under direct instruction from Maren Kessler, that the falsification concealed ore veins worth more than anything ever pulled out of these highlands, and that she did it to keep the claim secret while she arranged to buy the surrounding parcels cheap."

"Yes."

"And the guide. Halloran." Corren's voice didn't change, but something behind it did. "The one they found at the portage."

"He died protecting the source's location. Bo told us what happened — his uncle wrote about it before—" Tam stopped. Swallowed. "Before Maren cut the retreat off. Before the flood took the camp."

"Six people died in that camp."

"I know."

"And you let the territory believe it was bandit trouble. Bad luck. High water."

"I filed the report myself," Tam said. "I sat across a desk very much like yours and I lied with a straight face because Maren Kessler was standing behind me, and because I told myself there was nothing I could do. That's the whole confession, Magistrate. It isn't complicated. I'm not asking you to understand it. I'm asking you to record it."

Corren studied him for a long moment. Outside the window, Vell's Landing went about its morning — carts on the levee road, gulls fighting over fish guts, the river running high and brown with monsoon melt, faster than anyone liked.

"You understand," she said, "that this makes you an accessory. Falsification of a territorial survey is fraud. Concealment connected to deaths during a licensed expedition — that could be manslaughter by negligence, depending on what the inquiry finds. You could hang for this, Mr. Tam. Or whatever your full name is."

"Tam is my full name. Has been since I ran from the last one." He managed something that wasn't quite a smile. "And yes. I understand. I've been understanding it since Kettle Falls. The hanging part took longer."

"Then why?"

The question landed harder than any accusation. Tam looked at his hands — rope-scarred, ink-stained, steady now in a way they hadn't been in years — and found that the answer came without rehearsal at all.

"Because someone asked me once what stopping costs," he said. "And I've spent six years finding out."

* * *

It took three days.

Corren was thorough the way river people are thorough — she'd seen too many flash floods and too many slick claims to trust anything that arrived all at once. She sent runners upriver to confirm the drowned camp, the weir, the remains at the portage. She interviewed Odile, who came in still limping from the descent and gave two hours of precise engineering testimony about a dam no map recorded, built by contract crews whose pay chits bore the Kessler Trading Company seal. She interviewed Bo, who laid out his uncle's letters like a man dealing cards he'd already counted. She even interviewed Maren.

Maren, held in the customs house under guard, delirious by turns and lucid by turns, said little that was useful except once — when Corren read her the charge list and she laughed, a dry cracked sound, and said, "Ask the navigator. Whatever he signed, I signed. We were a matched pair of pens."

She said it looking directly at Tam, who had insisted on being present. It was meant as cruelty. It landed instead as confirmation, and Tam nodded and said, "Yes. We were," and watched something flicker behind Maren's eyes that might have been surprise — that he wouldn't flinch, that he'd chosen to stand inside the blast radius of his own guilt rather than step aside and let it fall on her alone.

By the third day, Corren had what she needed. She convened a formal session in the landing's largest room — the assay hall, usually used for weighing ore samples, which Tam thought was fitting — and read the findings aloud to a packed gallery of river folk, parcel holders, and the territorial assessor's man, who had arrived on the morning packet looking like a man whose week had just been ruined.

"The mineral claim registered to Maren Kessler, covering the headwaters of the Kessler River, is hereby voided," Corren read. "Grounds: fraudulent concealment of assay data; unlicensed construction obstructing a navigable waterway; deaths arising from deliberate isolation of expedition personnel. The source itself—" here she paused, and Tam saw her glance at the flooded-river reports stacked beside her, "—is presently underwater and will remain so. There is nothing left to claim. There never should have been."

The assessor's man protested. Corren let him protest for exactly as long as it amused her, then produced Bo's uncle's letter with the vein measurements and said, mildly, "Would you care to explain to this room why your office accepted a survey report with three pages replaced by clean paper, and didn't notice?" The assessor's man decided he had business elsewhere.

But the part Tam had come for came last.

"Regarding culpability," Corren said, and the room went quiet enough to hear the river. "Territorial law requires that responsibility for the falsified report be assigned. The report bears one signature."

Tam stood up. His legs were steadier than he expected. "Mine," he said. "It's mine. Every page of the forgery is in my hand, drawn under her instruction but drawn by me, and I'll sign whatever you put in front of me. But the crew that came down this time — Silas Pemberton, Odile Ferre, Bo Halloran — none of them knew. None of them signed anything. They came up that river carrying my lie without knowing it existed, and they nearly died for it. Whatever penalty falls, it falls on me. Not them."

He heard Odile exhale behind him. He heard Bo say, quietly, "That's not—" and stop himself, because it was, and they all knew it was.

Corren regarded him for a long moment over her glasses.

"Mr. Tam. For services rendered — and I am advised by Ranger Pemberton that services were considerable, including the recovery of evidence, the exposure of Ms. Kessler's operation, and the survival of four persons whom the Kessler River had every reason to kill — the territory is prepared to weigh cooperation against culpability." She set down her papers. "Six months' labor, assigned to river works. Levee repair, channel marking, salvage. Supervised. Then we'll speak again about what remains of your sentence."

Someone in the gallery made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. Six months of hard labor was not mercy, exactly. But it was not a rope, and it was not running, and when Tam signed the disposition form with the same careful hand that had signed the lie six years ago, the ink felt different this time. Heavier. Cleaner.

Afterward, in the hallway, Silas was waiting. She leaned against the wall with her arms crossed, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"You didn't have to do that," she said finally. "Stand up and take all of it."

"Yeah, I did." Tam looked out the window at the brown water. "You know what the worst part was? Up there, at Kettle Falls, when I read the logbook out loud — I thought confessing would be the hard thing. Like the words would cost something. But they didn't. They were cheap. What was expensive was everything after — watching all of you decide whether I was worth keeping." He paused. "You kept me. So the least I could do was make sure the ledger balanced."

Silas was quiet a moment. "Maren's being transported east next week. Territorial prison, pending trial. She asked for visitors. Nobody's going."

"No," Tam agreed. "Nobody is."

"She asked for you specifically."

That one landed low, below the ribs. Tam thought about the matched pair of pens. About six years of telling himself there was nothing he could do, and eleven days of learning exactly how much a person could do when he finally tried. "Tell her I'll send the report," he said. "Filed copy. She can read how it ends."

Something moved through Silas's face — not quite a smile, but the shape of one, the way ice takes the shape of water before it lets go. "The others are at the Landing house. Odile's already drawing plans for the waystation. Bo keeps talking about timber. They keep asking when you're coming."

Tam looked down at the disposition paper in his hand. Six months of supervised labor, most of it within twenty miles of the river. Most of it, if a man petitioned correctly and a magistrate was feeling reasonable, close enough to walk home from on Sundays.

"Tell them I'll be there when the levee work lets me," he said. "And tell Odile—" He stopped, laughed at himself, started over. "Tell them I'm done navigating away."

Down at the docks, the river ran fast and brown and honest, carrying everything it had taken and everything it had given back toward the sea. Tam watched it a long time. Then he rolled up his sleeves and went to find the levee foreman, because the flood season wasn't waiting, and neither, anymore, was he.
