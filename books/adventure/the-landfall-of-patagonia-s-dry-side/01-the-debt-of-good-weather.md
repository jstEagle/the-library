## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm was dying and Paloma Dunmore knew it before the clients did.

He felt it in his back teeth first, the way he always did—a loosening, a pressure letting go like a fist unclenching somewhere over the plateau to the northwest. Then he confirmed it with his eyes: the dust column ahead had gone from brown to a tired orange-gray, its top shearing off sideways in the upper wind instead of standing up straight like a fist raised at God. A living dust devil argues with the sky. This one had stopped arguing.

"Is it getting bigger?" asked the woman in the passenger seat, camera already up, elbow wedged against the window frame of the Land Cruiser. Her name was Margaret; she'd flown in from Bristol with her husband and paid four hundred dollars a day for what the brochure called *an authentic Patagonian weather expedition*. What that meant, in practice, was that Paloma drove them very fast toward things most people drove away from.

"It's done," he said. "Ten more minutes and it'll be a stain on the horizon."

Margaret lowered the camera. In the mirror he saw her husband, Clive, frown—the particular frown of a man doing arithmetic on a day rate.

"You said this one would build," Clive said. "This morning you said the pressure gradient favored it."

"I said it *might* build." Paloma kept his voice easy, hands loose on the wheel as the track bucked beneath them—two ruts and a memory of ruts, the scrub flashing past in gray-green rows. "The morning data favored it. The steppe doesn't sign contracts."

That was true, and it wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth was that he'd read the storm wrong on purpose—not badly, just enough. There had been a moment at dawn when the wind had come off the Andes' rain shadow and turned, and he'd felt the old lift in his chest, the animal certainty that today would be a big one. He could have chased it properly. He could have run them north along the old sheep tracks for two hours and put them under the leading edge of something with real shoulders, something that would have filled every card in Margaret's camera and made Clive forget the price.

Instead he'd angled south, toward a dying vortex, because a big storm takes all day and a dying one takes forty minutes, and Paloma needed to be back in Gobernador Gregores by three o'clock. He hadn't known why yet. He'd only known that for eleven days now the air around his life had gone still and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with meteorology, and still air, on the dry side, is how the land tells you something is coming.

The dust devil collapsed while they watched, folding into itself like a skirt dropped to the floor. Margaret got two photographs of it anyway. Clive didn't say anything else, but Paloma could feel the refund forming behind the man's eyes, itemized and resentful.

*Let him,* Paloma thought. *Refunds are the cheapest thing I owe anybody.*

They were twenty kilometers from Gregores when the wind changed again—and this time it wasn't a storm. It came out of the west flat and dry and smelling faintly of dust and diesel, the foehn wind the locals called *el viento que roba*, the wind that steals. It stole moisture, warmth, patience. It made men short-tempered and dogs mean and it blew for days without once changing its mind. Paloma rolled his window down two fingers' width and let it push through the cab, and thought: *three o'clock.* Whatever was coming had picked its day well. Bad-weather men always do.

* * *

The garage sat on the edge of town where the pavement gave up and the gravel began—a rented corrugated-iron shed with a roller door that stuck halfway unless you lifted with your knee, which Paloma did now, ducking under it into the smell of oil and cold metal. The Cruiser went in nose-first. The clients had been delivered to the hostería with a discount he couldn't afford and a promise of a better show tomorrow that he might not live to keep.

There was a black Ford Ranger parked inside his own garage, taking up half the floor, gleaming, absurd, like a shark in a fish tank.

Two men leaned against the workbench at the back, drinking mate from a shared gourd, passing it between them with the unhurried rhythm of brothers who had done it ten thousand times. The taller one, Emilio, wore a quilted vest despite the mild afternoon and had the open, sunburned face of a man you'd trust with your truck. The shorter one, Raúl, had a softer face and colder eyes and stood with his weight on both feet evenly, the way men stand who have never once needed to shift it.

"Fifty-one minutes late," Emilio said warmly, not looking at any clock. "We were starting to worry about you, Palomita."

"The road to Tucu-Tucu is washed out past the second cañadón. I took the long way." Paloma let the roller door down behind him. It stuck, as always, and he left it there, half-open, a strip of white daylight across the floor between himself and them. He didn't examine why. Some instincts you don't interrogate; you just obey them.

"We know," Raúl said. "We've been listening to your radio traffic. You're very good on the radio. Very professional." He passed the mate to Emilio, who drank, who handed it back, the gourd traveling its small orbit. "Very calm. Even when the clients are unhappy."

"They weren't unhappy."

"Clive was unhappy," Emilio said gently. "We had coffee with Clive. Lovely man. He showed us photographs." He took a phone from his vest pocket and turned the screen around: Margaret's picture of the collapsing dust devil, already sent, already shared, already in the hands of the Ferro brothers of Gregores, who were not, officially, in the business of anything at all. "It's a nice photograph. It looks like the end of something."

Paloma hung his hat on the peg by the door. It was a small ritual and he performed it slowly, buying the seconds, letting his heartbeat come down to where he wanted it. Fear, he'd learned, was mostly a matter of tempo. Slow yourself below the fear and you could think above it.

"You're in my garage," he said. "You're drinking my mate"—they were, in fact, using his own gourd and bombilla, taken from the shelf by the bench, which was somehow worse than if they'd brought their own—"and you've met my client. So I'm guessing this isn't a social call, and I'm guessing it isn't about fifty-one minutes."

Raúl smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which was the frightening part, because the eyes themselves never changed at all. "Emilio wanted to wait until Friday. I said no—Paloma's a professional, he'll want to handle these things promptly. That's what I like about you, Palomita. You're prompt. You read conditions early. You don't let a front get behind you." He set the mate down on the workbench with a small ceramic click. "So let's talk about fronts."

Paloma leaned against the Cruiser's warm flank and folded his arms. "Talk."

"Your account stands at thirty-eight thousand dollars." Emilio said it the way a man reads a weather report—flat, factual, no drama in it. "Principal, interest, the restructuring fee from March, the late fees since June. We've carried you a long time. We've been patient. Everyone will tell you we're patient."

Everyone did tell you that. It was the first thing anyone in Santa Cruz told you about the Ferros, usually in the same breath as an invitation to consider whether you really needed the loan, and always with a small glance sideways, checking the door. Their father had lent money to sheep estancias through the collapse years, cheerful and fair-seeming, and their mother had kept the books, and between the two of them they had built something that never appeared on any registry: a debt that behaved less like a number and more like weather. It was simply there. It pressed on everything. You planned your life around it the way farmers planned around drought, and no one had ever seen the exact spot where it started.

"Thirty-eight thousand," Paloma said. "And?"

"And we love you," Emilio said, spreading his hands, sincere as a priest. "You're good people. Your mother was good people. Your sister is good people—we see her at the co-op in Pico Truncado, always polite, always working. Which is why we're going to do you a favor." He paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a gift he intended to enjoy watching being opened. "We're resetting the terms."

"There's nothing to reset. We have terms. Signed."

"We *had* terms." Raúl picked it up seamlessly, the way they always did, one voice split between two mouths. "But circumstances have changed. The peso moved. Fuel moved. And frankly, Paloma, your revenue has moved—you're charging tourists four hundred a day to look at empty sky, and paying us, what, six hundred a month? At that rate we'll be paid off sometime after the next ice age, and my brother and I are not patient men forever. We're patient men *so far*." He smiled again. "There's a difference."

"What are the new terms?"

"Forty-six thousand. Effective today. The eight thousand is a reassessment fee."

"That's not a fee. That's a punishment."

"It's a *weather event*," Emilio said pleasantly. "Sometimes the wind changes and a man's costs go up. Nobody's punishing anybody. It's just conditions." He pushed off the workbench and walked a slow half-circle, boots ringing on the concrete, and stopped near the half-open door, silhouetted against the daylight strip, so that Paloma had to look slightly into the glare to hold his face. "Here's the part I like. We're also extending the term. Five years instead of three. Smaller payments. Very manageable. A man could breathe."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, availability." Raúl said the word like he was tasting it. "When we call, you answer. When we ask you to drive somewhere, you drive. Nothing illegal. Nothing you'd lose sleep over. We have interests that need a man who can cross country nobody else can cross, who can read the land the way you do. Honestly, Palomita, half the time we'd be doing you a favor—paying you to do what you already do."

The wind outside pushed a gust under the half-closed door and rattled a tin can of bolts across the floor, and in the silence after it Paloma heard, very clearly, the sound of a trap finishing its construction. Not the snapping shut—that would come later, and it would come fast, the way storms come off the plateau, visible for an hour and then on top of you. This was the quieter thing: the sound of the ground being prepared, the low-pressure trough settling in over his life, all the air beginning to move in one direction only.

His father had taught him to read weather on this same steppe, out beyond the wire fences, when Paloma was nine. *The land lies,* his father used to say, spitting into the dust. *The sky lies. But the pressure never lies. Feel the weight of the air, m'hijo. Everything that's coming announces itself first as weight.*

He felt the weight now.

"And if I say no?" Paloma asked.

Emilio laughed—a real laugh, delighted, head tipped back—and clapped his hands once, softly. "He asks! Raúl, he asks. I told you he'd ask." He wiped his eye with a knuckle. "Nobody says no, Palomita. That's the beautiful thing. Nobody says no to weather. They just decide whether to bring a coat." He stepped forward and gripped Paloma's shoulder, warm and heavy, a brotherly hand, and squeezed once. "Sign the paper. Breathe easier. And when our friend calls—and he will call soon, within the month, I'd bet the gourd on it—you answer the phone and you drive where he says. That's all. That's the whole coat."

Raúl had produced a single sheet of paper and a pen and laid them on the workbench, squared neatly, facing Paloma. Prepared. Of course it was prepared. Eleven days of still air, and here was the front, arrived exactly on schedule.

Paloma looked at the paper for a long time. The numbers swam and reassembled themselves into other numbers: the lease on his sister Lucía's workshop in Pico Truncado, which the Ferros held through a third party and which he'd discovered last spring, and never mentioned, and never forgotten. The four hundred dollars a day that Clive would demand half of back tomorrow. The thirty-eight thousand that had just become forty-six by the simple mechanism of two smiling men saying so.

"Three o'clock," he said quietly.

"What's that?"

"You waited until three o'clock. After the chase. You wanted me tired." He picked up the pen. His hand was steady, which surprised him. "Good instinct. Storms hit hardest when the target's already worn down."

Raúl's smile widened by a degree. "See? This is why we like him."

Paloma signed. The pen scratched, a tiny dry sound, like wind moving sand across stone, and when he finished, Emilio took the paper, blew on the ink theatrically, folded it into quarters, and tucked it into his vest over his heart.

"There," he said. "Now we're partners. Now it's official." He drained the last of the mate, set the gourd upside down on the bench—the traditional signal, *I am finished, we are done*—and the two brothers moved toward the door together, unhurried, brushing dust off each other's backs in the old familiar way.

At the threshold Emilio paused, half-turned, the daylight cutting across his cheerful face.

"One more thing, Palomita. Small thing. A courtesy." His voice stayed light, but something underneath it shifted register, the way the barometer drops before the front arrives—imperceptible to most people, unmistakable to those who knew how to feel. "A government man has been asking around town. Yesterday, the day before. Asking about you specifically—your routes, your range, whether you take private contracts. Quiet fellow. Glasses. He left a number." Emilio patted his vest pocket, where the signed paper lay. "We haven't given it to you yet, because we wanted to see your face when we mentioned it. And it was worth it." He grinned. "He wants to hire you to go find something the government lost. Out north, past the Deseado. Something with a lot of zeros attached."

The wind gusted again under the door, hard, carrying the smell of dust and distance, and Paloma felt it against his skin like a hand turning him by the shoulder to face north, toward the empty half of the province, toward whatever was waiting out there that a government would pay a fortune to recover and two smiling brothers found interesting enough to mention for free.

"Why tell me?" Paloma asked. "Why not take the job yourselves?"

Emilio's grin softened into something almost kind.

"Because, Palomita," he said, "we don't chase storms. We just own the man who does."

The roller door rattled down behind them, and Paloma stood alone in the sudden dark of his own garage, breathing the smell of oil and iron, listening to the wind build in the wires outside—and for the first time in eleven days, the pressure finally had a name, and a direction, and a number he could dial.
