## 2. A Contract Written in Pencil

The storm died two hundred kilometers short of Gaiman, and Paloma Dunmore drove through its corpse for an hour, watching the sky peel itself apart in the rearview mirror the way it always did on the dry side—no drama, no gratitude, just a slow unspooling of cloud into blue nothing. The clients in the back seat had stopped talking twenty minutes ago. They were bankers from Buenos Aires, or they said they were, and they had paid him four thousand pesos a head to feel the edge of a supercell that never quite committed to anything.

"Is that it?" the younger one asked. "That's all there is?"

"That was the leading edge," Paloma said. "The core collapsed over the meseta. Happens."

"It looked so big on your radar."

"They always do." He tapped the dash unit, a salvaged aviation display he'd wired into a truck that had once hauled sheep dip. "Out here the land lies about size. Storms lie about their intentions. You learn to read both or you stop chasing."

The banker nodded like a man who understood, which meant he didn't understand at all, and Paloma let it go. Four thousand pesos a head. It wouldn't cover the interest this month, let alone the principal. It wouldn't even cover the diesel he'd burned getting ahead of a storm that had decided, somewhere over the basalt, to die with its secrets intact.

He dropped them at the bus terminal in Trelew with their receipts and their mild disappointment, and then he sat in the idling truck in the emptying lot and did the arithmetic he did every night now, the arithmetic that had replaced prayer. Nine hundred forty thousand pesos. That was the number the Ferro brothers had written on a napkin eleven months ago, when the numbers had still been friendly. Interest compounded weekly. The number today, if he let himself calculate it—and he did, because not calculating it felt like letting them win—was one million one hundred twelve thousand and change. His sister Mirta's lease on the wool shed in Camarones sat underneath it like a stone under a blanket. Sign here, they'd said, it's just collateral, nobody's taking anything from anybody. The Ferros never took anything from anybody. They just arranged things so that people handed things over, smiling, grateful for the courtesy of it.

His phone buzzed against the console. Unknown number, Comodoro prefix.

"Dunmore." A woman's voice, clipped, administrative. "You're difficult to reach. I've been told you chase weather for tourists."

"Among other things. Who's asking?"

"My name is Quintana. I work for the provincial ministry. There's a matter that requires discretion and a vehicle capable of rough country. Yours has been recommended."

Paloma watched a gull walk the length of the terminal roof, unhurried, entitled. Government. The word arrived in his chest with a specific weight, the weight of every favor he'd ever seen called in, every permit that appeared or didn't, every wool contract that dried up overnight for reasons no one would name aloud.

"What kind of matter?"

"Not by phone. Tomorrow, ten o'clock, the Hotel Rayent. Ask at the desk for Señora Quintana. Come alone." A pause, and then, as if remembering how humans spoke: "There is money in it. Considerable money."

She hung up before he could ask how considerable, which told him she knew exactly what kind of man answered that kind of call, and knew he'd come anyway.

* * *

The Hotel Rayent smelled of floor wax and old cigarettes, the way government buildings smell everywhere in the world, as if bureaucracy itself had a flavor and it was the ghost of other people's vices. Quintana turned out to be fifty-ish, gray-bladed haircut, a linen jacket that had been expensive once and had decided to stop trying. She sat in the corner of the breakfast room with her back to the wall and two cups of coffee already on the table, one of them positioned across from her, waiting, an invitation shaped like a prop.

"Señor Dunmore." She didn't stand. "Sit. The coffee's terrible but it's hot."

He sat. He didn't touch the coffee. In his experience, hospitality from institutions was a down payment on something you hadn't agreed to sell yet.

"You came alone," she observed.

"I always come alone. It's cheaper."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not a smile, more the memory of one, filed away. She slid a folder across the table, thin, unmarked. "Do you know the Río Deseado basin? North of Puerto Deseado, the survey grid along the old estancia tracks."

"I know the country. Dry side. Guanaco, wind, and basalt. Nothing out there worth surveying except rocks."

"The ministry disagrees. There was a hydrological team working the grid. Six people. Geologists, a driller, support staff. They went in three weeks ago to take core samples from the aquifer formation." She opened the folder. Inside were photographs: a white Toyota Hilux with government plates, a drill rig folded flat on a trailer, six faces in a group shot taken outside some office building, everyone squinting, everyone alive. "They stopped reporting eleven days ago. Satellite phone dead. Emergency beacon never triggered. The last confirmed position is here—" she tapped a map, a pencil cross in the middle of nowhere, "—a camp site on the north fork."

"And the ministry wants me to find them." Paloma turned the photograph of the Hilux toward himself. "Search and rescue is Sarmiento's job. They have aircraft."

"Sarmiento has been notified. Aircraft have flown the corridor twice." Her voice stayed level, bored even, but her eyes didn't. "They found nothing. No smoke, no signal mirrors, no tracks leaving the camp. Either six trained field people vanished into the ground, or they don't want to be found, or someone found them first. The ministry needs a man on the ground who can read that country. Your name came up."

"From whom?"

"Does it matter?"

It mattered enormously, but he let it go, the way you let go of a rope you suspect is tied to something heavy. "And if I find them? Or find what's left of them?"

"Recovery of equipment is part of the contract. Instruments, data drives, sample cores. Anything official. Personal effects go to the families, eventually." She said *eventually* the way accountants say *pending*. "And you tell no one. Not the press, not the families, not your drinking friends in Comodoro. The ministry is managing this quietly until we understand what happened. Panic serves no one."

"There it is," Paloma said.

Quintana raised an eyebrow.

"'Tell no one.' Every job I've ever taken that paid too well had those words in it somewhere. Usually right before the part where the person saying them stops returning calls." He leaned back. The gull from the terminal had followed him here in his head; he could almost hear it laughing. "What's the number, Señora Quintana? Since you mentioned considerable."

"Two point four million pesos. Half on signature, half on delivery of the equipment to a location we'll provide." She said it without blinking, without the little performance of reluctance that honest negotiators put on. Two point four million. Twice his debt, twice, with enough left over to fix the truck's transfer case and buy Mirta out from under that lease outright and still eat meat twice a week for a year.

He made himself look at the map instead of her face. The pencil cross sat in country he knew the way you know a scar—by touch, in the dark, without looking. Flat steppe broken by canyons, the Deseado cutting its shallow green line through tan nothing, wind that came off the South Atlantic with salt in its teeth and then forgot the ocean existed by the time it reached the mesetas. Good tracking country, actually. Soft caliche held prints for days. Any vehicle that had gone in or out would have left a story written in dust, and he was fluent in dust.

Which was the problem. Because if aircraft had flown the corridor and found nothing, and the ministry still needed a bloodhound, then whatever was written in that dust was something they didn't want to read themselves. And two point four million pesos was not a search fee. Search fees were five figures and a fuel stipend. Two point four million was a silence fee wearing a search fee's clothes.

"Half up front," he said.

"Yes."

"In cash?"

"In a manner you won't have to explain to anyone." She finally picked up her own coffee, drank, grimaced slightly—the first honest thing she'd done. "Señor Dunmore. I'll be direct, since your reputation suggests you appreciate directness. Six people are missing. The ministry has obligations—to the families, to the press cycle, to its own credibility—that require this matter resolved in a specific way. You are not being asked to do anything illegal. You are being asked to find equipment and keep your mouth shut. If you happen to find people, alive or otherwise, you report to me and no one else. That's the entire job."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I offer it to the next name on the list, and you go back to charging bankers four thousand pesos to watch storms die." She set the cup down. "No threats, Dunmore. I don't deal in threats. I deal in options. This is simply the best option currently on your table, and we both know it, because I know what you owe and to whom, and I know what happens to men in your position when the interest outruns the income."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere behind the wainscoting a pipe knocked twice, like a fist on a door.

"You did your homework."

"It's my job." For a moment—half a second, no more—something human crossed her face, tired and old. "For what it's worth, I hope you find them alive. I signed the requisition that funded their expedition. I'd prefer that on my record over the alternative."

*And there it is,* Paloma thought. *The alternative.* She'd said it herself, casually, the way you mention weather. The alternative. Six people, a pencil cross on a map, and a ministry that needed the story to resolve *in a specific way*.

Every instinct he owned, the ones that had kept him alive through whiteout squalls on the Chubut mesetas and flash floods in cañadones that had no business holding water, stood up inside him and said *walk away*. This was the shape of every bad job. Easy money was a lure color, bright and wrong, the way certain frogs are bright. The land taught you that. Anything on the dry side that looked generous—a green seep, a fat guanaco standing calm near a waterhole—was usually guarding something that would kill you.

But poverty argued louder than instinct. Poverty had a voice like Mirta's on the phone last month, trying not to cry, saying *it's fine, Pali, we'll manage* while the Ferros' man stood in her yard measuring the wool shed for a sign that said SOLD. Poverty had a voice like the Ferros themselves, Raúl and Bautista, cheerful as wedding singers, patient as erosion. *We're not worried about you, Paloma. We like you. That's why we're flexible.* Flexibility, with the Ferros, was a word that meant the knife went in slower.

Two point four million pesos. Half now. Mirta free and clear by Friday.

"How soon would I leave?" he heard himself ask.

"Tomorrow, if you can. Sooner if you will." Quintana produced a pen—not hers, a cheap ballpoint, government issue—and laid it beside the folder. "The contract is written in pencil, Señor Dunmore. Everything in it. The amount, the terms, the confidentiality. Pencil erases easily, in either direction. I want you to understand that going in."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"No. It's supposed to make you careful." She stood, buttoned the jacket that had given up, and left a folded paper on the table. "Coordinates for the advance payment. A satellite number that reaches only me. And a copy of the team's last radio log—it makes grim reading, gaps where there should be voices. You strike me as a man who reads gaps professionally."

At the doorway she paused, half-turned, morning light from the window cutting her face in half.

"One more thing. The team leader was a Dr. Cabral. Hydrogeologist. Brilliant, stubborn, disliked in certain offices for asking inconvenient questions." A beat. "If you find her, Dunmore—find her first. Before anyone else does. There are other parties interested in the survey grid, and the ministry would prefer its own contractor arrive at any survivors before strangers do."

"Strangers," Paloma repeated. "Meaning who?"

"Meaning exactly what I said." And she was gone, heels ticking down the corridor, precise as a metronome winding down.

* * *

He sat in the truck outside the hotel for a long time, the folder on his passenger seat like a sleeping animal he wasn't sure was harmless. The radio log was worse than grim. Three weeks of routine telemetry, water levels, drilling depths—and then, in the final days, the intervals between transmissions stretching like taffy. Eight hours. Nineteen hours. Then a final burst, thirty-one seconds long, mostly static, and in the static a voice—he played it six times in the cab, engine off, windows up—saying words he couldn't fully separate. Something like *sealing*. Something like *not filed*. And then, clear as a bell over the noise, a man's voice, calm and terribly controlled: *"Kill the transmitter. We're done talking to them."*

We're done talking to *them*. Not *we're moving camp*. Not *radio's failing*. Done talking to them, as if the team had looked at the ministry on the other end of the signal and made a decision.

Paloma stared through the windshield at Trelew going about its morning—a school bus, a dog trotting with purpose, the Welsh chapels' slate roofs dark against the blue. Ordinary town. Ordinary day. Somewhere north of here, six people had stopped talking to their government on purpose or by force, and the government wanted the story back before anyone else read it.

He thought about walking away. He genuinely tried. He sat there and constructed the refusal, rehearsed the phone call to Quintana, imagined the relief of it, the clean feeling of a small life continuing. And then poverty stood up in him, that patient advocate, and began its argument the way it always did, item by item: the transfer case, the diesel card maxed out, the nine hundred forty thousand that had become one million one hundred twelve, Mirta's voice saying *we'll manage*, the Ferros smiling their wedding-singer smiles, and underneath all of it the oldest argument, the one with no rhetoric at all—two point four million, half today, and by Friday his sister owned her own roof again and no man in a good jacket ever measured her yard for a sign.

Instinct said the job was a trap with a salary.

Poverty said traps paid better than honesty, and at least a trap you could see was one you might walk out of.

Paloma started the truck. The transfer case clunked its familiar complaint, and he patted the dash the way you'd steady a nervous horse. North, then. Along the dry side, following a pencil cross into country that lied about size and hid what it killed.

He was three blocks gone before he noticed the gray Ford parked outside the hotel, two men in it, engine running, who pulled out a full minute after he passed and made no effort whatsoever to hide that they were following him.

The ministry, apparently, liked its contractors supervised.

Or somebody else wanted to know exactly where the bloodhound was pointed—and had gotten there first.
