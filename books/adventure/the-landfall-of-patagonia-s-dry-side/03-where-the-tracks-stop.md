## 3. Where the Tracks Stop

The dry side of Patagonia doesn't forgive you for being in a hurry. It doesn't punish you either. It just watches, flat and patient as a creditor, while you burn fuel and water and the last of your good humor against a horizon that never comes any closer.

I'd been on the survey team's route for four days when I understood that I was no longer following them so much as keeping their company.

Their tracks were easy enough to read at first — two heavy trucks and a support trailer, government-issue tires with the deep lugs worn to half-depth, running north along the old sheep trail that paralleled the Río Deseado's dry course. The wind had been working on them for eleven days by my count, softening the edges, filling the treads with sand, but the pattern was still legible if you knew how to squint at country the way you squint at an old letter. They'd driven like men on a schedule. Steady speed, no wandering, no stops longer than a lunch break. That told me something. Survey teams that are worried about what they'll find drive slow. These men had driven like the answer was already written down somewhere.

By the third day the tracks began to lie to me.

Not badly. Not all at once. But the spacing between the tire marks changed where there was no reason for it to change — no washouts, no soft ground, nothing but flat steppe stitched with coirón grass and the white bones of long-dead estancia fences. A truck that swerves for nothing is a truck whose driver isn't looking at the road. And twice, near a low rise of basalt, the tracks stopped entirely for thirty meters of bare gravel before resuming on the far side, neat as a signature.

Somebody had swept them. Somebody had taken a branch or a chain behind a vehicle and dragged the ground clean, and they'd done it in only two places, which meant it wasn't habit. It was intention. You sweep tracks where someone might be looking up from below, from the south, from the direction of towns and questions. You don't bother anywhere else.

I sat on my hood at midday with the engine ticking and looked north at those two clean scars in the earth, and I let myself think the thought I'd been refusing since Puerto Deseado: *a job this easy-paying is never easy.*

Then I drove on, because poverty argues louder than instinct, and poverty had been winning every argument I'd had for three years.

* * *

The land changed character as I went north, the way a face changes when its owner stops smiling. The steppe along the coast has a kind of shabby hospitality — abandoned shearing sheds, rusted stock pens, the occasional guanaco standing in the distance like a question mark. But forty kilometers inland the human evidence thinned out and the country turned inward on itself. The wind came off the plateau in long unbroken pushes, carrying grit that hissed against the truck's paint like static. The sky went from blue to a pale enamel white, the color of the inside of a shell, and stayed there.

This was weather country, and I read it the way other men read newspapers.

There was a front building over the Andes — I could feel it in the way the pressure sat on my ears, faint but persistent, like a finger tapping glass. Nothing urgent. Three days out, maybe four, and probably nothing much when it arrived; the dry side eats most fronts alive before they cross the hundredth meridian. But it was there, and I logged it the way I log everything, because the Ferro brothers hadn't lent me money against my truck and my reputation for nothing. My reputation, such as it was, rested on exactly this: I noticed things about the sky that instruments caught too late, and I'd pulled three paying clients out of whiteouts on the wet side that every forecast had called clear. Weather was the one ledger where I'd never been in arrears.

I checked my own gauges anyway — barometer steady, wind northwest at fifteen knots and gusting higher than the anemometer admitted — and made camp early, in the lee of a cutbank, because tomorrow I expected to find people.

The route sheet the fixer had given me said the team's next planned stop was a grid point marked only as *N-7*, a drilling site on a bench above a dry tributary. Eleven days gone. Nine days overdue for radio contact. The ministry man in his pressed shirt had used the word *salvage* three times in ten minutes, and each time he'd said it he'd looked at my hands instead of my eyes, as though checking whether they were honest hands or merely available ones.

I fell asleep listening to the wind sort itself through the coirón, and dreamed of nothing, which on the dry side is a gift.

* * *

I found the camp at nine the next morning, and I knew something was wrong with it before I knew anything else about it.

Wrongness has a texture. Anyone who's spent enough time alone in empty country learns to feel it through the soles of their boots before their eyes confirm it. A living camp hums — even an empty one, even a dead one, there's a residue of motion, of fire-smoke worked into the dirt, of things set down carelessly because hands will pick them up again. This camp didn't hum. It *posed.*

It sat on the bench above the dry tributary exactly where the route sheet promised, three vehicles arranged in a protective crescent facing south, tents staked in textbook formation, the drill rig folded and secured beside a borehole casing that stood out of the ground like a metal tooth. Everything correct. Everything orderly. Everything arranged the way a photograph of a camp would be arranged.

And nobody home.

I stopped the truck two hundred meters out and sat with my hands on the wheel and watched it through the windshield for a long time. No movement. No birds picking at anything. On the steppe, birds tell you the truth faster than anything — they descend on camps within minutes of humans leaving, because humans spill things. There wasn't a single chimango circling. Not a cara-cara walking the perimeter with its insolent strut. The camp was food-rich and bird-empty, and that combination doesn't occur naturally.

I got out with the rifle slung — not raised, just present — and walked in the way I'd want someone to walk into a place where I'd disappeared: slowly, visibly, talking to myself in a low voice so anything listening would hear a man and not a threat.

"Paloma Dunmore," I said to the tents. "Salvage contract. Nobody's here, I hope."

Nobody answered, which was the first honest thing the camp did.

The mess tent was the heart of it, and the heart was still beating. That's the only way I can describe what I found there. A folding table set for six. Tin plates with food on them — actual food, mutton stew gone gray and stiff with age, bread gone to stone, mate gourds sitting in their ring of salt-crusted wood. A kettle sat on a cold fire-ring outside, and when I lifted it, water sloshed inside. Half full. Someone had filled a kettle, set it to boil, and then — what? Walked away? In the middle of making tea?

Eleven days. In eleven days the foxes should have emptied every plate and scattered everything with a smell across fifty meters of steppe. The stew was untouched. The bread was untouched. Even the open sugar tin sat with its lid leaning against it, pristine, not a single ant track through it, and out here ants will find a sugar tin in an hour.

I stood in that tent with the flap breathing in the wind and felt the hair lift on my forearms. Not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The feeling you get when you realize a room has been staged for your benefit — that somewhere, someone arranged this scene knowing that eventually a pair of eyes would come looking, and wanted those eyes to see exactly this.

Radios intact. That was the detail that finished me. Two field radios sat on the equipment table, antennas up, cases closed, and when I opened the first one the battery indicator glowed green. Charged. A survey team that goes silent for nine days does not leave charged radios sitting in their tents unless the silence was chosen. Radios get smashed, drowned, drained, carried off. They do not sit in perfect condition waiting for salvage men.

I walked the whole site twice, reading it like weather. The drill rig was folded down properly, not abandoned mid-operation — but the borehole beside it had been capped with a steel plate bolted down with fresh torque marks, bolts so new they'd barely begun to oxidize. A crew that planned to come back and keep drilling caps a hole loosely. A crew that plans never to return caps it like a grave.

Six bedrolls. Six places at the table. Six names on the manifest the fixer had given me. I counted sleeping bags twice, then a third time, because the arithmetic mattered: six bags, six plates, and not one item of personal effect missing. Wallets in the footlockers — I didn't open them, just saw them through half-zipped flaps. Boots by the tent doors. One man's paperback novel lying face-down on his cot, spine cracked at the halfway mark, holding his place like a promise he'd meant to keep.

No tracks leaving.

That was the part I kept coming back to, walking the perimeter in widening circles until I was half a kilometer out and the camp was a smudge of canvas against the enamel sky. The approach tracks were there — the same two trucks and trailer, arriving from the south, the way I'd followed them for four days. Around the camp, the ground was churned with a week of ordinary work traffic. But there was no departure. No outbound tracks heading north, east, west. South, the arrival tracks simply ended at the edge of the work-churned ground, as if the trucks had grown there, like rocks.

Wind doesn't erase tire tracks selectively. Wind takes everything or nothing, and it had taken nothing. The arrival story was written complete on the ground, and the departure page had been torn out.

I crouched at the southern edge of the churned earth and put my fingers in the dirt and thought about the two swept stretches of road, sixty kilometers back. About the capped borehole with its fresh bolts. About the kettle with water in it, and the charged radios, and the birds that wouldn't come.

Here's what I knew, in the wordless way I know a storm is stacking itself beyond the mountains: this camp had not been abandoned. It had been *closed.* Closed by people who came after the survey team left — or after the survey team was made to leave — and who had then spent time arranging the scene. Time means hours. Hours mean vehicles and men and a deliberate, unhurried purpose. And then they had driven away south, dragging a brush behind them in two careful places, leaving the wilderness to hold the secret and, apparently, to advertise it.

Because that was the part that made no sense, and the part I couldn't stop turning over. If you're going to erase a crew off the face of the earth, why leave the camp so *legible?* Why leave the kettle half-full and the radios charged and the table set? A truly erased site looks like nothing — burned, scattered, collapsed, sad. This looked like a message. This looked like something that wanted to be found, by the right person, late enough that finding it wouldn't matter anymore.

The wilderness had hidden something here. And it wanted me to know it was hiding it.

I've spent my whole life being talked to by weather — pressure and light and the particular hush a steppe gets twenty minutes before a dust wall arrives. I know how it feels when the country is trying to tell you something. Standing at the edge of that churned ground, with the wind pushing grit past my ankles toward the north, I felt it again, stronger than any front I'd ever tracked: the sense of a held breath. Of six men somewhere under this enormous flat sky, or under the ground, or gone altogether, and one woman's voice — I couldn't have told you why a woman, then — waiting somewhere in the silence for someone stubborn enough to listen.

I walked back to the camp and did the things a salvage contractor does. Photographed everything. Logged positions. Drained the kettle and packed the radios, because gear was gear and gear was money, and money was the Ferro brothers' arithmetic running in my head whether I wanted it or not.

At the borehole, I knelt and laid my palm flat against the steel cap. Cold, even in the noon sun. Beneath it, whatever the team had drilled down to find was still down there, sealed and waiting, and I had the sudden animal certainty that the hole went deeper than any survey depth on my route sheet.

The barometer ticked downward, once, barely — the Andean front, still days out, still nothing. I noted it and forgot it.

Then, as I straightened up, I saw it. Out past the northern edge of the camp, where the bench dropped toward the dry tributary, a patch of coirón grass lay flattened in a line — not wind-flattened, which lies in waves, but pressed in a single direction, southeast, away from the camp, toward the broken country of the gorge. Dragged. Something heavy, or someone, taken or crawling, out into the maze of arroyos where my truck couldn't follow and my rifle wouldn't matter.

The line of flattened grass ran straight for twenty meters and then the ground broke apart into stone and thorn and shadow, and whatever story continued down there, the surface refused to write it.

I got back in the truck and sat for a moment with my hands on the wheel, looking at that dark seam in the earth.

Every instinct I owned said drive south, report the site, collect the fee, let the ministry send men with badges to walk into that gorge. Every debt I owed said the same thing louder.

But the kettle had been half-full. And somebody, somewhere out in that stone labyrinth, had left a scene that could only be read by a person who reads signs for a living — and I was beginning to suspect I hadn't found this camp by following tracks at all.

I'd been *invited.*

I started the engine and turned the nose of the truck toward the gorge.
