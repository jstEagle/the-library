## 4. The Woman Who Wouldn't Be Dead

Eleven days before a stranger pulled her out of a pit of guanaco bones, Dr. Inés Cabral knelt in the dust of the Río Deseado basin and watched a number ruin her life.

The number was 4.7. It lived on the screen of the portable spectrometer balanced on her knee, and it was wrong. Not instrument-wrong — she had calibrated twice that morning against the standard card, in the shade of the truck, cursing the wind that kept trying to sandblast the display. Not sample-wrong either, because this was the third core from the third hole in as many days, and all three told the same story: arsenic at 4.7 parts per million in the shallow aquifer, where the provincial water authority's own published survey said there should be nothing but clean fossil water and honest sand.

She sat back on her heels and looked east, where the steppe ran flat and yellow to a horizon so distant it seemed less like an edge than an opinion. The wind came off it the way it always did here, steady from the southwest, carrying the smell of dry grass and something faintly mineral, like licked stone. Somewhere behind her, the drill rig coughed through its rhythm — compress, lift, log — and Tomás Ferreyra was singing badly under his breath, some cumbia from his phone speaker, half-swallowed by engine noise.

"Inés." He leaned out of the rig cab, sweat-dark shirt, grin already loaded. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you make before you ruin someone's quarter."

She held up the spectrometer so he could see the screen across four meters of trampled ground. His grin died by degrees, like a lamp on a failing battery.

"That can't be right," he said.

"Third hole. Same number."

He climbed down and took the unit from her with both hands, the way you take bad news about family. He read it for a long time. Around them the camp went on being ordinary: the cook tent snapping in the wind, the generator's drone, Marcos Villalba — their team leader — standing at the tailgate of the lead truck with a satellite phone pressed to his ear, his back to them, his voice too low to carry over the rig.

Tomás looked up at her. "Run it again."

"I ran it three times."

"Run it again."

* * *

They ran it again. They ran everything again, that night and the next day and the day after that, while the rig chewed its patient holes down the survey grid and the wind polished the sky. Inés built a little empire of evidence inside her tent: printouts weighted with core samples in zip-lock bags, hand-drawn cross-sections of the aquifer showing the contamination plume spreading south and west from the old mining district near Jaramillo, sixty kilometers upstream in hydrological terms if not in any terms that appeared on a map. The plume had been moving for years. Decades, probably. The readings didn't lie about that; isotope dating never did.

What lied was the file.

On the fourth night she found it — or rather she failed not to find it, because she hadn't been looking, exactly, only cross-referencing their baseline data against the ministry's published aquifer survey for the Deseado basin, which every hydrologist in the province used, which she herself had used to design the grid. The published survey covered their exact coordinates. It listed sampling dates, well numbers, laboratory results. Clean results. And buried in the metadata, where almost no one ever looked, were the original lab submission codes.

She ran one. The archive returned a record: sample received, sample logged, sample **voided — administrative**.

She ran another. Voided. Another. Voided. Eleven of eleven.

There was no such thing as an administrative void in a government lab. Samples got voided for chain-of-custody breaks, for container failures, for clerical error — and each void carried a reason code and a signature. These carried none. Someone had reached into the record after the fact and erased the chemistry, and then someone else had written a tidy published survey on top of the erasure, and the tidy survey said the water was fine, and farmers were drilling into that water all along the dry side, and towns were piping it, and children were drinking it.

Inés sat in her tent with the laptop glowing and her hands very still on her knees, and she thought about the word *decade*, and she did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: how long a plume takes to move sixty kilometers through that kind of substrate, how long the wells downstream had been drawing from it, how many parts per million a growing body tolerates before the skin lesions start, before the cancers. She was a scientist. She knew how to make numbers mean people. That was supposed to be the whole point of her.

Outside, Marcos's tent glowed amber against the dark. She could hear him talking on the sat phone again, low and even, the same tone he'd used all week. Marcos talked to Buenos Aires every night. That wasn't strange; team leaders reported. But he always walked away from camp to do it, out past the trucks, into the wind, and when he came back he never said what had been said, and lately he came back with a particular set to his shoulders that she had finally, tonight, put a name to.

It was the set of a man carrying instructions he doesn't like.

* * *

She confronted him at breakfast, because she was thirty-nine years old and had spent twenty of those years in the field and had learned that ambushes work best in daylight, over mate, when no one can pretend later that tempers were high.

"The baseline data's been scrubbed," she said, and slid the printout across the folding table.

Marcos didn't look at it. That was the first thing. A man surprised by information looks at it. Marcos looked at her instead, and his face did something careful.

"Say that again."

"The ministry's published survey for this basin is fabricated. The underlying samples were voided administratively — no reason codes, no signatures. Our cores match the voided originals' coordinates exactly, which means whoever killed the data knew precisely where the contamination was worst and sampled there first." She kept her voice level, conversational, pitched under the wind. "Marcos. There are estancias on this aquifer. There's a school in Puerto Deseado on this aquifer."

He turned his mate gourd slowly in his hands, once, twice. Around them the crew moved through morning routine — Tomás fueling the compressor, young Delgado coiling cable, the dogs sleeping under the trucks in the last patch of shadow. Ordinary sounds. She would remember them afterward with a terrible clarity, the way you remember the light in a photograph of someone who is about to die.

"You should have come to me privately," Marcos said.

"I am privately. This is as private as it gets."

"It isn't." He glanced, just briefly, toward the ridge line north of camp. Empty ground. Nothing on it. But he glanced, and she saw it, and something cold began walking down her spine. "Listen to me, Inés. You're going to seal your data. All of it — cores, logs, spectra, your notes. Everything goes into the sealed case, and the case goes in my custody, per protocol, until we're back in Comodoro."

"Protocol is that raw data gets duplicated—"

"Protocol changed. Ministry directive, two weeks ago, flagged confidential. I couldn't tell you before deployment." He said it flatly, without apology, which was its own kind of confession: he had known since before they left. He had stood in the briefing room in Comodoro and watched her present her sampling plan — her beautiful, rigorous plan, designed around the published survey — and he had known the survey was a fiction, and he had said nothing, and driven them six hundred kilometers into the middle of it.

"Who signed the directive?" she asked.

"You know I can't give you that."

"Then give me this: is anyone acting on it? Is there a remediation order pending? A health advisory?"

Marcos looked at her for a long moment, and whatever moved behind his eyes was older and more tired than the man himself. "Inés," he said quietly, "you're asking whether the people who buried the data intend to dig it back up. Think about what you're asking."

The wind pushed against the cook tent. One of the dogs lifted its head, considered the horizon, and lay back down.

"Seal the data," Marcos said. "Please. For your own sake, seal it, and let me handle the rest. I've been doing this longer than you've been credentialed. There are ways to move things like this. Slow ways. Safe ways."

"And Tomás? Delgado? Do they seal too?"

"They don't know anything yet. Keep it that way."

She took the printout back. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "You walked away from camp to make your calls," she said. "Every night. Who are you reporting to, Marcos? Because it isn't the ministry science office. I know their reporting schedule. This is daily."

For one unguarded instant — she would keep that instant for the rest of her life, turn it over in the dark like a stone — Marcos Villalba looked frightened. Not of her. Of the question.

"Seal the data," he said, and picked up his gourd, and walked toward the rig, and did not look back.

* * *

That evening she pretended to comply.

She filled the sealed case with printouts — real ones, real data, but the duplicates, the second-run confirmations whose serial numbers she'd quietly altered by one digit weeks ago out of field habit, because instruments drift and you always keep a verification trail. The originals, the true chain, went into a place no protocol covered: the hollow drill casings, the ones Tomás had pulled and stacked for return shipment, capped and numbered and destined for a warehouse in Comodoro that nobody audited. Tomás helped her do it after midnight, working by red headlamp between the trucks, saying nothing except once, softly, as he threaded the drive casing home:

"My mother's village is on this water."

"I know," Inés said.

"So we're not stopping."

"We're not stopping."

It was, she understood even then, already too late for caution of any kind. Marcos made his call that night standing on the ridge, silhouetted against the last grey light, and when he came down he wouldn't meet her eyes, and the next morning the satellite phone was gone from the equipment manifest — signed out, transferred, *administrative* — and the radio check schedule changed without explanation, and Marcos started watching the northern horizon the way sailors watch weather.

On the ninth day, Delgado asked her, casually, over the generator noise, why the boss had asked him how far it was to the Bajo Caracoles turnoff. On the tenth day, a vehicle passed on the distant track — a white pickup with government plates, slowing, studying the camp through what she was nearly sure was a camera — and Marcos stood in the open with his hands visible at his sides until it went.

And on the eleventh day, Inés Cabral woke before dawn with the certainty of a falling barometer in her chest, and lay in her sleeping bag looking at the roof of her tent, and assembled the truth the way she assembled any dataset: without flinching, however much it hurt.

The disappearance had been arranged before they ever left Comodoro.

Not theirs — theirs was improvised, desperate, still being negotiated in whispers between her and Tomás. But the *story* of a vanished survey team, the convenient blank where eleven scientists and their inconvenient cores used to be — that had been drafted in an office somewhere, months ago, filed and approved, waiting only for the field season to provide the cast. Marcos hadn't led them into danger. Marcos had delivered them. And the fact that he spent his nights on the ridge arguing with someone, the fact that his hands shook now when he poured the mate, meant only that he had loved them a little, and obeyed anyway.

She lay in the dark and listened to the wind move over the steppe, and she thought: they will not shoot us. That would leave bodies, questions, a shape. They will simply remove us — our vehicles found abandoned, our radios intact, a meal half-eaten, tracks that stop. The wilderness will be blamed. The wilderness hides things; everyone knows that. And somewhere a minister will sign a condolence letter, and the sealed case will go into a warehouse, and the published survey will stand, and the water will stay fine, officially, forever.

Unless the data came out.

Unless someone walked out of the blank they'd drawn on the map, carrying it.

She got up in the cold, and woke Tomás with two fingers pressed to his shoulder, and put her mouth close to his ear.

"Tonight," she whispered. "Before they decide the scene. We go south, on foot, no lights, and we take the casings' index with us, and you—" her voice caught, steadied— "you write down where everything is, in your head, nowhere else. If we get separated, the location dies with one of us. Never both."

Tomás looked at her in the red dark, and nodded, and smiled — that terrible, gallant smile of his, the one that made jokes at funerals — and began, silently, to pack.

Above the tents, the southern sky was beginning to pale, and the wind had shifted, and somewhere out on the black steppe a guanaco screamed once, briefly, and stopped.

Inés stood in the doorway of her tent and looked north, toward the empty ridge where Marcos had made his last report, and felt the whole vast machinery of the state settle into motion around her, patient and quiet as the desert itself.

They had eleven hours, maybe fewer.

She did not yet know that she would spend most of them underground, breathing through a reed, listening to boots.
