## 5. One Survivor, No Story

The guanaco pits sat a kilometer off the survey line, downwind of everything, which was the whole point of them. The estancia that owned the concession rendered carcasses there—bones and blood and the sweet-rotten smell of a place where things went to be forgotten. I'd smelled it from two ridges away and almost turned back. Almost.

I'd spent the morning doing what I'd done for three days: reading the steppe like a page someone had tried to erase. The survey team's tracks ran north along the dry side, orderly as ledger lines, and then they didn't stop so much as thin out, the way a sentence trails off when the writer loses his nerve. No skid marks. No scatter. Vehicles don't vanish; people do.

But the wind had been working on this country for eleven days, and wind is honest even when men aren't. It had pulled a thread of plastic from a shallow depression near the third pit—blue tarp, government gray underneath—and tarp doesn't walk off by itself.

So I walked toward the smell.

The pit was maybe four meters across, scooped into the caliche, half full of desiccated carcasses stacked with the indifference of a job nobody wanted. Guanaco mostly, one sheep, the white bone showing through split hide like ribs through wet paper. And in among them, folded into the shadow of a ribcage the size of a doorway, something moved.

I had the pistol out before my brain finished the thought. Three days alone on the dry side does that to you. I stood at the lip of the pit with the sun flat on my back and said, in the tone you use for animals and creditors, "I can see you."

The thing in the bones didn't move.

"I've got a rifle," I added, which was true, though it was slung across my back where she—if it was a she—couldn't see it, and we both knew how conversations like this actually go.

A long minute. Then a voice, cracked down to the wire but level, said: "Then shoot, or help me out. Standing here costs me water."

It was a woman's voice, and it was angry, and anger meant fuel, and fuel meant alive. I holstered the pistol and looked down into the pit properly.

She was wedged between two carcasses like driftwood jammed against a piling. Gray field jacket, government issue, name tape torn off. Hair matted with dust and something darker. Her left boot was missing its laces—tied with wire, I noticed later, stripped from a fence or a drill casing. Her lips were split and her eyes were the color of tea gone cold, and they were looking at me the way you look at a card you've just been dealt: deciding what game it belongs to.

"How long have you been down there?" I asked.

"Since Tuesday." She didn't know what day it was. She'd picked a day at random and committed to it, and I remember thinking, even then, *that's not shock, that's discipline.*

"Tuesday was six days ago."

"Then since Tuesday," she said, and held out her hand.

* * *

Getting her out took twenty minutes and cost me most of my patience. She weighed nothing, which frightened me more than if she'd weighed too much—the body eating itself, running on whatever the mind kept feeding it. But her hands were steady on the rope, and when I told her to put her foot there, she put her foot there, first time, no argument. That kind of obedience isn't weakness. It's a person who has decided to survive and delegated nothing to luck.

She came over the lip and stood swaying in the sun like a fence post after a flood, and then she did something I still think about. She turned around and looked down into the pit—at the carcasses, at the dent in the caliche where her body had pressed a hollow over six days—and she nodded once, small, like a woman thanking a house.

"Water," she said.

I gave her the bottle and watched her drink the way you're supposed to watch anyone coming out of the dead country: small sips, timed, no gulping. She rationed herself better than I would have. When she handed it back there was a third of it gone and she looked like she could have drunk a lake and had chosen not to, and I understood I wasn't looking at a victim. I was looking at a professional.

"Name," I said.

"Cabral. Inés Cabral." A beat. "Bióloga. Río Deseado survey."

The name meant nothing to me, which was the first lie, because the fixer in Puerto Deseado had given me a list of seven names and hers hadn't been on it—or rather, it had, but attached to a photograph of a rounder face and a different haircut, and grief and starvation will hollow a person fast enough to explain that. So maybe it wasn't a lie. Maybe it was just a truth wearing a coat that didn't fit.

"And the rest of your team?"

"Gone." She said it flat, the way you say a word you've said so many times it's worn smooth. "We were hit at night. Not bandits—they didn't take anything. They took us." Her eyes came up to mine. "You're not police."

"No."

"Not military."

"No."

"Salvage, then." She said the word like she was tasting it. "Someone sent you to pick over what's left of us."

I let that sit. On the dry side, silence is a tool same as a shovel—you use it to see what fills the hole. She filled it herself, which told me plenty.

"The ministry contracted recovery of equipment," I said. "Vehicles, instruments, records. Personnel recovery if encountered." That last part was nearly true. The contract had said *personnel*, singular, in pencil, in a fixer's handwriting, and the fixer had smiled when he wrote it, and I had been too busy counting zeros to ask why a rescue clause was written like an afterthought.

"Instruments," she repeated. "Records." Something moved behind her eyes, quick as a lizard under a stone. "And what do you do with personnel, when you encounter them? Contractually speaking."

"Contractually speaking, I bring them in."

"To whom?"

"The ministry."

She looked at me for a long moment. The wind came off the pit and brought the smell with it, and neither of us flinched, which was its own kind of introduction.

"You should know," she said finally, "that I'm going to tell you a story now. And you should decide whether it matters to you whether it's true."

That's not what people say. People lie and hope you swallow it, or they tell the truth and hope you reward it. Nobody hands you the knife handle-first unless they want to see what you do with it.

"Tell it," I said.

"We were drilling cores along the grid. Routine hydrology. Eleven nights ago, vehicles came without lights—I counted four, maybe five. Tomás heard them first. He always heard things first." Her voice didn't waver, but it changed key, the way a road changes surface. "They separated us. Took the trucks, the samples, the radios. They put me in that pit because dead things go there and nobody looks twice. They thought the coyotes would finish the accounting." She paused. "They don't come downwind of their own kills. Coyotes. I learned that the hard way, the first night."

"And you stayed six days in a carcass pit instead of walking out."

"Walk where?" She spread her arms, taking in the steppe—the flat horizon, the heat shimmer, the hundred kilometers of nothing in every direction. "There's one road and whoever used it to bury us owns it. I stayed where they put me because a body in a pit is invisible and a body walking is a target. I drank condensation off the tarps at dawn. I ate—" she glanced at the pit, and for the first time something human crossed her face, something tired and disgusted and proud all at once, "—I ate things I won't describe to a man who might buy me lunch someday."

It was a good story. That was the problem. Good stories are load-bearing; they hold weight because every beam is cut to fit. Six days in a pit, surviving on dew and discipline—it explained the state of her, the missing laces, the wire, the rationed water. It explained everything except why a government biologist talked about her own ministry like a woman describing the man who burned down her house.

* * *

I got her into the truck and pointed it south, toward the fuel cache at Gobernador Gregores, and for an hour neither of us spoke. She sat with her ruined boots on my toolbox and watched the country slide past like she was memorizing it, and I watched her watching it, and I did arithmetic.

Seven names on the list. One survivor. A salvage contract paying double my debt, written in pencil, with a cleanup clause I'd skimmed past at the time and now couldn't stop hearing in the fixer's cheerful voice: *recover all materials of interest, Mr. Dunmore. All.* Materials of interest. Instruments. Records. Personnel.

I'd told myself it was a search job. Lying to yourself is easy on the dry side; there's no one out here to contradict you.

"Your team leader," I said, to watch her flinch. She didn't. "What was his name?"

"Marcos Ferreyra." Instant, clean. Too clean—a name rehearsed until it came out polished. "He'd have fought them. He was ex-army."

"Ex-army men leave tracks when they fight. There were no tracks."

"There were no tracks because they buried him where they buried the samples, and you're not looking for him, you're looking for instruments." She turned those cold-tea eyes on me. "You keep saying *records*. Why does a salvage man care about records?"

Because the contract pays triple for them, I didn't say. Because the fixer underlined the word twice, I didn't say. Because I've been poor long enough to know that when rich men underline a word, the word is worth more than the people around it.

"Habit," I said. "Paper tells you where the money went."

Something passed over her face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a healthier woman. "Yes," she said. "It does."

We stopped at a dry wash to check the truck's shackle mounts, an excuse to stand somewhere other than inside each other's silence. While I worked the wrench she crouched at the wash edge, cupped a little sand, let it run through her fingers, and I realized she was orienting. Reading the country. Marking something south-southeast of us with a look that lasted half a second too long.

"What's out there?" I asked.

"Nothing." She stood, brushed her hands. "Scenery."

Second lie. I was keeping count now, the way I keep count of pressure drops before a storm. First lie: the name, possibly. Second lie: the direction. Both lies pointing the same way, which meant they weren't lies at all, really—they were a map she was drawing for herself and letting me glimpse the corner of.

Here's what I knew about myself by then: I read weather nobody else can feel. My father called it the family curse—we stand in air other men call clear and taste the storm three days out. And standing in that wash with a starving liar and a contract written in pencil, I tasted something coming that had nothing to do with sky. Pressure falling. Two systems converging: a woman carrying something worth killing for, and men paid to make sure it never arrived. I was standing in the seam between them, and both sides thought I was theirs.

"Mr. Dunmore." She'd read my name off the rental papers in the glovebox while I pretended not to notice. "When we reach Gregores—who do you call?"

"The ministry. Standard protocol."

"And if I asked you not to?"

I set the wrench down slowly. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because your contract says *personnel, if encountered*," she said, "and I've been thinking about what that word was doing in a salvage agreement at all. Rescue clauses aren't afterthoughts, Mr. Dunmore. Somebody wrote me into your paperwork the way you write a line item. Which means somebody knows I'm a line item. Which means—" she lifted one shoulder, a small, terrible shrug, "—somebody knows I'm not supposed to be found alive, and budgeted accordingly."

The wind moved through the wash and carried the faint sweetness of the pits after us, and I thought about the fixer's smile, and the underlined word, and the second crew I hadn't met yet and would meet sooner than either of us knew.

"So here's my offer," Inés Cabral said, and her voice had stopped being a survivor's voice and become something older and harder, a voice from before the pit, maybe from before the survey, a voice that had made decisions in rooms I'd never see. "You drive me where I need to go, and I'll show you what your *records* are worth. Not to the ministry." Her eyes held mine and didn't blink. "To you."

"And where do you need to go?"

"South," she said, glancing once, briefly, at the empty quarter southeast of the wash—the direction she'd marked with sand running through her fingers. "There's something I buried before they came. Something they didn't find. And you're the only vehicle on this side of the steppe that isn't looking for it."

I should have driven her to Gregores. I should have made the call, collected the fee, paid the Ferros, gone back to chasing dying storms for tourists who wanted photographs of weather at a safe distance. Every instinct I owned—and my instincts were the one inheritance my father left me besides the debt—said *this woman is a locked room and the men who own you hold the key.*

Instead I heard myself say: "Show me on the map."

She smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all week, because it was the smile of a woman who has just decided you might be useful, and I recognized it, because I was wearing the same one.
