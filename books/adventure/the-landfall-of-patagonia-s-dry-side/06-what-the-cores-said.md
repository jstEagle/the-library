## 6. What the Cores Said

The core sample came up at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Ines Cabral knew before the barrel cleared the casing that something was wrong with it.

She knew by the smell.

Eleven days ago, and she could still summon that smell now, lying in a guanaco pit with her broken arm cradled against her chest like something stolen. Sulfur, faint but unmistakable, threaded through the wet mineral scent of deep groundwater. Water from an aquifer forty meters down should have smelled of nothing at all. Clean water is shy. It doesn't announce itself. This water had walked into the room wearing cologne.

"Tomás," she said. "Come smell this."

He came across the drill pad with his usual unhurried gait, the one that drove Marta crazy because he never hurried for anything, not weather, not deadlines, not the ministerial schedule that governed their lives like a second sun. He leaned over the split barrel, sniffed once, and his face did something small and terrible. It went still. Not surprised—still. The way a man's face goes still when he recognizes a voice he'd hoped never to hear again.

"Huh," he said.

"That's all? *Huh?*"

"What do you want me to say, Ines. I can't say what we're both thinking out here." He glanced toward the truck where their team leader, Salgado, sat with the satellite phone pressed to his ear, doing his twice-daily check-in with the ministry. "Log it. Bag it. We'll talk tonight."

They talked that night in the survey tent while Salgado slept in the cab of the pickup, and Tomás laid it out with the flat calm he reserved for things that frightened him most. He'd been on the Río Deseado grid before—not this survey, an earlier one, three years back, a hydrological baseline study. He remembered the numbers. He remembered them because they'd been good numbers, publishable numbers, the kind that made a young hydrologist feel like the steppe was a place you could trust.

The aquifer beneath the Deseado basin fed eleven estancias, two towns, and the seasonal wetlands that kept half the region's birdlife alive. Three years ago it ran clean. Now the cores were showing conductivity readings off the chart, sulfate and heavy metals in concentrations that made Ines's stomach fold over on itself, and the plume signature—the shape of the contamination as it moved through the rock—was textbook mining runoff. Tailings leachate, moving south-southwest through the fractured basalt, exactly along the gradient you'd expect if someone upstream had been dumping or leaking for years.

The nearest mine was the Cerro Vanguardia complex, a hundred and forty kilometers north.

"There's no way that's natural," Ines said. "The isotopes alone will show—"

"They'll show," Tomás agreed. "That's the problem."

* * *

Ines had spent her career believing in a simple architecture: you took the measurement, you wrote down the truth, you filed the report, and the truth did its slow work in the world. She was thirty-four years old and she had built her whole life on top of that architecture the way a house sits on a foundation, never once examining it, because examining your foundation means admitting it could fail.

On the fourth morning after the first bad core, Salgado called a team meeting at breakfast and told them, pleasantly, that the ministry wanted the hydrological module of the survey expedited. No more coring on the southern grid squares. Move north, finish the geomorphology work, come home early. Budget reasons, he said. He smiled when he said it. He had very good teeth and used them often, and until that morning Ines had found him merely tiresome.

Marta asked the obvious question: what about the anomalous samples?

"Flagged and sealed," Salgado said. "Standard procedure for data under review. You don't want to release preliminary numbers that might embarrass the ministry before verification, do you?" He said it lightly, jokingly, the way you'd tease a junior colleague about a typo. Then he looked at each of them in turn, and the joke drained out of his eyes and left them flat as the horizon. "Sealed means sealed. The drives stay with me."

That night Ines lay in her tent listening to the wind comb through the coiron grass and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding its level, that the disappearance had been arranged before they ever left Comodoro.

Not their disappearance. The *data's*. They were already, in some ledger she hadn't been shown, a closed account. A survey team whose findings would never be verified because the team would never file them. She didn't yet know what form it would take—an accident, a reassignment, a quiet bureaucratic erasure—but she knew the shape of it the way she knew the shape of a contamination plume: by its gradient. By which way the pressure pointed.

Salgado's satellite phone calls happened at the same hours every day. His tent faced away from theirs. On the fifth night she saw him burning paper in a coffee can, and the smoke smelled of toner.

* * *

It was Tomás who moved first, because Tomás had grown up in Puerto Deseado when the fishing fleet still worked, and he had learned early what men did when the sea stopped giving. He didn't panic. He *prepared*.

"Ines," he said, crouched beside her at the drill rig on the sixth day, feeding casing into the hole while Salgado stood fifty meters off talking to the ministry about nothing, "how good are you at memorizing?"

"Why?"

"Because we're going to hide copies of everything, and then we're going to hide the fact that we hid it." He turned the casing wrench with a soft metallic click. "Salgado checks the main drives every night. Fine. Let him. But every sample we've run has a duplicate log, and every core has a duplicate assay pending, and I've been writing it all to these." From his breast pocket he drew three memory drives, small as seeds, dull gray, unremarkable. "Field redundancy protocol. Every legitimate survey runs one. Nobody audits redundancy drives because nobody thinks about them. That's our whole plan: to be the thing nobody thinks about."

"And where do they go?"

He smiled, and for the first time since the sulfur smell, it reached his eyes. "Where we've been drilling holes for six days. Think about it. What's the one place on this steppe where a metal tube full of instruments is supposed to be?"

She got it a beat later and laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound. "The casings."

"The casings. Sealed, capped, logged, and mapped. Anyone who comes out here looking finds exactly what the paperwork says is here: boreholes. And if anyone ever pulls a casing—" he shrugged, "—they find a drive taped inside the upper joint, above the water table, dry as a sermon. I've got the coordinates written three ways. One copy goes in my boot. One goes in your field notebook, coded as bench marks. And one stays in my head, because my head is the one place they can't search without asking."

"You've thought about them searching us."

"I've thought about Salgado's coffee-can fires," Tomás said quietly. "I've thought about how a man burns paper only after he's decided the paper shouldn't exist. Yeah. I've thought about it."

* * *

Over the next two days they hid four drives in four casings across the southern grid, working in plain sight, in daylight, because the best hiding place is the middle of ordinary work. Ines coded the coordinates into her notebook as elevation benchmarks—BM-1 through BM-4—and felt, absurdly, like a child playing a game whose rules adults couldn't see. Meanwhile Salgado grew more cheerful by the hour. That was the detail that frightened her most, afterward. Not the sealed drives, not the burned paper. The cheerfulness. A man planning murder is grim; a man planning paperwork is happy.

On the eighth evening, Marta cornered her by the water drums. Marta was the geophysicist, twenty-nine, loud, brilliant, the kind of woman who argued with instruments and won.

"You and Tomás are doing something," she said. Not a question.

"We're following protocol."

"Don't." Marta glanced toward Salgado's tent, then back, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Don't follow protocol at me. I ran the EM sweep over the southern grid myself. I know what's under us. I flagged it in my own private log two days before you pulled the first dirty core, and I watched Salgado read my summary sheet and smile at it. Smile, Ines. Like it was good news." Her hands were shaking, so she shoved them in her pockets, and Ines saw that Marta had been carrying this alone for days and had nearly been crushed by the weight of pretending. "So either you tell me what you two are doing, or I assume you're part of it, and I promise you I will make noise like this steppe has never heard."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. Then she took out her field notebook, opened it to the benchmark page, and put it in Marta's hands.

Marta read it twice. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than Ines had ever seen them.

"Bench marks," Marta said. "God. Okay. Okay. What do you need?"

"Nothing," Ines said. "That's the point. You need to know nothing, visibly, loudly. Be annoyed with us. Complain that Tomás hogs the rig. If they're watching—and they are—give them a version of you that's harmless."

"And if it goes wrong?"

Ines didn't answer, because there was no honest answer, and Marta nodded slowly as if Ines had spoken anyway.

"If it goes wrong," Marta said, "you get it out. Not the ministry. Not the department head—he plays golf with the mining secretary, everyone knows it. Out. Press, courts, whatever it takes. Promise me."

"I promise."

Marta held out her hand, and they shook on it over the water drums, under a sky going bruise-colored in the east, and it was the last time Ines would ever touch her friend's hand, though of course neither of them knew it. That's the cruelty of last times. They wear the costume of ordinary times. They shake your hand and complain about the coffee and walk away whistling.

* * *

The ninth day was the day Salgado announced they were breaking camp.

"Ministry directive," he said, folding the printout with great care. "Survey suspended pending review. We pull out tomorrow at first light. All equipment, all samples, all drives." He beamed at them. "Congratulations, team. You've done excellent work."

Excellent work. Ines excused herself, walked out past the drill pads into the grass, and was sick against a coiron clump, and knelt there afterward with her forehead nearly touching the earth, feeling through her knees the faint vibration of the aquifer below—forty meters down, poisoned, patient, invisible—and thought: *this is what it feels like to be buried alive and keep working.*

That night Tomás came to her tent last, after lights-out, and crouched in the dark doorway.

"Change of plan," he whispered. "We don't wait for them to decide what happens to us. Tomorrow, when we break camp, I'm driving the support truck with the casings. There's a delay I can build into the loading—twenty minutes, maybe thirty, while Salgado does his final call. That's your window. Take the quad, take the western track, get to the route, flag down any vehicle heading south. You carry the coordinates; I keep the drives with me. Separated, we're harder to erase. Together we're one accident."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not leaving *me*, you're leaving *with the truth*." His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Ines. Listen to me. Everything we did, we did so that one person walks out of here knowing where the bodies are buried—literally, in this case, the casings. If we both stay and it goes wrong, the steppe keeps its secret and Cerro Vanguardia keeps its profits and eleven estancias drink poison for another decade. One of us walks. That was always the math."

"And you decided the math without me."

"I decided it Tuesday," he said. "You were busy being sick behind the coiron."

She grabbed his sleeve, and in the dark his hand covered hers, steady as bedrock.

"Tomás. Say it back to me. Where are they?"

"BM-1 through BM-4. Southern grid. Upper joints, above the water line. Casing three is the master—it has the full redundant set plus the EM sweeps Marta smuggled onto my laptop the night before she—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Plus the sweeps. You know the rest."

"Say the coordinates."

He said them. She made him say them twice, and then a third time, and then she made him quiz her until she could recite them cold, forward and backward, the way her father had made her recite tide tables as a girl. Somewhere in the middle of the third repetition she realized she was crying, silently, and that Tomás was letting her, and that neither of them mentioned it, because some things you seal and log and file in the place where no one audits.

"Tomorrow," he said. "First light. Whatever you hear behind you—engine trouble, shouting, anything—you don't stop. You don't come back. You go south until you find a road, and then you keep going south until you find a person, and then you tell them everything except where the drives are, until you find someone worth telling."

"And if I find no one?"

"Then you walk to Comodoro," he said, "and you knock on doors until one opens."

He squeezed her hand once, hard, and was gone into the dark between the tents, and Ines lay awake the rest of the night listening to the wind and memorizing the sound of her friends breathing in their tents thirty meters away, because some part of her already understood she was building an archive of them—that she would spend the rest of her life reaching for the sound of Tomás's slow breath and Marta's snoring and Salgado's absence of conscience, and that memory was the last redundancy drive, the one no one could confiscate.

* * *

First light came up the color of old bone.

She heard the engines start, heard the loading begin, heard Tomás swear artistically about a stuck tie-down strap exactly on cue. She walked to the quad with her pack, nodded good morning to Salgado, who smiled his excellent teeth at her and said, "Ready for home?" and she said, "Can't wait," and meant it in a way he would never understand.

She was forty meters past the perimeter, engine running, when she heard the shot.

One shot. Flat, small, swallowed instantly by the size of the country. Then a second, farther, ragged. Then Salgado's voice, high and wrong, saying words she couldn't parse, and then a silence so complete she could hear her own blood.

Every plan she had said: *go.* Tomás's voice in her head said: *you don't stop.*

She stopped.

She turned the quad around, because grief is not obedient, and love does not follow protocol, and she was back at the camp in ninety seconds that lasted nine years, and what she found there rearranged the rest of her life into the shape it would hold forever: the support truck door open, Tomás on the ground beside the rear wheel with his boot half off, one hand under him, the other stretched toward the open casing locker where the drives lay in their foam slots like eggs in a nest. Marta's body by the water drums, turned away from her, and Ines would be grateful for that small mercy for the rest of her life, that she never saw her friend's face again after the handshake.

And Salgado standing by the radio mast with a pistol held low and loose, looking at her with an expression of genuine, almost apologetic regret, saying, "Dr. Cabral. You should have taken the quad west."

What happened next happened fast and badly and saved her life by accident: she reversed the quad at full throttle, blind, and the world became dust and engine scream and a single impact that folded her against the fuel tank and broke her left arm in two places, and she heard rather than felt the bullet that passed through the space her head had occupied, and then she was down in the arroyo behind the camp with the quad dead and her arm screaming and the coordinates running through her head like a prayer wheel—BM-1 through BM-4, upper joints, above the water line, casing three is the master—

She crawled. For how long she never knew. An hour, three. The steppe closed over her like water over a dropped stone, and somewhere in the crawling she passed the guanaco carcass pit—a rendering crew's dump, bones and hide and the sweet-rot stink of the dry side—and some animal instinct older than science told her: *here. Down. Disappear.*

She pulled the carcass waste over herself with her right hand, arranged her broken arm against her chest, and lay in the dark of the pit breathing through her mouth, listening to vehicles move around the camp above, doors opening and closing, Salgado's voice giving calm instructions, the world above her being tidied into a story with no survivors.

And in that dark, in that stink, with her friends' blood cooling two hundred meters away, the grief in her chest did the only thing grief knows how to do when it refuses to die: it hardened. Set like cement. Became structure. Became load-bearing.

*Bring it out,* she told herself, in Tomás's voice, in Marta's voice, in her own. *Or die beside it. Those are the only two endings on the menu.*

Above her, an engine revved and faded south, taking the liars away.

Below her, forty meters down, the poisoned water moved through the dark rock, patient as a debt.

And somewhere out on the steppe, she would learn much later, a man who owed money to dangerous brothers was following a set of tracks that stopped for no reason, driving toward the only survivor of the Río Deseado survey, toward the pit where she lay keeping her appointment with the truth.

She didn't know his name yet. She didn't know yet whether he'd be her rescue or her executioner, and wouldn't for days.

But she was alive, and the coordinates were intact, and the drives waited in their steel sleeves under the coiron grass like seeds planted against a spring that hadn't come yet.

Ines Cabral closed her eyes in the carcass pit and began, methodically, to plan how a dead woman would dig.
