## 7. The Second Crew

The fuel stop at Gobernador Costa was the kind of place that existed only because two roads happened to cross there and nobody had the energy to move them. A single pump under a corrugated awning, a kiosk selling warm soda and cold empanadas of uncertain vintage, a dog asleep in the shade of a water tank with its paws twitching through some dream of rabbits or death. The wind came off the steppe flat and constant, carrying the smell of dry grass and dust and nothing else, the way it did for three hundred kilometers in every direction.

Paloma had been running on four hours of sleep and a thermos of coffee he'd brewed at dawn in the cab of his truck while Ines slept under the tarp in the back, curled around her backpack like it was a child she didn't trust him with. Which, to be fair, it was.

He'd left her there on purpose. Not out of malice — he'd told himself — but because a woman who'd survived eleven days alone on the dry side deserved forty minutes of uninterrupted sleep, and because he wanted fuel and food without having to narrate every transaction to someone who counted his words the way he counted his money: carefully, and always expecting less than advertised.

The pump attendant was a boy of maybe nineteen with a sunburned neck and the particular slouch of someone who had grown up in a place where the horizon never changed. Paloma paid cash for diesel, as he always did, and bought the empanadas, and was carrying them back across the gravel lot when he saw the truck.

It was parked at the far end of the awning, angled so its rear doors faced away from the road. A Toyota Hilux, white once, now the color of old teeth, with a bull bar welded on and a roof rack loaded down with jerry cans and a spare wheel and something lashed under a tarp that had the wrong shape for luggage. Dust caked it thick enough to write in, which somebody had — a child's hand-drawn flower on the rear fender, half erased by wind.

None of that was remarkable. Half the trucks on the dry side looked like that. What stopped Paloma mid-stride, what turned the warm empanadas in his hand suddenly heavy, was the sticker on the back window: a faded decal of a condor over a mountain range, the kind given away by drilling companies at trade shows in Comodoro five, six years ago.

He knew that sticker. He'd seen it eleven days ago, pressed into the mud beside an abandoned camp where the kettles were still warm.

* * *

Paloma did not look directly at the truck again. That was the first rule of reading country, and it applied just as well to reading men: you didn't stare at the thing you were trying to understand. You took it in sideways, in pieces, the way you read a sky — one cloud at a time, building the picture in your head where no one could see you doing it.

He walked to the kiosk instead. Bought a bag of alfajores he didn't want, stood in the doorway eating one, and let his eyes do their slow work.

Two men sat at a plastic table outside the kiosk's side door, playing cards. One was big through the shoulders with a shaved head going gray at the temples and forearms that had been broken at least once and set by someone who wasn't a doctor. The other was thin and quick-moving, with a mustache trimmed too carefully for the steppe and sunglasses pushed up into his hair even though the light was flat and merciless and any sensible man would have been wearing them.

They weren't dressed like gauchos and they weren't dressed like oil workers and they weren't dressed like police. They were dressed the way men dress when they've been told to look unremarkable by someone who has never been unremarkable in his life: clean boots, new jackets, a truck washed more recently than anything that far from pavement should be. The Hilux's windshield had that faint rainbow sheen of glass replaced within the last month.

And there was a second vehicle. Parked behind the water tank, mostly hidden, nose pointed south. A Ford Ranger, black, with a canopy over the bed and antennas — two of them — mounted on the back corners.

Paloma ate his alfajor slowly and thought about arithmetic.

Eleven days since the Río Deseado team vanished. Nine days since the fixer in Comodoro slid a contract across a table written in pencil, paying double what salvage was worth, with a clause about telling no one that had been phrased not like secrecy but like grief — *the ministry wants this handled quietly, for the families.* Eight days since he'd driven north with a satellite phone he hadn't asked for and coordinates he couldn't verify and a bad feeling he'd drowned in the thought of his sister's lease and the Ferro brothers' cheerful voices.

Three days since he'd pulled Dr. Ines Cabral out of a guanaco pit.

And now, four hundred kilometers south of where the tracks stopped, sitting at a fuel stop on the only road between him and Comodoro, were two trucks full of men whose equipment said *expedition* and whose clothes said *not expedition* and whose posture said *we are waiting for someone.*

He finished the alfajor. He bought a second bag, for Ines, and a bottle of water, and he made himself walk back past the pump at an ordinary pace, and as he passed the card table he let his gaze slide across the two men the way a man's gaze slides across anything when he's thinking about lunch.

The big one wasn't looking at him. The thin one was.

Not staring. Just watching him over the top of his cards with the unhurried attention of a man checking a list, and when Paloma met the look — accidentally, naturally, the way strangers meet looks at fuel stops all across Patagonia — the thin man smiled and nodded, friendly as Sunday.

Paloma nodded back and kept walking.

His hands were steady. His heart was not.

* * *

He drove two kilometers down the road before pulling off behind a low rise of basalt, killing the engine, and climbing into the back of the truck.

Ines woke the way she'd clearly learned to wake out here — instantly, completely, one hand already inside the tarp where she kept the knife he'd pretended not to notice her taking from his toolbox. Then she saw it was him and the hand came out empty and her face went back to its public arrangement: guarded, dry, faintly amused, a woman permanently ready to be disappointed.

"You're back. Did you bring food or did you bring news? Your face says news."

"Empanadas." He handed her the bag. "Eat while I talk."

She sat up, unwrapped one, and waited. She was good at waiting. Eleven days buried in a carcass pit will teach a person patience, or select ruthlessly for those who already have it.

"There's a truck back at the station," Paloma said. "White Hilux. Condor-and-mountains sticker on the back window."

Something changed in her face. Small, controlled, but real — the flicker of a woman seeing a ghost walk through a wall. "That's Yacimientos Sur," she said. "Drilling services. They subcontracted logistics for our survey. Their trucks moved our gear north from Comodoro."

"I know. I saw the same sticker in the mud at your camp."

"So it's coincidence." She said it like a scientist stating a hypothesis she intended to enjoy disproving. "Half the drill fleets in the province use—"

"There were two vehicles. The second one's got a comms array that costs more than my truck. Two men playing cards like they've got nowhere to be, wearing jackets that haven't seen a day of work. Windshield's been replaced recently — you can see the tint doesn't match the rest of the glass." He crouched so his eyes were level with hers. "Doc, nobody replaces a windshield on the dry side unless they broke it doing something they needed a windshield for. And nobody drives four hundred kilometers of gravel with two radios unless they're talking to somebody who isn't on this road."

Ines set the empanada down. He watched her run the same arithmetic he had, watched her arrive at the same number and hate it.

"They're looking for me," she said.

"Maybe. Or they're looking for what you're carrying. Or—" He made himself say the rest of it, because saying it out loud would tell him how true it was. "Or they already know where you are, and they're waiting to see which way we come."

The wind moved the tarp. Somewhere far off, a caracara called, that harsh descending note that always sounded like laughter at someone's expense.

"The fixer," Ines said quietly. "The man who hired you. What exactly did he ask you to recover?"

"Equipment. Data if convenient. And—" Paloma heard his own voice go flat, the way it went when he read a sky he didn't like. "And to report location immediately on finding survivors. Before moving them. There was a number to call."

"And did you call it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Because the number hadn't felt right, he'd told himself. Because government numbers always felt wrong. Because he'd wanted a day with her first, to figure out what she knew, to figure out what she was worth. All true. None of it the truth.

"Because a job that easy-paying is never easy," he said. "I knew that when I signed. I signed anyway. That's on me."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Out here her face had lost whatever softness a laboratory allows and gained something older — the look of stone that has decided what shape it is. "Then listen to me, Paloma Dunmore, because I've had eleven days to think about this and you've had three minutes." Her voice stayed low and even, which somehow made it worse. "Our team leader, Marcos, got his orders sealed before we ever left Comodoro. I know that now. Someone at the ministry knew what we'd find under that grid two years ago and sent us out anyway — not to find it, to *confirm* it, quietly, so the file would exist and then disappear. When Tomás hid the duplicate cores, when we started asking questions, we didn't vanish because of weather or bandits or bad luck. We vanished because we became a problem, and someone paid to make problems go away." She picked the empanada back up, deliberately, and took a bite, and chewed, and swallowed, a small act of ordinary humanity performed like defiance. "You weren't hired to salvage anything. You were hired because dogs find things faster than men do. You're the bloodhound. They let you loose on our scent, and these gentlemen—" she tilted her head back toward the road, toward the fuel stop invisible beyond the rise, "—are the leash."

* * *

Paloma sat on the tailgate and ate an empanada he could not taste and made himself think like a weatherman, because that was the only thinking he trusted.

Weathermen didn't panic at clouds. Weathermen looked at pressure, at wind direction, at what the system was doing upstream, and built a picture of what was coming and when. So.

Fact: the Ferro brothers held his debt. The Ferro brothers were cheerful, patient, and had once explained to him, over wine, with genuine warmth, exactly which hospital his mother used and how long the drive was from their office. Fact: the fixer who'd hired him had known things about his finances that only the Ferros should have known — had mentioned, casually, that certain parties were *motivated* to see him solvent again. Paloma had filed that at the time as a lender leaning on a client. He was filing it differently now.

Fact: the Ferros' money came from somewhere. Lenders that size, patient that long, in a province that poor, didn't live on interest from truck drivers and shopkeepers. They lived on contracts. On being useful to people whose names never appeared on paper.

Fact: a government that buried an aquifer needed deniable muscle. And deniable muscle needed a bloodhound that didn't know it was a bloodhound.

The picture assembled itself in his head the way storm cells did — not one cloud but a structure, a rotation, everything feeding everything. The Ferros had lent him money for years. The Ferros had squeezed him until he'd take any contract. The Ferros' friend had offered him the contract. The contract had put him on the trail of a vanished team carrying evidence of a burial order two years deep. And a cleanup crew with new windshields and government-grade radios was parked on the only road south, drinking coffee and playing cards and waiting for word of where the dog had pointed.

He was not a salvage contractor. He had never been a salvage contractor. He was a search warrant with legs.

And Ines Cabral — the woman he'd pulled out of the pit, the woman sleeping in his truck, the woman whose data drive was currently wrapped in a sock inside her pack — was not cargo to deliver. She was the thing the whole machine existed to erase.

"Okay," he said aloud.

"Okay?" Ines raised an eyebrow.

"Okay. New plan." He stood, brushed crumbs off his jeans, and looked south along the road, where the land ran flat and brown to a horizon shimmering with heat haze, and somewhere on it, two trucks and a crew of men whose job description was him. "We don't go back through the station. We cut west onto the old drover track and come down through the mesas. It adds a day. It puts a ridge between us and that radio antenna."

"A day," Ines repeated. "With them behind us and your creditors ahead of us and—" she touched her ribs, where the bruising from the pit had gone yellow-green and spectacular, "—me running on biscuits and stubbornness."

"Yes."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's a *plan*. Terrible plans beat good intentions out here. Ask your teammates—"

He stopped. Too late. The words were out and hanging in the air between them like something spilled.

Ines looked at him. She didn't flinch and she didn't soften, and somehow that was worse than either. "Yes," she said. "Let's ask them. Let's ask Tomás, who's still lying in a shallow grave next to his own drill casing because I couldn't carry him and I couldn't bury him properly because I had no shovel and no water and eleven days of staying alive took everything I had." She picked up the last empanada and held it without eating it. "So yes, Dunmore. Terrible plans. I'm listening."

He climbed into the cab and started the engine and didn't look at her, because there are moments when the kindest thing a man can do is pretend his eyes are on the road.

* * *

The drover track was barely a track — two ruts worn by sheep herds fifty years dead, wandering west across the steppe toward a skyline of dark mesas that looked, in the afternoon glare, like the backs of submerged animals. Paloma drove it at a speed that felt insulting after weeks of open-road running, crawling through the washouts, walking sections ahead on foot where the crust might not hold the truck's weight.

Behind them, the world stayed quiet. No dust plume on the southern horizon. No glint of glass on a ridge line. But he'd spent his whole life learning that the most dangerous weather was the kind that showed you nothing, the blue-sky killer that built itself beyond the curvature of the earth while everyone below talked about what a lovely afternoon it was.

At the last high ground before dark, he stopped and glassed the back trail through binoculars until his arms shook.

Nothing.

Which meant either they hadn't followed — unlikely, if the thin man's smile had meant what smiles like that mean — or they knew exactly where he was going and were letting him run the scent for them, saving fuel, saving effort, planning to close the trap where the terrain did the work for them.

There was one place ahead where the track funneled. One pass through the mesas, thirty kilometers south, where a thousand years of water had cut a canyon through the rock and every wheeled thing on the dry side had to squeeze through it, because the alternatives were two hundred kilometers of salt pans and basalt fields that ate axles.

If he were Vidal — and he didn't know yet that the cleanup chief's name was Vidal; he wouldn't learn that name until later, spoken in a voice calm as a customs officer's — if he were the man running that crew, he wouldn't chase a bloodhound across open country. He'd park at the funnel and wait.

Paloma lowered the binoculars. The evening light was going copper, and far off to the northwest, over the mountains, the sky had begun doing something he recognized the way other men recognized faces. A haze where there should have been clarity. A brownish smudge riding above the peaks, too high to be smoke, too wide to be cloud. Pressure falling in his bad knee, the one he'd broken at twenty-two chasing a twister near Sarmiento.

A dust front. A big one. Two days out, maybe three, moving southeast.

Most men driving that road would have called it a dirty sunset and thought no more about it.

Paloma Dunmore stood on the mesa in the failing light with a fugitive scientist, a stolen drive, a debt he couldn't pay, and a canyon pass ahead full of men paid to erase them both — and he watched that distant brown wall build itself against the sky, and felt, for the first time since the garage where the Ferro brothers had smiled at him over a folder of figures, something that was almost comfort.

Because the wilderness forgave nothing. But it kept honest books. And tonight, for once, he knew something about the coming weather that the men hunting him did not.

"We'll reach the pass by tomorrow noon," Ines said from the truck, her voice carrying flat across the stone.

"Then tomorrow morning," Paloma said, climbing back into the cab, "we learn whether I'm a better weatherman than they are."

He dropped the truck into gear and turned its nose toward the darkening mesas, and behind them, far to the south, unseen, the second crew began to move.
