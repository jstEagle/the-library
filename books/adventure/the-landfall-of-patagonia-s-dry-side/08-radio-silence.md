## 8. Radio Silence

**PALOMA**

The wind came off the plateau at dusk with the smell of thorn and cold stone, and Paloma Dunmore sat in the cab of his truck with the engine off and the radio on, listening to static the way other men listened to confessions.

Ines Cabral was twenty meters away, standing at the lip of the arroyo with her back to him, watching the last light go out of the western escarpment. She had refused to sit in the cab. She had refused coffee. She had accepted a blanket, which she wore like a general accepting a white flag from an enemy she intended to outlive.

He turned the radio dial slowly, sweeping the bands. Nothing but the long hiss of the dry side. Somewhere south, a government frequency muttered numbers to no one. He'd been sweeping every hour since they'd made camp, and every hour the static said the same thing: *you are alone, and alone is a word with two meanings out here.*

He thought about the truck at the fuel stop. The white Toyota with the government plates that weren't government plates, the four men who'd looked at him exactly as long as strangers look at each other and no longer. He thought about the Ferros' cheerful voices on the satellite phone, and the arithmetic of his debt, and the fact that somewhere between the arroyo and Comodoro Rivadavia there was a crew whose job was to make sure nothing came back from the steppe — and that he was currently sitting in the middle of the steppe with the thing that wasn't supposed to come back.

He keyed the mic. "Radio check," he said, though she was twenty meters away and could have heard him if he'd whispered. It wasn't for her. It was for the discipline of it. A man who stops checking his radio out here starts checking other things, and then he stops checking anything at all, and then the steppe keeps him.

Static answered. Then, faintly, from the direction of the arroyo, so did Ines.

"Your radio is fine. Your judgment is what I'm worried about."

He looked over. She hadn't turned around. The blanket moved in the wind.

"You've been sweeping that band all day," she said. "You're not listening for weather."

"No," he agreed. "I'm listening for trucks."

"And if you hear them?"

"Then we're already dead, and I'd like to know the time of death. I'm precise that way."

She turned then, finally, and walked toward the truck with the unhurried stride of a woman who had decided, sometime in the last hour, that the conversation was happening whether she liked it or not. She stopped at the passenger door and looked at him through the glass. In the last of the light her face was all planes and shadow, the face of someone the desert had been sanding down for eleven days.

"Get out of the truck," she said. "If we're going to do this, we do it on the ground. Trucks are for people who are leaving."

He got out of the truck.

* * *

**INES**

She made him build the fire small, down in the cut of the arroyo where the flame would be a rumor and not an announcement. He did it without arguing, which told her something. A man who argued about fire safety was a man performing caution for an audience. Paloma Dunmore performed nothing. He crouched over the little flame with the economy of a man who had learned every calorie the hard way, and she hated how much that reminded her of Tomás, who had been the same, who was now a shape in the ground near the drill casings, and she put that thought away in the box where she kept Tomás, and Marta, and Esteban, and the kettle still warm on the stove of a camp that had been erased.

"You found me in a pit," she said. "Half under a guanaco carcass. You had every reason to believe I was a corpse with opinions."

"You were a corpse with excellent posture."

"You reported to your fixer that you'd found one survivor. That's what the contract said — survivors are to be reported. So the question I've been chewing for two days is a simple one." She looked at him across the fire. "Why am I not in a satellite call right now? Why am I not a line item in a report to the ministry?"

The wind moved the flame. He didn't look away from her, and she gave him credit for that, because most men looked away when the arithmetic got ugly.

"Because I've been doing my own arithmetic," he said. "And the numbers stopped adding up."

"Tell me the numbers."

"Easy-paying jobs aren't easy. That's the first one. A contract that pays double my debt to find *gear* — gear, Ines, drill casings and sample boxes, not people — is a contract that's paying for something else. The second number is a white Toyota I saw at the fuel stop in Tres Cerros. Four men, government plates, and the plates were wrong. I know wrong plates the way you know a wrong core sample. The third number is that the Ferro brothers hold my debt, and the Ferro brothers are cheerful men, and cheerful men with money in a salvage contract means the money came from somewhere, and the somewhere is the same somewhere as the Toyota." He fed a twig to the fire. "So either I'm the bloodhound for a search-and-destroy, or I'm the last honest man in Patagonia, and I've never once been mistaken for that."

She felt the truth of it settle in her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She had known. She had known since the moment he'd pulled her out of the pit and she'd looked into his eyes and seen a man measuring her the way she was measuring him — not with cruelty, but with the cold professional interest of someone deciding what a thing was worth. She had given him a false name and a false story and watched him accept both with a small smile that said *I know, and I'll let you find out that I know.*

"And so you've kept me off the radio," she said. "Off the record. A survivor who doesn't exist."

"A survivor who doesn't exist can't be disappeared twice."

There it was. She looked at him and understood that this — this exact sentence, said plainly, with no performance of virtue — was the most honest thing anyone had said to her since the survey team had rolled north out of Comodoro. Not *I want to help you.* Not *I'm a good man.* Just: the math, and the math said keep you off the books, and keeping you off the books happens to be the only reason you're still breathing.

"Why should I trust you?" she asked. It wasn't a rhetorical question. She wanted the answer the way she wanted a bearing.

"You shouldn't," Paloma said. "Trust is for people with better options. But I'll tell you what I am instead of what you should think of me. I'm a man who reads weather nobody else can read. I'm a man who owes money to people who are patient the way a landslide is patient. And I'm a man who, two days ago, sat in a fuel stop and watched four wrong-plated men buy coffee and realized that my contract and their contract were written by the same hand — and that mine was written in pencil." He looked up. "You're the only person on this steppe who knows what's actually buried out here. That makes you the most valuable thing I've ever found, and I have never once in my life destroyed the most valuable thing I've found. That's not virtue. That's just who I am."

* * *

**PALOMA**

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the fire burned down to a red eye and the cold came up out of the ground and into their boots. He let the silence do its work. Silence was weather too, and he could read it — the way her jaw set and un-set, the way her hands stayed flat on her knees, pressing down, as if the plateau might otherwise take her.

When she finally spoke, her voice had changed. The performance was gone. What was underneath was tired and hard and younger than her face.

"There were five of us," she said. "You found one."

He didn't move. He had learned, chasing storms, that the worst thing you could do in the presence of something enormous was flinch.

"Tomás, Marta, Esteban, and our team lead, Duarte. Duarte I'm not sure counts as dead. Duarte I think counts as *theirs*." She said the word *theirs* the way you'd spit out grit. "We were drilling core samples on the aquifer under the survey grid. Routine. That's what we were told. Routine monitoring, file the data, go home. Except the data wasn't routine. The aquifer is poisoned, Paloma. Mining runoff, years of it, in the water under half the estancias on the dry side. And the readings didn't just show it. The readings showed that the ministry *knew* — that someone signed off on burying it two years ago, and our survey was the burial party. We weren't measuring the aquifer. We were generating the paperwork that would let everyone keep pretending it was clean."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the plain, a fox screamed once and stopped.

"Duarte ordered the data sealed. Sealed, not deleted — that was the tell. You delete what's false. You seal what's true and dangerous. And then eleven days ago I woke up and the camp was gone. Kettles warm. Radios intact. No tracks. Marta's boots still by her cot." Her voice did not break. It did something worse — it went flat, the way water goes flat before it freezes. "The disappearance was arranged before we ever left Comodoro. We were never a survey team. We were a cleanup crew's alibi."

"The drives," Paloma said. "You said drives, plural, the first night. You said there was data. I'm asking you now, with the fire low and no one listening, because if I'm going to stop being a bloodhound I need to know what I'm refusing to fetch."

"Tomás hid duplicate drives in the drill casings. Before they came for us, before — whatever happened. He was the careful one. He knew the one place no one would look is the place the contract said to *find*." She almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing Paloma had seen in years. "They sent you to recover the gear, Paloma. The gear is the evidence. The joke is that the contract was honest the whole time."

* * *

**INES**

He laughed, then. One short, ugly bark of a laugh, aimed at nothing, and she understood it completely. It was the laugh of a man who has just seen the shape of his own cage and found it elegant.

"So I deliver the drives," he said, "and I'm paid, and the aquifer stays a rumor, and three people stay missing, and the Ferros stay cheerful."

"That's the deal you signed."

"That's the deal I *burned* the day I didn't radio in from the pit." He looked at her across the coals, and she watched him make the decision in real time — watched it move through his face like weather moving across the escarpment, cloud shadow, then light. "Here's my counteroffer, Doctor Cabral. You lead me to the casings. I dig. We get the drives. And then I don't take you to the ministry, and I don't take you to my fixer. I take you to Comodoro Rivadavia. There's a journalist there — I know her, she owes me for a story I fed her about the Ferros' fuel smuggling two years back, and she's one of maybe three people in this province who can't be bought because nobody's ever had enough money to try. You give her the data. You give her the names. You give her Tomás, and Marta, and Esteban, and the kettles that were still warm."

"And you?" she asked. "What do you get?"

"I get to not be the man who dug you up just to hand you over." He said it without drama, which was how she was learning he said everything true. "And maybe I get to keep breathing, which is less certain. The Ferros don't forgive. They renegotiate."

"They'll come for you."

"They're already coming for both of us. The Toyota didn't drive out here to admire the guanacos." He poked the coals apart, and the heat came up against their faces. "So we do this properly. No more false names. No more cover stories. You don't trust me — fine, I'm asking you not to. I'm asking you to *verify* me. Watch every decision I make. If I steer us toward the ministry, you'll know. If I steer us toward the Ferros, you'll know. A man can lie with his mouth all day, but out here, on the dry side, he can't lie with his route. The steppe keeps the books."

She thought about it. Not quickly — she made him sit in the silence, and she could tell he respected that, because he didn't fill it. She thought about Tomás's careful hands packing drives into casings. She thought about the pit and the carcass and the eleven days of discipline that had kept her heart beating when her mind had wanted to stop. She thought about the fact that every option she had left in the world was a bad one, and that bad options were best taken with someone who could read a storm.

"Comodoro," she said at last. "The journalist. Not the ministry. Not the fixer. And the drives — I carry them. Not you."

"Agreed."

"And if we get separated, you don't come back for me. You take the route south and you don't stop. I've survived eleven days alone on this steppe. I can survive your absence."

"Agreed," he said again, and then, because he was who he was: "But I'm bad at that particular instruction. Ask anyone I've ever abandoned. There's a very short list."

She almost laughed. It surprised her, the almost. She filed it away with a small, private suspicion that she would be doing more of that, and that she would hate herself for it later, and that later was a luxury.

* * *

**PALOMA**

They shook on it, out over the dead fire, and her hand was calloused and cold and did not let go one second before his did.

Then she took the map from her jacket — a real paper map, folded soft as cloth from handling — and spread it on the ground, weighting the corners with stones, and for the first time she showed him something true.

"Here," she said, her finger on a nameless fold of ground north of the dry arroyo. "Drill site four. Tomás's casings. We can reach it in a day if we drive through the night, and we should, because the Toyota will be working its way up the fuel stops, and fuel stops are where you found them, which means fuel stops are where they'll find us."

Paloma studied the map, then lifted his face to the south and read the horizon the way she'd watched him do it — not looking at anything, looking at *pressure*. His eyes narrowed.

"We drive through the night," he said. "And we dig in the dark, and we're gone before dawn. But Ines—" He paused, and she saw the weather change in him, the way it must have changed a hundred times in front of paying clients, the small stillness that meant the sky had told him something. "There's a front building down off the Andes. Two days out, maybe three. A dry storm, a dust-front, the kind that lies about its size until it's on top of you. Nobody else would even call it weather yet." He folded the map and handed it back to her. "But if it comes while we're still on the steppe, it's going to be either the thing that kills us or the thing that saves us. And I'd very much like to know which before it decides."

She looked south too, at the clean dark horizon, at nothing.

"I don't see anything," she said.

"No," Paloma said, and began kicking dirt over the coals. "Nobody ever does. That's the whole point of me."

They broke camp in the dark, and the truck rolled north toward the drill site with its lights off, and forty kilometers behind them, at a fuel station under a dead sodium lamp, a white Toyota turned onto the steppe road and began to follow.
