## 9. Tomás's Casing

We waited for full dark because Ines said the site had to be approached like a wound—slowly, and with clean hands.

The moon was a sliver, barely enough to silver the tops of the coironé grass, and the cold came up out of the ground the way it does on the dry side after sundown: not gradually but all at once, as if the steppe exhaled everything it had borrowed from the sun. I killed the truck's lights two kilometers out and drove the last stretch by memory and starlight, following the faint seam of an old seismic track that only someone who'd grown up reading this country would have found at all.

Ines sat in the passenger seat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, watching the dark ahead of us the way you watch a door you're afraid will open. She hadn't spoken more than twenty words since we'd left the riverbed at dusk. I'd learned in four days of driving with her that her silences came in two kinds: the kind where she was deciding whether to trust me, and the kind where she was somewhere else entirely—eleven days ago, in a tent with dead men, holding data that could burn down a ministry. Tonight was the second kind. I didn't interrupt it. My father taught me that on the steppe you don't talk into another man's weather.

The drill site announced itself before I saw it—a change in the texture of the ground, the grass flattened in a wide fan where vehicles had turned and turned again. Then the mast, lying on its side like a felled tree, its lattice catching the starlight in thin bright lines. Three core holes, capped with steel plates. A cache of fuel drums, one of them tipped and empty, its shadow long and black across the dirt.

And around all of it, the wrongness I'd felt at the abandoned camp, stronger here, concentrated. No scavenger birds. No fox sign. The guanaco bones that littered every other square meter of the steppe were absent from this circle, as if even the carrion-eaters had decided there was nothing here worth taking.

"They left in a hurry," I said, because saying something ordinary helped.

"No," Ines said quietly. "They left exactly on schedule. That's what frightens me."

She got out before I did. I watched her stand at the edge of the flattened ground, small against the vast black plain, and take one breath that shook on the way in and came out steady. Whatever she was doing, it was discipline—the same discipline that had kept her alive eleven days in a carcass pit eating things I didn't want described. I'd seen soldiers do less with more training.

"Which casing?" I asked.

"The north hole." She pointed without looking at me. "Tomás logged it himself. He said if anyone ever came back, they should pull the third section, two meters down. He said—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat. "He said the government would never dig up its own lie."

I fetched the tools from the truck bed: shovels, a pry bar, the socket wrench I'd traded a case of wine for in Perito Moreno and never regretted. The caps came off the first try, which surprised me—they'd been seated for weeks and should have seized. Then I understood, and my stomach went cold. Someone had opened them recently. The bolt threads were bright, freshly cut with oil.

"Ines."

She came over. I held the flashlight low so the beam wouldn't carry, and we looked together at the bore below the cap. It wasn't empty. Someone had backfilled it—not with the original core, but with loose soil and rock, tamped down in layers, the work of hands in a hurry but hands that knew what they were hiding.

"They came back," she whispered. "Vidal's people. They dug."

"Or they started to." I dropped the flashlight beam deeper. "Look at the walls. They got maybe a meter down and stopped. See the slump? They hit water seepage and their sides caved twice. Digging a borehole by hand is a bastard's work. They'd need the rig." I nodded at the fallen mast. "And the rig's been disabled. Injector lines pulled, control box smashed. Somebody on that crew knew drilling."

"Tomás," she said. "Before they took him. He bought time."

"Then he bought enough. We dig where they stopped and go deeper."

* * *

We worked in shifts, because the hole was barely wide enough for one body and the air down in it came back thin and mineral-tasting. I went first. Every shovel of spoil I passed up, Ines stacked and tamped along the rim so the pile wouldn't silhouette against the sky. Neither of us spoke about the fresh digging above ours. We didn't have to. It hung over the work like the smell of blood.

A meter down, the temperature changed. Two meters, and the soil turned to compacted drill cuttings, gray-blue and fine as flour, and my shovel began ringing off something hard. Not rock. Rock doesn't ring.

"Casing joint," I called up softly. "Third section, like he said."

Ines's face appeared at the rim, upside down against the stars, lit faintly by my headlamp. "Can you get it out?"

"It's threaded into the string below. Give me the wrench."

Working blind, by feel, in a space where my elbows scraped both walls, I broke the joint loose and hauled the section up hand over hand. It came free with a sucking sound, trailing a rope of damp cuttings, and Ines dragged it away from the lip and laid it flat. Inside the pipe, wedged at the midpoint where no inspector would ever look, was a bundle wrapped in drill-site plastic and sealed with core-sample tape.

She peeled it open with fingers that trembled only slightly. Inside: two hard drives in anti-static bags, a folded paper map annotated in a cramped engineer's hand, and a short letter on survey letterhead, signed by three names. I read the names over her shoulder. Tomás Aguirre's was last.

"He hid two," she said. Her voice had gone strange—careful, level, the voice of someone carrying something fragile across ice. "One here. One—" She stopped.

"One where?"

Her silence answered before she did. "With him," she said finally. "Wherever they put him."

* * *

It was Ines who found him, and I will carry the sound she made for the rest of my life.

We'd agreed to search the perimeter before dawn took away our cover. The cleanup crew had been thorough with the camp itself—burned the paperwork, stripped the tents—but they'd been careless with the ground beyond the vehicle tracks, or perhaps contemptuous of it. Forty meters east, in a shallow depression where the wind had piled sand against a shelf of caliche rock, the surface rose in a mound too regular to be natural. And from its edge, half uncovered by the same wind that had built it, extended a boot.

Not scattered. Not dragged. Placed. The cleanup crew hadn't buried Tomás Aguirre; they'd planted him, shallow, facing the sky, the way you leave a message.

Ines knelt beside the mound and brushed the sand away with her bare hands, and I let her, because I understood that this was hers to do. It took minutes that lasted years. First the boot, then the leg, then the coat—his survey jacket, name tape still stitched at the breast—and then his shoulders, and then his face.

He'd been dead for weeks but the dry cold had kept him, leathery and still, his features settled into an expression I recognized from bodies I'd seen on the steppe before: not terror, not pain. Endurance. The face of a man who had been made to wait for something and had refused to give it.

His hands were the worst part. They were raised against his chest, curled inward, and between them, gripped so tightly that rigor had fused his fingers around it, was a length of steel pipe cut from a casing joint, thirty centimeters long, painted with the survey team's orange band. A marker. The second marker. He had died holding the pointer to his own grave—or rather, Ines realized it a heartbeat before I did, holding the pointer to what was buried *beside* him.

"Oh, Tomás," Ines said. Just that. His name, and nothing else, and then the sound came out of her.

I have heard women grieve in a hundred registers—the keening of the coast, the silent shaking of city funerals—but I had never heard anything like what came out of Dr. Inés Cabral kneeling in the sand. It wasn't loud. That was what made it unbearable. It was low and continuous, a sound with no performance in it at all, the sound of a person being hollowed out from the inside, and she did not stop working while she made it. Her hands kept moving. She cleared the sand from his arms, from his chest, folding back the jacket with a gentleness that undid me more than any sobbing could have, and all the while that low sound ran through her like current through wire.

I stood back and gave her the room. There was nothing else to give.

When she reached his hands, she stopped. She looked at the casing marker clamped in his dead fingers for a long time, and then she looked up at me, and her face in the starlight was wet and absolutely calm, and that combination frightened me more than Vidal's whole crew.

"He held onto it," she said. "They searched him—I can see where they went through his pockets, see the seams torn? They took his documents, his watch, everything. But they didn't open his fists. They thought he was just—" She swallowed. "They thought the dead man was holding nothing. But Tomás never held nothing in his life. He held instruments. He held data. He held on."

"Give me your knife," she said.

I gave it to her. She worked the blade under his fingers with the same care she'd use on a core sample, prying each stiff finger back one at a time, murmuring apologies in Portuguese under her breath—I hadn't known until then that she slipped into Portuguese when she was at the bottom of herself—and when the marker came free she cradled it in both hands like a relic.

Inside the pipe, plugged into the bore with a machined cap, was the second drive. And taped to its sleeve, a note in Tomás's handwriting, written fast, the letters leaning like men running:

*If you are reading this, you are either Cabral or you are the problem. Check the third number on the map. It is not a survey point. It is a name.*

* * *

We buried him properly. It cost us an hour we couldn't afford and I would not trade it back now.

There was no depth to be had in caliche country—we cut into the soft sand below the rock shelf and got him eighty centimeters down, which on the dry side is a decent grave. Ines wrapped him in his own groundsheet. I said what words I knew, the ones my father used, old steppe words about the wind remembering everyone it has carried, and Ines stood with her hand flat on the finished mound and said nothing at all, and the silence she kept was more of a prayer than anything I managed.

Then she straightened, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand—once, efficiently, done—and when she turned to me she was someone different. Not harder. Sharper. A blade that had been a hammer all week.

"I need to tell you what I've been carrying," she said. "All of it. Because I watched them plant him in that sand and I understood something tonight that I should have understood in the pit." She held up the drive. "This isn't the ministry's evidence anymore. It stopped being theirs when they buried my team to keep it. It's theirs—" and she nodded at the grave— "theirs. Tomás's. Marta's. Duarte's. I'm not delivering a report. I'm finishing what three dead people started, and I will get it to Comodoro if I have to crawl there on my hands."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and the starlight showed me her eyes red-rimmed and burning. "Because tomorrow morning you make your satellite call to whoever owns you, Paloma Dunmore. I've watched you check that phone like a man checking a wound. So before the sun comes up, you decide which side of this ground you're standing on. Because I'm done being useful to people who are also serving the other side. Even you."

The word *even* landed harder than the rest of it.

I thought about telling her. About the Ferros, the debt, my sister's lease, the arithmetic of fear I'd been doing since Perito Moreno. Some of it, anyway. But dawn was coming, and with it the call, and with the call a choice I hadn't finished making—and I knew, standing over Tomás Aguirre's grave with his drive warm in her hands, that whatever I said next would decide whether I ended this job as a bloodhound or as something else.

"There's a third number on that map," I said instead. "Let's find out whose name a dead man thought was worth dying holding."

Ines unfolded the map in the truck's cab, and her finger stopped on the third coordinate, and the color went out of her face so completely that I grabbed the paper myself.

The coordinate wasn't on the steppe at all. It was south. Days south, near Bajo Caracoles.

And beside it, in Tomás's cramped hand, a single word: *Ferro.*
