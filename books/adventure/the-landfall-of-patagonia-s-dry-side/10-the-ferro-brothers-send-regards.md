## 10. The Ferro Brothers Send Regards

The satellite phone rang at dusk, when the light on the steppe had gone the color of old bone and every shadow stretched out long enough to touch its neighbor.

Paloma had been expecting it for two days. That was the thing about the Ferros that people who'd never owed them money didn't understand — they were never late, and they were never early. They arrived exactly when you'd started to hope they might have forgotten. He'd been driving south along the old survey cut since morning, Ines asleep in the passenger seat with her head against the window and her hands still dirty from Tomás's grave, and he'd watched the horizon all day knowing the call was coming the way a man watches a cloud bank knowing it means rain by nightfall.

He let it ring four times. Long enough to seem busy. Not so long as to seem afraid.

"Paloma." The voice was warm, unhurried, faintly amused — the voice of a man settling into a chair he owned. "You're a hard man to reach."

"I've been in a canyon," Paloma said. "Canyons are bad for satellites."

"So I'm told." A pause, and behind it, faintly, the sound of an espresso machine hissing somewhere in Puerto Deseado, or maybe just wind through a window left open over water. "This is Emilio."

Of course it was. It was always Emilio first. Rafael made the decisions; Emilio delivered them, softly, the way a nurse delivers a diagnosis. You could go years owing the Ferros money and only ever hear Emilio's voice, pleasant as a radio announcer's, and somehow that was worse than shouting. Shouting meant anger, and anger meant the end of something. Emilio's gentleness meant the thing was still alive, still growing, still being fed.

"Emilio," Paloma said. "I assume this isn't about my payment schedule."

"It's about your success, actually." A low chuckle. "We're very pleased with you. Truly. When we heard you'd taken the ministry contract, we thought — Paloma will find them, or Paloma will die trying, and either way we'll know where to send flowers. But you found her. One woman out of six, half-dead in a hole in the ground, and you found her anyway. Do you know what Rafael said when the report came through? He said, *that's why we lend to him.*"

The truck hummed under him. In the mirror, the road unspooled back toward the north, toward the drill site, toward the shallow grave they'd refilled because leaving Tomás open to the sky had felt like a second murder. Ines hadn't spoken for an hour afterward. She'd sat with her palms flat on her knees like a woman holding down a table in an earthquake.

"You have people watching me," Paloma said.

"We have people everywhere, Paloma. It's not watching. It's *interest*. There's a difference. Watching is what police do. Interest is what family does." Papers rustled, or something did — a small domestic sound, a man comfortable in his evening. "Now. Let me tell you what happens next, because I'd hate for there to be confusion, and you know how I hate confusion."

Paloma pulled off the road. It wasn't a decision so much as a reflex — you didn't take a call like this while driving, the same way you didn't argue with a riptide while swimming. He stopped in the lee of a low ridge, killed the headlights, and looked over at Ines. She was awake now. Of course she was. She'd been awake since the second ring; he could see it in the way she held herself, absolutely still, listening with her whole body the way she must have listened for eleven days in that pit, cataloguing every sound, sorting it into harmless and not.

He turned the phone slightly away from her. Old habit. Pointless now.

"The ministry hired you to find the survey team," Emilio said. "Recover equipment, confirm deaths, keep it quiet. A tidy little job. But the ministry has many hands, Paloma, and some of those hands are dirtier than others, and one of those hands belongs to a friend of ours. A friend who is very concerned about what Dr. Cabral might be carrying. Concerned enough that our friend asked us — asked us personally, which flattered Rafael enormously — to make sure certain materials come back through a different door than the front one."

"And if they don't?"

"If they don't, then a salvage contractor named Paloma Dunmore failed to recover government property, and the government will want to know why, and the government asks questions with less charm than we do." Emilio sighed, a soft, regretful sound, a father disappointed by a report card. "But that's the stick, and I don't want to talk about sticks. I want to talk about the number."

"The number."

"Two hundred and forty-one thousand pesos," Emilio said. "Give or take the interest that's accrued since March, which Rafael has generously agreed to freeze — frozen, Paloma, not forgiven, there's a difference — pending the outcome of our conversation tonight. And here is the outcome I'm authorized to offer." A pause, deliberate, letting the word *authorized* do its work. "You deliver the drive and the doctor to our contact at the rail siding near Gobernador Gregores. Both of them, intact, cooperative, and quiet. And in exchange, everything clears. Everything. The principal, the interest, the penalties, the — what did Rafael call them once? — the *sentimental charges*. Gone. Zero. You walk out of our books cleaner than the day you walked in. No other lender in Patagonia could make you that offer, because no other lender holds paper on you the way we do."

Paloma said nothing. Outside, the light was dying in earnest now, the last of it pooling in the hollows like spilled oil. Somewhere north, a guanaco called, that thin broken-glass cry they made, and the steppe swallowed it.

*Zero.* He made himself look at the number honestly, the way he made himself read barometers honestly, because a man who lied to himself about weather died in it. Two hundred and forty-one thousand. Four years of his life, itemized. The engine rebuild he'd financed when the old Toyota threw a rod outside Perito Moreno. The winter his mother's treatments weren't covered and pride was a luxury nobody in the family could afford. The bad season when three clients cancelled and the fuel bills came anyway. And underneath all of it, the original sin: the lease on Lucía's workshop in Comodoro, co-signed by a brother who'd been too broke to say no and too proud to say why.

Lucía. He closed his eyes and saw her the way she'd looked at the bus station last spring — twenty-six years old, grease to the elbows, laughing at something one of her apprentices had said, entirely unaware that her whole working life rested on a signature he'd put down in a moment of weakness. Entirely unaware that men like the Ferros knew exactly where she slept.

"Say something, Paloma," Emilio said gently. "Silence makes me nervous, and when I'm nervous, Rafael gets involved, and you and I both prefer it when Rafael stays decorative."

"What happens to her?" Paloma asked. "Cabral."

Another pause. Longer. In the passenger seat, Ines had gone from stillness to something harder than stillness — she was watching him now, and her face in the dark was unreadable, and he understood suddenly that she was doing the same arithmetic he was. Weighing herself against his debt. Deciding what a man was worth.

"That's not your concern," Emilio said.

"It becomes my concern when you ask me to hand a living person to someone whose name you won't say."

"Our contact is a professional. Professionals are gentle with assets." The warmth in Emilio's voice thinned, just barely, like ice flexing under a boot. "Paloma. I'm going to say something now, and I want you to hear it the way I mean it, which is kindly. Your sister's lease comes up for renewal in November. The building's owner is a client of ours. It would be a shame — a real shame, I mean it, I've seen her work, the little motorcycle shop, she does beautiful things with those old BMWs — it would be a shame if a renewal became complicated. Buildings change hands. Rents adjust. Sometimes a landlord simply needs the space. These things happen to everyone, but they happen more often to people whose brothers make choices we can't account for."

There it was. Delivered like a gift, wrapped in admiration for Lucía's welding, tied with a bow of genuine-seeming regret. That was the Ferro genius, Paloma thought. Rafael was the blade, but Emilio was the surgeon — he never threatened, he merely described futures, and let you understand that the description was a menu.

"She doesn't know anything about any of this," Paloma said.

"No. And that's the point, isn't it? That's the whole architecture of it." Emilio's voice softened further, almost tender. "You carry it so she doesn't have to. It's why we liked you from the start. Most men in your position would have run years ago. Argentina is big and borders are porous. But you stayed, and you paid, and you kept your sister's name out of it, because you understand that debt isn't really about money. Debt is about who you'd let suffer before you break your word." A breath. "So. The siding at Gregores. Thursday, sundown. Our contact will identify himself with a red rag on the antenna. You bring the drive and the doctor. We bring you a clean slate and a future where your sister's rent is boring forever. Or—" and here the tenderness fell away entirely, not into menace but into something worse, administrative calm, "—you don't, and we begin having conversations with other people in your life. I'd rather not. Rafael would rather not. Nobody wants that. Say yes, Paloma, and this is the last unpleasant conversation we ever have."

Paloma looked at Ines.

She was looking back at him, and she'd heard all of it — of course she had, the truck cab was the size of a coffin — and what was in her face wasn't fear. It was assessment. He recognized it because he'd been wearing the same expression since the guanaco pit: two liars taking each other's measure, each deciding whether the other was a door or a wall. Eleven days buried alive hadn't broken her, and a dead man's grave hadn't broken her, and now she sat in a stranger's truck listening to a stranger's creditor price her life against a motorcycle shop in Comodoro, and she didn't flinch. She just waited to see what he was.

And the terrible thing, the thing that sat in his chest like a stone dropped down a well, was that he wanted to say yes. Not because he was a coward — though he'd earned the right to that word honestly enough over the years — but because *zero* was a number with a taste. Two hundred and forty-one thousand pesos of weight lifting off his spine in a single transaction. Lucía safe until the sun burned out. His own name, his own truck, his own weather, belonging to nobody. He could feel the relief already, phantom and sweet, the way you could smell rain forty kilometers off across dry country — coming, coming, not here yet, but coming.

All it cost was a woman who'd crawled out of a mass grave carrying the truth about poisoned water, and whatever remained of Tomás and the others, and every person downstream of that aquifer who'd drink themselves sick for a decade without knowing why.

"Thursday," Paloma said. "Sundown. Red rag on the antenna."

"Sundown Thursday," Emilio repeated, satisfied, warm again, the sun coming back out. "You see how easy that was? This is why we never worried about you, Paloma. You're a practical man. Practical men live long lives." A click of a tongue, almost playful. "One more thing. Small thing. The drive — our contact says the survey data may be incomplete. Corrupted in places. If you find that some of it doesn't read properly, don't trouble yourself over it. What matters is delivery, not quality. Agreed?"

Something cold walked up Paloma's spine.

Corrupted. He thought of the night in the truck with the laptop balanced on his knees, the recovered drive coughing up directories, and the files that wouldn't open, and the ones that opened onto tables of numbers that made no sense — readings that contradicted the reports Ines had described, dates that didn't line up, a version of the truth with holes in it shaped exactly like names. He'd told himself it was damage. Water, grit, eleven days in a drill casing. He'd told himself that because the alternative was that the woman sleeping three feet from him had handed him a curated truth and kept the sharp edges for herself.

*Don't trouble yourself over it,* Emilio said, and Paloma understood, with the flat certainty he usually reserved for pressure drops, that the Ferros' contact had already seen the contents of that drive. Which meant someone had copied it. Which meant either Ines had dealt with the cleanup crew before he'd found her — impossible, she'd been buried — or there was a third copy moving through the world, or the drive itself was a decoy and the real one was somewhere else, in someone else's hands, in Tomás's hands, in the ground.

Or Ines was playing all of them. Him included.

"Agreed," Paloma said.

"Wonderful. Thursday, then. Give the doctor our regards — no, actually, don't. Better she arrives surprised." A laugh, light as gravel shifting. "Goodnight, Paloma. Drive carefully. Weather's turning, they say."

The line went dead.

For a long time neither of them moved. The engine ticked as it cooled. Out past the ridge, the first stars were coming up over the plateau, hard and white, and the land lay black beneath them like a sea with the tide out.

"He knows about the corruption," Ines said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"He knows something." Paloma set the phone on the dash, face down, as if it might watch them. "Either your drive is doctored and their contact has seen the original, or your drive is real and they want you thinking it's worthless so you'll trade the backup. Either way, somebody's lying to me, and historically when people lie to me, I'm the one who ends up in the hole."

Ines turned toward the window. Her reflection hung there against the stars, ghost-pale, and when she spoke her voice had the flat steadiness of a woman reading instruments in a storm. "Tomás kept two drives. He told me one was for the ministry — the version they'd expect, the one with the inconvenient parts filed off. Insurance. He said the real one goes to a person, not a place." She looked back at him. "I don't know where the second casing marker leads. I know it isn't Gregores."

"Then Thursday we drive to Gregores," Paloma said, and started the engine, and the headlights carved a yellow tunnel into the dark. "With a red rag on the antenna, and a decoy in my pocket, and you in the seat beside me — right up until the moment I decide otherwise. Because here's what you need to understand about me, Doctor. I just sold you. Out loud, on a recorded line, to the two most patient men in southern Patagonia. And the only reason you're still breathing is that I haven't figured out yet how to sell you twice."

Ines studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled — a thin, tired, dangerous thing.

"Good," she said. "Because I sold you first. Eleven days ago, in that pit, when I decided you'd believe my story. Now we're even, and we can finally get somewhere."

He put the truck in gear and turned it south, toward Gregores, toward Thursday, toward the rail siding where a red rag would flap on an antenna and everything he owed and everything he loved would stand on one side of a scale. Behind them the steppe went on being enormous and indifferent. Ahead, far off at the edge of the dark, lightning flickered inside a cloud bank with no sound attached — dry lightning, heat lightning, the kind that promised fire without promising rain.

Paloma watched it and did the math he was built for.

Three days. Enough time for a storm to grow teeth. Enough time to find a second casing marker, if it existed. Enough time to become, one last time, the kind of man who read the sky better than anyone — including the sky's owners.

He drove into the dark, and the dark drove with him.
