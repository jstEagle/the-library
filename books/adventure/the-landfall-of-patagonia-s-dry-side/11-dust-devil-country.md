## 11. Dust Devil Country

The land south of the survey grid did not forgive a straight line. It looked flat from the ridge—ten thousand square kilometers of yellow grass and grey gravel stitched together by nothing—but the steppe was a liar in the way the sea was a liar, all its violence stored below the surface. Every kilometer hid a dry wash, a basalt shelf, a guanaco trail worn hip-deep into the earth that would swallow a truck's axle and keep it. Paloma drove like a man reading a book the rest of us couldn't see, one hand on the wheel, the other resting on the door frame, his eyes never still. Twice in the first hour he turned us off the track for no reason I could detect, and twice, minutes later, we passed the reason: a washout wide enough to break a differential, or a fence line with a gate that had been wired shut since the last dictatorship.

"Who taught you to read this?" I asked, because silence between two people who are lying to each other is a kind of rot, and I would rather have conversation, even false conversation, than rot.

"My father," he said. "He ran freight up this side before the highway. Said the steppe talks to you if you shut up long enough to hear it. Pressure, light, the way the birds sit on the fences. Most people look at Patagonia and see empty. It's not empty. It's just quiet."

"And you listen."

"I owe money to people who don't like quiet," he said, and smiled, and the smile was a door closing.

I watched the mirror instead of him after that. Behind us, thirty kilometers back and closing by inches, the dust of the second crew hung in the air like a smudge on glass. Vidal's trucks. I had seen them once, at the fuel stop before Paloma understood what I already knew—that the ministry hadn't sent one salvage man, it had sent two kinds, and the second kind carried rifles in cases marked as survey equipment. The dust didn't move like weather. Weather drifts. That dust *pursued*.

* * *

We stopped at noon in the lee of a basalt outcrop, engines ticking, and Paloma handed me mate with the bitter, ceremonial gravity of a man who uses ritual to avoid intimacy. I drank. The wind came off the plateau in long slow breaths that smelled of dust and thyme and, underneath, faintly, of sulfur from the mines two hundred kilometers west—the smell I had been breathing for eleven days, the smell that had gotten into the cores, into the aquifer, into the report the ministry had signed and sealed and then erased four people to protect.

"Your debt," I said. "The Ferros. Tell me the shape of it."

He didn't pretend not to know what I meant. I gave him credit for that. "Why?"

"Because a man who knows what he's driving toward makes better decisions than a man driving toward a shape he won't look at. You taught me that. Chapter one of reading country: know what's under you."

Something moved in his face—not amusement, exactly. Recognition. "You interrogate like my mother."

"Answer the question, Paloma."

He looked out at the dust on the horizon, then at his own hands, and for a moment the mercenary went out of him the way a lamp goes out, and what was left looked young and tired and frightened in a way he would have denied with a gun in his hand.

"Fourteen years ago my mother got sick. The good clinic, the one in Comodoro, the one that actually works—it's cash up front, and it's a lot of cash. My father was dead by then. I had a truck and a talent for weather and nothing else. So I borrowed. The Ferros lent to people the banks wouldn't touch, and they were—this is the part people don't believe—*kind* about it. Smiling. Patient. They came to the hospital with fruit. They paid for the private room." He turned the mate cup in his hands. "She died anyway. And the debt stayed. And debts with the Ferros don't stay debts. They become arrangements. They become *relationships*. You pay the interest forever and the principal is a hook they keep in you in case they ever need a man who can read weather, or drive at night, or find things."

"Find things," I said.

"Find things," he agreed.

"Like a survey team that was never meant to be found."

He looked at me then, fully, and I saw him decide something—decide how much of me he was willing to let exist in the same truck as the truth. "The fixer who hired me didn't say that. He said salvage. Gear recovery. But I did the arithmetic in the garage while the Ferros were resetting my terms, and the arithmetic said: nobody pays double a man's debt for kettles and core barrels."

"And your sister," I said. I had heard the name on the satellite call, one word, *Valentina*, said in a voice as warm as the Ferros' voices were said to be, and I had watched Paloma's spine change.

His jaw worked. "They hold her lease. She has a shop in Puerto Deseado—textiles, nothing, a nothing shop, a life the size of a matchbox. The lease comes up for renewal in March. It renews, or it doesn't. That's the whole hostage. That's how they work. They never hold a gun. They hold a *March*."

I looked at this man—the man who had pulled me out of a carcass pit without asking my name first, who had checked my shoulder wound with hands gentler than his face, who had lied to me about his destination and I had lied to him about mine—and I felt the first hairline crack in the thing I had built out of Tomás's death, which was a simple architecture: everyone connected to the burial was an enemy, and everyone paid by the burial was a weapon, and weapons don't get grieved.

He was not a weapon. He was a man in a hole, and the hole had been dug by the same ministry that had dug mine, and he had climbed into it the way poor men always climb into holes, carrying someone they love on their back.

I hated that. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of my discipline: I hated it the way you hate a fever. It was a weakness arriving at a bad time. I had a drive full of dead men's truth in my pack and a crew of killers on my horizon and a promise to keep to three people who could no longer release me from it, and here was this mercenary with a dead mother and a hostage sister, and my chest did something unprofessional about it. I drank my mate and hated it.

* * *

The dust devils started at three in the afternoon.

I had seen them before—every child on the dry side has seen them, the little whirling columns that stand up out of nowhere, walk a hundred meters, and die. But this was not that. This was a day of them, dozens, dozens of dozens, the whole southern horizon growing a forest of slow grey pillars that leaned and wandered and sometimes merged, and the light went the color of old bone, and the air pressure dropped so fast my ears filled and my shoulder wound ached like a struck bell.

Paloma stopped the truck on a rise and got out and just stood there, facing south, doing something with his face that I finally understood was *listening*.

"That's not weather," I said, when he came back. "That's an argument."

"It's a front. Dry storm—dust and wind and static, no rain, maybe never any rain, but it'll hit like a wall and it'll kill visibility for a hundred kilometers and it'll kill engines that aren't sealed." He was already re-stowing the jerrycans, lashing everything down with the economy of a man who had done this in worse places. "Vidal's crew is running straight down the main track. Straight line, fast, confident. They think this is their country."

"Is it?"

"No," he said. "It's nobody's. That's the point." He looked at me across the truck bed, and there was something new in his face, or something old that had finally turned toward me. "Ines. If we run south fast we stay ahead of them and ahead of the storm, and we reach Bajo Caracoles by dark, and we do it clean. That's the sensible line."

"But."

"But the sensible line leaves our dust on the main track like a signature. And Vidal's good. He'll read it. Or—" he touched the horizon with two fingers, the way another man might touch a map, "—we cut southwest now, cross the bad country, let the storm come down behind us and erase everything, and we arrive at the estancia tonight looking like ghosts. It costs us the truck's suspension and probably my nerves. It buys us a night nobody can find."

"You want to drive into the storm."

"I want to drive *with* it. There's a difference. The storm is the only thing on this steppe bigger than Vidal, and it doesn't take orders from the ministry." He paused, and the mercenary came back into his voice for one sentence, honest the way a scar is honest: "And I'm good at this. It's the only thing I've ever been good at that nobody owns yet."

I got in the truck. I want it recorded, in the record I am keeping for Tomás and for Marisa and for Andrés, who will never read it, that I got in the truck without an argument, and that it was not because I trusted him. It was because I had watched him read the country for two days the way Tomás used to read data—totally, hungrily, with the whole of himself—and I had decided that a man who loved something that much, even something as indifferent as weather, had a floor to him. A floor you could stand on.

We turned southwest, off the track, into the bad country, and behind us the forest of dust devils began, very slowly, to lean east and gather itself into a single moving wall.

* * *

The bad country was exactly as advertised. We forded two washes and winched out of a third. The truck took a hit on the sump that made Paloma wince like a physical blow, and once, crossing a plain of black gravel where the wind came in flat and screaming, we drove for twenty minutes by compass alone inside a brownness so complete that I could not see my own hand on the knee of my wounded leg. I talked to him through it—directions off the GPS, distances, headings—because it gave me something to do besides be afraid, and because I had noticed that when I talked he drove better, as if my voice were another instrument he was reading.

"You could still take the deal," I said at one point, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "Drive and woman, delivered. Debt forgiven. Valentina's lease renewed. It's on the table. It's been on the table since the satellite call."

The wind screamed. The wipers made no difference at all.

"I did the arithmetic on that too," he said finally. "You want to hear it?"

"Yes."

"If I deliver you, I'm clean, and Valentina's safe until the next time the Ferros need a man who can read weather, which will be soon, because men like that always need one. And I spend the rest of my life knowing what's under the aquifer, and knowing that the water table under forty estancias is poisoned, and knowing that I'm the reason the story died in a truck on the dry side." A wash of dust hit us and the world went solid brown. "My mother spent her last month in a clinic that cost more than this truck. She'd have hated me. Not for the debt. For the arithmetic."

I said nothing for a long time, because there was a knot in my throat the size of Tomás's name.

"And you?" he said, into the brown. "The drive. What's actually on it? Because I've seen it, Ines. Part of it's corrupted, or part of it's been doctored, and I've been driving on trust for two days and trust is not a fuel I have a lot of."

There it was. The crack I had been waiting for, arriving at last, in a dust storm, at eighty kilometers an hour. I could have lied. I had a lie ready; I have always had a lie ready; the ministry trained me better than it knew. And I looked at the shape of him in the driver's seat, hands steady on the wheel of a truck he was driving into a wall for a woman he didn't trust, for money he'd never see, and I heard myself say:

"There's a source. Inside the ministry. The names that matter—the signature chain, who ordered the burial, who ordered *you*—they're not on the drive. They're in a separate file, and I'm the only copy, and it's in my head, and I held it back because the source is still alive and still inside, and if the names come out wrong the source dies and the story dies with it."

The wipers beat. The wind screamed. He drove.

"That's the whole truth?" he said.

"That's the whole truth."

"Okay," he said, and nothing else, and the truck went on south through the brown, and something passed between us that had no name I was willing to give it, and I hated myself a little, and I held on.

* * *

The storm broke over us an hour after dark and we drove inside it like men inside a lung. Paloma navigated by pressure and by a sense I cannot describe and did not try to, and near midnight the brown tore open and there they were: three lights in a black fold of the land, a windbreak of poplars, a long low roof.

The estancia at Bajo Caracoles. An old man on the porch with a lamp, who looked at our truck, and then at Paloma's face, and said something in a voice roughened by fifty years of this wind that I didn't catch.

Paloma caught it. He went very still.

"What is it?" I asked. "What did he say?"

The old man said it again, louder, and this time the words came through the wind plain as a bullet: that there were three trucks on the northern track an hour behind the storm, running without lights, and that they had stopped at the fuel station at Tres Cerros and asked questions, and that they did not leave witnesses.

Paloma looked at me across the lamplight, and the storm roared behind us like a sea making landfall, and he said, "Ines. They're not behind us anymore. They're ahead."
