## 12. The Estancia at Bajo Caracoles

The light was going amber and long when the wind changed, and I knew we were close before the old man's fence posts appeared out of the scrub like a row of bad teeth.

I'd been reading the country all afternoon—the way the guanacos had stopped grazing and started standing with their noses into the southwest, the way the dust off the track hung in the air instead of falling, the way my back teeth ached in that dull, familiar rhythm they'd learned from my father. Pressure dropping out of the Andes' rain shadow, sliding down onto the steppe like something patient coming down off a shelf. There was weather coming. Not tonight, maybe not tomorrow, but soon, and it would be bigger than anyone without my father's blood in them would believe.

Ines hadn't spoken in two hours. Her shoulder was bound tight under her jacket, the wound dressed with tape and gauze from the kit I kept for clients who fell off horses, and she'd gone past pain into that flat gray place where you stop being a person and become a problem to be solved. She sat in the passenger seat with the drive in a plastic bag taped to her ribs, on the side away from the window, and every time the truck hit one of the ruts the frost heaves leave behind, she made no sound at all. That discipline scared me more than screaming would have.

"We're stopping?" she said finally, when I slowed for the gate.

"We're stopping."

"I thought we were running south."

"We are. But not in the dark, on a road Vidal knows better than we do, with your arm leaking and no fuel." I nodded through the windshield at the buildings rising out of the flat—a low stone house, a windmill turning its slow rusted circles, a stock shed, a corral empty of everything but wind. "And not past this place. This is Bajo Caracoles. Or what's left of it."

"You know him," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching me drive for three days; she'd learned to read my face the way I read sky.

"My father ran with him. Before I was born." I killed the engine and sat a moment, hands on the wheel, feeling the day's vibration drain out of them. "His name is Anselmo Requena. When I was nine years old he taught me that the dry side lies about its storms. He's the only man alive besides me who'll believe what I tell him about tomorrow."

"And if he sells us?"

"Then we were dead anyway, and I'd rather be dead warm."

* * *

He came out of the house before I knocked, which meant he'd heard the truck ten minutes ago and been watching us through a gap in the curtain, deciding. He was seventy if he was a day, built like the fence posts—weathered upright, leaning slightly, impossible to imagine fallen—and his eyes went from me to Ines to the bandage showing at her collar and back again, cataloguing everything, saying nothing.

"Paloma Dunmore," he said.

"Anselmo."

"You have your mother's face and your father's habits." He looked at the sky over my shoulder, southwest, where the horizon had gone a strange bruised yellow under the blue. "You came up the Deseado road."

"Yes."

"Then someone's behind you."

"Someone's behind us."

He grunted, turned, and walked inside without inviting us, which among men of his generation and country was an invitation of the most binding kind. Ines looked at me. I shrugged and followed.

The house smelled of woodsmoke, mate, and sheep fat, the smell that has been the same in Patagonian kitchens for a hundred years and will be the same for a hundred more. A stove, a table scarred white, photographs in cheap frames on the wall—one of them, I saw with a jolt, was my father, thirty years old and laughing, standing beside a younger Anselmo in front of a truck so old it should have been in a museum. They were both holding rifles and neither was looking at the camera. They were looking at the sky.

"He never told me about you," Ines said quietly.

"He didn't know. Nobody knew what my father did out here except the men he did it with." I set our bags by the door. "That was the point."

Anselmo put water on for mate and pointed at a chair until Ines sat in it, and there was something in the way he handled her—firm, impersonal, gentle—that made her eyes go bright and dangerous for a second, the way people do when kindness catches them off guard after too long without it. Then he looked at me across the steam.

"Eat first," he said. "Talk after. Wounded women don't answer questions well and lying men don't either."

So we ate: lamb off a bone, bread hard as slate, canned peaches that tasted like another century. Ines ate everything and asked for more, and Anselmo fed her without comment, and I watched the color come back into her face degree by degree, like watching dawn come up over the steppe—slowly, then all at once.

Afterward he repacked her dressing himself, with hands that had done field surgery on animals and probably men, and while he worked she told him nothing and he asked nothing, and when it was done he poured three fingers of some liquor that could have stripped paint and gave one to each of us.

"Now," he said. "Tell me who's hunting you, and don't waste my time being clever about it."

I told him. Most of it. The salvage contract, the vanished survey team, Ines found alive, the drives, the second crew, Vidal. I left out the Ferros, because some debts you don't say aloud in front of witnesses even when the witness is trustworthy, especially then. Anselmo listened the way stone listens, and when I finished he was quiet for a long time, looking at the fire.

"Vidal," he said at last. "Santos Vidal."

"You know him."

"Everyone out here knows the name now, the way you know the name of a disease." He turned his glass slowly on the table. "Two years ago a man worked fuel stations along this road asking questions. Polite man. Took notes. Bought diesel he didn't need. Six months later the station owner at Tres Cerros disappeared—not closed up, not moved, disappeared. His truck was found at Comodoro with the keys in it and no owner. The wife got a settlement from nobody, signed by nobody, and stopped answering letters." He looked up. "Vidal doesn't leave loose ends, boy. He doesn't leave witnesses. He doesn't even leave the memory of witnesses. Whatever you're carrying, understand that the price of it isn't your life. It's everyone who ever saw your face."

Ines had gone very still. "The station owner," she said. "What was his name?"

"Quiroga. Why?"

"Because he pumped our fuel at Tres Cerros four days ago," she said, "and he was alive then, and he remembered Paloma's truck."

Anselmo and I looked at each other, and I felt the floor of the evening tilt slightly, the way it does when the ground moves under a certainty. Four days. Vidal had been a week behind us at the pass. If Quiroga had talked—if Vidal had already collected him—

"Then we've been losing time we didn't know we were losing," I said. "We need to be moving at first light. Earlier."

"You'll move when I say," Anselmo said mildly, and refilled her glass. "Your friend needs sleep and a clean dressing, and your truck needs diesel and its oil checked, and neither of those things happens in the dark. Besides—" he nodded at the window, at the yellow bruise on the horizon that had deepened since we arrived—"you couldn't outrun what's coming anyway. Not tonight."

"It's just dust," I said. "High cloud, pressure fall, maybe a front off the cordillera by tomorrow night."

"That's what it looks like," he agreed. "Now tell me what it is."

I got up and went to the window and really looked, the way my father had taught me, the way Anselmo had helped teach me, before the drinking took him south and the debt took everything else. The cloud wasn't high anymore. It had organized while we ate—there was a shelf edge to it now, a hard line under the yellow, and beneath the line the air had that glassy, held-breath quality that means the atmosphere has loaded itself and is waiting for a reason. Behind the front, where the light was dying, the whole southwestern quadrant of the sky had gone the color of a dead man's lips.

"It's a haboob structure," I said slowly. "Dry front, cold pool aloft, dust entrainment all the way to the top. It'll travel on the nocturnal jet. Two hundred kilometers if it organizes right. Maybe more."

"And how big will it look when it comes?"

"Small. At distance, small. It lies about its size until it's on you, and then it's everywhere at once and the sun goes out at noon." I turned around. Both of them were watching me. "My father died in one of these. Or near one. We never found enough of him to ask."

The fire ticked. The windmill creaked its slow circle outside, counting out the seconds of the ordinary world.

"So," Anselmo said. "A storm that lies, a killer who erases, a woman with data worth killing for, and you, Paloma Dunmore, who owes money to men whose names you won't say in my kitchen." He smiled, and it was my father's smile, or nearly. "You always did travel light on luck and heavy on trouble."

"The money doesn't matter anymore," I said, and heard the truth of it land in my own chest like a dropped tool. Somewhere between the pass and this kitchen, between Tomás's shallow grave and Ines's shoulder bleeding through my shirt, the debt had stopped being the biggest thing in my life. It was still there. It would still kill me, eventually, or worse, take my sister's roof. But it had become a secondary system. "The data matters. Getting it to Comodoro matters. That's all."

Anselmo studied me for a long moment, and whatever he saw seemed to settle something in him.

"In the morning," he said, "I'll give you diesel, food, and the southern track—the old one, west of the lake chain, that isn't on any map the government prints. Vidal's people will watch the main road. They won't think of the old track, because their maps are newer than my memory." He raised a hand before I could speak. "And before you reach for your wallet, understand: you won't pay me. Either of you."

"Anselmo—"

"Your father," he said, "carried me forty kilometers on his back through a storm exactly like the one that's coming, with my leg broken in two places, because I was fool enough to trust a weather report instead of my teeth. He never let me repay it. Said debts like that get paid forward or they rot." He looked at Ines, then at me, and his voice went quiet and hard as the bread. "So this is me paying it forward. You'll pay yours forward someday, to someone you haven't met yet, in a place you can't imagine. That's how it works on the dry side. Everything else is just arithmetic, and arithmetic is for the Ferros of this world."

Neither of us said anything. Ines had her good hand pressed flat against the table, and I saw her throat work once, twice, and I understood that she was thinking of Tomás, of the marker in his hands, of the debt she owed the dead that no living person could ever collect. Some debts get paid forward or they rot. Hers would be paid forward to every town downstream of that poisoned aquifer, for fifty years, whether anyone ever knew her name or not.

"Thank you," she said, finally, and it came out rough, like something dragged over gravel.

"Don't thank me," Anselmo said. "Survive. That thanks me."

* * *

We slept in shifts—I took first watch out of habit, sitting by the window with the rifle across my knees, watching the southwest go black. Around midnight the wind arrived ahead of the front, a hot dry breath that smelled of a thousand kilometers of dust, and the temperature rose five degrees in an hour, which is the dry side's cruelest trick: the storm warms you as it comes, like a hand on your shoulder, like reassurance, right up until it kills you.

Ines woke near two and sat with me without being asked, wrapped in a wool blanket, her wounded arm cradled. For a while we watched the dark together.

"Your father sounds like he was a good man," she said.

"He was a good man who was bad with money and worse with promises. The Ferros bought his debts when he died. I inherited them at nineteen like furniture." I paused. "They're cheerful about it. That's the worst part. They smile. They call me *sobrino*. Nephew."

"And tomorrow you deliver their property to a journalist instead."

"They don't know yet."

"They will."

"They will," I agreed. "But that's tomorrow's weather."

She almost smiled. Outside, far off toward the southwest, there came a sound like the world clearing its throat—low, continuous, below hearing and above imagination—and both of us went still, listening to a storm still two hundred kilometers away announce that it had begun to move.

"That's early," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "It shouldn't be moving yet. The jet doesn't switch on till dawn."

"How fast?"

"If it rides the jet the way it wants to—" I did the arithmetic and didn't like any of the answers. "It could be on us by mid-morning. On the main road by noon. On anything that moves between here and Comodoro by afternoon."

"Then Vidal's trucks will be driving in it."

"Vidal's trucks will be driving in it," I said, "if Vidal's trucks are still behind us. Which means either we run ahead of it and beat it to Comodoro, or we let it catch us and hide inside it, and let it blind everyone hunting us—including, possibly, us."

Ines looked at me for a long moment in the dark kitchen, and I saw her arrive at the same place I'd been arriving at for days: that the wilderness forgives nothing, but it takes sides, sometimes, if you speak its language fluently enough.

"Which do we choose?" she asked.

Before I could answer, the door opened and Anselmo stood there in his coat, fully dressed, a lantern in his hand, looking out at the black southwest with an expression I recognized from the photograph on his wall—the expression of a man reading the sky and not liking the sentence it was writing.

"Change of plan," he said. "You leave now. Both of you. Because that storm isn't going to pass us by, and this old house won't survive it, and neither will the truth if it stops here." He set the lantern on the table and looked at me. "How good are you, Paloma? Really? Your father once drove inside one of these things for six hours by feel alone. Tell me honestly whether you're that good, because if you're not, we're all going to die arguing about maps."

I looked at the darkness pressing against the windows, and felt the pressure falling through my back teeth like a countdown, and told him the truth:

"I'm better."
