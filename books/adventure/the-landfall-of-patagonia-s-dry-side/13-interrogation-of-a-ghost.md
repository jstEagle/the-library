## 13. Interrogation of a Ghost

The wind at the fuel station came off the plateau in long, flat pushes, and it carried the smell of diesel and dry grass and nothing else for two hundred kilometers in any direction. Vidal stood at the edge of the forecourt with his hands in his pockets and watched the man who owned the place lock the pumps for the night.

His name was Ezequiel Barrientos. Fifty-one years old. Widower. One daughter in Comodoro, studying nursing. He had run this station for nineteen years and in that time had learned exactly one lesson that mattered: the steppe did not forgive mistakes, and neither did the people who drove through it. Vidal had read the file on the drive down from Perito Moreno. It was a thin file. Thin files meant careful men, and careful men were easy to work with, because careful men could count.

Barrientos finished with the lock, turned, and saw him. Vidal watched the man's face do the arithmetic — stranger, city clothes wrong for the country, a white Toyota Hilux parked neatly at the edge of the gravel with its engine off and its windows down — and arrive at the answer he had been trained by nineteen years of steppe to arrive at, which was: be polite, be quick, be done.

"Closed," Barrientos said. "Come back at seven."

"I don't need fuel." Vidal crossed the forecourt at an unhurried pace. "I need ten minutes, and then I'll be gone, and you'll never see me again. That's a better offer than most people get out here."

Barrientos's eyes went to the Toyota, then back. Looking for the second man. There was no second man — Vidal worked alone at this stage, by preference and by doctrine — but the looking was useful. A man who checked for accomplices had already decided to be afraid, and a man who had decided to be afraid would answer questions the way water runs downhill.

"There's a bench," Barrientos said.

They sat on the bench by the door, under the buzzing fluorescent tube that lit the forecourt and made a small island of false daylight in all that darkness. Vidal took out a notebook — paper, not a phone. Phones recorded. Phones were evidence. Paper was a man thinking out loud, and a man thinking out loud could be reasoned with.

"Sunday," Vidal said. "Between four and six in the afternoon. A truck came through. Grey, older model, the kind with the rust along the door seam. Two people in it. A man driving, a woman in the passenger seat with her arm in a sling or a bandage — something that made her hold it against her chest." He said it flatly, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "They bought fuel. Diesel, probably, but maybe nafta if he was being careless. They bought food. They bought water, because the water out here is bad and they knew it, which means at least one of them has driven this country before."

Barrientos had gone very still. Not the stillness of a man deciding whether to lie. The stillness of a man discovering that the question had already been answered before it was asked, and that his only remaining choice was how much dignity to keep while confirming it.

"I close the till at night," he said. "I don't remember every—"

"Ezequiel." Vidal let the name sit there for a moment, friendly as a hand on the shoulder. "I'm not the police. If I were the police, this would be easier for you, and I want you to understand that it isn't. The police need a reason to make your life difficult. I don't need a reason. I need only a result. And the result I need is very small. I need to know which way a truck went. That's all. Two hundred kilometers of road in every direction, and I need to know which two hundred kilometers not to waste my time on."

The fluorescent tube buzzed. Somewhere behind the station, a dog that wasn't really a pet and wasn't really wild scratched itself against the tin wall and thought about barking and decided against it, the way everything on the dry side eventually decided against it.

"They took diesel," Barrientos said. "Forty liters, maybe forty-five. And the food — empanadas, the ones my niece brings frozen from Gobernador. Six. And water, four of the big bottles." He paused, and Vidal let him pause, because the pause was where the useful thing lived. "The man paid cash. Counted it out exact. But the woman — she asked me something."

Vidal turned a page in the notebook, though he wrote nothing. Turning pages made men talk. "What did she ask?"

"Whether the south road was washed out at the Cañadón. The ford." Barrientos rubbed his jaw. "I told her it was passable if she stayed to the left bank, the gravel bar, because the right side goes soft after rain and there hasn't been rain, but there's rain coming, I can feel it in my knee. And she thanked me. Polite woman. Tired. She had that look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone who's been sleeping in the truck." He shrugged. "You see it out here. People running from something, or toward something. Same look, mostly."

Vidal closed the notebook. "Thank you, Ezequiel. You've been very helpful."

"That's it? That's all?"

"That's all." Vidal stood. Then, because doctrine said to leave the anchor, he turned back and smiled — a real smile, unhurried, the kind that reached the eyes because the eyes had nothing to fear. "One more thing, and it's not a question. In a day, maybe two, other people will come through here asking about that truck. They won't be like me. I read files. They burn them. If they ask you what you told me, you tell them the truth — that a man came, asked about a truck, and left. But if you've kept a guest book, or camera footage, or a receipt with a plate number on it, and those men find it before I do—" he spread his hands, apologetic, a man regretting the weather, "—then they'll know you knew, and knowing is a thing out here that people get hurt for. So tonight, before you sleep, you might check what you have, and you might decide some of it is missing. Not for me. For your daughter in Comodoro. She's in her second year, isn't she? Nursing is a good profession. Stable."

Barrientos's face did not change, which was itself an answer, and Vidal nodded as if the answer had been yes, thank you, I'll see to it, and walked back across the forecourt to the Toyota, and by the time the station owner had found his keys and his courage, the white truck was a pair of taillights shrinking north along the Ruta, and the steppe had closed over it like water.

* * *

In the truck, with the dome light off, Vidal worked the map by the glow of his tablet, the way he had worked every night for nine days, because the hunt was not a matter of luck or instinct. Instinct was for the people he was hunting. He had method.

He drew the line first. Gobernador Gregores to the fuel station: one hundred and sixty kilometers, driven in — Barrientos's till had said 17:40, and the last known sighting from the estancia at the northern end, an old man named Ferreyra who had fed them and, Ferreyra swore, asked nothing — put their average at under sixty kilometers an hour. Slow. Deliberate. A man driving tired, or a man driving careful, or a man driving a woman who could not be bounced over washboard at speed with a hole in her shoulder.

The woman. Vidal had her file open on the tablet, the photograph from the ministry ID: Dr. Inés Cabral, hydrogeologist, thirty-four years old, listed as missing presumed dead along with four colleagues of the Río Deseado survey. Presumed dead was a bureaucratic invention, and Vidal had spent twenty-two years in the business of converting bureaucratic inventions back into facts. She was alive. The estancia had confirmed it. The fuel station had confirmed it. She was alive, she was wounded, she was moving south, and she was carrying something that had caused four men in Buenos Aires to stop answering their phones and one man to start.

He plotted the ford at the Cañadón. The woman had asked about it, which meant they intended to cross it, which meant they were not following the Ruta south toward Bajo Caracoles and the highway — too obvious, and they had already been seen there once. No. The ford put them on the old stock route, the track that followed the dry riverbed southeast through the cañadones. It was slower. It was rougher. It was also, Vidal noted, almost entirely unobserved: no stations, no estancias, nothing for two hundred and ten kilometers except an abandoned shearing shed and a rail siding that had not seen a train in six years.

Which meant they were choosing to be invisible. Which meant they knew they were followed. Which meant Paloma Dunmore — he turned to the second file, the salvage contractor, the storm chaser with the debts — had told her, or she had told him, and either way the two of them were now a single organism with a single plan, and single plans could be intercepted.

He ran the arithmetic. Their fuel: forty-five liters at the station, on top of whatever the grey truck held, call it eighty in the tank at best. The stock route was rough; call it twenty liters per hundred kilometers, generous to them. Two hundred and ten kilometers to the siding, then — where? Comodoro was four hundred more. They would need fuel again. There were three places to get it on that side of the plateau: the shearing shed had an old tank, probably dry, possibly not; the siding had nothing; and there was a ranchero named Ocampo who sold diesel out of a bowser to whoever came down off the meseta, no questions, cash, because Ocampo had never in his life asked a question he didn't already know the answer to.

Vidal marked Ocampo. Then he marked the siding. Then he sat back and looked at the two marks and let the map do what maps do, which is tell you where a thing must happen if it happens at all.

They could not reach Comodoro without fuel. They could not take the highway without being seen — he had already put feelers at every pump from Tres Cerros to Comodoro, and the feelers were cheap: a photograph, a phone number, a promise of ten thousand pesos for a call. The net was not to catch them. The net was to make them feel caught, because men who feel caught make mistakes, and mistakes on the dry side were usually terminal without any help from him at all.

So: the siding. The siding had a water tower, a loading ramp, and a rail line that ran — he checked, and smiled at the check, because the check rewarded him — straight south to Comodoro. Freight still ran it twice a week, and the crews could be paid to look elsewhere. If they were smart, they would try to ride the rails or flag a freight or cache themselves at the siding until dark and push through on the maintenance track. If they were smart, they would do exactly what he would do.

And if they were not smart — if they were tired, and bleeding, and running out of water — they would do something dumber, and dumber was fine too. Dumber just meant more kilometers on his own truck, and his truck was fueled, and his men were fueled, and his men were not like him. He had chosen them for that. Chacón, who had been a gendarme and understood checkpoints and roadblocks and the geometry of a kill box. The twins, Rueda and Rueda, who said nothing, drank nothing, and could drive all night across country that had no roads and call it a Tuesday. And Osorio, who kept the radios and the drones and the quiet, patient electronics that could hear a truck engine at four kilometers across open steppe if you knew where to stand.

Six men, counting himself. Two vehicles plus the drone. Against a wounded woman and a debt-ridden storm chaser in a tired grey truck.

It should have been enough. It was enough. Vidal knew it was enough the way he knew the load rating of a bridge — by numbers, not by faith. And yet.

He opened the weather file.

It was the one thing about Dunmore that the Ferro brothers' dossier had flagged and then, in Vidal's opinion, criminally under-weighted. The man chased storms. Not as a hobby — as a trade, as a craft, as the single thing he was known for across half of Patagonia. The dossier called it a novelty. Clients paid him to put them under storm fronts for photographs. There was a line in a magazine profile, quoted in the file: *Dunmore reads the dry side the way sailors read water — he feels the storm coming hours before the instruments agree with him.*

Vidal had spent the afternoon doing what he always did with an asset he did not control: he had studied it. He had pulled the synoptic charts for the next seventy-two hours, and he had sat with them for a long time in the cab of the truck, and what he saw he did not entirely like.

A front was coming down off the cordillera. It would cross the Andes tomorrow, lose its rain over the western slopes the way fronts always did, and arrive on the dry side as something else — as wind, as dust, as a wall of grit two hundred kilometers wide that would drop visibility to nothing and stay nothing for the better part of a day. The charts said it would make landfall on the plateau in roughly thirty-six hours. The charts said its track would pass directly over the cañadones, the stock route, and the rail siding.

The charts, Vidal reflected, were drawn by men in offices who had never stood on the dry side and watched the sky lie about its own intentions.

He thought about it the way he thought about everything: as a variable to be managed. A storm was a problem for tracking, yes. But a storm was also a problem for them. A grey truck with a wounded woman, driving blind through a dust front on roads that didn't exist, with the cañadón walls on either side — people died in those conditions, and not rarely. The steppe did the work and no one had to sign anything. There was a version of the next three days in which Vidal's entire contribution was to park his trucks at the southern exits of the cañadones and let the weather do the interview.

He almost preferred that version. It was cleaner. It left fewer shapes to explain.

But he did not trust it, because he did not trust Dunmore, and the reason he did not trust Dunmore was the fuel station, and the reason was the question the woman had asked about the ford. They were not running blind. They were running a route. A man who chases storms for a living does not pick a route through a dust front by accident. He picks it the way Vidal picked a kill box — because the weather is on his side of the argument.

Which meant the storm was not an obstacle in their path. It was a weapon in their hands.

Vidal turned the tablet off and sat in the dark and listened to the wind push at the Toyota's body, a long flat shove, then another, like something enormous turning over in its sleep to the west. He had interrogated the fuel station owner and gotten the truth, and the truth had told him the route, and the route had told him the place, and the place had told him the hour, and every number in the chain was good except the one number no one could give him, which was what the sky would do in thirty-six hours, and whether the man driving the grey truck had already read it better than the charts had.

He picked up the satellite phone and dialed the number he had been given for exactly one purpose, and when it connected he said, without greeting, "They're on the stock route, heading for the rail siding. I'll have them inside two days. But there's weather coming, and the man they're with is the kind who uses it. If you want this finished clean, I need authority to move tonight, not tomorrow."

The voice on the other end was quiet and unhurried, and it said, "You have tonight. And Vidal — the drive matters more than the woman, and the woman matters more than the contractor. In that order. If the weather takes the contractor, write it down as an act of God. Everyone out here believes in those."

Vidal looked west, where the last light was going out of the sky and the first stars were coming up hard and cold over the black line of the plateau, and somewhere out there under all that dark, a grey truck was rolling south with its lights low, carrying a dead man's data and a living man's debt, and between it and Comodoro stood six men, one siding, and a storm that had not yet decided whose side it was on.

"Understood," he said, and hung up, and started the engine.

The wind pushed at the truck again, harder now, and it had changed. He noticed it the way a man notices a change in a voice — not the words, the tone. It had come around a few points to the northwest, and it had brought something with it, something faint and mineral, the smell of dust that had been lifted off country two hundred kilometers away and was already traveling.

The front was early.

Vidal put the Toyota on the road south and drove into the dark at exactly the speed limit, because a man who hurries on the dry side is a man who has already lost, and he did not intend to lose. Behind him, the fuel station's fluorescent tube buzzed alone over its empty forecourt, and Ezequiel Barrientos, inside, was feeding his guest book into the wood stove page by page, and the fire took each page the way the steppe took everything, without comment, without remainder, without ever telling anyone where it had put it.
