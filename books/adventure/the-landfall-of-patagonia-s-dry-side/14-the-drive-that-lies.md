## 14. The Drive That Lies

We drove south through the blue hour with the windows cracked and the engine running rough, because the fuel at Bajo Caracoles had been cut with something the old man apologized for twice. Ines slept against the door with her wounded arm strapped across her chest like a sling she'd made of herself, and I let her sleep, and I told myself I wasn't watching her.

I was watching her.

The drive sat in my jacket pocket, warm from my body heat, a rectangle of black plastic no bigger than a pack of cigarettes. Tomás's casing marker was still zip-tied to it — a strip of survey tape with coordinates in his cramped hand, the ink bleeding where groundwater or grief had gotten to it. Every bump in the road pressed it against my ribs like a second heart.

At the next settlement with power — a one-pump place called Tres Cerros where the wind combed the gravel flat — I bought diesel, mate, and an hour of the owner's generator for more than the diesel cost. There was a back room with a dead television, a folding table, and a socket that still worked. I told Ines I wanted to check the truck's wiring. She believed me, or she let me believe she believed me, which on this steppe amounted to the same courtesy.

I plugged the drive into the owner's ancient laptop and waited for the light.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about data: it has a smell. Not literal — but after ten years of chasing weather, of reading pressure traces and radar returns and the lies satellites tell, I'd learned to feel when a record was wrong the way you feel a wrong note in a familiar song. A storm that's been doctored — a radar loop edited to hide a hook echo, a warning issued late on purpose — carries a seam in it. Something too clean where chaos should live.

The files opened. Folder trees bloomed down the screen: core logs, spectrometry reads, water tables, ministry correspondence. Tomás had been meticulous. Timestamps marched in order. Filenames were sober and numbered. It looked like truth the way a wax fruit looks like a pear.

Then I found the first seam.

A gap. Eleven hours missing between sample batch 44 and batch 45, on the night of March 9th — not deleted, nothing so crude as deleted, but the timestamps simply skipped over it, the way a man telling a story skips the part that damns him. And inside batch 45, the arsenic readings that had made Ines go white at the drill site were there, all right, but their calibration header didn't match the instrument serial number recorded two folders up. Same model. Different unit. As if someone had re-run the numbers through a different machine — or copied them from somewhere else entirely.

I sat very still in the dead-TV room while the generator coughed outside and the wind pushed grit against the window like fingers testing the glass.

Corrupted, I told myself. Buried drives get damp. Casings flood. Bits rot.

Except rot doesn't skip eleven hours neatly. Rot doesn't swap serial numbers. Rot makes noise; this was quiet. This was *tidy*. Someone had gone through this drive the way Vidal's crew would have gone through the camp — removing what mattered, leaving enough behind to make the scene look honest.

And there was exactly one person who could have done it before Tomás ever buried the casings. Or after he buried them, if she'd gone back. Or during the eleven days she'd spent alone on the steppe with nothing but time and a reason to curate.

Ines Cabral had given me a cover story within an hour of me pulling her out of a pit of guanaco carcasses. She'd negotiated our alliance like a woman trading horses. She knew things she hadn't said — names she'd admitted holding back, officials shielded behind a "source" she wouldn't name. I'd accepted that. I'd even admired it.

But admiration and trust are different animals, and only one of them bites.

I thought about the Ferros, cheerful and patient, resetting my debt with smiles like a knife laid flat on a table. I thought about the second crew, the cleanup men, and how they always seemed half a day behind us — close enough to frighten, never quite close enough to catch us. Close enough to herd us.

What if the salvage contract had never been about destroying evidence? What if it was about *delivering* it? What if the Ferros' government contact didn't need the real data destroyed — needed it controlled, curated, pointed like a weapon at the right people and away from the wrong ones? What if the bloodhound they hired was meant to fetch home not a survivor but a package, and the package had already been wrapped by the person carrying it?

Eleven days alone on the steppe. Time enough to open a casing, edit a drive, seal it again. Time enough to decide which truths survived.

Or time enough to survive, and be framed afterward by whoever killed her friends.

Both stories fit. That was the worst part. I turned them over like two stones of equal weight and could not find the flaw in either, and outside the generator died mid-cycle and the room went dark except for the laptop's glow, and in that glow I understood something simple and sick: I had stopped reading the landscape the moment I started trusting a person. Out here, on the dry side, that's how people die. You stop checking the horizon because someone else says the sky is clear.

* * *

I copied everything — the whole tree, seams and all — onto a second card I'd bought off the fuel-station owner along with his generator hour, then wiped my fingerprints off the keyboard with my sleeve like a superstitious man crossing himself. When I came out, Ines was awake, sitting on the tailgate with her good arm around a mug of mate, watching the southern sky where a band of high cloud had come in off the Andes, thin as smoke.

"Storm?" she asked.

"Dry lightning, maybe. Far side of the plateau." I handed her a mug. "How's the shoulder?"

"Healing. Which means it hurts properly now." She drank. Her eyes went to my jacket pocket without meaning to — I saw it happen, the flick, the correction — and I felt the fracture run through me like ice on a pond, hearing its own crack.

She knew I'd looked at the drive. Or she suspected. And she hadn't said anything, which meant she was deciding, right now, how much of her silence was mercy and how much was strategy — and I was doing the identical arithmetic, and we sat there drinking mate and lying to each other with our whole relaxed bodies.

"The laptop in there," I said casually. "Owner's. Does yours have a port that fits the drive? I want to make sure Tomás's files are intact before we risk the coast roads."

Something moved behind her eyes. Small. A shutter adjusting to light.

"It's fine," she said. "Tomás was careful."

"Everyone's careful until the ground moves." I smiled. "Humor me. We're near Comodoro. Two days out. If the drive's damaged, better to know now."

"In Paloma—" She stopped. Started again, softer. "It's intact. I checked it myself. After the pass."

"After the ambush," I said. "While I was dragging you uphill with a bullet hole in you."

The silence stretched long enough to have a shape. Down the road a dog barked twice and gave up, the way everything out here eventually gives up.

"Yes," she said.

"You checked the data. All of it."

"I checked that it opened." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb had found the rim of the mug and was tracing it, around and around, a surveyor mapping the edge of something. "Why are you asking like that?"

"Because there's an eleven-hour hole in it," I said. "Because the calibration headers don't match the instrument serials. Because somebody built this drive to tell a specific story, and I've spent my whole life spotting when a record's been edited, and Ines — this one has been. Recently. Carefully."

I watched her take that in, watched her weigh her answers, and I realized with a cold clarity that I was watching a person choose a lie in real time, and that I could no longer tell whether the lie would protect me or bury me.

"You don't understand what you're looking at," she said finally.

"Then help me understand."

"I can't." And there it was — not denial, not anger, just a wall going up brick by brick, and behind her eyes a calculation I couldn't read, and every instinct I owned stood up and screamed at once.

* * *

We drove another hour in a silence so complete I could hear the differential whine change pitch on the grades. The high cloud thickened ahead, bruising violet, throwing the first dry shadows across the steppe. No rain would come out of it — not tonight, maybe not for weeks — but it made the land look like it remembered water, which on the dry side is its cruelest trick.

I pulled off at a dry riverbed where the banks rose chest-high and the gravel floor ran smooth as a road, a natural corral with one way in. If Vidal's trucks found us here, we'd hear them coming twenty minutes early. If Ines planned to run, she'd have to do it on foot. I told myself both reasons. Only one of them was true.

I shut off the engine. The ticking of cooling metal was the loudest thing for fifty kilometers.

"We're stopping here," I said.

She looked at the banks, then at me, and I saw her do her own arithmetic — see the walls, count the exits, reach the same total I had. "Why here?"

"Because tomorrow we hit Comodoro, and whatever happens after Comodoro happens forever. And because one of us is lying, and I'd rather find out who with the engine cold and nowhere to be."

"And if it's you?"

"Then you'll know soon enough."

She set the mug down on the dash with exaggerated care, the way you put down a thing you might throw. "You pulled me out of a pit," she said quietly. "You dug up my friend's body. You took a bullet graze getting me out of that pass. And now you sit there and call me a liar."

"I'm calling the drive a liar. So far you're just refusing to defend it."

"That's not—"

"What happened in those eleven hours, Ines? Before I found you. What did you do with the drive?"

Her jaw worked. Outside, the first gust came down the riverbed and rattled the truck, carrying dust and the faint mineral smell of a storm still two hundred kilometers away — a storm I'd been feeling build since dawn, big and slow and honest, the only thing left in my life that didn't lie to me.

"You think I doctored it," she said.

"I think somebody did. I think the version we're carrying points the scandal at certain names and shields others, and I think you told me yourself you're protecting a source. And I think the Ferros' contact doesn't want the truth destroyed — he wants it *delivered*, aimed, and I was hired to carry the arrow. So either you're part of that aim, Ines, or somebody used your grief to bend the data after your team died, and you know which it is, and you won't say."

The wind gusted again. Somewhere north, thunderless lightning flickered inside the cloud like a lamp behind a curtain.

"If I tell you," she said at last, very low, "you'll do something stupid and noble and we'll both die on this steppe for nothing."

"Try me. I'm already stupid and noble. It's the dying part I'm flexible on."

She almost laughed — a broken little sound, gone as fast as it came. Then she looked at me for a long moment, measuring me the way she'd measured me in the pit, except this time I could feel the scales tipping, feel her deciding whether I was the trap or the way out.

"There are names in the original files," she said. "Three names. One of them is the reason any of us are still breathing, and if it goes public before the right journalist holds the rest of it, that person will bury the whole story — the aquifer, the team, us — under an official inquiry that takes nine years and ends in nothing. I held those names back. I built the gaps. Me, Paloma. Not the Ferros. Not Vidal. Me."

The riverbed seemed to tilt. "You edited it yourself."

"I protected it." Her voice cracked down the middle, and underneath the crack was eleven days of carcass pits and dead friends and a truth carried alone through country that kills the unprepared. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference, or Tomás died for a folder of corrupted plastic."

"Where's the original?"

She looked at me, and I watched the last wall come down — not all the way, but enough to see the shape of what stood behind it.

"Half of it's in my head," she said. "The other half is hidden somewhere you'll never find without me. So before you decide whether I'm the trap, Paloma, you should decide something else: whether you can afford to lose me. Because right now, I'm both."

Northward, inside the bruised cloud, the lightning came again — closer this time, and this time I heard the thunder roll down the plateau toward us, long and low, like something clearing its throat.
