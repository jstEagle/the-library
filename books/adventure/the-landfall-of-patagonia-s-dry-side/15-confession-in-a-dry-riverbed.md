## 15. Confession in a Dry Riverbed

**Paloma**

The riverbed had been dry for longer than either of them had been alive. Paloma could read that in the banks—steep, crumbled, unhealed by water, the gravel pale as bone where the sun had bleached it for decades. A river that died and stayed dead. He'd picked the spot for exactly that reason: nothing came here. No guanacos, no fuel trucks, no men named Vidal. Just wind moving down the channel like a slow animal, and the two of them sitting on opposite sides of a cold fire because neither of them trusted the other enough to share warmth.

The drive sat between them on a flat stone like evidence at a trial.

He'd spent the whole day not talking about it. Driving south with the dust of the second crew hanging behind them like a debt, Ines navigating from the passenger seat with her shoulder strapped and her face gray, both of them performing a version of partnership that had gone rotten somewhere around the moment he'd plugged the drive into his laptop under a tarp outside Perito Moreno and watched the files open like a locked room with half the furniture missing.

Names missing. Dates missing. The ministry signatures redacted—not blacked out, *removed*, cleanly, professionally, the way you'd remove a tumor.

She'd curated it. Or someone had. And he couldn't tell which possibility was worse: that she was lying to him, or that she'd been lying to herself since the day she crawled out of that pit.

Now the light was going copper over the canyon rim and he was done performing.

"Tell me about the names," he said.

Ines didn't look up from the fire she was pretending to build. "What names?"

"The ones that aren't there." He turned the laptop toward her, screen glowing in the dusk. "I've been reading this thing for two days, Doctor. I know what a poisoned aquifer looks like on paper. I don't know who signed off on it. Every signature block is empty. Every name field is clean. You don't get clean like that by accident."

* * *

**Ines**

She had known this was coming since the fuel stop, when he'd looked at her across the roof of the truck with that flat storm-reader's stare and she'd understood that he'd opened the drive without asking her permission. She'd told herself she had two days to prepare an answer. She'd used them the way she used all her time lately—badly, and awake.

The truth was simple and she'd been carrying it like a stone in her boot for eleven days: she had held back three names. Not because they were false. Because one of them was the only person left alive who could make the rest of the data mean anything—a hydrologist inside the ministry, Marta Quiroga, who had signed the original 2022 internal warning and then spent two years watching her own signature get buried. Tomás had trusted her. Ines had promised him, in the last real conversation they ever had, that if anything happened she would protect that thread even if it cost everything else.

Everything else had turned out to include three friends, one arm's full range of motion, and now possibly this man sitting across from her with his dead river and his honest eyes.

"You're right," she said. "I removed them."

His face didn't change. That was the frightening thing about Paloma Dunmore—weather moved across him constantly and none of it showed.

"Marta Quiroga," Ines said. "Deputy directorate of hydrology. She wrote the first contamination assessment before anyone decided it shouldn't exist. Tomás knew her from university. If the story breaks without her testimony, it becomes a scandal about a file. With her, it becomes a crime with a paper trail." She fed a stick of dead ñire into the fire she wasn't building. "I took the names out so that if the drive was taken from us, whoever took it would learn nothing about her. Including your Ferros. Including their contact. Including Vidal's crew."

"And including me."

"Including you," she agreed. "You were the man paid to find me. Forgive me for not handing you my last living source on day one."

"I'm not that man anymore."

"Aren't you?" The question came out sharper than she intended, and she let it stay sharp, because softness between them now would be a kind of lie worse than the redaction. "You haven't told me who holds your leash, Paloma. I've given you a dead team, a buried aquifer, and the only reason I'm still breathing. You've given me a cover story about a salvage contract and a first name. So before we talk about what I withheld—" she nodded at the laptop, at him, at the whole arrangement of their lives, "—you go first."

* * *

**Paloma**

There it was. The bill, presented politely, in a dry riverbed at dusk.

He thought about lying. It was almost funny—he was good at lying, had kept himself alive on it for years, cheerful lies to the Ferros about payment dates, competent lies to clients about how safe storm-chasing was, lies to Marisol on the phone every Sunday about how things were *fine, sí, fine*. Lying was the family trade of everyone who owed money.

But Ines Cabral had crawled out of a carcass pit with a broken collarbone and eleven days of steppe behind her, and she had just handed him the one thing Vidal's crew would kill for, unprompted, because he'd asked. There were maybe four people in the world who would do that. He was married to the idea of becoming one of them, and you didn't get there by keeping the ledger one-sided.

"The Ferro brothers," he said. "Ricardo and Ernesto. They lend money in Comodoro—equipment leases, truck notes, cash against future work. Cheerful men. Patient. They've never once threatened me." He smiled, and it felt like showing a scar. "That's how you know they're serious. My sister Marisol runs a guesthouse on a lease the Ferros hold. Three years left on it. Every Sunday she calls me and tells me business is good, and every Sunday I hear the lease underneath her voice like a foundation crack."

Ines had gone very still. Firelight moved on her face and he watched her assemble it—the satellite call at the pass, the Ferros' offer, the way he'd flinched at nothing and said *they can't touch my family* and refused to elaborate.

"They hold her lease," Ines said quietly. "So when they offered to forgive your debt—"

"It wasn't just my debt. It was her roof. Her kid's school. The whole architecture." He turned the laptop shut, gently, like closing a coffin. "I've been carrying that since chapter one of this disaster, Doctor, and telling you exactly nothing. So we're even. Worse than even—I knew what they wanted before you did. I was the bloodhound they hired to sniff you out, and I took the contract anyway, because poverty argues louder than instinct. You want confessions? That's mine. I found you and for about six hours I genuinely considered delivering you."

"What changed?"

He looked at her—at the strapped shoulder, the burned hands, the discipline that had kept her alive in a pit while her team died around her—and found he didn't have a clever answer.

"You asked me to take you to a journalist instead of the ministry," he said. "And I believed you. That was the whole thing. Six hours of arithmetic and then one sentence of belief, and the arithmetic lost." He shrugged. "I'm a weather man. I know when a front means what it says."

* * *

**Ines**

She hadn't expected the honesty to hurt. But it did—it landed in her chest like a core sample, a clean cylinder drilled out of him, and she could see the strata: debt, fear, love for his sister, shame, and running through all of it that strange gift for reading pressure systems that had probably saved her life twice already.

They sat with it. The wind came down the dead riverbed and stirred the fire at last, and she finally built it properly, feeding it small and tight, and the act of making something burn steadied her.

"I need to tell you the rest," she said. "Not the part I hid from you. The part I hid from myself."

He waited. He was good at waiting; it was half of what storm-chasing was.

"When I found the readings that didn't match any filed report, Tomás already knew. He'd known for weeks. He'd hidden duplicate drives in the drill casings before I ever saw a contaminated sample—and he never told me, because he'd already worked out that the disappearance was arranged before we left Comodoro, and he didn't want me carrying knowledge I could be tortured into giving up." Her voice went level, which was how she knew she was close to the edge. "He protected me by excluding me. And I survived because of it, and I hate that. I hate that his distrust of our own government was more accurate than my faith in it. So when I curated that drive, I told myself I was protecting Marta. Part of me was also protecting the version of events where I'm the careful one. Where withholding is wisdom."

"And now?"

"Now I think withholding is just burial with better manners." She looked at him across the fire. "Marta stays protected—that doesn't change, and if you have a problem with it, say it now. But the rest goes in whole. Names, dates, the ministry sign-off, everything, unedited, tonight, in front of you. You'll have seen all of it. If you sell me to the Ferros after that, at least you'll know exactly what you sold."

"That's a hell of a wedding vow."

It surprised a laugh out of her—short, cracked, real—and she realized with something like vertigo that it was the first time she'd laughed in twelve days, and that it was this man, this mercenary with his sister's roof held hostage, who'd pulled it out of her.

"Is that what this is?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

* * *

**Paloma**

He should have deflected. Deflection was load-bearing too; he'd built whole years on it.

Instead he looked at the fire and answered honestly, because the riverbed seemed to demand it, because the day was dying and Vidal was somewhere behind them reconstructing their route out of fuel receipts, because there might not be another night quiet enough for truth.

"I don't know what this is," he said. "I know it isn't tender. Tender needs safety and we don't have any. But I know that when you were feverish two nights ago you grabbed my wrist and told me the coordinates for the casings, and I wrote them down with your hand still crushing mine, and afterward I didn't want to let go. I know that when the Ferros mentioned Marisol I got so angry I couldn't see straight, but when Vidal's crew shot you, I stopped being angry and started being *exact*, and exact is worse. I know that whatever happens tomorrow, I'm not handing you to anyone. That's not affection. That's structural." He met her eyes. "You're holding the roof up. So am I. Turns out that looks a lot like love from the inside."

Ines stared at him for a long moment. Then she reached across the dead riverbed, past the fire, past the drive on its stone, and took his wrist—the same grip, deliberate this time, no fever in it.

"Load-bearing," she said. "Fine. But understand the engineering, Paloma. If you betray me now, the whole structure comes down on both of us. There's no partial collapse."

"Noted." He turned his hand until their fingers laced, rough palm against burned one. "Same goes for you and Marta Quiroga. We sink together or we stand."

"We stand," she said, and it sounded like a survey marker driven into rock.

* * *

**Paloma**

They worked through the night by headlamp, restoring the redactions from her memory while she dictated and he typed, her voice steady through the ministry names and breaking only once—on Tomás's field notes, where he'd written *if found, tell Ines she was right about everything*—and by the time the eastern sky went the color of old tin, the drive held the whole ugly truth, complete, unburied, and duplicated onto a second card Paloma taped inside the lining of his jacket, because people who dealt with the Ferros learned redundancy young.

They broke camp fast, cold coffee drunk standing, the truck loaded in the gray half-light. Ines climbed into the passenger seat with her shoulder stiff and her eyes red-rimmed and clearer than he'd ever seen them.

"Route?" he asked.

"South through the pass before noon. After that, open country to Route 40, then Comodoro." She checked the pistol he'd taught her to strip and reassemble, clumsy with her left hand, stubborn about it. "Vidal will guess the pass. It's the only way through with a wounded woman and a heavy truck."

"Then we don't give him noon." Paloma started the engine and looked east, where the horizon had gone a strange bruised yellow-brown, and felt it—the pressure dropping through his back teeth, the wind dying when it should be rising, the huge silent weight of dust stacked up beyond the hills like a wave that hadn't been told yet that it was a wave. He'd been tracking it since dawn without saying so. It was early for a front that size. It was lying about its size, the way fronts did here, and it was coming down the same corridor they needed.

Or the corridor they needed was coming to them.

"Ines," he said slowly, turning the wheel toward the pass, "how do you feel about driving into a storm on purpose?"

She followed his gaze to the bruised horizon and, to her credit, only hesitated a second.

"How big?"

"Big enough to hide in." He grinned, and it wasn't a nice grin. "Big enough to lose trucks in. Big enough that Vidal's methodical little crew has to choose between turning back and going blind."

The old guide's voice came back to him—*storms on the dry side come fast and lie about their size*—and Paloma put his foot down and drove them both into the yellow dark.
