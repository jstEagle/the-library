## 16. Ambush at the Pass

The canyon had a name on no map I trusted—Cañadón de los Huesos, the old guide had called it, half a joke and half a warning—and by midmorning it was doing exactly what canyons do, which is funnel everything: wind, light, and now us.

I'd felt the storm building since dawn. Not seen it—there was nothing to see yet, just a sky scrubbed too clean over the southern horizon and a pressure in my back teeth like a filling going bad. On the dry side of Patagonia, storms don't announce themselves with clouds. They announce themselves with silence. The guanacos stop moving. The wind dies so completely you can hear your own blood. And somewhere out past the edge of sight, a wall of dust the size of a province starts walking north, patient as debt.

I knew it was coming the way I know most things nobody pays me to know anymore.

What I didn't know, until it was far too late to matter, was that Vidal knew this canyon too.

* * *

We came into it fast because Ines wanted fast. Her shoulder had been aching since Bajo Caracoles—not the wound itself, she said, but the weather in it, which told me she was either lying or more useful than I'd credited her. The drive sat in a lead-lined film bag against her ribs, taped where Tomás's casing marker had been, and she touched it every ninety seconds like a rosary.

"Slow down," I said, for the third time.

"You said the storm buys us hours."

"The storm buys *them* hours too. Men like Vidal don't stop for weather. They just get angrier."

She didn't slow down. The truck took a bend hard enough that the cargo net snapped against the roll bar, and that's when I saw it—the thing my eyes caught a half-second before my brain agreed to understand it.

A tire track. Fresh, crisp-edged, cut into the canyon floor's crust where rain hadn't touched it in weeks. Running parallel to ours. Heading deeper into the pass.

I stood on the brakes. The truck slewed, fishtailed, stopped in a curtain of our own dust, and in that dust I heard what I should have heard ten seconds earlier: an engine, idling, somewhere above us. Not moving. Waiting.

"Out," I said. "Now. Leave the bags."

Ines was already moving. She'd learned something in eleven days alone on the steppe, and it wasn't survival trivia—it was obedience to other people's fear. We went over the tailgate and into the rocks on the south side while the first round came off the rim above us and hit the truck's fuel tank with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The engine didn't catch fire. It just died, hissing, and I remember thinking with absurd clarity: *that's the deposit gone. That's another year of Ferro arithmetic.* Then a second round came in and blew out the windshield and I stopped doing math entirely.

"Ines. Up-slope. Find shadow."

"There's no shadow," she said, and she was right—the sun stood high and stupid in a cloudless sky—but there were ledges, and beneath the ledges there were seams in the rock, and we folded ourselves into one while the shooting walked down the slope toward our abandoned truck with the unhurried rhythm of men who had done this before and enjoyed the parts they were good at.

Four shooters. Maybe five. I counted muzzle flashes the way I count lightning strokes—interval, bearing, elevation—and built a picture of their positions without ever lifting my head. Two high on the north rim, bracketing the road. One on the south side ahead of us, cutting off the exit. One mobile, working down toward the truck to confirm kills.

Vidal didn't waste men. That was the thing about him I hated most. He was economical the way a butcher is economical.

"They'll sweep the south face," I whispered. "They know we didn't have time to cross."

"Then we cross," Ines said.

"It's two hundred meters of open ground."

"I've run further than that." Her voice was flat and steady and I understood suddenly that she had already decided to die somewhere on this steppe, probably days ago, and that every hour since was borrowed and she spent them without flinching. "Paloma. They're not here to take the drive. They're here to make sure nothing walks out. You know that. So we don't walk—we run."

* * *

We ran.

Two hundred meters of open scree under rifle fire is not a distance, it's a lifetime. I went first because I was faster and because if I drew the fire, she might live long enough to hate me properly. The ground came apart around my boots in little gray flowers. Somewhere behind me Ines breathed in the rhythm runners use—two steps in, two steps out—and then the rhythm broke.

She didn't scream. That's the detail that stays. A round went through her left shoulder—through, not into, the angle of it later told me, clipping bone on its way out—and she made a sound like someone remembering a bill they'd forgotten, a short exhale, almost annoyed, and she kept running for four more strides before her legs filed their objection.

I caught her by the pack straps and dragged us both behind a fallen slab of basalt the size of a bus, and the world narrowed to blood and breathing and the drive bag, which she still had. Of course she still had it. She'd have traded the arm first.

"How bad?"

"Bad." She said it honestly, which I loved her for and hated her for. Blood sheeted down her forearm and dripped off her fingers onto the stone, each drop landing with its own small finality. "It's through. It's—I can hold the drive. I can't hold a rifle."

"You weren't holding a rifle anyway."

"Then we're perfectly matched."

Above us, the shooting paused. Not stopped—paused. I pressed my ear to the stone and listened past my own pulse, and I heard the thing I'd been dreading: boots on scree, descending. Two sets, maybe three. They weren't going to wait us out. They were coming down to finish it, because Vidal had a schedule and a storm coming and no patience for a siege.

And that was when I did the thing I swore off.

* * *

You need to understand what I mean by swearing off, because it sounds like a vice, and it isn't. It's worse. It's a gift.

My father had it before me, and it killed him the way gifts kill people who can't put them down. He could feel weather in his body—pressure, charge, the static weight of a sky deciding what it wants to be—and on the dry side that makes you a prophet, because everyone else reads the steppe with their eyes, and out here eyes are useless. The dry-side storm doesn't show itself. It tells you. Through the floor of your stomach. Through the ache in old scars. Through the way the air goes glassy and dead an hour before the front arrives, so still that sound carries five kilometers and lies flat along the ground like water finding its level.

I'd used it for money my whole life. Chasing storms for tourists, seeding rain for estancieros, once—only once—leading a convoy of smugglers through a front that buried their pursuers in whiteout and cost two of those pursuers their lives when their trucks rolled in zero visibility. After that I promised myself I'd never again point the weather at people. Weather doesn't take sides. That's the whole religion of it. The moment you make it take yours, you're not reading it anymore—you're gambling, and the house always has better odds.

But Ines was bleeding out by drops against the basalt, and three men with rifles were coming down the slope with all the time in the world, and the storm I'd been feeling since dawn was forty minutes south of us and building like a held breath.

So I gambled.

"Can you move?" I asked her.

"If you define moving generously."

"Then listen. There's a slot canyon, sixty meters east. It climbs to the rim and it narrows to shoulder-width halfway up. If we're in it when the front hits, we live. If they're on open ground when it hits—"

"You're going to steer a storm at them." She looked at me with blood on her teeth where she'd bitten her lip, and there was no horror in her face. Eleven days alone will burn the horror out of a person. What was in her face instead was worse: recognition. "Your father's son," she said quietly. "The old man told me. At the estancia. He said you read weather like scripture and you'd sworn never to preach it."

"He talk too much?"

"He talked about you like you were a saint who'd left the church."

I got her arm over my neck and took her weight, and we started for the slot canyon in the dead, ringing silence that comes before landfall, and every step I took I was listening downward, into the ground, into the pressure sitting on my molars like a thumb.

Thirty minutes. Maybe twenty-five. The front was walking north at the speed of a tired horse, and it was big—I could feel its edges, hundreds of kilometers of dust and grit and static, a brown ocean standing on its hind legs. Out on the flats it would be beautiful. In the canyon it would be a meat grinder of airborne rock.

Twenty minutes would have to be enough.

* * *

The slot canyon was exactly as cruel as I remembered such places being. Sixty meters of open ground first—I carried her, because she'd stopped pretending she could run, and she weighed less than she should have, which frightened me more than the rifles did. Rounds chased us across the scree, kicking up stone, and one of them found the drive bag's strap and tore it half loose and for one full second the drive swung free against her hip, exposed, and I saw one of the shooters on the rim adjust his aim toward it and understood that Vidal had briefed them on the object, not just the people.

Ines got her good hand on the bag and clamped it to her chest and we fell into the slot together as a round sparked off the rim six inches from my ear.

Then we climbed.

She climbed. God help me, she climbed—with a shoulder shot through, using her right arm only, fitting herself into the crack of the canyon like water fits into a fissure, making sounds through her teeth that were not words. I climbed below her so that if she fell she'd fall onto me, and twice she did, briefly, and both times she apologized, which is the kind of thing that breaks your heart at the worst possible moment.

Below and behind us, the boots reached the base of the slot. Voices, clipped and professional. A flashlight beam even though the sun was up—they were checking the depth of the crack, calculating.

And I stopped climbing, pressed my cheek to warm stone, and listened south.

It was close now. Close enough that I could hear it the way you hear a train through a wall—not the storm itself but its approach, a low grinding hush, the sound of a hundred thousand tons of topsoil being lifted and told to travel. The pressure dropped so fast my ears popped. The temperature fell eight degrees in as many minutes, that strange cold breath the fronts push ahead of them, and the light changed—not dimming, but turning the color of old brass, the whole world lit like the inside of a bell.

"Ines," I said. "Faster."

"I'm going as—"

"Faster than that."

She went faster than that.

* * *

The front made landfall at 3:40 in the afternoon, and I know the time because I was watching my father's battered altimeter when the pressure bottomed out, and because some part of me will always be taking readings, even then, especially then.

It came over the rim of the canyon like judgment with no opinions—a wall of dust two hundred meters high, rolling, boiling, swallowing the sun whole. The light died between one heartbeat and the next. The wind arrived not as wind but as a solid thing, a fist of grit and gravel that stripped paint and skin with equal indifference, and the temperature plunged thirty degrees, and the world became a brown roaring nothing in which a man could stand three meters from another man and neither would ever know.

We were in the slot, wedged into a widening pocket halfway up, backs to stone, Ines tucked into my chest with the drive bag between us like a shared organ. Above the rim, the storm howled. Inside the slot, the wind funneled and screamed but the rock held, and we held, and I put my mouth against her ear and shouted the only prayer I know, which is a set of numbers: pressure, interval, duration. Twenty minutes of the worst of it. Forty until visibility returned to killing range.

Somewhere below us, at the mouth of the slot, the shooters' flashlights came on—three weak needles in the brown dark—and then, one after another, went out. Not broken. Buried. Grit packs into every seal and switch and lens in minutes; I've watched electronics die by the dozen in fronts like this. Their rifles would be full of Patagonia by now. Their trucks, parked broadside on the canyon floor, would be sandblasted to bare metal and blind as stones.

And Vidal's men, professionals to the last, would do what professionals do: shelter, wait, and report that the quarry had vanished into weather no sane person enters.

Which meant we had forty minutes of cover and a two-hour head start on anyone digging optics out of their trucks.

"We move on my count," I shouted into Ines's ear. "Down and out the north end, into the storm's wake, where the air's still thick enough to hide us. Can you walk?"

She pulled her head back far enough to look at me. In the brass-colored gloom her face was gray with blood loss and plastered with dust, and she was smiling—actually smiling, a crooked, furious, alive smile.

"Paloma," she said, shouting over the roar, "you steered a storm at them."

"I gambled. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference is I win this one."

She laughed then—a real laugh, cracked and bloody and brief—and pressed the drive bag harder against her ribs, and I took the weight of her again, and we started down into the brown roaring dark with the whole wrecked sky at our backs.

Behind us, faint under the storm, I thought I heard an engine turn over and fail, turn over and fail, a man trying to coax life into a drowned machine. Vidal, or one of his. Still alive. Still counting.

Let him count. By the time the air cleared, we'd be a rumor on the road south, and the drive would be sixty kilometers closer to Comodoro, and the only thing he'd have recovered from Cañadón de los Huesos was a dead truck and a mouthful of Patagonia.

The storm, grudgingly, had taken my side.

I intended to spend that luck before it noticed.
