## 17. The Storm Only He Can Read

The storm had been building since dawn, and I had been listening to it the way other men listen to a debt collector's boots on a stair.

It started as nothing. A brown smear on the southern horizon while Ines slept in the passenger seat with her shoulder bound and her fever climbing, the truck humming north along a track that wasn't on any map worth buying. By mid-morning it was a bruise of color at the edge of vision, too wide to be a cloud and too low to be anything kind. By noon I could feel it in my teeth. That sounds like poetry. It isn't. It's pressure — a change in the weight of the air against your eardrums, a dry electric itch behind the sinuses, a taste like coins and hot stone on the back of the tongue. My father taught me to read it before he taught me to read words. On the dry side of Patagonia, the sky doesn't speak in rain. It speaks in dust, and if you don't learn its language, it will bury you without ever learning your name.

Behind us, Vidal was learning his own lesson about names.

I'd left him an hour ago in a canyon pass full of smoke and echo, three trucks' worth of men shooting at shadows because I had put them there. We'd come out of the ambush on foot, Ines bleeding through my improvised bandage, the drive taped flat against my ribs under my shirt where sweat and fear had glued it to my skin like a second heart. The truck waited two kilometers south where I'd staged it that morning — because you always stage a second vehicle when you're planning to be hunted, and I had been planning this since before Vidal knew he was hunting anyone.

Ines climbed into the cab with her good arm and looked at me with eyes that were fever-bright and absolutely clear.

"You're smiling," she said. "People are trying to kill us and you're smiling."

"I'm not smiling." I checked the mirror, put the truck in gear. "I'm calculating."

"Paloma." She said my name the way she said everything now — stripped down, no decoration, the way people talk when they've decided they might die today and don't have time for furniture in their sentences. "What are we driving into?"

I told her the truth. It felt strange in my mouth, truth, but we'd spent it all in that riverbed two nights ago, every secret laid out on the gravel between us like engine parts, and there was nothing left over for lying.

"There's a front coming up from the southwest," I said. "Dust wall, maybe thirty kilometers wide. Ground wind behind it hitting eighty, ninety kilometers an hour. Visibility inside it: zero. Duration: six hours, maybe eight."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "And we're going to meet it on purpose."

"We're going to meet it *first*," I said. "Vidal's trucks are fast but they're heavy, and he doesn't know this country. He's following our tracks right now because tracks are the only map he has. So I'm going to give him tracks that lead somewhere beautiful."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the steppe: it looks empty and it isn't. It's full of traps for the unwary — arroyos that open like wounds, basalt fields that eat tires, salt pans with a crust thin as communion wafer over mud that swallows a truck to the axles. To an outsider it's a blank. To me it's a page covered in handwriting, and I've been reading it since I was seven years old.

I drove west first, cutting off the northward line, because the storm would come up from the southwest and I wanted to hit its leading edge at an angle, slide along the skirt of it like a man walking the edge of a cliff instead of stepping off. Behind me — I couldn't see him yet, but I could feel him the way you feel someone standing too close in a dark room — Vidal would find the tire marks, read the turn, and make the obvious deduction: the prey is fleeing west, toward the mountains, toward Chile, toward escape.

Good. Let him deduce. Deduction requires information, and information in a dust storm dies faster than a radio battery in cold weather.

Ines watched the horizon with me. The smear had grown. It was no longer a smear; it was architecture. A wall maybe eight hundred meters tall, brown-black at the base shading to a sickly orange where the sun hit it, rolling north across the steppe with the patient hunger of something that had never once needed to hurry. Underneath it, the land ahead of us began to dim, as if a hand were slowly closing over the sun.

"My grandmother came from the dry side," Ines said suddenly. "Near Puerto Deseado. She used to say the storms here don't kill you. They just take away the world and see what you do without it."

"She was right," I said. "That's exactly what they do."

"And what do you do without the world, Paloma?"

I glanced at her. Sweat had pasted her hair to her forehead. Her shoulder was wrong under the jacket — swollen, misshapen — and she'd refused, twice, to let me try to set it until we were clear. Stubborn as a core sample, that woman.

"I navigate," I said. "Without the world, you navigate by what's left. Pressure. Wind direction on your neck. The sound of your own engine — you learn what it sounds like on hard ground versus soft. The stars, if the dust thins high up, which it does sometimes. And memory." I tapped my temple. "This whole piece of country lives in here. Every arroyo, every well, every place the ground lies about being solid. Vidal has satellites and radios and nine men. I have forty years of the desert talking to me."

"That's not nothing," she admitted.

"No," I said. "It's everything. It's the only currency out here the Ferros can't print and Vidal can't buy."

* * *

We hit the edge of the storm at four in the afternoon.

There was no dramatic announcement. There was a change in the light — sudden, total, like a door slamming — and then the wind arrived, not gradually but all at once, slamming the truck sideways with a hand the size of the sky. Sand came off the scrub in sheets. The temperature dropped ten degrees in a minute. And the noise — God, the noise. It wasn't wind anymore. It was the sound of the entire continent being sanded down, a billion particles screaming past the windows, hissing against the paint, finding every gap in the seals and writing fine red lines of grit across everything inside the cab.

Ines didn't flinch. I'll give her that forever. She braced her bad arm against the dash and watched the brown darkness swallow the world behind us and said only: "He'll follow us in."

"He'll follow us in," I agreed. "Because stopping means losing us, and losing us means explaining to the ministry how a dead woman and a debt-ridden chaser walked their evidence out of Patagonia. He has to follow. That's the trap."

"And us? What do we do inside it?"

"We do what the guanacos do." I killed the headlights — they were useless, just two blurry coins of glare bouncing back at us off the dust. "When a storm hits, the herds stop running. They turn their backs to the wind, hunch down, and wait. Running blind kills them. But I'm not going to stop, because stopping is what he expects us to do eventually — hide and pray. So I'm going to drive. Slow, by instruments, by memory, along a route I picked this morning. And he's going to drive fast, by guesswork, along a route he doesn't know. Guesswork in zero visibility, at speed, on ground full of arroyos." I let that sit. "The desert forgives nothing, Ines. But tonight it knows which side I've fed it for forty years."

* * *

For the next hour I drove by touch.

Not physical touch — a stranger kind. The steering wheel told me things. Hardpan rang slightly different under the tires than loose sand; I could hear the difference through the frame, could feel it in the vibration climbing my arms. When the ground fell away into one of the shallow braided channels that lace this country like veins, the air pressure shifted against the doors, a faint suck and release, and I corrected before the wheels found the drop. Twice I stopped entirely, shut the engine, and listened — once to a fence line singing somewhere off our left flank, wire humming in the wind, which meant I was paralleling an estancia boundary I knew, which meant I knew exactly where I was to within half a kilometer.

Ines navigated beside me in her own fashion, calling out compass bearings from the dash unit whenever the needle steadied enough to trust, logging our turns in a waterproof notebook with her left hand, ugly, careful letters. Two liars, two weeks ago. Now she was the instrument panel of my memory made flesh, and neither of us said anything about what that meant, because saying it might break it, and we needed it whole.

"He's stopped," she said at one point, holding up the handheld receiver I'd pulled from the wrecked comms kit. We couldn't transmit — transmitting was dying — but Vidal's men talked among themselves on frequencies anyone with a scanner could drink in, and panic makes men forget discipline. The voices came through shredded by static and wind-roar, but audible:

"—lost the tracks—"

"—can't be more than fifty meters, Ramiro, keep going—"

"—there's a drop, there's a *drop*, back up, BACK UP—"

A pause. Then, distant even through the speaker: metal striking earth, hard, final.

Ines lowered the scanner. Neither of us spoke. Somewhere out in that screaming brown dark, one of Vidal's trucks had found an arroyo the hard way, and the arithmetic of the hunt had just changed.

"They'll regroup," she said finally. "Vidal won't stop."

"No. He'll pull back to the last solid ground, wait for the eye or the tail of the front, and run our line with night optics when visibility opens. Which gives us—" I checked my watch, useless in the dark, did it by the pressure anyway, by the feel of the wind shifting two points on my neck, "—about four hours of cover. Four hours to get to the rail siding at Kilometer 112 and put this drive on a train, a plane, anything moving south."

"And after that?"

"After that," I said, "we deal with whatever's left of Vidal in the daylight. Because he'll still be there. Men like him are always still there."

* * *

We drove through the belly of the storm for six hours.

I want to describe it honestly, because later — when the story got told in bars, badly, by people who hadn't been there — everyone wanted it to be heroic. It wasn't heroic. It was small and grinding and terrifying. It was hands cramped on the wheel at ten to two, eyes burning from dust that got in despite everything, driving at fifteen kilometers an hour through a darkness so complete that my own hood ornament vanished if I leaned forward. It was Ines silently vomiting from pain around kilometer three and then wiping her mouth and saying, "Bearings again?" as if nothing happened. It was the two of us existing for six hours inside a cocoon of engine noise and wind-noise with no world beyond the windshield, no up, no horizon, nothing but each other's profiles lit by the green ghost-glow of the dashboard.

And it was, God help me, the most alive I had felt in years.

Because this — this was the thing I was actually *for*. Not chasing storms for tourists with cameras. Not groveling across desks from cheerful, dangerous men. This: alone inside the largest violence the dry side could produce, reading it, riding it, letting it carry me exactly where I chose to go while it ate everything else. My father's voice in my ear: *The desert doesn't take sides, m'hijo. But it remembers who respects it.* Tonight it remembered. Tonight it took my side, and I loved it for that with a love I had no business feeling and didn't try to suppress.

At some point Ines's hand found mine on the gearstick and stayed there. Bad arm and all. Neither of us looked at the other. Some contracts are signed that way.

* * *

The tail of the front passed us near midnight, and the world came back like a developing photograph — first the stars, shockingly thick overhead, then the pale ghost-line of the southern horizon, then the land itself, scoured, rearranged, new dunes lying across old tracks like fresh snowfall erasing a crime.

Including ours. Including Vidal's. The storm had swept the ledger clean for everyone.

We crested a rise and there it was below us: the rail siding at Kilometer 112, a water tower, a loading ramp, a single sodium lamp burning yellow over empty track, and — parked neatly beside the maintenance shed with its lights off — a black pickup truck with government plates.

Two figures stood beside it. One was tall and motionless in the way that Vidal was motionless, the stillness of a man who never wastes movement. The other held a rifle with the easy, terrible comfort of long practice.

They had beaten us here. Of course they had. Vidal hadn't been chasing us through the storm — he'd been *driving around it* on the paved road east, calm as Sunday, straight to the only place we could possibly deliver the evidence. While I'd been playing the desert like an instrument, he'd simply read the same score and taken the shortcut.

Ines exhaled slowly beside me. "You led us into the storm," she said quietly, "and he drove around it."

"Yeah," I said. Below us, Vidal lifted his face toward our ridge, unhurried, and raised one hand — not a wave, an invitation. "But look at it from his side. He thinks the storm broke us. He thinks we're desperate now." I reached into my shirt and touched the drive taped against my ribs, and felt, beneath it, the shape of the second one — Tomás's casing marker, the decoy I'd prepared from the corrupted files, loaded with garbage wrapped in the right packaging. Everything rides on what happens next. "So let's go down and show him how desperate we are."

The truck rolled forward down the slope, toward the lamp, toward the rifles, toward the man who owned our tomorrow — and somewhere far to the southwest, already gathering over the cordillera, the next front was building, and I could feel it in my teeth like a promise.
