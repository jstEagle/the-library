## 18. What Vidal Offers

Vidal had learned long ago that the desert did not forgive, but it also did not lie. Cities lied. Ministries lied. Men in clean offices with wet handshakes and dry eyes lied professionally, the way other men laid brick. But the steppe only told you what it was: wind, gravel, distance, thirst. It was why he preferred working out here. The landscape kept honest books.

Tonight the books said he had them.

He stood at the rail siding eleven kilometers south of Pampa del Castillo, where a spur of rusted track ran out toward a loading ramp that hadn't seen a freight car in nine years. His three trucks were arranged in a shallow arc behind him, headlights off, engines ticking as they cooled. Ahead, maybe four hundred meters, Paloma Dunmore's Land Cruiser sat angled against the ramp's concrete abutment like an animal that had run itself into a corner and knew it. One headlight still worked. The other had died somewhere on the washboard south of the pass, along with the rear window and, Vidal suspected, a good portion of Dunmore's luck.

Vidal checked his watch out of habit rather than need. 21:40. The barometer on the dash of his truck had been falling all afternoon—slowly, then not slowly—and he'd watched Dunmore watch the western horizon for two days now with the particular attention of a man reading a language nobody else spoke. Vidal respected that. He'd made his living assuming other men were competent, because the ones who weren't died early and taught you nothing.

The woman was another matter. Cabral had taken a round through the left shoulder at the pass and should have been dead or delirious by now. Instead she'd walked—walked—two hundred meters of broken ground under fire, held pressure on her own wound with a knee and her teeth while she reloaded nothing, because she carried no weapon, and then gotten herself back into the truck when any rational person would have lain down and waited to be found. Vidal had reviewed the intercepts from the pass twice. There was something in it he didn't have a word for. Not courage exactly. Courage spent itself. Whatever she had renewed itself.

He took the flashlight, the thermos, and the folder, and he walked out alone into the dark between the trucks and the ramp. This was the part of the work he liked best, though he'd never have admitted it to anyone: the arithmetic of a man's life laid out on a table, all the numbers visible, and the man invited to do the sum himself. Violence was a failure state. Vidal had used it perhaps a dozen times in nineteen years, and every time it had cost more than it bought. Persuasion was cheaper. Persuasion, done properly, let a man keep his dignity and his pulse, and walk away believing he'd chosen.

He stopped thirty meters from the Land Cruiser, well inside rifle range and well outside panic range, and clicked the flashlight twice against his palm so they could see him standing open-handed. Then he set the thermos on the gravel where the light could reach it.

"Señor Dunmore," he called, in the tone a man uses across a fence to a neighbor. "You've been driving since dawn. Your friend is bleeding through that dressing—I can see it from here, even if you can't. I brought coffee. Real coffee, not that chicory you bought at Bajo Caracoles."

Silence. Wind moved grit along the rails with a sound like slow breathing.

"I'm going to talk for a few minutes," Vidal continued. "You don't have to answer. You don't even have to listen. But I'd ask you to keep one fact in mind while I do it." He paused, letting the night carry his voice. "I know about the storm."

That landed. He heard it land—the small shift of weight inside the cab, the creak of a seat.

"You felt it building this morning before my meteorologist did. You turned south twenty minutes before the front shifted, which is the only reason you're alive right now, because my drivers drove straight into the edge of it and lost forty minutes digging a Hilux out of a sand drift. So we understand each other about who reads weather and who doesn't." A beat. "Which means you also understand that the front coming down off the cordillera tonight is going to reach this siding around midnight, and that when it does, visibility will be zero, engines will choke, and men who try to move in it die stupid deaths. You can't outrun it. You know better than anyone there's nowhere to run *to*."

He opened the folder now, though the pages were mostly theater—he'd memorized everything in it weeks ago.

"Here is what I know, and I'll say it once. You owe money to the Ferro brothers. A great deal of money, and the terms were recently made worse for you, which I imagine stung. Your sister holds a lease on a property in Comodoro whose renewal depends on people who depend on the Ferros. Two days ago the Ferros received a satellite call offering them a way out of their exposure, and they took it, because the Ferros are many things but they are not sentimental. They subcontracted me. My instructions were originally to make sure nothing came back from the Río Deseado survey. Those instructions changed yesterday. Now they are simpler." He closed the folder. "Bring back the drive and the doctor. Everything else is forgiven. The debt, the lease, the interest, the threats—all of it, erased by morning. Not reduced. Erased."

He let that sit. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered without conviction, a dry storm clearing its throat.

"And here is what I think you already know, Señor Dunmore, because you strike me as a man who does his sums. That drive is corrupted. Partially. Whether by accident or design, I don't know—but the ministry's own analysts will tell you within a day whether what's on it is worth the paper it would burn. What is *not* corrupted, what is whole and verified and worth a great deal to certain people, is the woman. Dr. Inés Cabral is the only living person who can authenticate that data, reproduce it, testify to it. She is the entire value of this expedition. The drive is a souvenir. The woman is the asset."

The wind picked up, tugging at his jacket, carrying the first mineral smell of the approaching front. Vidal breathed it in like a sommelier.

"So I am going to offer you the only honest deal you will get on this steppe, from the only man involved in this business who has never once lied to a counterpart. Walk out here. Drink my coffee. Hand over the drive and Dr. Cabral. By sunrise your sister's lease is renewed for ten years, your debt is a story you tell at bars, and you go back to chasing storms for tourists with clean hands and empty ledgers." He tilted his head. "Or you stay in that truck until midnight, and then the storm comes, and I stop needing to persuade anyone, and the Ferros find out what their money bought anyway. I would prefer the first thing. I suspect you would too."

He set the folder on top of the thermos, stepped back, and stood waiting in the dark with his hands loose at his sides, a patient man in patient country, listening to the storm come.

* * *

Paloma watched the man through the cracked windshield and thought: *he's good.*

Not good the way the Ferros were good—all charm and hidden teeth. Good the way a surgeon was good. Vidal had walked out alone, unarmed-looking, into the killing zone of his own rifles, and offered coffee. He'd named the debt, the sister, the lease, the deadline, the storm. Every card face-up. And that was the trick, Paloma realized. When a liar shows you his whole hand, you stop looking for the deck.

Beside him, Inés sat propped against the door with her shoulder bound in a shirt and tape, gray-faced, fever-bright. She'd heard every word. Her good hand rested on her thigh, and under it, wrapped in a wool sock inside a canvas bag, lay the surviving drive—the real one, the one Tomás had sealed in the second casing, the one Vidal's analysts would weep over. The decoy—the corrupted drive, the one they'd let themselves "discover" first—was in the glovebox, warm from the heater vent, wearing its little costume of importance.

"He's right about the storm," Inés said quietly. Her voice was rougher than it had been that morning. "It'll hit before midnight. I grew up in Neuquén; I know what a dry front sounds like when it's still two hours out." She looked at him. "And he's right about me. I'm the asset. If you give him the drive and keep me, you lose nothing that matters."

"Don't," Paloma said.

"It's arithmetic. You taught me arithmetic. Out here it's the only honest subject."

"Out here," Paloma said, "the only honest subject is weather, and the weather says we've got maybe two hours to decide what kind of liars we're going to be." He wiped the windshield with his sleeve, clearing a porthole of grit. "Here's what he didn't tell you. The Ferros don't erase debts. They *transfer* them. Ten years from now I'd be doing salvage runs for whatever ministry replaces this one, hauling whatever evidence they want buried, and my sister's lease would still be a leash. Vidal knows that. He's counting on me being too tired and too scared to do the math past the sunrise he's selling."

"And if you're wrong? If he's actually honest?"

"Then I'm a fool who dies free instead of a coward who lives leased." He almost smiled. "My father had a saying about rail sidings. He said they're where the country goes to change its mind."

Inés studied him for a long moment. Fever had burned away most of her pretenses over the last two days; what was left was direct and unadorned and, he was beginning to understand, far more dangerous than anything Vidal had brought to the steppe.

"The recording," she said. "If this goes wrong—if we don't walk away from this siding—you get the drive to Comodoro yourself. Not the ministry. The journalist. Promise me again."

"On Tomás's name."

She nodded once, satisfied, and slid down low in the seat, and Paloma understood she wasn't hiding. She was getting ready.

He got out of the truck.

The wind met him immediately, warmer than it should have been, tasting of dust and iron—that specific electric flatness that meant the front was closer than comfortable. Forty meters out, Vidal waited beside the thermos, and up close the man was unremarkable: mid-fifties, neat, the face of an accountant who'd been very good at his job for a very long time.

"Señor Dunmore." Vidal poured coffee into the thermos cup and held it out. "You look terrible."

"I feel wonderful. First time in months nobody's asked me for money." Paloma didn't take the cup. "Let's skip to the end. Drive and doctor, debt gone, sister safe. That's the offer."

"That's the offer."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then in ten minutes my men secure the doctor, we radio the Ferros, and you drive south with a full tank and a receipt." Vidal's eyes didn't move. "You're wondering if I'll shoot you anyway. Don't. Dead contractors generate paperwork, and I despise paperwork. My reputation is the only collateral I have in this trade, and I've kept it for nineteen years. Ask anyone I've ever dealt with. The ones who are alive to ask, that is."

Paloma laughed—a short, ugly sound the wind took away. "There it is. That's the part everyone forgets to price in. You're not buying the drive. You're buying *silence*, and silence has a maintenance fee. It comes due every year, forever. My sister's lease gets renewed by people who now own a piece of me. The debt disappears and gets replaced by something worse, because at least the Ferros only wanted money." He shook his head slowly. "You're offering me everything I wanted, Vidal. That's how I know it's poison. Nobody hands you what you actually want unless what they're really buying is what you'd never sell."

Something flickered behind Vidal's eyes—not anger. Recalculation. Paloma had watched storms recalculate too, right before they committed.

"You're a romantic," Vidal said. "That's a luxury. Romanticism is the last thing a man in debt affords."

"No. It's the first thing he's got left." Paloma glanced west, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and felt the pressure drop against his eardrums like a hand closing gently around his skull. The front was early. Of course it was. Storms on the dry side lied about their size, and this one had been lying since dawn. "Last question. Honest answer, and I'll consider the deal. The survey team. The kettles, the radios, the camp abandoned mid-meal. Was that your crew?"

For the first time, Vidal hesitated. It was a small hesitation, professional and brief, but Paloma had spent his whole life reading things nobody else could read, and he read it perfectly.

"The original plan was relocation," Vidal said finally. "It became something else. I wasn't there. I was told it was handled." He paused. "Does the answer change your arithmetic?"

"No," Paloma said. "But it changes yours." He turned and started walking back toward the truck, and behind him Vidal's voice followed, still level, still reasonable:

"Midnight, Señor Dunmore. After that, I stop negotiating and start operating."

Paloma climbed in. Inés was already watching him, the bag with the drive cradled against her wounded side, and outside, far to the west, the first wall of the storm lifted off the earth like something raising its head.

"He offered you everything," she said.

"Yeah." Paloma started the engine. The one good headlight carved a yellow tunnel through the gathering dark. "Now watch me spend it."
