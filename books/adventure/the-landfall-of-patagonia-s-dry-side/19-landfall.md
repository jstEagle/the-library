## 19. Landfall

The rail siding had a name on no map that mattered: Kilómetro 147, a water tower gone rust-red, a loading ramp of creosoted timber split by forty years of frost, and a single line of track running north toward Comodoro like a stitch through the hide of the world. Vidal's men had chosen it because it was the last place a road crossed anything, and because there was nowhere to run except along the rails, and everyone knew you couldn't outrun trucks on foot.

Everyone but me.

I stood in the lee of the water tower with my hands visible and my heart going like a drill bit, and I watched the storm come.

It was still an hour out—maybe less, maybe more; storms on the dry side lie about their size, old Aurelio had told us, and this one had been lying since dawn. From the south it looked like nothing, a brown smudge on the horizon, a smudge of dirt kicked up by cattle. But I'd been reading it all day, the way other men read faces. The pressure had been falling in stair-steps since morning, each drop a little steeper than the last, and the wind had swung from the west to the northwest and gone strangely quiet, the way air goes quiet when it's being sucked into something bigger. Above the smudge, the sky was the color of a bruise going yellow. Birds had stopped flying. Even the guanacos had vanished off the flats, and guanacos don't vanish for anything less than weather or death.

Vidal stood ten meters away with his hands in his pockets, a compact man in a dust-colored jacket, patient as arithmetic. Two of his trucks idled behind him, headlights burning uselessly against the growing murk. A third man sat on the loading ramp with a rifle across his knees, not aiming it anywhere in particular, which was its own kind of message.

"You understand," Vidal said, "that this is the only conversation we're having."

"I understand."

"The drive. The woman walks to my truck. You walk to your truck. By Thursday you're a man with no debts." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man doing sums. "The Ferros are generous when they get what they paid for. And your sister keeps her lease. Her little shop in Caleta Olivia stays hers. All of it, Paloma. Everything you've been bleeding for, handed back."

Everything I'd been bleeding for. I let myself look at it, honestly, the way you have to if you're going to refuse it properly: the weight gone off my chest, the phone calls that made my stomach drop answered cheerfully instead of dreadfully, Marta's face when I told her she was safe. It was all standing right there in the dust, priced at one woman and one drive full of dead men's truth.

Ines stood beside me, shoulder bound under a jacket sleeve stiff with dried blood, her face gray with pain and upright anyway. She hadn't sat down since the pass. She wouldn't sit down now. She looked at Vidal the way you look at a snake you've already been bitten by once—no fear left, just inventory.

"Say it again," I said to Vidal. "The part about who ordered it. Who told you what to make sure never came back."

Vidal's eyes flickered. Just a flicker, just a hairline crack in the patience. "That's not relevant to our arrangement."

"It is to me. I want to hear whose voice gave the order. I want to know how far up it goes before I hand over the only copy of—" I patted my jacket pocket, where the drive rode in a padded pouch, heavy as a sin. "Before I hand over the only thing keeping me honest."

Something moved behind Vidal's eyes—the small recalculation of a man deciding whether a question is insolence or procedure. He decided, apparently, that a man about to sell everything deserved one answer. That was his mistake. Mine would have been believing he'd keep his word after.

"The ministry," he said. "Undersecretary's office. A man named Arrieta signs the authorizations. The Ferros were told to find a cleanup contractor, and the Ferros found me, and I don't ask Arrieta why any more than you asked the Ferros why they wanted a bloodhound." He spread his hands. "There. Now we're all equally dirty. Give me the drive."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about storms in twenty years of chasing them across the ugliest country on earth: they always arrive earlier than they look like they will, and they always arrive from the direction you weren't watching.

The decoy drive had cost me half the night and most of my remaining faith in my own cleverness. Ines had a second device—the surviving recorder, the one Tomás had rigged into a drill casing with a backup of everything, the one she'd carried against her skin for eleven days while she buried herself alive in a guanaco pit and breathed through a reed and listened to men walk around looking for her body. On it, besides the aquifer data, was something she'd started recording the night at Bajo Caracoles and never told me about until this morning, when she pressed it into my hand and said, "If I die, it still needs to reach Comodoro. If I live, I want to be holding it when the moment comes."

The moment, she meant, when someone said out loud what had only ever been implied.

So the drive in my pocket was a fake—a cheap consumer unit I'd bought at the fuel stop in Tres Cerros, wiped and re-labeled with tape and a marker, weighted with a drill bit so it felt right in the hand. The real recorder rode flat against Ines's ribs under her shirt, already recording, its little red light hidden by cloth. She'd switched it on when we walked into the siding. Everything Vidal said now was going onto it forever.

All I had to do was keep him talking until the storm arrived, and then survive the arrival.

"You'll forgive me," I said, taking the pouch out slowly, letting it catch the dying light, "if I count the money first."

"There's no money here."

"There's no drive here either, until there's money. Or at least a call. I want to hear the Ferros say it. Debt cleared. Lease transferred to Marta's name. Today, on the phone, while you watch."

Vidal exhaled through his nose. Behind him, the man on the ramp shifted the rifle. The wind had begun to move again—not blowing so much as leaning, a long steady push from the south-southwest that smelled of earth torn loose two hundred kilometers away. I could taste it. Iron and dust and the particular dry sweetness of a front that has been gathering all day over country with nothing to slow it down.

"A call takes time," Vidal said.

"Not much. My satellite phone works fine." I nodded at my truck, parked fifty meters off, angled toward the north with the engine cold. "One call. Then the drive changes hands, the woman gets in your truck, and everybody lives happily ever after in whatever way people like us live happily ever after."

He considered it. I watched him consider it, and I understood that he was going to say yes, and that saying yes was itself the trap—he had no intention of letting either of us walk anywhere. The moment the drive touched his hand, the arithmetic changed. Dead men tell no tales and neither do live ones who've seen too much. He'd shoot us both at the water tower and report that the salvage contractor and the survivor had killed each other over the evidence, a tidy tragedy, case closed, aquifer safe, Arrieta pleased, Ferros paid.

But he needed the drive first. And I needed the storm.

Over his shoulder, the southern horizon had changed character. The smudge had become a wall—not tall, maybe eight hundred meters, but wide, wide as the whole sky, and moving with the terrible unhurried speed of something that does not need to hurry because it cannot be escaped. The light had gone the color of weak tea. The first advance gust hit us, warm and gritty, carrying a smell like a struck match, static crawling on every metal surface. The man on the ramp stood up, frowning south, and even Vidal turned his head a quarter-inch.

"Now?" Ines said quietly, in Quechua-inflected Spanish only I could hear.

"Not yet." Because the recorder was still working, and Vidal was still talking, and every second he talked was another sentence of confession. "Tell me about the pass," I said loudly. "At the canyon. Your shooter put a round through her shoulder. Was that the authorization too? Kill the survey team, kill the witness, or just wound them and let the desert finish it?"

"Paloma." Vidal's voice dropped into something almost gentle. "You're negotiating with the wrong end of this. Take the deal."

"The deal requires a phone call."

"The deal requires nothing. The deal is me being kind." He took his hands out of his pockets. "Give me the drive, and I give you your life, which is more than Arrieta authorized me to give anyone."

There it was. Out loud, on the record, in his own voice: *Arrieta authorized*. Ines's breath caught beside me—I heard it, small and fierce—and her hand found the recorder through her shirt, pressing it flat, as if she could hold the sound inside it by will alone.

"And the survey team?" I said. "Eleven days ago. Eight people. Whose authorization was that?"

Vidal looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him decide that I was a dead man anyway, and that dead men can be told anything. "Same signature. Different memo. They drilled where they shouldn't have, they read what they shouldn't have, and their team leader was smart enough to sell them out—which is why seven of them are in a pit and Dr. Cabral was supposed to be with them." He shrugged. "She was supposed to be in the pit, Paloma. Instead she's standing here bleeding on my schedule. So yes. Same authorization. Are you satisfied?"

The wall of dust swallowed the horizon.

It came faster than it looked—that was the trick of it, the oldest lie the dry side tells. One breath it was distant thunder made of earth; the next the leading edge was on us, a rolling brown surf three hundred meters high, and the light died between one heartbeat and the next, noon becoming a filthy twilight, the sun a pale coin drowning in grit. The temperature fell ten degrees in a minute. The wind hit like a door slammed by God, and the world became noise—sand screaming against steel, the water tower humming a low organ note, the trucks' headlights reduced to two dim coins floating in brown chaos.

I had planned for exactly ninety seconds of this. Ninety seconds of blindness in which a man with a rifle cannot see past his own muzzle flash, in which trucks cannot pursue what they cannot see, in which the whole world is erased and redrawn and the only person on the steppe who knows how to navigate inside the belly of the beast is the man who spent his whole life learning to read it from the outside.

"Drive!" I shouted—or screamed it, because shouting was useless; I put the pouch into Vidal's reaching hand myself, pressed it there, felt his fingers close on it, and in the same motion shoved him hard into the path of his own men's confusion and turned and grabbed Ines and ran.

We ran north along the rails, into the teeth of it.

The sand hit like flaying. I kept one arm around Ines, one hand on the rail itself, feeling for the joints—every rail joint clicks under your palm at the same interval, and if you count them you know your speed, and if you know your speed you know where you are even when the world has been deleted. Twenty-two joints per hundred meters. I counted. Ines ran bent double beside me, wounded shoulder forward, making sounds she didn't let become screams, and I loved her in that moment with a love that had no tenderness in it at all, only the load-bearing kind, the kind that holds a roof up.

Behind us, muzzle flashes bloomed and died in the murk, aimed at nothing, aimed at ghosts. Someone was shouting into a radio that static had eaten. A truck engine revved, lurched, and I heard the unmistakable crunch of steel finding the loading ramp at speed—Vidal's own blind convoy wrecking itself on the furniture of the world. The storm didn't care. The storm took sides for no one; it simply erased the difference between hunter and hunted, and tonight, for once, it erased more of them than it erased of us.

Two hundred meters. Three hundred. My lungs filled with grit; I tasted blood in my throat. Ines stumbled and I hauled her up by the good shoulder and we kept counting joints, twenty-two at a time, the rails singing under the wind like a plucked wire running to the horizon.

Then the truck appeared.

Out of the brown, broadside, sliding—one of Vidal's, driver blind, tires finding the soft shoulder beside the rails, slewing toward us with its headlights swinging like searchlights. I saw it a half-second before the driver did, because I'd been reading the ground under the storm the whole time, feeling where the gravel firmed up and where it went to powder, and I knew the truck would break left at the culvert marker the way water breaks at a stone.

"Ines! Down!"

We flattened into the drainage ditch as the truck fishtailed past close enough to slap us with its slipstream, rolled once, fantastically, and settled on its roof thirty meters on, wheels turning, wipers sweeping uselessly across a windshield full of nothing. The driver crawled out coughing, rifle lost, eyes streaming, a boy of maybe twenty-five who looked at me through the murk with pure animal terror and made the smartest decision of his young life: he turned and walked south, back into the storm, away from all of us.

I let him go. There had been enough pits on this steppe.

* * *

The truck was Vidal's command vehicle. I knew it by the antenna array and the satellite terminal bolted behind the cab seats, and I knew what that meant: the Ferros' channel, open, waiting. Ines slumped against the overturned chassis, gray-faced, shaking, laughing a little in the way people laugh when the alternative is worse, and I climbed in through the shattered rear window and found the handset.

For a moment I held it and didn't speak. Through the sand-scoured glass I could see almost nothing—just the brown roar, and somewhere in it, faintly, the flare of torchlight where Vidal's remaining men had lit the water tower's base, trying to make an island of visibility in the erasure. Vidal was alive. Vidal had my decoy drive in his pocket and my voice in his memory promising a phone call that was never coming, and within an hour he would plug that drive into a reader and find a fuel-stop consumer unit full of nothing, and then he would be very motivated indeed.

Which was exactly the point. Let him discover the forgery. Let him radio the Ferros in a rage. Let every word of it travel down a channel I was about to be listening to from the other end—because Ines had done something in the night at Bajo Caracoles that I hadn't known about until this morning: she'd copied the recorder's configuration onto a spare handheld, and the spare handheld was in my pocket, and it could receive as well as send.

They would talk. Men like Vidal and men like the Ferros always talk when a plan collapses—they talk to assign blame, and blame is just confession wearing a work uniform. Everything Arrieta signed. Everything the Ferros brokered. Everything Vidal admitted at the water tower, already sealed on the recorder against Ines's ribs. By tomorrow there would be copies in three places, and by Thursday—

Thursday. The word landed in me like a hook. Thursday was when the Ferros expected delivery. Thursday was when Marta's lease came due. I thought of her shop in Caleta Olivia, the smell of paint thinner and coffee, her laugh through the wall. What I was about to do would either save her or hang us both, and there was no third outcome, and the storm outside didn't care which.

I keyed the handset and spoke in my steadiest voice, the voice of a man closing a deal.

"Ferro brothers, this is Paloma Dunmore, Kilómetro 147. I have the drive. I have the doctor. Vidal has a fake and doesn't know it yet, and his convoy is in a ditch. Come collect what you paid for—and bring the debt papers, because I'm only signing once."

Static. Then, faint and cheerful as Sunday, Nicolás Ferro's voice: *"Well, well. We always said you were worth the investment, Paloma."*

Beside the truck, half-blinded, shot through the shoulder, holding a dead man's recorder against her ribs, Ines Cabral began to laugh.
