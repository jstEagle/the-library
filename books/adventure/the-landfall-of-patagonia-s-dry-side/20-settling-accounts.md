## 20. Settling Accounts

The Ferro brothers kept their office above a funeral home on Calle Rivadavia, which Paloma had always taken as either a joke or a warning. He'd climbed those stairs a dozen times over three years—once to borrow, twice to renegotiate, the rest of the times to be smiled at while the numbers grew like mold in a damp wall. He knew the smell of the place: old paper, mate herb, and beneath it something faintly chemical, like money that had been laundered too many times.

Today he climbed the stairs with his hands empty and his heart going like a drill motor.

He'd slept four hours in a hostel near the bus terminal, showered off the steppe, and put on the one clean shirt he owned—the blue one, the one Marta said made him look honest. He'd considered the irony of that for a long time in front of the mirror. Then he'd buttoned it to the throat and gone out into the gray morning light of Comodoro Rivadavia, where the wind came off the gulf smelling of salt and diesel, and where somewhere across town Ines Cabral lay in a hospital bed with a hole through her shoulder and a copy of everything on a drive taped under the mattress of a woman she'd never met.

The insurance was the point. The insurance was everything.

* * *

Nicolás Ferro opened the door himself, which was unusual. Nicolás did doors the way he did everything—by delegation—and so the fact of him standing there in shirtsleeves, sleeves actually rolled, told Paloma the brothers had been waiting and had been nervous about it. Behind him, the office looked the same as always: two desks facing each other like opposing counsel, a map of Chubut pinned to the wall with darts instead of pins, a window overlooking the funeral home's hearse bay where two black vehicles sat gleaming in the rain.

"Palomita." Nicolás's smile was the old smile, warm as a stove. "You look terrible."

"I've been camping."

"So we heard." Nicolás stepped aside to let him in. "Sit. Emilio's making coffee. Real coffee, not the office swill. You've earned real coffee, I think."

Emilio Ferro stood at the sideboard with his back half-turned, pouring from a steel thermos into three cups. He was the older brother by six years and the quieter one by a factor of ten, and everyone in Comodoro who dealt with the Ferros understood that you negotiated with Nicolás but you survived by watching Emilio. He set the cups down without ceremony and took his seat behind the far desk, folding his hands, and Paloma sat in the client's chair between them and felt, for the first time in three years, what it was like to sit in this room without wanting anything.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference. Every other time he'd come up these stairs he'd been a man who wanted something—time, patience, another week, another extension. Today he wanted nothing they had. It changed the geometry of the room. He could feel it change, could feel both brothers feel it.

"You're back early," Emilio said. "We didn't expect you until Friday."

"The weather turned fair." Paloma wrapped his hands around the coffee cup. It was good coffee. Of course it was; the Ferros never drank anything cheap except their clients' time. "And I finished the job."

Nicolás laughed, delighted, settling onto the corner of his own desk the way he did when the news was good. "The salvage? Already? And here we thought—" He caught himself, waved a hand. "Doesn't matter what we thought. You have the drive? The government piece?"

"I have it."

"And the woman?"

"She's alive. She's in Comodoro. She'll stay there."

Something moved behind Nicolás's eyes—not disappointment exactly, more the small recalibration of a man whose script has gone off its page. "Our contact was specific about the woman, Palomita. Specific is a word we take seriously."

"I know." Paloma drank his coffee. Let the silence sit. It was a trick he'd learned from storms: the pause before landfall, when the pressure drops and every animal in the country goes quiet and even the wind seems to hold its breath. Men like the Ferros hated silence because silence meant someone else was controlling the clock. "Before we talk about delivery, I want to show you something. Both of you. It'll take five minutes, and then we can settle accounts however you like."

Emilio hadn't moved. His hands were still folded, his eyes still on Paloma, steady as a man reading a gauge. "Show us," he said.

Paloma took out his phone, laid it flat on the desk between them, and pressed play.

* * *

He'd cut the footage himself at four in the morning, sitting in the hostel's common room with a laptop borrowed from a backpacking German and an editing app he barely understood. It wasn't elegant. It didn't need to be.

The video showed Vidal—the cleanup chief, the man with the methodical voice and the truck-mounted jammer—sitting in the cab of a pickup at the rail siding outside Sarmiento, forty minutes after the storm had eaten his convoy. Rain still sheeted down the windshield. Vidal's nose was broken and crusted, his shirt torn at the collar, and he was talking because Paloma had asked him questions and Vidal was a man who believed, even ruined, that information was currency and should be spent rather than lost.

"—the ministry contact doesn't use us directly, obviously," Vidal was saying, voice thick through the damaged nose. "He uses the Ferros. The Ferros are deniable. Petty lenders, muscle for hire, no government fingerprints anywhere. When the survey team needed to disappear, the order went ministry to Ferro to me, and if it ever surfaced, the Ferros eat it alone. That was always the arrangement. That's why they got paid so well and why they'll never see the inside of the file." A wet laugh. "Your creditors aren't your friends, storm-chaser. You're the padding between them and the bullet."

Paloma stopped the video there. He let the screen go dark and looked up.

Neither brother had spoken. Nicolás had come off the corner of the desk at some point during the playback and was standing now, very still, looking at the dead phone the way a man looks at a snake that has appeared in a room he thought was sealed. Emilio hadn't moved at all, but his folded hands had tightened, knuckles going pale, and that was worse.

"Where did you get this," Nicolás said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"He talked. After the storm. People do, when they think they're dying." Paloma set the phone down. "Here's the rest of it, and then I'll get to the part you actually care about. Your ministry contact—Ministerio de Obras Públicas, hydrology division, a man named Sosa—signed off on burying the aquifer data two years ago. The Río Deseado basin is poisoned. Mining runoff, arsenic, heavy metals, moving toward three towns' worth of wells. They knew. They commissioned a survey to confirm it, and when the survey confirmed it, they erased the survey. Four people. One of them buried in a shallow grave holding a marker casing in his hands. Your contract paid for that, gentlemen. Maybe you didn't know what the job was when you took it. But you know now."

"We didn't know," Nicolás said quickly, and then stopped himself, because the speed of it had sounded like guilt, and guilt was a currency the Ferros did not trade in. He straightened his cuffs. Started again, slower. "We broker work. We don't audit it. If a client lies to us—"

"Then you've been lied to," Paloma agreed pleasantly. "Which is exactly what I'm offering you the chance to prove."

Emilio spoke for the first time since the video ended. His voice was quiet and completely level, the way the air is level before a dry storm fronts up. "Offering."

"There's a copy of everything," Paloma said. "Vidal's confession, the ministry sign-off memo, the core sample readings, photographs of the drill casings, Tomás Reyes's name and the names of the other three. It's already out of my hands. If anything happens to me—if I'm arrested, if I disappear, if I fall down your stairs and break my neck—a journalist in this city gets all of it tomorrow morning, along with the Ferro brothers' name in the first paragraph, spelled correctly, because I checked the spelling twice. Sosa used you as deniable muscle. This makes sure you're not deniable anymore. You're just visible."

The room was silent. Out the window, the hearse bay glittered. Somewhere below, the funeral home's door chimed for a customer, absurdly domestic.

"You understand what you're doing," Emilio said. "You're threatening us."

"No." Paloma shook his head. "I'm saving you. There's a difference, and it matters, so listen. Right now, today, you are the men who arranged the murder of a government survey team on behalf of a ministry official who will hang you out to dry the moment this surfaces—which it will surface, brothers, because the story breaks this week whether you like it or not. Ines Cabral is alive. She's talking. The aquifer story is coming out, and when it comes out, Sosa burns, and the first thing a burning man does is name everyone who touched the fire. You want to be a name in his mouth? Or do you want to walk into the prosecutor's office before the story breaks, hand over your records of the Sosa contract, and be the cooperative witnesses? The men who were deceived? The ones who helped bring it down?"

Nicolás had gone very pale under the tan. He looked at his brother, and Paloma watched the whole negotiation happen in the space between them without a single word—sixty seconds of silence in which three years of interest rates and threats and smiling dread were weighed against a paragraph in a newspaper, and found lighter than paper.

"You'd burn us," Nicolás said finally, and there was something almost wondering in it. "Three years. We advanced you money when no bank would look at you. We were patient. We were *kind*, Palomita."

"You were patient the way a spider is patient," Paloma said. "And I paid interest the whole time. I'm not burning you. I'm handing you a fire extinguisher. There's a difference, and it's the same difference as the one between a threat and a rescue, and you're smart enough to see it even if you hate seeing it."

Emilio unfolded his hands. He reached into his desk drawer—Paloma's pulse jumped, some animal part of him certain it was a gun—but what came out was a ledger. Old-fashioned, cloth-bound, the spine cracked white. Emilio set it on the desk between them and opened it to a page near the middle, and there was Paloma's name in faded ink, and column after column of numbers, three years of them, each one a week he'd spent afraid.

"What do you want," Emilio said. Flat. Not angry. A man asking for terms because the alternative was war, and Emilio Ferro had been in enough wars to know the arithmetic of them.

"The debt dies. All of it. Today, in writing, signed by both of you, witnessed." Paloma nodded at the ledger. "That page comes out. You keep the page—I'm not stupid, I know you keep copies of everything—but you mark it settled, and you never call me again, and you never go near my sister's lease in Puerto Madryn. The lease transfers clean to her name, unencumbered, or I make one phone call and the journalist gets a second package with your brokerage history in it. Every job you've run for Sosa. Dates, amounts, routes. I know you keep records, Emilio. Men like you always keep records. It's the only reason men like you survive."

A long moment. Outside, the wind had come around to the south and was pushing rain against the glass in long silver strokes, and Paloma listened to it the way other men listen to music.

"And the woman?" Nicolás asked. "She just walks away?"

"She was never yours to walk anywhere. And neither was I, it turns out." Paloma stood. He left the phone on the desk, deliberately, face-up. "Keep that. Watch it as many times as you like. Show it to your lawyer—he'll tell you I'm right, and then he'll bill you for the hour and you'll deserve that too. I'll come back Thursday for the paperwork and the release on the lease. Have it ready. And brothers—" he paused at the door, hand on the frame, and gave them the smile they'd given him a hundred times, warm as a stove and meaning nothing kind, "—thanks for the coffee. It really was excellent."

* * *

The stairs took longer going down. His legs had decided, now that it was over, to inform him how afraid he'd been, and he gripped the rail and took each step like a man descending from altitude. At the bottom, in the funeral home's entryway, among the plastic ferns and the notice board of the recently departed, he stopped and breathed until his hands stopped shaking.

It was done. Or it would be done Thursday, ink on paper, and until then he was walking on ice that held. He'd spent three years being prey—prey to the Ferros, prey to the fixer's pencil-written contract, prey to the steppe itself—and the strange thing, the thing he'd carry out of that building and into whatever came next, was how simple it had been once he stopped flinching. The debt had never really been money. Money was just how it was measured. The debt had been fear, compounding weekly, and fear died the moment he'd walked in carrying something they needed more than they needed him ruined.

His phone buzzed. A number he didn't recognize, Comodoro prefix. He answered.

"It's me." Ines's voice was rougher than the steppe had left it, hospital-thin, but underneath the roughness there was something new. Something that sounded almost like lightness. "The journalist's name is Beatriz Ocampo. She's been calling me all morning. Paloma—the story's moving. There's a deputy minister resigning already, they're saying it on the radio, they're using Tomás's name, his *name*, on the news." A breath, unsteady. "Where are you?"

"Under a plastic fern," Paloma said, "on Calle Rivadavia. Being free."

A pause. Then, quietly, the way people say things that matter most when they're too tired to dress them up: "Come to the hospital. I want to see your face when you tell me it actually worked."

He pushed through the door into the rain-washed street. The wind hit him full in the chest, off the gulf, cold and enormous and clean, and far to the southwest, over the water, the sky had begun to build the tall gray towers of a real storm—the kind he used to chase for money, the kind that ate trucks and buried tracks and forgave nothing.

He stood in the middle of the sidewalk and watched it come, and for the first time in his adult life, he wasn't running toward it or away from it.

He was just a man in a blue shirt, standing in the weather, going to see a woman who wouldn't be dead.
