## 22. Weather Coming Fair

The estancia at Bajo Caracoles looked smaller in spring, the way old things do when you come back to them honest. The windmill still turned with its broken rhythm, one blade catching, two missing, and the fence wire still sang its single note against the dry air. But the light was different now—October light, thin and clean, the kind that made even the guanaco bones on the flats look like they'd been arranged on purpose.

Paloma Dunmore stood beside his truck at the end of the track and let himself feel it: the weight of arriving somewhere without a lie in his pocket.

It had been seven months since Comodoro Rivadavia. Seven months since the story broke like a dam—the aquifer, the buried survey, the ministry signatures that read like confessions written in advance. Seven months since Vidal's voice, recorded through a dust storm thick enough to chew, admitted on tape that he'd been ordered to make sure nothing came back from the steppe. Seven months since he'd walked into the Ferro brothers' office with a copy of everything and watched two cheerful, patient, dangerous men realize their government contact had used them as deniable muscle—and decide, with the same charm they'd used to ruin him, that Paloma Dunmore was more useful as a free man than a dead one.

The debt was gone. So was the money. Both had left him around the same time, which told him something about how the world kept its books.

He was broke again. Properly broke—not the desperate kind, where men did arithmetic about their sister's lease at three in the morning, but the ordinary kind, where you counted fuel in jerry cans and ate what the land offered. His clients had dried up after the scandal; half the storm-chasing trade wanted nothing to do with the man who'd burned a government contract on national television. The other half wanted him precisely because of it, but they were journalists and documentarians, and journalists paid in exposure, and exposure didn't buy diesel.

So he'd driven north slowly, fixing fences for ranchers who remembered his father, chasing one small storm in December just to remember how it felt when no one was paying him to stand in its path. And then Ines had called—or rather, a radio operator in Perito Moreno had relayed a message, because Ines Cabral did nothing so traceable as calling directly—and the message said only: *Bajo Caracoles. When the rains come. Bring nothing you owe anyone.*

He'd laughed at that alone in the cab. Bring nothing you owe anyone. As if he owned anything at all.

The door of the estancia house opened before he reached it, and old Fermín Sosa came out onto the porch leaning on a stick he didn't need, because Fermín had never needed a stick in his life and never would; the stick was theater, and Fermín had always understood theater better than any man Paloma had known.

"Look at this," Fermín said. "The famous storm reader. Come all this way and he parks his truck fifty meters out like a man visiting a grave."

"It's polite distance," Paloma said.

"It's cowardice with manners." Fermín embraced him anyway, hard, smelling of woodsmoke and mate and forty years of this country. He held Paloma at arm's length and studied him the way he'd once studied weather off the eastern horizon. "You're thinner. You're poorer. You're—" He paused, squinting past Paloma's shoulder at the empty track behind him. "You came alone?"

"She's already here," Paloma said. "Arrived Tuesday. She wouldn't tell me when she was coming either. I think we've both learned not to announce ourselves."

Fermín's laugh rolled out over the yard, startling a pair of chimango caracaras off the windmill. "Two liars under one roof and neither one told the other. You deserve each other. Come inside before the wind changes its mind about you."

* * *

Ines was in the kitchen, and she looked—well. She looked healed, mostly. The shoulder wound from the pass had taken four months and two surgeries to quiet down, and she still favored her left arm when she thought no one was watching, which meant she was watching everyone constantly, because Ines Cabral had survived eleven days alone on the steppe by noticing everything and trusting nothing, and some habits outlive their usefulness.

She stood at the stove with her back to him, arguing with a kettle, and Paloma took the moment to simply look at her. Not the way he'd looked at her in the guanaco pit—measuring, calculating, a bloodhound assessing whether the quarry was worth the hunt. Not the way he'd looked at her in the dry riverbed, either, when honesty had stripped them both down to load-bearing walls. Just looking. A woman in a borrowed sweater, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of inner tube, swearing softly at a kettle in three languages.

"You're staring," she said without turning.

"I'm admiring the view. There's a difference."

"There is not, and you know it." She turned, and her face did the thing it always did when she saw him—the quick flicker of assessment, followed by the slower, harder-won thing underneath it, which was gladness. "You're late."

"The road between Gobernador Gregores and here flooded. Which is a sentence I never expected to say about Patagonia's dry side."

"The climate doesn't care what we expect it to do. We established that." She crossed the kitchen and kissed him—briefly, firmly, the way she did everything—and then stepped back and hit him on the good shoulder. "That's for taking the long way. I sat here for two days listening to Fermín tell stories about your father that are obviously lies."

"They're all true."

"He says your father outran a dust front on horseback in '81."

"That one's true."

"He says your father once read a storm coming three days early by the way the sheep lay down."

"That one's also true, although the sheep were lying down because of a dog. He read the storm separately. The story got merged over the years. Families do that."

Ines shook her head, but she was smiling, and Paloma realized he'd missed that smile the way you miss water—a low constant thirst you stop noticing until it's quenched. She'd gone back to Buenos Aires in January, testified before the commission, buried Tomás properly at last with his name restored and his family weeping in the front rows. She'd carried the drives herself, every kilometer, refusing to let them out of her sight until the last checksum verified. And then she'd vanished into the machinery of the investigation, and Paloma had spent the winter telling himself he wasn't waiting, and failing.

"And now?" he asked. "The ministry finally let you go?"

"The ministry has larger concerns. Two deputy ministers resigned. The third one is negotiating." Her mouth went flat. "They always negotiate. But the aquifer is designated protected, the drilling permits are frozen, and the Río Deseado basin gets a real remediation study—one run by people who weren't in the room when the first report was buried." She looked at her hands. "Tomás's name is on the original data now. All of it. Core samples, readings, the duplicate drives. It says *Cabral, Reyes, Ocampo, Ferreyra* on the authorship line, and it will say that forever."

Paloma touched her shoulder—the wounded one, gently, because he knew she hated being handled carefully and loved him slightly more than she hated it. She leaned into his hand for exactly two seconds, then straightened.

"Fermín tells me you've been fixing fences," she said.

"Fermín tells too many stories."

"Is it true?"

"It's true. There's a ranch south of town lost half its western line in the winter winds. I worked a month for meat and diesel." He shrugged. "It turns out I'm good at reading weather and bad at monetizing it."

"You were never bad at monetizing it. You were bad at selling yourself to people who deserved to buy." She said it plainly, without pity, which was why he could bear it. "That's over now."

"Is it? Because I checked my account in Gregores, and—"

"I don't mean the money." She took his face in her hands, and her eyes were the gray-brown of the steppe after rain, and absolutely steady. "I mean the owing. You don't owe anyone anymore. Not the Ferros. Not the government. Not your father's ghost. Sit down, Paloma. Eat something. The kettle and I have reached an understanding."

From the doorway, Fermín snorted. "The kettle won. It always wins. That's why I drink coffee."

* * *

They ate lamb roasted over coals outside, because October in Bajo Caracoles was warm enough by day and cold enough by night to make fire a pleasure rather than a necessity, and Fermín told stories until the stars came out in the hard Patagonian way—close, sharp, unromantic. Stories about Paloma's father, most of them true. Stories about the cleanup crew, none of them funny. One story, delivered with great ceremony, about a mare he'd owned in 1974 that could predict rain better than the meteorological service, which Paloma suspected was the sheep-and-dog story wearing a costume.

Ines laughed at that one. Really laughed, head back, wounded shoulder and all, and Paloma watched Fermín watch her with an expression the old man would have denied under torture: tenderness.

Later, when the fire had settled to coals and Ines had gone in, Fermin poured himself one measured finger of aguardiente and offered the bottle to Paloma, who declined, and the old man nodded approval.

"So," Fermín said. "The famous question."

"Which famous question?"

"The one every man who comes back from a war asks himself. What now?" Fermín sipped. "You're thirty-four years old, Dunmore. You can read pressure like a priest reads scripture. You survived a dust front that killed three armed men in trucks. You burned a government contract and lived. And you're broke, unemployed, and sitting in my yard drinking nothing." He smiled. "What now?"

Paloma looked east, where the land ran flat and dark toward the coast, toward the dry side, toward everything that had nearly killed him and everything that had saved him. "I don't know. I keep thinking I should want something big. Revenge, or money, or—significance. Something to match what happened." He shook his head. "But mostly I want to fix fences and chase storms I choose myself. I want to drive toward weather because it's beautiful, not because someone's paying me to stand in its teeth. Is that small?"

"It's enormous." Fermín knocked the ash from his pipe against his boot heel. "Most men never learn to want something small on purpose. They spend their whole lives wanting big things badly." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Your father wanted exactly what you want. He was terrible at explaining it and worse at keeping it, but he wanted it. Go to bed. Tomorrow there's work."

"What work?"

"Fence work. The western line. You didn't think you drove all this way to sit around being admired, did you?" Fermín shuffled toward the house, then stopped. "And Dunmore. The girl. Don't break her. She's held together with discipline and grief, and grief doesn't heal the way wounds do."

"I know," Paloma said. "I'm not planning to break anything ever again."

"Good answer." Fermín's silhouette filled the doorway. "Now prove it for the next fifty years and I'll believe you."

* * *

They worked the fence line together for six days, and it was the best week of Paloma's life, though he'd have died before saying so out loud where Ines could hear.

There was a language to it, fence work—tension and give, post and wire, the way you learned ground by driving stakes into it. Ines learned fast, the way she learned everything, and by the third day she could set a strainer post plumb without checking, and by the fifth she'd stopped favoring the shoulder entirely, working through the ache because the work mattered more than the pain. They talked while they worked, or didn't talk, and both silences were comfortable, which was new, and both conversations were honest, which was newer.

On the sixth evening they sat on the tailgate of the truck eating hard cheese and watching the sun go down over the flats, and Ines said, without preamble:

"I dreamed about Tomás last night. Not the pit. Before. The drill site, the day he hid the drives. He was laughing about something—I couldn't hear what. He handed me a core sample and said, 'This one's for the future,' and I woke up crying, but it was—" She searched for the word. "Clean. It was clean crying. Not the other kind."

"The other kind leaves a residue," Paloma said. "Like dust."

"Exactly like dust." She leaned into him. "Do you dream about the pass?"

"Sometimes. Mostly I dream about the storm. The one I led them into." He was quiet a moment. "In the dreams it talks to me. Not words. More like—it knows I'm there. Like the wilderness forgave me for using it, but it wants me to remember that it chose to. That it could have taken me instead."

"Would that be so bad? Being taken by something that forgives you?"

"It would be honest," Paloma said. "Which is more than I deserved, going in."

Ines was silent for a while. Then: "I got an offer. Yesterday, on the radio relay. The university in Comodoro wants me to lead the remediation study. Five years. Real funding. My name on the program." She turned to look at him. "It's everything I said I wanted. The truth made official. The burial reversed in broad daylight."

"But?"

"But it means staying. Buildings, committees, budgets. Five years indoors." She looked east again, where the horizon had gone the color of hammered copper. "And I keep thinking about the steppe. About how it hid us and found us and hid Tomás and gave him back. I owe it something. Not data. Something else."

Paloma didn't answer right away. Out past the fence line, a herd of guanaco moved along the horizon, unhurried, ancient, indifferent to the affairs of men. He watched them until the feeling in his chest settled into words.

"Then don't take the job," he said. "Take the work. Do the study from the field. Sample the basin yourself, on foot, in a truck, the way Tomás would have. Tell the university their offices are in Comodoro but their aquifer is four hundred kilometers north, and if they want the truth they'll have to come out and get it." He shrugged. "Worst case, they say no, and you and I go back to fixing fences, which pays terribly but has excellent views."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she started to laugh—that same real laugh from the fire, helpless and bright—and hit him with the cheese.

"You're proposing I spend five years camping."

"I'm proposing you spend five years *choosing*. There's a difference. I've done both."

"And where exactly would I find a field man for such an operation? Someone who reads weather? Someone cheap?"

"Broke, actually. Completely broke. But I hear he's reliable in a dust front."

"Mm." She kissed him, tasting of cheese and dust and the future. "We'll see. I don't commit to contractors without references."

* * *

The storm came on the seventh morning.

Paloma felt it before dawn—not saw, *felt*, the way he always had, the pressure dropping through the soles of his boots and up through his teeth, the air going strange and sweet, the guanaco moving west in tight nervous lines. He stood outside in the gray half-light with his hands in his pockets and read it: a front building far to the northeast, over the coast, big and slow and full of rain it would drop on country that hadn't seen proper rain in a season. A good storm. An honest one. It would arrive tomorrow evening, maybe the day after, and it would be magnificent, and nobody was paying him to stand in its path, and nobody was hunting him through it, and for the first time in his adult life a storm was just a storm.

Ines came out and stood beside him, following his gaze.

"That one?" she asked.

"That one. Northeast, over the gulf. It'll swing south-west across the flats." He tilted his head, listening to something below hearing. "Big. Slow. The kind that lies about its size until it doesn't."

"Are we running from it?"

"No." He smiled. "For once, we're just going to watch it come in."

Behind them, the screen door banged and Fermín emerged with three cups of coffee, squinting at the horizon with the professional contempt of a man who'd outlived every forecast ever made against him. He handed out the cups, studied the sky, and grunted.

"Dust first, then rain," he said. "Front'll stall over the Coyle around midday tomorrow, then push through overnight. Your father would have said the same."

"Your father," Ines murmured, "or the sheep?"

"The sheep," said Fermín, deadpan, and drank his coffee.

They stood together on the porch as the sun came up, three cups steaming in the cold bright air, the storm building far away where only Paloma could feel it gathering itself over the sea. The windmill turned its broken rhythm. The fence wire sang its one note. Somewhere out on the flats, the guanaco had stopped running and begun to graze, which was the oldest weather instrument in the world, and it said: *rain, real rain, coming fair.*

Paloma drank his coffee and felt the pressure fall and fall, sweet and enormous, and thought: *this is what it weighs when nothing is attached to it. This is what weather costs when you're free.*

Ines's hand found his. Her grip was strong, and getting stronger.

"Well," she said, watching the horizon begin to darken at the edges, copper going to bronze going to a deep bruised violet. "Storm chaser. Where do we go to meet it?"

Paloma grinned, and pointed his cup northeast, into the heart of the coming dark.

"Anywhere we like," he said. "It's not chasing us anymore. Now we're just two people going to see it home."

Far off, the first thunder rolled across the dry side like a door opening—and this time, walking toward it, he had nothing in his pockets but his own name.
