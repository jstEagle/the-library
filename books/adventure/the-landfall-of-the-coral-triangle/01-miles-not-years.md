## 1. Miles, Not Years

The supercell was forty-one kilometers out and closing at a bearing of two-sixty, and I loved it more than I had ever loved a person.

That's not a confession I make lightly. It's just arithmetic. A storm has never lied to me about who it was. It doesn't say *I'll call you* and then not call. It doesn't smile while it doubles your debt. A storm tells you exactly what it is—pressure, moisture, instability—and then it keeps its word. There's a decency in that that people have never managed.

I sat on the hood of the Hilux with the anemometer humming in my fist and watched the thing build itself out over the Gulf of Carpentaria like God showing off for an empty house. The updraft base went green-black at its heart, the color of deep water, the color of the inside of a wave. Hail shafts fell out of it in gray curtains you could almost read through. Somewhere under all that, lightning was stitching cloud to ground in strokes longer than my whole miserable life added end to end.

Forty-one kilometers. At the cell's forward speed of thirty-two knots, that gave me seventy-odd minutes before the gust front arrived and turned my beautiful data run into a bad hair day. Seventy minutes is a number you can hold in your hand. Seventy minutes doesn't ask you what you're doing with your life.

I'd learned this trick young, though I didn't know it was a trick until much later. When the school counselor asked about my mother—I was eleven, she'd been gone eight months, and everyone kept using words like *processing*—I answered in kilometers. The distance from our house to the cemetery was six point two. The distance from the cemetery to anywhere else was whatever I wanted it to be. Grief lives in time. Time is where it grows. But distance? Distance is finite. You can close distance. You can put your back against it and push. Nobody ever taught me how to push against a Tuesday afternoon in an empty house, so I became a person who measured everything in how far, never in how long.

It's worked out great, thanks for asking.

The radar app on my phone refreshed. The cell had hooked slightly north—classic right-mover behavior inverted by the sea breeze boundary pushing up from the coast—and that bought me maybe ten extra minutes if I repositioned east along the dirt track. Ten minutes. Another number. Another thing to hold instead of the other things.

Because here's what the other things looked like when I let them in:

There was a letter in the glovebox of the Hilux. Cream-colored stock, heavy enough to be expensive, which was its own kind of insult. The Voss Syndicate didn't send emails. Emails could be ignored, deleted, lost in the wash of a spam filter. Voss sent paper. Voss sent paper with a watermark of a stylized anchor wrapped in a laurel—their idea of wit, maritime law as a family crest—and the paper said, in fonts chosen by someone paid to make threats look like invitations:

*RE: ACCOUNT NO. 4471-C — QUENTIN OYELARAN*

*Outstanding balance: $312,840 AUD.*

*This notice supersedes all prior correspondence. Terms renegotiable only through authorized Syndicate representation.*

And then, because Mara Iles had a sense of humor like a scalpel laid flat on a table so you could see it: *We remain optimistic regarding your situation.*

Optimistic. I'd been chewing on that word for three days now, the way you chew a stone to see if there's gold in it. There wasn't. There was just stone.

Three hundred twelve thousand, eight hundred and forty dollars. I knew the number the way other people know their mother's birthday. I'd inherited it honestly, which is to say stupidly: a chase vehicle financed through a broker who turned out to be Voss-adjacent, then a refinance when the season went dry, then a bridge loan against the equipment, each signature a little smaller and more desperate than the last until the handwriting barely qualified as mine. The interest compounded daily. I'd done the math once, drunk, and then done it again sober to confirm, and then stopped doing it because some numbers are load-bearing and you don't inspect them too closely or the ceiling comes down.

Daily compounding meant that every hour I spent watching this storm cost me roughly four dollars and eleven cents.

I did that math too. I always did that math. And then I stayed anyway, because four dollars an hour was a price I could pay for seventy minutes of not being alone with myself, and loneliness had never quoted me a rate. It just took payment in full, whenever it felt like it.

* * *

The gust front hit at 16:42, nine minutes later than my revised estimate, and I took that personally in the best way—a gift, a small grace note from a universe that otherwise communicated exclusively through invoices.

I'd retreated to the cab by then, camera running through the windshield, and when the shelf cloud came over me it came like a wall falling in slow motion. The light went the color of old brass. Dust lifted off the salt pan in a rolling sheet two hundred meters high, and above it the wall cloud rotated with the lazy menace of something that knows exactly how big it is. The wind arrived first as a whisper in the wire grass, then as a shout, then as a physical hand shoving the Hilux sideways on its springs.

"Talk to me," I murmured to it, which is embarrassing to admit but true. "Come on. Show me."

And it did. That's the thing. It always does. The mesocyclone tightened on the radar feed, velocities wrapping into a couplet tight enough to make my pulse do something unprofessional, and for ninety seconds I was the happiest person in the Gulf of Carpentaria, possibly the happiest person in the Southern Hemisphere, hunched over a screen in a shaking truck while the world outside tried to sandblast the paint off.

Then the cell crossed the coastline boundary and began its long collapse over water it couldn't feed on, and the show ended the way shows end—all at once and too soon—and I was left sitting in the after-rain silence with wet dust on the glass and a heart rate I had no further use for.

The quiet after a storm is the worst quiet there is. People think the loud part is the dangerous part. The loud part is fine. The loud part has structure, purpose, a job to do. It's the quiet after that gets in. In the quiet, the numbers come home to roost. Four dollars and eleven cents an hour. Three hundred twelve thousand and change. Thirty-four years old, and the sum total of my estate was a truck with hail dents, sixty thousand dollars of meteorological equipment worth maybe twenty on a good day, and a debt growing faster than any storm I'd ever chased.

In the quiet, the question I'd been outrunning since I was eleven finally caught up, the way it always does, wearing different clothes each time. This time it wore the voice of my sister, Nneka, who called twice a year from Melbourne and always opened with the same line:

*"Quentin, when are you going to be somewhere?"*

Not *what are you doing*. Not *how's work*. *When are you going to be somewhere.* She'd found the exact phrasing years ago, the one that slipped past all my deflections, and she used it like a surgeon uses a blade she's sharpened specifically for me.

I didn't know the answer. I'd never known the answer. Being somewhere required staying, and staying required believing that a place wouldn't eventually turn on me the way places do—by emptying out, or filling up with someone else's family, or simply revealing that they were never mine to begin with. Motion was safer. Motion meant I left before I could be left. Motion meant every goodbye was technically my idea.

The trouble with motion, nobody warns you, is that it works. You can outrun grief, outrun debt collectors, outrun your own reflection for years at a time. It works so well that you forget it's supposed to be temporary. You wake up at thirty-four in a rented room in Karumba with a duffel bag packed and a go-bag packed and a third bag packed that you don't even remember packing, and you realize that somewhere along the way the escape route became the destination. You don't live a life and travel through it. You travel, and the traveling is the life, and there's no *there* at the end of it because *there* was never on the itinerary.

The rain started again, soft and directionless, the storm's dying breath. I wiped the windshield with my sleeve and watched the water bead and run.

* * *

Karumba at dusk is a town that exists mostly as a rumor of itself. A pub, a boat ramp, a caravan park full of people escaping southern winters, and a horizon so flat and wide it looks like the ocean got bored halfway through making it. I ate a barramundi burger at the pub because eating well would have required optimism I didn't have, and I spread my paperwork on the corner table like a man playing solitaire with his own ruin.

The Voss notice. The bank statement. The insurance renewal for equipment I couldn't afford to insure and couldn't afford to lose. A printout of my account balance, which I'd folded in half so I wouldn't have to look at it, and which I unfolded anyway, because I'm nothing if not consistent in my self-harm.

Three hundred twelve thousand, eight hundred and forty. Plus today's accrual. Plus tomorrow's. The number had a gravity to it now, a mass. It bent my days around itself. Every decision I made passed through its field: where I chased, where I slept, whether I replaced the cracked windshield or just kept telling myself the crack was structural character. I had become a satellite of my own debt, orbiting it, unable to achieve escape velocity, unable to crash into it either because crashing would mean stopping and stopping was—

Well. Stopping was the thing I didn't do.

"You want another one, love?" The barmaid, a woman built like weather herself, nodded at my untouched second beer.

"I'm driving."

"In this town?" She snorted. "Half the road's river and the other half's mud. Nobody's driving anywhere fast." But she took the hint and left me alone, which is the highest courtesy Karumba extends.

I looked at the Voss letter again. *Authorized Syndicate representation.* I knew what that meant. It meant Mara Iles, and Mara Iles meant a meeting in a nice café in Darwin where she'd order something with foam in it and smile at me with teeth that had never bitten anything harder than a croissant, and somewhere in the middle of the pleasantries the deadline would move and the number would grow and I'd walk out into the sun feeling like I'd been pickpocketed by someone I thanked on the way out.

I'd met her twice. Both times she'd called me *Quentin* without being invited to, both times she'd remembered details about my life that made clear someone was keeping files, and both times she'd ended with some variation of the same benediction: *"The Syndicate believes in you, Quentin. We invest in people we believe in."*

Dangerous optimists, my father used to call men like that—not that I'd known he was describing them at the time. He'd meant the missionaries, the mining scouts, the government men who came through the district promising roads and schools and left with the land. Men who believed everything would work out. For them. Optimism as a business model, with the risk externalized onto people whose belief wasn't required.

Mara Iles was optimistic about me the way a farmer is optimistic about rain.

My phone buzzed on the table. A message from the broker—the legitimate one, the one who still pretended our relationship was normal:

*Season's dead up north till October. Word is there's charter money in the west if you're mobile. Broome way. Call me.*

Broome. Sixteen hundred kilometers, give or take. I caught myself doing it—converting the ache into mileage, converting the dread into fuel stops—and laughed out loud, alone at a corner table in a nearly empty pub, the laugh coming out wrong, too sharp.

Sixteen hundred kilometers. Two days' drive. A number I could hold.

What I couldn't hold, what no distance could fix, was the arithmetic underneath: that I was thirty-four years old and the only entity on earth that knew my schedule was a debt collection agency. That when the storms stopped, I stopped having a reason, and when I stopped having a reason, the silence moved in like water finding a crack. That I had built an entire existence designed to prevent exactly the thing that had happened anyway, which was to end up alone in a room with a piece of cream-colored paper telling me what my future cost.

I folded the papers away. I drank the beer after all—it seemed suddenly important not to waste anything, including my own small rebellions—and I walked out into the Karumba night, where the air lay on my skin like a warm wet cloth and the stars came down almost to the mangroves.

Somewhere out there, past the flat dark lip of the Gulf, the Coral Triangle waited. I didn't know that yet. I didn't know about Edda Kwan or her waterproofed chart or the promise that would cost me everything I had and give me back something better. All I knew was that my phone had a message about Broome, my account had a number in it shaped like a noose, and the sky over the water was doing something complicated with the moonlight that I stood and watched for ten full minutes because it was free.

Ten minutes. Another number. I collected them like other people collect stamps.

The next morning I pointed the Hilux west. Sixteen hundred kilometers of nothing, which is exactly the amount of nothing I needed. The road unspooled ahead, straight as a ruled line, and I drove into it the way I always drove into things—fast enough that the mirrors couldn't tell me stories, slow enough that the gas lasted, and with the radio off, because the radio had too many songs about home.

Behind me, the debt grew by four dollars and eleven cents an hour.

Ahead of me, sixteen hundred kilometers of open country, and at the end of it, though I couldn't have known it, a hospice bed in Broome where a dying woman was waiting to trade me the only map that mattered—for a promise I had no idea yet how to keep.
