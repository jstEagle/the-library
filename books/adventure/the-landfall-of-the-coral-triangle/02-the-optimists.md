## 2. The Optimists

The Voss Syndicate kept its Darwin office above a pearl showroom on Stokes Street, which told you everything you needed to know about how they saw themselves. Pearls: irritation turned into an asset. Something ugly at the center, wrapped in enough smooth layers that people paid a premium for it.

I arrived eleven minutes early because arriving late to a meeting with the Voss Syndicate was a way of donating your kneecaps to science. The girl at reception—nineteen maybe, with a lanyard and a headset and the thousand-yard stare of someone who answered phones for men who broke legs—smiled at me like I was already a widow.

"Ms. Oyelaran. Ms. Iles will see you now."

*Now.* Eleven minutes early and it was *now*. That was the first lesson of the day: your time belonged to them before you even sat down.

* * *

Mara Iles occupied a corner office with a view of the harbor that she clearly never looked at. She was standing at the window when I came in, hands clasped behind her back like a woman reviewing troops, and when she turned she gave me a smile so warm and unhurried that my spine went cold in self-defense.

I'd done my homework on the flight down from the Gulf. Mara Iles, thirty-eight, formerly of the federal insurance fraud unit, formerly of a private investigations firm in Singapore that had dissolved amid litigation nobody would discuss on the record. Recruited by Voss four years ago. Promoted twice. Her file—what little of it existed—read like a résumé written by someone who understood that institutions are just machines for converting other people's mistakes into money.

"Quentin Oyelaran." She said my name like she was tasting it. "The storm chaser. Sit, please. Can I get you anything? Coffee? Water? We have excellent water."

"I'm fine."

"Of course you are." She sat across from me, folding herself into the chair with the ease of someone who had never once been uncomfortable in her life. "You know, I read your incident reports. All of them. The Gulf of Carpentaria intercept last month—the one where you put your vehicle between a tornado and a road train full of fuel. Do you know what struck me about it?"

"The wind shear?"

"What struck me," she continued, as if I hadn't spoken, "was that you didn't run. Everyone else on that highway ran. You drove *toward* it. There's a photograph—you're standing on the roof of your truck filming the wall cloud while everyone else is in the culvert. It's quite striking. You look very calm."

"It's a job."

"It's a *symptom*," she said pleasantly. "But we'll come back to that."

She opened a leather folder on the desk. I recognized my own signature at the bottom of the top page, from three years ago, back when eighty thousand dollars had seemed like a number instead of a noose.

"So," she said. "Let's talk about your situation."

"My situation."

"Your debt. Your timeline. Your options." She ticked each word off on manicured fingers. "You currently owe the syndicate one hundred and fourteen thousand dollars, principal and accrued interest. Your original schedule called for repayment over eighteen months. You've made—" she consulted the folder with theatrical care— "two payments. Totaling four thousand dollars. Which covered, roughly, the interest on the interest."

"The season was bad. Chasing doesn't pay like it used to. Insurance companies got cheap."

"Mm. And yet you keep doing it." She leaned forward, and the smile stayed exactly where it was, fixed and bright, like a lamp bolted to a wall. "That's the part I find fascinating, Quentin. Not the debt. Anyone can have a debt. But you—a woman who can calculate a supercell's track to within two kilometers, who reads pressure gradients the way priests read scripture—you signed a loan with terms you couldn't meet. Why?"

"Because the man across the desk lied to me about the rate."

"He did," she agreed cheerfully. "Rudy was always a poor mathematician. He's been reassigned. To something involving fishing. So let's not pretend the problem is dishonesty—we both know what you bought that money for, and we both know you'd sign again tomorrow. The problem is arithmetic. Yours has stopped working."

I said nothing. In my experience, the most dangerous thing you could do in a room like this was agree too fast or disagree too loud.

Mara watched me not-speak with evident enjoyment, the way a cat watches a mouse practice stillness.

"Here's what I'm authorized to offer," she said. "And Quentin, I want you to understand—I fought for this. I went upstairs and I made the case that you were worth restructuring. Because I believe in you. Genuinely. I think you're one of the most capable people this office has ever lent money to, and capability should be rewarded, don't you think?"

"What's the offer."

"We double your timeline." She spread her hands, beneficent. "Thirty-six months. Reduced monthly payments. A grace period through cyclone season. And all we ask in return—"

"There it is."

"—is a small thing," she finished smoothly. "A formality, really. We need a vessel surveyed. An asset recovery operation, out of Darwin. Six weeks, perhaps eight. You have a boat license, a master's ticket, and a reputation for bringing crews home alive. We need someone with exactly those qualities to take a ship to a set of coordinates and bring back what's there."

My mouth was dry. "What kind of asset?"

"A wrecked one." She smiled wider. "Does the destination matter? You're a storm chaser. Half your profession is driving toward things other people flee from. Consider this the same instinct, better compensated."

"And if I say no?"

The smile didn't move. That was the thing about Mara Iles—her face operated independently of the conversation, like weather over open ocean. "Then the original terms resume. Original terms include our standard collection protocols. I'll be honest with you, Quentin, because I respect you: those protocols are unpleasant. They involve your truck. Your equipment. Eventually they involve places you sleep. Nobody wants that. I certainly don't. I want the version of this story where everyone wins."

"Everyone wins," I repeated.

"Optimism," she said warmly. "It's underrated in our line of work."

* * *

She walked me to the door herself, which I understood was either a courtesy or a message—that she wanted the receptionist to see us leaving together, friendly, settled, a closed transaction. At the threshold she touched my arm, light as a moth landing.

"One more thing," she said. "A piece of advice, professional to professional. Whatever you decide—and I hope you decide well—don't go looking for alternatives. Don't borrow against the truck. Don't sell stories to journalists. And please, please don't take any interesting phone calls from old prospectors."

The floor tilted slightly under me. Only slightly. Enough that I felt it in my knees.

"I don't know any prospectors."

"No," she agreed. "Not yet." And she smiled at me with all the warmth of a heat lamp, and closed the door between us with a click soft as a lock engaging.

* * *

I sat in the truck on Stokes Street for twenty minutes with the engine off and the windows down, letting the harbor smell in—diesel and brine and pearl-showroom air conditioning leaking out the door behind me. Somewhere down the block a tour group was laughing about crocodiles.

Let me tell you what I knew by then, and what I only thought I knew.

What I knew: the Voss Syndicate didn't lend money. They bought people, at retail, and sold them back to themselves at markup. What I knew: Mara Iles hadn't doubled my deadline out of mercy. Doubling a deadline on a drowning debtor isn't kindness—it's farming. You don't gut the fish on day one; you feed it, keep the water aerated, wait until it's worth more. A dead Quentin Oyelaran was worth a repossessed truck. A working Quentin Oyelaran, grateful and scared and sailing wherever she was pointed, was worth a great deal more.

What I only thought I knew: that the coordinates she'd dangle in front of me later, the wreck, the "asset recovery"—that it would be a coincidence. That the timing with Broome, with Edda Kwan, with everything, would be chance.

Chance. Right. I'd spent ten years learning to read the sky, and I still couldn't read the room.

Here's the thing about optimists—I'd met plenty in the chasing world. Guys who'd drive into the red polygon with a consumer drone and a prayer, certain the storm would miss them, certain the hail would stay small, certain the lightning would pick somebody else. Mostly they got lucky. Mostly. The ones who didn't became statistics, and the statistics had names, and I'd been to two of the funerals.

But those optimists were honest. Their optimism cost themselves.

The dangerous kind was different. The dangerous kind was Mara Iles, smiling across a desk with a folder full of my failures, saying *everyone wins* in a voice like warm honey, and meaning it—meaning it completely, sincerely, with her whole heart—because in her arithmetic, *everyone* had always meant *her side of the table*. The dangerous optimists aren't the fools in the culvert's shadow. They're the ones who believe everything works out, provided they're the ones holding the pen when the story gets written. They're cheerful the way a riptide is cheerful. All that pleasant surface motion, and underneath, a single fixed intention, patient as geology.

She'd doubled my deadline. Thirty-six months instead of eighteen. On paper, breathing room. In truth, a longer leash so the collar wouldn't chafe.

And she'd known. Sitting there with her lamp-bolted smile, she had already known about Broome—or suspected, or arranged, or simply laid bait in every direction and waited to see which one I'd swim toward. *Don't take any interesting phone calls from old prospectors.* You don't say that to a woman who knows nothing. You say it to plant the idea, or you say it to watch her flinch, and either way you learn something, and either way she wins the exchange.

I started the truck. The debt meter in my head ticked up another few dollars while I sat there idling, and I made myself stop calculating it, because the numbers had stopped being information and started being a heartbeat.

Six weeks, perhaps eight, she'd said. A vessel surveyed. Coordinates supplied. Bring back what's there.

I could refuse. I could spend the next three years paying four dollars and eleven cents an hour for the privilege of slowly drowning, waiting for the collection protocols to reach whatever I loved next. Or I could sail for the syndicate, do their salvage, hand them whatever the sea was keeping, and buy myself thirty-six months of borrowed time in a cell they owned.

Or—

And here was the thought I hadn't earned yet, the thought that wouldn't fully arrive until a hospice bed in Broome, but which stirred even then, some animal premonition rising off the harbor water:

Or I could take the job and make it mine. Take their coordinates and their deadline and their leash, and somewhere between Darwin and the Coral Triangle, quietly, without telling a soul, turn their errand into something else entirely.

I didn't know yet what that something else was. I didn't know about ledgers, or sinkings-for-insurance, or a family drowned thirty years ago in a hull the syndicate had written off with a smile. I didn't know about Edda Kwan's thirty years of savings folded into envelopes marked with a diver's name, an engineer's name, a cook's name.

All I knew, pulling out onto Stokes Street with the pearl showroom shrinking in my mirrors, was this: Mara Iles believed everything worked out for people like her. And I had just decided, in the dumb stubborn way I decided to drive toward tornadoes, that I was going to be in the room when the arithmetic finally caught up with her.

The phone buzzed against the console. Unknown number. Area code for Broome.

I looked at it for a long time at the red light, watching it ring, hearing Mara's voice in my head: *please, please don't take any interesting phone calls.*

I took the call.
