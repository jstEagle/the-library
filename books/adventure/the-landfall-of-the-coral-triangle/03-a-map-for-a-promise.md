## 3. A Map for a Promise

Broome in the wet season is a town that has given up pretending to be anything but what it is: a furnace with a beach attached. I arrived on a bus that smelled of diesel and durian, with four hundred and eleven dollars to my name and a collection notice folded into my shirt pocket like a splinter working its way toward my heart.

The hospice sat at the end of a street of pearlers' ruins, a low white building with a bougainvillea eating one whole wall. It was the kind of place where the air conditioning hummed at exactly the pitch of resignation. I hadn't come for the architecture. I'd come because a woman named Edda Kwan had sent me three letters over two years, and I'd answered none of them, and then she'd sent a fourth that said only: *I'm dying faster than you're running. Come collect.*

People don't usually put it that way to me. Usually they say *come home*, which is a word I treat like a weather warning—acknowledged, not obeyed.

The nurse at the desk looked at me the way nurses look at people who've clearly slept in their vehicle. "You here for someone?"

"Kwan," I said. "Edda Kwan."

"She's been asking after you all week." The nurse's tone suggested Edda had been asking after me the way other patients ask after morphine. "Room four. And—" she caught my sleeve as I turned, "—she's sharp. Don't let the oxygen fool you."

* * *

Edda Kwan was small in the bed the way old driftwood is small—a thing that had once been substantial and had been worn down by salt and time into something dense and hard. Her hands were the give-away. Prospector's hands, diver's hands, knuckles like mooring cleats. She was watching the ceiling fan when I came in, and she didn't turn her head, just said:

"You walk like a woman counting exits."

"Occupational hazard."

"What's your occupation this month? Last I heard you were chasing storms across the Top End for grainy footage nobody buys."

"That was last year. This year I'm between occupations." I dragged the visitor's chair closer. It complained. Everything in that room complained except her. "You've got me at a disadvantage, Ms. Kwan. You know my résumé and I only know your reputation."

"My reputation." She snorted, which triggered a coughing fit that lasted long enough for me to notice the chart of her medications taped inside the wardrobe door. It read like a grocery list for a very sick person. When she surfaced again, her eyes were wet but furious about it. "My reputation is that I found things and told no one. Thirty years in the Triangle. Wrecks, shoals, a couple of sites the governments would pay good money to keep quiet. And I kept quiet, because keeping quiet was how you stayed alive out there."

"And now?"

"Now I'm in a room where the loudest decision I make all day is whether to have the jelly or the custard." She turned her head and looked at me properly for the first time. Her eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, the eyes of deep water. "So I've decided to stop being quiet. Selectively. To one person. Congratulations. You're the person."

"Why me?" I asked, because it seemed important to establish before whatever came next established itself. "We've never met. Your letters were the first I knew you existed."

"I watched you work." She said it simply, without embarrassment. "Two years ago, Port Hedland. You were running storm footage for a charter outfit, and your skipper wanted to push through a squall line to beat another boat to the fuel dock. You talked him down. Made him anchor up and wait two hours." She paused. "He called you a coward in front of everyone. You took it. Because you knew he was wrong and you knew saying so twice wouldn't help."

"You were there?"

"Fishing. Sitting in a tinny with a line out, watching a fool argue with the sea." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You waited. The squall killed a man on the other side of the harbor who didn't wait. And you never once said *told you so*. That's the whole interview, Ms. Oyelaran. That's why you're the person. You can be told you're wrong by an idiot and still do the right thing, and you don't need applause for it."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I said nothing, which she apparently took as agreement.

She reached under her pillow and brought out a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with diving cord. Even before she set it on the blanket I knew its shape. Anyone who's spent time around wrecks knows the shape of a rolled chart through canvas.

"There's a ship," she said, "in the Coral Triangle. Deep water, behind a reef system that doesn't appear correctly on any chart you can buy. She went down in '61, officially. Unofficially, she went down because certain people wanted her to go down, and they've spent sixty years making sure nobody goes looking." Her fingers moved on the oilcloth, restless, mapping territory only she could see. "Her name was the *Meridian Star*. Cargo manifest says rubber and tin. Actual cargo included a strongroom full of paper."

"Paper."

"Ledgers. Correspondence. Sixty years ago and thirty years ago both—the same company, same handwriting, same method. They sank ships for the insurance, Ms. Oyelaran. Dozens of them. And when crews started asking questions, they sank those too." She looked at me, and I saw the grief underneath arrive like a swell you feel in your feet before you see it break. "My father was chief engineer on a trawler that asked questions in 1979. Fourteen souls. The inquiry called it bad weather. I was nine years old, and I have spent every year since learning enough to prove them liars, and now I'm out of years."

The fan ticked overhead. Somewhere down the hall a television laughed at something unfunny.

"So take it," she said, and pushed the package toward me. "The chart. The coordinates. The approach notes—I wrote them out myself, tide windows, the gap in the reef, everything. It's waterproofed; it'll survive a dunking, though I'd advise against testing that."

"Edda—"

"Let me finish, because I get tired and when I get tired I start being polite, and polite gets us nowhere." She drew a breath that cost her visibly. "Two conditions. Two promises, and I want them spoken aloud, because I'm old-fashioned and because I want to hear your voice lie or not lie."

I should have walked out then. Any sensible person would have. A dying stranger handing you a treasure map with strings attached is either a con or a curse, and I was already carrying one debt shaped like a syndicate. But her hands were steady on the oilcloth, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had carried something heavy for forty years and needed, more than anything in the world, to set it down in the right place.

"Say them," I said. "I'll tell you honestly if I can't make them."

"First promise. Whoever sails that chart—you bring them home alive. Every soul. Not most of them. Not all but one. All. If it comes down to the find or the people, you drown the find."

"That's an easy promise," I said quietly. "Paper doesn't breathe."

"It's harder than you think. People have died for less than what's in that hold, and people will want you to trade lives for it. Promise me anyway."

"I promise. Whoever sails it comes home alive. All of them."

Something in her shoulders loosened a centimeter. "Second promise. Tell no one what you find. Not the authorities—they're bought, some of them, and the rest are slow, and slow gets people killed. Not the press—they'll turn fourteen drowned sailors into a headline and my father into a paragraph. No one. You find it, you know it, and you carry it however you have to carry it, and the world goes on not knowing until the world is ready, and if the world is never ready—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, I could see her hating it, "—then it stays ours. Mine and yours. A secret with fourteen names in it."

And there it was. The catch. Not gold—she didn't care about gold, or she cared about it only as ballast for the truth. She was asking me to become a vault. To spend months and money and risk finding the proof of a massacre and then sit on it, maybe forever.

"Edda," I said carefully, "you understand that if I find evidence of what you're describing, every part of me will want to use it. People who do what those people did—"

"—should burn. Yes. I've had forty years to want that." She closed her eyes. "Wanting is easy. It's the cheapest thing there is. What's expensive is patience. You burn that evidence tomorrow, and their lawyers eat it, and their accountants rebuild around the ashes, and my father stays dead of bad weather. You wait, you choose your moment, you choose your ground—and maybe nothing happens in your lifetime either. Maybe the win is just that it exists. That somewhere, in someone's hands, the truth survived them." She opened her eyes. "Can you live with that? Because if you can't, say so now, and I'll wrap this back up and leave it to the sea, and we'll both have lost nothing but an afternoon."

I thought about the collection notice in my pocket. I thought about Mara Iles and her smile, though I wouldn't meet her for another week yet—no, that's wrong, I'd already met her once by then, briefly, in a Darwin office that smelled of frangipani and menace. I thought about how a person accumulates debts the way a hull accumulates barnacles, invisibly, and then one day you can't remember what the clean bottom felt like.

Here's the thing I couldn't have explained to her then, and can barely explain now: I wasn't looking for redemption. I was looking for a job. But some part of me—the part that anchors up in squalls while fools call me coward—recognized the shape of what she was offering. Not treasure. Weight. Something heavy enough to hold me to one spot long enough to become a person instead of a trajectory.

"I can't promise I'll be perfect at it," I said. "But I promise: I tell no one what we find. Not until it can do good instead of harm. And if it never can—" I swallowed. "Then it stays ours."

Edda Kwan looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, like a judge accepting a plea.

"The chart shows a wreck at eleven degrees south, behind the Selayar approaches—no, I won't spoil it, you'll read the notes. There's a strongroom aft of the engine room. Intact, as far as my survey gear could tell, and my gear was good. Bring cutting equipment. Bring a diver who doesn't panic in confined dark. And bring—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd entered, she looked genuinely uncertain, "—bring people you trust. I know you don't have any. I've done my homework too, Ms. Oyelaran. You run alone because alone can't be betrayed. But this job can't be done alone, and the promise you just made means you'll have to find out whether you're the kind of person who collects crew or the kind who collects casualties."

"That's a hell of a thing to say to a dying woman's guest."

"I'm dying. I get to say hell of a things." She pushed the package fully into my hands. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Or maybe that was just the promise settling in. "There's money. Not much—not expedition money, but seed money. Forty thousand Australian, in an account under a name I used in '88. Details are in the oilcloth. Consider it my savings buying passage for the truth."

"Edda, I can't take your—"

"You can and you will, because it isn't a gift, it's freight. You're carrying my father's name to that wreck, and freight costs money, and I've paid it. Thirty years of dividends from a pearl lease I never told anyone about. Every dollar of it earned quiet, saved quiet, for this." Her breath caught. "Do you know what it's like to save for a thing for thirty years knowing you'll never see it finished?"

"No," I said honestly. "I've never saved for anything. I've only ever outrun things."

"Well." She smiled, and it transformed her face into something I suspected it had been fifty years ago, before the sea took her family and gave her back a mission. "Then this will be good for you. Finishing something. Staying somewhere, even if the somewhere is a promise."

We talked for another hour. She gave me the reef gaps from memory, cross-checking herself against the written notes with the ferocity of a woman who refused to let a dying brain lose a single datum. She told me about her father—Aldous Kwan, chief engineer, who sang off-key in the engine room and mailed her a postcard from every port, and whose last postcard arrived three weeks after the inquiry closed. She told me about the company. She never named it outright, and I understood why even then: names in rooms with thin walls get people hurt. But she described its habits the way you'd describe a shark by its wake, and I felt something cold move through my stomach, because I recognized the wake. I owed that wake money.

By the time the evening nurse came to shoo me out, my legs were stiff from the chair and my face ached from holding itself together. At the door I stopped.

"Edda. Why not go yourself? You know the water better than anyone alive."

She was quiet for a moment. "Because I'd want to dive it myself," she said finally. "And I can't lift a tank anymore. And because—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—because if I went down there and saw his ship, I'd want to stay. Understand? Some wrecks you salvage. Some wrecks you join. I know which kind that one would be for me. So I'm sending someone with less reason to drown and more reason to come back." She looked at me. "Come back, Quentin. That's the whole job. Everything else is detail."

* * *

I walked out of the hospice into the wet-season dusk, the air thick enough to chew, and stood under the bougainvillea trying to decide what I'd just done. Taken a job. Inherited a ghost. Accepted forty thousand dollars and a promise from a dying woman I'd known for one afternoon, in exchange for sailing into waters where a syndicate had spent sixty years demonstrating what it did to curious people.

Any sensible person would have gone back inside and given the chart back.

I unrolled it that night in my room above the tavern, on a table sticky with someone else's beer. The drafting was beautiful—depths in her own hand, current arrows, a gap in the reef marked *Kwan Passage, 4m at high water slack, DO NOT attempt falling tide*, and beneath the wreck symbol, in small careful letters: *M.S. Meridian Star — 14 souls, 1979, and others. Ask them why.*

Ask them why.

I sat there a long time with my thumb on those words, listening to the tavern noise below and the rain starting on the tin roof, and I thought about the notice in my pocket, and about how the Voss Syndicate collected debts in currencies you didn't know you were trading in.

Then I got out a pen and started a list. An engineer. A diver. A cook who could navigate. Somebody young and desperate enough to say yes.

It occurred to me, somewhere around the second page, that I was assembling a crew out of other people's debts to balance my own—that I was building a lifeboat out of drowning people—and that Edda Kwan had probably known I would, because she'd watched me wait out a squall and understood something I hadn't yet learned about myself:

That I couldn't leave people behind. I'd just never stayed long enough to find out.

The rain came down harder. I wrote one more name at the top of the list—mine—and underlined it twice, and went to sleep with the chart weighted flat under my bag, dreaming of a strongroom full of paper at eleven degrees south, and a voice saying *come back, Quentin*, like an anchor going down.
