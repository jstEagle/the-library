## 4. Assembling the Impossible

You can't buy a crew with money you don't have, so I bought one with something cheaper and harder to counterfeit: a story.

I told it four times in nine days, in four different ports along the Top End, and each time it got a little truer. That's the thing about lies — no. Not a lie. A promise. Edda Kwan's promise, which was now mine, and promises have a way of becoming true if you say them enough times into the right ears.

The chart lived inside my jacket, against my ribs, waterproofed in waxed canvas that Edda herself had sealed. Some nights in Darwin I could feel it there like a second heartbeat, warm from my body, patient as a debt. Which is exactly what it was. The Voss Syndicate had given me thirty days to find forty thousand dollars or find out what their optimists did to people who ran out of time. Edda had given me a wreck at the bottom of the Coral Triangle and one instruction: *bring whoever sails it home alive.* Two deadlines, two debts, and only one of them came with a map.

So I went looking for people who were also running out of time. It turns out they're easy to find. They stand at the edges of things — the end of the bar, the end of the pier, the end of their luck — and they watch the water like it owes them an apology.

* * *

Tomas Reyes found me before I found him, which should have been my first warning.

I'd posted a notice on the Darwin waterfront board — the honest kind of notice, which is to say vague: *Expedition vessel seeks engineer. Long voyage. Hazardous. Pay on completion.* Underneath, where a sensible person would put a figure, I'd written: *Ask me.*

He was already sitting at my table when I came back for my bag at the Sailing Club, a man of maybe fifty with forearms like mooring line and reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair. He had my notice folded on the table in front of him, creased into precise quarters.

"Pay on completion," he said. "Completion of what?"

"A salvage survey. Coral Triangle."

"Coral Triangle's a big place."

"I have a chart."

His eyes did something quick and careful behind the glasses, the way an accountant's do when a number doesn't reconcile. "And who's funding this survey?"

"A dead woman," I said, because lying to a stranger is easy but lying badly wastes everyone's time. "She paid in advance. Thirty years of advance."

I expected him to laugh. Instead he took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirt hem, and put them back on like a man deciding to look at something clearly. "I worked accounts for eleven years," he said. "Darwin, then Singapore, then places I don't name. I know what money looks like when it's been waiting a long time. It looks desperate." He tapped the notice once. "Who else have you got?"

"Nobody yet."

"Good. Nobody yet means nobody's been lied to yet." He stood, offered a hand like a shackle being unlocked. "Tomas Reyes. I'll take the job. But I want it understood — I'm not coming aboard for the pay. I owe a man in Dili eleven thousand dollars, and men like him don't send collection notices. They send cousins." He shook my hand once, hard. "You get us home, I square myself. That's the whole contract."

"You'll want it in writing."

"I want it in nothing. Paper's how people like us get caught." He picked up his bag — he'd apparently packed before we ever spoke, another warning I chose not to read — and said, "One more thing. If anyone asks what we're carrying, the answer is survey equipment. Say it like you believe it."

"Why?"

"Because someday someone will ask who believes it less." And he walked off toward the fuel dock like a man returning to work after lunch.

* * *

Priya Nair I found the way you find divers: by standing near water and looking solvent.

She surfaced out of the harbor at the end of a commercial dive job, spat her regulator at the ladder, and looked up at me with the flat appraisal of someone who has been underestimated professionally her entire life. Twenty-eight, maybe. Shoulders like a swimmer, hands scarred white with old coral cuts.

"You're the one posting salvage notices," she said, wringing out her hair. "Everyone down here's been talking. They say you're paying on completion." She said *completion* the way Tomas hadn't — not suspicious, amused. "That's a word people use when they're broke."

"I'm broke," I agreed. "But I'm not lying about the wreck."

"Everybody lying about wrecks says that."

So I did something I'd sworn not to do. I took her around the corner of the fish sheds, away from eyes, and let her look at the chart. Just the edge of it — Edda's handwriting, the depth soundings in faded ink, the coordinates corrected three times over decades in three different hands. Priya studied it for a long moment, longer than a curious person would, shorter than a thief would.

"This sounding's wrong," she said finally, tapping a number. "Or the reef moved. Either way, whoever drew this dove it. More than once. You don't correct a chart from memory unless you've been down there in bad light and come back angry."

"Edda Kwan. She spent thirty years trying to get back to it."

"And died trying?"

"Died before she could try again."

Priya rolled the chart closed with unexpected gentleness, like closing a book over a sleeping child, and handed it back. "I owe a dive shop in Cairns six thousand dollars and my certification, because my ex-partner signed both my name and his to some insurance paperwork that went sideways. He's free. I'm not." She shrugged. "Also I'm good. Better than your wreck deserves, probably. When do we sail?"

"That's it? No questions about hazard pay, about—"

"Quentin." She said my name like she'd already read it somewhere. "People who ask a lot of questions before signing on are people planning to leave early. I plan to arrive." She pulled her gear bag onto her shoulder. "Just tell me one thing honestly. Is anyone hunting this wreck besides us?"

I thought about Mara Iles's smile, the doubled deadline, the word *optimists* deployed like a scalpel.

"Not yet," I said.

"Then we're ahead," said Priya Nair, and walked away whistling.

* * *

Old Sef I didn't recruit so much as surrender to.

He came with the boat. The *Corvus* was a forty-year-old trawler tied up at Stokes Hill Wharf with a FOR SALE sign that had weathered into poetry, and Sef was living aboard, cooking on a single burner, and pretending not to be dying of the same slow bankruptcy as the vessel. Seventy if he was a day, built like driftwood, with a face so creased it looked navigational.

"She won't make the Triangle," he told me, the first time I stepped aboard to inspect her. "Not without a new mast step, fresh planking below the waterline, and an engine overhaul. That's three months' work and eight thousand dollars."

"I have six weeks and no dollars."

He looked at me for a long time. Somewhere in that face, tides turned. "Then you'll be wanting a cook-navigator too," he said, "because a fool sailing that far on that hull needs someone in the galley who can read weather and lie to the sea on her behalf." He wiped his hands on an apron that had outlived several nations. "Forty-one years afloat, girl. Trawlers, pearling luggers, one very bad year on a research vessel I don't discuss. I know the Timor Sea the way you know your own debts — intimately and with regret."

"What do you owe?"

"Nothing to anybody." He said it fast, which meant it wasn't true. "I owe the sea. Forty years of taking from her and never once saying thank you properly. This trip — your madwoman's chart, your dead prospector — this is how a man settles up." He jabbed a thumb at the engine hatch. "Reyes can fix the step. I've watched him work. He drinks his coffee like a man doing penance; those types always know engines."

"You've already decided to come."

"I decided the day you walked past and didn't haggle over the price sign." For the first time, Old Sef smiled, and it rearranged his whole face into something younger and sadder. "Men who don't haggle are either rich or running. I smelled which one you were from the wharf."

* * *

Junie I found by accident, which is the only way anyone finds Junie.

She was seventeen, maybe — she claimed nineteen with the conviction of someone rehearsing — and she was hiding in the *Corvus*'s aft hold among the spare lines when I came aboard at dawn to check on the repairs. She'd been there, it turned out, for two days. Eating Sef's leftovers. Sleeping in the rope locker. Leaving everything exactly where she found it, because whatever she was running from had taught her that tidiness was survival.

I knew all this within ten minutes, because Sef told me, having known it within ten seconds.

"There's a stowaway," he said, cracking eggs into a pan like a man announcing rain. "Skinny kid. Been stealing my rice porridge. I let her, because she folds the blanket after. You don't report someone who folds the blanket."

I went down to the hold and found her wedged between a coil of hawser and the hull, watching me with the stillness of prey that has learned stillness works. Dark hair chopped short with what looked like kitchen scissors. A duffel bag clutched to her chest like ballast.

"I'm not stealing," she said. "I was going to work it off. I can splice. I can paint. I can—"

"Breathe," I said. "Nobody's calling anyone."

Her eyes flicked to the companionway, calculating exits. I recognized the math. I'd done it my whole life — every room measured in doors, every kindness audited for the hook hidden inside it.

"Here's the situation," I said, sitting down on the hawser coil, putting myself below her eye level, which is a trick I learned from horses and liars. "In about five weeks, this boat sails for the Coral Triangle with a crew of three and a dead woman's map. We need a deckhand. The pay is nothing until the end, the work is brutal, and the destination is classified. In exchange: food, a bunk, and nobody asking where you came from or who you're running from or why."

Silence. Her knuckles had gone white on the duffel.

"That's it?" she whispered. "No conditions?"

"One condition." I held up a finger. "You fold the blanket."

Something happened in her face then — a crack, a thaw, the first millimeter of a door opening in a wall she'd probably been building since she was small. "Sef told you that?"

"Sef tells me everything. It's his superpower and my burden."

A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh in a former life. "Junie," she said. "Just Junie."

"Just Junie," I agreed. "Welcome aboard. Try not to die; the paperwork's murder."

* * *

We sailed — no. We didn't sail yet. Let me keep the order straight, because memory likes to shuffle decks.

First there was the week of making the impossible seaworthy: Tomas under the hull in a diving mask with a caulking iron, surfacing with numbers instead of complaints; Priya rigging dive gear and quietly inventorying everyone's equipment like a quartermaster of a tiny nation; Sef cooking for four strangers as though feeding them were a form of navigation; Junie splicing lines with hands that shook until they didn't.

And one evening, on the fifth night, we all ended up in the cockpit with Sef's stew and a bottle of cheap rum, and I looked around at them — the accountant fleeing cousins, the diver fleeing paperwork, the cook fleeing nothing he'd name, the kid fleeing everything — and I felt something I hadn't felt since I was too young to defend against it.

Hope. Cautious, embarrassed, badly repaired hope.

It terrified me more than the syndicate did. Debts you can calculate. People you can't.

"So," said Priya, raising the rum bottle, "to the dead woman who funded us."

"To Edda Kwan," said Tomas.

"To the wreck," said Junie, and then, quieter, "and to folding blankets."

Old Sef poured a measure into the sea itself, an offering, forty years overdue. "To arriving," he said. "However we manage it."

I drank last, and I drank looking north, toward the Timor Sea and the deep water beyond, where a chart against my ribs said something waited that could buy all our freedoms or cost all our lives, depending entirely on what we found and who else wanted it.

Five days later, the radio crackled with a cyclone warning for the Arafura, and Sef read the sky, said nothing, and began cooking double rations.

We sailed anyway. Waiting, after all, costs money we don't have.
