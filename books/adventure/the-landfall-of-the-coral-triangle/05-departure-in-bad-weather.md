## 5. Departure in Bad Weather

I have been reading weather for forty years, and I will tell you the first thing a man learns: the sky never lies, but it does negotiate.

The morning we left Darwin, the sky was negotiating hard.

I stood on the deck of the *Corvus* with my coffee going cold in my hand and watched the northwest horizon do that thing it does before it means business—a bruise-colored band sitting low and patient, like a debt collector who knows you're home. The barometer had dropped eleven millibars since midnight. The gulls had gone inland. And the radio, which I'd been nursing at four in the morning like a guilty pleasure, was promising a tropical low forming in the Arafura Sea, expected to intensify, all vessels advised to seek shelter.

All vessels advised to seek shelter. Forty years I've heard those words, and forty years they've meant one thing to me: *you can afford to wait.* Waiting is a luxury item. It's the champagne of the sea. You buy it with fuel money, with harbor fees, with days of your life spent bobbing at anchor while your provisions dwindle and your creditors sharpen their arithmetic.

We could not afford champagne.

"Old Sef." Quentin came up the companionway with a clipboard she didn't need—she's the kind of woman who carries paper so her hands have something to strangle—and stood beside me at the rail. She looked at the horizon too. She's not a sailor, not really; she reads storms from the inside of them, which is a different animal entirely. Chasers look at a supercell the way gamblers look at cards. Sailors look at the same cloud and start counting their fingers, checking they've still got ten.

"You seeing what I'm seeing?" she asked.

"I'm seeing a low-pressure system that hasn't decided what it wants to be yet," I said. "Could turn south. Could die over the Tiwis. Could come sit on our heads."

"But?"

"But nothing. That's the honest forecast. *But* is for people who can afford one."

She was quiet a while. Down in the harbor, the fishing fleet was doing the sensible thing—doubling lines, battening hatches, that slow ritual choreography of boats deciding to stay put. The *Corvus* sat among them like a stray dog at a dog show. Forty years old if she was a day, patched hull, an engine Tomas swore he could keep alive through anything, which is the sort of thing engineers say right up until the moment they can't.

"The Voss deadline moved," Quentin said. "Again."

"Mara Iles moved it."

"She called it a gesture of good faith." Quentin's mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Two weeks instead of one. She doubled my time to find the wreck and doubled my debt in the same phone call. Very generous."

I'd met men like Mara Iles my whole working life. They smile because smiling costs nothing and everything else does. They give you more rope because rope has a second use they're already thinking about. When I was a younger man running cargo out of Kupang, I knew a harbormaster who used to extend deadlines the same way—warm voice, generous terms—and every boat that took his generosity ended up owing him its hull. I learned young that when a dangerous man gives you time, he isn't giving you time. He's giving himself a schedule.

"So we leave today," Quentin said. It wasn't quite a question, which told me she'd already decided and wanted my agreement the way people want a priest's blessing—not information, just absolution.

I took a long pull of the cold coffee. Out past the breakwater, the water had gone that flat, oily gray-green that old fishermen call *the held breath*. The wind was still light, southeasterly, gentle as a lie. That's the thing about cyclones that catches greenhorns every time: the worst day of your life often starts as the calmest.

"If we wait out this low," I said, "we burn three days at anchor. Fuel, food, harbor fees, and Priya's dive tanks sitting in a hot hold losing their rubber seals. Then we cross anyway, because we have to, except by then the system's matured and we cross into its teeth instead of ahead of them."

"And if we go now?"

"Now, we run ahead of it. Stay west of its track, keep the *Corvus* moving, and we might slip past the whole mess before it organizes itself. Might." I set down the mug. "Or it turns north faster than the models say, and we spend four days hove-to under bare poles wishing we were anyone else."

Quentin nodded slowly. "Which would you bet on?"

Here's where I should tell you I gave her the seaman's answer. Here's where I should say I told her no sane master sails into a named system with a patched trawler and a crew of strangers, and that safety comes first, always, no exceptions, and that any man who says otherwise is selling something.

I didn't say that. Because I'd done my own arithmetic in the dark hours before dawn, and my numbers came out the same as hers.

"We go now," I said. "West-northwest, tight against the Timor coast until we clear the Cape, then make our crossing. We stay ahead of it or we don't, but either way we're not sitting in Darwin watching the meter run."

Something in her shoulders let go. Not much. Quentin Oyelaran doesn't let go of much; I've watched her carry things the size of houses on a frame built for carrying secrets. But a little.

"Thank you, Sef."

"Don't thank me. Thank whoever invented harbor fees. They're the reason half the great voyages in history happened—nobody could afford to stay put."

* * *

The crew assembled the way bad ideas assemble: one piece at a time, each one reasonable on its own.

Tomas Reyes came aboard first, as engineers do, because ships are theirs and everyone else is a guest. He'd spent the morning in the engine room up to his elbows in the *Corvus*'s ancient heart, and he surfaced wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime last decade. He looked at the sky exactly once, said "barometer's falling," and went back below. Engineers trust machines over weather. It's a faith I envy sometimes—the belief that if you just tighten the right bolt, the world holds together.

Priya Nair arrived with her gear in two duffel bags and a face that suggested she'd read the forecast twice and made her peace with it. Divers are a strange breed. They spend their lives in the space between two atmospheres, where a mistake doesn't punish you tomorrow but right now, immediately, personally. You'd think it would make them cautious. Mostly it makes them precise. She stowed her tanks, checked my chart table without asking permission—which I allowed, because a navigator who asks permission to check charts is a navigator you should worry about—and said, "Wind shifts northeast by afternoon. If we're leaving, leave before noon."

Junie came last, the way runaways always do, half-hoping someone will tell her she can't come. Seventeen maybe, eighteen, all elbows and watchfulness, with a duffel bag that weighed less than my toolbox because everything she owned fit in it and most of what she carried didn't weigh anything at all. Her uncle's fishing boat was moored two piers down. I'd seen him yesterday, a big man with a voice that carried across water whether you wanted it to or not, and I'd seen the way Junie looked at that pier—like it was a door she'd locked behind her and could hear rattling.

Quentin had signed her aboard without questions. I understood why. Every person on this crew owed somebody something—that was practically the job description. Tomas owed his silence to men he used to work for. Priya owed a certification board a very expensive apology. I owed the sea forty years and it hadn't collected yet, though it keeps books better than the Voss Syndicate ever will. And Quentin owed the kind of people who collect with interest and violence both.

A crew of debtors, sailing to pay one debt by finding another. There's poetry in that if you squint. Most poetry is like that—you have to squint, and even then you're mostly looking at your own reflection.

By eleven we had lines off. By quarter past, the *Corvus* was easing out of Stokes Hill Wharf, and I'll be honest with you: there are prettier departures. No brass band, no crowd, no gulls even—they'd all gone sensibly inland to wait out the weather like creatures with sense. Just us and a harbor full of boats staying put, and I felt their eyes on our backs. Old Sef, taking a forty-year-old trawler out ahead of a cyclone warning. They'd talk about it in the pub tonight. *Madness,* they'd say, and pour another, and be glad it wasn't them.

Maybe they were right. But madness is just survival wearing a different coat, depending on which side of the ledger you're standing on.

* * *

By midafternoon we'd cleared the Vernon Islands and the wind had begun its slow rotation, backing around, coming at us now out of the northeast with a smell in it I didn't care for. Rain smell and salt smell and underneath both something older—the smell the air gets when a big system starts breathing somewhere over open water and hasn't picked a name yet.

Tomas came up to report on the engine, which was running, he said, "like a sewing machine with a grudge." He stayed at the rail a moment, looking back southeast toward where the horizon had organized itself into a proper wall now, blue-black and towering, with the first anvil head spreading out on top like a fist opening.

"That's getting bigger," he said.

"They generally do."

"You're not worried."

"I'm worried," I said. "I'm just not surprised. Worry without surprise is half the weight."

He almost smiled. First time I'd seen it. Underneath the engineer's armor, there was a man who'd once done sums for the Voss Syndicate, and I'd figured out early that he hadn't left that work because he found better numbers—he'd left because he'd seen what the numbers bought. Some men carry guilt like ballast. It sits low and steady and keeps you from capsizing, and you never speak of it, and you never put it down.

Junie appeared at the rail beside him, quiet as she always was, watching the storm build with an expression I recognized. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. Kids who grew up on fishing boats know weather in their bones before they know it in their heads, and whatever else her uncle had given her, he'd given her that.

"It's going to miss us, right?" she asked.

I looked at the sky. I read it the way I've read it ten thousand times—the cloud streets, the pressure of the light, the way the swell was starting to come in long and wrong from the north, a swell with no local wind to explain it. That swell was a message from far away. Somewhere out there, a big engine of wind was turning, and it was sending its regards ahead of itself.

And here is the thing I did not say. Here is the thing I kept behind my teeth, where old sailors keep the true forecasts.

The system wasn't going to turn south. The swell told me it was tracking west, dead on our crossing line, and it was moving faster than the radio models guessed, and by tomorrow night we would either be through the worst of its path or square in the middle of it, and there was no third option, because turning back cost three days we didn't have and continuing cost only courage, which is the one commodity a crew like ours has in surplus.

Why didn't I say it? Because saying it changed nothing. Because Quentin had made her choice with open eyes and my job was to get this crew through whatever the choice cost. Because Junie was seventeen and scared enough already, and fear spreads on a small boat faster than bilge water. Because I am an old man who has learned that some truths are cargo you carry yourself.

So I said, "It's going to be rough tonight. Get your gear lashed down, and if you're prone to seasickness, eat light."

She nodded and went forward. Tomas lingered, looking at me with those accountant's eyes that missed less than people thought, and I saw him decide not to ask. Another carrier of unsaid things. We were going to get along fine.

* * *

Night came down gray and restless. I took the first watch alone, which is how I prefer it—just me and the wheel and the compass and the *Corvus* pushing west into a sea that was learning to be angry.

The phosphorescence was up, green fire in our wake, and every so often the bow wave would throw sparks like the sea was striking matches. Behind us, the lightning had started—silent from this distance, just a strobing glow along the southern horizon, a city burning somewhere in the clouds. The barometer had dropped another six millibars. My knees ached, which they do when the pressure falls fast, an old man's barometer more honest than any instrument aboard.

At midnight, Quentin came up. She couldn't sleep—I'd have been more surprised if she could—and she stood beside me in the wheelhouse, watching the lightning count itself out on the horizon.

"How bad?" she asked. She didn't dress it up. I liked that about her. Everyone else on this boat asked questions hoping for comfort. Quentin asked questions hoping for truth, even when she suspected the truth was ugly.

"Bad enough," I said. "It's turned west. It's coming across our track faster than they said."

"How much faster?"

"Fast enough that we'll know by tomorrow night whether we beat it."

She absorbed that. I watched her do the arithmetic I'd done at dawn—the fuel, the distance, the margin, all of it narrowing to a single channel with rocks on both sides.

"You knew this morning," she said. Not accusing. Just knowing.

"I suspected. Suspecting and knowing are different animals. This morning I had a suspicion with a swell in it. Now I have knowledge with a barometer in it."

"And you didn't say."

"No," I agreed. "I didn't say."

She was quiet a long moment. The *Corvus* rolled through a long trough, and the rigging sang that low note it sings, the one that sounds like a ship thinking.

"Why not?"

"Because you'd already chosen," I said. "And because a crew needs one person who isn't afraid, and I'm old enough to play that part convincingly. And because—" I gestured at the darkness astern, where Darwin was already a rumor, and ahead, where the Coral Triangle waited with its dead ship and its dead men's papers. "Because waiting costs money we don't have, and the truth costs something worse. It costs the choice. And you'd already paid it."

Quentin looked at me for a long time. Lightning lit her face in stutters, and I saw something there I couldn't name—anger, maybe, or gratitude, or the strange respect one stubborn soul pays another.

"Next time," she said finally, "you tell me. Even if it changes nothing. Even if it costs the choice. I'd rather walk into hell informed than walk into heaven surprised."

"Aye," I said. "That's fair."

"And Sef?" She turned back to the window, to the black water rushing past. "Thank you for playing the part. But don't ever mistake me for someone who needs protecting from the truth. I've lived inside storms. I know what they sound like from the middle."

She went below, and I stood my watch alone again, and the *Corvus* ran west into the dark with a cyclone on our heels and a promise in our hold, and I thought about Edda Kwan, whom I'd never met, dying in a hospice bed in Broome, trading her life's savings for a stranger's word. Bring them home alive. Tell no one what you find.

Old woman, I thought to the darkness, I hope you knew what you were buying. Because I've sailed a lot of cargoes in forty years—copra, contraband, refugees, rice—but I have never once carried anything heavier than a promise.

The wind backed another point. The swell came longer. And somewhere out ahead of us, in the deep water between here and the Triangle, the sea was getting ready to ask us all what we were willing to pay.

I put the wheel over two degrees and held our course, and I said nothing.
