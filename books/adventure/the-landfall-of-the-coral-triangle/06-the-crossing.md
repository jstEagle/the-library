## 6. The Crossing

I have spent eleven years mapping reefs, which means I have spent eleven years learning to read things that are trying to kill you. A reef doesn't lie. It shows you exactly what it is—every bommie, every drop-off, every patch of fire coral waiting to ruin your week—if you're patient enough to look. People are harder. People show you a nice sandy lagoon and keep the wall of teeth hidden until you're already swimming over it.

So on the third day out of Darwin, alone at the bow of the *Corvus* with the Arafura Sea rolling gray-green beneath us, I did what I always do. I started making a chart.

Not on paper. In my head, the way I hold every reef I've ever dived: shapes, hazards, depths, the places where the bottom falls away without warning.

The vessel first, because a vessel is just a reef that moves. The *Corvus* was forty years old if she was a day, a trawler that had clearly been fished hard and put away wet more times than anyone wanted to count. Tomas had fallen in love with her engine room on sight, which told me he was either a genius or a masochist, and after watching him coax two extra knots out of a fuel pump held together with hose clamps and prayer, I'd settled on both. Quentin Oyelaran owned her—or owed on her, which in my experience amounted to the same thing—and steered her like a woman arguing with an old friend who kept bringing up embarrassing history.

Then the crew.

Quentin herself was the easiest to sound. Storm chaser. Debtor. That particular flat calm some people get when they've been frightened for so long it stops registering as fear and starts registering as weather. She stood watches like she was paying off a mortgage one hour at a time. She ate standing up. When Junie talked too much—which was always—Quentin's mouth did this thing, a twitch at one corner, not quite a smile, not quite disapproval. I logged it as uncharted water. Something down there, but no depth readings yet.

Tomas Reyes mapped himself early and honestly, the way a good chart does. Ex-Voss accountant, he said it himself, second day, unprompted, over reconstituted eggs. "They'll tell you I cooked books for them. True. They'll tell you I got out before they could cook me. Also true." Then he went back to his engine. A man who volunteers his worst feature before you find it is either very brave or very smart about hiding the actual worst feature. With accountants, I've learned, it's usually the second one. But his hands were steady and his engine ran, so I marked him: hazard known, navigable with care.

Old Sef refused mapping entirely, which is its own kind of reading. He was sixty if he was fifty, cooked like the sea owed him money, and navigated by methods he declined to explain—"the sky told me" being apparently a complete sentence in his cosmology. On our second night he changed course by four degrees without saying anything to anybody. By morning we'd dodged a squall cell that Quentin, checking her instruments, went quiet about. Sef caught me watching him and said only: "Reefs and weather are the same thing, girl. Both of them tell you everything if you shut up." I decided I liked him enormously and would never say so.

And then there was Junie.

Junie was the hardest sounding of all, because she was shallow everywhere except where she wasn't. Seventeen, maybe. Runaway—I'd guessed that before Quentin confirmed it, from the way the kid flinched when Quentin raised her voice about a fouled line, the way she catalogued exits in every room, the way she ate. Fast, hunched over the plate, like food might be confiscated. I know that eating style. I grew up next to it. My mother fed half the runaway kids in our neighborhood for exactly that reason, and I learned young what hunger does to posture.

Junie also knew boats—not trawler work, but small craft, fishing boats, the kind of knowledge you absorb through your hands before you're old enough to have opinions. And she asked questions. Endless questions. About diving, about charts, about the Coral Triangle, about why the wreck we were hunting might matter to anyone. Half of it was teenage restlessness. But some of it, I noticed, she aimed carefully—at me, mostly—and then watched my answers the way I watch a new reef for current direction.

The kid was mapping us back.

Good, I thought. Company.

* * *

The rituals assembled themselves the way coral builds a reef—accidentally, incrementally, out of material nobody planned to use.

It started with the watches. Quentin divided them into pairs, and the pairings rotated, which meant that within four days everyone had spent six hours alone with everyone else, which is the fastest way I know to convert strangers into acquaintances or enemies, depending. I drew Sef twice. First time, he taught me three card games and cheated at all of them, openly, daring me to object. Second time, he said nothing for five hours and it was somehow the best conversation I'd had in years.

"You dive long?" he asked around hour four, apropos of nothing, the sky black above us and the running lights smearing green across the swells.

"Eleven years. Survey work, mostly. Before that, salvage, briefly. Badly."

"Mm." He adjusted nothing on the wheel, because the wheel didn't need adjusting; he touched it anyway, the way old men touch things. "Badly how?"

"I took a job recovering something from thirty meters without doing my decompression math right." I flexed my left hand, which still ached in cold weather, a souvenir of the bends that had decided to be generous and take only the nerve instead of the arm. "Learned the math forever after."

"And now you map reefs."

"Now I map reefs."

He nodded as if this were a complete and satisfactory life story, and maybe it was. That was Sef's whole method, I came to realize. He let people be exactly as deep as they wanted to be and didn't pretend the shallows were the whole story.

With Tomas, my watch produced different cargo. He talked when he was nervous, and he was nervous roughly always, so he talked constantly, in a low accountant's murmur, about everything except the Voss Syndicate, which meant he was thinking about nothing else.

"—so the injector pump's a 1980s Yanmar pattern, which sounds terrible, except it means every fishing village between here and Ambon has parts for it, which is why I picked her," he was saying, gesturing at the engine housing below even though we were on deck. "You don't buy the boat that works. You buy the boat that can be fixed anywhere."

"That's either wisdom or trauma," I said.

"In my experience those arrive in the same envelope."

I laughed before I could stop myself. He looked pleased, then embarrassed about being pleased. Accountant. I updated the chart: *Tomas Reyes — runs from things by fixing them.*

Quentin I got alone once, on the dawn watch, and it went differently. She stood at the wheel with coffee she wasn't drinking, and for a while neither of us spoke, which was fine, which was actually good, two professionals sharing silence. Then, out of nowhere:

"Priya. Your contract—it's day rate plus completion bonus?"

"Standard expedition terms. Why?"

"No reason. Just—" she turned the phrase over like a stone she suspected had a crab under it. "When we make landfall, whatever happens, nobody on this boat goes unpaid. I want that understood."

"It's understood," I said, because it was true, and also because the way she said *whatever happens* deserved to be left alone. Some reef features you note and swim past. You don't go poking.

* * *

The card games started night two and became constitutionally mandatory by night four. Tomas brought a deck missing the queen of hearts, which he claimed to have lost and which Junie insisted he'd lost *on purpose* for luck, and the games adapted around the absence the way fish adapt around a wreck—the gap became part of the structure. Old Sef cheated. Openly, cheerfully, magnificently. Once I caught him palming cards with hands like driftwood and he looked me dead in the eye and said, "Life's unfair, girl. Game should be too." Nobody objected. It became, in a way none of us discussed, a test of observation: if you couldn't catch Sef cheating, you weren't paying attention, and if you weren't paying attention at cards you shouldn't be on watch.

Junie was terrible at cards and magnificent at everything else. She could splice line better than Quentin. She could name every fitting on deck. She memorized the galley roster after one look. But put a hand of seven in front of her and she'd bet like someone who believed losing was the natural order and winning was a clerical error soon to be corrected.

"Where'd you learn boats?" I asked her one night, after she'd correctly identified a fouling problem in the winch before Tomas did.

She shrugged with her whole body, a teenager's shrug, designed to repel boarders. "Around."

"Your uncle fished?" I said it lightly, testing depth, and immediately regretted it, because her face did the thing—shuttered, all the light going behind glass. "Sorry. None of my business."

"No, it's—" She picked at the cards. Laid down a discard that made no sense. "He had a boat. I used to crew for him. Before." The *before* had walls around it, high ones. "He wasn't bad all the time," she added, to the cards, which was the saddest sentence I'd heard in years, because I recognized it. Everyone who's survived someone says that. *They weren't bad all the time.* As if the good hours were supposed to offset the others, like a ledger.

"Tomas says you buy the boat that can be fixed anywhere," I said instead.

She looked up, startled into almost-smiling. "That's the weirdest non-sequitur anyone's ever—"

"He's right, though. About boats and about everything." I gathered the cards for the next deal. "For the record: nobody on this vessel reports anyone to anyone. Quentin made that rule before we cleared the harbor. You're crew, not cargo."

Junie stared at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't fully chart—something between suspicion and a thirst so old it had forgotten it was thirsty.

"Cool," she said finally, in the universal teenage dialect for *that mattered enormously and I will die before admitting it.*

Progress. I logged it.

* * *

The nightmares found her on night five.

I heard it first because my bunk was aft, near hers—a sound like an animal caught somewhere, muffled, urgent. Then Quentin's footsteps overhead, quick, and a pause at the hatch, and then not going down. I lay in the dark and listened to Quentin stand outside the compartment for a full minute, deciding, before she knocked softly and said, "Junie. It's Q. You're on the *Corvus.* We're two days out of Darwin. Nobody's here but us." Just that, repeated twice, calm as a lighthouse. Then her footsteps receding, deliberately loud enough to be heard leaving.

The next morning Junie avoided everyone until lunch, when Sef set a bowl in front of her and said, "Night terrors run in crews. Had 'em myself till '98. Sea stops eventually." And that was the entire discussion, because Sef understood that some things heal only when nobody looks directly at them.

But it changed the ship's weather, subtly. After that, people started sleeping with their cabin doors cracked. Nobody announced it. Tomas started leaving the galley light on low overnight—"for the chart table," he said, unconvincingly, since the chart table was nowhere near the galley. I found myself keeping my watch reports a little warmer, a little more detailed, telling Junie about the seabirds tracking us, the water temperature climbing as we came north, the first faint changes that meant the Coral Triangle was approaching. And Junie started sitting closer to the circle at cards. Not in it, quite. Near it. Near counts.

On night eight she had the nightmare again, and this time I heard something new: footsteps going *toward* her hatch before Quentin's even moved. Sef's, slow and deliberate, stopping just outside, and his voice, low: "Corvus. Two days out of Darwin. Nobody here but us." The words passed along like a relay, like a ritual acquiring liturgy. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Sleep, little fish. The reef holds."

I stared at the overhead in the dark and felt something in my chest do a thing I hadn't authorized.

* * *

By the tenth day I had to admit the chart had gone wrong.

Not wrong, exactly. Incomplete in a way that suggested my methodology was flawed. Because here was the thing about reefs: you finish mapping them. There's a last transect, a last data point, and then the reef is a known quantity forever, filed and done. This—whatever was accreting aboard the *Corvus*—showed no sign of closing. Every day it added structure. Sef teaching Junie celestial nav with a sextant older than both of them combined, Junie pretending not to care and then hitting the sun shot dead-on twice. Tomas rigging a freshwater shower from the engine's heat exchanger and acting like it was no big deal while everyone acted like it was Christmas. Quentin, on the twelfth morning, laughing. Actually laughing, at something stupid Junie said about flying fish, a real laugh with weight in it, and then looking startled at herself, like she'd found a door in a wall she thought was solid.

I sat on the forepeak that evening with my actual charts spread out, plotting tomorrow's positions, and thought about Edda Kwan, the dying prospector whose map was folded in Quentin's cabin. I'd never met her. Everything I knew was secondhand: an old woman trading a lifetime's obsession for a promise. Bring whoever sails it home alive. Tell no one what they find.

Home alive. I turned the phrase over. Eleven days ago it would have sounded like sentiment. Now I looked aft at the glow of the galley, where Sef was teaching Junie to make bread and Tomas was narrating yeast behavior like a quarterly report, and I thought: *oh.* The promise wasn't about survival. It was about this. Whatever this was that kept building, layer on layer, without anyone authorizing construction.

People aren't reefs, I wrote in my logbook that night, because I am the kind of person who writes things in logbooks. *Reefs you finish. Crews you don't.*

* * *

Day thirteen. The water had gone that deep luminous blue that means real ocean, real depth, the Coral Triangle pulling us north like a tide. Landfall in two days, maybe three. The mood aboard was buoyant—too buoyant, I'd have said, if anyone had asked, and nobody asked, because we were all busy being optimistic.

I was on afternoon watch with Quentin when the radio crackled.

Not the weather band. Not the fishing chatter we'd been半-listening to for days. A clean, disciplined voice, clipped and official, calling a vessel by name.

"*Fishing vessel Corvus, fishing vessel Corvus, this is patrol vessel Vosper-2, calling on channel one-six. We are conducting routine customs operations in this area. Request you confirm position, port of origin, and nature of cargo. Over.*"

Quentin went very still. Not frozen—still the way water goes still over a deep spot. I watched her hand hover near the transmit switch, and I watched her face assemble itself into smoothness, brick by brick, in about a second and a half.

"Priya," she said pleasantly, eyes on the horizon, "how's our chart looking ahead?"

I understood the assignment. "Clear," I said, matching her tone. "Nothing out here but us."

She keyed the mic.

Somewhere behind us, I heard Junie stop mid-sentence, and Sef's knife pause on the cutting board, and the whole ship go quiet as a held breath, listening to Quentin Oyelaran lie to the sea.

Her voice, when it came, was warm, bored, cooperative—the voice of a woman with absolutely nothing to hide.

And I thought, updating my chart one final time: *Quentin Oyelaran — depth unknown. Current strong. Something big moving underneath.*
