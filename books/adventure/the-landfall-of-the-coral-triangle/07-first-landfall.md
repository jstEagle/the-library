## 7. First Landfall

The atoll came up out of the sea the way secrets do—gradually, then all at once.

I was on bow watch, which was a joke of an assignment because I didn't know a bow from a stern three weeks ago, but Quentin had put me there anyway. "You've got young eyes," she said, which was generous, since my eyes had spent the last six days mostly red from crying in the head at three in the morning and pretending I hadn't. "Watch for white water. Reef shows itself before land does."

And she'd been right. A line of foam stitched itself across the horizon like something sewn by a very nervous hand, and then behind it, green. Actual green. After a week of nothing but the blue-gray argument between sky and water, that green hit me somewhere I hadn't expected. I gripped the rail and didn't say anything, because I'd learned on this boat that if you let yourself feel a thing out loud, Old Sef would appear beside you and hand you tea, and then you'd have to have a whole conversation about it.

"Land ho," I said anyway, because I figured that was the law.

"Land ho," Quentin repeated from the wheel, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not relief exactly. More like a person who'd been holding her breath since Darwin finally letting a little of it out, but carefully, the way you'd let air out of a tire you suspected was already damaged.

* * *

The atoll had no name on our charts. Old Sef had three names for it, all from different eras and at least one of them possibly invented. "The pearlers called it the Tooth," he said, squinting at the lagoon through the gap where our mast used to hold sails we couldn't afford. "The Japanese charts call it something I can't pronounce. And I call it Tuesday, because that's the day I first anchored here, in 1987, and I was so happy to see it I named it after the best day of the week."

"You're a sentimental old man," said Tomas, who was elbow-deep in the engine compartment and had been for most of the crossing, talking to the engine the way some people talk to horses.

"I'm a *prepared* old man. There's a difference, and the difference is I brought fresh water."

The Corvus slid through the reef pass with Sef conning from the bow and Quentin at the wheel, and the whole crew went quiet the way people do in church, except I wouldn't know, I'd only been to a church once, when I was nine, and Uncle Ray had taken me there to make a show of being respectable before going home and doing the thing that made respectable a costume. So maybe quiet like that. Quiet like a place where something bigger than you might be listening.

The lagoon was the color I'd only ever seen in the fake postcards at the Darwin servo, the ones with the hula girls and the sharks with teeth like a picket fence, except it was real, and it was better, and it was ours for now. Fish moved under the hull in silver schools that broke apart and reformed like they were one animal deciding to be many. The water was so clear it felt like cheating, like looking through glass that didn't exist.

I stood at the rail and thought: *I could disappear here.* That was my whole skill, disappearing. I'd done it from Port Hedland with forty-one dollars and my uncle's spare phone SIM, and I'd done it on the Corvus by being small and useful and never asking questions about anyone else's past, because I'd noticed early that everyone on this boat had a past they weren't asking questions about. That was the deal none of us had spoken aloud. We were all fugitives from something, and the truce was silence.

Except the lagoon ruined it. Because you can't disappear into a place this beautiful. It looks back at you.

* * *

It happened at dinner, which was Old Sef's fish curry, which was the first meal I'd had on this boat that I didn't eat standing up, braced for it to be taken away.

Quentin had the chart spread on the table, weighted with a tin of condensed milk and Priya's dive slate, and she was tracing the lagoon's outline with her finger. "Wreck should be on the northern shoulder, in about thirty meters, according to our benefactor's marks."

"Our benefactor," Priya said, "who we know nothing about."

"Who paid for this expedition," Quentin said, "which makes her our employer, and you don't audit your employer, Priya, you invoice her."

"I *was* an auditor. That's how I know everyone lies."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and then everyone looked at me, and I felt my face do the thing it does when it's been caught being a person. Six days I'd managed to be furniture. Good furniture—furniture that scrubbed the head and coiled line without being asked—but furniture.

"You've been quiet," Quentin said. Not accusing. Worse. *Noticing.*

"Just thinking."

"Dangerous habit on this boat. Tomas thinks and now the engine only runs if you apologize to it first."

"It runs fine," Tomas said, without looking up from his plate. "It runs *differently* now."

Everyone laughed, and I laughed too, and then the laugh caught on something in my chest and came out wrong, and Priya—because she missed nothing, because she'd spent a week mapping all of us the way she mapped reefs, quietly, from a respectful distance, noting the hazards—set down her fork and said, "Junie. How old are you?"

"Seventeen," I said. "Nearly."

"*Nearly*," Priya repeated, and I watched her do the math I'd been hoping nobody would do. Seventeen and nearly meant seventeen and not, which meant, in the eyes of every authority from here to Perth, a minor. A missing person. Somebody's daughter, somebody's niece, somebody's *reported absence*.

"I'm not anyone's," I said, too fast, too loud. The words came out like a dropped plate. "Nobody's looking for me. Nobody's going to come looking, I promise, I'm not— I'm not a problem. I work. I don't take up space. You can check with—"

"With who?" Priya asked gently, and that gentleness was the worst part, because I'd spent a year learning every tone my uncle's voice could make, and gentle was the one that came right before the door locked.

I stood up. I don't even remember deciding to. My body just did what it had practiced for a year, which was leave before the leaving got taken away from me.

I made it as far as the rail.

Quentin didn't follow me. That's the thing I keep coming back to. She stayed at the table, and I stood at the rail with my knuckles white on the metal, watching fish, waiting for the speech, the *you have to understand our position*, the *it's not personal, it's the law*, all the sentences adults had ever said to me right before handing me back.

Instead she said, at normal volume, to the whole table, "Anyone here planning to report Junie to anyone?"

Silence.

"Anyone here *able* to? We're in international waters on an unlicensed trawler with forged safety certifications and a debt to a syndicate. The moment any of us contacts an authority, we all go down, and I don't mean emotionally." A pause. "Junie. Sit down and finish your curry. It's the best thing on this boat and Sef gets wounded."

"It's true," Sef said. "I sulk for days."

I sat down. I finished the curry. I cried a little into it, quietly, the way I'd learned, except Priya passed me the water glass without comment and Sef put a second piece of flatbread on my plate like nothing was happening, and nobody said anything about the crying, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had ever done for me. They just let it be weather. Something that passed through and didn't change the shape of the day.

Later, Quentin found me coiling line on deck, because I'd discovered that when my head got loud, rope helped. Rope only wants one thing from you and it's a thing you can actually give.

"For the record," she said, leaning on the rail beside me, not looking at me, which I understood was a gift, "I'm not your mother, I'm not your rescuer, and I'm not running a charity. You work, you earn your berth, that's the whole arrangement."

"Okay."

"But nobody gets handed back on my boat. Nobody gets sent anywhere they're running from. That's not charity either. That's just—" she searched for the word, and I watched a woman who navigated by numbers fail to find one, and land on it anyway— "that's just the rule. The only rule."

I nodded and kept coiling, and if my eyes were wet it was the salt spray, and if she noticed, she had the grace of a good captain, which is to say she looked at the horizon and let the sea take the blame for both of us.

* * *

The dive was the next morning, and I wasn't supposed to go down. I was surface cover, which meant I sat in the tender above the site, fed out Priya's line, and watched the lagoon do its impossible blue thing while my whole chest ached with wanting to be under there instead of on top of it.

Priya went first, then Quentin. Tomas stayed on the Corvus, arguing with the compressor. Sef smoked his pipe and read the sky, which he did constantly, the way other men checked their phones.

"Thirty meters," Sef said, to no one. "Good holding ground. Good visibility." Then, quieter, to the sky more than to me: "Too good."

I didn't ask what he meant. I'd learned that from the crew. You let the old man's sentences finish landing when they land.

They were down forty minutes. When they came up, Quentin broke the surface first and treaded water, and even from the tender I could see her face had changed. She wasn't smiling. She was doing the thing she did with charts, the rapid-eye calculation thing, except there was nothing to calculate, so what was she—

"It's real," she called up, and her voice cracked open on *real* like a coconut. "Junie, it's *real*. She wasn't lying. A barque, pre-war, hull's intact, cargo hold's— there's *stuff* down there. Cases, crates, a whole—"

"Ledgers," Priya said, pulling her mask off. Her voice was flat in a way I'd learned to be afraid of, because flat was what people got when the feeling was too big to have a shape. "Ship's papers. A strongroom. Quentin, there's a strongroom, and it's *open*."

"Empty?"

"Empty. But the door was opened from the inside, and recently. The hinges are bright. No growth."

Sef had gone very still, pipe halfway to his mouth.

"And there's this," Priya said, and held up her hand.

Even at the distance, I knew what it was, because I'd seen a dozen of them rusting on the jetty at Port Hedland. An anchor. Not the great bird-beaked thing a barque that age would have carried. A modern one. Danforth-pattern, galvanized steel, the flukes still bright under the film of the sea.

"You're saying someone else anchored here," Tomas said, from the Corvus's rail.

"I'm saying someone else *moored* here." Priya swam to the tender's ladder and started up, water sheeting off her. "Three of them, set in a line over the wreck. Big ones, with chain. That's not a fishing boat having a bad day. That's a work site. Somebody's been working this wreck, and not long ago—the chain scar on the coral's still pink."

Nobody said anything for a second. The lagoon kept being beautiful, which felt suddenly like a lie it was telling on purpose.

"Voss?" I said.

The word came out of me before I thought, and everyone turned, and I shrugged, defensive. "I listen. It's not a crime. You all talk like I'm furniture, and furniture hears things. Quentin talks to Sef about the syndicate when she thinks I'm asleep. Debt. Collection notices. A woman named Mara Iles who smiles too much."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for the anger, the *you don't get to know my business*, the whole familiar litany. Instead she said, "Furniture hears things," like she was filing it away, like maybe it was useful, like maybe I was useful in a way that had nothing to do with scrubbing the head.

"Voss is a money problem," she said finally. "Money problems I know how to survive. This—" she nodded at the anchor in Priya's fist— "this is a *who* problem. And who problems are the ones that get people buried at sea."

"So we leave," Tomas said.

"So we *think*," Quentin said. "We think, and we dive again, and we find out what's in those crates before we decide what kind of trouble we're standing in. Edda Kwan paid for this expedition, and I made her a promise, and I don't break promises to dying women. Even the stupid ones."

"That's most of them," Sef said, around his pipe. "The promises to dying women. Stupid ones, brave ones, they're all the same shape. You just find out which too late."

He was looking at the northern shoulder of the lagoon, where the water darkened over the wreck, and I followed his eyes down through all that impossible blue to the thing lying on the bottom that we'd crossed a sea to find, and I felt the wonder of the morning curdle slowly into something else. Something with teeth.

Because here's what I knew that they were only starting to learn: bright hinges and pink coral scars and galvanized anchors don't belong to the dead. The dead don't need moorings. Somebody had come back to this wreck—recently, deliberately, in workmanlike fashion—and somebody had opened the strongroom from the inside, and then, for reasons I didn't yet understand but already dreaded, had left it open.

Like a door someone means to come back through.

Quentin was already pulling the chart toward her, already calculating, and Priya was already writing on her slate, and Sef was already reading the sky, and I sat in the tender with the anchor's cold twin image in my head and thought about my uncle, and how the worst thing he ever did was never the loud thing. It was the quiet thing. The waiting thing. The way he'd leave a door open that he knew I'd walk through, and be standing on the other side of it when I did.

Somebody had left this wreck open like that.

And I had the awful, certain feeling that when we went back down tomorrow, we'd find out who was standing on the other side.
