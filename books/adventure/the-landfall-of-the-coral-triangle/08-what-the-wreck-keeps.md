## 8. What the Wreck Keeps

There are three kinds of numbers in the world, and I spent eleven years learning to tell them apart.

The first kind are honest numbers. Fuel gauges, tide tables, the count of rivets left in a corroded hull. They don't care about you. They simply are, and there's a strange comfort in that—the way there's comfort in a dog that bites everyone equally.

The second kind are hopeful numbers. Projections, forecasts, the figures a man writes down when he wants to believe something. I used to produce those by the thousand at Voss Maritime Holdings, floor nine, Darwin. A ship too old to insure? Adjust the depreciation schedule until it isn't. A cargo manifest that doesn't match the hold? The hold is wrong, then. Numbers bend for whoever holds the pen.

The third kind are the numbers nobody writes down because writing them down would be a confession.

I was thinking about all this because I was elbow-deep in a silted iron box at the bottom of a dead ship's hold, forty meters under the Coral Triangle, and my dive light had just found paper.

Paper. After—by Edda Kwan's chart's best guess—sixty years underwater.

Not possible, said the part of my brain that had audited impossibility for a living. And yet: a tin document case, soldered shut, tarred over, wedged behind a collapsed bulkhead like something somebody had wanted very badly to survive.

* * *

Priya found me staring at it.

We were on the second dive of the day, working the hold in pairs while Quentin kept watch topside with the radio turned low, paranoid as a cat in a room full of rocking chairs. Priya hovered at my shoulder, her bubbles steady, her hands still. She'd been a reef surveyor before she owed someone money—I'd never asked who, and she'd never offered—but she read wrecks the way I used to read balance sheets: looking for what didn't add up.

She tapped my arm and pointed at the case. Then at me. Then made the gesture we'd all learned to dread: *surface. Now.*

I shook my head and held up one finger. One more minute. She narrowed her eyes behind her mask, and I could feel the argument she wasn't having through forty meters of water pressure, and then she took the case from me—gently, the way you handle anything that might be load-bearing—and jerked her thumb upward with the finality of a woman closing a ledger.

Fine. We surfaced.

* * *

The thing about the Corvus was that it had exactly one table, and everything important happened on it. Meals, arguments, Junie's homework (Quentin was teaching her long division out of some misplaced guilt; Junie was teaching her card sharping out of pure opportunism), and now, an open tin case from a sixty-year-old wreck, surrounded by four people who had all stopped pretending they weren't interested.

Old Sef pretended hardest. He sat at the far end mending a net he did not need mended, eyes down, but his hands had gone slow, which is how Sef listened.

"Tarred and soldered," Priya said. She'd cut the seal with a dive knife, careful as surgery. "Somebody wanted this to last."

"Somebody wanted this to be *found*," Tomas said, which was when I remembered I was supposed to be narrating my own life instead of watching it. "You don't waterproof a case and hide it behind a bulkhead unless you're leaving it for whoever comes next. Question is whether 'whoever comes next' was meant to be a friend or a prosecutor."

"And the difference matters?" Junie asked. She was seventeen and had the moral architecture of a cat burglar, which is to say: sound foundations, unconventional layout.

"The difference," I said, "is whether opening this makes us witnesses or accomplices."

Quentin looked at me across the table. She had that expression she got when she was doing arithmetic she hated—distances, mostly, though lately I suspected time had started creeping into her sums. "Open it, Tomas."

So I did.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff as bark: ledgers. Three of them. Ship's logs, actually—or fragments of them, along with correspondence, insurance schedules, and a stack of letters bound with wire that had rusted to the color of dried blood. The paper was dark but intact. Saltwater is patient, but tar is more patient still.

And the handwriting—

I want to be precise here, because precision was my whole career, and because what happened next happened in the space between one number and the next.

The top ledger was written in a hand I knew. Not the person—I never met him, or her, whoever kept the books aboard this ship in its last year. But the *hand*. The particular forward slant. The way the numerals 4 and 7 were crossed with two strokes instead of one. The habit of underlining totals twice.

It was the house style of Voss Maritime Holdings. Floor nine. They trained it into you in your first week—neat figures, double-underlined totals, no corrections visible. A ledger should look like it has nothing to hide. That was practically the company motto, right under the actual motto, which was *Discretion Is Our Hull.*

"Say something, Tomas," Quentin said quietly.

I laid the ledger flat. Turned pages with a pencil because you don't touch old paper with fingers that have been holding a dive knife. Cargo manifests. Insurance valuations revised downward, then sharply upward, then claims filed. Dates. Signatures. And in the margins, in that same tidy hand, small annotations:

*Hull value adjusted per Mr. V.'s instruction.*
*Second survey waived — see attached letter.*
*Total loss confirmed. Payout released.*

Ship after ship. Decade after decade—the ledgers spanned three different vessels' papers, three eras of ink. Old ships, overloaded, under-crewed, sent into weather their surveys said they couldn't survive, insured to values no honest actuary would sign. Sinkings-for-insurance. It wasn't even sophisticated. That was the worst part. It was arithmetic. Men do not drown in arithmetic, so men don't fear it.

Then Junie, reading upside down as was her nature, went very still and said, "This one. This ship here. *Kestrel Star.*"

Sef's needle stopped.

"What about it?" Quentin asked.

Junie turned the ledger around and slid it across the table to where Sef sat, and I watched an old man read a name he already knew, because his hands told me before his face did—they'd gone completely still, both of them, resting on the net like birds that had decided not to exist.

"My grandmother's boat," Sef said finally. His voice was level in the way water is level right before it goes over the edge. "Nineteen sixty-one. Official finding was navigational error. My mother never believed it. Never forgave the sea for it, either." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "Sea didn't do it."

Nobody spoke. The Corvus creaked around us, filling the silence with the sounds of a ship that had, so far, declined to join the list.

"It's Edda's family," Priya said softly. "Kestrel Star. That's why the chart. That's why the promise." She looked at me. "Tomas. You said you recognized the handwriting. What does that mean, exactly?"

What it meant, exactly, was that I had spent eleven years keeping books for the machine that killed her family, and that somewhere in those eleven years I had probably transcribed, summarized, or filed away the descendants of these very numbers. That the sinking of the Kestrel Star was, somewhere in the Voss archives, a closed file with a neat double-underlined total.

"That hand," I said, "is taught. On floor nine. In Darwin. To every junior accountant Voss hires, including—" I paused, because honesty was the one currency I'd sworn I'd stop counterfeiting when I walked out with my severance and my shame, "—including me. These ledgers weren't kept by a whistleblower hiding them. They were kept by the company. Somebody aboard was doing the books properly, right up until the ship went down, and somebody else put the record in a tin and tarred it shut. Maybe the same person. Maybe a clerk with a conscience and thirty seconds and nowhere to run."

"So it's proof," Quentin said.

"It's better than proof. Proof can be argued with. This is *pattern*. Three ships, thirty years, same hand-style, same margin notes, same signature block. You could burn any one page and the rest would testify." I heard my own voice go flat and professional and hated it. "I could build you a case off these that would take a team of forensic accountants two years to pull apart, and they wouldn't manage it."

"Could you build a case against them?" Junie asked, bright-eyed, already three moves ahead, bless her.

"I could build a case that would get every one of us drowned before filing," I said. "Yes."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was standard, but the flavor of it was new.

I went up on deck because the hold of the Corvus felt suddenly too much like the hold I'd been diving, and found Quentin at the wheel station with a chart spread out and no course drawn on it. Just looking. She did that—stood over charts the way other people stood over graves.

"You knew," she said, without turning around. "When you saw the handwriting. Before you said anything, your face did something."

"My face is a poorly maintained vessel."

"Your face told me we were in trouble before your mouth agreed to." She turned. Moonlight made her look younger and tireder simultaneously, which I believe is the moon's specialty. "Walk me through it. Accountant to—whatever I am to you. Debtor?"

"Crew," I said, and was surprised to find I meant it.

Something moved through her expression like weather. "Crew. All right. Walk me through it anyway."

So I did. The structure of it. How a syndicate like Voss doesn't think of itself as criminal; it thinks of itself as *liquid*. Ships are assets, crews are costs, and a total loss is simply a conversion of one into the other at favorable rates. The ledgers below us weren't an aberration. They were a *method*, refined over decades, and the only reason it had never surfaced was that every witness was either complicit, bought, or at the bottom of the sea holding a tin case.

"Edda Kwan spent thirty years buying this expedition," I said. "Her savings, her map, her promise extracted out of you in a hospice bed. And what she bought—" I gestured at the deck beneath our feet, at the sealed case now locked in the chain locker under Sef's personal padlock, "—is the death certificate of everyone who ever signed one of these margins. Including whoever wrote the Kestrel Star entry."

"Including her own family's killers."

"Including them. And here's the part that's going to keep you up tonight, skipper, since it's already got me: the promise you made her was to tell *no one* what we found. Her exact words, if the crew's gossip is accurate. Silence. But Edda Kwan didn't spend thirty years and every cent she had to purchase silence. Nobody buys silence at that price. She bought *truth*, and she dressed it as silence because she was dying and afraid, maybe, that the truth would die with whoever carried it. Or—" and this was the thought I'd been circling all night like a gull over a fish school, "—or she knew exactly what she was asking, and the contradiction is the message."

Quentin was quiet a long moment. Below us, faintly, I could hear Junie laughing at something Priya had said, and Sef's voice rumbling underneath it, and the ordinary music of people who had, without anyone signing anything, become each other's problem and privilege.

"You're saying the promise might be a test," she said.

"I'm saying promises are assets," I said, "and like all assets, they appreciate or depreciate depending on what you do with them. Right now yours is depreciating. Every day we sit on this, it gets heavier and worth less."

"And if we surface it?"

"Then we stop being debtors and start being targets. There's a difference between owing the Voss Syndicate money and owing them *silence*, and the second debt doesn't have a number on it. No payoff schedule. No negotiated settlement." I looked at her. "They don't send collection notices for that one, Quentin. They send boats."

She looked back toward the dark water, where somewhere below lay a dead ship holding its secret in a soldered tin, and where somewhere beyond the reef, sooner or later, a live one would come asking questions.

"Get some sleep, Tomas," she said at last.

"You first."

"I will." She didn't move. Neither did I, and neither of us believed her, and that was fine. That was crew.

I went below past the chain locker and put my hand flat against the door, the way you check a fever. Inside, under Sef's padlock, three ledgers waited with their neat double-underlined totals, patient as arithmetic, heavy as the Kestrel Star.

Eleven years I'd kept books for men who converted people into payouts. I'd told myself I only recorded the numbers; I didn't make them.

But tomorrow, or the day after, the radio would crackle and a polite voice would ask about customs, and I understood—with the awful clarity of a man whose totals always balanced—that the question of what we owed had just changed denominations.

And debts like the one sitting in that locker don't collect interest.

They collect *people*.
