## 9. The Radio Call

The radio crackled to life at 0400, which is the hour the sea reserves for bad news. It has a sense of theater that way.

"Motor vessel Corvus, motor vessel Corvus, this is patrol vessel Kestrel on behalf of regional maritime authorities. You are being hailed for a routine customs inspection. Please respond."

Routine. In my experience, nothing conducted at four in the morning is routine. Weddings, maybe. Everything else is either an emergency or a pretext wearing an emergency's clothes.

I lay in my bunk for three full seconds and let the voice wash over me—clipped, neutral, faintly bored, the vocal equivalent of a manila folder. Then I swung out of bed, pulled on the shirt I'd left draped over the chair like a responsible captain who sleeps in shifts instead of a woman who'd been staring at the overhead since midnight doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

The arithmetic was simple. The Kestrel was Voss. Not because the voice said so—the voice said *customs*, and customs officers don't hail trawlers two hundred miles from anywhere at 0400—but because of the timing. We'd been at the wreck site six days. Six days was enough to find something. Mara Iles had told me, back in that office that smelled of coffee and money, that they kept loose tabs on anything Edda Kwan had ever touched. I'd thought it was a figure of speech. I was learning that with the Voss Syndicate, nothing is a figure of speech. Everything is a line item.

Tomas was already in the wheelhouse when I got there, which meant he'd heard it too, which meant he'd been awake, which meant we were all doing the same arithmetic in separate bunks like a fleet of insomniac accountants.

"You heard," he said.

"I heard."

"It's them."

"It's customs," I said, and put just enough dryness into it that he understood I knew he knew.

Priya appeared at the companionway with her hair tied back and her dive knife already on her belt, which was either habit or instinct, and with Priya the two had never once diverged. "Junie's awake," she said quietly. "She's scared."

"Tell her to be scared quietly and stay below."

"That'll work great."

It wouldn't. But there are lies you tell for morale, and I was apparently in the business now.

Old Sef came up last, unhurried as tide, carrying two mugs of coffee. He handed me one without asking if I wanted it, which is the correct way to hand someone coffee at 0400. He looked at the radar screen where the contact sat fourteen miles out, closing at a speed no fisheries inspector uses unless he's late for dinner.

"They've been shadowing us since yesterday," Sef said. "I saw their wake pattern at dusk. Thought it might be a longliner."

"And?"

"And longliners don't hold station overnight." He sipped his coffee. "You want me to say it plain?"

"No."

"Good. Because you already know it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lying: it's not hard. That's the horror of it. Somewhere along the way—I could map the exact years if I wanted to punish myself—I had become fluent in a language I never chose to learn. Debt teaches it. Debt is an immersion course. You start by saying *the check's in the mail* and within five years you can look a man in the eye and describe cargo that doesn't exist aboard a vessel you're standing on, and your pulse doesn't even climb. Your hands stay level on the rail. And some small cold part of you takes notes, files it away, and says: *look how good you've gotten.*

The Kestrel closed to half a mile and hove to off our port beam, floodlights sweeping our hull, and I picked up the handset.

"Corvus, this is Kestrel. Prepare to receive boarding party. Have your papers ready."

"Papers are ready," I said, warm as morning. "Fair warning, officer—we're a working boat and she's not pretty below decks. My cook will be mortified."

A pause. Then, lightly: "What's your cargo, Captain?"

"General stores out of Darwin, headed for the island trade. Fuel drums, rice, diesel parts, some building materials for a mission station." I let a smile into my voice; they can hear smiles over radio, I'm convinced of it. "You're welcome to count the rice. It's the most exciting thing aboard."

Another pause, longer. On the Kestrel's deck, shapes moved behind the floodlights. Counting them: six. Six men to inspect rice.

"We'll send across a boarding team," the voice said. "Please have all crew muster on deck with identification."

"All crew on deck," I repeated pleasantly. "One moment while I wake the rest."

I set down the handset and turned to my crew, gathered in the wheelhouse shadows, and here is what I actually felt: nothing in my face. That was the skill. Underneath, my stomach had gone to iron and my mind was running three columns—what they know, what they suspect, what they can prove—and the third column was empty, thank God, because Tomas had stowed the ledgers in the ballast tank two days ago wrapped in oilcloth and wax, sealed against water, invisible to any inspection that didn't involve cutting open my ship.

But the fourth column, the one I didn't say aloud, was: *what happens if they come back with a cutting torch.*

"Tomas," I said. "How watertight is that hiding place?"

"Against a flashlight? Perfect. Against a determined search?" He pushed his glasses up. "Quentin, I hid those ledgers using methods I learned from them. They taught me every trick because I used to audit the tricks. Which means they also know every trick, because they trained me." A beat. "There's no perfect hiding place from your own teachers."

"Then we need a second option. If they find them—if it goes wrong—those papers go over the side. All of them. Weighted."

Silence in the wheelhouse. Priya looked at me. Sef looked at me. Even Junie, who had materialized on the stairs despite explicit orders, looked at me, and hers was the worst look of all, because she was seventeen and believed I was the kind of person who kept promises cleanly.

"You mean destroy them," Priya said.

"I mean be ready to."

"They're proof. Thirty years of sinkings. Edda's family's ship is in those pages—"

"I know what's in those pages, Priya." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "I've read every page. Twice."

"Then how can you even say it?"

Because duty is a word with two faces, and nobody warns you. There's the duty you owe the dead—to the truth, to the record, to Edda Kwan dying in a hospice bed with thirty years of savings spent on a chart and a promise. And there's the duty you owe the living—the four people currently looking at me in a wheelhouse at 0430, whose survival I had signed for in a different room, in different ink. The dead do not drown. The living do. That was the whole of the mathematics, and I hated it, and I hated more that I could do the mathematics at all.

"Because if those men find those papers," I said, "they won't arrest us and write a report. They'll scuttle us with everything aboard and log it as an uninsured loss, which would be funny if it weren't their entire business model. Nobody surfaces the truth from the bottom of the sea, Priya. Not ever. Buried evidence protects no one. Drowned evidence protects us."

"And after?" Junie asked, small voice from the stairs. "After they leave. Do we still have to keep the promise? Tell no one?"

I looked at her and couldn't answer honestly, so I answered like a captain.

"After they leave, we sail home. One problem at a time."

* * *

The boarding party came across in a rigid inflatable, six men in matching dark gear with customs patches sewn on over what I'd have bet my remaining kidney were Voss insignia. Their leader was a broad man with a sunburnt neck and the patient eyes of someone who enjoys searches. He introduced himself as Inspector Dane. He did not offer a first name. Inspectors with first names get remembered; this one intended to be forgotten.

My crew mustered on deck as ordered, and I watched Dane read them the way you'd read a manifest. Old Sef, sixty-something, weathered, harmless-looking, the best disguise in maritime history. Priya, arms crossed, chin up, radiating exactly the amount of annoyance a legitimate cook-averse deckhand should feel at having her sleep interrupted. Junie, pale, jaw set, seventeen and terrified and hiding it behind the universal teenage armor of contempt. And Tomas, whose file, I happened to know, listed him as a former Voss Syndicate accountant—and whose name, I realized with a slow cold drop in my gut, might appear in certain internal records Dane had access to.

Dane's eyes passed over Tomas and stopped. Just for a moment. Half a second. The way a man's eyes stop on a familiar face in an unexpected place.

"Your engineer looks familiar," Dane said.

"He gets that a lot," I said breezily. "Very symmetrical face."

Nobody laughed. Dane smiled anyway, professionally, and clapped his hands. "Papers first. Then we'll have a look around."

I handed over the Corvus's registration, my license, the cargo manifest I'd drafted in Darwin listing rice, fuel, and building materials with the loving detail of a woman who had once, briefly, considered a career in fiction. Dane read it slowly. Behind him, his men split up—two toward the hold, one to the engine room, one lingering near the wheelhouse, and two simply standing watch on deck, which is what men do when they're not inspectors but security.

The search took ninety minutes. I know because I watched the sky lighten through all of it, the east going from tar to ash to a bruised pink, and the whole time I stood at the rail beside Sef making conversation about fishing grounds while inside my head a single sentence repeated like a pump running dry: *don't find it, don't find it, don't find it.*

They found rice. They found fuel drums. They found Junie's contraband paperback romance novels, which produced the only genuine moment of the morning when she went scarlet and Dane, human for one flickering instant, pretended not to see them. They found the ballast tank access hatch and shone lights into it and saw, as Tomas had promised, forty tons of seawater and iron and nothing else, because the oilcloth bundles were lashed beneath the floor plates in a cavity Tomas had built himself, welded himself, in a corner of the tank no survey diagram acknowledged.

And then Dane climbed back aboard the Corvus's deck, handed my papers back with a nod, and said, "You check out, Captain."

"Wonderful," I said. "Sorry about the rice excitement."

He grunted. His men reboarded the inflatable. And as the engine started, Dane paused, one boot on the tube, and looked back at me with those patient eyes.

"Rough waters ahead this season," he said. "Storms come up fast out here. A boat alone, far from help—" He shrugged. "Be careful, Captain."

"We always are," I said, and smiled, and both of us knew exactly what had just been said in both directions.

* * *

The Kestrel stood off for another hour before turning away, as if reluctant, as if hoping we'd do something interesting. Then it shrank to a smudge, then to nothing, and the radio went quiet, and the ordinary sounds of the ship came back—water against hull, Sef below starting breakfast, the wind in the rigging.

I gave it twenty minutes. Then I went below, alone, and opened the ballast access, and knelt on the cold plates, and pulled out the oilcloth bundle, and held it.

Thirty years of dead men's signatures. Insurance claims on ships that never should have sunk. Crew lists ending in blank space where survivors' names should have been. And one vessel, the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands in 1989—Edda Kwan's family aboard, though the ledger didn't say so. Ledgers never say the human part. That's what ledgers are *for*.

The promise I'd made was silence. Bring them home, tell no one. I'd built my whole end of the bargain on the belief that silence was mercy—that some truths are anchors that drag everyone who holds them down to the same depth. And I still believed it, mostly. But kneeling there with the bundle in my lap, I understood something had shifted, and I couldn't yet name it, and the not-naming itched like salt in a wound.

Edda had traded me this chart with her last strength. She hadn't traded me her wish. Those were two different things, and I'd only promised the first.

I should have destroyed them. Right then, with the Kestrel's wake still visible on the horizon, with my crew alive and my debt unpaid and every rational column balanced—that was the moment. Weighted bundle, deep water, done. Duty to the living, paid in full.

I got as far as the rail. I stood there with the bundle in both hands and the sea waiting, patient as everything else the Voss owned.

I couldn't.

My hands wouldn't open. Some stubborn, stupid, grieving part of me—the part that had sat beside a dying woman and held her hand and said *yes, I promise*—refused to let thirty years of dead people dissolve into indifferent water. They'd waited long enough. They'd waited under a reef while the men who sank them filed tidy paperwork and bought bigger boats.

So I did the coward's compromise, which is to say the survivor's compromise. I carried the bundle back down, rewrapped it, and hid it somewhere new—a place I won't write down even here, even now, because old habits are the last thing to sink. Then I went up on deck, where the sun was fully up and my crew was waiting with questions in their faces, and I lied to them too, easily, smoothly, telling them I'd thrown the ledgers to the sea as ordered.

Sef looked at me for a long moment over the galley table. Forty years afloat reads a face better than any instrument.

"Right," he said finally, and served the eggs.

That night, the radio crackled again at 2300—not the Kestrel, not customs, just a weather advisory, updated cyclone tracking, a system forming northwest and strengthening. Category three, possibly four, moving southeast. Toward us. Toward the reef that sheltered us and could just as easily trap us.

I stood in the wheelhouse with the chart spread out and drew the system's likely track with a grease pencil, and the line ended where we were, and beyond it the pencil could go no further.

Behind the reef or out to sea. Shelter or flight. And somewhere behind us, I was sure, the Kestrel was watching to see which way we'd run.
