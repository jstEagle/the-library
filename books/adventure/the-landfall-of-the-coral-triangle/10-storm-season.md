## 10. Storm Season

I've been reading weather for forty years, and I'll tell you the truth nobody puts in the almanacs: the sky doesn't warn you. It negotiates. And by the time you hear its terms, you've already signed.

I knew it that morning before anyone else did. The way the light came off the water—flat, yellowish, like old brass. The way the birds had gone quiet and inland. The way my knees ached in a rhythm I'd learned to trust more than any barometer.

"Barometer's falling like a drunk down stairs," I told Quentin over breakfast, which was rice and tinned fish because everything on this boat was rice and tinned fish now. "We need to move her behind the reef."

Quentin looked up from the chart she'd been staring at for an hour without seeing it. She does that. Stares at paper like it owes her money. "How long?"

"If we're lucky? Six hours to get anchored proper. If we're not lucky, we're already late."

"And if we stay out here?"

I looked at the horizon, where the clouds were stacking themselves into something with ambition. "Then we find out what the Corvus is made of. Which is rust, mostly."

She didn't laugh. That was how I knew she understood me.

* * *

Getting behind the reef was the easy part, and the easy part nearly killed us anyway. The passage was narrow, maybe forty meters of navigable water between coral walls that would open this hull like a tin of sardines, and the tide was already turning against us. Tomas took the wheel while I called depths from the bow, watching the color of the water the way other men watch faces for lies.

"Green," I'd call. "Green. Green. *Brown—hard to port!*"

Tomas ported hard. The Corvus groaned and swung, her keel kissing something soft, and then we were through, into the lagoon, and the reef closed behind us like a door.

For about two hours, it felt like winning.

The lagoon was a mile across, turquoise and deceptively calm, ringed by coral that broke the swell into white lace. We dropped both anchors with scope enough to shame a battleship, backed them down hard, set a third off the stern as a brake. Priya dove the anchors just to be sure—came up shaking her head in admiration at the holding ground. "Sand and rubble," she said. "They'll hold."

"They'll hold," I agreed, "if the wind stays under ninety knots. Above that, nothing holds. You just hope whatever drags you drags you somewhere survivable."

Junie asked what ninety knots felt like. I told her it felt like being inside a freight train that hates you personally.

She laughed. Kids always laugh at that part. They think the storm is the exciting part. Nobody tells them the storm is just the opening act—the real show is what the storm makes of people.

By noon the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong. By one o'clock the wind arrived—not gradually, not politely, but all at once, like it had been waiting offshore for permission.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about a category-four cyclone: it isn't loud the way thunder is loud. It's loud the way the ocean is loud when it decides to become the sky. There was no inside or outside anymore. The rain moved sideways in sheets so dense you couldn't see the bow from the wheelhouse, and the wind screamed through the rigging in every key at once, a choir of things dying.

We were trapped behind our own salvation. The same reef that kept the full fury of the open sea off us also kept us penned in, and the lagoon turned into a washing machine—confused seas bouncing off coral, coming back at us from three directions at once. The Corvus pitched and yawed and rolled, sometimes all three at once, which is a thing boats are not supposed to do.

Tomas lashed himself to the wheel. I lashed myself next to him, because two sets of hands on the helm is one set more than one, and because I'm too old to die alone at a wheel I love.

Quentin came aft through the chaos, moving hand-over-hand along the lifelines like she was born doing it, which maybe she was. Her face was gray with salt spray and something worse than fear.

"How long?" she shouted.

"As long as it wants!" I shouted back. "Cyclones don't keep schedules!"

"What do we do?"

"We survive! That's the whole plan! Survive until it gets bored!"

She nodded like that was a reasonable strategy, which it was, because it's the only strategy there ever was.

The first anchor dragged at 1500 hours. I felt it in my feet before the instruments confirmed it—that sickening shudder, the hull's whole attitude changing, the particular vibration of a boat being pulled somewhere she shouldn't go. Tomas read the GPS and went pale under his tan.

"We're moving. Two knots, toward the north reef."

"Slack the main anchor, take strain on the stern line," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was doing arithmetic I didn't like: distance to reef, rate of drag, depth of water over coral. If we went onto that coral broadside, we'd break up in twenty minutes. In a cyclone. With nowhere to run.

So we fought. For hours, we fought—a war conducted in inches, easing one line, taking up another, using the storm's own violence against itself. It's a strange kind of seamanship, cyclone work. You don't conquer anything. You negotiate. You give the sea exactly as much as you must and not one inch more, and you pray it accepts your terms.

Somewhere in the worst of it—I couldn't tell you when, time goes strange in weather like that—the mast went.

It didn't snap so much as surrender. A groan from the step, a shriek of tearing aluminum, and then seventy feet of mast came down across the deck like God clearing His throat, taking the radar, the antenna, half the rail, and most of my dignity with it. The boom swung wild and caught the wheelhouse a glancing blow that would have taken Tomas's head off if he'd been standing a foot to starboard.

After that we were blind. No radar, no radio, no engine—we'd shut it down rather than foul the prop in the lines. Just a wounded trawler dragging her anchors across a lagoon the size of a dinner plate, in the dark, in a category-four, with a broken mast rolling around on deck like a battering ram.

And then Junie went overboard.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night.

She'd gone forward to clear the mast wreckage—it was her idea, and it was a good idea, and good ideas kill sailors all the time. A wave came aboard. Not a wave, really; the whole sea stood up and walked across the foredeck, and when it left, it took her with it. One moment she was there in her harness, and the next the harness was empty, the tether snapped clean, and the night had swallowed her.

Priya screamed her name. I heard it even through the wind, a sound I will carry to my grave.

What happened next happened fast, and I saw most of it from the wheelhouse window, which is to say I saw almost nothing: a shape at the rail. Quentin. Unclipping her own tether. Going into that water on purpose.

You have to understand what that means. Going over the side in a cyclone, in a lagoon, at night, is not bravery. Bravery implies odds. This was arithmetic with only one answer. The water was a churning black chaos full of debris and coral heads, and the moment she went in, the sea owned her too. Now we had two people overboard instead of one, and the Corvus dragging toward the reef, and a broken mast, and no engine, and me at a wheel that wasn't steering anything because the rudder was half out of the water.

Old Sef, I said to myself, you are watching your whole crew die in the order they were born, youngest first.

But here's the thing about Quentin Oyelaran that I figured out that night, watching her swim: she doesn't fight the sea. Everybody else fights the sea. She reads it. I watched her go limp in the worst of the chop, let a wave carry her instead of fighting it, angle herself—not toward Junie, at first, but *across* the current, the way you cross a river, letting the drift do the work. Forty years I've been on water and I've seen maybe three people move like that. It's not swimming. It's conversation.

Priya got a line rigged—a heaving line off the stern winch, the only machinery still working—and I steered us as close to the drag point as I dared, which wasn't close, and threw what I could throw, which wasn't far, and prayed, which I don't do, except I did.

Two minutes. Three. An eternity measured in heartbeats.

Then the searchlight—what was left of it, mounted on the wheelhouse roof, swinging wild—caught them. Both of them. Junie clinging to Quentin's back, arms locked around her throat, and Quentin swimming one-armed, the other arm hooked under the girl's chin, keeping her face above the water, making headway that was barely headway at all, more like refusing to lose.

Priya hauled the line to them. Tomas hauled the line in. And I got to the rail in time to help pull them over, and the first thing that came aboard was Quentin's hand, white-knuckled around mine, and the second thing was Junie, coughing up half the lagoon, alive, *alive*, and the third thing was Quentin herself, flat on the deck, breathing like a bellows, grinning at the sky like it had told her a joke.

"You're insane," Priya shouted at her, and it wasn't an insult; it was a diagnosis delivered with love.

"I know," Quentin said, and passed out.

* * *

The eye found us near midnight.

That's the cruel trick of cyclones—they give you a rest just long enough to understand what's coming. The wind died to nothing, the rain stopped, and the stars came out overhead like nothing had happened, like the sky hadn't spent six hours trying to murder us. Around us, the wall of the eyewall stood up all the way to heaven, a coliseum of black cloud, and somewhere on the far side of it, the second half of the storm waited to finish the job.

In that terrible calm, we worked.

Tomas and I got the mast wreckage cut loose and shoved overboard—it went down with a sound like a sigh, forty years of fittings and my favorite winch handle with it. We re-rigged the anchors, checked the lines, counted the crew. All present. All breathing. Junie wrapped in blankets in the wheelhouse, shaking, teeth chattering, alive. Priya wouldn't leave her side. Quentin sat propped against the wheelhouse door with a blanket around her shoulders, watching the eyewall come, calm as Sunday.

"Sef," she said quietly, while the others worked. "Thank you for getting us here. Behind the reef. Anywhere else, we'd already be gone."

"Don't thank me yet. Back half's coming."

"I know." She looked at the wall of cloud, and I saw something in her face I hadn't seen before. Not fear. Something older and quieter. "You know what I was thinking, in the water? When I had her?"

"Tell me."

"That I've spent my whole life calculating distances. Miles to the next storm. Miles to the next payment. Miles between me and everybody." She flexed her fingers, like they remembered the weight of a drowning girl. "Out there, there weren't any miles. There was just her. Just getting her back."

I didn't say anything. Old men learn when a moment needs silence more than wisdom.

The eyewall hit at 0110, and it was worse than the first half, because the first half had been honest anger and the second half was spite. But we were ready for spite. We'd spent the calm learning the storm's tricks, and when it tried the same moves, we countered them. The anchors held—barely, furiously, groaning like living things. The Corvus dragged thirty meters and stopped. Dragged ten more and stopped again. And at dawn, gray and exhausted and utterly changed, the wind finally began to die, sulking away to the southeast like a bully who'd lost interest.

* * *

Morning came up gold and obscene, the lagoon flat as glass, the reef wearing its white lace again as if last night had never happened. Debris floated everywhere—palm fronds, a fishing float, half of someone's roof from some island we'd never find. The sun came out and warmed the deck and dried our clothes and pretended to apologize.

We surveyed the damage like survivors of a war walking their own streets. Mast gone. Radar gone. Radio gone. Rail stove in. Deck scarred. Engine intact—Tomas actually kissed the engine, which I chose not to witness formally. The Corvus was crippled, stranded behind a reef on an unnamed atoll in the middle of the Coral Triangle, carrying cargo none of us could talk about, with no way to call for help and no way home.

Should have been the worst morning of my life.

Instead, I stood at the rail drinking burnt coffee and watched my crew—my *crew*, and when did that word start meaning what it means—move around the deck checking on each other. Priya re-wrapping Junie's rope burns. Tomas already sketching repair plans in the dirt with a stick. Quentin sitting on the hatch coaming with Junie beside her, not talking, just sitting, shoulder to shoulder, the way people sit when words would only get in the way.

Forty years afloat, and I'd kept myself crewless on purpose. Crews are liabilities. Crews are people you bury. I'd told myself that since '84, and I believed it right up until last night, when I watched a woman jump into a cyclone for a runaway kid she'd known three weeks, and realized I'd been lying to myself for four decades.

The sea takes everyone eventually. That's the deal. But last night the sea reached out its hand twice, and twice somebody on this boat said no.

"Hey, Sef." Junie, appearing at my elbow, hair still stiff with salt. "What happens now?"

I looked at the broken mast, the dead radio, the beautiful hostile ocean all around us. Then at the chart room, where Quentin was already unrolling charts, because of course she was. Because stopping has never once occurred to that woman.

"Now?" I said. "Now we fix her. Now we figure out what we're carrying and what it's worth and who's coming for it. Now we decide what kind of people we're going to be."

Junie frowned. "That sounds complicated."

"It is." I drained my coffee and headed for the chart room, because the old man still had work to do. "Welcome to the family."
