## 11. Repair and Reckoning

The mast came down in the cyclone, and the Corvus came to rest behind the reef like a drunk leaning on a fence, and for three days nobody said the word *stranded* out loud, because saying it would make it true.

I counted instead. It's what I do. I count things when I can't fix them.

Eleven hull planks sprung or cracked. One rudder gudgeon sheared clean off. The watermaker limping along at half capacity. Forty-one hours of engine time left in the fuel bladder if we rationed, which meant thirty-eight once Sef stopped pretending he wasn't running the stove twice a day because Junie needed hot food after nearly drowning. And one mast, snapped eight feet above the deck, lying across the boom like a broken arm someone had splinted badly and then walked away from.

Tomas stood at the base of it on the first morning, hands on his hips, doing accountant math out loud. "We can't step a new spar. We don't have one. We could jury-rig with the spinnaker pole and the remains of the boom, but that's a fair-weather rig, and we are not in fair weather."

"We're not in anything," Sef said. "That's the problem."

The atoll gave us nothing and everything. A horseshoe of coral and sand no wider than a runway, a lagoon flat as poured glass, coconut palms leaning like they were eavesdropping. No name on any chart Quentin would show us, though she had one folded in her pocket that she touched more often than she touched people. Fresh water in a seep on the leeward side, crabs fat enough to apologize for eating, and a reef passage too shallow for the patrol boat that had hailed us a week ago — which was luck, or providence, or the sea's idea of a joke with a long setup.

We could hide here. We could not leave here. Not yet. Maybe not soon.

So we repaired. That's what bodies do when the mind refuses to make decisions: they sand things, they epoxy things, they haul things. Tomas turned into a surgeon with the hull, feeding oakum into seams by lantern light and swearing in three languages, two of them accounting terms. Sef rebuilt the rudder fitting with a piece of scrap bronze he'd been hoarding "for an emergency," and when Junie asked how he'd known this was the emergency, he said, "I didn't. Bronze doesn't care." Junie herself worked like someone paying off a debt — hauling, scrubbing, diving the anchor line four times a day to check the holding ground, as if the sea might change its mind about letting her live and she wanted to be useful before it did.

And Quentin climbed the broken mast every morning.

Not to fix it. There was nothing up there but the stub and the sky. She'd shinny up the remaining section, sit with her legs dangling over the wreckage of our departure options, and look south toward the shipping lanes, toward Darwin, toward the syndicate, toward whatever arithmetic she was running behind those eyes. Then she'd climb down and say something brisk about caulking or crab traps, and we'd all pretend we hadn't watched her measure the distance between us and trouble.

I let it go for three days. I'm a diver. I know how long you can hold your breath before the body makes the decision for you.

Three days was up.

* * *

It happened at dusk, which is when reefs go honest. The colors drain out, the fish stop performing, and everything that lives in the cracks comes out to feed. I'd spent the afternoon surveying the inner reef — partly for anchorage, partly because mapping is how I pray — and I came back to find the crew scattered around a driftwood fire, eating crab, and Quentin apart from them by exactly the length of a conversation nobody wanted to have.

She was sitting on the overturned dinghy, going through the waterproof satchel. Our satchel. The one we'd pulled out of the wreck's strongroom, the one with the ledgers inside — Voss Syndicate ledgers, decades of them, ships sunk on purpose and written off as weather, premiums collected on corpses. Edda Kwan's wreck. Edda Kwan's proof. Edda Kwan's thirty years of dying slowly so this evidence could someday see daylight.

Quentin had the satchel open and was reading by firelight with her lips pressed together, and I thought: there it is. The breath held too long.

I sat down next to her. She didn't flinch, which I noted, because flinching is honest and she wasn't doing much that was honest lately.

"Good dive?" she asked.

"Beautiful. Three new species of nudibranch I can't name, a green turtle that's older than everyone on this boat except Sef, and a bommie at twelve meters shaped exactly like a fist." I pulled a crab leg out of the pot Sef handed me without being asked. "Also I mapped where the patrol boat will come through if it comes back, and it's a fifty-fifty whether they ground themselves on the outer bar or find the deep channel. So that's cheerful."

"Very you."

"Thank you." I ate. I let the fire crackle. Then I said, "Whose silence are we keeping?"

She didn't look at me. "What?"

"The promise. You made Edda a promise. Bring the crew home alive, tell no one what we found. Fine. Beautiful promise. Very noble, very heavy, I've watched you carry it around like ballast for two weeks." I set the crab leg down, because this deserved both hands. "But I've been thinking about silence, Quentin. Silence isn't neutral. Silence has a shape, and the shape depends on who it protects. Right now that silence is protecting us. It's also protecting Voss. Every day those ledgers sit in a bag instead of in front of a journalist or a magistrate or literally anyone, the syndicate keeps sinking ships and collecting on the drownings. So whose silence is it? Because I don't think it's Edda's anymore."

The fire popped. Across the beach, Junie laughed at something Sef said, a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she hadn't managed in the first week. Tomas was showing her card tricks with a deck missing the queens, and she was pretending to be fooled, and he was pretending not to notice she was pretending.

"You don't know what Edda wanted," Quentin said quietly.

"I know what you told us she wanted. Which is silence. Which is interesting, actually, because everything else about that woman — the map, the money, the thirty years — screams the opposite. People who want secrets buried don't finance expeditions to dig them up." I kept my voice level. Diver's trick: work below the surface, never let them see you strain. "People who want secrets buried leave them buried. They don't hire a stranger with debts and a fast boat."

"She was dying, Priya. Grieving people say strange—"

"Grieving people don't spend their life savings on a salvage contract with a morality clause." I turned to face her fully. "You've read those ledgers more than anyone. You know what's in them. The *Meridian Star*. The *Halcyon B*. Ships full of people, sunk for insurance, and the same handwriting signing off on all of it. You know, and you're choosing quiet, and you're dressing the choice up as loyalty to a dead woman who can't correct you."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness went overboard with the mast. What's left is honesty, and honesty is asking: when you promised Edda silence, did she mean *keep this safe*, or did she mean *keep this buried*? And if you're not sure — and I've watched your face, Quentin, you're not sure — then whose silence are you actually keeping? Yours? Because it's easier than deciding?"

She finally looked at me, and I'll give her this: she didn't do the charm thing. She didn't do the brisk-captain thing. She looked tired and cornered and young, younger than she had any right to be given the mileage.

"You think I haven't turned it over?" Her voice stayed low, pitched under the fire noise. "Every night. You think I like sitting on proof that could burn the Voss Syndicate to the waterline while Mara Iles smiles at me across a radio? I made a promise. My word is the only asset I've got left that isn't mortgaged. If I start deciding which promises count based on my mood—"

"It's not your mood. It's the body count."

"—then I'm just another person who breaks faith when it's inconvenient." She rubbed her face. "Edda asked me for one thing. One. She gave me the only thing she had left, and she asked for one thing in return, and I said yes, and yes has to mean something even when yes gets expensive."

"And if yes gets other people killed?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind with weight. The fire crackled into it and sounded ashamed.

"Has it?" she asked finally. "Gotten anyone killed? Or are we borrowing trouble?"

"Mara Iles doubled your deadline while smiling. The patrol boat found us in open ocean in a search area bigger than Texas. Quentin, they're not looking for customs violations. They're looking for us, and they'll keep finding us, and eventually they'll come with more than a hail. When that happens, every person on this beach is standing between you and your debt. Sef, who's forty years afloat and deserves a porch somewhere. Junie, who's seventeen and already almost drowned once on your watch. Tomas, who — did you know he was Voss? Did you know your ex-accountant used to work for the people hunting us?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "He told you that?"

"He told me that the night before the storm, when he thought I was asleep. He recognized the handwriting in the ledgers, Quentin. He's known since the wreck. He's been carrying it alone because he's afraid of what you'll do when you find out he used to be one of them." I let that land. "See, this is what silence does. It doesn't protect people. It just makes them carry things separately, alone, in the dark, until the load breaks somebody. I've dived enough wrecks to know how cargo shifts. Everything stowed in separate compartments sinks the whole ship."

Quentin stared at the fire for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone rough.

"If I put those ledgers in front of the world, Voss doesn't collapse politely. They send boats. They send men like the ones Mara drinks with. And the story dies with whoever told it, and the ledgers die with the boat, and Edda's thirty years die with us, and nothing changes except the number of graves." She shook her head. "You want to talk about whose silence it is? Sometimes silence is the only way a thing survives long enough to matter."

"Then say that to them." I nodded across the fire, at our ragged family of runaways and debtors. "Not to me. To all of us. Tell them what the ledgers are, what the promise was, what the stakes are, and let them choose. You assembled a crew of people who each owe someone too much. You think they can't handle one hard truth? We've been handling hard truths since Darwin. What we can't handle is being managed. Being protected. Being kept in the dark by someone who thinks darkness is a kindness." I stood, brushed sand off my knees. "Trust isn't keeping people safe from information, Quentin. Trust is believing they'll survive it. You taught us that when you pulled Junie out of the water. Now finish the lesson."

I walked toward the waterline, because I'd said my piece and divers know when to surface slow. Behind me I heard her exhale, long and shaky, and then — footsteps. She crossed the sand to the fire, satchel in hand, and I heard her voice, not loud, carrying anyway:

"Everyone. Put the cards down. We need to talk about what's actually in this bag."

Junie's laugh cut off. Tomas's cards stopped mid-shuffle. Sef looked up from the coals with the expression of a man who'd been waiting three days for someone to say it.

I stood at the edge of the dark with the tide licking my ankles and watched, and I will admit — privately, only here, only now — that my hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the particular vertigo of having said the true thing out loud and not knowing yet whether it was going to save us or sink us.

Quentin opened the satchel. And from underneath the ledgers, from a folder none of us had seen her set aside, she drew out a bundle of letters tied with fishing twine, yellowed soft as cloth, addressed in a careful hand: *For the one who brings them home.*

She hadn't mentioned these to anyone. Not to me. Not to anyone.

"Edda wrote these," Quentin said, and her voice cracked on the name. "Thirty years of letters. I found them the night before the storm and I haven't—" She stopped. Started again. "I haven't been able to read past the first page."

Sef rose without a word and fed the fire until it stood up tall, and the whole crew leaned in, five faces lit orange against the black lagoon, waiting.

Quentin untied the twine.
