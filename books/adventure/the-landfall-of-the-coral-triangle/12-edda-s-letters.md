## 12. Edda's Letters

The rain had been falling on the Corvus for three days, a soft grey drizzle that made the whole world sound like static, and I'd run out of ways to avoid the box.

It sat in my cabin under the chart table where I kept things I wasn't ready to look at — spare fuses, a photograph of my mother I still hadn't decided whether to keep, and now a wooden crate of papers salvaged from the wreck's chart room, wrapped in oilcloth and stubborn as a debt. Tomas had catalogued most of it: ledgers, manifests, correspondence, all of it evidence of thirty years of Voss Syndicate ships going down on purpose so the insurance money could float up instead. The ledgers were the bomb. The ledgers were what Mara Iles would burn islands to recover.

But it was the letters at the bottom of the box, addressed in a spidery hand to *E. Kwan, care of the Broome General Post*, that I couldn't make myself open.

I told myself it was strategy. A good chaser knows when not to fly into the cell — you circle, you wait, you let the atmosphere decide. That was the professional framing. The honest framing was that I was afraid of paper. Paper is just weather that doesn't move. You can't outrun it, and unlike a supercell, it never weakens over land.

On the fourth day Junie banged on my door with the particular rhythm she used when she wanted something and thought I didn't know it.

"Old Sef says dinner's ready," she said, then lingered, dripping, in my doorway like a cat deciding whether to come in out of principle or spite. "You've been staring at that box for four days. Priya says you're grieving. Tomas says you're doing accounting. I say you're being a coward."

"Any of them could be right."

"It's her stuff, isn't it. Edda's."

"Some of it."

Junie came in without invitation, which was her way, and picked up one of the letters, turning it over. "You promised her something," she said. It wasn't a question. Everyone on this boat knew I'd promised Edda Kwan something; they just didn't know the shape of it. "And now the promise is getting heavy."

"Promises are light when you make them," I said. "It's the carrying that adds weight."

She set the letter down with exaggerated care, the way she handled anything that belonged to someone dead, which was better manners than I managed, and left me alone with the static and the box.

* * *

I opened the first letter that night because sleep had become a rumor and the rain wouldn't stop its whispering.

The handwriting slanted right, cramped, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page. Someone who wrote like every word cost money. The date was eleven years old.

*Mr. Oyelaran will not do,* it began. *He wants the salvage rights, not the truth. He asked twice about resale value and not once about the crew manifest. Strike him from consideration.*

I sat up in my bunk.

There were more. Dozens more, rubber-banded by decade, and once I started I couldn't stop, the way you can't stop watching a storm cell organize itself on radar — each frame worse and more beautiful than the last. Edda Kwan had been running an expedition out of a hospice bed for thirty years. Letter after letter to marine surveyors, salvage brokers, charter captains, lawyers. Replies copied in her own hand. Budgets drafted on the backs of envelopes. She had approached forty-one people before me. I counted. Forty-one expeditions proposed, funded, scheduled, and collapsed — captains who took her deposit and vanished, surveyors who laughed at her coordinates, one man who sold her route to Voss and got her blacklisted from three ports in a single season.

And through all of it, deposits paid out of a pension that couldn't have been more than a few hundred dollars a month. Thirty years of small savings, bled dry, refilled, bled again. She'd worked cannery seasons at seventy. There was a receipt from a blood plasma center in Darwin, dated eight years ago, stapled to a note in her hand: *Every little bit floats the boat.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, somewhere between a laugh and something else, and Sef would have called it what it was if he'd heard it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing I'd been circling for four days like a cell I refused to enter.

Her last letter to herself — drafts, unsent, the way lonely people write — said it plainly. *If they find the ledgers, they must not bury them. Burying is what Voss does. Burying is how the Meridian stayed a lie for fifty years. Whatever it costs, whatever it risks, the names go on record. Papa's name. Mama's. Lien's. Let the sea give them back.*

The *Meridian*. The ship that went down with Edda's family aboard when she was nine years old. The ship whose sinking was, according to the ledgers rotting in our hold, scheduled by a Voss accountant with tidy handwriting — the same tidy handwriting Tomas had gone white looking at, because he'd learned his numbers from the man who wrote it.

I had promised a dying woman two things: bring whoever sailed it home alive, and tell no one what we found.

She had spent thirty years and her entire life's blood trying to buy the opposite of the second promise.

* * *

I brought the letters to the galley at dawn, because there are problems you solve alone and problems you solve in front of witnesses, and I'd finally learned — at forty-one, the hard way, the expensive way — which kind this was.

They assembled the way my crews always did: Sef already there, coffee going, pretending he hadn't slept in the chair; Priya arriving second with wet hair and the particular stillness she wore when she'd already guessed; Tomas last, buttoned up, accountant's face firmly in place. Junie didn't pretend to be invited. She just appeared, as she did, occupying space like weather.

I laid the letters out on the table between the mugs.

"Edda didn't want this buried," I said. No preamble. Preamble is for people with time. "Thirty years. Forty-one failed expeditions. She funded this out of a pension and a plasma clinic. The promise I made her — the silence part — I made it to comfort a dying woman. And it turns out I was comforting her with the exact thing she spent her life fighting."

Nobody spoke. Rain on the deck above, soft, patient.

Tomas picked up the oldest letter and read it with his lips moving slightly, an old habit he thought nobody noticed. When he looked up, his face had done something complicated. "The Voss pay well for silence," he said quietly. "I know. I processed the payments." He set it down. "She's the only person I ever met who paid for noise."

"That's not the problem," Priya said. "The problem is what the noise costs. We've seen their patrol boat once. There'll be more. If we surface those ledgers, every one of us becomes a name on a list, and Voss lists don't have erasers."

"I know what they cost," I said. "I'm the one holding the invoice."

"Are you?" Priya's voice stayed level, which with Priya meant she was angrier than shouting would have shown. "Because from where I sit, you've been holding it alone for four days, and that's the actual problem, Quentin. Not the ledgers. Not Voss. You made a promise to a dead woman and you've been deciding alone whether to break it, as if the rest of us are cargo."

The word landed harder than she probably intended, or exactly as hard as she intended — with Priya it was always impossible to tell, and I suspected she engineered it that way.

"She asked me," I said. "Not you. Not any of us. Me. In a hospice bed, with maybe a week left, she looked at me and asked, and I said yes, because saying no to a dying woman felt like—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "It felt like something I couldn't do."

"And now?" Junie said. Small voice, direct aim. "Now that she's not dying in front of you?"

"Now it turns out I promised her a lie."

Sef poured coffee into the mug nearest my elbow without asking, which was his answer to most questions. "Forty years on water," he said, "I've learned promises are like anchors. Right size, right bottom, they hold you. Wrong size, they drown you." He tapped the table. "Question isn't whether to drop it. Question's what bottom you're over."

"The bottom," Tomas said, "is that we have evidence of fifty years of murder-by-ledger, a syndicate that already sent a patrol boat to find us, and a promise that contradicts itself." He arranged the letters into a neat stack, squaring the edges, and I understood suddenly that tidiness was how he prayed. "There's no course through this that doesn't hit something."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."

"So we choose what we hit," Priya said.

* * *

We argued until the rain stopped, which in this season was less a change in weather than a held breath. We argued about exposure and timing, about whether the audit angle Tomas kept sketching on napkins was genius or suicide, about whether Port Moresby archives would even accept documents with no chain of custody, about whether Junie — fifteen, runaway, wanted in exactly zero jurisdictions but convinced otherwise — should get a vote. She should, Priya said. She does, Sef said, she's had one since Darwin, hasn't anyone noticed? I hadn't noticed. I noted it, filed it, felt something in my chest rearrange itself around the fact that my crew considered itself a democracy and had simply never informed the captain.

By noon we had nothing settled except that we had until the repairs finished — three days, maybe four, Priya's estimate on the shaft seal — to decide what the Corvus would carry home. Truth or silence. Noise or the grave. Edda's wish or Edda's words, because we could no longer have both.

I went back to my cabin and read the letters again, all of them, start to finish, and this time I read them the way you read a storm — not frame by frame but whole, structure and motion together. And what I saw, finally, was not a contradiction.

It was an inheritance.

Here's what I mean. My whole life I'd treated promises as transactions — you give your word, you owe the debt, you pay it, done. Clean accounting. But Edda's promise wasn't a transaction. It was a woman with a week to live handing a stranger everything she had and asking for one thing: *don't let it be for nothing.* The silence clause wasn't the point. It was fear talking — fear that the ledgers would end up in another broker's drawer, another Voss filing cabinet, another forty-one failures. She'd asked for silence because everyone she'd ever trusted had taught her that trust gets sold.

And then she'd met me. Or rather, she'd bet on me, the way she'd bet on forty-one others, except this time the bet landed. We found the wreck. We found the ledgers. Her money — her cannery shifts, her plasma, her thirty years — bought exactly what she paid for.

The promise wasn't broken by surfacing the truth. The promise was *completed* by it. The silence was scaffolding, meant to come down once the building stood. She just hadn't lived long enough to tell me which parts of her words were load-bearing.

I sat with that for a long time while the held-breath sky outside decided what it wanted to be.

Then I gathered the letters, squared them the way Tomas would have, and carried them back to the galley, where the crew had drifted one by one throughout the afternoon the way crews do — pretending to work on charts, on inventory, on nothing — waiting.

"We surface it," I said. "All of it. The ledgers, the letters, her name on every page. Not to the press, not yet — Tomas's way first, quiet, through channels Voss can't buy off fast enough. But it goes on record. Every name in those books goes home." I put Edda's letters in the middle of the table. "That's the promise now. The real one. Bring everyone home alive, and bring her family's names home with us."

Priya nodded slowly, and some knot in her shoulders I hadn't known was there came loose. Sef grunted, which from Sef was a standing ovation. Junie grinned like she'd personally won something.

Tomas didn't nod. He was standing at the aft window with his head tilted, listening to something that wasn't the rain, which had stopped, or the wind, which hadn't.

"Quentin," he said. "How far out is the reef passage?"

"Fourteen miles. Why?"

"Because there's a boat coming through it," he said, "and it's flying no lights, and it's too big to be a fisherman."

I reached for the binoculars and the sky, obligingly, split open — one clean seam of sunset light down the western strait, enough to catch the shape of a hull moving with purpose toward our little triangle of coral, our stranded trawler, our box of thirty-year secrets.

Mara Iles had said she'd find us. I'd hoped she was bluffing. Dangerous optimists, Edda had written once, in a margin, in that embossed hand: *men who believe everything works out — for them.*

Time to find out which of us the optimism belonged to.
