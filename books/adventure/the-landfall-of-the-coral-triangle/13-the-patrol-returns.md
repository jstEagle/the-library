## 13. The Patrol Returns

There is a particular sound a man makes when he recognizes his own handwriting on a document that could hang him. I know, because I made it—somewhere between a cough and a prayer—in the hold of the *Corvus* at four in the morning, three days after we pulled Edda Kwan's ledgers out of a dead ship's belly.

I had spent eleven years at Voss Maritime Holdings writing numbers that lied beautifully. Depreciation schedules with the moral flexibility of a contortionist. Insurance valuations that treated human cargo as rounding errors. I was good at it. That's the part nobody wants to hear: not that I was trapped, or coerced, or young and foolish—though all of those were true—but that I was *good*. Mara Iles once told me I had the cleanest hands in dirty water she'd ever seen. She meant it as a compliment. She still does, probably.

So when I opened the waterproof satchel and saw the 1987 entries—the *Meridian Star*, "total constructive loss," payout approved, signature initialed T.R.—I understood two things simultaneously. First: the ship that killed Edda Kwan's family had been murdered by men who then billed for the funeral. Second: my own initials were going to be the thing that proved it.

The universe has a sense of humor. It's just never been funny to me.

* * *

Quentin found me in the hold at dawn, sitting on an oil drum like a man waiting for a bus that goes nowhere.

"You've been down here six hours," she said.

"I've been down here thirty years," I said. "Six hours is just where you caught up."

She didn't laugh. Quentin Oyelaran doesn't laugh when she's doing arithmetic about people, and I could see her running mine: what does Tomas know, what will Tomas do, can Tomas be trusted with the thing that gets us all killed. Fair questions. I'd asked them of myself.

"The ledgers," she said. "You've read them."

"Every page. Twice." I handed her the satchel. "The Voss didn't just sink ships for insurance, Quentin. They sank ships for *leverage*. Every loss was a debt forgiven, a rival erased, a witness removed. The *Meridian Star* was carrying a family that had refused to sell their berth rights. Edda Kwan was eight years old. She survived because she was in the water long enough to stop being a witness and start being a rumor." I watched her face do what faces do when they absorb something too large. "And the man who signed off on the payout used the same shorthand I invented. Voss taught me the trade. Turns out I was learning a dialect."

"So it's your handwriting."

"It's my *system*. The handwriting is older. But the architecture—" I tapped my temple. "That's mine. They're still using it. Page forty-one, the *Corvus*'s own registration, filed through a shell company in Singapore. I built that shell company. I named it after my mother."

Silence. Somewhere above us, Old Sef was arguing with the windlass, which he did every morning as a form of devotion.

"What do we do?" Quentin asked, and I want to be honest here: it was the first time anyone had asked me that question expecting my answer to matter.

"We hide them," I said. "Not destroy them. Hide them somewhere even Mara Iles won't look, because looking there would mean admitting what they are."

"Which is?"

"An admission." I stood, knees cracking like the old deck planks. "Voss doesn't search for evidence, Quentin. Voss searches for things that could be *used against them*, which is a different category entirely. Their boarding protocol has a hierarchy. Cargo, navigation equipment, communications, personal effects. They'll tear apart the galley and Junie's bunk because those are where runaways hide things. They will never—" I put my hand flat on the hull, below the waterline, where forty years of ballast sat in its iron silence. "They will never open the ballast. Because opening the ballast means admitting there might be something worth hiding from themselves."

* * *

We worked through the morning while Priya kept watch and Sef ran the engine loud enough to justify any noise. The ballast keel of a trawler like the *Corvus* is a series of iron pigs—ingots, essentially—slotted into channels along the keel line. To get at them you pull floor panels that exist only if you know they exist, and I knew, because I'd signed off on the refit specifications for twelve vessels of this class. Including, God help me, this one. The *Corvus* had been a Voss asset before she was ours. Her papers were forged by a department I used to manage. When I told Quentin this, she looked at me for a long moment and said, "You're telling me we stole our own ship?"

"I'm telling you the universe has a sense of humor," I said, "and it's still not funny."

We greased the pigs with bilge sludge until they looked like forty years of neglect instead of three hours of conspiracy. Then we wrapped the ledgers in Edda's own waterproofing—she'd been thorough, the way only someone with thirty years of obsession can be—and slid them into the channel beneath the third pig from the bow, and sealed the panels with marine epoxy, and stood back.

It looked like nothing. That was the whole art of it. Voss accounting was the art of making enormous crimes look like nothing, and I had simply turned the technique around, pointed it at its makers. Somewhere in that transformation I felt something I hadn't felt since I resigned—or fled; the distinction depends on the day—a feeling with no better name than *usefulness*.

Junie came down while we were cleaning up. She looked at the floor panels, at the grease on our hands, and said nothing, which from Junie is a full conversation. But she brought us coffee, real coffee, hoarded from Darwin, and set the mugs down without being asked, and stayed to sit on the drum across from us, legs swinging.

"They're coming back, aren't they," she said.

"Yes," I said.

"And we're just going to let them aboard?"

"We're going to let them aboard," I said, "and we're going to be so boring that they leave. Boring is armor, Junie. Boring is the one thing money can't buy and suspicion can't penetrate."

She considered this. "My uncle always said boring people are hiding something."

"Your uncle," I said, "sounds like he learned everything he knows from the same manual I did."

* * *

They came back on the fourth day, mid-afternoon, when the light is worst for seeing and best for being seen. The patrol boat came around the atoll's eastern shoulder flying customs colors, and behind the customs colors, unmistakable to anyone who'd spent a decade in Voss offices, flew the flag of ownership. Not literally. Metaphorically. In the way the boat held its wake, in the way it approached without hurry, because hurry is for people who fear refusal.

Mara Iles stood at the rail.

I recognized her silhouette before I recognized her face, which is a strange thing to say about a woman I'd sat across from three hundred times. But silhouettes don't lie the way faces are trained to. Hers was the same silhouette from the Singapore office: the slight forward lean, the weight on the balls of her feet, a woman permanently about to step into a room and improve it.

"Boarding party," Quentin said quietly beside me. "Customs inspection. We have paperwork?"

"We have paperwork," I said. "Forged by my former department. It will pass, because I designed the forgery protocol and I made it pass-proof against exactly this kind of inspection." A beat. "Which means it will fail, because Mara knows my work the way I know hers."

"Then why forge it?"

"Because the forgery isn't the point. The point is that we *have* paperwork. Guilty people scramble. Innocent people produce documents with mild irritation. We are going to be mildly irritated, Quentin. Can you be mildly irritated?"

"I'm already there."

The patrol boat came alongside with a fender's width of precision. Mara stepped across first—of course she did; lieutenants who send others first are lieutenants who get replaced—and her boots hit our deck with the confidence of a woman walking into property she technically owned.

"Tomas Reyes." Her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her face agreed to it. "They told me you were dead. Or worse—freelance."

"Mara. You look well. Retirement suits you."

"I don't retire. I rotate." She turned to Quentin, extending a hand. "Mara Iles, maritime compliance. We have boarding authority under the regional customs compact. Routine inspection. You'll hardly notice us."

Nobody believed that, including her, and the beauty of it was that everyone proceeded anyway, because that is how civilization works: a mutual agreement not to say the true thing aloud.

* * *

They searched for six hours.

I will give Voss this much: their people are thorough. Two officers went through the galley with the tenderness of tax auditors, lifting floorboards, unscrewing panel trim, reading Junie's paperback novels as though contraband might be hidden in chapter seven. Another pair took the engine room, where Sef received them with a monologue about fuel-line corrosion so dense and technical that one of them actually apologized for interrupting. Priya answered questions about dive logs with a diver's gift for tedium—bottom times, surface intervals, nitrogen loads—until their eyes glazed.

I played my part. I sat in the wheelhouse with a mug of tea and reviewed maintenance logs with visible boredom, and when Mara finally came to find me—I knew she would; she always saved me for last, a habit from the old days, saving the best interrogation for dessert—she leaned against the doorframe and studied me with the expression of a woman doing a valuation.

"Ballast," she said.

"Pardon?"

"Your idea, wasn't it? The epoxy-sealed floor panels in the refit spec. 'Security against tampering.' You wrote that memo. I remember because I asked you why a trawler needed anti-tamper ballast and you gave me a forty-minute presentation on hull integrity." She smiled. "I've been thinking about that memo for four days, Tomas."

"Four days?"

"Since the *Corvus* was flagged leaving Darwin. Since I pulled her history and found my own company's fingerprints on her refit." She stepped closer. "You hid something in the ballast. I don't know what. But you used our methods, and our methods are the only ones I trust, which means I know they're there, and knowing is almost as good as having."

Here is what I did not do: flinch, sweat, glance toward the keel, or perform any of the theatrics that guilt demands. Here is what I did instead, drawing on eleven years of watching men lie to Mara Iles and die of it: I yawned.

"Mara," I said, "if I wanted to hide something from you, would I use a method you personally signed off on? Think. You taught me that the best concealment is the one your opponent has already imagined. So yes—I sealed the ballast panels. With epoxy. Because the *Corvus* is twenty years old, her keel bolts are corroding, and if you crack those panels open in open water, you will find rust, sludge, and iron, and you will also find yourself explaining to the syndicate why you flooded a customs inspection." I sipped my tea. "Open them if you like. I'll watch. It'll be the most interesting thing to happen on this boat all week."

She stared at me for a long time. And I understood, in that moment, the terrible symmetry of our education: she couldn't tell whether I was lying, because I had learned to lie from her, and she had learned to detect lies from liars like me, and we had spiraled together into a place where truth and forgery wore the same clothes.

That's when I understood the memo trick would work—not because she believed me, but because she *couldn't afford not to*. If she cracked the ballast and found nothing, she'd look paranoid. If she cracked it and found the ledgers, she'd have to explain how she knew to look, which meant explaining her knowledge of internal concealment protocols, which meant explaining her familiarity with exactly the kind of evidence that ends careers. Mara Iles was trapped by her own competence. I had merely built the trap out of parts she'd supplied.

"Rust," she said finally.

"Sludge," I confirmed. "And iron. Very boring iron."

Her eyes held mine a moment longer, and in them I saw the calculation finish and file itself away under *inconclusive—revisit later*, which in Mara's taxonomy was the closest thing to defeat she permitted herself.

* * *

They left at dusk. No citations, no seizures, just Mara at the rail with that forward lean, delivering her farewell like a promise wrapped in courtesy.

"Lovely boat," she said. "Terrible survey history, though. These old hulls. You never know what's loose in them." Her gaze dropped, briefly, deliberately, to the waterline. "Do take care of your ballast, Tomas. Neglect sinks more ships than storms."

"Goodbye, Mara," I said. "Give my regards to the shareholders. Tell them the books balance."

The patrol boat pulled away into the red water, and none of us moved until the sound of her engines dissolved into the ordinary noise of sea and wind. Then Quentin exhaled, and Junie laughed—one sharp, disbelieving bark—and Old Sef, from the engine room hatch, said the only thing he'd said all day:

"She'll be back."

"Yes," I said. I was still holding my tea. My hand, which had betrayed no tremor for six hours, chose that moment to shake hard enough to rattle the mug against my teeth. "But she'll come back certain next time. Today she left uncertain, and uncertainty is the only currency we have. So tonight, while she's recalculating, we move."

"Move where?" Quentin asked.

"Move the ledgers," I said. "Out of the ballast. Into something she'll never suspect twice." I looked at the four faces around me—runaways, liars, and cooks, every one of them owing somebody—and felt the strange, unfamiliar weight of being trusted with a plan. "Because Mara Iles doesn't make the same mistake once. Next time she boards this ship, she comes with cutting torches and no pretense at all. We have maybe two days before she decides uncertainty is more expensive than paranoia."

Quentin nodded slowly. "Two days to do what, exactly?"

"To decide," I said, "what we're willing to become in order to survive what's coming. Because the ledgers aren't just evidence anymore. They're a countdown."
