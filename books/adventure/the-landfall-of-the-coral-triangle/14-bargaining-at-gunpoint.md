## 14. Bargaining at Gunpoint

There's a particular quality to the light when a gun is pointed at you—not dramatic, not cinematic, just flat and honest, like the world has finally stopped lying about what it is. The morning sun came over the reef in a hard white sheet, and Mara Iles stood on my deck with a sidearm held low and casual, the way you'd hold a clipboard, and I thought: *this is the most truthful light I've ever stood in.*

"Quentin." She said my name like we were old friends splitting a bar tab. "You look terrible."

"I've been at sea." I spread my hands. "The spa facilities are limited."

Behind her, four of her men had come over the Corvus's rail with the practiced boredom of people who did this weekly. They'd boarded from the patrol boat that had been sitting on our horizon for two days like a debt itself—patient, compounding, inevitable. My crew was lined up along the starboard side by one of them: Tomas with his hands laced behind his head like he'd done it before, Priya with her chin up and her eyes doing arithmetic, Old Sef leaning against the mast as if this were all mildly beneath him, and Junie—Junie looking at me with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it once, at fifteen, watching adults decide something about my life without me.

I hated it on her face more than I'd ever hated it on mine.

"You found it," Mara said. Not a question.

"Mara—"

"Don't insult us both. We tracked your fuel purchases through three ports. We know where you went, how long you stayed, and that you came back out through a strait no sane pilot would attempt." Her smile didn't move but something behind it did, some small recalibration. "That was impressive, by the way. Grounding the *Meridian* on the shoals. Voss is going to have words with me about that."

"Tell them it was the weather."

"Quentin." She sighed, and for a moment—just a moment—the charm dropped away and I saw the actual machinery underneath, the cold bookkeeping of a woman who had doubled my deadline with a smile two years ago and meant every second of it. "Let's not do theater. You're standing on a rotting trawler with no fuel, no mast worth the name, and a hold full of paper that could hang half the board of my employer. I'm offering you a way out. The least you can do is let me finish the sentence."

*The hold full of paper.* So they knew about the ledgers. Of course they knew. Tomas had hidden them in the ballast using methods he'd learned inside Voss's own accounting department, and I'd allowed myself six hours of feeling clever before the patrol boat appeared. There is no hiding from an organization that taught you the hiding places.

"Finish the sentence," I said.

"The find comes to us. All of it—the ledgers, the charts, whatever else the wreck gave up. In exchange, your debt is erased. Not restructured. Erased. Clean slate, Quentin. You walk away owing nothing to anyone." She let that land, watched it land, the way she always did—Mara understood debt the way priests understand sin, as the fundamental condition of the human animal. "And the crew disperses. Each of them goes their own way. No black marks, no follow-up, no interest accruing in the dark. They sail off into their separate sunsets and nobody thinks about any of them again."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we take everything anyway, and the debt survives, and I've been authorized to make an example of the captain who cost us a patrol boat." She said it lightly, almost apologetically, like a sommelier describing the house wine. "I'd genuinely rather not. Examples are expensive. Bodies create paperwork. I'm an optimist, Quentin—I told you that. I believe things work out. For us."

*Dangerous optimists.* Edda Kwan had never said those exact words, but she'd described the type in her hospice bed with her voice down to a whisper: *the ones who believe everything works out, because so far it always has—for them. The trick, girl, is becoming someone they can't afford.*

I looked at my crew.

Tomas caught my eye first. He gave me nothing—no nod, no shake, just his level accountant's gaze that said *your call, and I'll live with either answer, because I already lived with worse.* Priya's eyes were still doing arithmetic; now I could read the sum. Four of them, one gun visible, probably two more below decks. Bad odds. But Priya had spent her life reading reefs, and reefs teach you that the dangerous thing is never the rock—it's the water moving around it.

Old Sef was watching the sky. Actually watching the sky, over the shoulder of the man holding a rifle near him, cataloguing cloud shapes while his life hung in the balance, because Sef had forty years afloat and had long ago concluded that weather kills more sailors than men do and he wasn't going to break the habit for a little thing like armed robbery. I loved him for it. I don't know when that happened—when these four became people I *loved* rather than liabilities with useful skills—but there it was, sitting in my chest like ballast, heavy and stabilizing and impossible to jettison.

Junie was staring at her feet. Sixteen years old, runaway, nightmare-haunted, the kid who navigated us through uncharted shoals at night by memory of her uncle's fishing charts and saved every one of our lives—and she was staring at her feet because somewhere in her short brutal experience, adults deciding things had only ever gone one way.

*Bring whoever sails it home alive.*

Edda's promise sat in me like a swallowed stone. I had made it to a dying woman in exchange for a map, thinking it was a formality, thinking promises were currency like anything else—you issue them, you spend them, the ledger balances. I had spent two years learning that some currencies don't convert.

"Here's my problem, Mara," I said.

"Please. Share."

"You're offering to erase a number." I turned to face her fully, and I was surprised to find my hands weren't shaking. Somewhere between the cyclone and the strait, apparently, I'd used up all my shaking. "Two hundred and sixty thousand, last statement, though your interest calculations could bend light. And you're right—that number has been the whole shape of my life. Every storm I chased, every mile I ran, every bad decision, all of it downstream of that number. I have spent years organizing my entire existence around avoiding the moment when someone like you came to collect." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's hat. "So you'd think erasing it would be the easiest yes of my life."

"But?"

"But you also want the crew dispersed." I shook my head slowly. "And here's the thing I've figured out, Mara, and it took me embarrassingly long—an engineer who talks to engines because people disappointed him, a diver who maps everyone she meets because mapping keeps them safe, a cook who reads the sky instead of the room, and a sixteen-year-old who flinches at raised voices and then steers a burning ship through shoals that should have killed us all. That's my crew. That's—" My throat did something inconvenient. I pushed through it. "That's the first family I've ever had that I chose, and that chose me back, and you're asking me to sell them for a clean balance sheet."

"Quentin." Mara's voice softened into something almost kind, which was somehow worse than the gun. "They're strangers. You met them weeks ago. Whatever you think you've built out here, it's salt water and proximity. It doesn't survive shore. Take the deal. Go chase your storms. In a year you won't remember their surnames."

"That's the difference between us," I said. "You think love is a liability that appreciates or depreciates depending on the quarter. I've been running the numbers my whole life and I'm telling you as a professional: you're wrong about which assets matter."

Something flickered across her face—irritation, maybe, or the faint discomfort of a person encountering a variable her model didn't contain.

"Captain." One of her men shifted. "We taking the hold or not?"

"In a minute." She hadn't raised her voice. She never needed to. "Last chance, Quentin. I want to be clear about what refusal costs. It isn't just you. Refuse, and they're witnesses, and witnesses get processed. That girl especially—unregistered minor, no papers, no family claiming her. Do you know what happens to kids like that in the system? Because I do. I've seen the intake forms." She tilted her head. "Refusing to save yourself is romantic. Refusing to save *them* is just vanity."

And there it was. The hook under the bait. She'd found the exact pressure point—my promise, my guilt, my brand-new terrifying capacity to be hurt through other people—and pressed it with a professional's thumb.

I thought about saying yes. God help me, I actually considered it. Hand over the ledgers, take the clean slate, and then spend the rest of my life finding another way to surface what Edda spent thirty years buying—the truth, the names, the ship that killed her family sunk twice, once by water and once by insurance arithmetic. There were other ways. There were always other ways. I could tell myself that. I could tell myself I was being strategic, sacrificing the copies to keep the crew, fighting another day.

But Edda's letters were in my cabin, and I'd read them again last night by lamplight, and one line kept surfacing like a body that wouldn't stay down: *A truth you trade away isn't surfaced, girl. It's just sold to a new owner. I didn't spend thirty years buying it so it could change hands.*

And underneath that, simpler and heavier: *bring whoever sails it home alive.*

Both halves of the promise. Both pulling. And Mara offering me the elegant lie that I could keep one by selling the other.

"No," I said.

Mara exhaled through her nose. "No?"

"No. Not trading the ledgers, not dispersing the crew, not taking your deal. All of it stays. All of *them* stay. That's my answer."

"Quentin—"

"You want to know the funny part?" I laughed, and it came out rougher than I intended. "This is the first time in my adult life I've ever chosen something over survival. Debt, storms, syndicates—I've run from all of it, professionally, for years. Running is the one skill I have certifications in. And you just offered me the exit and I'm standing here telling you no, because it turns out there are four people on this rotten boat whose company I prefer to my own freedom." I looked her dead in the eye. "Love against duty, Mara. I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds. Say it anyway: I choose them."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The reef clattered softly against the hull. Somewhere below, the bilge pump cycled, faithful and absurd.

Then Mara smiled, and it was the real one this time, the one from the meeting in Darwin, the one that meant the deadline was doubling.

"Well," she said. "That's unfortunate."

She nodded to her men. "Take the hold. Ballast too—we know where they hid it, Tomas, don't look surprised, you learned that trick in our own warehouse. And bring the captain. Ms. Oyelaran is going to come aboard the *Meridian* and explain her decision to people with less patience than me."

Hands closed on my arms. Junie made a sound—small, furious, sixteen—and started forward, and Priya's arm shot out and caught her, and I wanted to say something to them, something captain-like and reassuring, but what came out was just:

"Sef. Watch the sky."

He nodded, solemn as church. "Always, girl. Always."

They walked me to the rail. Below, the patrol boat's deck waited, grey and orderly, and beyond it the open sea lay flat and blue and completely indifferent to the fact that I had finally, at thirty-four years old, become someone who could lose something worse than money.

As they lifted me over the side, I heard Tomas say quietly, behind me, in the tone of a man checking a figure he'd already calculated twice:

"Not yet. Wait for the transfer."

I didn't know what he meant.

But I'd spent enough years around accountants to know what it sounded like when one of them was watching a number move—and I let them take me aboard smiling.
