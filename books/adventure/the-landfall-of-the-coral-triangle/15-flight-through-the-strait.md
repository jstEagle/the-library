## 15. Flight Through the Strait

Here's a thing nobody tells you about running away: you spend years memorizing exits, and then one night somebody hands you a wheel and says *you're the exit now*.

Quentin said it at 2340 hours, in the dark, with the patrol boat's searchlight sweeping the water behind us like a lighthouse that had decided to become a predator. She didn't say it dramatically. That was the worst part. She just put her hand on my shoulder — Junie's shoulder, seventeen years old, four months of sea time, zero qualifications — and said, "You know these waters better than any of us. Take us through."

And because I am apparently incapable of saying no to people who feed me, I took the wheel.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've learned from Old Sef that a story without its weather is just gossip.

We'd been hiding behind Pulo Kecil for two days, engine cold, running dark, while Tomas counted ballast bolts and Quentin stared at the horizon like she could out-stare it. The patrol boat had come once before, back in chapter whatever-it-was of our collective bad decisions, and Mara Iles had stood on its deck smiling her smile — the one that meant she'd already done the arithmetic on all of us and found we came up short.

Then tonight, Priya, who'd been swimming the reef line every evening like a woman visiting old friends, came up the ladder faster than I'd ever seen her move and said two words: "They're moving."

The patrol boat was coming around the atoll's northern point. No pretense this time. No customs inspection, no polite hail on the radio. Just running lights and a wake like a knife being drawn.

Quentin made the call in about nine seconds. "We can't outrun them in open water. They're faster, they're armed, and Iles knows exactly what we are." She looked at the chart spread on the table, then at me, and something in her face did a thing I didn't have a name for yet. "Junie. Your uncle fished these straits."

"My uncle," I said, "was a bastard who beat me for reading his charts wrong."

"Did you read them wrong?"

"No."

"Then you know them right." She tapped the water between Pulo Kecil and the mainland shelf, where the chart showed nothing but blank paper and a warning legend: *unsurveyed — local knowledge only.* "There's a channel through there. Sef thinks so. He remembers a gap in the reef from forty years ago, but he can't hold the details anymore, and he'll tell you honestly he shouldn't be steering tonight. Can you?"

I want to tell you I said something brave. What I actually said was: "If I kill us all, does that count as bringing everyone home alive?"

Quentin laughed — a real one, short and startled, the kind you don't expect from a woman with syndicate debt and a dead prospector's promise sitting on her chest like ballast. "Ask me again if we make it."

So that's how I ended up here, at the helm of the *Corvus*, in the dark, with my uncle's voice in my head and his fists somewhere further back than that.

* * *

You should understand what my uncle's charts were, because they weren't charts, not really. They were a lifetime of grudges against the sea, drawn in pencil on the backs of cigarette packets and the margins of tide tables. He fished the straits between the atolls for thirty years, and he hated every meter of them, and he wrote down everything the water ever did to him. *Shoal moves after big swell — trust nothing east of the marker palm.* *Coral head here, sits two feet under at low tide, cost me a propeller, cost me a week of wages.* Little annotations in a handwriting like barbed wire. I used to copy them out at night, in the dark of the room I wasn't allowed a lamp in, tracing the channels with my finger until I could see them with my eyes shut.

I thought I was memorizing them to escape him. Turns out I was memorizing them to escape *with*.

"Talk to me," Quentin said. She'd stationed herself beside the helm, not touching anything, just present, the way she'd been since the storm — since Junie nearly drowned and Quentin went in after her without a second's hesitation, which is a thing I still haven't figured out how to repay and suspect I'm not supposed to.

"There's a passage," I said. "My uncle called it the Needle's Eye. It runs between the outer shoal and the coral shelf, maybe sixty meters wide at high water. It's high water in—" I checked my watch, hands shaking only a little, thank you very much— "forty minutes. We go now, we scrape. We wait, they catch us in open water where they're faster."

"So we scrape."

"We scrape."

Behind us, the patrol boat's searchlight found the empty anchorage where we'd been an hour ago and paused there, thinking. You could almost hear it thinking.

Old Sef appeared at my elbow with a mug of tea I hadn't asked for. He set it in the holder by the wheel, which is a thing helmsmen do for each other, and said, "Your uncle drew the shoals as he hated them. Hate's honest. Better than most surveys." Then he went forward to stand lookout, because that's Sef — he gives you exactly one sentence of wisdom and then gets out of the way of it.

Tomas killed every light we had. Priya went up the mast stub with a handheld spotlight she'd rigged with a red filter — red doesn't carry far over water, doesn't wreck your night eyes — and became our eyes where mine couldn't reach. And the *Corvus*, trawler, refugee, home, turned her nose toward a gap in the reef that existed officially nowhere at all.

* * *

Here is what navigating by memory feels like: it feels like walking through your childhood house during a power cut. You know where the furniture is. You know where the stairs turn. But some part of you keeps waiting for the lights to come on and prove you wrong.

"Slow ahead," I said, and felt Tomas ease the engine down through the deck plates. The patrol boat was maybe three kilometers back and closing, but speed was death in here. Speed was the coral head that cost my uncle a propeller. In the straits, slow was survival, and patience was a weapon, and nobody had ever told me that the things I learned to survive my uncle would be the same things I'd use to survive *this*.

"Priya sees white water starboard," Quentin relayed, quiet. "That mean anything?"

"It means the shoal's awake. Good. My uncle's notes say the breaking water marks its edge — if we keep the breakers off our starboard bow and the dark water off our port, we're in the channel."

"And if both sides go quiet?"

"Then we're lost, and you should all start being nice to each other, because apparently that's when people say true things."

Another laugh from her, tighter this time. "Noted."

The first hour was the worst hour of my life, and I include the night I left home with a duffel bag and a stolen wad of cash in that ranking. The channel narrowed. The breakers on our starboard side got louder, a long continuous roar like the sea grinding its teeth, and the dark water to port stayed dark in a way that felt less like deep water and more like *waiting* water. Twice Priya called down corrections — "hard to port, ten degrees, there's coral under the surface, no white on it" — and twice I obeyed a woman hanging off a mast stub in the dark on faith alone, and both times the *Corvus* slid through a gap I couldn't see and didn't argue with.

Somewhere in there my uncle's voice started up. It does that, when things get hard. *You'll amount to nothing. You read those charts like a girl.* Well. I was reading them like a girl right now, and a girl was keeping seventy meters of steel and five lives off the coral, so maybe girls should read more charts.

"You're doing well," Quentin said, and I realized I'd been muttering some of that out loud.

"I'm doing the only thing I know how to do."

"That's what doing well looks like. Nobody's calm inside. They just keep their hands steady anyway." A pause. "Sef taught me that. Or maybe a storm did. Hard to remember which."

The Needle's Eye proper came at us at 0117, according to my watch, which I was checking approximately every eleven seconds. Sixty meters wide, my uncle's note said, at high water, and the tide had turned twenty minutes ago, which meant we had maybe fifty meters of usable width and a current starting to run against us. Ahead, the channel bent left around a coral head my uncle had named, in his loving way, *the Widow*. There was a whole family of names like that in his margins. The Widow. the Hangman. Bastard's Teeth. I used to think he was dramatic.

I no longer think that.

"Widow's coming up," I said. "Hard to port around her, then straighten fast, or we kiss the Teeth on the far side."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that Sef should hold onto something."

"I heard my name," Sef called from the bow, placid as Sunday. "Holding."

We came around the Widow at four knots with her breaker-slick shoulders shining red in Priya's filtered light, close enough that I could have thrown a biscuit onto her coral, and the *Corvus* leaned into the turn like a tired animal doing one last favor, and then I straightened her up and there — there — was open black water beyond the Teeth, and the far side of the strait, and no more pencil-margined horrors between us and the sea room beyond.

I did not cry. I want that on the record. My eyes were simply responding to salt spray, of which there was a normal amount.

* * *

The patrol boat never made the Needle's Eye.

We heard it before we saw it — engines pushing too hard for the water they were in, the arrogance of a vessel whose captain had never needed to respect a chart in his life. Priya, still up her mast, reported the searchlight swinging across the entrance, sweeping, confident, hunting.

"They're going in," she called down. "They're actually going in."

"They don't have a Junie," Quentin said.

What happened next took about ninety seconds and sounded, from our distance, like the sea clearing its throat. First a change in the engine note — that sudden hollow *boom-boom-boom* of a propeller suddenly finding air instead of water, which every fisherman's kid knows is the sound of a boat hitting bottom. Then the searchlight swung wildly, uselessly, painting the sky and the breakers and nothing helpful. Then a lot of shouting carried flat across the water, the specific flat shouting of men discovering that confidence is not a draft.

"They're on the shoal," Priya confirmed, sliding down the mast with the biggest grin I have ever seen on a human face. "Hard aground. Bow's up on the coral like a beaching whale. They are *not* going anywhere tonight."

"Not going anywhere this *week*," Tomas said, appearing from below with the particular satisfaction of an ex-Voss accountant watching other people's assets take a write-down. "A patrol boat that size, grounded on coral? They'll need a commercial salvage tug. From the mainland. With paperwork. Voss paperwork." He smiled the small dry smile that was, for Tomas, the equivalent of dancing on a table. "I give them three days minimum before they're free, and a formal inquiry into why their captain ignored marked hazards after that."

"The Widow got her widow," Sef observed from the bow, and even in the dark I could hear him smiling.

Quentin put her hand on the back of my neck, brief and warm, the way you'd touch something you were afraid might not be real. "Junie Oyelaran," she said — she'd given me her surname somewhere around the cyclone, unasked, the way you hand someone a key — "remind me to never, ever get on your bad side."

"Too late," I said. "I'm already planning it."

But my hands were shaking now, the delayed kind, the kind that only starts when it's safe to shake, and she saw, and she didn't say anything about it, just steered me gently away from the wheel and into the galley where Old Sef pressed a fresh mug of tea into my hands like it was a medal.

* * *

We ran south through the small hours, dark and slow, putting distance on a patrol boat that was busy becoming a landmark. Toward dawn, the others slept in shifts, and I sat in the galley with Sef, who couldn't sleep either, and who had gotten out his ancient tin of biscuits and was dealing them out like cards.

"You knew," I said. "Back in Darwin. When you looked at the sky and said nothing. You knew things would be like this."

He considered that, chewing. "I knew the sky," he said. "People I'm still learning." He nudged the biscuit tin toward me. "You did well tonight, little navigator."

"Little navigator," I repeated. "Is that my name now?"

"Names are earned out here. That one's yours." He looked at me with his old, weathered face doing something soft around the edges. "Your uncle gave you a bad inheritance. But you made it yours. That's more than most of us manage with what we're given."

I didn't say anything, because my throat had gone unreliable, and Sef, being Sef, pretended not to notice and refilled my tea.

It was almost full light when Tomas came up from below at a pace that made us all look up. He had the radio log in his hand and an expression I'd learned to dread, because on this boat, good news traveled slowly and bad news moved like fire.

"Voss traffic on the band," he said. "Encrypted burst transmissions, but I caught fragments. Quentin needs to hear this." He hesitated, and I watched him decide whether to soften it, and watched him decide not to, because Tomas doesn't lie to crew. "They're not sending a salvage tug for the patrol boat. They're sending Mara Iles. Directly. To wherever we make landfall next."

Quentin came up the companionway already dressed, already awake, already wearing the face she wore when the arithmetic came out bad. "How long?" she asked.

Tomas checked his figures. "Depends on their fuel. But if we're reading this right—" he glanced at the fuel gauge, which had been trending toward empty for two days, toward *empty* the way a fever trends toward a number— "they won't need to chase us at all. We're going to run out of ocean before we run out of them."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The engine ticked. Somewhere behind us, the Widow ground patiently against a patrol boat's hull, keeping what she kept.

Quentin looked at the fuel gauge, then at each of us, and I watched her arrive at the same place we all were: the *Corvus* had maybe half a day of running left in her, and no harbor would take us, and the sea we'd just escaped was about to become the thing we were trapped in.

"Get me Sef," she said quietly. "We need to talk about what this ship can still do — and what she can't."
