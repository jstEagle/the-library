## 16. The Price of Survival

Forty years at sea teaches you a few things, and most of them are variations on the same lesson: everything ends, and it's better when you're the one who decides how.

I learned it first from my father's boat, the *Marianne*, which went down off Timor with half our livelihood in her hold and all of our luck. I learned it again from three marriages, two of them to women who rightly concluded that I loved the water more than them, and one to a woman who understood that the water was just where I kept my grief so it wouldn't spill into the kitchen. I learned it from engines, from charts, from the particular way a hull sounds when it's tired.

And now, on the morning the *Corvus* ran dry eleven miles short of anywhere, I learned it one more time.

* * *

The engine didn't die dramatically. That's not how old machines go. She coughed twice, dropped to a sputter, and then settled into a silence so complete I could hear Junie's cards shuffling in the wheelhouse and Priya's breathing from the foredeck where she was pretending to sleep. Forty years and I still flinch at that silence. A stopped engine is a question the sea asks you, and it only asks once.

"Fuel's done," I told Quentin, because she was standing next to me and someone had to say it out loud before we all started pretending otherwise. "Gauge is honest. Tanks are dry as a sermon."

Quentin looked at the gauge like it had insulted her mother. "We have the jerry cans."

"We have four jerry cans, which gets us maybe eight miles if the sea cooperates, and the sea hasn't cooperated since Darwin." I wiped my hands on a rag that had been black longer than Junie had been alive. "Nearest fuel is Kupang. Nearest anything is Kupang. We're eleven miles off a charted reef system and about nine hundred miles from a friend."

Tomas came down the ladder with his ledger-face on—the one he wears when the numbers have already told him something he doesn't want repeated aloud. He'd been doing sums since dawn. I could tell by the pencil behind his ear and the way he held his coffee without drinking it, like a prop in a play about a man drinking coffee.

"Even rationed," he said, "we can't make landfall. Not Indonesia, not Australia, not the fishing platforms. And the patrol boat—"

"The patrol boat is grounded," Junie said from the wheelhouse doorway. She'd earned the right to be smug about that; she'd threaded us through shoals no chart acknowledged while a Voss corvette tried to follow and ended up kissing the sand hard enough to need a tug. But there wasn't much smugness in her voice now. Smugness doesn't keep. Fear does.

"For now," Tomas said gently. "They'll float her off. They'll radio ahead. Every port in the triangle will have our description by Thursday." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to say the rest. Tomas always decides whether to say the rest; it's what makes him an ex-accountant instead of an ex-convict. "And we're carrying the ledgers."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The ledgers. Three centuries of Voss sins in oilcloth and brass, sitting in our bilge like ballast made of guilt. Proof that could hang men—or get us all drowned quietly somewhere with no witnesses but fish.

"So we dump them overboard," Priya said from the foredeck, not opening her eyes. "Weight for weight, they're worth less than the anchor chain."

"They're worth more than all of us," Quentin said. "That's the problem."

She said it quietly, but I've spent forty years listening to quiet things. The sea doesn't shout either. It just keeps saying the same true thing until you hear it.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about owning nothing: every decision you make, you make with your whole self. A man with money makes decisions with a fraction of himself and hires the rest out. We were five people and a boat, and every choice cost all of each of us.

I went below to think, which is where I do my best thinking, in the engine room, where the machinery smells like honesty. The *Corvus* and I had an arrangement, she and I. I'd bought her at auction nineteen years ago, a gutted trawler with a name too long and a keel too honest, and I'd rebuilt her plank by plank, fitting by fitting, the way you rebuild anything worth keeping. She was the last thing I owned outright. No syndicate held paper on her. No bank. No woman. Just me and forty years of salt in her bones.

I put my palm flat against her frame plates and felt the heat draining out of the engine, slow as a body cooling.

That's when the thought arrived. It didn't knock. Thoughts like that never do—they just sit down beside you like they've always lived there.

* * *

I called them together on the aft deck, because I wanted sky overhead when I said it. You can say terrible things in an engine room and they stay contained, mechanical, survivable. Say them under open sky and they become real weather.

"I'm going to propose something," I said, "and I want you to let me finish before anyone throws anything. Junie, I'm looking at you specifically."

Junie raised both hands, holding a wrench in one of them, which rather undermined the gesture.

"We can't reach port. The patrol will float free within a day or two, and when it does, we're a slow trawler full of evidence with nowhere to hide. If the Voss take those ledgers back, Edda Kwan died for nothing, and worse—everything she bought with thirty years of savings dies with her." I looked at each of them. Priya, arms crossed, already three moves ahead and hating it. Tomas, whose face had gone carefully blank, which meant he'd seen this coming. Quentin, who hadn't blinked once. "But we don't need to reach port. We need to reach the reef system six miles south. There's a cay there—a triangle of coral, dry at low tide, big enough to beach her. And there's a channel through the shoals that I've known about since before any of you could swim."

"You want to run aground on purpose," Priya said.

"I want to scuttle her," I said. "On the reef. With the ledgers in the hold."

The wrench hit the deck. Junie had thrown it after all; she just waited until I finished, which I'll count as growth.

"You're talking about burning the *Corvus*," Quentin said. Her voice did something strange on the word *Corvus*, bent in the middle like a knee giving way.

"I'm talking about ending her the way she should end," I said. "Doing something. There are two ways a boat dies, girl. One is slow, in some government yard, stripped for scrap by strangers who never knew her name. The other is fast, on her own terms, with her crew standing on dry land watching her go out like a captain." I heard my own voice crack on *captain* and decided to let it. At my age you earn your cracks. "Those ledgers burned in a fire we set—that's a story the Voss can't buy back, can't dredge up, can't threaten anyone over. Evidence destroyed by circumstance. Tragic. Unfortunate. Nobody's fault. And nobody left alive who can prove otherwise, because the proof will be slag at the bottom of a coral lagoon."

"And the *Corvus*?" Junie asked. She'd come to stand near me, close enough that I could see she'd been crying already, which she'd deny under oath. "Sef, she's your—"

"She's a boat," I said, which was the biggest lie I've told in forty years of telling small ones, and everyone on that deck knew it, and nobody called it, because that's what family is: people who let you lie kindly and don't make you say the true version out loud.

* * *

We voted, because Quentin insisted—we'd voted on everything since the strait, a habit she'd built deliberately, brick by brick, so that no one aboard ever again had decisions made *for* them. I knew what that habit cost her. I'd heard enough nightmares through bulkheads to piece together the shape of a childhood where other people voted and she lived with the results.

Priya voted yes first. "It's the only move that ends this," she said. "I map reefs for a living. I know exactly what kind of grave that lagoon makes. Nothing comes back from it." Then, quieter: "Nothing *should*."

Tomas voted yes, and I watched him do it with relief, because Tomas had been carrying the ledgers like a second spine. "My old employers taught me that documents are only dangerous while they exist," he said. "It's the one lesson I'm glad to have kept."

Junie took longest. She looked at the boat, then at me, and I saw her doing arithmetic of her own—not numbers, but debts. What she owed the *Corvus* for the nights she'd slept safe in her fo'c'sle. What she owed me for teaching her the engine, for never asking about the uncle, for standing between her and the world's paperwork.

"Yes," she finally said. "But we save what we can carry. The photos. The letters. Edda's letters." She pointed at the deck. "The truth doesn't have to burn. Just the proof of it."

Clever girl. Out of the mouths of runaways.

Four yeses. One vote left.

Quentin stood at the rail with her back to us, looking south toward the reef we couldn't see yet, and for a long time she didn't turn around. I've watched her calculate distances across the Gulf of Carpentaria, across oceans, across the whole geography of running away—and I recognized the posture. She was measuring something. Not miles.

"My whole life," she said, still facing the water, "I've paid for things by leaving them. Boats. Cities. Names. People." A pause long enough to sail through. "This is the first debt I can pay by staying put on a rock and letting something go."

"Sef's boat," Priya said softly. "Your call shouldn't outweigh his."

"It doesn't." Quentin turned around, and her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and I understood that she considered wiping them a kind of dishonesty. "It's his sacrifice. But it's my promise. Edda gave it to *me*. Bring whoever sails it home alive. Tell no one what they find." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I've been trying to figure out how to keep both halves. Burn the ledgers, keep the crew. This is how. This is the only how there is."

She looked at me.

"Yes," she said, and her voice broke clean through the middle of the word, and I will carry the sound of it to whatever grave I eventually get, which may well be a coral one. "Yes. God forgive me. Yes."

* * *

Afterward, I went below alone, because some farewells you do in company and some you don't.

I opened every locker. I took down the photograph of my father taping over the crack in the *Marianne*'s wheelhouse glass, 1961, squinting into a sun that would kill him nine years later. I took the brass compass I'd navigated by since I was fifteen, though it wandered two degrees west and always had, like it was homesick for somewhere it had never been. I took the little tin of my third wife's tea, long stale, that I'd carried through two refits and one near-sinking because throwing it away would have meant admitting she was gone and keeping it meant she was just waiting, and I preferred waiting.

The rest stayed. The tools. The spare parts. The galley pots Priya had scrubbed. Junie's hammock hook. The dent in the companionway frame where Quentin had cracked her head our first week out and cursed in three languages. A boat isn't wood and iron. A boat is the shape that people leave in her, and I was about to sink the shape of everyone I loved into six fathoms of coral water, and I found—to my considerable surprise—that I could bear it. Because that's the trick of it, the last lesson, the one that takes forty years: you don't lose a thing by giving it a good ending. You fix it there. Like an anchor. Like a name carved somewhere it can't be sanded off.

The *Corvus* would end as a reef marker, a story, a grave with a view. Better than a scrapyard. Better than evidence in a syndicate's strongroom. Better than rusting at anchor while the world got worse around her.

I climbed back on deck with my arms full of tin and brass and photographs, and the crew was already at work—Priya plotting the channel, Junie rigging lines, Tomas calculating tide windows, Quentin at the helm with her jaw set like a woman steering into weather instead of away from it.

"Six miles south," I said. "Slack water at 1400. We'll want the flood tide behind us when we drive her onto the cay."

Quentin nodded and put the wheel over, and the *Corvus* leaned into the turn like she knew—like she'd known for years, patient old thing, and had simply been waiting for me to say it out loud.

Six miles. For the first time in forty years, I wasn't running from anything, or toward anything. I was just taking my family home the long way, by way of a funeral.

Behind us, far astern, a smudge of smoke sat on the horizon where a grounded patrol boat was being pulled off the sand. We had maybe a day. Maybe less.

I went forward to check the tow lines, and I caught myself humming the old hymn my father used to sing when the weather turned, the one about building your house on rock instead of sand.

Coral, in our case. Close enough.
