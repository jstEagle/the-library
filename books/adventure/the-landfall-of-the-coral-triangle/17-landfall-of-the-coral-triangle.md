## 17. Landfall of the Coral Triangle

The Corvus burned the way old things burn—slowly at first, then all at once, as if she'd only been waiting for permission.

I stood at the wheel and gave it to her.

"Course is two-seven-five," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "We ride her in."

Nobody argued. That was the strange part. Forty hours since Sef had stood on a fuel-less deck and proposed killing his own ship, forty hours of voting and crying and Junie refusing to look at anyone, and now that the moment had actually arrived, there was nothing left to argue about. The sea had taken our fuel, the patrol boat was somewhere behind us licking its wounds on a shoal, and the ledgers were still in the hold—the ledgers, Edda's thirty years of savings and grief pressed into paper, the thing that could buy my freedom or hang every one of us.

The thing I had promised to bury.

"Quentin." Priya stood beside me, salt-stiff hair plastered to her skull, watching the triangle of coral rise off the bow like a rumor. "Say it out loud. One more time. So we all hear you choose it."

"I'm choosing it," I said. "We beach her, we burn her, and what's down there goes with her. No proof. No leverage. No debt paid with other people's lives." I swallowed. "Edda asked me to tell no one what we found. Turns out the hardest part isn't keeping the secret from the world. It's keeping it from myself."

Tomas made a sound from the companionway—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. "You know what an accountant would call this?"

"Don't," Priya said.

"A controlled demolition," Tomas said anyway. "Write-off. You torch the asset so nobody can audit your soul."

"That man spent too many years at Voss," Sef rumbled from the engine hatch, where he'd been feeding the fire's appetite with everything flammable that wasn't structural. "Come away from there, accountant. We're done with books."

* * *

The approach was ugly and perfect.

A triangle of coral doesn't invite you; it permits you, grudgingly, the way a landlord permits a tenant he intends to ruin. Sef took us through the gap on the western face with the tide just high enough to forgive us, hands moving on the wheel like he was conducting something older than music. Junie stood at the bow calling depths, her voice cracking on every number but never wrong once. The reef rose around us in shelves of blue and bone-white, and the water over it was so clear it looked like the ocean had decided, at the end, to be honest with us too.

"Hundred meters," Junie called. "Eighty. Fifty—Quentin, the sand shelf starts right there, you'll feel her take the bottom."

"I know," I said. "That's the idea."

Behind me, in the hold, the fire I'd lit twenty minutes ago was doing its patient work. Not the kind of blaze that takes a ship fast—I'd learned enough from watching Voss do their dirty arithmetic to know that a slow burn leaves time for regret, and I wanted the time. I wanted to stand in the smoke of it and feel how much it cost, because a sacrifice you don't feel isn't a sacrifice, it's just logistics.

The Corvus groaned. Her hull kissed coral with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then again, longer, and then we were sliding up the sand shelf with the whole dying weight of her, listing hard to port, water already boiling white around her stern.

"Everyone off," I said. "Now."

Sef went last. Of course he did. He stopped at the gangway with his hand flat against the bulkhead, the way you'd touch a horse you'd raised from a foal, and I watched him say something to her that I will never repeat to anyone, because some prayers belong to the person praying them.

Then we were all in the shallows, five people and a dinghy and a duffel of things that suddenly mattered absurdly much—water, the tarp, Junie's uncle's charts, Edda's letters—and behind us the Corvus settled onto the coral like a tired animal lying down in the sun.

* * *

We dragged ourselves onto the widest point of the triangle and turned to watch.

The smoke came first, thin and gray, curling out of her hatches like breath. Then a tongue of orange found the deck seams, and the wind off the lagoon leaned into it, and the Corvus began to burn in earnest.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The fire crackled. Somewhere under the crackle, if you knew where to listen—and I did, God help me, I could have drawn the layout of that hold blindfolded by then—you could hear the exact spot where the ledgers were. Shelf three, aft of the water tanks, wrapped in Tomas's oilcloth. Decades of Voss sinkings-for-insurance. The name of the ship that killed Edda's family, written in a handwriting Tomas recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

Dissolving. Page by page, into ash and steam and the indifferent Pacific wind.

"It should feel like more," Junie said finally. She was sitting with her knees pulled up, chin on them, firelight on her face. "It should feel like winning or losing. It just feels like—" She waved a hand at the burning ship, at the horizon, at everything.

"Grief," Priya said. "And release. They come braided together. You don't get one without pulling the other through."

"You'd know," Junie said, and there was no edge in it, which from Junie was practically an embrace.

"I'd know," Priya agreed.

Tomas sat down heavily in the sand beside me. He smelled of oilcloth and smoke and the particular exhaustion of a man who has betrayed his employers twice in one week, once with his hands and once with his heart. "For what it's worth," he said quietly, "the numbers say you made the wrong choice. That evidence was worth more than any debt. It could have bought all of us out ten times over."

"The numbers also said the Titanic was unsinkable," I said.

He huffed. Almost a laugh. "That's not—"

"Tomas. Look at it." I nodded at the fire. "Every page going up is a page Mara Iles can't use to make an example of us. Every page is a name Edda wanted protected until the truth could be told properly, not extorted with. We didn't lose the story. We just lost the proof. And proof," I said, and heard Edda's voice underneath my own, "was always the syndicate's currency. Not truth. Proof. Truth they can't touch if nobody holds it hostage."

He was quiet a while. The fire roared, and a section of the deck collapsed inward with a soft whump, sending sparks spiraling up into the darkening sky like the world's most expensive fireworks.

"My mother kept a recipe box," he said at last. "Burned in a house fire when I was nineteen. For years I mourned the recipes. Then one day I realized what I actually missed wasn't the cards. It was Sunday afternoons. The smell of her kitchen. The way she hummed off-key." He shrugged. "The paper was never the point. The paper was just where the point used to live."

"Yeah," I said. "That."

* * *

We camped that night on the coral, such as it was—a spine of ancient limestone no wider than a football pitch, tufted with salt-killed scrub, ringed by water so shallow and bright it seemed lit from below. Sef built a driftwood fire even though we had a perfectly good inferno already going fifty meters away, because Sef maintained that a cooking fire and a funeral fire are different animals and should never share wood.

He cooked, because Sef always cooked. Rice and tinned fish and the last of the limes, and we ate it sitting in a row like seabirds while the Corvus burned down to her waterline and the sky went from copper to violet to a black so complete the stars came out like spilled salt.

"She was a good ship," Sef said, to no one and everyone. "Forty-one years. She deserved better than this."

"She deserved better than carrying Voss evidence in her belly," Priya said gently.

"Everything deserves better than that," Sef said. "Ships. People. Me." He ladled more rice onto Junie's plate without asking, the way he'd been doing for weeks, and Junie let him, which was its own kind of miracle.

Later, when the others had curled into the tarp like puppies, I walked back down to the waterline alone.

The Corvus was still glowing. Low and sullen now, mostly embers, the superstructure gone, the hull a black ribcage against the phosphorescent water. At low tide you could walk almost to her, and I did, boots in six inches of warm sea, until I stood close enough to see into the hold.

Ash floated on the water there. Gray flakes of it, drifting in little constellations, catching the starlight. Ledger pages, or what was left of them. I crouched and touched the water and one of the flakes landed on my fingertip, weightless, illegible, gone.

"I'm sorry," I told Edda Kwan, out loud, to the ash. "You wanted it surfaced. I buried it at sea instead. I broke my promise to keep a bigger one—to bring them home alive. All four of them. Alive and free and unowned." My throat tightened. "If that's the wrong reading of your map, then haunt me. But do it gently. I've had enough storms this season."

The ember-light shifted. The hull ticked and settled. And I swear—I know what heat does to metal, I know about expansion and contraction, I've chased storms long enough to respect physics over sentiment—but I swear the Corvus sighed.

Or maybe that was me.

* * *

Morning came gray-gold and merciless, the way mornings do after you've burned something you loved. The Corvus was a skeleton now, half-submerged, waves combing through her ribs. Gulls had already claimed her. Life is quick like that; it doesn't wait for your grief to finish.

We packed the dinghy. There wasn't much: water, the tarp, the charts, the letters, a flare gun with two flares, and Sef's knife, which he surrendered to the duffel with visible pain. The nearest charted land was a fishing outpost ninety miles west—Junie's uncle's charts showed a passage, and for once the knowledge she'd run away from was the rope we'd climb down.

"One last vote," I said, standing at the tideline. "Not that it matters. Dinghy seats five either way. But I want it on record." I looked at each of them. "Does anyone here regret it? The burn. Say it now and I'll carry it, whatever it costs me."

Silence. The gulls screamed over the wreck.

Then Junie stood up, brushed sand off her knees, and said, "I regret nothing except that I never got to drive her," and Priya laughed for the first time in two days, and Tomas said something dry about depreciation schedules, and Sef just put his hand on my shoulder, heavy as an anchor, warm as a hearth.

"No regrets," Priya said. "Only debts. And we just paid the big one."

"Not all of them," I said.

They went still. They knew. Of course they knew—Mara Iles doesn't forget a debt just because the collateral burned. Somewhere behind us, in the world of phones and ports and men who smile while doubling deadlines, the Voss Syndicate was already recalculating. A burned ship, a missing crew, a vanished find. Mara would read that as theft. Mara would come collecting.

And there was nowhere left to run. No miles. No ship. Just a dinghy, ninety miles of open water, and whatever waited on the far shore.

"Sef," I said, turning toward the passage. "How's your dinghy seamanship?"

"Forty years afloat," he said, climbing aboard. "Three of them in boats smaller than this."

"Good." I took the tiller. The current caught us, gentle, insistent, pulling us west through the reef gap toward the open sea and whatever Mara Iles had waiting on the other side of it. "Because I have a feeling the next landfall is going to cost us."

The gulls wheeled over the black ribs of the Corvus, screaming their indifference, and the Coral Triangle closed behind us like a door.
