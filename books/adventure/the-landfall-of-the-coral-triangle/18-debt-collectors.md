## 18. Debt Collectors

The tide did most of the work. That felt right, somehow—after all the miles I'd put between myself and every consequence I'd ever owed, the ocean itself carried the *Corvus* the last hundred meters onto the coral shelf and set her down like a mother laying down a child she couldn't feed anymore.

We stood in the shallows and watched her burn. Sef had done it clean: diesel in the bilge, a timer on the stove, and forty years of seamanship distilled into one last act of arson with dignity. The fire found the ledgers in the hold first—I'd made sure of that, stacking them where the flames would reach them before anything else—and for a few minutes the smoke ran strange colors, ink and old paper and Voss Syndicate secrets going up in greasy black ribbons that the wind pulled apart and scattered over water no court would ever dredge.

Nobody spoke. Junie cried without noise, which is a skill I recognized because I'd taught it to myself at fourteen. Tomas held his hat against his chest like we were at a funeral, which, I suppose, we were. Priya kept her eyes on the horizon the whole time, and Old Sef kept his on the ship, and I kept mine on neither, because I was looking down the beach at the thing waiting for us there.

Three figures by the tree line. One of them had a satellite phone and good boots.

"Well," I said. "Company."

"You knew they'd come," Priya said. Not an accusation. Just checking her math.

"I hoped they wouldn't." I wrung saltwater out of my sleeve. "But I stopped betting on hope around the same time I started betting on storms."

The smoke leaned east. The *Corvus* groaned as her deck plates let go, one after another, like a slow round of applause for a performance nobody had paid to see. I thought about Edda Kwan in her hospice bed, folding that waterproofed chart into my hands like she was handing me a live grenade with the pin already gone. *Bring them home,* she'd said. All of them. That was the promise. The telling-no-one part had seemed like the hard half, back when I took it.

Turns out the hard half was what came after you kept it.

"Quentin," Sef said quietly. He'd read my face the way he read the sky—hours ahead of the weather everyone else could see coming. "Whatever you're calculating over there. Don't."

"It's simple arithmetic, old man."

"Simple arithmetic is how people die."

"And complicated arithmetic is how *crews* die." I looked at each of them, one at a time, because I wanted the picture. For later. For whatever later turned out to be. Tomas with his accountant's hands and his traitor's heart; Priya who mapped reefs and people with the same patient precision; Junie, nineteen years old and already carrying more survival than most people manage in a lifetime; Sef, who had given up his ship so the rest of us would have tomorrow morning.

I'd spent my whole adult life running distances instead of counting time. Miles to the storm cell. Kilometers to the fuel stop. Nautical leagues to anywhere but here. And now here I stood on a triangle of coral with no vessel, no fuel, no map worth burning, and the bill collector walking toward me across white sand, and there were finally no miles left. Only time. Time to do the one thing I'd never once done in thirty-eight years of motion.

Stand still.

* * *

Mara Iles came up the beach alone. The enforcers stayed back by the trees—one big, one bigger, both wearing the particular patience of men paid by the hour to be frightening. She'd changed since Darwin. Or maybe I'd just never seen her out of air conditioning before. Her shirt was pressed. Her boots were dry. Somewhere behind that pleasant face was a speedboat and a helicopter budget, while behind mine was a burning trawler and four people I'd have died for about six hours ago, which meant I needed to recalibrate, because dying for them wasn't on the menu anymore.

Dying *instead of* them might be.

"Captain Oyelaran." She smiled the way she always smiled, like a door opening onto a room you immediately wished you hadn't entered. "You look terrible."

"You should see the boat."

"Oh, I did. From about two klicks out. Very dramatic." She glanced at the pyre, and something flickered across her face—not regret, nothing so human as regret, more like an auditor noting a discrepancy. "You know, for a while there I thought you might actually make a run for Indonesian waters. I had money on it."

"Sorry to cost you."

"You didn't. I bet against you." She turned that smile full force on me. "I've been betting against you since the day you signed your first collection notice. It's the only consistent winner in my portfolio."

Behind me, gravel shifted—Sef and the others moving up the beach, closing ranks without discussing it, the way they'd been doing since the cyclone. I raised one hand, low, without turning. *Stay.* Sef grumbled somewhere in his chest, a sound like an idling engine, but he stayed.

"Mara." I gave her the courtesy of her name, because I wanted her listening. People listen harder when you use their name; it feels like intimacy, and intimacy makes them stupid. "Let's skip the part where you threaten everyone and I pretend I don't know exactly why you're here."

"That would save time."

"We don't have any. Tide comes back in six hours and your boat draws too much to sit off this shelf comfortably, and I know you didn't fly all this way to watch your shoes get wet." I nodded past her, at the treeline. "So. Here's the transaction."

Her eyebrows went up a millimeter. Genuine interest. I'd spent enough years reading barometric pressure to read a face like hers.

"The ledgers are ash," I said. "Every page. The copies, if there were any—and I'm not confirming there were—you'll never find, because the person holding them is better at disappearing than your entire syndicate combined. The evidence is gone, Mara. You can torture the location out of nobody, because there isn't one. What happened on that wreck stays on that wreck."

"You realize that's not how debt works."

"I know exactly how debt works. I owe you two hundred and sixty thousand, plus penalties, plus the interest you've been compounding monthly like a disease." I'd done the math during the storm, actually, wedged against the wheel while Sef screamed headings and Junie hung off a lifeline. There's something clarifying about a category-four cyclone. It strips everything down to numbers. "And I'm here to settle it."

"With what? Your charm?" She laughed, and the enforcers by the trees didn't, because they were professionals.

"With me."

The word came out steadier than anything I'd said in years. Maybe ever. I'd shouted course corrections through hurricane winds with less conviction than I put into that single syllable.

Mara went still. It was the first honest reaction I'd ever gotten from her—the smile dropping away like a mask with a broken strap, and underneath it, briefly, something genuinely surprised. Then, worse, something pleased.

"Go on," she said softly.

"No more chase. No more collections. No more patrol boats burning fuel looking for a trawler that's currently contributing to global warming off that reef." I kept my voice level, kept my hands loose at my sides. Behind me I could feel four people not breathing. "You take me. In settlement. Full amount. My crew walks—every one of them, clean slate, nothing owed, nothing collected, their names never crossing your desk again. You want an example? I'm the example. The famous Quentin Oyelaran, caught at last, surrendered voluntarily, shipped wherever you want to ship her. Story writes itself. Every debtor in your ledger hears about it inside a month."

"You're offering yourself as collateral."

"I'm offering myself as *payment*. Collateral implies you give me back."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the only sounds were the fire eating the *Corvus* and the reef chewing gently at the sand and Junie's breath hitching once, badly, behind me.

Then Mara smiled again—but it was a different smile now, stripped of its charm, and somehow more honest for it. "Do you know what I admire about you, Captain?"

"My winning personality."

"Your arithmetic." She stepped closer, close enough that I could smell her cologne, expensive and wrong against all this salt. "Most people in your position offer excuses. Circumstances. A payment plan. You looked at the board and offered the only piece you actually own." She tilted her head. "Here's my problem. A debtor who surrenders is worth exactly what I can extract from her. Two-sixty plus penalties doesn't cover my time, my fuel, my *embarrassment*—you made me look careless in front of the board, Quentin. Twice. But a debtor who surrenders *publicly*, dramatically, after leading the Syndicate across three hundred nautical miles of open ocean—" her eyes glittered, "—that debtor has *narrative value*. That debtor becomes a lesson."

"So we have a deal."

"We have a deal." She stuck out her hand, and I shook it, because a deal's a deal even when it's a trap, especially when it's a trap you built yourself. Her grip was dry and cool and strong. "My people will take you aboard. We'll transit to Kupang tonight. From there—" she waved vaguely north, "—arrangements."

"Arrangements," I repeated. The word tasted like ballast water.

"Nothing permanent," she said, with the kindness of a woman describing weather to someone standing in it. "A few years working off narrative value. You're resourceful. You might even enjoy some of it." She turned, then paused, and looked back at my crew—at Sef with his arms crossed and his jaw set like a cleat, at Priya whose stillness had gone sharp as coral, at Junie who was crying again and hating it, at Tomas who was watching me with an expression I couldn't afford to translate.

"They walk," I said. "That was the deal."

"They walk." Mara shrugged. "They're nobodies. Small debts, bad paper. Honestly, chasing them costs more than they're worth." She started down the beach, then added over her shoulder, almost as an afterthought: "Though the girl's uncle has an open file. If he ever files a claim—"

"Junie's uncle will never hear her name from the Syndicate," I said, "or I'll spend every day of my arrangement making noise. I'm very good at noise. Ask anyone who's tried to insure a fleet in the Timor Sea."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes—recalculation, fast and cold—and then she laughed, genuinely, and gestured to the enforcers. They came up the beach unhurried, and one of them took my arm, not cruelly, just firmly, the way you'd handle equipment you intended to keep.

* * *

They walked me toward the treeline. I made myself go slow. Not resistance—just slowness. A woman taking her time on the last stretch of beach she'd ever stand on as a free person, cataloguing it: the heat of the sand through my ruined boots, the smell of burning paint and diesel, the weight of salt drying tight on my skin. If this was the price, I wanted the receipt.

Sef fell into step beside me. One enforcer twitched; Mara, watching from near the trees, lifted two fingers and let it happen.

"You could run," Sef said quietly. "Priya says the reef drops off north side. Swim, float, take the dinghy we didn't tell them about."

"There's no dinghy."

"There is now. I built one out of spite yesterday."

I almost laughed. It hurt somewhere under my ribs. "And then what, old man? They torch the atoll looking for me, and the next port, and the one after that. You've seen what they do when someone embarrasses them. I have. I used to be the weather they sent." I shook my head. "This is the fix. This is the only fix. Everything else is just miles."

He walked in silence for a dozen steps. Forty years afloat, and I'd never heard him take that long to answer.

"Edda Kwan," he said finally, "paid thirty years for this expedition. You think she bought it so you could hand yourself over to the people who sank her family's ship?" His voice was low and rough, gravel over keel. "You think that's what 'bring them home' means? Home, girl. Not a holding cell in Kupang. Not a lesson for other debtors. *Home.*"

"Home is whoever you bring back," I said. It slipped out before I could stop it—one of Edda's lines, the one I hadn't understood yet, and Sef went quiet in a different way, the way a man goes quiet when he's decided to stop arguing and start planning. I knew that quiet. I'd heard it before departure days, before storms, before every decision that mattered.

"Sef," I warned. "Whatever you're doing with that face, don't."

"I'm seventy-one years old," he said. "I'm doing nothing. Walking. Looking at birds." But his eyes were doing arithmetic, and I recognized the kind. Complicated arithmetic. The dangerous kind.

At the treeline, the enforcers separated us. Hands on my shoulders, steering me toward the inflatable and the grey shape of the patrol boat beyond the reef flat. I looked back once—just once, because I'm not a saint and I wanted the picture, wanted it burned into me the way the ledgers had burned into the hold.

Tomas stood with his hat in his hands. Priya had her arms wrapped around Junie, holding her steady, and Junie stared at me with a ferocity that had nothing of grief in it and everything of *promise*. And Sef—Sef tipped two fingers off his brow, casual as a man seeing off a ferry, and then turned and said something to Priya that I couldn't hear over the surf.

I didn't need to hear it. I've spent my life reading skies at distance, and I know a building pressure system when I see one.

They were going to do something incredibly stupid.

And God help me, standing in the inflatable with my hands zip-tied and Mara Iles smiling beside me like she'd won the lottery, I realized I was already planning how to help them.

The patrol boat's engines woke with a cough and a roar. Ahead lay open water, Kupang, arrangements. Behind me, shrinking fast on that triangle of coral, four people stood around the smoking bones of a ship named for a crow—and crows, my grandmother used to say, are the only birds that remember every face that's ever wronged them.

Mara checked her watch. "Comfortable, Captain?"

"Never better," I lied, smoothly, easily—and hated, as always, how well it came. "Where are you taking me?"

"Somewhere with excellent security," she said, "and very poor reception."

She smiled at the horizon. She thought the story was finished.

I'd chased storms long enough to know: the sky always looks calmest right before the cell builds.
