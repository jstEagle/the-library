## 19. What the Crew Owes

I have spent my life reading water. Give me a reef and I can tell you in ten minutes where it will kill you, where it will feed you, and where it merely wants to be left alone. Water is honest that way. It doesn't pretend to be anything other than what it is.

People are the opposite. People will smile at you across a table while their lawyers draft the paperwork to erase you, and they will call it a fair deal.

Mara Iles had smiled at Quentin for eleven minutes on the beach at Landfall of the Coral Triangle — I timed it, because timing things is how I keep from screaming — and then she had accepted Quentin's surrender like a woman accepting a gift she'd already wrapped herself. Take me instead, Quentin had said, and Mara had said yes as though it were the most reasonable thing in the world, as though trading one human being for another were simply arithmetic.

The crew stood in the shallows and watched them go. Watched Quentin walk up the sand with her hands empty and her spine straight, toward the black inflatable waiting beyond the surf line, toward the yacht anchored off the point like a knife laid flat against the sea.

Old Sef said nothing, which from Sef is a shout. Junie was crying without any noise at all, tears just falling off her chin into the water, and Tomas stood beside me with his jaw working like a man chewing on something too big to swallow.

"She bought us," he said finally. "That's what that was. She paid."

"No," I said. "She *offered*. There's a difference, and the difference is ours."

Tomas looked at me. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning nobody signed anything yet."

* * *

Here is what people don't understand about debt: it only works if everyone agrees it exists.

A reef doesn't care if you believe in it. You sail into it anyway and you bleed. But a debt — a debt is paper. A debt is a number somebody wrote down and everybody else agreed to treat as real. And numbers, unlike reefs, can be argued with. Numbers can be audited. Numbers can be shown, under the right light, to have been written by liars.

I learned this from Tomas Reyes, who spent nine years inside the Voss Syndicate's accounts department before he ran, and who carries his guilt around the way I carry my dive knife — always within reach, mostly out of habit, occasionally the only thing between you and drowning.

We sat that night on the Corvus — or what was left of the Corvus, her hull wedged on the coral shelf where we'd run her aground, half-burned, listing like a tired animal — and Tomas spread Junie's stolen ledger copies on a chart table slick with salt spray.

Junie had copied them during the storm repairs. Nobody had asked her to. She'd sat in the flooded hold with a waterproof bag and a pencil, transcribing page after page by headlamp while the cyclone tried to peel the sky open above us, because she said later that copying numbers made her feel like the storm couldn't take everything. Numbers were proof the world still had an order to it, even when the order was rotten.

"I don't understand half of this," she admitted now, hugging her knees on the bench seat. "It's just... transfers. Dates. Account codes."

"That's exactly what it is," Tomas said. "And that's exactly why it works." He tapped a column. "Voss Marine Salvage, subsidiary of Voss Holdings, payment to Kestrel Underwriters, claim settlement, vessel *Meridian Star*, total loss, 2011. Except the Meridian Star didn't sink. I processed her insurance myself. She was scuttled off Ambon with a crew of six, two of whom didn't swim away."

The lantern flame bent in the wind coming through the broken portlight. Nobody spoke. Somewhere out past the reef, the patrol boat's lights moved along the horizon, patient as a shark.

"The ledgers prove the pattern," Tomas went on. "Thirty years of it. Ships bought, insured, sunk, claimed. Edda Kwan's family's ship is in here — the *Cormorant*, 1994. And here—" his finger stopped on a line, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do, which was break slightly, "—here's my own signature. Countersigning the Cormorant settlement. I was twenty-six years old. I thought I was doing bookkeeping."

"You were doing bookkeeping," Sef said from the doorway. He'd come down without anyone hearing him, the way he does. "Bookkeeping is just deciding whose lives count as numbers."

"So what do we do with it?" Junie asked. "Quentin said tell no one. That was the promise."

"Quentin also said take me instead," I said. "And we're not doing that either. The promise was to bring whoever sails under that chart home alive. All of us. Including her. Edda never said anything about letting the Voss win."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Then he pulled a second sheet from his jacket — not a ledger copy but a printout, creased soft from handling. "There's a thing you need to know about how Voss moves money. They're not a bank. They're a web of shells, forty-odd companies that pass money in circles until it comes out clean. It works beautifully right up until someone freezes one link mid-transfer. Then the whole chain jams, because every shell owes the next shell money that doesn't exist until the transfer completes. It's a conga line. You trip one dancer—"

"—the whole line goes down," Junie finished. She was leaning forward now, eyes bright in the lantern light, and I remembered that this kid had grown up watching her uncle cook books for fishing quotas. Some inheritances are useful.

"There's a transfer scheduled," Tomas said. "Big one. End of the quarter, they roll the whole year's cleaned money through a holding account in Singapore before dispersal. If an audit landed on that account during the window — even a routine regulatory flag, even an anonymous tip with documentation attached — the freeze would cascade. Every shell. Everything."

"Who audits?" Sef asked.

"Anyone with standing. Financial crimes units, insurance regulators, the underwriters themselves once they smell blood. The trick isn't the audit. The trick is getting credible evidence to someone with standing, anonymously, timed to the transfer window." He looked around the table. "We have three days. Maybe four, depending on when Mara processes Quentin's 'payment' — and she'll process it fast, because a live example is worth more than a dead debtor."

"And if we're wrong," Sef said, "if it doesn't work, we've burned our only leverage and Quentin pays either way."

"Yes," Tomas said simply. "That's the risk."

Sef looked at each of us in turn, the way he reads weather. Then he nodded once, slow, like a man accepting a forecast he doesn't like. "Then we'd better not be wrong."

* * *

You would think the hard part would be the plan. It wasn't. The hard part was the radio.

Because the plan required contacting the outside world, and the outside world included, potentially, anyone Voss had bought — and Voss had bought a lot of someones over thirty years. So we couldn't just broadcast. We needed a channel that couldn't be traced back to four castaways on a burnt trawler.

It was Junie who solved it, because Junie had spent two years on her uncle's boat learning that the most dangerous thing about powerful men is that they assume nobody small is paying attention.

"Satellite phone," she said. "Not ours. The patrol boat's crew uses a relay service out of Darwin — I heard the callsigns when they boarded us. Fishermen use it too. Hundreds of boats. You route through the relay, spoof a caller ID, and you're just another voice in the noise."

"You know how to do that?" Tomas asked.

"My uncle taught me to hide things from inspectors," she said, and there was no shame in it anymore, just fact, the way you'd say *my uncle taught me to tie knots*. "Hiding is hiding. Doesn't matter if it's cigarettes or phone signals."

So that became my job, oddly enough. Not the technical part — that was Junie and Tomas, heads together over the radio bay like surgeons — but the message itself. Because evidence is one thing; evidence needs a story wrapped around it or it sits in an inbox forever. I'd watched Priya-the-diver do this a hundred times underwater: you don't just show a fisherman where the fish are, you show him why it's worth his boat and his fuel to go there.

We drafted the anonymous package six times. Tomas built the financial architecture of the argument — the transfer window, the account numbers, the specific shells, cross-referenced to Junie's copies. I wrote the cover letter, short and cold, addressed to an insurance fraud task force in Singapore whose existence Tomas confirmed from memory. No names. No vessel. Just: *Attached are internal records of Voss Holdings demonstrating systematic insurance fraud spanning three decades. The attached transfer is scheduled to clear account [number] on [date]. Act before it clears, or don't act at all.*

The last line was mine. Sef read it and grunted, which from Sef is applause.

"It has to land during the window," Tomas said. "Too early and they file it. Too late and the money's gone. We send it ninety minutes before the transfer initiates."

"Which means we need to know the exact time," Junie said.

Tomas smiled for the first time in days, and it was not a nice smile. "Quarter-end transfers initiate at 0900 Singapore time. Always. Voss is punctual. Punctuality is how they convince themselves they're legitimate."

* * *

The night before we sent it, I swam.

I know how that sounds — we were planning an act of war against a criminal syndicate and I went for a swim. But the lagoon behind the reef was calm, black glass under more stars than the sky should legally hold, and I needed to remember what I was for. Underwater, everything simplifies. Your heartbeat is the loudest thing in the world. Your problems shrink to air, depth, direction. Three dials and a decision.

I floated on my back in the dark and thought about Edda Kwan, whom I'd met for exactly one hour in a Broome hospice, a woman folded up small in a bed by the window, handing over a chart sealed in plastic like she was passing a torch in a relay race she'd been running alone for thirty years.

*Bring whoever sails it home alive,* she'd said. *Tell no one what you find.*

I'd taken the promise lightly then, the way you take all promises made to dying strangers — sincerely, but at arm's length, because grief is contagious and you protect yourself. I hadn't understood that the promise was a load-bearing wall. That removing one clause would bring the whole house down on all of us.

But promises aren't contracts. Contracts are paper. Promises are what you owe the person, not the words. And what Edda owed — what she'd spent thirty years of hospice savings buying — was not silence. Silence was just the shape her despair had taken. What she wanted, underneath, in the place where the *Cormorant* went down with her family aboard, was for it to matter. For the sinking to have been seen. For the numbers to finally count the way she counted: one mother, one father, one brother, irreplaceable, unpayable.

The ledgers going to Singapore wouldn't put names on a memorial. But it would make the men who signed those settlements answerable to something other than their own bookkeeping. It would jam the machine. And sometimes that's all justice gets to be — not a resurrection, just a breakdown.

I swam back to the wreck of the Corvus and climbed aboard, and found Sef on watch, drinking tea he'd brewed on a camp stove because the galley was char.

"She'd have wanted this," I told him. "Edda. In case you were wondering."

Sef looked out at the dark water for a while. "Wasn't wondering," he said. "Been wondering why the rest of you took so long."

* * *

We sent the package at 0730 Singapore time, ninety minutes before the transfer.

I won't describe the sending in detail, partly because Junie asked me not to — "if I ever get questioned, I want plausible deniability down to the details" — and partly because the truth is it was anticlimactic. Two kids hunched over a radio in a burnt-out wheelhouse, a spoofed signal hopping through a relay, a compressed file going out into the ether like a message in a bottle thrown at a very specific bottle-collector. Then done. Then nothing. Then the ordinary sounds of morning: wind in the rigging remnants, Sef frying whatever passed for breakfast, the sea doing its eternal indifferent work against the reef.

Then waiting, which is the worst part of everything, and the part no one writes songs about.

We waited the way divers wait during decompression stops — hanging in the water column, unable to hurry, unable to help, trusting the process and hating it. Tomas checked news feeds through the relay whenever Junie could risk a connection. Nothing. Nothing. A shipping report. A cricket score. Nothing.

At 0947, Junie made a sound I'd never heard from her — a small punched-out breath — and turned her screen around.

Voss Holdings shares suspended pending regulatory inquiry. Emergency filing. Unnamed financial authority freezes assets of fourteen related entities. Investigation into "irregularities in historical claims settlements."

Fourteen entities. Tomas had said forty-odd shells. Fourteen frozen meant the cascade had caught the first rank of the conga line and the rest were stumbling.

"It worked," Junie whispered. "It actually — we did that. Us. From a *wreck*."

"Not us," Tomas said quietly. He was staring at the screen, and his face was doing several things at once, relief and grief and something older being set down. "Edda. Thirty years of her savings, and her map, and her stubbornness. We just carried it the last mile." He looked up. "The syndicate's assets are frozen. Which means—"

"Which means Mara Iles can't sell Quentin," I finished. "You can't make an example of someone when your entire organization is being audited. You can't even pay the fuel bill on your own yacht."

Sef laughed. Actually laughed, a short bark like a seal, and clapped Tomas on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

But I was already looking north, toward the point, toward the anchorage where the yacht sat. Because freezing assets is one thing, and human beings are another, and cornered people with guns don't check the news before they act.

"We have maybe hours," I said. "Before Mara realizes what's happened, or before she decides it doesn't matter. Before she decides that if she can't have the example, she can at least have the witness." I stood up. "Get the dive gear ready. All of it. And Junie — start figuring out how far that spoofed signal can reach, because in about two hours we're going to need to make a very loud, very traceable call to the Australian Maritime Authority, and I want them to hear every word."

"Why?" Junie asked.

"Because Mara Iles is holding our captain on that yacht," I said, "and the best time to rescue someone is when the people holding her suddenly have much bigger problems than us."

Out past the reef, the patrol boat's lights had stopped moving. They were just sitting there, dark and still, like something listening.

Like something that had just gotten very bad news, and was deciding what to do about it.
