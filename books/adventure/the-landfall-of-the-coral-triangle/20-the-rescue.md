## 20. The Rescue

I spent eleven years at the Voss Syndicate learning exactly one skill: how money moves when it's afraid.

It moves fast, and it moves through accounts that look like nothing—a shell here, a holding company there, a shipping concern in a jurisdiction so small its entire GDP wouldn't cover Mara Iles's fuel bill. When an audit lands on a syndicate like Voss, the first thing they do is freeze the quiet accounts and start shuffling the loud ones. And the second thing they do—this is the part nobody writes down—is stop paying attention to anything that isn't a ledger.

Which is why, standing on the fish market pier at Port Moresby with a burner phone showing me a cascade of frozen transfers and a harbor full of confused syndicate men shouting into their own phones, I allowed myself to think: this might actually work.

Then I looked at the yacht.

Mara's vessel sat at the end of the private berth like a knife laid across a table—white hull, black glass, too many antennas for a pleasure craft and not enough guns visible to be honest about what it was. Quentin was somewhere aboard it. She'd walked up that gangway eighteen hours ago with her hands open and her chin level, and she hadn't come back down, and every hour since had been another hour of me doing arithmetic I hated: how long does a woman stay valuable to Mara Iles once she's stopped being useful?

"Stop doing that," Priya said beside me. She was crouched under a stack of crab traps, wearing a wetsuit under a fishmonger's apron, which was somehow both the most ridiculous and most reassuring thing I'd seen all week. "The math. You're doing the math. It always comes out wrong when you're scared."

"It never comes out wrong," I said. "That's my whole problem."

She snorted. Under the apron she patted the dive bag between us: two rebreathers, a spool of line, a pry bar, and the thing we'd pulled off the patrol boat's mooring buoy three nights ago while its crew slept—a magnetic transponder clamp, syndicate issue, the kind they use to track their own boats. We'd been carrying it around like a grenade ever since, waiting for it to become useful.

Across the water, Old Sef made his move.

* * *

You have to understand something about Sef: forty years on the water had taught him that the sea doesn't care about your plan, only your timing. So his distraction wasn't clever. It was *timed*.

He came down the main pier pushing a handcart of fish crates toward the fuel dock where the Corvus's replacement trawler—hired, borrowed, and insured under a name Tomas Reyes absolutely did not put on any real document—was tied up. And he timed his arrival to the moment the audit hit the syndicate's operating accounts, because Sef couldn't read a spreadsheet but he could read a crowd, and the crowd had been reading their phones all morning with faces going from bored to gray.

He picked a fight with the fuel attendant. Loudly. About the price of diesel, the quality of diesel, the moral character of everyone who had ever touched a diesel pump. He knocked over a crate. Fish went everywhere. Two syndicate security men drifted over—not because they cared about fish, but because a scene draws eyes and eyes are either threat or opportunity, and you check which.

Sef gave them a show. He was magnificent. He called the attendant's grandmother into question, then the attendant's boat, then boats generally, then the sea itself, which even I found offensive. The security men started laughing despite themselves, because you cannot watch Old Sef perform outrage without feeling better about the world, and that, of course, was the trap. Every eye on the fuel dock was an eye not on the black-hulled yacht at the end of the private pier.

My phone buzzed. Junie.

*"In position."*

Three words. She'd been practicing them all week, whispering them in the dark of the hired trawler's cabin until they sounded casual instead of terrified. Junie, sixteen years old and lighter than the mooring lines she'd grown up handling, had gone over the breakwater wall at low tide with a knife, a coil of line, and the specific silence of someone who learned young that being unheard was survival. She was now somewhere beneath Mara Iles's yacht, in water I would not have entered for any amount of money, cutting things loose.

I checked my watch. Priya checked hers. Somewhere under the pier, the tide was turning, and the current in the channel would shift from slack to running in about four minutes, and everything after that happened fast or not at all.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I said, not because I needed convincing but because saying it out loud steadied my hands.

Priya didn't look at me. She watched the yacht, and her voice went quiet in the way it did when she meant every word. "Because she walked up that gangway so we could walk away. That's not a debt, Tomas. That's family. And family doesn't do accounting."

I am an ex-Voss accountant. I have spent my adult life believing that everything reduces to entries—that every kindness is a credit somewhere, every betrayal a debit, and the books always balance. Standing there with fish guts on my boots and a stolen transponder in a bag, I felt the oldest entry in my ledger quietly reverse itself, and I understood that some columns don't balance. They just carry forward.

"Go," I said.

* * *

Priya slipped off the pier like she'd been poured out of it. No splash—I genuinely don't know how she does it; I've seen her enter the water eight times now and I've heard it maybe twice. She went down the shadowed side of the pilings, under the hulls of the fishing boats, along the channel edge where the water goes from green to brown to nothing, heading for the stern of the yacht where the swim platform sits a meter above the waterline.

My part was the part I hated: the front door.

I crossed the private pier at a walk, because people who run get stopped and people who stride get assumed to belong. I wore the shirt of a syndicate marine-services contractor—the actual shirt, bought from an actual contractor who owed me money from a previous life—and carried a clipboard, because no security apparatus on earth has developed an immune response to a man with a clipboard. On the clipboard was a work order I had forged that morning authorizing inspection of the yacht's ballast systems, which was funny, because the last time I hid something in a Voss ballast tank it was thirty years of murder ledgers, and I doubted Mara would appreciate the callback.

Two men at the gangway. One big, one bigger.

"Ballast survey," I said, and showed him the clipboard at a distance calculated to be unreadable. "Scheduled before she clears port. Insurance requirement."

The big one squinted at it. Here is the thing about syndicate muscle: they're paid by organizations currently discovering their bank accounts don't work, and men whose paychecks are late develop a sudden philosophical flexibility. He glanced at his partner. His partner glanced at his phone, which was still showing whatever the audit had done to his direct deposit.

"Make it quick."

I got six steps up the gangway before everything went wrong, which, for our operation, was roughly on schedule.

* * *

Junie cut the mooring lines at 14:07.

I know because the yacht moved. Not much—she was heavy, and the harbor was calm—but she moved, drifting off the pier with the turned tide, and the gangway I was standing on swung away from the dock with a groan of steel that every guard on that boat heard in his spine.

Shouts. The big guard grabbed for his radio. Below, out on the main pier, Old Sef chose that exact moment to knock over a second crate of fish, escalating his one-man war against the fuel industry into something approaching opera, and half the harbor's attention snapped back to him like iron filings to a magnet.

But not all of it. Two guards came down to the aft deck, and one of them had a gun, and the gun was pointed at me, and I remembered—with the strange clarity of a man about to be shot—that I had never actually planned past this moment. My plan had a gap in it shaped exactly like my own cowardice.

"Tomas?" said a voice from the wheelhouse doorway, and it was wrecked, hoarse, unmistakable.

Quentin Oyelaran stood in the light with a split lip and a bruise coming up along her jaw, and her hands were zip-tied in front of her, and she was grinning like a woman watching her own rescue arrive late and badly dressed.

"You look terrible," she rasped.

"You should see the other guy," I said, which was a lie, because as far as I knew there was no other guy, and also because my voice cracked halfway through and ruined it entirely.

* * *

What happened next took ninety seconds and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it properly.

The gun-guard raised his weapon, and the deck tilted—because the yacht, freed of her lines, was swinging beam-on to the harbor chop now, and Mara Iles's beautiful knife of a ship rolled like any other boat—and the shot went wide and shattered a rail light. Priya came up the swim platform ladder behind them like something out of a nightmare the syndicate hadn't commissioned yet, dripping and calm, and put a pry bar across the back of the nearest man's knees. He folded. The gun-guard turned, and Priya threw seawater in his face—actually threw it, a handful off her own hair, the crudest trick in diving, blinding and shocking—and Junie came over the transom after her, small and soaked and holding the cut mooring lines like trophies.

And Quentin—zip-tied, beaten, half-starved by the look of her—did the most Quentin thing I have ever witnessed. While four people fought her battle around her, she stepped forward, dropped her bound hands over the gun-guard's extended wrist, and simply *took the pistol*, rotating it out of his grip with the efficiency of a woman who'd clearly been planning her own escape anyway and was mildly annoyed at the competition.

"Hi," she told him. "We're leaving."

"Ma'am," he said, hands up, "I honestly don't get paid enough," which may have been the truest sentence spoken in Port Moresby that day.

Mara Iles appeared at the top of the companionway stairs.

I want to be fair to her, in the way you're fair to a storm system. She stood there in a white linen shirt with her hair perfect, taking in the scene—her guards down, her prisoner armed, her yacht adrift, her syndicate's accounts frozen solid somewhere in a server farm she'd never see—and I watched her do the calculation I knew she'd do, because it was the calculation I used to do for her employers. Assets at risk versus cost of recovery. The yacht was drifting. The audit was live. Every enforcer she could call was busy guarding doors that led to empty rooms.

Her eyes found mine, and for a moment neither of us performed anything.

"The accountant," she said. "Of course. You were always the one I wondered about, Reyes. The quiet ones."

"I kept good books," I said. "Somebody had to. Nobody else was counting the bodies."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman realizing the audit on her screen had a handwriting she knew.

Quentin leveled the pistol at the deck, not at Mara, which was its own message. "The debt's void, Mara. Your own auditors will confirm it by tonight—every account, every transfer, every sinking you papered over for thirty years. There's a copy of everything somewhere you'll never find, and it stays buried exactly as long as your people forget we exist."

It was a bluff. There was no copy. There was a burned hold on a triangle of coral and ash dissolving in saltwater, and I watched Quentin say otherwise with the smooth ease that she hated herself for, and I understood finally why she'd surrendered yesterday: not to pay the debt, but to buy us time to make sure it could never be collected from anyone else.

Mara looked at her for a long moment. Then at the open water beyond the rail, where a hired trawler with a forged name was already turning toward the harbor mouth, Old Sef at the helm, Junie's cut lines trailing from its cleats like the yacht had simply been unhooked from the world.

"Get off my boat," Mara said quietly.

We got off her boat.

* * *

The swim to the tender was the longest hundred meters of my life, mostly because I am not a diver, and Priya ended up towing me by the collar while I contributed what dignity remained. Junie went over the side like she was born there, which, given the uncle, she essentially was. Quentin went last, pausing at the rail to set the pistol down on the deck, unloaded, action open, a courtesy and a warning in one gesture.

Then we were in the tender, and then we were alongside the trawler, and then hands were hauling us over the gunwale—Sef's hands, enormous and certain, gripping my arm hard enough to leave a mark I'll keep.

On deck, nobody spoke for a full minute. The harbor fell away behind us. Somewhere in the town, servers were executing an audit that would take the Voss Syndicate apart piece by piece over the coming weeks, but out here there was just the water, and the wind, and five people breathing hard and looking at each other like survivors of the same shipwreck, which, in every way that mattered, we were.

Quentin sat down on the fish crates. Her split lip was bleeding again. Junie handed her a rag without being asked, and Priya started checking her ribs with a diver's brisk hands, and Sef put the wheel over and pointed us at open water without a word, because there was nowhere to run anymore and he steered toward it anyway, out of habit, out of peace.

"Well," Quentin said finally, and laughed—a broken, salt-hoarse laugh that turned into something wetter halfway through. "You absolute idiots. I told you to let me go."

"Yeah," Priya said, not looking up from the ribs. "We discussed it. We voted."

"The vote was unanimous," I added. "Four to nothing. You weren't given a ballot."

She looked at each of us then—really looked, the way she read weather—and whatever she saw made her stop arguing, which I suspect was a first in her life.

It lasted almost a minute. Then Sef cleared his throat from the helm and said, in the tone of a man delivering news he'd been sitting on for hours: "There's mail for you, Captain. Came through on the relay this morning, addressed to the Corvus, care of a dead woman's name."

He held out a single sheet, forwarded twice, creased soft from handling. Edda Kwan's name on the envelope, in Edda Kwan's careful hand, posted years ago to a lawyer with instructions that had apparently just now ripened.

Quentin took it like it might go off. She looked at the water, at us, at the horizon where the Coral Triangle lay behind us like a closed book—and she opened the letter.

I watched her read it. I watched her face do something I had no column for.

"Turn us around," she said quietly. "There's one more thing Edda wants us to find."
