## 22. The Next Chart

### Quentin

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes after you've stopped running, and for the first month I couldn't stand it. I kept catching myself calculating distances — Broome to Darwin, Darwin to the Triangle, the Triangle to anywhere else — the way a gambler's fingers twitch over cards he's sworn off. Motion had been my whole architecture. Take it away and you find out what you're built on, and for a while the answer was: nothing much. A woman made of headings and fuel estimates.

Then one morning in the Darwin marina I woke up and realized I'd slept through the night without dreaming of the Corvus burning. I lay there in the bunk of the rented room above the chandlery and listened to the harbor working — halyards clanking, a forklift beeping somewhere, Old Sef already down on the pontoon arguing with a welder about zincs — and I thought: this is what anchored feels like. It doesn't feel like stopping. It feels like being held.

The syndicate had taken eleven weeks to die. Tomas's audit had gone in like a harpoon — clean, deep, angled to hit the spine — and the Voss accounts had bled out in front of a federal receiver who, it turned out, had been waiting years for someone to hand him a thread he could pull. Mara Iles went to trial. I testified. I had promised Edda Kwan I'd tell no one what the wreck held, and I kept that promise to the letter: I never said what we found. I said what the ledgers proved, and there is a difference, and I have made my peace with the difference the way you make peace with a scar. Edda wanted the truth surfaced. She never said it had to come up with the paper still on it.

The Corvus was gone. Some things you don't get back, and shouldn't. But insurance — the honest kind, the kind that pays out because the paperwork is real — covered enough, plus what was left of Edda's trust, plus a grant Tomas found from a maritime heritage foundation that had no idea how much irony it was funding. Enough for a hull. Not a trawler this time. A proper survey vessel, sixty feet, steel, ugly as sin, built in 1987 for a fisheries agency that had let her rot at a mooring in Cullen Bay.

Her name was painted on the bow in flaking white: *Kestrel*. We kept it. Junie said a bird that hovers before it strikes was the right patron for a crew like ours, and nobody argued, because arguing with Junie about names was a losing sport.

We were refitting her ourselves, which meant we were refitting her wrong, then right, then wrong again. Which meant we were home.

### Tomas

People assume an accountant's skill is arithmetic. It isn't. It's reading what a document wants you to believe, and then finding the three lines that contradict it. I spent nine years doing that for the Voss Syndicate and five months in a witness protection motel doing it for the Commonwealth, and I will tell you the honest difference: the Commonwealth's paperwork is worse, but nobody drowns accountants who find discrepancies in it. Usually.

The *Kestrel*'s paperwork was a masterclass in contradiction. Survey certificates from three different decades, an engine log that claimed the same main bearing had been replaced in both 2014 and 2019, and a previous owner listed as a company that the receiver had dissolved with the rest of the Voss holdings — which meant, for one absurd week, we technically owned a boat from a dead syndicate shell. Priya wanted to keep the name of the shell on the registration as a joke. I explained, gently, that I had spent enough of my life inside Voss entities to never want my name adjacent to one again, even a dissolved one, even ironically.

So we re-registered her clean. Every name on the documentation in full, spelled out, witnessed: Quentin Oyelaran, master. Priya Nair, first mate and dive supervisor. Myself, engineer — and I put my real name there, the one I'd used at Voss, because hiding it had stopped making sense the day the syndicate stopped existing. Old Sef, whose legal name turned out to be Sefanuel Tuiravakadua and who had been concealing it for forty years out of what he called "professional modesty." And Junie — Junie Marsh, seventeen as of March, whose uncle had signed away guardianship in a Darwin courtroom without looking up from his phone, and whose name now appeared on a vessel's articles for the first time in her life.

I watched her read it. She stood at the chart table with the registration sheet in both hands and read her own name four times, the way you'd check a sum that seemed too good.

"That's me," she said, as if reporting a discrepancy.

"That's you," I said. "Logged. Witnessed. Findable."

She looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse at first, and then I could: it was the face of someone discovering that being findable and being trapped are not the same thing. She folded the sheet with the precision of someone handling a chart of shoal water and put it in the plastic sleeve over her bunk, next to the fishing charts of her uncle's she'd navigated us out of the strait with. She kept those too. I asked her once why, and she said, "Because I read them and I'm still here." Fair enough. Most of my filing system runs on the same logic.

The refit itself was the usual litany: new tanks, new wiring, a main engine that Tomas-the-engineer — that is, me, wearing a hat I only partially deserved — rebuilt over six weeks with a manual, a torque wrench, and a level of profanity that Sef graded daily. But the thing I'm proudest of isn't mechanical. It's in the wheelhouse, bolted to the bulkhead beside the chart table: a glass-fronted case, and inside it, a chart. Not the waterproofed one Edda gave Quentin — that one was ash with the Corvus, and rightly so. A copy. Quentin redrew it from memory, every sounding she could recall, and then we did something Edda's original never did.

We labeled it. *Survey of the Coral Triangle approaches, per E. Kwan, commenced by the crew of the Corvus, continued by the crew of the Kestrel.* Names. Dates. Openly. The whole point, written where any inspector, any rival, any historian could read it: we are not hiding. We were never treasure hunters. We are finishing a dead woman's map.

### Priya

I have mapped a great many reefs, and I will tell you the thing about reefs that nobody puts in the brochures: they look like chaos from above and like architecture from below. From the surface it's all broken color and white water. Get under, move slow, and you see that every collapse is load-bearing, every dead patch is feeding something, every ugly thing is holding the structure up. A reef is a ruin that refuses to admit it's finished.

The crew was a reef. I'd known it since the crossing, when I started mapping them the way I map coral — by what they did under stress, in current, at three in the morning. Quentin was the bommie, the one everything else grew around, all sharp edges and shade. Sef was the old growth, slow and immovable and secretly holding the whole slope together. Tomas was the sand — underestimated, everywhere, quietly filling every gap. Junie was new coral, the fragile bright stuff that shows up after a storm and means the reef has decided to keep going.

And me, I suppose I was the water. Moving through, taking notes.

We'd been at the mooring in Cullen Bay four months when the foundation's survey contract came through — proper terms, proper money, a deliverables list that read like a love letter to everything Edda Kwan had wanted: hydrographic survey of the Triangle approaches, wreck site documentation, all findings published, all names credited. I read it twice and then carried it up to the wheelhouse where Quentin was drawing.

She drew the way she did everything — fast, then correcting, then fast again — and I stood in the doorway watching her lay a ruler across the blank chart and draw the first course line. From Darwin, southeast, into all that broken water where a trawler had burned and a patrol boat had grounded and a woman had pulled a girl out of the sea and decided, without saying it, that she was done paying for her life with herself.

"You're smiling," she said, not looking up.

"I'm auditing," I said. "It's what I do. I look at a structure and determine whether it's sound."

"And?"

I came over and put my finger on the course line, on the pencil mark still faintly graphite-shiny. "And this is the soundest thing I've surveyed in years."

She covered my finger with her hand. We stayed like that for a moment, two people who had once had a shouting match in a repair camp about whose silence they were keeping, and who had since learned that the answer was: nobody's. Silence was over. The chart was going to speak.

"First dive is the atoll pass," she said. "You'll run the transect. Junie's your tender. Sef insists on cooking something for the night we make the outer anchorage and refuses to tell me what."

"Then it's either a celebration or an intervention," I said. "With Sef, the menu's identical."

She laughed — the real one, the one that had come back sometime in the trial months, rusty at first and then reliable — and went back to her line, and I went below to check my dive gear for the fourth time, because some forms of love are just maintenance performed in advance.

### Old Sef

Forty-one years afloat, and I will tell you the sea's oldest joke: it gives you exactly what you asked for and bills you for the asking. I asked the sea to keep me moving so I'd never have to stay anywhere long enough to be left. It obliged for four decades. Then it sank my ship, and the crew I was keeping at arm's length pulled me out of the water along with everything else, and now I am moored in a harbor I can name, cooking in a galley I built with my own hands, and I have discovered — and I say this with some professional resentment — that staying is not the same as being trapped. The sea never mentioned this. The sea keeps its best secrets for people who stop asking.

The galley on the *Kestrel* was twice the size of the Corvus's, which meant twice the room to make trouble in. I had claimed it on day one by the simple method of moving my knives in and looking at everyone until they left. The welder from the marina, a Tongan boy named Filipe who reminded me of myself at that age except that he'd never been running from anything, came down most afternoons and I fed him and he pretended not to notice that this was the entire arrangement.

For the night we made the outer anchorage — the first night of the actual survey, the night the *Kestrel* would sit over Edda's water with her lights on and her names on her papers — I was making lovo-style pork in the deck oven, because some occasions require earth-cooked meat and no occasion in my experience has ever been harmed by it. Quentin came down the companionway while I was basting.

"Smells like a decision," she said.

"Smells like dinner. Decisions are your department."

She leaned on the doorframe. She'd gotten better at standing still, I'd noticed. Still tapped her fingers on things — charts, railings, the rim of her coffee mug — but slower now, like a metronome instead of a countdown.

"Sef. Can I ask you something? When you voted to scuttle the Corvus. Did you regret it? After."

I thought about it properly, because she had asked properly, and because the pork had another twenty minutes.

"I regretted it the way you regret amputating a leg that's already gone," I said. "The ship was finished. The question was only who got to say so. I'd rather it was us." I tapped the oven. "A man spends forty years learning that the sea takes what it takes. The only thing you control is what you put back in the water after. We put back a truth with no name on it. Now we're going back to put a name on it. That's not regret, girl. That's bookkeeping."

"Tomás would be flattered."

"Tomás is flattered by everything. It's how he survived."

She laughed and took the basting brush from me and did a turn of the pork, badly, and I let her, and we stood there in the galley of a ship that owed nothing to anyone, and I thought: forty-one years, and the best berth I ever held was the one I stopped running from.

### Junie

Here's what nobody tells you about getting your name on a boat: you spend your whole life being cargo — carried, moved, shipped off, handed over — and then one day a piece of paper says you're crew, and crew is not cargo, crew is the thing the cargo is *for*, and you have to go sit by yourself in the forepeak for a while about it.

I did that. Sat in the forepeak among the spare anchor rode with the registration sheet and read my name until it stopped looking like a typo. *Junie Marsh, deckhand.* Deckhand. Of the *Kestrel*. Whose articles were posted in the wheelhouse where anyone could read them, because we were the kind of operation now that didn't need to hide.

My uncle had a boat. I never put my name on it because his boats were for hiding things — the catch he didn't declare, the cash he didn't bank, the niece he didn't report. On his boats you were valuable exactly insofar as you were invisible. I got good at invisible. Then I got on the Corvus, and Quentin looked right at me on day one and said, "You eat at the table or you don't eat," which is apparently how you say *you're a person* in storm-chaser.

And then the strait. My uncle's charts, the ones I'd stolen the night I ran — every shoal and sand spit he'd ever cursed over, memorized because knowing them was the only power I had in that house. And I'd read them out loud in the dark with the patrol boat behind us and my hands shaking, and the crew had *believed* me. A seventeen-year-old runaway with stolen charts, and the whole ship had turned on my word. That was the night I stopped being cargo. Nobody announced it. That's how you know it's real — nobody announces it.

Now I was Junie Marsh, deckhand, and in three days we'd sail for the Triangle, and my job was to stand a proper watch and log it in the ship's log in my own handwriting, time and position and name, *J. Marsh, 0400–0800*, like a person who existed.

I went up to the wheelhouse in the evening. Quentin was at the chart table. The new chart was nearly done — her redraw of Edda's water, every reef labeled, and in the corner a title block, and in the title block, names. All five of ours. Edda Kwan's at the top.

"Am I allowed to draw on this?" I asked.

"Surveyor's privilege," she said, and handed me the pencil.

So I drew the pass through the atoll — the one we'd come through in the dark with the mast gone, the one I'd steered by memory and terror. I drew it clean, with the soundings we'd taken since, and I wrote the depth figures in my own hand, and under it, small: *first transited under emergency, corrected by survey.* That was the whole thing, really. That was what we were doing, all of us. Going back over the lines we'd drawn in emergency and making them true.

### Quentin

We sailed on a Tuesday, because Sef said Mondays were unlucky and Tomas pointed out that superstition was not a load-bearing structure, and Sef said neither was Tomas's torque wrench, and we compromised on Tuesday.

The *Kestrel* went out of Darwin harbor at slack water with her papers in order, her names on her stern, her tanks full, and her debts — all of them, every kind — paid or dissolved. No one was chasing us. That took some getting used to. For the first hour I kept checking the radar out of habit, sweeping the horizon for the shape of consequences, and the horizon kept offering me nothing but water and weather, and gradually my shoulders came down from around my ears.

The crew fell into watch rotations like they'd never done anything else. Priya ran her dive prep with the calm of a woman cataloguing a cathedral. Tomas had the engine humming at a note he described as "financially responsible." Junie took the 0400 watch and logged it in handwriting that got more confident every day. Sef produced food at intervals that could only be described as prophetic.

And I stood at the chart table with Edda's redrawn water in front of me, and I drew the first course line of the real survey. Not a fleeing line. Not a debt line. A line that started where we were and went where the work was, and had our names written along it in the title block for anyone to see.

Thirty years ago a prospector started buying this expedition one savings deposit at a time, and she died in a hospice bed handing a stranger a chart and asking for two things: bring them home, and tell no one. I kept both promises as best a promise can be kept, and then the promises turned out to be pointing in opposite directions, and in the end we found the third road — the one where you tell everyone, and what you tell them is the map.

I set the pencil down. Ahead, the Arafura Sea lay flat and silver to the horizon, and somewhere past that horizon was a triangle of coral where a trawler had burned and a truth had gone into the water, and we were going back — not to dig it up, but to chart the ground it rested in, honestly, openly, in daylight.

Junie's voice came down from the bow, where she'd stationed herself the way young sailors do, facing forward: "Landfall in four days!"

"Then we'll make landfall," I said, and the *Kestrel* carried us out, five names on the articles, one name on the chart, and no miles left that we were running — only miles we were choosing, one course line at a time, home being, as Edda wrote in the letter I keep in the wheelhouse case next to her map, whoever you bring back.

We had brought each other. Now we were going to finish the survey.
