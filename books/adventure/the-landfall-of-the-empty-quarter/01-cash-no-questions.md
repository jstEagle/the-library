## 1. Cash, No Questions

The border town of Kettle's Rest had two seasons: the season when the wind came off the ice fields and stripped the paint from everything that stood still too long, and the season when the wind came from the other direction and did the same job with more enthusiasm. August Nakamura had arrived in the first season and was now, three weeks into a salvage contract, living through the second. The wind found every gap in the boarding house walls and played them like a badly tuned instrument, and August lay awake under a blanket that smelled of mildew and someone else's tobacco, listening to it, thinking about rent.

Rent was due Friday. Rent was always due somewhere, in some town, in some currency, and this was the tax August paid for the life they'd built — a life with no fixed address, no fixed name on any document that mattered, no fixed anything except the tools in the kit bag and the hands that knew how to use them.

The salvage dig sat in the slag terraces above the old smelter road, where a pre-collapse settlement had been buried under its own industrial refuse and then, decades later, half-unburied by a flood nobody had bothered to record. The license — such as it was — belonged to a man named Dovik who had a face like a dropped pie and the moral flexibility of wet rope. What Dovik licensed, the government did not recognize; what the government recognized, Dovik could not afford. So the work happened at night, by lamplight, in the company of six other diggers who all understood without discussion that names were for forms and forms were for people who intended to be found later.

August was good at the work. That was the trouble with competence: it followed you from town to town like a debt collector. By the second week, Dovik had stopped assigning August to the coarse screens and started putting them on the fine matrix — the layers where a careless trowel could shear through a bead of pre-colonial glass or crush a pressed-bark amulet into confetti. August worked those layers the way other people prayed: slowly, silently, with total attention, and with the private understanding that whatever was being said back was not being said to them personally.

Tonight the find was a child's bracelet, copper gone green, worked with a spiral pattern that repeated four times and then broke off deliberately, unfinished, the way the old makers ended things — not because they ran out of metal but because a finished circle trapped the spirit inside. August held it up to the lamp and felt the familiar ache, the one that had nothing to do with cold or tiredness. Somebody had loved a child enough to leave the door open.

"Put it down," said Dovik, materializing the way he did, smelling of kerosene and boiled mutton. "Buyer wants it unrestored."

August put it down. It was not their history to argue over, and besides, arguing took time, and time in Kettle's Rest cost money.

* * *

The fixer came on Thursday, which was either coincidence or evidence that somebody had been watching longer than August liked to think about.

August noticed her the way you notice weather — peripherally first, then all at once. She was seated at the far end of the Salt Room, the tavern dug into the smelter's old brine cellar where the ceiling sweated and the lamps burned low because bright light made the regulars nervous. Not one of the regulars. Wrong coat for the town: tailored, insulated, the kind of garment that had never been patched. Wrong boots, too — expedition-grade, broken in but not broken down. And she drank tea, not liquor, holding the cup in both hands like a woman warming herself on purpose rather than out of need.

She waited until August had finished eating, which told August something about patience, and then she crossed the room without hurrying and set a folded paper on the table before asking permission with her eyes.

"You're the one who reads strata," she said. Her voice was flat and even, a voice used to being heard in wind. "Dovik says you can tell how deep a thing is buried by the color of the dirt coming out of a hole."

"Dovik says a lot of things."

"He also says you don't ask questions." She sat down uninvited, which August respected. People who asked permission to sit were usually people who would ask permission for larger things later. "I have work if that's true."

August turned the folded paper over. Plain stock, no watermark, no letterhead. Good. Letterhead was a trail.

"I ask plenty of questions," August said. "I just don't expect answers."

"That'll do." She leaned forward slightly. Up close she was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed entirely for function. "A government survey team went into the Empty Quarter eleven weeks ago. Six people. Ice-route reconnaissance ahead of the calving season — mapping the corridor through the glacier fields before the shelf calves and opens the pass. Standard work. They checked in on schedule for nine days."

"And then?"

"And then nothing. No distress call. No radio silence protocol. Nothing." She let that sit. "Their last known position is a supply cache at the moraine head. A recovery party went as far as the ice line last week. They found the camp intact."

August frowned. "Intact how?"

"Tents pitched. Stoves cold but serviceable. Food cached and sealed against scavengers — properly sealed, the way trained personnel seal things. Radios functional, batteries charged." She paused, and here her voice changed by a degree, the way ice changes before it cracks. "No tracks leading out. None leading in, either, past a certain point. Snowfall accounts for some of it, but not all. The recovery officer called it anomalous. His superiors called it closed. Case file sealed, families notified, compensation processed."

"Processed," August repeated. Eleven weeks. That was fast for grief paperwork and slow for a rescue, and the arithmetic of it sat wrong in August's chest.

"The team included a cartographer named Ilse Brandt," the woman continued, as though August hadn't spoken. "Her sister has money and doesn't care how it's spent. I represent interests adjacent to that money. Triple your current rate, paid half now, half on delivery of information. Cash. No paperwork. No names — mine, yours, anyone's. You go up the route, you read the ground the way you read that dirt tonight, and you tell us what happened to six trained people who apparently walked off the face of a glacier without disturbing the snow."

August looked at the paper. Under the lamp's grime-yellow light it was just a paper, but August had spent ten years learning what papers meant, and this one meant that somebody with authority wanted a question answered quietly, and somebody else with money wanted it answered at all.

"Why me?" August asked. "There are recovery specialists. Licensed ones."

"Licensed ones file reports." She said it without contempt, the way you'd state the melting point of water. "Reports become records. Records become subpoenas. My client needs someone whose findings arrive as a conversation, not a document."

"There it is, then." August pushed the tea dregs around the bottom of their cup. "You want deniability with legs."

"I want competence with discretion. Whether it's deniable is between you and your conscience." She produced an envelope — heavy, sealed, plain — and placed it beside the folded paper. "Half now. The route map is inside, along with the cache coordinates and the recovery party's photographs. You'd want to move soon. The calving window opens in nineteen days. After the shelf goes, the pass seals until next season, and whatever's up there becomes a rumor for another year."

Nineteen days. August thought about the boarding house, and Friday, and the landlord whose patience was a renewable resource only in theory. Thought about the kit bag packed for exactly this kind of departure, because every bag August owned was packed for exactly this kind of departure — that was the whole architecture of the life, the load-bearing wall of it. Go light. Go ready. Leave before the questions get specific.

Ten years of leaving. Ten years since the warrant, since the name that wasn't theirs got stapled to a crime that was, since August learned that the safest identity was the one that changed towns faster than records could. Running was expensive. Everyone thought the expensive part was the travel. It wasn't. The expensive part was that you could never own anything heavy — no furniture, no archives, no friendships with roots — and so you paid full price, forever, for everything, in cash, in advance, alone.

Triple rate bought a lot of advance.

"What happens," August asked, "if I find them alive?"

The fixer's expression didn't move, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly banked, like a lamp turned down rather than out. "Then you bring back word of where they are, and people with better equipment than yours go and get them."

"And if I find them dead?"

"Then you bring back word of why." She stood, buttoning the wrong coat for Kettle's Rest, and looked down at August with something that might, in a different life, have been sympathy. "My client's sister needs a body or a reason, Mr.—"

"Nakamura," August said, and heard the lie settle into place, smooth as a stone in a wall. It wasn't the name on any warrant. It wasn't even the name August had been born with. It was just the latest coat of paint, and it would peel in another town, and that was fine. That was the design.

"Nakamura," she repeated, filing it somewhere shallow. "Envelope stays whether you take the work or not. But the second half only comes with answers."

She left the way she'd arrived — unhurried, unnoticed, except by August, who watched her go and noted that she didn't look back, and that the two men by the door who hadn't been there an hour ago watched her leave, and then watched August.

So. Someone else's eyes in the Salt Room. Either hers, making sure the bait was taken, or someone else's entirely, making sure August took it. On a night like this, with the wind playing its tuneless song through the seams of the world, the distinction felt academic.

August picked up the envelope. It was heavier than it looked. Inside, August knew without opening it yet, would be a route map drawn by someone who had never loved the ice, and photographs of a camp that should have been empty and wasn't, and the coordinates of a place where six people had stopped existing on paper.

Outside, the wind shifted, and for a moment it came down off the Empty Quarter itself, carrying that smell August knew better than any home — cold stone, ancient dust, distance. Nineteen days until the calving. Nineteen days until the pass opened, and whatever was sealed behind the glacier fields came within reach.

August tucked the envelope inside the coat, next to the heart, where cash always rode, and finished the cold tea.

Friday's rent, at least, was solved. Everything else was waiting up in the white.
