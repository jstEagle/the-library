## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The pass took two days to give up its secrets, and it gave them grudgingly, the way a miser counts out coins.

August climbed into the glacier fields on the morning of the third day, when the light came up blue and hard over the ice and the wind had not yet found its voice. Below, the border town was a smudge of rust and smoke, already forgotten. Above, the Empty Quarter did what it always did: it erased. Wind scrubbed the snowfields clean each night, crevasses opened and closed like slow breathing, and the whole white country seemed designed by something patient and indifferent to unmake whatever humans dared to leave on it.

Which was exactly why the camp bothered August before they even reached it.

They had come up the survey team's plotted route — Ilse Brandt's route, though August didn't know her name yet, only the neat cartographic hand on the stolen copy of the expedition's filed plan. The fixer had provided it along with the cash advance: *here is where they went, here is where they stopped being.* The route threaded the calving corridor, that seasonal seam where the ice shelf pulled back from the moraine just long enough each year to let a determined party through. Eleven days of open passage, then a winter of sealed white silence. The team had gone in during the window. They had not come out.

August climbed with the economy of long practice. Thirty kilos, no more. Rope, pitons, the good tent, a stove that could burn anything liquid. A pick worn to fit their grip the way a tool fits the hand that refuses to put it down. Ten years of digging other people's forgotten things out of other people's forbidden ground had pared August down to this: light, fast, unmemorable. A person built like a rumor.

The camp sat in a shallow bowl below the first icefall, sheltered from the prevailing wind, close enough to meltwater to be practical, far enough to be safe. Anyone who chose that spot knew glaciers. August stood at the rim of the bowl for a long moment, letting the cold air pool against their face, and felt the first true wrongness settle over the scene like frost.

Four dome tents, pitched taut. Guy lines anchored with deadman burials in the snow — proper technique, textbook, the kind of anchors that would hold through a storm. The tents were still standing. Not collapsed, not shredded, not slumped under weeks of snowfall the way abandoned fabric surrenders. Someone had maintained them, or the weather had been unnaturally kind, or —

No. August circled the perimeter slowly, boots crunching on the crust. The snow around the tents was scoured smooth by wind, but the drift patterns told the story plainly enough for anyone who could read them. Snow had piled against the windward walls and thinned in the lee. Weeks of accumulation, undisturbed. No one had crawled out of these tents. No one had struck them in haste or left them in order. They simply stood there, four orange exclamation points in an empty sentence.

August unzipped the nearest one.

Inside: sleeping bags rolled and strapped. Foam pads squared beneath them. A mess kit hung from the pole, dry and clean. On the small shelf of packed snow beside one bag, a paperback novel lay open, face-down, holding its place at page two hundred and six as if the reader intended to return after dinner.

Nobody walks away from a book at page two hundred and six. Nobody walks away from a rolled sleeping bag. People leave camps in panic — gear scattered, tents half-collapsed, the archaeology of fear written large. Or they leave in winter, and the camp becomes a grave marker. This was neither. This was a stage set. This was a camp arranged to be found looking lived-in, which meant someone had wanted searchers to find it and draw the obvious conclusion: *storm, misadventure, tragedy, regrettable.* Case closed, file stamped, bodies never recovered because the glacier keeps what it takes.

Except the glacier hadn't taken anyone. That was the second wrongness, and it was worse.

* * *

August spent the afternoon doing what they were paid to do, which was notice things.

The food cache was a wooden crate buried at the camp's edge, marked with a cairn of three stones. Inside: ration bricks stacked in wax paper, fuel cans, chocolate, a medical kit with its seal broken and only the gauze and tape used. Enough food for eight people for six more weeks. The rations were government issue — the gray-green wrappers, the lot numbers stenciled in the ministry's font. August turned one over in their mittened hands. The lot number dated the shipment to the season the team went missing. Fresh stores, cached carefully, untouched.

Eight people. The filed manifest listed nine.

August noted that too, and let it sit in the back of the skull like a stone swallowed.

The radios were the third thing. Two units, both functional — August checked, thumbed the power switches, watched the needles wake and tremble. One sat on the crate that served as a table, antenna run out through a tent flap, positioned exactly where a radio operator would keep it. Charged batteries beside it, wrapped against the cold. In nine weeks, with a working radio and charged cells, no distress call had gone out. Or calls had gone out and been received by people who preferred silence. August filed that possibility away as well, in the drawer where they kept all the possibilities that made work dangerous.

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of old bone, and August allowed themselves the luxury of the stove, melting snow for water inside the second tent while the wind began its evening argument with the ridge above. They ate ration brick and hard cheese and thought about rent, about the fixer's triple rate, about the particular arithmetic of a life spent outrunning a warrant that had never been earned — a warrant with their mother's crime stapled to it like a tag on merchandise. Running was expensive. Being nobody cost everything you might otherwise have been.

Then they went back out into the blue dusk and looked for tracks, and found the absence that changed the shape of the whole job.

There were no tracks leading out.

Not faded tracks. Not wind-erased tracks. *No* tracks — and August understood the difference the way a forger understands real signatures. The wind here scrubbed snow flat within days, yes. But it didn't scrub evenly. It combed. It left sastrugi, ripple-marks, scalloped drifts in the lee of every stone and tent wall. A party of eight walking out of this camp would have compressed the snow along their line of march, and compression changes how wind scours — the drifted surface over a buried boot-print melts and refreezes into a faint lens of ice, a ghost-track that lasts a season. August had followed such ghosts across three countries. They were the bread crumbs of the frozen world.

August walked the entire perimeter twice, then worked outward in a spiral, head down, reading the snowfield the way other people read scripture. Nothing. The snow around the camp was a page torn cleanly from its binding. Eight people had walked into this bowl and no version of them had walked out — not toward the corridor, not back the way they'd come, not toward the icefall, not anywhere. It was as if the camp had been set down by a giant's careless hand, complete and occupied, and then vacated by magic.

Or removed by professionals who understood snow the way August did. Who had walked out on laid boards or packed snow-shoes and then swept the surface with ropes and shovels, breaking up the refreeze lenses, erasing themselves with the same patience the glacier used. August knelt at the eastern edge of the bowl and found it: a single thread of green synthetic fiber, snagged on a rock crystal, the kind used in military-grade sled harness. Government surplus. The inspection party — because that was what it would have been called, August was sure of it now, some official phrase with the word *inspection* in it — had been thorough. Thorough enough to erase eight adults from a snowfield. Thorough enough to leave a camp dressed like a diorama.

Thorough enough to miss one thread.

August stood in the failing light with the fiber pinched between gloved fingers and felt the unease turn over inside them, slowly, like a body rolling in shallow water — and underneath the unease, unwelcome and warm as brandy, the other thing. The thrill. The hunter's pulse. Ten years of digging in the dark for other people's secrets, and August had learned to be honest about most appetites but never this one: the clean, singing joy of a mystery that fights back. A disappearance this well-made was not an accident. It was a message, and messages meant authors, and authors meant motive, and motive meant there was something down in this ice worth erasing eight people to protect.

August hated themselves a little for how good that felt. Then they went back inside and got to work.

* * *

The survey log was in the fourth tent, the one furthest from the others, and it was torn.

Not damaged — *torn*, deliberately, a clean vertical rip through the middle pages, the remaining stubs left in the binder like the roots of pulled teeth. Whoever searched this camp had done it well everywhere else and gotten sloppy here, or hurried, or interrupted. August paged the surviving fragments by stove-light, careful as a surgeon.

The early pages were routine: barometric readings, route notes in that same precise cartographic hand, supply tallies, a joke about someone's snoring repeated in two different handwriting styles. The team had names once. August read them and tried not to think of them as already gone.

Then the last intact fragment, dated eleven days into the window, the ink cramped, the writer hurrying:

*— moraine field east of the corridor mouth does not match any colonial-era record. Structure confirmed at depth. Intact. Older than anything in the registry by centuries. We have voted 8–0 to document fully before filing. If the ministry schedules the cut before review, this all goes under the pit without a single photograph. R. says we are fools to write it down at all. Perhaps. But if we are erased, let the log say we saw it —*

The rest was torn away.

August sat very still in the orange circle of the stove-light while the wind rose to a shriek outside and the tent walls snapped like sails. *If we are erased.* Not *if we die*. Not *if the ice takes us*. Erased was a word with an author behind it. Somewhere east of here, under the moraine, lay something older than the registries, older than the colony, something the ministry intended to strip-mine the moment the calving season opened the pass — and a survey team of eight had seen it, voted to save it with photographs and coordinates, and then walked out of history leaving their boots behind.

August folded the fragment into an inner pocket, next to their own forged papers, and lay back on the foam pad and did not sleep for a long time.

Somewhere out in the dark, between this dressed-up graveyard of a camp and the corridor mouth, either eight bodies waited under the snow — or one survivor was still moving, still hiding, still carrying whatever the torn pages had died to describe. And whoever had erased the rest would eventually come back up this same route to make certain of the last of it.

August decided, staring at the roof of the tent, that they would very much like to find the survivor first.

In the morning, they broke camp and headed east, toward the moraine, following a thread of green fiber and the faintest ghost of a trail that someone — one someone, small-footed, limping on the left side — had left leading away from the tents and into the white.
