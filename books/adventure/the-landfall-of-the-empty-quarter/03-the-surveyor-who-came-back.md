## 3. The Surveyor Who Came Back

The cabin had been dead for thirty years and Ilse Brandt had spent nine days making it look that way.

She'd learned the trick of it in the border wars her government insisted on calling survey seasons: a place should read as abandoned from the outside and only barely alive within. So the trapper's hut sat under its burden of drifted snow with no smoke at its chimney, no footprints at its door — she came and went through the collapsed ice cellar, a crawl of black space beneath the floorboards where the old trapper had once hung his pelts to freeze. Inside, she permitted herself one candle stub, burned low behind a tin reflector so that even the light behaved like something ashamed of itself.

Her hands were the problem. Frostbite is honest in a way people are not; it announces itself. The two smallest fingers of her right hand had gone the color of candle wax on the third day out of the camp, and by the fifth they had begun to blister, and now they throbbed in a slow tidal rhythm that kept time with her heartbeat and made drawing impossible. She was a cartographer who could no longer hold a pencil steady. She found this funny sometimes, at three in the morning, alone, with the wind worrying the eaves like an animal trying to get at meat.

The data drive she kept against her sternum, strapped there in a waterproof pouch she'd taken off a dead man. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Nine days. She had counted them by the candle: she allowed herself four hours of flame per night, no more, and each stub lasted three nights, and she had burned through three of them. Outside, the Empty Quarter did what it always did — it erased. Snow filled the creases of the moraine, smoothed the scree, took the sharp edges off the world until the whole glacier field looked like something unfinished, a first draft the weather hadn't decided whether to keep. Somewhere south of her, down the route, the inspection party would be doing its work. Making things smooth. Making things unfinished.

She thought about Voss most nights. Not the way he died — she had made herself stop replaying that, or tried; the mind is a bad archivist, it keeps pulling the file back open — but the way he'd stood at the edge of the excavation with his mittens pushed up under his arms despite the cold, saying, *We map it properly or we don't map it at all.* A joke, mostly. Voss joked the way other men prayed.

They had mapped it properly. That was the whole catastrophe.

* * *

August Nakamura came down the route the way Ilse had been praying someone would: slowly, and reading the ground.

She saw him first from the ice cellar's vent, a seam between two floorboards where decades of frost-heave had opened a gap the width of two fingers. Through it she could see a slice of the slope below the cabin, white going blue as the afternoon leaned into evening, and a figure moving along the contour with the particular gait she recognized before she recognized anything else about him. Diggers walk differently than surveyors. Surveyors move like they own the land provisionally, checking their claim against instruments. Diggers move like trespassers who have decided trespass is a form of expertise. This figure moved with his weight low, testing each step, eyes down and then up and then down again — reading the snow the way she read contour lines, for what it concealed.

He stopped at the cabin's south wall. He stood there long enough that she lost feeling in her knees, crouched as she was in the cellar dark. Then he circled the structure once, wide, and she understood he was checking for tracks — hers, anyone's, the absence of them — and she felt the strange double pulse of fear and recognition that she would come to know as the beginning of everything.

Because he stopped at the point where her tracks ended. Nine days ago she had walked a half-kilometer upwind across the wind-scoured shelf and back again on her own line, letting the drift fill behind her, but the last forty meters she had done differently: she had walked backward in her own prints, then brushed them with a pine bough, then climbed down through the ice cellar. It was a good deception. It had fooled the drone sweep on day six — she'd heard the whine of it pass overhead twice and held her breath in the dark like a child under blankets.

This man stood over the erased forty meters for a long time. Then he crouched, and she watched his mitten hover above the snow without touching it, and though she could not see his face clearly through the gap in the boards, she saw the angle of his head, and she knew exactly what he was thinking, because it was what she would have been thinking.

*Someone erased these. Recently. And they were good enough to be worth finding.*

* * *

August stood over the ghost-tracks and felt the old thrill come up through his boots like heat from a stove.

He hated that he felt it. He had spent ten years cultivating a personality with no handles on it — no name spoken aloud, no fixed address, no debts except rent, no attachments except a set of tools worn to the shape of his hands. Impermanence as identity. But the body keeps its own ledger, and the body loved this: the puzzle of absence, the hunt for the person who didn't want to be found. Someone had walked here and un-walked themselves. That took discipline. That took fear. Fear was information.

The cabin looked dead. That was the second thing he noted, and the thing that convinced him. Dead cabins in the Quarter were common enough, but they died in a specific way — doors sagged open, snow entered and stayed, the interior became a museum of whatever the last occupant left. This cabin's door was shut. The snow against it lay in a smooth apron with no melt-shadow at the sill. A dead cabin breathes a little, warmth leaking from any crack; this one held its breath.

He drew his knife, considered the door, and went instead to the collapsed corner where the ground fell away toward the old stream course — because a cabin built by trappers always had a cold cellar, and cold cellars needed access, and access meant a second mouth to the building. He found it under a slab of drifted snow, half-choked with ice, breathing that faint animal smell of enclosed earth.

"Whoever you are," he said quietly, to the dark, "I'm coming in low and empty-handed."

He heard nothing. But the quality of the nothing changed.

* * *

Ilse heard him coming through the cellar and made her decision in the four seconds it took his boots to find the ladder rungs.

She could take the drive deeper — there was a fissure system behind the cabin she'd mapped on her second day, a crawl-space network she'd judged survivable if not comfortable. She could be gone into it and sealed behind a snow block, and he might search for a day and leave. That was the plan she'd been living inside for nine days: never be where the looking happens.

But her hands. She looked at them in the candlelight, the wax-colored fingers, the blisters weeping clear fluid, and she understood with a clarity that felt like fever that she could not survive another nine days alone, let alone the eleven-day window when the route opened — if it opened — if she could even walk it. She needed a digger. She needed someone whose entire profession was reading ground and moving through it unseen. She needed, God help her, help.

And this one had found her in nine days, which meant he was very good, which meant he was probably expensive, which meant she had better make her case fast and well.

So when August Nakamura rose out of the cellar hatch into the dim amber cone of the reflected candle, he found a woman sitting against the far wall with her back straight and her ruined hands visible in her lap, deliberately, the way you show an empty holster. Thin face, frost-nipped cheeks, eyes the pale grey of glacial ice under overcast — eyes that had been crying recently and recently stopped, and had decided to stop.

"Don't," she said, before he could speak. "Whatever the opening line is. I've had nine days to imagine all of them."

August stood half-crouched in the hatch, knife still in his hand, taking her in the way he took in terrain. "You're from the survey team."

"I'm the cartographer." Her voice was roughened, sanded down by disuse and cold. "Ilse Brandt. And before you decide what that's worth and to whom — understand that everyone else on my team is dead, the men who killed them are still on this glacier, and the only proof of any of it is strapped to my chest." She lifted her chin slightly. "So your opening line matters more than mine."

He didn't answer immediately. He came fully up into the room, slow, and settled onto the trapper's bench opposite her, keeping the candle between them like a border both sides respected. Up close he was younger than his hands suggested, and tired in the specific way of someone who has been running so long the running has become the shape of the life. She recognized it. She had seen it in mirrors.

"You erased your tracks at the south approach," he said. "Backward-step and bough-brush. Where'd you learn that?"

"Border surveys, 2141. We crossed into territory we weren't cleared for." A ghost of something crossed her face. "You recognized it."

"Took me twenty minutes." He turned his knife idly, not a threat, just a habit, a fidget with an edge. "Drone passed over six days ago. You were under the floor."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you're alive, and because the drone's flight path bent twice over this grid square, and whoever was flying it wasn't looking for geology." He looked at her then, directly, and Ilse felt the peculiar vertigo of being truly seen after nine days of being nobody. "I was hired to find your team. Off the books. Cash, no names. I've been telling myself it was a recovery job." He paused. "It isn't, is it."

"No," Ilse said. "It isn't."

She reached up, slowly, and pulled the strap of the pouch over her head, and laid the drive on the bench between them — not surrendering it, placing it, the way you put a card face-up. "My team found a burial ground under the east moraine. Pre-colonial. Intact. Thousands of years older than anything in the national register, and the mining schedule has it flagged for stripping the moment the calving season opens the pass. We documented it anyway. All of it. Every chamber, every artifact, every grave." Her voice held level by an effort she didn't bother hiding. "The next morning, an inspection party arrived that wasn't on any schedule. They had radios and rifles and a list of our names. Voss bought me ninety seconds with a diversion and it cost him—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The drive is the site. When the glacier takes it, this is all that will ever have existed."

August looked at the drive for a long moment. Then at her hands, the frostbitten fingers curled like winter branches. Then at the candle, burning its rationed hours behind its tin shield.

"Why show me?" he asked. "You don't know who hired me. You don't know what I'm paid to do with you."

"Because you found me in nine days and didn't radio it in." She nodded at his pack, at the radio riding unused on its hip strap. "A man working for the people who killed my team would have called this position in from the south slope. You stood over my erased tracks for six minutes deciding." Her grey eyes didn't waver. "I know what it looks like when someone decides whether to sell you. I've been watched my whole career. You decided something else."

Outside, the light was failing, and the wind had begun its nightly argument with the roof, and somewhere far to the north — so faint it might have been imagination, might have been the mountain settling — came a sound like a shot fired under the world. Ice, calving early. The season turning.

August picked up the drive. Turned it over once in his fingers. Set it back down in front of her, closer to her side of the bench than his.

"Tell me about the route east," he said. "The one that closes in eleven days."
