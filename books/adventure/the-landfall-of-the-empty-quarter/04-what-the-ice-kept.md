## 4. What the Ice Kept

The stove ticked as it cooled, and in that small domestic sound Ilse Brandt found the courage to begin.

She sat with her bandaged hands cradled in her lap like something borrowed, like the hands of a stranger she was minding until its owner returned. The frostbite had taken feeling from three fingers and left the rest of her hands tender as new skin. She could not hold a pencil. She had not been able to hold a pencil for eleven days, and for a cartographer that was its own kind of dying. So she would talk instead. She would put the map into words and let this digger — August, who gave no surname, whose eyes went to the window every time a board creaked — carry it out of here.

"The moraine," she said. "You crossed it yesterday. You remember how it looked."

August nodded once. "Grey country. Rock on top of rock."

"Rock on top of rock." Ilse smiled without humor. "That is what we thought too. That is what the schedule said — *terminal moraine, low archaeological probability, recommended for overburden stripping.* Those words. I copied them out of the mining annex because I did not believe them. *Overburden.* As if the ground were only packaging for what was underneath. As if nothing underneath could matter unless it was ore."

Outside, wind moved through the spruce with a sound like a long exhale. The trapper's cabin leaned into the hillside, one room, walls silvered by forty winters of weather. A fox pelt hung stiff on the far wall. Ilse had spent eleven days watching that pelt and thinking about things that were kept.

"We found it on the fourth day of the survey," she said. "A melt channel opened along the south flank of the moraine — the glacier has been retreating faster than anyone modeled, and where the ice pulled back it took a curtain of gravel with it. Beneath: a floor. Not bedrock. A built floor. Stone set in clay, fitted so precisely that water still runs off it in channels after — " she paused, recalculating, "— after four hundred years, perhaps six. We don't know. There is nothing to date it against. That is part of what makes it terrible. It is *old*, older than any colonial record, older than the maps my profession pretends begin all history, and there is not one written word anywhere near it."

August had stopped pretending to clean their gear. They sat very still, the way people sit around fires in high country, listening with their whole bodies.

"There were cairns," Ilse went on. "Nineteen of them, in an arc facing the ice. Each one packed around a core of birch — birch that doesn't grow within two hundred kilometers of here, which means they carried it, or traded for it, or it grew here when the climate was different and they remembered it. Under each cairn, a burial. Intact. All nineteen intact. The permafrost had — " Her voice caught, and she pressed her ruined hands together harder. "The cold had kept them. Cloth, hair, the lines of their faces. One of them wore a copper pendant worked into the shape of a fish, and the fish had eyes of blue glass, and I stood over that pendant for — I don't know how long. Tomas had to say my name twice."

* * *

She got up then, though her legs protested, and went to the shelf above the bunk where she kept the drive wrapped in a wool sock inside a tin. She didn't open it. She just rested her palm against the tin, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure you wanted to go through.

"Every reading we took said the same thing the schedule said: nothing here. The government's own instruments, the ones we calibrated ourselves at base camp — dead readings across the whole grid. Someone had zeroed them. Do you understand? Before we ever arrived. The survey was designed to find nothing. We were sent to certify an absence."

"And instead you found nineteen graves," August said quietly.

"Nineteen people." Ilse turned back. "Graves is what makes them data. These were people who carried birch two hundred kilometers to bury their dead facing a glacier, because whatever that ice meant to them, it meant enough to face it in death. And our employers wrote *overburden* on the file."

She came back to the bench but didn't sit. Standing was easier; standing let her pace, and pacing let the grief move through her instead of pooling.

"Tomas wanted to report it immediately. Mira said no — report it and it enters the system, and the system zeroes it like everything else. So we voted. Six of us. It wasn't even hard. We voted to document it ourselves, off the record, full photogrammetry, full context, everything duplicated onto a drive that never touched a government terminal. Mira called it *insurance*. Devi called it *testimony*. I remember thinking those were the same word wearing different coats."

The stove ticked again. Somewhere under the floor, a mouse ran its small errand along a joist.

"It took us nine days. Nine days of lying to our own daily reports — *moraine transect proceeding, conditions nominal* — while we photographed the dead and measured the birch and mapped the drainage channels and argued about ethics at night in a tent that smelled of paraffin and boot leather. Devi sang while she worked. Did you know that? She had a song for every task. The leveling song. The packing-up song. She made one up for the cairns, slow, in a minor key, and none of us asked her what the words meant because I think we were afraid she'd tell us."

Ilse stopped pacing. When she spoke again her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they already know is thin.

"On the morning of the tenth day, before first light, the radio woke us. An inspection party, inbound. Unannounced. Four names given, no affiliation beyond 'regulatory.' They landed at the ice strip at 0640. I watched them come up the route through binoculars. They were not regulators. Regulators carry clipboards. These carried rifles slung forward and a case I now understand held replacement instruments — pre-zeroed, reading nothing. They walked the site. They looked at the cairns for perhaps four minutes. And then their leader asked Tomas, very politely, to hand over all survey media for verification."

"Verification," August repeated.

"A gentle word. We gave them the official drives — the ones with the doctored daily logs. Mira had prepared for that; she'd kept the real documentation segregated from day one. What she hadn't prepared for was that they already knew. The leader told Tomas the team would be extracted early, that the season was closing, that transport was arranged. He said it like a kindness. And we understood — I understood standing there with the wind coming off the glacier — that the extraction was not going to end where it began."

* * *

Ilse sat down finally, because her knees had begun to shake, and told the rest of it in a voice that stayed level only through enormous expenditure of will.

"They separated us. That's the first thing they do — I've read enough history to know it, and still I watched it happen to me and thought, *no, this is a misunderstanding.* They took the others toward the aircraft in pairs. I was last, because I'd gone back for the transit case. I heard the aircraft take off. Twice. And I stood alone on the ice strip with a case full of other people's work and no other people, and I understood that I was either meant to be on one of those flights or meant to be found frozen in spring, and I chose a third option nobody had scheduled."

"You ran up-route," August said. "Into the worst of it."

"I ran up-route because down-route was where they were." Ilse almost laughed. "Eleven days, August. Eleven days in a white world with frostbitten hands and a tin against my chest, navigating by dead reckoning because I wouldn't risk the radio. I lost my mittens on the second day. I lost feeling on the third. On the fifth night I heard an aircraft circling the corridor and I buried myself in a drift and let the snow cover me, and I lay there under half a meter of white listening to the engines and thinking about Devi's song, and I promised the glacier — I *negotiated* with it, the way you do when you're losing your mind — I promised it that if it kept me, I would make the ice keep them too. All nineteen. Their names, their faces, the fish with the glass eyes. Kept somewhere the machines can't zero."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. August fed it a split of birch — actual birch, hauled to this cabin by some trapper who never knew he was handling cargo from two hundred kilometers away — and the flames rose with a sound like a held breath released.

"So the drive," August said. "It's not evidence of a crime. It's evidence of a theft."

"It's both." Ilse looked at them across the small orange light. "The mining schedule strips that moraine the moment the pass opens. Calving season. Eleven days of open corridor, and then the ice seals and the machines come in behind it. Everything under the moraine goes to tailings. The people, the birch, the fish — *overburden.* Unless what's on this drive reaches someone outside the ministry's reach before the first charge is set."

August was quiet for a long time. Ilse watched them work through it — she was learning to read this person, the small tells, the jaw tightening not in anger but in arithmetic. Counting days. Counting checkpoints. Counting the cost of a thing they hadn't yet agreed to.

And then August said, "Show me the route you came down. Every camp, every hour. If they tracked you this far, they'll have watchers on the corridor already, and I need to know what they saw."

Not *yes*. Not yet. But not *no*, and Ilse Brandt, who had survived eleven days on the difference between those two words, unrolled the map with her broken hands and began.

Outside, far off down-valley, so faint it might have been imagination, came the first long groan of the glacier, settling into the season — or waking.
