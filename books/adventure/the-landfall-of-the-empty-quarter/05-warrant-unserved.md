## 5. Warrant, Unserved

The town was called Meridian Cut on the maps and nothing at all in the mouths of the people who lived there, because the people who lived there had learned that names were the first thing the government took when it wanted what you had. August had been back four days — long enough to sell two crates of salvage from the survey camp's outer cache, short enough that the frost hadn't fully left their knuckles — when they understood that someone had been asking after them.

They understood it the way you understand weather: not by seeing it arrive but by noticing what it displaced. The woman who ran the fuel depot wouldn't meet their eyes. The usual table at the back of the Kettlehouse was occupied by a man in a company parka with a crease down one sleeve, pressed by a machine, worn by a man who had never once patched anything himself. And old Sefa, who sold August their ration cards at a markup that was really a kindness, pressed an extra card into their palm without being asked and said, too quietly for anyone else to hear: *You've got a shadow, digger.*

August drank their tea and thought about routes.

There were three out of Meridian Cut in winter. The pass road, which meant checkpoints. The river ice, which meant the river, which meant drowning slowly in water so cold it felt like burning, which some part of them noted with professional detachment was still better than the alternative. And the long way south over the shoulder of the moraine, eleven days on foot through country where your own breath became a burden you carried.

They'd run before. They were good at running. It was, if August was honest — and honesty, like everything else, was something they rationed — the only thing they had ever been unambiguously good at.

But Ilse Brandt's hands were still wrapped in grease and gauze up at the trapper's cabin, and the drive was sewn into the lining of her coat, and neither of those things could cross the moraine in this weather. So August finished their tea, left coins under the cup, and walked back out into the blue afternoon light knowing full well that walking away calmly was exactly what a hunted animal does right before the snare takes its leg.

* * *

The knock came at the room above the chandlery at 0400, which told August two things: whoever it was knew they kept watch hours, and didn't care whether they slept. Both facts were messages. Everything about the next hour would be messages.

August stood to one side of the door with the pry bar from their pack and said, "State yourself."

"Mr. Nakamura." A voice like gravel poured over velvet. "Or should I say Ms.? I confess I never did settle on which name you prefer. You've used both."

That landed the way a blade lands flat against bone — not cutting, but letting you feel the edge. August set the bar down, unlocked the door, and let in a man built like a filing cabinet: broad, gray, efficient, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten by everyone except the people he needed to remember him. Behind him, in the stairwell, stood the fixer — Voss, triple rate, cash no questions — looking at the floor with the expression of a dog that has just realized whose hand holds the leash.

"You found me," August said.

"You found *yourself*," the man said pleasantly. "I merely read the same trail you did. The camp, the log fragment, the route notes. You're thorough. That's why we hired you." He stepped inside without invitation, took in the room — the gear laid out in rows, the map weighted at the corners with tins, the window facing the alley rather than the street — and nodded as though confirming a purchase. "My name is Aldous Renk. I represent interests upstream of the survey office. Sit, please."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." Renk lowered himself onto the single chair anyway, folding his hands over his knee. Up close he smelled of engine oil and peppermint, an oddly domestic combination, the smell of a man who traveled in comfort and expected others to accommodate him. "Let's dispense with the theater. Where is Ilse Brandt?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Into the ice. She was frostbitten past saving when I reached her. I buried her under a cairn at the third moraine marker. You can send someone to dig if you like. Bring a shovel and a priest."

It was a good lie. August had spent ten years learning to make lies load-bearing, and this one had weight distribution, foundation, drainage. Renk listened to all of it with his head tilted slightly, the way a man listens to a radio he already knows is playing a recording.

"Mm," he said. "And the drive she carried?"

"Buried with her."

"The drive," Renk said, "that she stole from her own team. The drive containing the full spectral and subsurface survey of grid sector nine — the sector my principals intend to develop once the calving season opens the pass." He smiled. It was a small smile, precise as a compass bearing. "You see the difficulty. We sent six people into that sector to confirm the ore body. Six people came back as zero. And now a digger with a warrant outstanding is seen buying rations and selling salvage in a border town, alone, having gone up the same route the team went down. Alone."

The word hung there. August let it hang. Any movement at all would have been a confession.

"So here is the arithmetic," Renk continued, and his voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Option one. You return Brandt and the drive within seventy-two hours, and this conversation becomes a memory none of us need keep. Option two." He produced, from his breast pocket, a folded sheet of paper — actual paper, which in itself was a message; paper could be served, filed, entered into a record — and laid it on the map between the tins. "You know what this is. I suspect you've known since you were nineteen years old."

August looked down at it.

They had never seen the warrant itself. Warrants were things that happened to other people, things that existed in the abstract the way glaciers exist in photographs — vast, slow, theoretical. But they recognized the case number. Of course they did. They'd invented a dozen different memories of it over the years, and every version began with the same string of digits, because August had recited it to themselves on bad nights the way other people recited prayers: the number that made them a fugitive, the number that had followed them across four territories and nine assumed names, the number that was not theirs.

*Halcyon Nakamura.* Their mother's name. Their mother's crime — the falsified heritage clearances, the site stripped and sold before the review board ever convened, the scandal that had ended a celebrated career and begun, without anyone asking its opinion, August's life on the run. August had been nineteen. August had signed nothing, stolen nothing, known nothing until the marshals came for the daughter instead of the mother, because the mother had already crossed an ocean and the paperwork needed a body attached to it.

"You've had ten years to serve that warrant," August said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of it. "Why now?"

"Because ten years ago it wasn't useful." Renk rose, smoothing his parka. "A warrant is a tool, Mr. Nakamura. Like a drill, or a lease option. It sits in a drawer until someone needs a hole." At the door he paused, and for one moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was simply tired, bureaucratic appetite, vast and patient as the ice itself. "Your mother taught this territory everything it knows about making inconvenient history disappear. There's a certain poetry in using her child to do it again. Seventy-two hours. Voss knows how to reach me."

Then he was gone, and the stairwell swallowed him, and August stood alone in the cold room with the paper lying on the map like a stone dropped in still water.

* * *

Voss lingered. That was the surprise. August had expected him to scurry after his master like a kicked dog, but instead he stood at the top of the stairs, hat in his hands, and said to the wall rather than to August:

"I didn't know what the job was. Locate a missing team, that's what I was told. Cash, no questions — that phrase cuts both ways, you understand? Nobody asks me questions either. Not even the ones I ask myself." He turned then, and his face was older than it had been a week ago, aged by whatever he'd learned sitting in rooms with men like Renk. "Six people, digger. I drank with two of them at the depot last spring. The cartographer — Brandt — she drew me a chart of the shallows off Cape Verity once, for free, because my boy fishes there." His jaw worked. "If she's alive—"

"She's alive," August said, and immediately regretted it, and immediately, beneath the regret, did not regret it at all.

Voss closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a debt gets heavier. Then he went down the stairs and out into the dark, and August bolted the door and sat on the cot and did the thing they had trained themselves never to do, which was to think about the past on purpose.

Ten years. Ten years of rented rooms and burned names, of leaving towns at night with the bill unpaid and the bed unslept-in, of flinching at the sound of their own first name. They had believed — had *needed* to believe — that the running was payment. That somewhere in those ten cold years they had settled the account their mother opened, that the interest accrued in loneliness and frostbite and fear had balanced the ledger, and that one day, in some unnamed town, the debt would quietly lapse and August Nakamura could walk into daylight under their own sky.

And now a man in a machine-pressed parka had shown them the truth with the neatness of an auditor: the debt had never been theirs to pay. It had been held in trust. Preserved. *Maintained*, the way you maintain equipment, against the day it became useful. Every year August ran had not been penance. It had been storage.

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind came down off the glacier fields and moved through the town's empty streets with a sound like a great file being drawn across metal, sharpening something.

August looked at the map. Eleven days until the calving window opened the corridor. Seventy-two hours until the warrant was served. The arithmetic was ugly and clean, and at the center of it sat a woman with ruined hands in a trapper's cabin, sewing a dead civilization's testimony into the lining of her coat.

They thought about running. God help them, they thought about it honestly, thoroughly, the way they thought about everything — the southern route, the river ice, the small merciful anonymity of becoming no one again. It would work. It had always worked.

But Ilse Brandt couldn't run. And the drive couldn't run. And somewhere under the moraine lay a burial ground with six graves' worth of blood already on the schedule, waiting for the same machinery that had erased August's mother's sites, that had erased the team, that would erase August too the moment it stopped being useful — and the only person positioned to do anything about any of it was a fugitive with seventy-two hours and a warrant that had finally come home to roost.

August blew out the lamp, packed by touch, and wrote two words on the corner of the map in pencil, in the handwriting Ilse would later learn to trust with her life:

*Eleven days.*

Then they shouldered the pack, left the warrant lying where Renk had placed it — let him wonder whether it had been read — and went up into the dark toward the cabin, toward the ice, toward the first honest decision of their adult life.

Behind them, far to the north, the glacier groaned in its sleep, counting down.
