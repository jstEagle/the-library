## 6. The Calving Window

*August*

The trapper's cabin smelled of spruce smoke and old blood, and August had learned in four days that both smells belonged to Ilse Brandt — the smoke because she fed the stove like a woman who had once gone cold and meant never to repeat the experience, the blood because she still changed the dressings on her hands twice a day and did it without flinching, without looking away, as if pain were a ledger she intended to keep balanced to the last entry.

August spread the route map across the scarred plank table and weighted its corners with a tin cup, a coil of rope, a knife, and the drive itself — black, matte, small enough to swallow. Outside, the wind moved through the glacier fields with a sound like a crowd murmuring before a verdict.

"Eleven days," August said.

Ilse didn't look up from her hands. "You're certain."

"The shelf calves on the twenty-third. It does it every year, same week, give or take a tide. The pass opens when the ice goes out, stays open while the meltwater runs, then seals again until next season." August tapped the map where the corridor narrowed to a throat between two walls of blue ice. "Eleven days, maybe twelve if the weather forgives us. After that, this whole route is a wall."

"And the checkpoints?"

"Two that matter." August drew a line with one finger, slow, deliberate. "Here, at the moraine — a fixed post, radio, probably six bodies. And here, at the landfall, where the route meets the coast road. That one will be staffed by whoever sent the inspection party. They'll be watching for you specifically now. Which means they're watching for anyone moving south with gear they can't account for."

Ilse finally raised her head. Her eyes were pale as shallow water, and steady in a way that made August want to look elsewhere. "You've run checkpoints before."

"I've run from things before. Sometimes those things were checkpoints."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." August straightened, rolled a shoulder that had been aching since the moraine. "Here's what I know. We can't go around — there is no around. We can't wait them out — the window closes and takes the route with it. So we go through, and we go through clean. No fires after dark. No tracks where tracks can be read. We move on the glacier itself, where the surface renews every night, and we time the moraine crossing for the hour when the post changes shift."

"You've scouted it."

"I scouted it yesterday while you slept. Six men, one dog, a stove they feed too well. The dog is the problem. Dogs don't change shifts."

Something almost like a smile touched Ilse's mouth — the first August had seen, thin and wintry. "I knew a cartographer who'd have liked you. He used to say every obstacle was just information you hadn't drawn yet."

"Was he the one who voted to document the site?"

The smile went out. She looked back down at her bandaged hands, flexing the fingers one at a time, testing what the frostbite had left her. "He was the one who insisted. Tomas. He said a map that leaves things off isn't a map, it's a lie with gridlines." A pause. The wind filled it. "They made him leave things off. In the end they made him leave everything off."

August said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either pity or promise, and August had spent ten years issuing neither.

Instead: "Show me the drive's contents. All of it. If I'm carrying my life next to yours across eleven days of ice, I want to know exactly what weight I'm hauling."

* * *

*Ilse*

She had rehearsed this, in the cabin's long nights — how she would show the data, how she would make a stranger understand why it mattered enough to die for. But rehearsal dissolved the moment she plugged the drive into August's battered field terminal and the first images came up, because understanding wasn't something you could argue a person into. It arrived or it didn't, the way weather arrives.

The screen filled with the burial ground.

It lay beneath the moraine in a hollow the glacier had guarded for centuries — terraced stone, cairns aligned to a winter sunrise no living person had witnessed, grave goods of bone and copper and carved antler laid out in patterns the survey team had spent nine days photographing before they understood what the government's schedule already knew: that the ore body ran directly beneath it, and that the calving season that opened their escape route would also open the pass to the machines.

"They filed it as an obstruction," Ilse said quietly. "Not a site. An obstruction. Line item forty-one in the mining schedule. Blasting date: the day after the pass opens."

August sat very still, the way people sit when they are keeping themselves from standing. The firelight moved on their face. "And your team voted."

"Six to one." Ilse's voice caught on the arithmetic. "We documented everything. Every cairn, every artifact, every alignment. Full survey, full provenance, sealed copies routed three ways. That's what the inspection party came to erase. Not us — the record. We were just the record's inconvenient custodians." She looked at her ruined hands. "Tomas hid the drive inside a core sample case. Told me to walk north while they were still reading our radios. Said someone would come down the route eventually, because someone always comes down the route. Diggers, salvagers, people who move quiet." She glanced up. "He described you before he ever saw you. 'Someone tired,' he said. 'Someone who checks exits first.'"

August's jaw tightened, but they didn't deny it.

"There's more," Ilse said, and switched screens. "My own documentation. I kept a parallel log — dates, names, the inspection party's call signs, the order they were given. Verbal order, but orders leave echoes. Radio traffic, supply requisitions, the schedule itself. If the drive is the body, this is the confession."

For a long moment August said nothing. Then, in a voice stripped of all its practiced flatness: "Why me? You waited four days in a freezing cabin for a stranger. You could have walked out alone, found a town, found a journalist."

"A journalist gets a story. I need a route." Ilse closed the terminal lid with her palm, gently, the way she'd once handled the artifacts themselves. "And I watched you work the camp. You read ground the way Tomas read maps. You noticed the absence of tracks before you noticed anything else — which means absences matter to you. People who notice absences are people who've been one." She held August's gaze. "I'm not asking you to believe in the cause. I'm asking you to carry it eleven days. Belief can catch up later."

* * *

*August*

That night August sat by the stove long after Ilse slept, and did the thing they had not done in ten years: took out their own documentation and read it.

A leather folder, soft-cornered, travel-worn. Inside, a decade of quiet evidence gathered the way a magpie gathers — the original warrant application with its timeline that couldn't possibly be true; letters from a mother who had signed her confession with a shaking hand and died before trial; a copy of the forensic note, buried in an appendix, that pointed away from August Nakamura entirely and toward a man whose name had since been promoted twice. August had carried it from border town to border town like a stone in the chest, proof of innocence that proving would mean standing still, and standing still was the one thing running had taught them never to do.

Now they laid it beside the drive on the table. Two records. Two erasures. One route.

Eleven days, they thought. Eleven days to move a woman, a drive, and a dead mother's name past two checkpoints and out through a throat of ice that would close behind them like water closing over a dropped coin.

The plan assembled itself in the old familiar way, cold and clean. Move high on the glacier where the wind erased their sign nightly. Cross below the seracs in the pre-dawn cold, when the ice was locked and quiet. At the moraine, split — one visible, drawing eyes, buying the other the shadowed line. August turned the shape of it over and understood, with a sensation like a key turning, which role they had already assigned themselves without deciding to. Visible. Named. The decoy who walks into the light so the record can walk out through the dark.

It should have frightened them. It did frighten them. But underneath the fear was something older and stranger — relief, the relief of a runner who has finally been shown a finish line worth being caught at.

Near dawn, Ilse stirred, saw the folder open, and said nothing. She only rose, fed the stove, and set two cups on the table instead of one.

"We'll need names for each other," she said, settling across the table. "Real ones. If one of us doesn't come through, the other has to be able to say who they were walking with. To testify. To testify properly."

August looked at the folder, at the warrant, at the name written there in a clerk's careful hand — *Nakamura, August V., aka subject* — and heard their mother's voice saying, years ago, *a name is just the handle grief uses to find you.*

"Nakamura," they said aloud. "August Nakamura. That's the whole of it. No middle initial worth keeping."

Ilse nodded slowly, as if receiving something fragile. "Brandt. Ilse Brandt. Cartographer, second grade, suspended pending review — which is their word for disappeared." She wrapped her bandaged hands around the cup. "So. Nakamura. Eleven days. Two checkpoints. One dog."

"One dog," August agreed, and for the first time in longer than they could measure, the future sounded less like a wall and more like a door.

Outside, far up the valley, the glacier spoke — a single deep report, like a drum struck under the world, the first loose tooth of the calving season testing its socket. Both of them turned toward the sound. Neither smiled.

The window was opening. And somewhere south of the moraine, someone was already counting the days along with them.
