## 7. Checkpoint at the Moraine

The moraine came out of the white like the spine of something drowned.

August had been reading the glacier's moods for six days now — the creak of it settling in the cold hours before dawn, the wet whisper of meltwater running beneath the surface like a pulse, the way the light went from pewter to bone-white and back again. They knew ice the way some people knew a lover's breathing: by what it did when it thought no one was listening.

But this morning the ice said nothing, and that silence had a shape August didn't like.

They stopped where the route notes said to stop — a shelf of grey stone thrust up through the glacier's flank, studded with rock flour and old moraine debris, the kind of ground that left no tracks worth following. Ilse crouched beside them, her bandaged hands tucked into her armpits against the cold, breath steaming in short, controlled plumes. She'd learned to breathe like that somewhere bad, August thought. Somewhere that taught you your own exhale could give you away.

"Wind's wrong," she murmured.

"I know."

"It's been wrong since the saddle."

"I know." August unslung their pack and knelt, making a show of adjusting straps, buying thirty seconds with their eyes on the horizon instead. The wind had swung north sometime in the night, and with it had come a smell that didn't belong on the ice: diesel. Faint, thread-thin, but there. Machines idling somewhere downwind, patient as spiders.

"They're already here," Ilse said. Not a question. Her voice was flat the way a frozen lake is flat — smooth until you understand how deep the water under it goes.

"Or they want us to think they are." August scanned the moraine ridge. Grey stone, grey sky, grey shadow. Nothing moved. That was the problem. On a morning like this there should have been ptarmigan working the scree, or at least the wind-stirred glitter of blowing snow off the crest. Instead the ridge sat perfectly still, like a held breath. "See anything?"

"No. That's worse." Ilse's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, more like a scar trying to remember how to be one. "The inspection party doesn't hide well because they don't have to. They're not hunters. They're administrators with rifles."

* * *

They'd argued about it the night before, in the blue dark of the ice cave, headlamps hooded so the glow wouldn't leak out onto the glacier's face.

Ilse had wanted to split early. Take the high route over the shoulder of the moraine while the storm cover lasted, drive sealed in its waterproof sleeve against her chest, and let August walk into whatever waited below as nothing but a hired digger with no name worth checking. It was sensible. It was the arithmetic August had been doing for ten years — trade one person's exposure for another's escape, always be the one who walks away clean because there's nothing on them worth finding.

"You don't owe me," Ilse had said. Her frostbitten fingers had fumbled the zipper of her parka, and she'd stared at them with an expression August recognized too well: fury at your own body for being a liability. "You were paid to find a missing team. You found one survivor. Job done. Walk south, file nothing, forget my face."

"And the drive?"

"The drive is mine. My colleagues died for it. You don't get to carry their ghosts, Nakamura." She'd used the name deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to check if it still hurts. August hadn't given it to her; she'd read it off the survey log's margin notes weeks ago, back when the team was still alive and comparing handwriting samples like schoolchildren. "It doesn't even have to be true. It just has to be the name they're looking for."

That was the thing about Ilse Brandt. Four days ago she'd been a stranger shivering in a trapper's cabin, and now she could find the exact seam in August's chest and pick at it with surgical patience. Survivors learned to do that. You couldn't outrun what you wouldn't look at.

Now, crouched behind the moraine shelf with diesel on the wind, August understood that the argument was already over. It had been over since the moment they'd seen the ridge sitting too still.

Because August had done something Ilse didn't know about. Two nights ago, hunched over the camp stove while Ilse slept, they'd taken a blank data drive from their salvage kit — the kind of cheap, unbranded unit every digger carried for black-market copies — and spent four hours loading it. Survey coordinates, real ones, but scrubbed. The burial ground reduced to a geological anomaly. The team's photographs replaced with corrupted files. Enough structure to survive a casual inspection, enough emptiness to protect everything that mattered. A decoy built from the bones of the truth.

And then, in the last hour before dawn, August had written something else. A route note, in their own cramped hand, describing a cache point two days south of here — a false cache, a dead end, a place where anyone following the paper trail would dig through scree until their hands bled and find nothing but August's lies. They'd tucked it into the decoy pack alongside the drive, where a searcher would find it and believe they'd found a plan.

Let them chase the ghost. Let them waste the calving window digging in the wrong valley while Ilse walked the real route south with the real drive pressed against her heart.

The only cost was the one thing August had never planned to pay.

* * *

They came over the ridge at noon, when the light was flattest and shadows shortest — six figures in matching white shells, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they stood on. No skis. No climbing gear worth the name. One of them dragged a sled-mounted scanner, its antenna whipping in the wind like a fishing line.

August stood up out of the rocks and let them see it happen.

There was a strange mathematics to surrender. You calculated the distance to the nearest rifle, the angle of the slope, the seconds it would take Ilse to reach the low route if she started the moment attention fixed on you. You calculated, and then you stopped calculating, because the whole point was to be a body that blocked the equation.

"Hold it there." The lead figure raised a hand — not a weapon, just a palm, which was somehow worse. Bureaucracy made flesh. He was fifty-ish, square-built, with the wind-chapped complexion of a man who spent his field seasons in heated vehicles. His eyes went over August's gear with the quick inventory of someone who'd read a file. "Digger. Salvage class. Working alone?"

"Working alone." August kept their hands visible, loose at their sides. Behind the lead man's shoulder, two of the others had peeled off toward the low route — toward where Ilse had been twenty minutes ago, though by now she'd be three hundred meters further along and invisible against the icefall's broken shadow. Every second this man talked was a second she gained. So August gave him words to talk with. "Found the camp six days back. Tents up, radios live, nobody home. Been following the route since."

"Following it for whom?"

"For whoever pays triple rate and doesn't ask questions." August shrugged, letting the motion carry the lie loose and easy. "Same as everyone works the Quarter."

The man smiled without warmth. From his inside pocket he drew a folded sheet of paper — actual paper, which meant it mattered enough to print — and opened it against the wind. "Then you'll have no objection to showing us what you're carrying."

Here it was, then. The hinge of the whole ten-day run, balanced on a cheap data drive and a forged note in a dead woman's pack.

August unslung the decoy pack slowly, set it on the scree, and stepped back three paces. The scanner operator waded forward and ran the wand over it twice, then pulled the drive from its sleeve and slotted it into a handheld reader. For ninety seconds the only sound was wind and the small electronic chirp of files indexing. August watched the reader's screen the way you watch a seismograph, waiting for the needle to jump.

It didn't. Coordinates, corrupted images, a route note promising a cache two days south. The operator grunted and handed the drive to the lead man, who turned it over twice in his chapped fingers like a man judging a coin.

"There's a survey team missing on this glacier," he said. "Government contract. Six specialists. You're telling me you've seen no sign of personnel."

"I'm telling you I found a camp with no footprints leaving it." August met his eyes and held them, and discovered — with a lurch that felt almost like vertigo — that the terror and the calm were the same sensation wearing different coats. "Whatever happened to them, the ice has had six days to edit it. I dig what's left. I don't write the endings."

Something flickered across the man's face. Doubt, maybe. Or the particular frustration of an administrator whose script has been followed too literally. He looked down at the paper in his hand, and August saw the header block, the official letterhead, the field marked *SUBJECT* — and understood that the paper wasn't a warrant at all. It was a description. A wanted notice. And the subject line didn't say *Nakamura*.

It said *Brandt, I.* — cartographer, survey team member, last seen at the trapper's cabin. They weren't hunting August at all. They were hunting the survivor, and August had been nothing but the bloodhound they'd loosed on her trail.

Which meant the name August was about to give would mean nothing to him. Which meant it would mean everything.

"Name," the man said. "For the record. We log all contacts on government ice."

The wind gusted off the ridge, carrying grit and glitter. Somewhere south, beyond the icefall, a woman with ruined hands was walking a route drawn in a stranger's handwriting, carrying six dead colleagues' final testimony against her chest. All August had to do was stay smoke. Stay impermanent. Give him the work-name, the border-town alias, the identity that dissolved like breath off glass — *Ash, they call me, Ash, no surname, no record* — and walk away from this ridge the same person who'd walked onto it.

Ten years of practice. Ten years of being no one, flawlessly.

August heard themselves speak, and the voice that came out was rusty, formal, almost ceremonious — the voice of a person opening a door that had been welded shut for a decade.

"Nakamura. August Nakamura."

The name hung in the cold air, absurdly small, absurdly enormous. Saying it felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had grown around its straps — and like stepping off a ledge with nothing below. The lead man wrote it down without ceremony, one more entry in a log full of entries, unaware he'd just witnessed a resurrection. But August felt it land in their chest like a calving crack: the sound of something enormous letting go.

He handed back the drive. "Stay off the high route. Sealing season starts soon." Then the white shells were turning, re-forming, flowing back over the ridge toward their machines and their diesel patience — except for the two who'd gone low, who did not come back, who were even now following a decoy note toward a cache that existed only in August's handwriting.

August stood alone on the moraine with their own name ringing in their ears and their heart going like a drill bit, and did not move for a long time. Being visible, it turned out, felt like standing in open wind after years underground: blinding, freezing, and impossibly, terrifyingly clean.

Then they shouldered the empty pack, turned south, and started walking — fast now, faster than caution allowed, because the two men on the low route would find nothing in the false cache, and when they found nothing, they would come back asking questions.

And August had just told them exactly who to ask.
