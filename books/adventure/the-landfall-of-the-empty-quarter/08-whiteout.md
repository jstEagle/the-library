## 8. Whiteout

The storm found her an hour after August turned back.

Ilse had watched them go — a dark figure shrinking down the white corridor of the moraine, walking with that unhurried, deliberate gait, as if the ice were a room they were leaving tidily. Then the wind came off the shelf in a long gray wall and took even the memory of them away, and she was alone with a data drive zipped into her inner pocket, riding against her sternum like a second heart.

She had crossed worse weather. She told herself this while she lashed her goggles down and checked the compass against the route notes for the fourth time. Twenty-two years surveying, eleven of them in the Empty Quarter's glacier fields. She had been whiteout-blind on the Vesterdal shelf, had dug a snow cave with her bare hands when the storm ate her tent on the Kolvik traverse. She knew what wind did to a person: first it took the horizon, then the sound, then the sense of which way was down.

But she had never crossed worse weather carrying something this heavy. The drive weighed nothing. That was the trick of it. Forty grams of metal and glass, and it held six weeks of her team — Tomas's laughter on the evening recordings, Priya's careful voice reading coordinates, the burial ground rendered in three hundred overlapping scans, every chamber, every cairn, every painted stone the government's mining schedule had marked with a red X and the word *overburden*.

Overburden. As if the dead were gravel to be stripped before the ore beneath.

The notes were folded in her left mitten, inside layer, where her body heat would keep the ink from freezing brittle. August had written them by headlamp at the trapper's cabin, kneeling on the floor, tongue between their teeth like a schoolchild, and Ilse had watched and thought: *this is a person who has spent ten years making sure no one could trace them, and they are writing their handwriting onto my survival.*

*Crevasse field begins at the split boulder. Do not trust the drift bridge east of the marker wands — I planted those to mislead anyone following us. Real line runs west-northwest, 285 degrees, toward the twin seracs. Count your steps. At four hundred, stop. Probe everything.*

She had asked, that night, why August carried a pencil instead of a pen. *Ink freezes,* they'd said, not looking up. *Pencil survives anything. You can erase yourself if you have to.* And then they'd paused, and added, quieter: *I'm done erasing myself. But the habit stays.*

Now she stood at the split boulder and could not see the boulder. She could feel it — a shoulder of granite under the wind-scoured ice, familiar under her boots the way a doorframe is familiar in a dark house — but the world had been erased around it, rubbed out to a uniform pearl-gray in every direction. No up. No down. Only the wind's hand pushing at her back and the compass card trembling in its housing like a small frightened animal.

Two-eighty-five. She turned until the needle agreed with the number, and stepped off the boulder into nothing.

* * *

Counting steps is a kind of prayer. You give your mind one task and it has no room left for fear. Ilse counted, and the wind counted over her, and somewhere beneath both counts the glacier went about its own arithmetic — settling, groaning, opening and closing its blue mouths in the dark under her feet.

At two hundred steps the wind shifted a point north and she corrected without stopping, because stopping was how people died out here; you stopped, you thought about warmth, you sat down, and the snow took you gently, like a mother tucking in a child who had finally stopped fussing. She had seen it. Everyone who worked the ice had seen it.

At three hundred steps she lost the count.

It happened the way it always happened — a gust slammed her sideways, she stumbled, caught herself on her probe, and the number simply wasn't there anymore. Was it three hundred or three hundred and forty? Had she double-counted at the stumble? The uncertainty opened under her like one of the crevasses themselves.

She stood in the featureless gray and made herself breathe. Four breaths. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way she'd taught the new hires, the way Tomas had never quite learned, God rest him, he was always breathing wrong on the rope.

Then she started again from one, and adjusted: if she had been at three hundred, she would reach the stopping point forty steps early, and probing early cost time but not life. Probing late cost both. She chose the error that forgave.

That was the whole discipline, really. Choose the error that forgives. Her team had voted to document the site knowing what it would cost, and Priya had said exactly that at the vote: *if we're going to be wrong, let's be wrong on the side of the dead.* Ilse carried the drive because she had been the only one fast enough, and being fast enough was its own wound, the survivor's arithmetic that never balanced.

At what should have been four hundred — or three hundred and sixty, or four hundred and forty — she stopped and probed.

The pole went through snow, through snow, through snow — and then rang on blue ice, solid, and she exhaled a fog of relief and moved forward one body length and probed again.

On the third probe the pole dropped through and kept dropping, and she felt it knock against something far below, a hollow knock, like a fist on a door in an empty house.

A crevasse. Hidden under the season's snow, invisible, patient. Exactly where August's notes said the field would begin.

She laughed — a short, ugly bark the wind stole instantly — and marked the edge with a wand from her pack, and probed her way along the lip until she found the bridge August had described: *a drift bridge at the narrowest point, maybe two meters wide, wind-packed, probably holding. Probably. Test it flat-footed, weight centered, and do not look down, because looking down is a lie you tell yourself that you're in control.*

* * *

The bridge looked like nothing. That was the horror of it. A smooth white lane across a gap she could not see the edges of, in a world where she could not see anything at all. The wind had risen another register; it screamed now in a single sustained note, and the snow came not from above but from everywhere, horizontal, stinging even through the foam padding of her hood.

She knelt at the edge and put her palm flat on the surface. Wind-packed firn, dense as concrete. Good sign. But she had signed off on bridges like this before, in another life, with a stamp and a salary and colleagues to share the blame. There was no stamp here. There was only her weight and the ice's patience.

*Do not look down.*

She tested it flat-footed, one step, weight centered. Held. Another step. Held. The wind tried to spin her — disorientation comes fast when there's no horizon, your inner ear fills with lies — and she fixed her eyes on her own boots, the only true thing in the universe, and stepped again.

Halfway across, the bridge spoke.

Not a crack — a *sigh*, a deep structural exhalation, the sound of a thousand tons of compacted snow reconsidering its opinion. Ilse froze, one foot forward, arms out, and felt the surface tremble under her like the skin of a drum struck softly somewhere far away.

Every instinct she owned said *run*. Run was death. Run was weight arriving all at once, rhythmically, the exact percussion that killed drift bridges. She made herself stand still, made herself breathe, made herself become as light as a woman of fifty-nine with frostbitten hands and a dead team could become.

And in the stillness, absurdly, she heard August's voice. Not remembered — invented, the mind supplying what the storm had taken: *You can erase yourself if you have to. I'm done erasing myself.*

Four days. She had known this person four days. A stranger who walked out of the white and offered water before questions, who read her frostbitten fingers with a gentleness that embarrassed them both, who wrote route notes in pencil because pencil survives anything. Who had gone back toward the checkpoints alone so she wouldn't have to, buying her passage with their own name — the name they'd spent a decade burying — and had handed her the real drive with both hands, like handing over a child.

*I'll draw you a line through the field,* August had said. *After that you're on your own, but the line will be true. It's the only thing I've got that's ever been true.*

The bridge sighed again. Somewhere below, in the blue dark, the knocked-against hollow waited with its mouth open.

Ilse thought about Tomas, who breathed wrong. About Priya, who was wrong on the side of the dead. About three hundred painted stones under a moraine marked *overburden*, waiting for calving season to open the pass and the machines to come through. About the transmission she had to make — the antenna rigged at the relay station past the field, the press frequencies August had written on the same paper as the route, in the same forgiving pencil.

If she fell here, the site died with her. Not violently — bureaucratically. A red X, a schedule line, a strip-mine, and centuries of the dead reclassified as spoil. That was the vanishing she had been running from since the inspection party came up the valley with their clean tools and their polite voices. Her team hadn't vanished. They had been *edited*. And editing was worse, because it left no tracks, just like the camp, just like the tents pitched neat and the radios functional and the snow swept smooth behind them.

She would not be edited.

She took the step.

The bridge held. She took another. Each step now was a transaction with the ice — her weight offered, the glacier's silence accepted — and she paid them one at a time, flat-footed, eyes down on her boots, counting again because counting was prayer and she needed every god she could get. Ninety. Eighty-five. The far side had to be close, though the world gave her no evidence of anything except white and wind and the tremble in her own knees.

At sixty steps remaining — she was counting backward now, a trick for the exhausted — the bridge exhaled a second time, longer, lower, and a seam opened under her left boot with a sound like a zipper the size of a valley.

She threw herself forward.

No decision in it — pure animal arithmetic, momentum over hesitation — and she landed flat across firm snow on the far side as the last meter of the bridge slumped and slid away behind her, pouring itself gently, almost politely, into the dark.

She lay there with her cheek against the snow, gasping, the drive pressing hard against her sternum, and laughed until the laughing turned into something else, and let the storm take the sound of that too.

* * *

The relay station was a black shape she walked into before she saw it — a hull of corrugated steel half-drifted, door on the lee side, exactly where August's final note promised: *door sticks, lift don't push, stove has fuel, I cached it in October when I scouted the route. I scout everything. It's how I stayed alive. Consider it paid forward.*

Inside, out of the wind's scream, the silence rang. Ilse got the stove lit with hands that barely answered her, and thawed them over the heat weeping without deciding to, and then unzipped her jacket and took out the drive and set it on the table under the lamp.

Small. Gray. Forty grams.

She rigged the antenna through the storm hatch with fingers she ordered around like a foreman ordering a broken crew, and powered the transmitter from the station's dying battery bank, and watched the frequency display climb to the numbers written in soft grey pencil on August's paper.

Before she keyed it, she took out her own notebook — the real one, the survey log she'd kept hidden inside her coat through everything — and turned to the last page, where the team's names were written in her own hand. Tomas. Priya. Devi. Anselm. Ruth. Five names the official record said had never existed at that site at all.

She copied them onto the transmission header, above the scan files, above the coordinates of the burial ground, above the mining schedule with its red X. Let the record carry all of them. Let the record carry August's name too — she added it at the bottom, *route and rescue: A. Nakamura*, spelling it carefully, because a name given away to save you deserves to be spelled correctly forever.

Outside, the wind changed pitch. Dropped. Rose again — but different now, and underneath it, faint through the steel walls, came a sound she knew from twenty-two years on the ice and had hoped never to hear this early:

A long, cracking boom, rolling up the glacier from the direction of the pass. Then another. The calving had begun ahead of schedule, and somewhere down-route, at the corridor's mouth, August was standing in front of it.

Ilse put on the headset, keyed the transmitter, and began to send.
