## 9. Landfall

The route's mouth was a seam of black water between two walls of ice, and August reached it at dawn with the taste of copper in their throat and eleven days of the Empty Quarter still clinging to their clothes like a second skin.

They had come down the corridor fast — faster than was sane, faster than the crevasse field forgave — because every hour bought Ilse another hour of signal. The transmitter she'd salvaged from the survey camp had a window of maybe six hours before the company's jamming sweep cycled back over this quadrant of the coast, and Ilse had needed all of them to climb to the ridge where the antenna could see past the moraine. Six hours to push a decade of buried history into the open air and hope someone was listening.

August's job was simpler and worse: stand at the bottom of the mountain and be seen.

The black water breathed against the ice walls. Somewhere beneath it, the glacier was already beginning to let go of itself. August could feel it through their boots — not sound exactly, but pressure, a deep tectonic restlessness like a sleeper turning over. The calving season was opening early this year. The pass would seal sooner than anyone planned for.

Which meant the mining company's man would be in a hurry too.

* * *

He came out of the fog at half past seven, walking the shoreline of the melt pool with the unhurried gait of someone who owned the ground he stood on, or believed he did. Behind him came the fixer — Voss, though August doubted that was any more his real name than Nakamura had been theirs until three days ago — and behind Voss, two men in company grey with the flat, patient posture of people paid to wait rather than act.

The fixer raised one hand, an odd little greeting, almost friendly.

"You made good time," Voss called across the water. His voice carried strangely off the ice, doubled and flattened. "I'll give you that."

"The decoy drive," August said. Their voice sounded foreign to them, loud in all that whiteness. "You've opened it by now. You know what you're looking at."

"Oh, we know." The mining man stopped at the water's edge. He was younger than August expected, soft-handed, with the careful tan of someone who flew into places rather than walked into them. "A very convincing fake. Weeks of work, I'd imagine. Which tells me the real one exists, and tells me you understand exactly what's at stake." He smiled the way men smile when they think kindness is a tool they've packed. "So here's what happens now. You tell us where the cartographer is. We collect the drive. And your warrant — the one from Meridian, the one that's followed you through four territories — quietly expires. Unfiled. You walk away clean. New name if you want it. We're reasonable people, Nakamura."

*August.* Not the name they'd signed at the border checkpoints, not the name on the salvage permits. The old name, the mother's name, spoken aloud on the ice like something exhumed.

"That's not my name anymore," August said.

"It's the only one you have." Voss shifted his weight, and August saw the exhaustion under his competence — a man who had been awake as long as they had, chasing them down a route he didn't fully understand. "Look. I don't care about you. I never did. You were a means of finding her. Give her up and none of this touches you. That's the whole offer. It doesn't get better, and it doesn't stay open long."

The black water lapped. High above, invisible in the fog, a radio mast was throwing everything Ilse Brandt knew into the sky — the burial ground under the moraine, the intact chambers, the government's own demolition schedule stamped and dated, the names of nine dead surveyors who had voted to document a history instead of erasing it. Every minute August kept these men standing here was a minute of signal.

"Nine people," August said. "Your inspection party killed nine people and pitched their tents so no one would come looking. And you want to talk to me about clean."

The mining man's smile thinned. "Allegations."

"In writing," August said. "In her handwriting. In their handwriting. Timestamped, geotagged, witnessed. You should have burned the camp instead of leaving it. Sloppy."

Something moved behind the mining man's eyes — the first honest thing on his face. He turned to Voss. "Is that true?"

Voss didn't answer him. He was watching August instead, and what was in his face wasn't anger. It was something wearier, almost admiring, the look of a professional recognizing a professional decision even when it was aimed at him.

"You understand what you're doing," Voss said quietly. "You're choosing the warrant."

"I'm choosing my name." August heard their own voice steady, and was surprised by it. Ten years of aliases, of paying cash, of leaving towns before the mail caught up, of flinching at official letterhead — ten years of being no one, carefully, expensively — and here at the end it took less effort than breathing to stand still. "You can serve it. Right now. I'd rather you did it properly than chased me through another decade."

The fog moved. Out on the melt pool, the light was changing, going from grey to a strange bruised gold, and August realized the sun was trying to rise through it and mostly failing.

The mining man looked at Voss, then at his two grey men, then back at August, recalculating. August could nearly hear the arithmetic: a witness who wanted to be arrested was worth nothing detained and everything dead, but dead witnesses on a route that sealed in days, with a transmission already possibly in flight—

The thought arrived in the mining man's face before it arrived anywhere else.

"She's transmitting," he said.

* * *

August didn't run. That was the discipline of it, the whole hard point. Every muscle they owned wanted to bolt up the ridge toward the antenna, toward Ilse, toward the crackle of the uplink, and instead they planted their boots in the frozen gravel and made themselves a fixed object on the landscape, the one thing in the Empty Quarter that refused to be moved.

"If she's transmitting," August said, "then killing me accomplishes nothing except adding a tenth name to the record. And if she isn't—" they shrugged, deliberately loose, "—then you need me talking, not bleeding. Either way, gentlemen, I'm the most valuable thing standing on this ice, and I'm not going anywhere."

Voss laughed. It was a short, genuine, exhausted sound. "You always were like this?"

"You've never met me before in your life."

"No," Voss agreed. "But I read the file. Your mother's file. She stood like that too, at the end. In the courtroom. Feet planted. Like the ground owed her something." He shook his head slowly. "I told them hiring you was a mistake. People who spend their lives running either break or turn. They never just hand over the goods."

"And which am I?"

"Neither." Voss looked out at the water, at the ice walls going gold and rotten in the failing light. "You're standing still. Nobody does that."

The first crack came then.

It rolled up out of the glacier's depths like a struck bell, vast and low, felt in the teeth before it was heard in the ears, and every face on the shoreline turned toward the ice wall on the far side of the melt pool. A fissure had opened along its face — a blue-black lightning stroke running vertical for two hundred meters — and as they watched, a slab the size of a chapel leaned away from the wall, hung, and let go.

The splash threw spray forty feet into the air. The wave came across the pool in a long grey shoulder, and the grey men scrambled back up the shingle, and the mining man shouted something about schedules, about the pass closing, about how much money was standing on this beach doing nothing, and above it all the glacier groaned again, deeper, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Through the fog, faint and broken, August heard it: the thin electronic whistle of a carrier tone from the ridge. Signal acquired. Or lost. Impossible to tell.

Ilse had six hours. She'd had maybe five.

"Hold him here," the mining man said to the grey men, already turning toward the boats staked at the shingle's edge. "If the drive goes out, we contain it on the legal side. If it doesn't—" he glanced back at August, and there was nothing soft left in his face at all, "—we won't need warrants where we're going."

"I wouldn't," August said clearly, "if I were you. Because in about ninety minutes, every heritage body, every press desk, and two parliamentary offices are going to receive a complete survey package with a cover letter naming everyone involved. Including you. By name, title, and flight manifest." They had invented none of this. They were gambling that Ilse had. From the set of the mining man's shoulders, the gamble was landing. "The only version of this where you're not the story is the version where you're on a plane before it breaks. Go."

For a long moment nobody moved except the water, slapping the new iceberg, and the ice itself, ticking and splitting as it settled into its next shape.

Then the mining man went. Voss lingered a heartbeat longer, and August saw him make some private calculation of his own — debts, survival, the simple arithmetic of a man whose employer's ship was visibly sinking.

"For what it's worth," Voss said, "the file on you is closed as of this morning. Someone upstairs decided a live witness who cooperated was worth more than a cold case." He almost smiled. "Congratulations, Nakamura. You're free."

"I know," August said. "That's why I'm staying."

Voss looked at them for a moment longer — really looked, the way one survivor looks at another — and then he followed his employer into the fog, and the sound of the boat engines faded north, and August stood alone on the shingle with two grey men who suddenly seemed very unsure of their instructions.

The glacier cracked again. Closer. A long tearing note, like fabric giving way.

And then, thin and triumphant through the fog from the ridge above, cutting through the groan of the calving ice like a needle through wool:

Three short tones. Pause. Three more.

Ilse's signal. Sent. Acknowledged. Received somewhere far south of the ice, by someone with a press credential and a conscience.

August closed their eyes and let the sound land — the transmission, the cracking glacier, the warrant dissolving like frost in sunlight — and for the first time in ten years they did not check the horizon for whoever might be coming for them.

Someone was coming. Let them come. August Nakamura had finally stopped running, and the ground under their feet was the first ground in a decade that felt like it belonged to them.

One of the grey men cleared his throat. "Sir? We've been ordered to bring you in."

August opened their eyes. "I know," they said, and held out their wrists. "Let's go meet the record."

Above them, the ice wall leaned, groaned, and began, magnificently, to fall.
