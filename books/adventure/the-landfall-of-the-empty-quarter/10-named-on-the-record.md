## 10. Named on the Record

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and old paper, and August Nakamura sat in it with their hands folded on a table scarred by decades of nervous fingers, and waited for someone to say their name.

Not *the digger*. Not *the salvage contractor known as Nak*. Not *you, in the chair, look up when you're addressed*. Their name. The whole of it, spoken into a microphone by a clerk with a flat voice and a clipboard, the way names get said in rooms where they become permanent.

"August Emiko Nakamura. Please state your full name for the record."

"I already did," August said, and then, because the clerk's face didn't move, "August Emiko Nakamura."

It was the third time in six weeks. The first time had been at the route's mouth, shouted across the ice to a man with a company badge and a pistol he never needed to draw — *my name is August Nakamura, write it down* — and the saying of it had felt like pulling a splinter that had lodged itself against bone for ten years. The second time had been at the arraignment, in a cell-block corridor that smelled of disinfectant and wet wool, to a public defender who'd looked at the warrant, looked at August, and said, quietly, "This should have been dissolved years ago. It was never valid. You know that, don't you?"

They had known it. That had been the whole sickness of it. A warrant earned by no one, pinned to a child because the child's mother had signed her name on documents she shouldn't have touched, and the state, embarrassed by its own famous archaeologist, had needed somewhere small to put its anger. August had run from a piece of paper that was, legally speaking, a ghost. Ten years of ghost-running. Border towns, cash digs, no paperwork, no names. Impermanence as an identity, worn like a coat until the coat grew into the skin.

The hearing officer — not a judge, exactly; a heritage tribunal officer with reading glasses on a chain and the patient expression of someone who had heard every possible version of every possible lie — turned a page.

"You'll understand," she said, "that we've read the survey data. All of it. Ms. Brandt's transmission reached four news services and two heritage bodies before the calving season closed the pass." She looked up over the glasses. "You'll also understand that we are obliged to ask you to describe, in your own words, how you came to possess knowledge of the site."

"In my own words," August said. "Or the true ones?"

A flicker at the corner of the officer's mouth. Someone in the gallery coughed. The room was half-full: two lawyers from the mining consortium sitting very straight with their hands flat on their folders like men holding down lids; a woman from the heritage trust who had not stopped taking notes since the session opened; a cluster of press at the back, quiet as mourners. And near the door, in the last row, Ilse Brandt, her frostbitten hands wrapped around a paper cup, watching August with the level gray patience of someone who had crossed a crevasse field on a stranger's handwriting and did not intend to be impressed or unimpressed by anything ever again.

"The true ones," the officer said.

"Then I was hired to find them," August said. "Cash, no questions. A fixer approached me in Corvane — triple rate, no paperwork, no names. I thought it was a recovery job. Missing team, government insurance, the usual. I took it because rent was due and running is expensive." They paused. "I took it because I've spent ten years being good at jobs where nobody asks who you are."

"And what did you find?"

"Tents pitched. Food cached. Radios functional. And not one set of tracks leading out of that camp. Nothing. Snow that hadn't been touched since it fell." August heard their own voice go flat and careful, the digger's voice, the one used for describing strata. "Eleven people don't leave a camp without leaving a mark unless they're carried out, or unless they leave in a way designed to look like nothing happened at all. It was the second one. Professional. Deliberate. Somebody wanted the Empty Quarter to keep them."

The lawyer from the consortium shifted. One of his colleagues whispered something behind a cupped hand.

"The survey log," the officer prompted. "Fragmentary."

"Torn. But enough. They'd found something under the moraine — something the mining schedule had flagged for demolition the moment the calving season opened the pass. A burial ground. Pre-colonial. Intact." August's throat tightened, and they let it. There was no percentage anymore in pretending the words didn't cost anything. "Ilse can tell you what it looked like better than I can. She stood inside it. I only ever saw the maps she drew from memory, after, with hands that couldn't feel the pencil. But I saw the schedule. I saw the demolition flags on a site older than every border on every map in this room. And I understood that the missing team wasn't missing. They were made to vanish, because they documented something the document was supposed to erase."

Silence, of the particular quality that silence takes on in rooms where lawyers are calculating. Then the senior counsel for the consortium rose, smoothed his jacket, and asked, in a voice like a well-ironed shirt, whether Mr. Nakamura — *Ms.*? He glanced at his notes; August let him flounder — whether the witness would care to address the matter of the decoy drive surrendered at the moraine checkpoint, and whether the witness understood that surrendering false evidence to a lawful inspection party might constitute—

"It wasn't evidence," August said. "It was a rock with a sticker on it. Your inspection party accepted it without powering it on, which tells the tribunal everything it needs to know about how lawful that party was. And yes, I gave them my name while I did it. First time in a decade I'd said it out loud to anyone official. It felt terrible." They looked directly at the man. "Also it felt like the first full breath I'd taken in ten years. You can put both of those in your filing if you like."

Someone in the gallery laughed — short, surprised, quickly smothered. The officer rapped her pen once against the table, but August saw the crease at the edge of her mouth deepen before she killed it.

The questioning went on through the morning. The warrant, produced and read aloud — August listened to their own history recited in bureaucratic cadence, the mother's name spoken with the hushed reverence people still used for her, the crime laid at the daughter's feet with the lazy arithmetic of people who believe blood is evidence. Then the dissolution order, read aloud too, the officer's voice gathering a strange gentleness on the final line: *the warrant is dissolved, and the record amended.*

Amended. As though ten years could be edited. As though a life could be re-filed under its correct heading.

But that was the thing August had come to understand, out there on the ice, in the whiteout, in the eleven-day window between the calving and the sealing: the record was not the life. The record was just where you chose to stand and be counted. Everything else — the running, the hiding, the coat of impermanence — had been the refusal to stand anywhere at all.

When the officer asked if there was anything further, August said there was.

"There's a site under the moraine in the Empty Quarter," they said. "And there will be, for about another nine days. Then the shelf calves, the corridor seals, and the ice takes it back — takes all of it, the burial ground, the equipment, the camp, the place where eleven people stopped existing on paper. Your legal protection came too late to save it physically. Everyone in this room knows that. The consortium knows it best of all; it's why they fought the injunction so politely for so long." They unfolded their hands. "So I want it on the record — my record, the one with my actual name on it now — that what's being protected isn't ground. It's the fact that it existed. The maps. The documentation. Ilse's drive. Eleven names that somebody tried to subtract. Ice erases. Records don't have to. That's the whole choice this room is making today, and I'd like everyone to make it with their eyes open."

The officer wrote for a long time. When she looked up, she said only, "Noted for the record, Mr.—" and then corrected herself, deliberately, "—for the record, August Emiko Nakamura. This tribunal stands adjourned pending ruling."

The gavel came down soft, more punctuation than percussion.

* * *

Outside, the light was the thin gold of late afternoon in a city far from any ice, and Ilse was waiting on the courthouse steps with two paper cups of coffee and no questions, which was, August had learned, her highest form of tenderness.

She handed one over. Her bandaged hands managed it clumsily; August steadied the cup with two fingers, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Well?" Ilse said.

"They dissolved it."

"I heard. The whole staircase heard. You were loud."

"I was thorough."

"You were showing off." She sipped her coffee, wincing at the heat against whatever sensation was coming back into her fingers. For a while they just stood there, two people who had survived by not being seen, standing in full view of a courthouse, a street, a sky. Traffic went by. A pigeon inspected Ilse's boot with professional interest.

"The site," Ilse said finally. "Nine days."

"Eight, maybe. The calving ran early last year."

"My maps will outlive it." She said it simply, without bravado, the way you'd state an elevation. "Every chamber. Every placement. Every grave. They'll have to quarry the archive to get rid of it now, and the archive has my handwriting all over it, and my handwriting," she added, with the ghost of the dry humor that had kept her alive in a trapper's cabin through a winter of waiting, "is famously illegible."

August laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising them both, rising up out of some sealed chamber of its own. Below the courthouse steps the city went on being a city, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere far to the north a glacier was groaning toward its own calendar, counting down days it didn't know it was keeping.

"So," Ilse said. She was looking at the traffic, not at August, offering the question the way she offered everything — sideways, deniable, easy to leave unanswered. "What does a person do, after? When the running stops and the record starts. What's next?"

August drank the coffee. It was bad coffee, courthouse coffee, burnt and overpriced, and it was the best thing they had tasted in ten years.

"I was thinking," August said, "about a dig. Legitimate. Permitted. Paperwork with my actual name on it, signed three times." They turned the cup in their hands, watching the steam. "There's a site I've been reading about. North. Cold. Nobody's mapped it properly since before the war, and the local records—" they smiled, and it felt strange on their face, like a muscle remembered from a previous life, "—the local records are a mess. Somebody would have to go in and set them straight."

Ilse looked at them then, fully, the gray eyes doing their level arithmetic.

"Somebody with experience," she said, "in places that don't want to be found."

"Somebody," August agreed, "who's done being one of them."

The wind came down the avenue carrying the smell of rain instead of snow, and August Nakamura — named, recorded, dissolvable no longer — finished the bad coffee and started down the steps toward it.
