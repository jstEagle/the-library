## 1. The Town That Forgot Its Name

The river came down out of the mountains the color of old tea, carrying silt and secrets in equal measure, and Karawari Landing sat in its mud like a thing that had given up arguing with the water long ago.

Dominic Montrose—though no one in Karawari Landing knew that name, and no one ever would—woke before dawn to the sound of rain on a corrugated roof and the smell of coal smoke drifting up from the landing below. He lay for a moment in the narrow cot above his workshop, listening to the town assemble itself out of the dark: the groan of mooring lines, a dog complaining somewhere near the fish market, the first barge horn, flat and mournful, announcing that another day of buying and hauling and lying had begun.

He had been Dan Corrick here for eleven months. Before that he had been Samuel Voss in a timber camp two hundred miles south, and before that a name he could not now recall without effort, worn smooth as a river stone from handling. Names were clothes. You put them on, you wore them until they frayed, and then you burned them and cut new ones. It was the only tailoring he was good at anymore.

He rose, dressed in the dark by touch, and went down the outside stair into the workshop with its iron smell and its oil-dark floorboards. The locomotive took up most of the shed's length—a narrow-gauge yard engine belonging to the Karawari Dredging Company, her boiler scaled with years of hard running, her driving rods worn to a knock that kept half the town awake at night. The company paid him four shillings a day and asked nothing about him except whether the engine would run, which suited him better than any wage ever had. An engine did not care what a man used to be. An engine only cared whether he understood fire, water, pressure, and steel, and Dominic understood those things the way some men understood scripture: completely, and with a certain grief.

He built a small fire under her boiler and stood watching the gauge climb while the rain thickened against the roof. Every window in the workshop was shuttered. Every door—the front, the back, the loft hatch—stood unlocked. This was the discipline he had settled on after two years of running, the arithmetic of fear reduced to its simplest terms: a locked door told a man there was something behind it worth forcing. An unlocked door said *nothing here but a mechanic and his rust*. Shutters said *a man who minds his own business*. Together they made him invisible, and invisibility was the whole of his ambition now, though God knew how exhausting an ambition it turned out to be. Invisibility was not rest. Invisibility was holding your breath for years.

By seven the engine was ticking and breathing, steam curling off her safety valve, and the dredge crews came down to take her out to the pontoons. Old Tamu, the head winchman, paused at the door as always and looked at Dominic over the rim of his mug.

"She'll run today?"

"She'll run today."

"You say that every day, Corrick."

"And she runs every day, Tamu."

Tamu grinned through his beard and went out into the rain, and Dominic stood alone in the warm iron-smelling dark and let himself feel, for exactly sixty seconds, the small clean satisfaction of a machine made right. Then he banked the fire, closed the firebox door, and began the day's other work: being nobody.

* * *

Karawari Landing had perhaps four hundred souls when the river was low and fewer when it was high, strung along a mile of mudbank like beads on a wet string. There was the company store, the mission chapel with its crooked cross, three drinking houses of descending reputation, a Chinese laundry, a brothel above the net-mender's that everyone pretended not to see, and a telegraph office whose line ran east toward the coast and west into country so broken that the wire itself seemed an act of defiance. The town had been called three other names in its short life—there was still a faded sign nailed to the customs shed reading PORT ALBERMARLE, and older residents spoke of Wren's Landing as if remembering a dead relative—and the fact that it kept shedding its names and taking up new ones was, Dominic thought, the reason he could bear to live in it. A town that forgot its own name did not ask a man to remember his.

He bought his bread and tinned milk at the company store just after eight, keeping to the boardwalk because the mud beyond it could swallow a boot to the ankle. Rain fell with the patience of something that intended to go on forever. The river slid past, brown and muscular, carrying logs, carrying silt, carrying once—he had seen it—a drowned bullock turning slowly like a planet. Everything came down the river eventually. That was the trouble with rivers. They delivered things.

He was halfway back to the workshop, bread under his oilskin, when he saw the clerk.

The man stood beneath the awning of the telegraph office, out of the rain, and he was wrong for Karawari Landing the way a ledger entry is wrong for a poem. His clothes were too good—not rich, but *finished*, wool pressed, boots leathered and heeled, a straw boater despite the weather held dry under one arm. He carried a leather document case and wore the expression of a man who found everything mildly insufficient. Around him the town went about its business in rubber boots and sacking capes, and he stood among them like a comma in a sentence of mud.

Dominic did not look at him twice. Looking twice was a habit he had trained out of himself the way other men trained themselves out of drink. He walked past at the pace of a tired mechanic thinking about a boiler, and behind him he heard the clerk ask the telegraph boy a question, and the boy's voice answering, and though he caught none of the words, he felt them land somewhere between his shoulder blades like light rain through a torn coat.

At noon the clerk came to the workshop.

Dominic heard him before he saw him—boots on the boardwalk with a different rhythm than workmen's boots, unhurried, deliberate—and he had time to set down his wrench, wipe his hands, and arrange his face into the mild blankness of Dan Corrick before the man appeared in the open doorway, ducking slightly under the lintel.

"Morning," the clerk said. "Hot enough to boil a crucifix out here." He smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind that arrived without engaging anything behind the eyes. "Corrick, is it? The dredge company's fitter?"

"That's me." Dominic kept his voice level, unhurried, faintly bored. Boredom was armor. "If you're wanting the passenger steamer looked at, she's berthed at the government jetty. I don't do marine work."

"No marine work." The clerk stepped inside, uninvited, and stood surveying the shop with the air of a man appraising livestock. His eyes moved across the tool wall, the lathe, the parts bins labeled in Dominic's careful block capitals, the cold forge. They did not linger anywhere, which was itself a kind of skill, and Dominic recognized it because he possessed it too. "Name's Pell. I'm up from the coast on company business. Consolidated Sepik Mining and Development." He said the full name the way a priest says the opening of a mass, giving each word its weight. "You'll know them, maybe. They built the mountain lines. Years back."

"Railways aren't much use to a dredging outfit," Dominic said. "We float our ore."

"So I'm told." Pell drifted along the bench, fingertips trailing the wood—not touching tools, never quite touching tools, a man who knew the law of evidence. "Funny thing, though. I've been asking around about the old surveys. The mountain grades. Line surveys, done—must be fifteen years ago now? Company records are a mess. Fires, floods, clerks who couldn't spell." He smiled again. "You'd be surprised what gets lost."

"I wouldn't know anything about railway surveys," Dominic said. "I fix engines."

"Mm." Pell picked up a scrap of chalk from the bench, turned it over, set it down. "Only, the fellow at the telegraph office says you read blueprints. Says when the mission school wanted their roof trusses drawn up, you did it free, proper drawings, elevations and all. Says your lettering was beautiful. Draftsman's lettering, he said." Pell turned, and for the first time his eyes came up and met Dominic's, and they were pale gray and very patient. "Where does a river mechanic learn draftsman's lettering, Mr. Corrick?"

"In a book," Dominic said. "Cost me two weeks' wages once. Best money I ever spent."

They looked at each other across the bench, across the smell of coal and oil, and Dominic felt the old sensation rise in him—that cold, clear, exhausting alertness, the sense of standing on a bridge girder in high wind, knowing exactly how many rivets were sound and how many were not. Fifteen years ago. The mountain grades. The surveys. He had drafted those numbers himself, or numbers very like them, in a drafting room that smelled of ink and cigarette smoke, under men whose names he had spent two years teaching himself not to think of. And now a clerk with a leather case and a priest's smile had come upriver asking after them, in a town of four hundred people where the biggest news in a month was a crocodile taking a dog.

"What's the syndicate want with old surveys?" Dominic asked. "Those lines are closed. Torn up, some of them. The mountain ate the rest."

"Housekeeping," Pell said lightly. "Companies do it. You'd be amazed how much paper a big concern generates. Sometimes you have to go back and tidy." He straightened his boater under his arm. "Sometimes things turn up in the tidying. Things people wrote down who shouldn't have. Little discrepancies." He glanced around the shop once more, and this time his gaze rested, briefly, on the loft hatch above the lathe—the unlocked hatch, the door that said *nothing here worth forcing*—and then came back to Dominic's face. "You keep your doors open, I notice."

"Doors are for going through."

"Windows are shuttered, though." Pell smiled his good smile. "Interesting combination, that. Open doors, shut windows. Man who wants to leave quick but doesn't want anyone looking in." He put out his hand suddenly, and Dominic took it, and the grip was dry and precise. "Well. Pleasure, Mr. Corrick. If anything should turn up—old papers, drawings, anything from the mountain days—you'd send word to the telegraph office, wouldn't you. Addressed to Pell, coast office. There might be something in it for you. Finding fees. Companies pay well for housekeeping."

"I don't find things," Dominic said. "I fix engines."

"Of course you do." Pell released his hand and moved to the doorway, then paused, rain glittering behind him on the boardwalk. "One more thing. Purely professional interest. The old mountain grades—they ran through the Sepik Basin range, up past Broken Narrows. You'd know the country?"

"I know it rains up there," Dominic said. "I know it snows. I know men who went up didn't all come down."

Pell considered that. Something moved behind the gray eyes—satisfaction, or appetite; with such men it was often the same thing. "No," he agreed pleasantly. "They don't all come down. That's rather the point of mountains." He tipped the boater an inch. "Good day, Mr. Corrick."

Then he was gone, boots receding along the boardwalk, unhurried, deliberate, and Dominic stood in his warm iron-smelling shop and discovered that his hands, resting on the bench, had gone white-knuckled without his permission.

* * *

That night he did not sleep.

He lay in the loft above the workshop with the lamp turned low and listened to the rain and to the river, and he performed the exercise he had performed every night for two years: he reviewed the day as an engineer reviews a structure, testing each joint for load. Had he said too much? No. Had he said too little? Perhaps—*I know men who went up didn't all come down* had been a risk, a flicker of knowledge where a true stranger would have shrugged. But Pell had already marked him before he entered the shop; the telegraph boy's gossip about the lettering had done that. The question was not whether Dominic had been careless. The question was why a syndicate clerk was in Karawari Landing at all, asking after surveys fifteen years cold, and what it meant that the asking had begun *now*, in this season, in this town, at the far end of the map of Dominic's former life.

Fifteen years. He had been young and ambitious and certain that the figures on his table were figures, not verdicts. He had inked elevation profiles for grades through the Basin range, and somewhere between his drafting table and the directors' chamber, numbers had changed—contours lowered, rock classifications softened, a seam's depth adjusted by feet that mattered enormously to men deciding where to blast. He had signed. He had told himself the engineers on site would catch any error. He had told himself a great many things, and then the reports had come back thin, and then the reports had stopped coming back at all, and then the lines had quietly closed, section by section, and the syndicate had folded the mountain's dead into the general category of *difficult terrain*, and Dominic had understood what his beautiful lettering had been used to write.

He had run. Not heroically, not with any plan—simply gone, one night, with a carpetbag and his drafting tools, and had been shedding names ever since, like a tree shedding leaves in a wind that never ended.

And now the wind had followed him upriver.

He got up, lit the lamp properly, and sat at the little table by the loft window with its shutter closed against the world. From the drawer beneath he took the tin box that held everything Daniel Corrick owned of value: forty-one pounds in mixed coin, a photograph of a woman long since dead whose face he permitted himself to look at twice a year, and—because he had never quite managed to burn it, though he had tried twice—a single sheet of tracing paper, soft with age, bearing a fragment of profile drawing in his own hand. Elevation, Chainage 44 to 47, the grade climbing toward a tunnel bore marked in the margin with a note in younger, angrier lettering: *figures altered — confirm before blasting — D.M.*

Confirm before blasting. He had written it and then he had not confirmed, and thirty-one men—no. He did not let himself count them. Counting was a door he kept locked even in his own head, though unlike the workshop doors, that lock had never once fooled anyone, least of all himself.

He sat looking at the tracing paper until the lamp burned low, and the rain went on, and the river went on delivering things, and somewhere out in the dark of Karawari Landing a clerk named Pell was writing his report by lamplight, patient as sediment, and Dominic understood with the clarity of exhaustion that the arithmetic of his invisibility had finally failed. Open doors, shut windows. A man who wants to leave quick but doesn't want anyone looking in. Pell had read him in ten minutes the way Dominic read a boiler plate—finding the flaw, tapping the thin place.

In the morning, he decided, he would draw his wages, walk down to the landing, and take the first boat going anywhere. New name. New town. The wind would follow, but the wind always followed, and distance was the only currency he had ever successfully saved.

He was still telling himself this, lying rigid in the dark with the lamp out, when the sound came.

Footsteps on the mud below the workshop—wrong footsteps, stumbling, dragging, a rhythm like a heart in failure. Then a weight falling against the outer stair, a hand slapping wet wood, and a voice, cracked and gasping, calling up into the rain:

"Corrick. The fitter. For God's sake—I was told—Corrick—"

Dominic was already moving, lamp lit, pistol taken from its hiding place in the flour tin and held low against his thigh, every instinct screaming that this was how it would be done, this was exactly how it would be done—bait crying at your own door in the dark. He eased the loft hatch, looked down into the rain-hazed yard, and saw a man collapsed against the bottom step. Old, gray-bearded, soaked through, one hand pressed to his belly in a way that left no doubt at all about what was underneath it, and the other hand raised, clutching something flat and dark toward the light spilling from the hatch.

A waterproof map case, river-stained, strapped shut, held up like an offering.

"They killed Elias Wren for this," the old man whispered, blood and rain shining on his chin. "And they'll kill whoever carries it next. So choose quick, fitter. Choose quick, and carry it to my girl."

Behind him, far down the muddy street, a lantern bobbed. Two of them. Maybe three. Coming on unhurried, deliberate, like men doing housekeeping.
