## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The rain came in off the Karawari that evening the way it always did—not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall of water that swallowed the far bank and turned the river to boiling milk. Dominic Montrose heard it coming twenty minutes before it hit, the way you hear anything on a frontier: not with your ears but with your skin, a pressure change, a hush in the insects, the dogs along the boardwalk going quiet all at once.

He was under the locomotive when it hit—a Number Seven narrow-gauge shunter, forty years old and held together by rivets and stubbornness—lying on his back on the greased planks with a lamp hooked to the axle above him, chasing a crack through the left cylinder casting. The work was honest and the pay was cash and neither fact had anything to do with why he did it. He did it because a man bent double inside an engine's guts cannot be seen from the door, and because the noise of hammering covered conversation, and because at the end of every day his hands were too filthy and too tired to hold anything heavier than a plate of rice.

Invisibility was a trade like any other. You got good at it or you starved at it.

The shed door banged open against its stop and let the storm in sideways.

Dominic's hand went to the wrench before his head came out from under the chassis. Three years in Karawari Landing had taught him the grammar of his own doorway: the creak of Odile Ferrant arriving with cargo complaints, the shuffle of the harbor boys wanting their launch patched, the particular knock—two soft, one hard—that meant someone from the town council wanted something for nothing. This was none of those. This was a body falling against wood, a wet gasp, the sound boots make when they no longer belong to the man wearing them.

"I need—" The voice cracked, restarted. "They said. Down at the landing. A man who fixes engines and asks nothing."

Dominic slid out from under the Number Seven and stood up slowly, wrench loose in his fist, lamplight catching him at chest height so his face stayed in shadow. Old habit. Old habits were the only kind he had left.

The man in the doorway was sixty if he was a day, built like the stumps they pulled out of new rail beds, and he was dying. There was no mistaking it once you'd seen it enough times—the gray under the brown of the skin, the way he stood propped against the frame as though standing were a job he'd been hired for and could be dismissed from any moment. His shirt was soaked through, and not only with rain. Below his left ribs the cloth hung dark and heavy, and a thin thread of red ran down his boot and pooled at the threshold, where the rain was already diluting it, carrying it out into the mud toward the river, toward the sea, as if the world itself wanted the evidence gone.

"Wrong shed," Dominic said. "Doctor's two buildings up."

"Doctor's syndicate." The man laughed, and the laugh cost him; he folded forward a degree, caught himself on the doorframe. "Man pays his debts to the company doctor, the company doctor reports what ails him. I know how it works. I've worked for them thirty years." He lifted his head, and even in the bad light Dominic saw the eyes: clear, furious, patient as bedrock. "You're Montrose."

The wrench stopped moving in his grip. Rain drummed on the tin roof like applause from a crowd of ten thousand.

"You've got the wrong—"

"I've got the right one." The old man pushed off the doorframe and took one step into the shed, and the step nearly finished him. He went down to one knee with a grunt, caught himself on the edge of the workbench, and stayed there, breathing in long deliberate pulls, a man negotiating with his own lungs. "Elias Wren. Surveyor. Prospector, lately. And you are the engineer who walked off the Sepik Basin extension four years ago and buried his name so deep even the syndicate's clerks quit digging." He looked up. Rain ran off his hat brim between them in a silver curtain. "I need a man who knows how to disappear, Montrose. Because I'm about to stop being able to do it myself."

* * *

Dominic should have sent him away. He knew that even then, standing in his lamplight with the storm sealing the world outside, he knew it the way you know the weight of a stone in your hand. Every instinct honed across four years of false names and shuttered windows said: *this is a hole in your wall, this is weather coming through it, close it.* He'd built a life the size of a coffin and he'd kept it swept, and here was a bleeding stranger tracking his real name across the floorboards.

But the man had said *surveyor*, and something old and cold had turned over in Dominic's chest.

He barred the door, dropped the canvas over the window facing the landing road—the other two were already shuttered; they were always already shuttered—and put a kettle on the brazier because there was nothing else useful his hands knew to do while his mind argued with itself. Then he knelt beside Elias Wren on the oil-stained boards and cut the shirt open with a rigging knife.

The wound was exactly as bad as it looked. Small entry below the ribs, low and to the left, the kind of hole a pistol makes when it isn't in a hurry. No exit he could find. The skin around it had gone the color of tallow.

"Gut shot," Wren said, watching Dominic's face rather than his own ruin. It wasn't a question.

"How long ago?"

"Two days. Upriver. They followed me down the Kanduan trail; I lost them at the falls, doubled back through the sago swamp." He smiled, a dry terrible thing. "Leeches ate better than I have all week."

"Who's *they*?"

Wren didn't answer immediately. Dominic worked while he waited—boiled water, salt, clean linen he kept for engine gaskets and now, apparently, for this. He packed the wound as gently as the circumstances allowed, which was not very. Wren took it without sound, gripping a length of scrap iron in both hands until his knuckles went white, and when it was done he lay back against a crate of cylinder oil and breathed for a while, and outside the rain kept up its enormous indifferent applause.

"The Meridian Consolidated," Wren said at last. "The Basin syndicate. Same as ever." He turned his head, and his eyes found Dominic's and held them. "You drafted surveys for them. The upper passes. Before you understood what your lines were being used for."

Dominic's hands went still over the brazier. "That's a story with a lot of endings," he said. "Most of them are mine to choose from, and none of them are yours."

"It's the truth," Wren said simply, "which is rarer than any ending. I carried a theodolite behind men like you for eleven years. I know a draftsman's hands when I see them shake."

"They're not shaking."

"No," Wren agreed. "Not tonight."

* * *

He talked in pieces after that, the way dying men do, sentences laid down like rails with gaps of silence between them where the sleep and the pain came through. Dominic sat on an overturned crate and listened, and the kettle sang and went unsung-to, and somewhere in the third hour he realized he had not thought about the Number Seven's cracked casting since sundown.

Wren had spent thirty years in the Basin country, first for the syndicate, then against it without meaning to. It happened the way these things happen: a survey party, a closed line, a stretch of the high grade the company sealed and struck from its maps with a neatness that bothered a man who had spent his life noticing what maps left out. He started asking questions the way a prospector does—slowly, alone, with a pan and a hammer, looking foolish and harmless. Foolish and harmless opened more doors than badges ever had.

"What's in the closed lines?" Dominic asked.

"Bodies," Wren said. "And paper." He fumbled at his coat, and Dominic helped him, and together they got the coat open and the thing beneath it free: a case of oiled canvas over brass, flat, the size of a hymnal, scarred and re-scarred, the waterproofing gone shiny at the corners where decades of hands had worried it. Wren held it against his chest with both arms, the way a man holds a child or a scripture. "Thirty-one names that don't appear on any payroll. Surveys signed twice—once honest, once cooked. Blast charges logged as rockfalls. A tunnel the company says never carried ore, carrying ore for six months before they collapsed it on the men inside." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. He spoke of it the way a man reads a freight manifest. "I have spent nine years copying everything I could prove. Every page. Every mark. It's all in here, and the key to reading it is a map, and the map is in here, and there are perhaps five men alive who can read it, and I am about to stop being one of them."

"Why me?" Dominic asked. The question had been building in him for an hour, pressure behind a dam. "You come stumbling into a stranger's shed with your blood on my floor and your ghosts in your coat, and you want me—me—to play courier? You know what I am. You said so yourself. I'm a man who ran."

"That's precisely why." Wren shifted against the crate, and the pain moved across his face like weather. "A brave man would take this map straight to a magistrate and get himself dead in a week. A clever man would sell it back to the syndicate and live rich and small forever. But a man who ran—" he stopped, coughed, went on quieter, "—a man who ran knows the price of running. Knows what it costs to carry a thing you should have set down. I watched your name come off those drawings four years ago, Montrose. I was in the assay office when the replacement draftsman traced over your work. Your lines were good. Honest lines. Somebody else's numbers went on top of them."

Dominic looked at his hands. They were steady. He made a study of being certain of that.

"I'm asking," Wren said, "for one delivery. Port Vessel. Coast side of the range. There's a woman there—Maren. My daughter." For the first time the bedrock patience in his voice developed a fault line. "She'll be twenty-six this winter. She thinks I died in the '19 slide, or worse, that I chose the hills over her. Both lies. I told them both myself, God forgive me, because a witness with a family is a witness with a handle, and I couldn't afford handles." He pressed the brass-edged case a little tighter. "Carry it to her. Tell her—" and here the old man's composure finally went, just for a moment, a crack in a dam face, water showing through, "—tell her the hills weren't the choice. Tell her I was always coming home. Tell her the snows were the only reason I was late."

The kettle had gone silent. The fire had burned down to a red breathing glow. Outside, the storm had settled from assault into endurance, the long steady fall that would last until morning and drown the roads and raise the river another foot.

"And the syndicate?" Dominic asked. "They killed you for this. They'll kill whoever carries it."

"They'll try." Wren almost smiled. "That's the adventure, son. That's the whole of it. Everything worth carrying weighs something."

* * *

Dawn found the rain thinner, the river higher, the shed smelling of coal smoke, blood, and boiled linen. Elias Wren slept in fits against the oil crates, each fit shallower than the last, a tide going out in increments too small to argue with. Dominic had not slept at all. He had spent the night doing the arithmetic he was best at—loads, grades, distances, tolerances—and the numbers kept coming out impossible.

Port Vessel lay beyond the range. The passes closed with the first snows, and the first snows were early this year; the harbor talk all week had been of whitecaps on the summits seen from the fish boats, weeks ahead of season. Between here and there lay the river, the portages, the foothill towns where the syndicate's money bought eyes the way other men bought tobacco. And the syndicate already knew Wren had run south with something worth killing for—they'd gut-shot him trying to keep it—and a dying man's destination was the easiest cipher in the world.

Every rational figure on the page said: bury the case in the river mud, move towns, start again under a new name in a new landing where nobody had heard of Montrose or Wren or the Sepik Basin at all. He had done it before. He was good at it. It was, in fact, the single skill of his life.

Wren woke near midmorning, gray as ash, and knew without being told that his tide was going. He held out the case with both hands, and his arms shook with the weight of it, three pounds of brass and canvas that might as well have been an anchor.

"Not a gift," he said. "A bargain. I don't want your charity, I want your promise. Sworn. Out loud. Men like us forget what we owe unless we say it where the air can hear."

Dominic took the case. It was colder than the shed, colder than the rain, as though it had spent nine years underground and hadn't yet accepted the sun. Inside it, faintly, papers shifted.

"Elias Wren," Dominic heard himself say, and his own voice sounded like a stranger's, like a man speaking from the bottom of a well, "I swear I'll carry this to Maren in Port Vessel before the snows close the passes. Alive or not at all."

"Alive," Wren corrected. His eyes had gone bright and terribly clear, the clarity of the last lamp burning before the oil gives out. "Say alive. Not-at-all is easy."

"Alive," Dominic repeated. "Or not at all—but alive first."

Something eased in the old man's face then, some final load transferred, some ballast shifted that let the hull sit level. He nodded, once, the way engineers nod at a gauge reading that finally matches the spec.

"She has her mother's temper," he whispered. "Don't flinch from it. It's how she'll know you're telling the truth."

He was gone within the hour, quietly, between one breath and the next breath that didn't come, with the rain easing off outside and the river running high and brown and fast toward a coast none of them would reach the same way.

Dominic sat for a long time afterward with the case on his knees, listening to the shed settle around him, to the water dripping from the eaves, to the town waking up beyond the canvas-covered window—the same town, the same sounds, the same small safe life he had built plank by plank out of silence.

Then he stood, took up his hammer, and began, methodically, to tear the loft apart for the gear he'd sworn he'd never need again: rope, rations, the river clothes he'd burned the smell of out of his memory years ago. Because there on the workbench, half under a coil of copper wire, he'd laid out the map case to check its seals—and through the gap in the worn canvas corner he had seen, for just a moment, the top margin of the sheet inside.

It was a survey drawing. Upper passes. Contours in a draftsman's fine hand, elevations marching up a line he knew the way a man knows his own signature.

Because it *was* his signature. Altered. Falsified. His own lines from four years ago, wearing somebody else's numbers—and beneath the falsified figures, pressed hard enough to emboss the vellum, someone had written a tally in Elias Wren's blunt field hand:

*31.*

The number of men the syndicate said had never existed.

Dominic buttoned the case shut, and his hands, he noted distantly, had begun at last to shake.
