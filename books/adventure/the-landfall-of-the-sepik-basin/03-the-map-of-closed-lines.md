## 3. The Map of Closed Lines

The lamp in Dominic's shed burned low, the way he liked it—low enough that the shadows could keep their secrets, bright enough to work by. He had trimmed the wick twice already and told himself a third time was thrift, not nerves. The oil was cheap tallow-smelling stuff from the chandlery, and it gave off a light the color of old bone.

The map case lay open on his workbench between a disassembled injector valve and a tin of rivets. It was a soldier's case, oilcloth over brass, the kind that cost more than a prospector should have been able to afford, and Dominic had spent the better part of an hour deciding whether opening it made him a courier or a corpse. In the end curiosity had won, as it always did, and curiosity was the sin that had put him in Karawari Landing in the first place.

Elias Wren's blood had dried on the canvas strap to a color like rusted rail. Dominic had scrubbed at it with river water and lye until his knuckles were raw, and still it clung, a brown shadow in the weave, so that every time he lifted the case he felt the weight of the dying man's hand on his wrist. *Port Vessel. Before the snows. You swear it.* He had sworn. He had not promised himself he would look inside first.

He looked inside.

The map unrolled across the bench with a sound like a long exhale, forty inches of waxed linen covered in ink fine as spider silk. It was a surveyor's map—not a prospector's scratch chart of creeks and color-shows but a proper engineering drawing, contour lines laid down with a rule, elevations noted to the foot, and running through it all, bold and confident, the track beds of lines that no longer existed. The Sepik Basin range spread across it like a sleeping animal: the Broken Narrows, the Anji foothills, the high country where the passes went white eight months of the year. And stitched through the mountains, drawn in a hand that had once been his own—

Dominic set down the lamp and stood very still.

He knew those marks. Not the way a man knows a face he has seen in passing; he knew them the way a man knows his own signature. The double-tick at station 440 meant hard conglomerate, blasting powder only, no drilling. The small circle with a cross-hatch meant water table within thirty feet. The little triangle with its apex clipped meant *unstable—do not cut*. He had invented half of those shorthand marks himself, eleven years ago, working nights in the syndicate's drafting office in Port Vessel with cold coffee going stale beside him and the belief—God help him, the clean, stupid belief—that he was drawing the spine of a country into being.

His name had been on the title block then. *D. A. Montrose, Assistant Engineer, Surveys.* His name was on nothing now but a false ledger in a false town under a false name, and yet here were his marks, marching up the linen like ants that had followed him out of his old life and refused to die.

But they were wrong.

That took him longer to see than it should have, because the eye forgives what the hand remembers. The contours were right. The elevations were right. But the annotations—the load-bearing facts, the numbers a construction crew would trust with their lives—had been altered. At Tunnel Nine the rock classification read *stable schist, drill-and-blast*, when Dominic knew—I know, he corrected himself, I knew then, I drew it myself—that the seam there ran through fractured serpentine, wet and treacherous, ground that wanted to close around a tunnel like a fist. Someone had taken his careful shorthand and rewritten it, in ink that matched, in a hand that almost matched. Almost. The altered figures sat a hair too tall, the way a man stands when he is wearing another man's coat.

He bent closer. The lamp flame leaned with him, and the shadows on the wall leaned too, and for a moment he had the irrational sense that the shed was full of people watching him read.

There were other changes. A spur line near the Broken Narrows had been redrawn to pass three hundred yards east of where the original survey—*his* original survey, done on foot, done in rain, done with a theodolite and a mule named Caesar who had hated him—had placed it. A creek crossing had been moved. And scattered along the closed grades, in Elias Wren's tighter, older hand, were additions: dates, initials, small crosses in red ink that had faded to the color of dried berries. Thirty-one crosses. Dominic counted them twice, because the first time he thought he must be miscounting, and the second time because counting them again changed nothing and he needed a moment where his hands had something honest to do.

Thirty-one crosses, clustered along the grades like cairns without stones. And beside each one, in Wren's hand, a name.

Not coordinates. Names. *A. Ferreira. T. Okonkwo. J. Lindqvist. M. Anahera.* Men's names, written small and upright, the letters of a man who had learned to write in a hurry and never unlearned it. Some had dates after them, all within the same eighteen months, all years ago now, back when the syndicate's rails had been new and its prospectuses fat with promise. Back when Dominic Montrose had been somebody whose numbers other men trusted.

He sat down on his stool, which complained under him the way everything in Karawari Landing complained—wood, tin, rope, men—and he rubbed his eyes until he saw sparks.

Here was what he understood, in the wordless way a man understands a load shifting in a freight car before the couplings scream: Elias Wren had not been a prospector. Prospectors did not carry engineer's maps. Prospectors did not annotate them with the patience of a man building a legal case one entry at a time. This was testimony. This was a ledger of the dead, kept in the only currency the dead had left, which was paper. And the syndicate—the syndicate that had gut-shot him in some muddy alley or lonely trail and let him crawl toward the river to die—had killed him for exactly this, for forty inches of waxed linen and thirty-one names in faded berry-red.

And the alterations—Dominic's finger hovered over the false rock classification at Tunnel Nine, not quite touching it, the way you hover over a stove burner you already know is hot—the alterations were old. The ink had aged evenly with the rest. Whoever had falsified his survey had done it before the map ever went into the field, before the crews ever climbed the grade with their drills and their black powder and their pay envelopes. Which meant the men who died in those passes had died believing numbers that were lies. Believing *his* numbers—or the thing wearing his numbers like a stolen coat.

Had he signed it? He tried to remember. Eleven years was a long time, and he had spent most of them trying not to remember anything at all. There had been a night in the drafting office—he could feel the memory the way your tongue finds a broken tooth, compulsively, painfully—when the chief surveyor, a soft-voiced man named Aldous Crane, had slid a finished sheet across the desk and said, *Just the formality, Montrose, the field teams confirmed everything,* and Dominic had signed because the sheet bore his own mark system and his own contours and it seemed impossible that it could be wrong, that anything built on his bones could be wrong. He had signed. He had signed with the same flourish he used now, when he signed himself *D. Corben* on the chandlery's books, except then the name under the flourish had been true.

Afterward there had been the cave-in at Nine. Afterward there had been the quiet, the terrible administrative quiet, the way the syndicate's reports had spoken of *consolidation delays* and *geological reassessment* and never once, not in any document he ever saw, of men. The line past Tunnel Nine had been closed. Then the next line. Then the whole basin, folded up like a fan, and the syndicate had turned its attention to rivers and shipping and the slow patient buying of towns, and Dominic had drunk for a year and then stopped drinking and then stopped being Dominic Montrose altogether, and he had told himself for eleven years that what happened in the passes had been geology. Ground doing what ground does. Nobody's fault, or everybody's, which is the same as nobody's.

The lamp fluttered. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, or at something, and Dominic realized his heart was going hard enough that he could hear it in his ears.

Because here was the arithmetic, plain as a freight manifest: if the surveys had been falsified before the fact—if the rock classifications had been rewritten in a hand that almost matched his—then the deaths had not been geology. They had been schedule. They had been a decision, made in an office, by men whose names appeared on no cross anywhere, that thirty-one lives were cheaper than six months' delay. And the proof of the decision was lying on his workbench under a tallow lamp in a town that had forgotten its own name, and the daughter of the man who had gathered that proof was waiting in Port Vessel, and the first snows were maybe five weeks out, and the syndicate had already sent a clerk to ask questions about old surveys.

They knew the map existed. That was why Wren was dead. That was why the clerk's questions had felt like fingers testing a lock.

Dominic stood and walked to the window and, out of eleven years of habit, checked the shutter seams. All tight. Through the gap he'd never quite managed to close—for a man who keeps every door unlocked keeps at least one window cracked, as a penance or a discipline, he'd never decided which—the town lay gray under the last light, mud streets and tin roofs and the river running brown and patient toward the coast. Somewhere downstream was Port Vessel. Somewhere beyond it, the syndicate's offices with their brass plaques and their ledgers full of consolidation delays. Somewhere in the ground beneath the closed grades, thirty-one men lay under rock that had been certified stable by a lie.

He went back to the bench and looked at the map a long time.

He could burn it. The thought arrived without shame and he examined it without shame, the way you examine any tool: the stove was right there, the linen would take flame like a wick, and by morning there would be nothing in the world but ash and his own silence, and he could go on being D. Corben until the river or the years finished the job. No daughter hunting killers. No syndicate gunboats. No names in berry-red ink demanding things of a man who had spent a decade learning how not to be demanded of.

But the names. He looked at the names. *T. Okonkwo.* He had known an Okonkwo once—a driller, big quiet man, sang hymns off-key on the morning climb. Had it been this Okonkwo? Was that even possible? The basin had swallowed hundreds of men and spat out none of their stories, and Dominic had spent eleven years telling himself the swallowing had been nobody's doing, and here was a dead prospector's hand saying: *somebody's. Here are the somebodies. Here are their names.*

You cannot burn a list of names. Or rather—you can, easily, in seconds—but you cannot do it and remain the kind of man who is bothered by it, and Dominic had discovered, in his long exile, that the one thing he had refused to surrender along with his name and his trade and his future was the particular discomfort of being bothered. It was a thin thread to hang a life on. It was, tonight, the only one he had.

He rolled the map with hands that were steadier than he expected, along the original creases, the way Wren had clearly rolled it a hundred times—there were wear-shadows in the linen, dark bands where the paper had been opened and opened again, in tents, in assay offices, in whatever lamplit rooms a man haunts for years while he builds a case against a company. The man had carried this weight up and down the basin for years. Now it was Dominic's.

He slid it into the oilcloth case, buckled the straps, and set it on the shelf behind the loose brick where he kept the money he pretended not to have. Then he blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the river, and did what engineers do, which is run the numbers.

Five weeks to the snows. Eleven days to Port Vessel by river, if the weather held and the syndicate's boats didn't ask questions. Fewer, if he went overland from the Anji foothills, but overland meant the passes, and the passes meant the closed grades, and the closed grades meant walking the length of his own confession with a dead man's map in his pack.

Or—his mind supplied it unasked, the traitor—*or you could take the map apart yourself. Copy the names, copy the alterations, send the copies out through three different hands so no single courier carries anything worth killing for.* It was what he would have designed, once, as a routing problem. It was also, he admitted, a way of keeping his hands on it, and he was honest enough, in the dark, to know the difference between a plan and a postponement.

The dog outside had stopped barking. In its place: footsteps on the boardwalk, unhurried, the cadence of a man strolling who wants to seem to be strolling. They passed the shed. They paused—two seconds, three, a pause just long enough to be deniable—and moved on.

Dominic did not move. He sat in the dark with his heartbeat loud and his breath shallow and counted to two hundred before he let himself believe the street was empty. Then he rose, took the map case from behind the brick, and did not put it back. He wrapped it in his spare shirt, bound it with cord, and tucked it into the bottom of his rucksack under the coil of rope, because a thing that someone has already killed for should not sleep in a wall.

Tomorrow he would go down to the landing and find out what passage cost. Tomorrow he would start becoming, briefly and unwillingly, Dominic Montrose again—a name he said aloud once, quietly, just to hear how it fit, and it fit like boots kept too long in a box: stiff, familiar, and smelling faintly of the grave they'd been stored in.

He banked the stove. He checked the shutter one last time.

And in the dark behind his eyes, all the way into a sleep that came late and thin, the map kept unrolling: the contours true, the numbers false, thirty-one small crosses fading toward brown, and beneath them, patient as water, the past refusing—line by line, name by name—to stay buried.
