## 4. Passage on the Kestrel

The Kestrel sat low in the brown water like a woman who had stopped apologizing for her weight long ago. She was a stern-wheeler of no particular beauty—hull patched with tin where the timber had rotted, a paddle wheel that slapped the river in an arrhythmic stutter, smokestack trailing a rag of black wool-smell into the morning mist. Her name was painted on the bow twice, once over an older name that someone had tried and failed to scrape away.

Dominic stood on the Karawari Landing boards with the map case strapped flat against his ribs under his coat, feeling it there the way a man feels a cracked tooth—with his tongue, constantly, involuntarily.

Elias Wren had been dead two days. They'd buried him behind the mission at dawn, in ground so wet the gravedigger had bailed water out of the hole with a tin pan before lowering the box. Four mourners: the priest, the gravedigger, a boy who sold rope, and Dominic. The priest had asked Elias's name for the register, and Dominic had given it honestly, because a man who dies asking you to keep a promise deserves at least that much truth.

Now he needed a boat.

* * *

He found Odile Ferrant on the Kestrel's aft deck, feet up on a coil of hemp line, cleaning a rifle that did not need cleaning. She was small and wiry, sun-browned to the color of old varnish, with grey shot through her black hair at the temples and eyes the flat green of river glass. She looked up when his shadow crossed her, and she did not put the rifle down.

"You're the third man this week to stand there looking at my boat like it owes him something," she said. "First two wanted passage downstream. What do you want?"

"Passage downstream."

"To where?"

"Port Vessel."

She snorted. "Everyone wants Port Vessel. Nobody wants the river between here and there." She worked the bolt, sighted down the barrel at nothing, lowered it. "You carrying cargo?"

"Myself."

"Nobody carries just themselves." She set the rifle across her knees and studied him with the unhurried attention of someone appraising livestock. "You've got river mud on your boots from upstream landing, not the shore here. You walk like a man used to standing on moving decks—railroad bridges, maybe, or engine cabs. Your coat's too good for this town and too worn to have come from money recently. And you keep touching your left side like you've got a bad rib or something you don't want found." Her mouth curved, not kindly. "So. Cargo."

Dominic held her gaze. He had spent four years learning how to be invisible, how to be a man whose face slid off memory like rain off oilcloth, and none of it was working on this woman. It was, he realized with something close to relief, almost restful to be seen.

"I need to reach Port Vessel before the passes snow shut," he said. "I can pay. I'm not going to tell you what I'm carrying, and I'm not going to ask what else you haul."

"Now that," Odile said, "is the first honest sentence anyone's offered me this month." She swung her feet off the line and stood, and she was shorter than he'd guessed, coming up only to his shoulder, but she stood like the boat belonged to the river and she belonged to both. "Standard fare to Vessel is forty. For you it's eighty."

"That's double."

"It is. Twenty extra for silence—I don't ask questions, you don't answer them. Twenty extra because whoever you're running from is going to make my life difficult, and difficult costs." She spat over the rail, considering the water. "And twenty because I like round numbers and I don't like you yet."

"That's three twenties."

"Math's free. Everything else isn't." She put out a hand, palm up. "Cash now. We cast off at slack water, midday. If you're not aboard, I don't wait, and I don't refund."

He counted the notes into her palm. She thumbed through them without counting visibly, folded them away somewhere inside her shirt, and turned back to her rifle as though he had already become furniture.

"One thing," she said, without looking up. "My boat, my rules. You eat when the crew eats, you sleep where I say, and if trouble comes aboard, you stand behind me until I tell you different. Not beside me. Behind me. I've buried passengers who thought they were helping."

"And if trouble comes and you decide I'm the trouble?"

She glanced up then, and for a moment the sharpness in her face went still, and he saw underneath it something older and wearier—the look of a woman who had made calculations like this before and had learned exactly what they cost.

"Then I'll put you ashore," she said quietly. "Alive. I don't drown men, whatever else they say about me. But I'll put you ashore wherever it's convenient for me, and inconvenient for you, and you'll wish I'd drowned you." She went back to the rifle. "Midday. Don't be late."

* * *

The Kestrel cast off at noon with a cargo deck stacked with kerosene drums, salted fish in barrels, and six passengers who paid honest fares: a rubber buyer, two missionaries, a family of traders with too many children and too little luggage. Dominic kept to the shadowed end of the cargo deck near the woodpile, where the boiler's heat came through the planks and the smell was tar and smoke and fish brine, and he watched the town of Karawari Landing fold itself backward into the mist and felt the peculiar loosening in his chest that leaving always brought—and the tightening that followed it, because leaving never meant escape. It only meant the pursuit would take longer to find him.

The map case rode against his ribs. He had taken it out twice in the night, by candle stub, and put it away both times without opening it. Some things you could only look at so often before they started looking back.

Odile ran her boat the way she talked—economically, precisely, with no wasted motion and no tolerance for waste in others. She stood at the wheel most of the day, one hand on the spokes, reading the water the way a priest reads scripture: every boil and eddy a sentence, every drifting log a warning she called down to her deckhand, a silent Papuan boy named Yusuf who moved cargo and caught lines and never spoke a word Dominic heard. By afternoon the river widened, braiding itself around islands the size of towns, and the jungle pressed down to the banks on both sides, dense and dripping, loud with birds that screamed like hinges.

It was near dusk, light going copper on the water, when Odile appeared beside him at the rail. She said nothing for a while. That was her way, he was learning. She spent silence like currency, and she never spent more than she had to.

"You touched that left side of yours eleven times today," she said finally. "I counted."

"Did you."

"I count everything. It's kept me alive." She nodded at the case's outline under his coat. "Papers?"

"Why do you care?"

"Because papers are heavier than gold." She leaned her forearms on the rail. "Gold, a man takes and runs and nobody chases far. Papers—papers change who owns things. Who's guilty. Who hangs. Men kill for papers the way they won't kill for anything else, and they don't stop at the man carrying them." She turned her head, and the last light caught the green of her eyes. "Whoever's after you. When they catch up—and they will—they won't just want you dead. They'll want everyone who helped you. Crew. Passengers. The woman who sold them fuel." She paused. "Me."

Dominic looked at her. "You knew that when you took my money."

"I know that about every passenger. Most of them aren't worth worrying over." She pushed off the rail. "Supper's at dark. Crew eats first. Don't be late for that either."

* * *

He was not late. Supper was rice and tinned mackerel eaten on deck in the blue half-light, the mosquitoes rising off the water in columns, and afterward the passengers bedded down under mosquito nets strung along the cabin roof, and the river went on talking to itself in the dark, endless and indifferent.

Dominic slept badly, as he always did, and woke before dawn to the sound of the paddle slowing.

Not stopping. Slowing—a deliberate easing, the wheel's slap softening from a drumbeat to a heartbeat. He lay still under his net and listened. Yusuf's bare feet padded past on the deck below. Somewhere forward, Odile's voice, low, giving an order too quiet to carry.

He slipped from his bunk and went to the rail.

The mist lay on the river in banks, and out of the downstream bank, perhaps half a mile back the way they'd come, came a shape that did not belong to any honest trade. It was long and low and black, with a superstructure squared off like a fist, and where a freighter would carry derricks it carried instead a mounting on the foredeck that even at this distance he recognized—the clean geometry of a machine gun under canvas. No running lights. No flag. A wake spreading behind it in a white V, patient as a signature being written.

A gunboat. Syndicate iron. He had seen hulls like it on the coast in the years before his fall, riding at anchor off company wharves, keeping the peace the company defined.

They were not overtaking. They were following—holding their distance, matching the Kestrel's speed, content for now simply to be present, the way a cat is present with a mouse it has decided not to eat yet.

"They came up from the Landing an hour after we cast off."

Odile's voice at his shoulder. He hadn't heard her climb; he was beginning to suspect she made no sound on principle. She stood with a mug of coffee in one hand and passed him a second without asking whether he wanted it. He took it. The tin was hot enough to hurt and he was grateful for the pain; it gave his hands something honest to do.

"You know her?" he asked.

"I know her type." Odile drank. "Company hull. Newer than anything honest money buys on this river. She'll do fourteen knots to my nine, burn twice my coal, and carry twelve men who get paid whether they shoot anybody or not." She said it evenly, the way she'd recited his fare. "Question is what she wants."

"You think it's me."

"I think the river's full of people, and gunboats don't follow all of them." She looked at him sideways. "Eleven touches, Montrose. Or whatever your name actually is."

The use of the false name, spoken with that faint irony, landed harder than his real name would have. He stared downstream at the black shape in the mist. "If they wanted me dead, they'd have boarded us in the night, midriver, and dropped me over with the boiler clinker."

"So they don't want you dead. Yet." Odile turned her mug slowly in her hands. "They want something you have. And they're following us to see where we stop, who we meet, whether you hand it off—because a courier alone is a problem, but a courier with contacts is a conspiracy, and conspiracies you can arrest whole." She shrugged. "Or I'm wrong, and they're chasing smugglers, and by tomorrow they'll turn off at the Bamu junction and we'll laugh about it."

"Do you believe that?"

"No." She drained her coffee. "But I believe in letting a bad night stay a bad night until it proves otherwise. Worrying ahead of schedule is how pilots run aground—you start steering toward the rock you fear instead of the channel you're in." She set the empty mug on the rail cap. "We make Anji in four days if the water holds. Until then, you stay on my deck, you keep that left side of yours still, and you let me do the thinking about that boat astern. I've been hunted on this river before. I'm still here."

"By whom?"

Something crossed her face then—quick, submerged, gone again, like a fish turning under brown water. "Ask me that in daylight," she said, "and I might charge you for the answer." She walked away toward the wheelhouse, and the paddle beat resumed its full rhythm, and behind them, unhurried, the black shape in the mist matched it stroke for stroke.

* * *

The second day taught Dominic the shape of the thing.

The gunboat held its distance through the morning, a smudge of smoke on the southern horizon whenever the river bent southward, invisible when the channel ran north—as if its master understood exactly how much presence was required to frighten and how little was required to provoke. Twice, native canoes put out from bank villages to trade with passing boats, and both times the canoes saw the black hull and turned hard for home. Word would travel ahead of them now, village to village, along the telegraph lines that had no wires: the iron boat is on the river, the iron boat is hunting.

At midday Odile shortened the watch rotations and moved the kerosene drums aft, casually, the way a woman rearranges furniture—but Dominic watched Yusuf lash them into a barricade around the wheelhouse, and understood.

In the afternoon she summoned him to the wheelhouse. It was hot as an oven in there, brass fittings blinding in the sun, the river unrolling ahead golden and slow. She steered with two fingers and did not look at him.

"There's a slough above Broken Narrows," she said. "Narrow, shallow, choked with snags—no draft for a hull that heavy. If I take the Kestrel into it, I might lose her, or I might lose twenty hours dragging her off a bar. Either way, that gunboat can't follow. Too deep-keeled."

"But?"

"But if I take the slough, I'm choosing sides. Openly. In front of witnesses, in front of that boat, in front of every trading post from here to the delta." She adjusted the wheel a degree, correcting for a current he couldn't see. "Right now I'm just a freighter minding her business with an unlucky passenger. There's still a version of tomorrow where they board us, search us, find nothing, and let us go, and I go on hauling cargo for another ten years. Take the slough, and that version dies."

Dominic stood in the heat and thought about Elias Wren dying on the floor of a shed with a stranger's hands pressing his wound, spending his last hour not on prayer or rage but on a promise extracted from a man he'd never met—because there had been no one else, and the map mattered more than the man carrying it.

"You could put me ashore," Dominic said. "Convenient for you. Inconvenient for me. You told me the terms."

"I know what I told you." Odile's jaw tightened. "I also told you I don't drown men. Putting you ashore at some landing with that thing on the river is drowning you slow, and I'd know it, and I'd have to live knowing it." She exhaled through her nose. "I hate that I'd know it. It would be easier to be the kind of woman who wouldn't."

"Then why offer?"

"Because I wanted to hear which man you were." She looked at him straight on then, pilot's eyes, reading depths. "The one who'd say take the slough and save yourself, or the one who'd say put me off and save yourself. Both answers cost you. I wanted to see which cost you'd choose."

"And if I said neither? If I said it's my problem, and you should keep your boat out of it?"

"Then I'd have thrown you overboard myself." The ghost of a smile. "No. I'd have put you ashore, and hated myself less." She faced forward again. "Well? Which is it?"

Dominic looked downstream, though the gunboat was miles back and hidden. He thought of Maren Wren, a name on a letter he'd never read, waiting in a city he'd never seen, for a father who was already rotting behind a mission in the mud. He thought of thirty-one names he didn't know yet, sleeping under a mountain. He thought of four years of unlocked doors and shuttered windows, of invisibility, of the exhausting arithmetic of a life subtracted down to nothing.

"The slough," he said. "But not for me. Put in at Broken Narrows first and drop the missionaries and the traders—let the innocent pay their own way from there. Then take the slough. If we lose the boat, I'll buy you a new one. If we lose our lives, I expect you to haunt me efficiently."

Odile laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her, the first unguarded sound he'd heard her make. "Efficiently," she repeated. "All right, Montrose. Or whatever your name is." She rang the engine bell twice, and below them the paddle slowed, and the Kestrel swung her blunt bow toward the narrowing channel and the dark wall of the Narrows, where the water ran fast and white over stone.

Behind them, far back in the haze, the black smoke of the gunboat thickened—coming on now, done with patience, done with shadows.

Odile watched it a moment, and her hand on the wheel was steady as bedrock.

"Hold on to something," she said.
