## 5. The Boy on the Wire

Teo had learned to read a wire the way other boys read faces.

The telegraph office at Karawari Landing smelled of lamp oil, wet rope, and the sour sweat of men who'd been drinking since the river boats came in. Teo sat behind the partition with his earpiece clamped tight and his pencil moving, taking down freight manifests for the syndicate office because Mr. Calloway was drunk again and the line didn't care who held the key. The brass sounder chattered its dry insect language, and Teo translated it into words without thinking, the way he breathed without thinking.

*FREIGHT 44 CASKS NAILS STOP 12 BOLTS CLOTH STOP*

Boring. Safe. He'd been safe for eleven days, which was some kind of record since he'd run from his uncle's house in Anji with nothing but a copy of the codebook sewn into his shirt lining. Eleven days of pretending he belonged here, of sleeping in the relay shed where the spare batteries were kept warm, of eating crackers and tinned milk when Calloway wasn't counting.

Then the wire changed its voice.

He felt it before he understood it—a different rhythm in the traffic, a formality, the clipped precision of messages that came from high up and expected to be answered fast. The priority chime. Teo's hand stilled over the manifest.

*PRIORITY PRIORITY SYNDICATE CENTRAL TO ALL STATIONS SEPTEMBER BASIN DISTRICT STOP*

His pencil hovered. This wasn't freight. This wasn't weather reports or pay schedules or the endless gossip of mine superintendents complaining about their mules. This had the weight of something that would get a man's name written down if he failed to act on it.

*RE COURIER EX WREN STOP SUBJECT TRAVELING DOWNRIVER STOP INTERCEPT THE COURIER STOP RECOVER MAP CASE INTACT STOP DISPOSITION OF COURIER AT OPERATIVES DISCRETION STOP*

*Disposition at operatives' discretion.*

Teo knew what discretion meant. Everyone in the basin knew what it meant when the syndicate used a soft word. It meant the river gave up bodies every spring that nobody claimed, bloated and face-down among the logs, and the magistrate wrote *accidental drowning* in his ledger and went back to his lunch.

*ACKNOWLEDGE STOP HUNT PARTY ASSEMBLED STOP HARROW COMMANDING STOP FERRANT VESSEL KESTREL BELIEVED ENGAGED STOP CONFIRM VISUAL WHEN SUBJECT BOARDS STOP*

Harrow. Even the name came through the wire cold. Teo had heard dockhands talk about Harrow the way they talked about weather—something that came through a place and left it worse. And Ferrant—Odile Ferrant ran the Kestrel out of Karawari Landing; everyone knew her boat, the flat-bottomed steamer with the patched paddle wheel that smelled of engine grease and dried fish. Which meant the courier—the man with the map case, the man whose name Teo didn't know yet—was going to board the Kestrel.

Which meant the Kestrel was going to be followed.

Teo sat very still. The lamp above the desk buzzed. Outside, rain worked at the tin roof in long silver sheets, and somewhere down the landing a man laughed too loud at nothing.

He should write it down and deliver it to Calloway like any other message. That was the job. That was how you stayed small and invisible and alive: you did your work, you asked nothing, you noticed nothing. His uncle had taught him that much before the beatings started—*a boy who sees nothing is a boy who eats*.

But his hands wouldn't move. Because he had seen something now, and the seeing couldn't be undone. If he delivered this message, he was one of them. He was the wire. He was the thing that let Harrow find the courier, and whatever happened to the courier would happen partly because a fourteen-year-old boy with ink-stained fingers had passed the word along clean and quick.

The sounder ticked. Rain ran down the black windows. Teo looked at the message flimsy in his hand—at *INTERCEPT THE COURIER*, at *DISPOSITION AT OPERATIVES' DISCRETION*—and felt the whole shape of his life tilt, slow as a boat taking water.

Eleven days ago he'd run because staying meant becoming his uncle. Now staying meant becoming the syndicate. There wasn't anywhere left to stay.

So there was only one direction, and it was downriver, and it was aboard the Kestrel.

* * *

Getting aboard was easier than it should have been, which told Teo something about how much attention Odile Ferrant paid to passengers she hadn't sold tickets to. He waited until the last hour of dark, when the rain thinned to mist and the deckhands were asleep in the fo'c'sle smelling of rum. He came up the stern line hand over hand, the way he'd climbed the telegraph poles outside Anji since he was nine, and folded himself into the space behind the coal bunker where the heat of the boiler came through the iron plates like a living animal's flank.

He'd brought things. He'd thought about it all day while pretending to take down manifests: a canvas satchel with two tins of condensed milk, a paper of hardtack, his father's folding knife with the bone handle worn smooth, a coil of copper wire—he always took wire, it was the one material in the world he trusted—and the message flimsy itself, folded into a square no bigger than a stamp and tucked into his boot, against his ankle, where it lay all night like a coal that wouldn't cool.

The Kestrel got underway at first light. Teo felt it in the deck's shiver, heard the paddle wheel bite the brown water, heard the landing slide away astern—the town that had never known his name, giving him up as easily as it had taken him in. He pressed his eye to the gap between bunker plates and watched the river unroll: mist standing in columns off the water, egrets lifting white as scraps of paper, the far bank a green wall dripping.

By midmorning the hiding stopped being a plan and became an endurance. The bunker space was hot enough to cook bread. Sweat ran into his eyes. Once a deckhand came aft to spit over the rail and stood so close Teo could count the stitches on his boots, and Teo held his breath until his vision sparked, until the man ambled away scratching himself. Twice he heard voices from the pilothouse—one low and gravelly, a man's; one sharp and dry as a whip-crack, unmistakably the pilot's—and once, worst of all, he heard the low cough of a gunboat's engine far upstream, a sound like a big animal clearing its throat, and the sharp voice said something he couldn't catch, and the Kestrel's engine note deepened as if the boat itself had flinched.

Toward evening he could bear it no longer. Hunger and heat had hollowed him out, and the flimsy against his ankle burned worse than the bunker plates. He crawled out stiff-limbed, meaning only to stretch, meaning only to breathe air that wasn't boiled iron and coal dust—

—and found himself looking up the barrel of a revolver held by a tall man with gray in his beard and exhaustion carved around his eyes.

They stared at each other across three feet of deck. Behind the man, at the wheel, the pilot woman turned, and her expression went through surprise and arrived somewhere colder.

"Well," she said. "We have a rat."

"I'm not—" Teo's voice cracked. He hated that it cracked. "I'm not a rat. I have to talk to you. Both of you. Please."

"Please," Odile Ferrant repeated, tasting it. She came down from the wheel with the easy roll of someone who'd spent her life on decks. Up close she was smaller than her voice, all sinew and salt, eyes the color of river water over slate. "Stowaways say please. Rats say please. Then they say other things." She held out her hand, palm up. "Satchel."

He gave it up. She went through it with brutal efficiency—tins, hardtack, knife, wire coil—and paused at the wire. "Telegraph boy."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Runaway?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"That's two kinds of trouble in one body." But something in her face shifted, a fraction, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to hold or break. The man with the revolver hadn't lowered it. "And the third kind?" Odile said. "Because nobody risks the river for milk and crackers. What are you carrying?"

Teo's heart slammed. This was the moment. He'd rehearsed it in the bunker until the words wore smooth, but rehearsal meant nothing now, with the wheel creaking and the current pulling and the gunboat's ghost-sound still lodged in his ears.

He reached down and drew the flimsy from his boot.

The tall man took it. Teo watched him read it—watched the reading change him, the way weather changes a field. His jaw tightened. The hand holding the paper wasn't quite steady, and Teo understood, with the strange clarity fear gives, that this was the courier. That the words on the flimsy were about him. *Intercept the courier.* This tired, gray-bearded man was worth killing, and everyone on this boat was now worth killing for standing near him.

"Harrow," the man said quietly.

"You know him?" Odile asked.

"I know the name." He looked at Teo, and his eyes were not unkind, which undid Teo more than cruelty would have. "You copied this yourself?"

"At the Karawari office. Priority traffic, syndicate central. There was more—I memorized it before I burned my draft copy." He recited it, station by station, name by name, the way he could recite anything the wire had ever spoken: the hunt party assembled, the stations ordered to confirm visual, the instruction that recovery of the map case mattered more than the courier's health. When he finished, the deck was silent except for the wheel and the water.

"The names," the man said. "Give me the names again. Slowly."

Teo gave them. Harrow, commanding. Voss, second. Dekker, Brandt, Okafor—men he'd never seen but already knew the way you know a storm by its wind. As he spoke them, the courier wrote them down inside his coat, and Odile Ferrant stood very still, and when Teo reached the name *Okafor* something crossed her face like a shadow of a bird.

"You burned your draft," the courier said. "Why?"

"Because I read it." Teo's voice wanted to crack again and he made it behave. "If I delivered it, I'd be helping. I've been helping people I hate my whole life. My uncle. The syndicate pays his store. I wasn't going to do it anymore. So I ran, and then the wire handed me this, and I thought—if I'm running anyway, I might as well run toward something instead of just away."

Odile snorted. "Fourteen years old and already writing speeches."

"It's true," Teo said, stung.

"I didn't say it wasn't true. I said it was a speech." But she was looking at him differently now—not at a rat, not quite at a person she trusted, but at a problem she hadn't solved yet, which from Odile Ferrant was nearly a welcome.

The courier folded the flimsy and put it away. "What's your name?"

"Teo."

"Just Teo?"

"For now."

Something moved at the corner of the man's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might grow. "Dominic Montrose," he said, and then, as if hearing himself, added, "or whatever name I'm using this week." He glanced at Odile. "There's a gunboat behind us. There's a boy in front of us. I'm starting to think the river has opinions."

"The river doesn't have opinions," Odile said. "People do. People with gunboats." She jerked her chin at Teo. "He stays in the open from now on. No more bunkers. If we're sunk for harboring him, I want to see it coming."

"Yes, ma'am," Teo said.

"And you work for your passage. You can splice line, I suppose, being a pole-climber."

"I can splice anything."

"We'll see." She turned back to the wheel, then paused. "There's hardtack and milk in your satchel. Eat. You look like a heron somebody taught to walk upright."

It was not kindness, exactly. But it was the closest thing to kindness Teo had been offered since he'd jumped the freight out of Anji, and he found, to his own surprise, that his eyes stung. He turned away fast and busied himself with the satchel, and neither of them remarked on it, and that—that silence, that deliberate not-seeing—felt like the most generous thing anyone had done for him in years.

* * *

Night came down on the river like a lid.

They tied up at a wooding station, a shelf of cut billets stacked under palm thatch, a single lamp burning yellow in the keeper's hut. Dominic took the middle watch. Teo couldn't sleep. The bunk in the cabin was too soft after eleven nights on shed floors, and the river talked to itself all night—water worrying the pilings, frogs in the reeds, the far-off complaint of some animal with a voice like a saw.

He found Dominic on the aft deck, sitting with his back against the rail, smoking a pipe he never seemed to light. The moon was up, half-eaten, laying a broken road of silver down the current. Mosquitoes hung in the lamplight from the wooding station, a fine gray smoke of them.

"Can't sleep either?" Dominic said, without turning.

"No, sir."

"Don't 'sir' me. I repair locomotives under a false name. Sirs have real names." He shifted, making room on the deck beside him. Teo sat. The planks were warm from the day. Below them the river slid past, huge and patient, carrying everything toward the sea without caring what it carried.

"How old are you really?" Dominic asked.

"Fourteen. Fifteen in the wet season."

"Fifteen." Dominic drew on the unlit pipe, an old habit's pantomime. "When I was fifteen I already had a drafting table and a supervisor telling me I'd go far. Going far seemed like the whole point, back then." He was quiet a moment. "You memorized that message. All of it. Stations, names, times. Do you do that with everything?"

"The wire talks all day. Most of it's nothing. But the important stuff—it sticks. Like it's heavier. Like it sinks down into you and stays." Teo hugged his knees. "Is that why you're doing this? Because the map stuck to you?"

Dominic was silent so long that Teo thought he wouldn't answer. Out on the water, something rose and sank—a dolphin, maybe, or a log wearing a dolphin's manners.

"A dying man gave me a promise to keep," Dominic said finally. "That's most of it. The rest is—" He stopped. Started again. "The rest is that I helped build some of what's on that map. Not the bad parts. I didn't know they were bad parts. But my marks are on those surveys, and when you put your mark on a thing, the thing becomes yours, even the parts you didn't mean." He tapped the bowl of the dead pipe against the rail. "You're young. Maybe you can still learn this faster than I did: the past doesn't drown. It just floats downstream slower than you do, and it catches up."

Teo thought about his uncle's store, the syndicate crates stacked in back, the ledger with his uncle's careful handwriting recording what the syndicate paid for silence about the condition of the mine cages. He'd found that ledger the night he ran. He'd read every page by candlelight, and then he'd left it, because leaving it felt like the only weapon he had that couldn't be traced.

"My uncle keeps books for them," Teo said. "Not money books. Silence books. I stole a look once." He swallowed. "I know what discretion means too."

Dominic looked at him then, fully, and in the half-light his exhausted face did something complicated—grief and recognition tangled together. "Then you understand what's chasing us."

"I understand better than you think."

"Maybe you do." Dominic put the pipe away. "Listen, Teo. Tomorrow, maybe the day after, Harrow's boat will find us. Odile will try to lose it—she's good, maybe good enough. But if it comes to bad, you get below. You don't argue, you don't watch, you get below and you stay there. A promise given to a stranger is heavy ballast, and I'm carrying enough. Don't add yours by getting hurt for me."

"But—"

"That's not a request. It's the price of riding this boat."

Teo looked out at the moon-road on the water, at the black walls of the banks sliding by, at the lamp in the wooding station where the keeper had gone to bed trusting the river to leave him alone. Nobody had ever set terms for him before. Terms meant you mattered enough to make rules about.

"All right," he said. "But if they board us, I can tell them things. I know how syndicate men talk. I know their signals. The wire uses little codes—double dots between words means *verified*, three means *urgent*, and there's a whistle signal Harrow's boats use when they come alongside, two short and one long. I could—"

"Teo." Dominic's voice was gentle and immovable as ballast. "Below. In the bad. Promise me."

Teo promised. It sat in his chest like a stone with a warm side, and he understood that this was what it felt like to be someone's responsibility—uncomfortable, heavy, and strangely sweet.

They sat together while the moon went down. At some point Odile appeared at the cabin door with three cups of something hot and bitter—chicory, stretched with river water—and handed them around without a word, and the three of them drank in silence, watching the dark, and Teo thought: *this is what family is, maybe. Not blood. Just people drinking bad coffee at midnight because the same danger is coming for all of you.*

He didn't say it aloud. Fourteen-year-olds who say things like that aloud get laughed back to the bunker. But he kept it, folded small, next to the memory of the flimsy against his ankle.

* * *

The gunboat found them two mornings later, at the mouth of the Sepik proper, where the river widened and the current slowed and there was nowhere left to hide.

Teo heard it before anyone: that throat-clearing engine note, deeper than before, closer. He was splicing line amidships—badly, Odile said, but less badly than yesterday—and his hands went still on the strands.

"Smoke," he said. "Aft. Low on the water."

Odile was up the wheelhouse ladder before he finished the sentence. Through her glass she read the truth: a syndicate gunboat, gray-painted, shallow-draft, a pivot gun forward under a tarpaulin that fooled no one. Running lights burning in broad daylight, which meant it wanted to be seen. Which meant it wasn't hunting yet. It was herding.

"They'll signal us to heave to," Odile said, coming down. Her voice was flat, but Teo had learned in two days to hear the currents under her flatness. "Inspection. Contraband search. They'll have papers for a man matching Dominic's description."

"Then we don't give them a man matching it," Dominic said.

They worked fast after that. Odile ran the Kestrel into a side channel choked with nipa palm, where the canopy closed overhead like a green roof and the air went thick and breathing. Dominic stripped off his coat and hat, rubbed river mud into his hair and beard until the gray vanished, and became a deckhand—a bent-backed, slack-jawed deckhand hauling billets, of which the Kestrel carried plenty. Teo took the wheel under Odile's eye, because a boy steering was a boy belonging to the boat, visible and innocent, and because—as Odile put it with grim humor—"if they shoot the pilot, someone should know which end of the boat is forward."

The gunboat's whistle sounded twice, then once. Two short and one long.

Teo's blood went cold. He knew that signal. He'd told them about that signal. Hearing it come across the water, real, aimed at them, made the wire's abstract language suddenly flesh and teeth.

"They're asking to come alongside," he whispered.

"I know," Odile said. "I invented half those signals, years ago, for cargo I don't talk about." Something crossed her face—guilt, old and well-worn as her wheel—and was gone. "Everyone stay in character. Boy steers. Deckhand hauls. Pilot is irritated at being delayed, because pilots are always irritated, and irritation is the best disguise in the world because nobody looks twice at an angry man."

The gunboat slid out of the sun-mist astern, gray and hungry, and Teo's hands sweated on the wheel spokes. Figures moved along its rail. On the bridge, a tall silhouette raised a speaking trumpet, and the voice that came across the water was pleasant, unhurried, and wrong the way a snare is wrong—you heard the music and only afterward saw the wire.

"*Kestrel, ahoy. Heave to for inspection.*"

Odile cupped her hands. "*Inspection of what? Firewood and dried fish! You want to count my fish?*"

"*Counting fish is government work,*" the trumpet said, and there was laughter along the gunboat's rail, the easy laughter of men who had never once been afraid of a river.

Teo steered. The channel narrowed. The nipa palms leaned close enough to touch. And in the last hundred yards, as the gunboat's wake began to reach them and the trumpet drew breath to call again, Odile Ferrant did the thing that Teo would remember for the rest of his life: she smiled—thin, private, dangerous—and put the wheel hard over, and the Kestrel dove into the green tunnel of the channel like a fox going to earth, while behind them the gray boat's anchor splashed down into water too shallow to hold it, and the shouting began.

The channel held. The Kestrel scraped, shuddered, held. The green roof closed behind them, and the gunboat's voice grew small, and then the engine note faded altogether, replaced by birdsong and the drip of leaves and Odile saying, to no one in particular:

"He'll cut loose by morning. Harrow doesn't chase. Harrow waits. Remember that, boy—men who wait are worse than men who chase."

Teo remembered. He steered the narrow water with his heart still hammering and his eyes stinging again, and this time he didn't turn away, because Odile was busy with the depth leads and Dominic was busy being a deckhand, and nobody saw a fourteen-year-old cry at a ship's wheel, and that was its own kind of mercy.

That night, anchored in a blind slough with the fire banked low, Dominic spread the map case open on a crate and studied it by shuttered lamplight, and his face did the field-changing-weather thing again, worse than before. Teo pretended to sleep in the cabin and listened to the two adults murmur—words like *survey marks*, *my own drafting*, *thirty-one*, and once, low and terrible, *they buried them*.

He didn't understand all of it. But he understood that the flimsy in his boot had been the first thread of something, and that threads, pulled, unravel whole ships. Somewhere ahead was a daughter named Maren, and somewhere behind was a man named Harrow who waited, and somewhere inside Teo was a boy who had spent his whole life passing other people's urgent words and had never once sent a message of his own.

Tomorrow, he decided, listening to the river and the murmuring adults and his own stubborn heart, tomorrow he would ask Dominic to teach him the Morse for *family*. He wasn't sure it existed as a word. He'd invent it if he had to.

He fell asleep to the sound of the paddle wheel ticking at its mooring, patient as a pulse, carrying them all—willing or not—toward the mountains and the snow.
