## 6. What Odile Knows

The Kestrel ran the night water like a woman who knew every lie the river told.

Odile Ferrant stood at the wheel with her boots bare against the deck planks, feeling the current through her soles the way other people felt it through their hands. The Sepik's middle reach was a black ribbon stitched with stars—reflected ones, false ones, the river's oldest trick—and she steered by the feel of the water against the hull rather than by any of that borrowed light. The channel here bent east around a drowned forest, and the drowned trees still stood in twelve feet of water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing up. She had run this reach forty times. She had run it once with cargo she still could not say out loud.

Behind her, the cabin lamp burned low. Montrose was in there with the boy, Teo, teaching him knots or card tricks or whatever a man did to keep a fourteen-year-old from thinking too hard about the gunboat that had been shadowing them since dusk.

Because there it was again. Odile tilted her chin and caught it between the dead masts: a running light, high-mounted, riding heavier than any trader would ride. Not following their wake exactly. Following *ahead* of it, the way a man follows a scent he already knows.

*The Vulture.* That was what they called her on the lower river, though her papers said something respectable. Captain Harrow's boat. Harrow's crew. Harrow's particular way of making problems on the water stop being problems.

Odile had known Harrow when he was only a name attached to manifests. That was before. Before the strike at Waukama. Before the smoke.

She turned the wheel two spokes and felt the Kestrel answer, sweet as a held breath. The old girl was thirty years of patched hull and honest engine, and Odile loved her the way you loved anything that had carried you away from yourself often enough.

"Passing the drowned forest," she called toward the cabin, because the boy liked being told things, and because saying it aloud kept her own mind on the water where it belonged. "Keep the lamp dimmed another hour."

Montrose appeared in the doorway, filling it, a big man made careful by practice. He'd been careful since Karawari Landing, careful since the shed, careful since Elias Wren died on his floor with a map case pressed into his hands. Careful men were easy to read if you'd spent your life reading them. This one read like a ledger with pages torn out.

"The light again?" he asked.

"Again."

"You know whose it is."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, because the answer had been sitting in her chest for two days like a swallowed hook. "I know whose it is."

He waited. That was the thing about Montrose—when he wanted truth out of a person, he didn't dig for it. He just stood there being a hole in the world shaped like patience, until you fell in.

Odile looked at the water. The drowned trees slid past, black and patient, their crowns combing the starlight.

"Not yet," she said. "When we clear the narrows. I'll tell you when we clear the narrows."

He nodded and went back inside, and she hated him a little for not pushing, because pushing would have let her stay angry, and anger was so much lighter than what she was carrying.

* * *

They cleared the narrows at moonrise and tied up in a cutbank willow stand, engines cold, lamp out, letting the river have its silence back. Montrose banked a small fire behind a screen of canvas—small enough to deny, warm enough to matter—and the three of them sat around it with tin cups of coffee gone bitter from boiling twice.

Teo was asleep sitting up, the way boys slept when they'd run too far on fear. His head tipped against a coil of rope, his telegrapher's fingers twitching in some dream of keys and clicks. Two days ago he'd come out of the cargo hold with a stolen message crumpled in his fist and terror all over his face, and instead of throwing him off at the next landing, they'd fed him. Odile still wasn't sure how that had happened. Things got aboard her boat and stayed aboard; that was the rule of rivers. Apparently it applied to people now too.

"He asked me today," Montrose said quietly, "what Harrow's boat does to boats it catches."

"What did you tell him?"

"That we wouldn't be caught."

"And he believed you?"

"He wanted to." Montrose turned his cup in his hands. "That's not the same, but it'll do for now."

Odile drank her coffee and watched the fire eat down through a knot of willow. Somewhere across the water a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then given up.

"I ran syndicate cargo," she said.

She hadn't planned to say it just then. But the words came up the way blood comes from a split lip—you taste it before you decide anything.

Montrose didn't move. Didn't blink. Just gave her the same patient hole in the world, and she fell in.

"Six years," she said. "Maybe seven. It started small. Machine parts, pay chests, sealed crates I wasn't supposed to ask about. The syndicate paid triple the going rate and never haggled, and a woman with a boat and debts doesn't ask why the crates are heavy or why they're loaded at night. You tell yourself it's ore samples. Survey equipment. You get very good at telling yourself things." She turned the cup. "Then one season the crates started coming back downriver lighter than they went up, and I stopped telling myself anything at all, because I knew what weighs less going down than going up, and it isn't machinery."

"How long?"

"Two runs. Maybe three." Her voice came out flat, which was the only way it could come out. "I never opened one. I want that understood—not because it makes me clean, but because I've had six years to wonder whether opening one would have changed what I did next, and I honestly don't know. That's the part nobody tells you about being bought. It isn't one decision. It's a hundred small ones, each one easier than the last, until one day you're steering past a burning village and your hands are steady on the wheel, and you realize your hands have been steady the whole time."

The fire popped. Teo stirred and didn't wake.

"Waukama," Montrose said.

So he'd heard. Of course he'd heard. A railroad engineer who'd worked the basin surveys would have heard the story the way everyone on the river had heard it—a strike reported, a village in the way, a village gone. The stories never used the word *burned*. They used *cleared*, which was a word with gloves on.

"I surveyed that strike," Odile said. "That's the part that's mine. Not the burning—I didn't burn anything, I wasn't even there when it burned—but the survey. My soundings, my charts, my mark on the map saying *here is where the copper runs*. I drew the line through Waukama, and the syndicate read my line, and they decided the village under it was a rounding error." She set the cup down because her hand had begun to shake and she refused to watch it shake. "Forty-one people. I've counted them more times than I've counted my own sins, and I've counted those plenty. Forty-one, and the company books called it a seasonal relocation. The magistrate's report called it an unverified incident. And I took the money for the survey, and I bought my boat free of debt, and I told myself the steadiness of my hands meant nothing."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because the man on that gunboat is the man who carried the torches." She looked at him straight, finally, and found it cost less than she'd feared. Guilt offered freely weighed half what guilt pried loose did—that was a thing her mother had said about confession, back when Odile had believed mothers were always right. "Harrow. He was syndicate security then, same as now. I saw him at the landings after Waukama, and he'd look at me like we shared something, and the worst of it is—we did. He knows what I am. If he takes this boat, he won't ransom me. He'll use me. One word from him about Waukama and I'm his again, and worse—he'll make me glad to be his, because being his is at least being something."

Montrose was quiet a long moment. The willows whispered over the cutbank. Downriver, faint as a thought, the Vulture's light moved against the stars.

"Elias Wren," Montrose said slowly, "was shot in my shed by men asking about a survey. Old lines. Closed lines." He looked up. "You said you helped survey strikes. Which ones?"

And there it was—the question she'd been dreading, arriving right on schedule, because honesty was a door and doors didn't stop halfway.

"All of them," she said. "Every strike the syndicate opened in the basin between the war years and the collapse, I sounded the ground for. I was good. That was the whole obscenity of it—I was the best pilot-surveyor on the water, and they knew it, and they kept me busy." She pulled the collar of her coat tighter against a cold that wasn't in the air. "If your map has syndicate marks on it, Montrose, there's a fair chance some of them are mine."

* * *

He brought the map case out into the firelight without being asked, which told her he'd already been thinking what she was thinking. He unrolled the waterproof sheet across a flat of driftwood, weighting the corners with stones, and the lamplight—borrowed, banked low—lay across the old ink like water across a grave.

Odile leaned over it and felt the years fall away with a lurch, the way the riverbed falls away under a hull that's run aground.

"I drew this bend." Her finger hovered above the great oxbow at the range's southern foot without quite touching the paper. "Sounding line, 1889. There was a sandbar then where the channel marker sits now. And this grade—" her finger moved north, into the closed lines, into the hatched country where the ink went strange, "—this is syndicate work. See the station marks? Even spacing, no regard for water or grade. That's a survey done from an office chair. Somebody sat in Port Vessel and drew a railroad where they wanted one to be, and sent men like me to make the ground agree."

"And did it?"

"Some of it." She traced the line up toward the pass, toward the tunnel symbol marked with the little annotation in Elias Wren's cramped hand—*Nine. Ask why.* "Some of the ground agreed. Some of it couldn't be made to, and that's when surveys start getting altered. That's when numbers stop meaning what they say." She looked up at him. "You'd know about that."

His face didn't change, but something behind it did—a shutter moving in a window he thought was dark. "What makes you say that?"

"Because you looked at these marks two nights ago like a man reading his own handwriting in a stranger's diary." She held his eyes. "I pilot a boat, Montrose. Reading people is half the trade. You don't have to tell me tonight. But understand what you're holding. That map isn't paper. It's a list. Everything the syndicate buried in those passes—every falsified number, every erased name—it's written down there in ink they think dried years ago. And Harrow's boat is on the river because somebody senior enough to be frightened knows it exists."

The fire had burned to a red eye of coals. Above the willows the moon was climbing, laying silver along the water, and far downriver the Vulture's light burned on, patient as a debt.

"There's something else," Odile said. "Something I should have said first, maybe, but it needed the rest of it under it or it wouldn't hold weight." She took a breath. "Harrow didn't burn Waukama on orders alone. I know because I carried the follow-up cargo out of that district—pay chests, and one crate of personal effects the company was 'returning to families.' Harrow rode with me on that run. Three days on my boat. He talked. Men like him talk to pilots the way priests talk to bartenders—like we're furniture that floats." Her mouth twisted. "He told me the strike reports were wrong. Deliberately. That the assays coming out of the basin were inflated to float the company's stock, and the rail grades were drawn to serve mines that didn't exist, and the whole upper basin was a costume on an empty stage. And the men who dug the tunnels and worked the seams—" she stopped, and made herself finish, "—the men who found out, the ones who kept real books, real counts—they didn't leave the basin. That's what he told me, laughing, drinking my coffee. *The basin keeps its own records,* he said."

Montrose had gone very still. In the coals' light his face looked carved, older than his years, and Odile watched him arrive somewhere she'd arrived long ago and never left: the understanding that the map in front of them was not a route but a reckoning.

"Thirty-one," he said quietly.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something Elias said. Or didn't say." He began rolling the map with hands that were almost steady. "Get some sleep. We run at first light, and we run wide of the main channel—there's an old logging cut above the second bend that Harrow won't know."

"He'll know it," Odile said. "He chartered boats on this river for six years. He knows every cut I know."

"Then we'll need to be faster than his knowing."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she looked downriver one more time, at that high-riding light threading the drowned forest's memory, and felt the old steadiness come into her hands—not the bought kind, not the guilty kind, but the kind that comes from finally knowing exactly what you're steering toward.

"One more thing," she said, as Montrose doused the coals. "Harrow's boat rides heavy. Deeper than a patrol boat should sit. I've been watching her draft since dusk." She met his eyes across the dying fire. "She's carrying men. A lot of them. Whatever he thinks we're carrying, he means to take it off us personally—and he means for none of us to be on the river to talk about it."

Above the cutbank, the willows went suddenly still, the way birds go still, the way water goes still, the way the whole world holds its breath a half-second before the truth comes ashore.
