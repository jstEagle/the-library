## 7. The Portage at Broken Narrows

The river told them before the rocks did.

Dominic heard it first as a change in the Kestrel's voice—a flattening of the water's murmur against the hull, a hush where there should have been chatter. He had spent eleven years listening to rivers talk to boats, back when his name was still his own and the only thing he'd ever wrecked was his own standing. He knew what a river sounded like when it was lying about its depth.

"Odile," he said quietly.

She was already at the wheel, one hand on the brass rim, her eyes narrowed at the water ahead. The Broken Narrows announced themselves the way liars do—smoothly, with a smile of glassy current over what the charts called *shoal rock, unmarked*. The gorge pinched down to half its width here, and the Sepik's tributary ran fast and shallow between walls of black stone furred with moss, the kind of water that looked deep because it moved like it meant it.

"I see it," Odile said. "I've run this stretch nine times."

"And?"

"Grounded twice." She said it without shame, the way another woman might report the weather. "Once going down with cargo, once coming up empty. It's the coming-up that kills you. The rocks don't move, but they rearrange themselves in your memory."

Teo appeared at Dominic's elbow, silent as he always was when the boat grew tense—the boy had a genius for knowing when to make himself small. His telegrapher's eyes flicked from Odile's face to the water and back, reading them both like tape.

"What can I carry?" Teo asked.

"Nothing yet," Dominic said. "Stay by the rail. If she says jump, you jump for the high bank and you don't look back."

"I don't look back," Teo repeated, in the flat way of a boy rehearsing instructions he intended to survive by.

The gorge swallowed them. Walls rose on either side, close enough that Dominic could have touched them with a boathook, dripping green, streaked white with bird lime, humming with the particular silence of places no road had ever found. The light came down in slats. Somewhere above, cockatoos argued about something eternal.

Then the river lied.

It happened the way it always happens—no drama, just a shudder through the hull like a great animal shrugging, and then the grinding, the horrible patient grinding of timber on stone, and the deck tilting under Dominic's feet at an angle no deck should hold. Odile killed the engine before the propeller could tear itself apart on the rock, and the sudden quiet was worse than the noise. The Kestrel settled with a groan, listing to port, held fast on something three feet under the surface that had every intention of holding her until the dry season, or forever.

"Well," Odile said. She lit a cigarette with hands that did not shake, which Dominic understood was its own kind of performance. "That's the ninth time and the third grounding. I'm getting worse at this, or the rocks are getting smarter."

"The engine?" Dominic asked.

"Alive. Shaft might be bent. Can't tell till we're off." She exhaled smoke at the gorge walls. "Can't get off till we're light. Can't get light without carrying everything overland to the downstream pool and floating her off on the tide of her own emptiness." She looked at him sideways. "Congratulations, railroad man. You're about to learn why river people hate mountains."

* * *

They unloaded her in the grey hour before true dawn, working by storm lantern because Odile refused firelight that could be seen from the water behind them. Everything came ashore: crates of rice and salt pork, the tool chest, coils of rope, canvas, the small iron stove, jerricans of kerosene, and—wrapped in oilcloth inside a rice crate marked FLOUR, an irony Dominic appreciated more each day—the map case, which never left his body except when his body needed to work, and then it went into Teo's keeping, because the boy was the fastest runner among them and the least likely to stand and fight if it came to running.

The portage route Odile laid out was two miles of misery mapped from memory: up the left bank, along a shelf of stone, then down through a stretch of swamp that fed a side channel rejoining the river below the narrows. They would carry the gear in relays, drag the lightened Kestrel off the rock with block and tackle anchored to a fig tree older than any government, and meet her at the downstream pool by nightfall. Two miles. Four trips apiece. In country where the mud had teeth.

The mud had teeth. That was not a figure of speech. Within twenty minutes of entering the swamp, Dominic felt the first leech attach itself above his boot-top, a cold slender thing that he only discovered when the blood ran warm down his calf. By the second relay he had stopped counting. The swamp was a brown universe of buttress roots and knee-deep water the temperature of breath, ferns tall as men, and a humidity that made the air itself feel like something you had to push through. Insects rose in clouds. The leeches dropped from leaves with the patience of assassins.

Teo took to it better than any of them, which surprised nobody who had watched the boy live rough. He learned within the first hour to check his cuffs, to smear mud on his skin as armor, to walk in the channels where the current kept the worst of the muck stirred. He carried more than his share and pretended he wasn't doing it, and Dominic let him pretend, because a fourteen-year-old runaway deserved the dignity of being strong at something.

It was on the third relay that the ankle went.

Dominic heard the cry from thirty yards back—a short, bitten-off sound, the sound of someone swallowing pain before it could become a weakness—and came splashing through the ferns to find Teo sitting in the shallows, white-faced, his right leg at an angle in the water, his hands pressed hard around the joint above the boot.

"Root hole," the boy gasped. "Went in to the knee. I'm fine. I'm fine, I can walk—"

He stood, put weight on it, and folded like wet paper. Dominic caught him before the swamp could.

"It's a sprain," Dominic said, after he'd gotten the boot off with a knife and seen the swelling already rising like bread dough. "Maybe bad. Doesn't matter how bad, because you're not walking on it for two days either way."

"We're being followed," Teo said, and his voice cracked on the word *followed*, and Dominic remembered that this boy had read the syndicate's wire with his own eyes, had seen the order that named them all as cargo to be intercepted. "I can't be carried slow. You need everyone fast."

"You need to shut up and let the adults solve this," said Odile, arriving out of the green with a crate of kerosene on her shoulder as though it were a basket of laundry. She set it down, looked at the ankle, looked at the boy's face, and something in her expression shifted—the sharpness softening into something older and wearier and infinitely more dangerous to her composure. "Ah, hell," she said quietly. "Come here, wire-rat."

She crouched, took his arm across her shoulders, and stood up with him in a fireman's carry that made the boy yelp and made Dominic revise several assumptions about river pilots. She was fifty pounds lighter than the load she now carried and moved like the arithmetic didn't interest her.

"Odile—" Dominic started.

"He weighs less than a crate of salt pork and complains less," she said. "Walk."

* * *

They made the downstream pool as the light failed, all of them, which Dominic counted as the day's single victory and refused to examine too closely lest it curdle. The Kestrel floated again—Odile and he had spent the middle hours of the day hauling on the tackle line until their palms were raw, and the old boat had come off the rock with a reluctance that felt personal—and she lay at anchor in the black pool looking, in the last light, like something rescued rather than merely recovered.

Teo sat propped against the wheelhouse with his foot wrapped in a compress of cold river mud and dock leaves, furious at his own body in the particular way of boys who had survived by never being the burden. Odile sat beside him, smoking, saying nothing, which from her was a form of tenderness. Every few minutes the boy tried to flex the ankle and every few minutes Odile reached over without looking and pushed the foot back down, and eventually he stopped trying.

Dominic stood the first watch because he could not have slept if the whole swamp had voted for it.

The night came down thick. Frogs set up their machinery. Fireflies stitched the dark above the water in slow, senseless patterns. And somewhere upstream—north, toward the range, toward the passes that the map drew in Elias Wren's dying handwriting—Dominic let himself think about Harrow's men.

They had been behind them since the second morning. Not close—he judged a day, maybe a day and a half, slowed by their own boats and their own certainty—but closing, the way weather closes. Teo's intercepted wire had given them names: Harrow commanding, six men, rifles, a launch with a shallow draft built for exactly this river. What the wire hadn't given them was a reason for the syndicate's urgency, but Dominic had stopped needing reasons. A man did not spend this much money chasing a dead prospector's map unless the map could hang him.

And it could. Dominic had read it by lamplight enough nights now to know it in his bones. Those weren't survey marks anymore to him; they were testimony. Elevations that didn't match the ground. Grades drawn gentler than the truth. And Tunnel Nine, marked in Elias's careful hand with a symbol Dominic had not yet decoded—or had decoded, perhaps, and did not want to say aloud even to himself.

He checked the map case where it lay against his ribs. Still there. Still heavy. Promises were heavy; nobody warned you about that at the making of them.

A shape moved at the edge of the firelight and he had the pistol half-drawn before his mind caught up with his hands and identified Odile, moving quiet along the bank, checking the mooring lines.

"You'll shoot yourself someday, doing that," she said.

"You'll be shot someday, walking like that."

"Fair." She came and leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and kerosene and, beneath it, the swamp's green rot that no amount of washing would lift for days. For a while neither of them spoke. The frogs filled the silence the way water fills a hull.

"He's a good boy," Odile said finally.

"He is."

"He shouldn't be here. None of us should. But especially him." She tapped ash into the water. "I've carried a lot of things down this river, Montrose. Cargo that shouldn't have been cargo. I told you some of it. Not all of it." Her jaw worked. "I used to tell myself the river washes everything downstream eventually. Nothing stays buried in moving water. But I think maybe I was the moving water. I was how it stayed buried."

Dominic said nothing. He had learned, in his years of hiding, that the most valuable thing you could give a person confessing at night was the absence of interruption.

"If Harrow catches us," she said, "he won't ask me questions. He already knows my answers. He bought them years ago, or thinks he did." She turned and looked at him, and in the dark her face was mostly eyes. "Whatever happens tomorrow, or the day after—you keep that boy moving. You keep the map moving. Don't spend anything on me that you can't afford."

"That's a strange thing to say," Dominic said slowly, "for a woman who just carried a sprained boy two miles through leech swamp."

"It's an honest thing to say." She pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep. I'll take midnight."

She walked away along the bank, quiet as the leeches, and Dominic stood his watch and thought about the difference between loyalty and honesty, and how he had spent eleven years confusing them, and how the woman walking away from him seemed determined to teach him the difference at gunpoint if necessary.

* * *

Dawn brought the smoke.

Not theirs. It rose thin and blue from the bend upstream, a half-mile maybe, where the gorge wall cut the current and any boat would have to slow. Woodsmoke. Breakfast smoke. The smoke of men confident enough to cook openly on a river they owned.

Dominic woke the others with a hand, not a voice. Odile was awake before he touched her shoulder—she slept like a cat aboard her own boat, one ear always open—and Teo came up off the deck planks with a knife in his fist that he clearly did not remember drawing, blinking at it as if it belonged to someone else.

"How many?" Odile whispered, glass in hand, studying the bend.

"One fire. So six, if it's Harrow. Maybe they split." She lowered the glass. "Current's with us. We can run the narrows' tailwater and be past them in an hour, but they'll hear the engine echo off these walls like a church bell, and their launch draws less water than we do. They'd run us down by noon."

"Or," Dominic said, "we go overland."

He said it reluctantly, because he had spent the night's watch studying the map, and the map showed what Odile's memory confirmed: an old surveyor's track leaving the river at the downstream pool, climbing east through the foothills toward the abandoned grade of the closed line. Three days on foot instead of two on water. Leech country the whole way. But trackless to anyone who didn't have Elias Wren's ink, and Harrow's men were rivermen, not walkers.

Odile studied the map when he showed her, her lips moving slightly as she translated contour lines into country she knew. Then she laughed—one short, harsh note with no humor in it.

"Wren's track. Of course. The old man walked every inch of this basin drawing that thing." She rolled the map closed and handed it back with something like reverence. "All right. Overland. But we travel light and we travel quiet, and somebody carries the wire-rat, because he doesn't walk on that ankle for three days, and I'll thank you not to argue with me about whose shoulder he rides on."

"No argument," Dominic said.

Teo opened his mouth to argue anyway, saw both their faces, and closed it. But his eyes were bright and wet and furious, and when Odile crouched to let him climb onto her back he gripped her shoulders harder than the ankle alone explained.

They stripped the Kestrel to her bones—gear redistributed, the boat herself hidden under cut ferns and brush below the high-water mark, moored to a deadfall where only a man looking for her would find her. Dominic took the front of the trail with the pistol loose in his belt and the map case strapped tight. Maren's town was four days ahead through country that got steeper every hour, and behind them the breakfast smoke thinned and vanished, and somewhere in the green silence a bird began to call in notes so like a human whistle that Teo flinched at every phrase of it.

They had been walking an hour when Odile stopped, head tilted, nostrils flaring like an animal's.

"Smoke again," she said. "Ahead of us this time. Small. Fresh." She looked back at Dominic, and for the first time since he'd known her, Odile Ferrant looked genuinely uncertain of the river. "There's a trading post on this track. Half a day on. And Montrose—I know who runs it."

"Friend of yours?"

"Worse," she said, and shifted Teo's weight, and started walking. "A friend of Harrow's."
