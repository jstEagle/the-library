## 8. The Syndicate's Offer

The trading post at Wau Creek Junction sat where the river gave up its argument with the mountains—a squat timber building on stilts, its veranda sagging under the weight of dried fish, kerosene tins, and the particular silence of a place that had learned not to ask questions of travelers. Dominic had been in such places before. He knew their grammar: the way the storekeeper's eyes flicked to your boots before your face, the way conversation stopped mid-sentence when a stranger crossed the threshold and resumed only when he'd bought something small enough to prove he was passing through.

He left Odile and Teo aboard the *Kestrel*, moored two bends downstream among the log rafts, and walked up alone. It was a matter of supplies—salt pork, lamp oil, a new whetstone for the knife Teo had taken to carrying like a talisman—and a matter of caution. Three people arriving together drew eyes. One tired man buying flour did not.

The morning had come up gray and close, the air thick enough to drink. Mist hung in the valley like laundry left out too long, and somewhere above it, invisible, the passes waited with their first snows. Dominic could feel them the way an old injury feels weather coming: a pressure behind the sternum, a countdown. Elias Wren's map case rode against his ribs inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a second heartbeat. He had taken to sleeping with it. He told himself it was prudence. He was beginning to suspect it was something closer to penance.

Inside, the trading post smelled of canvas, tobacco, and the sweet rot of overripe bananas stacked in a crate by the counter. The storekeeper was a narrow woman with spectacles fogged from the walk between rooms, and she took his list without comment, moving down her shelves with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand errands and expected to do ten thousand more.

He was counting coins on the counter when the door opened behind him.

He heard it the way you hear anything that matters—not as a sound but as a change in the room's breathing. The two drovers playing cards at the corner table went quiet. The storekeeper's hands paused over the salt pork. And the footsteps that came across the floorboards were unhurried, polished, entirely wrong for this country: the steps of a man who had never once worried about what the mud would do to his shoes.

"Mr. Corben," the man said pleasantly. "Or should I say—"

"Don't," Dominic said.

He turned. The agent was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in a traveling suit of good gray wool that must have cost more than the entire contents of the building. His face was the kind designed for boardrooms and funeral portraits—symmetrical, composed, faintly sympathetic even at rest. He carried no weapon that Dominic could see, which meant he carried several. Behind him, framed in the doorway, two men in canvas jackets stood on the veranda with the loose-limbed patience of dogs told to wait.

"You have me at a disadvantage," the agent said. "Though of course that's rather the point. May I?" He gestured at the counter without waiting for permission and set down a leather document case, squaring its edges with the counter's edge as though the world ought to align itself to him. "My name is Verrall. Augustin Verrall. I represent certain commercial interests in the basin."

"Certain commercial interests." Dominic kept his voice flat. "That's a long way of saying the syndicate."

"It's a polite way of saying it. Politeness costs nothing and buys a great deal." Verrall smiled. Up close, Dominic could see the smile didn't reach past the man's cheekbones; it lived entirely in the mouth, like a thing trained. "I'll be direct, Mr.—shall we say Corben? Directness is a courtesy between professionals. You are carrying something that belongs to my employers. A map case, waterproof, recently acquired from one Elias Wren."

Dominic felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped into still water. He made himself finish stacking the coins. Made himself slide them across the counter to the storekeeper, whose eyes were fixed very hard on her own ledger now, her pencil moving in small useless circles.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," Verrall agreed. "You wouldn't. That's the correct answer, and I appreciate you giving it. It means you're careful, and careful men can be reasoned with." He opened the document case and laid three things on the counter between them, arranging each with the same precision he'd given the case itself.

The first was a bank draft. Dominic didn't touch it, but he read the figure, and the figure read him back—more money than he had earned in the last six years combined, written out in a clerk's immaculate hand against a bank in Port Vessel.

The second was a folded newspaper clipping, yellowed at the creases. Dominic recognized it before he understood why: his own name, printed in bold beneath a photograph of a younger man standing beside a locomotive on a completed viaduct, his hand resting on the engine's flank like a bridegroom's on a shoulder. *MONTROSE COMPLETES THE HIGHLINE CROSSING.* He had been thirty-one in that photograph. He had believed, then, that steel laid honestly would hold the world together.

And the third was a single sheet of paper, unsigned, bearing a seal he knew too well—the syndicate's compass rose stamped in violet ink—and four lines of text promising, in the language of lawyers, the restoration of a name, the withdrawal of outstanding charges, and the full pardon and reinstatement of one Dominic Ambrose Montrose, civil engineer, upon surrender of specified property.

"There it is," Verrall said softly. "Your old life. Laid out on a counter in a trading post at the end of the world, priced and ready. Money, Mr. Montrose. Your name back. Your license. Your profession. The right to sign your own work again—to stand on a bridge you built and hear people call you by the name you earned instead of the alias you're hiding inside." He tapped the draft once with one finger. "All of it, for a piece of oiled canvas and some ink. That's not extortion. That's forgiveness. My employers are offering you forgiveness."

* * *

The storekeeper retreated into the back room with a mumbled excuse about inventory, and the drovers gathered their cards and left without settling their tab, which told Dominic everything about how long Verrall's people had been in the valley and how thoroughly they had prepared the ground. The two men on the veranda stayed where they were, watching the road, watching the river, watching nothing in particular with great attention.

Dominic looked at the pardon.

That was the trap of it, and he knew it was a trap, and he looked anyway. Because the paper was real—or real enough—and because the ache underneath it was realer than either. Six years. Six years of false names in ledgers, of ducking when clerks glanced twice at him, of every door unlocked and every window shuttered, of being a ghost who repaired machines for men who never asked where he'd learned the trade. He had told himself it was freedom, living off the books. Some nights, lying in the loft above his shed in Karawari Landing listening to the river work at its banks, he had almost believed it. But freedom that requires you to erase yourself is just a cage with better ventilation, and here was a man holding the key, smiling, offering to let him out.

His hands wanted the paper. That was the terrible truth of it. His hands wanted the weight of his own name.

"What happened to Elias Wren," Dominic said quietly, "before your employers' property came into his possession?"

Verrall's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks. "An accident. Prospectors live dangerous lives."

"He was shot in the back."

"These mountains are full of hunters. Sometimes the aim is poor." Verrall spread his hands, a gesture of regret so perfectly calibrated it might have been rehearsed in front of a mirror. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, Mr. Montrose. You've seen enough of how the basin works. Old man Wren found something in those closed lines that my employers prefer stay closed, and he was careless about who he showed it to. Men die of carelessness here. It's practically a local industry." He leaned forward slightly, and for the first time the pleasant mask thinned, and Dominic saw the actual face beneath it: patient, cold, and utterly certain of its arithmetic. "But here is what matters. That map changes nothing. The tunnels are sealed. The surveys are filed. The men who signed them—" his eyes rested on Dominic with terrible gentleness "—are dead or scattered or drinking themselves blind in river towns. Thirty-one names on a page don't raise the dead. They never have. All that paper can do is pull four more living people into the pass ahead of the snow, and we both know how that ends. Harrow is already on the river."

The name went through Dominic like a spike of cold. He kept his face still, but Verrall saw it land—he was built to see such things—and nodded as if a point had been scored in a game only he was keeping.

"You know the name. Good. Then you know the offer isn't idle, and neither is the alternative." Verrall straightened his cuffs. "Take the draft. Take the pardon. Hand me the case, and by spring you'll be drafting grades again for a railroad that pays its debts and buries its mistakes—which, Mr. Montrose, is what railroads *are*. Or keep the map, keep running, and become one more entry in a ledger nobody audits." He paused. "There's a third option, of course. You give me the case, and I give you the papers, and you also tell me where the pilot and the boy are moored. In that version, everyone walks away. Even them. My employers have no quarrel with a river pilot or a telegraph runaway. They're incidental. Collateral is expensive; we avoid it when we can."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'm afraid the offer expires. Offers like this always do." Verrall began, unhurriedly, to gather the clipping and the pardon, leaving the draft on the counter. "You have until I finish my tea. The storekeeper makes excellent tea. She doesn't know that yet, but she will."

* * *

Dominic stood in the smell of canvas and kerosene and let the silence stretch, and in that silence he did the honest arithmetic he had been avoiding since a gut-shot man bled out on his shed floor and put a promise in his hands heavy as ballast.

He thought about his name. Not the name on the clipping—*Montrose Completes the Highline Crossing*—but the name underneath it, the one no newspaper ever printed: the signature he had put on the altered survey eleven years ago, believing the syndicate's numbers because believing them was easier than asking what lay under Tunnel Nine. He had been young and ambitious and the figures had been beautiful, and beauty, he had learned too late, was exactly what falsification looked like from the inside. Every disgrace since had been interest paid on that original loan. And here was Verrall offering to forgive the debt—but not by erasing it. By refinancing it. That was all a pardon from these people would ever be: a new note drawn on the same rotten bank, his name restored so that his signature could be useful again, his honor returned the way you return a tool to a man because you need the tool.

Loyalty without honesty, he thought, is just another cage. He didn't know yet where the phrase came from—some sermon heard half-drunk in a river town, maybe, or something Elias Wren had muttered in his last hour—but it sat in him now like a keel.

"They were miners," Dominic said. "The thirty-one. Not casualties. Not entries. Miners with names, and someone wrote them out of the books, and someone else signed the numbers that buried them." He looked at Verrall, and found, to his own surprise, that his voice was steady. "I know because I was the someone else."

Something moved behind Verrall's eyes—the first genuine thing Dominic had seen there, and it was disappointment, thin and cold as frost.

"That's unfortunate," Verrall said. "Confession is touching, Mr. Montrose, but it has no market value. Neither does guilt." He picked up the bank draft, considered it, and then—deliberately, almost courteously—tucked it into Dominic's breast pocket, patting it flat over his heart, directly over the hidden map case, so that they both understood the geometry of the gesture. "Keep that. My employers believe in generous margins. If you reconsider—and men usually do, around the second night of snow—you present it anywhere the syndicate trades, and the offer stands. If you don't reconsider..." He buttoned his gray coat. "Well. Harrow is thorough. Thoroughness is cheaper than mercy; that's simply accounting."

He collected his document case, inclined his head with the precision of a man closing a meeting, and walked out onto the veranda. The two canvas-jacketed men fell in behind him. At the bottom of the steps Verrall paused, and without turning back, said:

"The daughter is not in Port Vessel, incidentally. You're walking into the mountains looking for a woman who isn't there. Consider whether that, too, might be part of someone's design." And then he was crossing the mud of the yard, unhurried, polished shoes printing neat crescents in it, gone into the mist toward the landing where—Dominic was sure of it now—a fast boat waited with an engine better than the *Kestrel's*.

The storekeeper came back from the back room and rang up his purchases in silence, and would not meet his eye, and gave him back his change with both hands, the way you pay off a debt to a marked man.

* * *

He walked down to the river through mist gone thin and gold, and the draft rode in his pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool. Twice he took it out. Once he stood at the water's edge with it in his fist, looking at the figure written there, doing the arithmetic of what that money would mean—Teo fed through winter, Odile's hull re-coppered, Maren found and kept safe, all of it bought with a piece of oiled canvas and thirty-one names that would stay dead and unnamed forever. It would be so easy. It would look, from the outside, exactly like wisdom.

He fed it to the river instead. The paper took the water reluctantly, the ink blooming violet, the compass rose blurring into a stain, and the current carried it under the log rafts and away south. It wasn't a grand gesture. It was just the cheapest honest thing available, and he took it, and felt lighter and more afraid in the same breath.

At the landing, Odile read his face before his boots touched the deck. "We're leaving," she said. Not a question.

"Now," Dominic said. "They know the boat. There'll be a gunboat upstream of us by dark if it isn't already." He swung the supply crates aboard and turned to find Teo watching him from the pilothouse roof, wire-thin, wide-eyed, having heard none of it and understood all of it the way children understand storms.

"And the girl?" Odile asked, untying the first line. "Still Port Vessel?"

Dominic hesitated. Verrall's parting words sat in him like a swallowed hook—*she is not in Port Vessel*—and he could not decide whether the man had lied to poison the journey or told the truth to shorten it. But Anji lay at the foot of the pass, the last town before the closed grade, and a prospector's daughter hunting her father's killers would sooner haunt assay offices than sit waiting on a coast.

"We stop at Anji first," he said. "If she's not there, we've lost nothing but a day. If she is—" He looked upriver, where the mist was peeling off the water and the mountains stood revealed for the first time that morning, white already at their crowns, white and getting whiter. "If she is, then her father's map finally gets delivered."

Odile threw the line aboard. The *Kestrel* took the current, and the current took them north, toward the passes, toward whatever was waiting in the country the syndicate had sealed—and somewhere behind them, faint under the river's voice, Dominic thought he could hear the low diesel knock of an engine that was not theirs, gaining.
