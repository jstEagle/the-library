## 9. Maren Wren, Alive

Anji smelled of sulfur and wet iron, the way mining towns always did, as if the earth itself had been made to bleed and had never quite stopped. The town clung to the foothills in terraces of timber and corrugated tin, each street climbing higher than the last, each one poorer and closer to the noise of the stamps. From the lowest street you could hear the ore-crushers working day and night, a rhythm like a giant heart beating under the mountain, and from the highest you could see the range itself—the passes Elias Wren's map had drawn, white-capped already in the last week of autumn, patient and indifferent as the dead.

Dominic stood on the middle terrace with the map case strapped under his coat and felt the weight of it change. For eleven days it had been a dead man's burden, a promise he carried the way a mule carries salt—without understanding, without complaint, because complaint changed nothing. Now, standing in the town where the daughter lived, the weight had begun to turn into something else. Fear, maybe. Or the particular dread of a man about to hand a stranger the last thing her father ever touched.

"You're doing the thing again," Odile said beside him. She had her hands in her pockets and her collar up, and she was watching the assay office across the street with the flat attention she gave to water she intended to run a boat through. "The thing where you rehearse a speech nobody wants to hear."

"I don't have a speech."

"Everyone has a speech. You've had one since Broken Narrows. *Your father sent me, I'm sorry for your loss, here's a map.*" She said it in a fair imitation of his voice, low and overcareful. "She's going to want more than that. Daughters always want more than that."

"And you'd know?"

"I know what it is to want a dead man to explain himself." Her jaw tightened, and for a moment Dominic saw the thing she kept locked below decks—the village, the smoke, the strike she had helped survey and Harrow had burned. She blinked it away. "The office closes at the six o'clock whistle. If we're going to do this, do it before she has time to think."

Teo came down the terrace steps two at a time, breathless, a boy again for the length of a staircase. "She's there," he said. "I saw her through the window. Dark hair, spectacles, sleeves rolled up—she was weighing something on the little brass scales. She's got ink on her fingers, like—" He stopped, hearing himself, and his ears went red. "Like she works there."

"You spoke to her?" Dominic said sharply.

"No. I bought tobacco for you and listened. There's a difference." Teo had learned the difference on the Kestrel, and learned it well; the boy could stand at a bar for an hour and walk away with the names of every man who'd drunk there and what they owed. "The clerk called her *Frau Wren*. Nobody uses her first name. And there's a notice board inside with a reward paper on it—handwritten, not official. *Information wanted regarding the death of Elias Wren, prospector, last seen Karawari Landing.* She's offering two hundred marks out of her own pay."

Odile whistled softly. "She's not waiting to be found. She's hunting."

"That's what I've been trying to tell you," Teo said, and there was something in his face that wasn't fear exactly—recognition, the look of a boy who had run from one set of hunters and found a woman running from the same ones. "She's like you two. She just doesn't know it yet."

The six o'clock whistle had not yet blown when they crossed the street. Dominic pushed open the assay office door into air thick with acid fumes and lamp oil, the smell of money being counted out of rock. Behind a counter scarred by crucibles stood a woman of perhaps thirty, sleeves pinned to the elbow, weighing a pinch of gray dust on brass scales with the absolute concentration of someone for whom the world had narrowed to a single measurement.

She did not look up. "Office is closed to trade," she said. "Assays only by appointment. If you've got ore, leave it and the clerk will—"

"Frau Wren."

The scales did not move. Her hands did not shake. But Dominic saw her go still in the way water goes still before it freezes—a whole surface changing state in an instant. She set down the tweezers with exaggerated care, as if the small precision of the act could hold off whatever was coming, and raised her eyes.

They were gray eyes, and they were her father's. Dominic knew it at once, the way you know a river you've only ever seen on a map. Elias Wren had had those eyes, steady and unimpressed, taking your measure even while dying.

"You knew my father," she said. It was not a question, and it was not soft.

"Yes."

"Then say how."

* * *

She would not hear it at the counter. She led them through the assay room—past the balances under glass bells, past the parting furnace with its dull red eye, past a wall of sample drawers each labeled in a hand Dominic was beginning to recognize from the map's margins—and into a back office barely wider than a berth. A desk, a stool, a cot in the corner, a single photograph tacked to the plaster: a younger Elias Wren squinting into sun, a girl of twelve on his shoulders, both of them laughing at whoever held the camera. Dominic had to look away from it.

Maren Wren shut the door and stood with her back against it, arms crossed, and said, "Now. Say how."

"He came to Karawari Landing in the last week of September," Dominic said. "He'd been in the range. He was hurt—shot. He made it as far as my shed."

"Your shed."

"I repair engines there. Under another name." He watched her absorb that—the false name, the hiding, the whole architecture of a man who had spent years making himself small—and she did not flinch from it, which told him something about her own architecture. "He was dying. He knew it. He gave me a map case and asked me to bring it to you in Port Vessel before the snows closed the passes. I've come eleven days to do that."

"Show me."

He unstrapped the case and laid it on her desk. She looked at it a long time before she touched it—looked at it the way you look at a body, Dominic thought, checking for breath—and when she finally opened the waterproof flap and saw the oilcloth and the rolled survey beneath it, her breath went out of her in a sound she had clearly been holding for years.

She read the map standing, both hands flat on the desk, head bowed, and Dominic watched her lips move over the notations, the elevations, the little marginal figures in her father's cramped hand. Teo stood by the door with his cap in his fists. Odile studied the ceiling. The parting furnace ticked as it cooled.

"He was shot," Maren said at last, without looking up. "Who shot him?"

"I don't know. He didn't say. He said—" Dominic hesitated, and then gave it whole, because a half-truth here would be a stone in her shoe for the rest of her life. "He said they'd know he had it. He said the syndicate had been looking for him since the summer. He made me promise to get it to you before the snows."

"The syndicate." She said the word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "The Consolidated Sepik Mining and Transport Company. My father worked for them for nine years. Chainman, then instrument man, then—well. He never told me what he stopped being." She straightened. "He left when I was nineteen. No letter for the first year. Then letters, always poste restante, always moving. Always the same sentence at the end: *Don't come looking. When it's time, I'll send for you.*" Her voice did not break, but it went thin as wire. "Eleven weeks ago I got a letter saying it was time. Telling me to wait in Port Vessel. I waited six weeks. Then I stopped waiting and started asking questions, and the questions led here, where he'd worked, where the people who'd know him were. I took a job weighing dirt and I've been pulling on that thread ever since."

"And the notice on the board," Teo said. "The reward."

"Two hundred marks is most of a year's wages." She finally looked up, and Dominic saw that under the grief there was a furnace banked and burning. "I don't care about the money. I care that whoever killed my father has a name, and that name is written down somewhere—in a ledger, in a payroll, in a letter—and I intend to read it."

Odile shifted. "Frau Wren. Maren. There are men following us. Syndicate men. A gunboat on the river, and now, I think, riders on the portage road. If you start pulling that thread in daylight, you'll draw them here."

"Then they'll have to stand in line." Maren rolled the map closed with a tenderness that contradicted every hard line of her face. "You came eleven days to hand me my father's last work and disappear. I can see it in you—all three of you. The courier, the pilot, the boy who listens at bars." Teo went scarlet. "But you've read this map. You've seen what's in these passes, or you suspect it. And you came here *first*, to Anji, before Port Vessel—don't deny it, the portage road only goes through this town. So you wanted to see me. Why?"

Dominic had no speech after all. He had rehearsed nothing, and what came out was only the truth, which was somehow harder. "Because a promise made to a dying man isn't finished when the map changes hands. Because you shouldn't read it alone. And because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I helped make some of what's in there. Years ago. Survey marks, grades, alignments. And some of them are wrong, and I need to know why, and I think you need to know it too."

For the first time, something in her face moved beyond grief and fury—something like recognition, the wary kinship of people who have been carrying the same stone up the same hill. She looked at him for a long moment, then at Odile, then at Teo, and Dominic understood that she was doing what he had done at every shuttered window in Karawari Landing: counting exits, weighing strangers, deciding how much of herself it was safe to spend.

"Sit down," she said. "All of you. I close the office at six, and the clerk drinks until eight, and in that time I'm going to show you why I stopped waiting in Port Vessel." She went to the desk, unlocked a drawer with a key from around her neck, and drew out a ledger bound in gray cardboard, its corners soft with handling. "Because my father didn't send me a map so I could frame it. He sent it because he was a witness. And so, it turns out, am I."

She opened the ledger on the desk between them, and the lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door, and outside the six o'clock whistle began to blow across the terraces of Anji, long and low, like something waking.

* * *

The ledger was a payroll book. Maren turned the pages with the care of a woman handling evidence, which was exactly what she was doing, and explained in a voice stripped down to bare wire.

"Assay offices see everything in a mining district. Ore values, tonnage, shipments. But the clerks talk, and clerks keep copies, and I have been a very friendly clerk for four months." She tapped a column of figures. "This is the syndicate's casualty list for the upper basin works. Nine years of it. Men listed as lost—rockfalls, floods, 'survey casualties,' that's the phrase they use, *survey casualties*, as though the mountain ate them by accident. Look at the names."

Dominic looked. And felt the floor of the world tilt.

He knew the names. Not all of them. But a third of them, at least, he knew—men he had eaten with in syndicate mess tents, men whose instrument lines he had walked, men who had carried his stakes and chains through country so steep the mules refused it. Jossel. Anahera. The two Okoro brothers. Men from Tunnel Nine's last shift.

"These men weren't lost," Maren said. "They were *paid*. Look at the dates. Wages drawn, rations drawn, kit issued—and then nothing. No death benefit filed. No next of kin notified. No body, no inquest, no grave. The syndicate lists them as casualties and then stops every record about them as though a pen had been laid down mid-word." She looked up, and her gray eyes had gone bright and terrible. "You don't erase a man by accident. You erase him with a purpose. My father saw this. I think he helped compile it, before he understood what he was compiling. And I think whatever's in that map—whatever's in those closed lines—is where they stopped being people and started being entries."

Teo had gone white. "Thirty-one," he said quietly. "I counted."

Odile said nothing at all. She stood by the window with her back half turned, looking down at the lamplit street, and Dominic saw her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, as though holding something shut.

"Maren," Dominic said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I am to you." He laid his finger on the map, on the switchback grade climbing toward a gap marked with his own drafting symbol, years old, a symbol he had invented himself and never expected to see again. "I drafted this line. I was the assistant surveyor on the upper basin extension. I signed the alignment reports. I believed the figures the syndicate gave me—the load ratings, the seam assays, all of it. If men died in those passes because of what I drew, then their names are partly on my hands, and I have spent six years hiding from that under a false name in a town that forgot itself." He met her eyes. "I didn't come here only to deliver a map. I came to find out what I did."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the stamps kept their patient, murderous rhythm.

Maren Wren looked at him for a long time. Dominic braced for the fury—it was owed, and he knew how fury worked—but what came was something stranger and more painful. She reached across the desk and turned the ledger toward him, to a page near the end, and put her finger on a single line.

"Then help me read it," she said. "Because here's what I can't do alone: I have entries and you have ground. I have names and you have the country where the names stopped. My father gave you the map because he couldn't carry it himself, and he gave it to *you*, of all people—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, "—because he must have believed you'd bring it to the passes, not away from them. Port Vessel is a courthouse. But the truth is in the range, before the snows."

Odile turned from the window. "You're talking about going up the closed grade. In November. With Harrow's people somewhere behind us on the portage road."

"I'm talking about thirty-one men who exist nowhere on earth except in this book and in my father's ink," Maren said, "and I am not going to let a mining company and a winter be the last word on them."

It was Teo who broke the silence, in the way of boys, too honest to be tactful. "It's like she's already family," he said, and then went red again, but nobody laughed, because it was true. Something had happened in the little office while the lamp burned down—some lock turning, some door opening onto a room none of them had lived in alone. A courier with a false name, a pilot with a burned village, a boy who listened at bars, and a woman with a ledger and her father's eyes: none of them had chosen each other, and all of them were now bound.

Maren began putting the office in order—map rolled, ledger locked, lamp trimmed—with the brisk economy of a woman who had already decided. "The last supply train to the head of the closed grade leaves Anji tomorrow at dawn. After that, the syndicate pulls the rolling stock down for winter and the passes are closed to everyone, including us." She shrugged into a coat, and at the door she paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the three of them with an expression that was almost—almost—a smile, and underneath it, grief like bedrock.

"Get some sleep," she said. "We climb at first light."

And it was then that Teo, who had drifted to the window to watch the street out of fourteen years of hard-learned habit, said quietly:

"Someone's been asking about us at the hotel. A man in a gray coat. And Maren—the reward paper on the notice board downstairs?" He swallowed. "There's a new one beside it. *Information wanted regarding four persons, last seen on the portage road.* Two thousand marks."

The whistle died away across the terraces. In the silence that followed, Maren blew out the lamp.
