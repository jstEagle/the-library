## 10. The Daughter's Ledger

The assay office in Anji smelled of acid and iron, a sharpness that got into the back of the throat and stayed there, the way grief did. Maren Wren had worked the night shift for eleven months because the night shift was quiet and quiet meant time—time to pull ledgers from the syndicate's own shelves and copy them by the light of a shuttered lamp while the day men slept off their beer in the boarding house across the street.

She heard them before she saw them: three sets of boots on the boardwalk outside, one of them dragging slightly, and a low voice she recognized from the waystation at Broken Narrows—the courier, Montrose, asking directions of a man who didn't know and didn't care. She had been watching the road east for two days, ever since the telegraph line had carried its garbled fragment down from the pass country: *courier sighted, Kestrel party, four souls.* Four. Her father had said *one man* in the letter that had taken nine months to reach her, the letter with the map case's twin key sewn into the lining.

Maren set down her pen. She blew out the lamp so the room went blue with dusk, and she waited behind the counter with her hand on the drawer where the ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth, and when the door opened she said, before any of them could speak:

"Shut it. Slowly. There are eyes on this street after dark."

The tall man in the doorway froze with his hand still on the latch. Behind him: a river woman with rope-scarred palms and a stare like a drawn blade; a boy, fourteen maybe, favoring one ankle, his collar too big for him and his eyes too old for the rest of him; and a girl—no, not a girl, a young woman with mud to the knees and a rifle slung across her back like she'd been born to it.

"You're Montrose," Maren said. "Dominic Montrose."

Something moved through the man's face—not surprise exactly, but the flinch of a man hearing his own name spoken aloud for the first time in years, spoken in a room he couldn't control. "I go by Doss hereabouts."

"I know what you go by." She came around the counter, keeping the lamp table between them out of habit older than sense. "I also know you signed a survey in the Sepik range in the spring of '89. I know your drafting hand. My father kept a sample of it." She reached into her shirt and drew out the folded paper she had carried against her skin for half a year, soft as cloth now from handling, and laid it on the counter between them. "He kept everything. That was his mistake, and mine, and possibly yours."

Montrose looked at the paper—a corner of an original grade profile, torn, bearing initials in a draftsman's precise slant—and Maren watched him understand, all in a single breath, that the dying man who had pressed a map case into his hands had known exactly whom he was choosing, and why.

"You're Wren's daughter," he said quietly.

"Maren." She let that stand a moment. "And you're late. He died in March. It's nearly November."

"We came as fast as the river and the mountains allowed." The river woman spoke up, arms crossed, voice dry as gunpowder. "Faster than sensible. If you're going to shoot somebody over the schedule, shoot me. I steered."

"Nobody's shooting anybody yet," Maren said. "Yet is the operative word. Sit down. All of you. And you—" she pointed at the boy, who was swaying on his bad ankle with the particular stubbornness of the young refusing to admit pain, "—sit down hardest. There's a stool behind you."

* * *

She lit the lamp again, having first gone to the window and checked the street both ways, having dropped the bolt and drawn the shade. The assay office at night was a cave of specimen drawers and reagent bottles, of brass scales and the faint chemical ghost of every ore that had ever passed across the counter. On the wall hung the syndicate's framed certificate of incorporation, gilt-lettered, and Maren had often thought about putting a bullet through it just to see whether the frame would splinter interestingly. She had never done it. Evidence was better than satisfaction. Evidence could be multiplied.

She unlocked the bottom drawer and lifted out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped it on the counter with the care of a surgeon and the reverence of a priest, and the others leaned in despite themselves, because there is something in a written record that pulls at people—the sense that ink is a kind of grave, or a kind of resurrection, depending on who's reading.

"Payroll registers," she said. "Sepik Consolidated Mining and Rail, northern division. Eleven months of nights, copying these by hand, because I can't carry the originals over the passes and I won't leave them here for whoever comes looking after me." She turned the top book toward them. "This is the quarterly payroll for the Tunnel Nine drive. Read me the casualty annex."

Montrose read. His lips moved at first, then stopped moving, which was worse. "'Survey casualties,'" he said. "'Non-production personnel lost during geotechnical operations.' Nine names."

"Nine names," Maren agreed. "Now look at the wage entries those names draw against."

He looked. She watched him look. The boy, Teo, craned up on his stool, and the river woman—Odile, Montrose had called her—went very still in the way of someone recognizing a shape from deep water.

"There are no wage entries," Montrose said slowly. "No pay numbers. No bank routing. No next-of-kin disbursements."

"No kin," Maren said. "No towns of origin. No signatures on receipt of effects. Nine men, dead in a tunnel, and not one of them ever drew a wage, sent money home, owned a coat, or had a mother." She turned the page with one finger. "Here's the same quarter, the mucking crews. Look how a real dead miner is recorded. Name, pay number, village, widow's allotment, freight rebate on personal effects shipped home. The company was punctilious about the dead it actually killed. It had to be—the allotments were contractually owed, and the union stewards counted heads. But these nine—" she tapped the annex, "—these nine exist only as a line item. Casualties invented to explain a number."

"What number?" Odile asked, and her voice had changed, gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading water ahead of a rock.

"The number of men who stopped appearing on the rolls in the week after the ninth of June, '89." Maren pulled the second ledger forward, a work-gang roster, its spine cracked, pages furred at the edges from her handling. "Thirty-one names come off the active rolls between the ninth and the sixteenth of June. Thirty-one. But the incident report filed with the territorial registrar lists nine. Survey casualties. Blamed on a misread seam angle, a routine collapse, an act of God with a company stamp on it." She looked up at Montrose. "Twenty-two men went into the ground and were never reported dead at all. They were never reported anything. They simply stopped being on the books, and the books were balanced, and the balance was the whole point."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"That's not possible," Teo said—but his voice cracked on it, and he knew even as he said it that it was entirely possible, that boys who ran telegraph wires learned early what companies did with inconvenient sums. "You'd need everyone to—clerks, paymasters, the registrar—"

"Clerks sign what they're given," Maren said. "Paymasters disburse what the annex authorizes. The registrar receives what the company files. A lie doesn't need everyone to believe it. It needs everyone to be paid, or frightened, or tired enough not to check." She had learned that sentence from her father, in a different context, when she was twelve, and it had taken her twenty years and his death to understand how completely he had meant it.

Montrose hadn't sat down. He stood at the counter with both hands flat on the wood, and Maren saw that they were shaking—not much, just a tremor at the fingertips, the tremor of a man holding himself to a counter to keep from doing something else with his hands.

"The map," he said. "Elias's map. It marks the closed lines, the grades, the tunnels. And the survey stations. And—" he stopped.

"And burial cairns," Maren finished for him. "Show them."

He unslung the waterproof case, worked the twin-key lock—her father's key met his inside the hasp, two halves of one mechanism, which told her more than anything else had told her about how Elias Wren had planned his insurance—and spread the map on the counter, weighting the corners with ore specimens. Lamplight pooled on it. The inked lines ran up the basin like veins, and along the closed grade above Tunnel Nine, in her father's small upright hand, were annotations she had never seen, because she had never seen the map—only his letters, only fragments, only the key.

Dates. Coordinates. Counts.

*9.vi.89 — 31 below, 22 unaccounted. Seam live. They knew. E.W.*

"He wasn't prospecting," Maren said. Her voice held. She had practiced holding it for months, alone, over these ledgers, and practice held now. "Everyone in Anji will tell you Elias Wren was a prospector, an old fool chasing color in country already claimed. That story was convenient to everyone, including me, for a long time. He was a witness. He'd been a chainman on the original surveys—he was too old for the tunnel crews but not too old to walk the grades with a stadia rod, checking their work, because the company hired independent verification for the insurance underwriters. He was above ground on the ninth of June. He saw the ventilation fans reversed. He saw the portal sealed with timber and blasting mats within four hours of the first shock, before any rescue could be mounted, before the dust had even settled. He saw the paymaster's wagon come up the grade the next morning carrying, instead of wages, a strongbox and a clerk with a list." Her finger rested on the annotation without touching the ink. "And he understood that the list was shorter than the roll, and that the difference was the product. Twenty-two men, mined shut like a seam, written off as nine, and the nine written off as nobody at all."

"Why?" The word came out of Montrose raw. "Why seal it? A collapse—you dig, you recover bodies, you grieve, you report. Even a live seam, even fire—you flood it, you vent it, you take the loss. You don't entomb thirty-one men and erase twenty-two of them unless—"

"Unless the seam wasn't supposed to be live," Odile said.

Every head turned to her. She stood with her back to the specimen cabinets, arms still crossed, and Maren watched her decide—watched the decision move through her face like weather—and then the river woman pushed off the cabinet and came to the map and put one scarred finger on the upper reach of the closed grade, above Tunnel Nine, where the contour lines crowded close.

"I ran cargo on this river for the syndicate for six years," Odile said. "Freight in, concentrate out. And for one season, survey equipment—cases marked GEOTECH, heavy as sin, handled like eggs. Dynamometers, core barrels, gas-detection gear. Gas-detection gear, going *up* the grade, in quantity, months before the drive was scheduled to reach the deep country." Her finger traced down. "They weren't surprised by the seam. They were measuring it. Firedamp readings, week over week, logged and forwarded—and I know they were forwarded, because I carried the dispatch cases myself, and I noticed that the cases went to the directors' office in Port Vessel and nowhere else. Not the works manager. Not the safety board. The directors." She straightened. "A live seam means stop work, ventilate, lose the season, maybe abandon the drive. Or it means you've found the richest coal body in the basin and you want it *now*, before the underwriters' survey confirms what your own instruments are telling you. So you push the crews past every reading. And when the mountain answers, you have perhaps four hours before anyone outside the valley knows anything has happened. Four hours to make the arithmetic come out right."

"Four hours," Maren repeated softly. "That matches my father's account to the minute."

The two halves of the truth came together then, in the lamplight, with an almost audible click, like the map case locking—her father's testimony from above ground, the syndicate's own numbers from below it, the erased men and the invented men, the reversed fans and the forwarded gas logs. Two half-truths that had traveled separate roads for fifteen years, hers in oilcloth in an assay-office drawer, his in waterproof canvas on a madman's shoulders over the passes, meeting at last on a counter that smelled of acid and iron.

Montrose made a sound. Maren looked at him and saw a man coming apart quietly, the way timber fails—first a fiber, then a seam, then the whole grain letting go. "I drafted the grade profile," he said. "The one your father kept a corner of. I signed the numbers they gave me. I believed—I needed to believe the figures were honest, because I'd built my name on honest figures, and if they weren't honest then nothing I'd built was standing on anything at all." His hands were flat on the counter again, pressing down as though the wood might hold him to the world. "My signature is on the document that justified driving through that country. Which means my signature is somewhere in the file that explains why no one stopped the drive. Which means—"

"Which means you're not just a courier," Maren said. "You're evidence."

The word landed in the room and sat there. The boy Teo looked from Montrose to Maren and back with the expression of someone watching two trains approach the same switch. Odile had gone to the window and was standing with one eye to the gap in the shade, and Maren noted that without comment—note it, always note who watches the door—but she let the silence run another moment before she spoke again, because the last thing had to be said aloud, in front of witnesses, so that it became real and could not be taken back.

"There's one more thing in the ledgers," she said. "I saved it because I didn't understand it until tonight." She turned to the final register, the most recent, the quarterly book for this very year, and opened it to a page she had marked with a thread. "After my father left the basin—after he ran—the company continued the fiction. Every quarter since, the casualty annex carries a tenth name. *E. Wren, surveyor, non-production personnel.* Listed as a survey casualty of '89. Dead on the company's books for fifteen years." She looked up, and let them see, finally, what the cost of holding her voice had been. "They killed him twice. Once in fact, in March, in Karawari Landing, by whatever hand they sent. And once on paper, fifteen years ago, because a man listed as dead can't testify, can't sue, can't inherit, can't be missed. They wrote my father's death before they committed it. All these years I thought I was hunting his killers. I was reading his obituary in advance. They're that thorough. They're that certain no one checks."

Teo broke the silence, because boys do, because someone has to. "So we check," he said. "We're checking. This is—it's everything, isn't it? Names, dates, the gas logs, the map, his account. It's enough?"

"It would be," Maren said, "if we lived long enough to publish it. Which brings me to the part I haven't said yet, and I'll say it quickly because Odile has been watching that window like it owes her money." She began wrapping the ledgers, swift and precise, her hands steadier now than they had been all evening—steadier, because purpose does that, because a thing shared is a thing that weighs less. "The night clerk at the telegraph office is paid to note who sends messages to Port Vessel. The assayer who signs off on incoming freight samples reports to the same man who reports to the district superintendent. Anji is a company town wearing a town's clothes. They knew I was pulling records months ago—they let me, because a thief in the dark leads you to the cache. I've been waiting for you not because I need an escort over the passes, Mr. Montrose, but because I needed to see who followed you in."

Odile turned from the window. "Lantern," she said. "Two of them. Coming up from the river road, walking easy, the way men walk when they're not in a hurry because they already know where they're going."

Maren snuffed the lamp. In the sudden dark, the ledgers felt warm against her chest where she pressed them, and the map case clicked shut between her father's key and Dominic Montrose's, and she found—to her own surprise—that she was not afraid. Fear was a country she had lived in for fifteen years. This was something else. This was the feeling of a half-truth, at last, becoming whole, and whole things, she had learned, were heavier than fear and harder to kill.

"Back door," she said. "There's a drainage cut behind the office that runs to the millrace. Stay low, stay silent, and follow me—and whatever happens, the ledgers and the map leave this town tonight, even if we don't."
