## 11. The Betrayal

Teo learned the waystation the way he learned everything now—by wire, or by what the wire left behind.

The place was called Kettle Rest, though no kettle had hung on its hook in years. It sat where the old portage road met the river again, a long low building of split timber with a stable that leaned like a drunk against its neighbor, and a telegraph shed out back whose line still hummed, faint and half-alive, running up toward Anji and the passes beyond. The syndicate had let the public traffic die but never cut the wire. Teo understood why the moment he saw it. A dead line told you nothing. A sleeping one told you everything.

He'd taken to walking the line each evening, pressing his ear to the porcelain insulators like a boy listening for bees in a log. Odile had laughed at him the first time—*you'll get a shock, little spark*—but she hadn't told him to stop. Nobody had told him to stop. That was the thing about these people, the thing he kept turning over in his chest like a river stone: nobody told him to stop. Dominic gave him rations to count and asked his opinion about them. Maren had started teaching him assay marks, tapping her pencil against the ledger and saying *look again* until he did. Even Odile, who trusted people the way cats trusted water, had begun leaving him alone with the boat's cash box without counting it after.

Four days ago he'd been a runaway with a stolen coat and a belly full of fear. Now he had a bedroll between two grown people who knew his name and used it like it belonged somewhere.

So when they pulled into Kettle Rest at dusk, rain coming down in gray ropes off the ridge, Teo went straight to the shed before he even ate. Habit. Duty. The small warm feeling of being useful to something larger than himself.

The line was quiet. But the pegboard inside wasn't.

Messages came through on paper tape, and the operator here—a one-eyed man named Brack who traded more than messages—kept his spools in a crate by the door, unsorted, because sorting cost effort and effort cost money. Teo knelt over the crate with a candle stub and went through the tape strip by strip, reading the punched holes the way other boys read print. Weather reports. Freight orders. A complaint about spoiled salt pork from some factor downriver.

Then his fingers stopped.

The holes were recent—the tape still smelled of machine oil, not mildew—and the routing code at the head was one he knew cold, because he'd copied it out a dozen times in his old life, sitting in the Anji relay office with his feet hooked on the chair rungs. Syndicate internal. Priority band. He read it twice, three times, his lips moving, translating the punch into letters into words:

*...courier party believed moving via Kettle Rest. Confirm occupancy. Harrow requests position marks along grade road. Compensation authorized per prior arrangement.*

Per prior arrangement.

Teo sat back on his heels. The candle guttered. Rain hammered the shingle roof and ran off in silver threads past the doorway, and somewhere across the yard Dominic's voice rose and fell as he argued with the stableman about dry feed for the mule.

*Confirm occupancy.* They were here. Anyone on the wire who cared to look would know they were here within the hour, unless—

Unless the message had come through already answered. Teo dug through the crate again, faster now, tape whispering through his fingers like sand. There. Outgoing spool, same priority band, sent that afternoon while they were still two miles downriver:

*Kettle Rest confirms four travelers, one mule. Route north via grade road expected at first light.*

His own heartbeat sounded loud in his ears, louder than the rain. Four travelers. One mule. Somebody in this yard had walked to this shed today, or paid Brack to walk for them, and described their party down to the animal.

He thought: Dominic wouldn't. He thought it instantly, completely, the way you know your own hand in the dark. Dominic had carried a dying man's promise through flood country with a gunboat on his wake; he didn't sell people. Maren—Maren wanted the syndicate bled dry; she'd sooner sell her own teeth. Which left—

No. No, that was stupid. That was the kind of thinking the syndicate wanted in a person, suspicion spread thin over everyone so it stuck to no one. Teo made himself breathe. Made himself think like an operator, not a scared kid. *Compensation authorized per prior arrangement.* Arrangement meant standing debt. Arrangement meant somebody owed somebody, and the syndicate always collected debts in the currency it liked best: obedience.

Who owed the syndicate?

He stood up too fast and knocked the candle, and flame licked the edge of the crate before he clapped it dead with his palm. The burn stung. He barely felt it. Because a memory had surfaced, ugly and bright: three nights ago, moored below Broken Narrows, he'd woken to low voices on deck and heard Odile talking to someone across the water—a trader in a skiff, lantern hooded—and he'd caught only fragments. *I know what I owe.* And then, softer, almost pleading: *Not them. Leave them out of it.*

He'd rolled over and gone back to sleep, telling himself it was cargo talk, pilot talk, the ordinary commerce of the river.

Now the fragments rearranged themselves into a sentence he couldn't unread.

* * *

He didn't confront her. He was fourteen, not foolish. Instead he spent the evening watching, and watching taught him things watching always teaches: that Odile checked the tree line every time she stepped out for wood, not casually, but with the flat deliberate sweep of someone confirming a sightline; that she'd argued—quietly, reasonably, reasonably being the strange part—for taking the grade road north instead of the high trail, when the high trail was harder ground and better cover; that she'd volunteered to take first watch and then taken second watch too, and slept badly through neither.

And that after midnight she took a lantern and walked, alone, toward the telegraph shed.

Teo was already there. He'd climbed in through the back window an hour earlier and sat in the dark among the smell of oil and mouse droppings, and when the door opened and lantern light swung across the room he pressed himself behind the tape crates, heart going like a telegraph key under his ribs.

Odile crossed to the key. She set the lantern down. She stood over it a long moment, hand resting on the brass knob, not sending—just touching it, the way you touch a dog you're about to have put down. Then she began to send. Short burst. Coordinates, probably; Teo knew rhythm well enough to hear structure even through a wall of his own blood. Thirty seconds, maybe less. She cut the circuit, killed the lantern, and stood in the dark a moment longer, and in the last of the light Teo saw her face, and it was the face of a woman at a graveside.

Then she left. Teo counted to two hundred before he moved.

He should have woken Dominic. Every sensible thread in him said so. But a colder thread said: say it wrong, and you lose her tonight, and whatever she is—traitor, debtor, prisoner—you lose the only person on this river who knows Harrow's water, Harrow's habits, the exact weight of the man's cruelty. And a stranger thread still, thinnest of all, said: *she said leave them out of it.* She had bargained. Whatever she'd been doing, she'd been doing it trying to keep *them* out of the sentence. That wasn't nothing. That might, Teo thought, be everything.

So he did the cowardly clever thing. He stayed awake, and he waited for morning, and he planned to tell Dominic on the move, privately, where a confession couldn't turn into a hanging argument in front of everyone.

Morning never got the chance.

* * *

They came at first light, out of the rain mist that lay along the tree line like breath on glass. Teo was in the yard saddling the mule when the first shot cracked—not aimed at anyone, just a voice, an announcement—and then men were simply *there*, six of them, seven, unfolding out of the treeline in oiled slickers, rifles already up, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where everyone would be standing.

Because they did know. Someone had told them to the yard, to the hour, to the mule.

"Down!" Dominic's roar, and then the world became noise and splinters. They scattered—Dominic hauling Teo by the collar behind the water trough, Maren sliding under the stable fence with her rifle already speaking, Odile vanishing toward the river side of the building with a pistol drawn that Teo had never once seen her carry.

Bullets chewed the trough rim an inch above Teo's scalp. He tasted mud and gun smoke and the copper of his own bitten cheek. Through the slats of the woodpile he could see the waystation burning already—someone had fired the telegraph shed, and the tape crates he'd crouched among all night were curling black, and every scrap of proof in them went up with a sound like applause.

"They knew," Maren shouted across the yard, reloading on her knees, hair plastered to her face. "Dom, they *knew*—nobody finds us this clean, nobody—"

"I know." Dominic's voice was terrible and calm. He put a fresh round in and fired, and one of the slickered shapes folded into the mud. "Kettle Rest! Around the back, go, go—"

They went. Behind the station the land dropped away in alder and rock toward the river shallows, and they crashed down it together, shots chasing them, kicking bark and spray. The mule bolted with half their food. Teo's boot found a wet root and the world spun and then Dominic's fist was in his jacket again, dragging him upright without breaking stride, and they were in the shallows and then the trees on the far bank, and the shooting fell behind them like weather passing.

They ran for an hour. Maybe more. When they finally stopped, in a hollow of mossed granite where the stream noise covered voices, they were all bleeding something—splinters, scrapes, Maren a furrow along her forearm she refused to discuss—and the rain had thickened into the fine cold drizzle that meant the season was turning, that meant snow, that meant everything Elias Wren had died warning them about.

Maren sat on a stone, shaking. Not from cold.

"Four travelers and a mule," she said. Her voice came out level, which was worse than screaming. "That's what the confirmation said. I read it upside down off Brack's desk while you were all eating. Four travelers and a mule, route north at first light." She looked up, and her eyes went around the circle and stopped. "Someone in this group has been writing our deaths down since before Broken Narrows."

The silence in the hollow had weight, like deep water.

"It isn't me," Maren said. "It isn't Dominic—he was the target before any of us existed to sell. The boy—" she glanced at Teo, and something in her face softened and hardened at once, "—the boy intercepted their orders. They want *him* found."

All eyes turned, then, the way eyes do, slow and reluctant as tectonic plates.

Odile stood apart, at the edge of the hollow, rain dripping off her jaw. She didn't flinch. She'd gone very still, the stillness of a woman standing on a rail bridge hearing the train come.

"Odile." Dominic said her name quietly. Not an accusation. Something worse—an opening. A door held open. "Tell me it isn't true."

Teo watched her hands. They were steady. He thought, absurdly, of how she'd carried him through the leech swamps at Broken Narrows, his ankle screaming, her breath ragged in his ear saying *hold on, little spark, hold on,* and how he'd fallen asleep against her shoulder on the Kestrel's deck and woken covered with her coat.

"The syndicate holds my paper," Odile said. Her voice was flat as the river in fog. "Every debt I ever ran up running their cargo—notes, judgments, a bond on my boat. Three weeks ago their agent bought the lot from the Port Vessel banks. All of it, called due at once, payable in one currency." She swallowed. "Reports. Positions. Names."

The rain filled the silence.

"You marked us," Maren said. She was on her feet now, and her hand was nowhere near her rifle, which somehow made it more frightening. "You sat at that key and you sent us north into their guns, and we've been sleeping next to you, and Teo—" her voice cracked, just once, "—Teo *carried your cash box*—"

"I know what he carried." For the first time Odile's steadiness broke, a seam of something raw showing through. "You think I don't lie awake doing that arithmetic? You think I chose this?" She took one step forward, into the circle, into the aim of four pairs of eyes. "Harrow burned a village for a strike I helped survey. Do you understand? Everything I am in debt for is built on top of thirty-one people I helped bury. And now they hold the paper on my soul too, and every night I send a message and every morning I pray it buys you one more day of open water, and I *hate*—" her voice tore clean through, "—I hate that it's the only prayer I've got left."

Nobody moved. The stream talked to itself over the stones. Somewhere upslope a bird made one small complaint and quit.

Teo found his voice before he'd decided to use it.

"She tried to keep us out of it." Everyone looked at him. He was crying, he realized distantly, rain or otherwise, and didn't care. "Below Broken Narrows. I heard her. She told the trader *not them, leave them out of it.* She's been paying them with lies about our route—wrong hours, wrong camps—buying time. Until Kettle Rest. Until someone else confirmed for her." His eyes went to Odile, searching. "Was it you at the key last night?"

She closed her eyes. "Yes."

"Coordinates?"

"Yes." A breath. "But not the ambush. I sent our position because Harrow's man already had it—I could feel it, the way you feel a hook set. If I'd gone silent, he'd have known I'd turned, and then nothing was protecting any of us. I sent it hoping—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "Hoping I could still steer between the rocks. I've been steering between rocks my whole life. Last night the channel closed."

Maren's hand had dropped away from the rifle entirely now. She looked older, suddenly, and exhausted, and like a woman doing arithmetic of her own. "The agent," she said slowly. "The one who bought your paper. What name?"

"Ferris. Works out of Anji."

Something moved through Maren's face like current under ice. "Ferris," she repeated. "I have payroll records with that name in them. He signed the casualty lists. He signed the lists that erased thirty-one men." She laughed, short and joyless. "Of course he did. Of course the same pen writes the debts and the dead."

Dominic had been silent through all of it, standing with the rain running off him, looking at Odile the way he must once have looked at survey numbers he'd trusted. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and rough.

"We're two days from the grade road. Harrow's between us and it, and now he knows exactly where we are, because the woman who knows this country best has been reporting to him." He held up a hand before Odile could speak. "I'm not casting you out. I'm telling you what's true. So here's my question, and everybody answers it, including you." He looked around the circle, and last of all at her. "What does Odile Ferrant know about Harrow that Harrow doesn't know she knows?"

Odile stared at him. And then, slowly, terribly, she began to smile—it was not a happy smile, it was the smile of a gambler finally allowed to show her cards.

"He thinks I'm still his," she said. "Which means he'll expect a report tonight. From the next settlement up the line." She glanced at the gray sky, at the direction of the pass, and her smile sharpened into something with edges. "And I know exactly what to tell him."

Teo looked around the hollow—at Maren's white furious face, at Dominic's steady one, at the woman who had sold them and saved them and was perhaps, if she was given the chance, about to do both at once—and felt the strange, painful thing that had been growing in his chest since Karawari Landing shift and settle into something with a decision inside it.

Trust, he understood suddenly, wasn't a thing you had or lost. It was a thing you sent, like a message down a wire, never knowing what answered—until it did.

Above them, far up the valley, the clouds had begun, very faintly, to snow.
