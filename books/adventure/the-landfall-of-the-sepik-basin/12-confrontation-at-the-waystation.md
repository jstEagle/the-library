## 12. Confrontation at the Waystation

The waystation had been built by a railroad that no longer existed, and it wore its abandonment like a man wears his own funeral clothes—everything fitted, everything wrong. Its walls were blackened timber gone silver at the seams, its roof sagged under a season of wet snowmelt it had never been shingled to carry, and the great iron stove in the center of the single room was cold because none of us dared smoke ourselves out to the hunters we knew were somewhere behind us on the switchbacks.

We had reached it at dusk, four travelers and one traitor, though only three of us knew the fifth word yet.

Teo had told me an hour before we arrived, his voice so low I had to bend my ear into the wind to catch it. We were walking drag, Maren and Odile ahead with the map case strapped between them like a coffin two pallbearers refuse to discuss, and the boy had fallen back beside me with his telegrapher's hands jammed deep in his pockets and his face the color of old tallow.

"She's been reporting," he said. "Since before Anji. Maybe since the river."

I made him say it again. Not because I hadn't heard—I had heard it the way you hear a bridge girder crack, once and completely—but because some part of me wanted the second reading to come out different.

"The syndicate bought her debts," Teo said. "Harrow's agent bought the paper on the *Kestrel*. Every payment she missed, every cargo she ran for them, all of it called due at once. She sends word ahead when we stop. She marks our route." His jaw worked. "I found the cipher strip in her coat while she was carrying me yesterday. After my ankle went. She thought I was asleep."

"And you waited until now to tell me?"

"I tried to tell you at Broken Narrows," he said, and there was such misery in it, such a boy's clean fury at being made the bearer of rot, "and then she carried me through the leech swamp on her back, and I couldn't—" He stopped. The wind came down off the summits and pushed against us like a hand flat on the chest. "I didn't know how to hate her, Mr. Montrose. That's the whole of it. I didn't know how."

I looked ahead at Odile Ferrant's broad shoulders and the set of her neck, the way she read the trail the way she read water, eyes always moving, always measuring—and I understood him completely. I had spent six years learning not to hate myself; I knew exactly how heavy a thing could get when you couldn't lift it all the way to hatred.

"Say nothing yet," I told him. "Not until we're behind walls. Not until I've thought."

He nodded, and I watched him carry the secret the rest of the way up the trail like a load of rail iron, and I hated myself a little for handing it to him.

* * *

The waystation room was thirty feet by twenty, with bunks along one wall and a freight counter along the other, and a window at each gable end shuttered with planks that had warped out of true. Maren got a fire going small and shielded, the way Elias Wren must have taught her a hundred camps ago, feeding it dry pine from her pack rather than the damp cordwood stacked outside where smoke would show. Odile checked the shutters. Teo sat on a bunk with his splinted ankle propped on a rolled blanket and did not look at anyone.

I stood by the cold stove and felt the room arrange itself around what I knew.

It is a strange arithmetic, suspicion. Before Teo spoke, the four of us had been a unit of trust with hairline fractures—Maren's grief, my shame, Odile's past, the boy's fear. After he spoke, every fracture became a fault line, and I could feel the ground shifting under all of us, quiet as snow loading a roof.

Odile came back from the north window and caught me watching her.

"You've got a face on you, engineer," she said. Lightly. Too lightly. Her hand rested on the counter's edge, and I saw the knuckles whiten, just slightly, the way they whitened when the *Kestrel* took a swell wrong.

"Do I."

"You do." She looked around the room—at Maren crouched at the fire, at Teo very carefully studying the ceiling—and whatever she saw in our faces, she read it the way she read water, fast and without flinching. And then, to my lasting astonishment, she laughed. One short bark of a sound with no humor in it at all. "Well. Hell."

She crossed to the middle of the room, put her back to the fire so her shadow fell long across all of us, and said: "Ask me."

Nobody moved.

"I said ask me." Her voice cracked out like a whip over river ice. "Because I can see it in your faces, and I have been carrying this thing up this mountain for nine days waiting for one of you to ask, and I am done carrying it. Ask me what I did."

Maren rose from the fire. She rose slowly, which was worse than if she'd risen fast, and her hand was nowhere near the pistol at her hip, which was worst of all, because I could see she was thinking about it—thinking about it the way a surveyor thinks about grade, calmly, professionally, measuring whether the line could be run.

"Teo," Maren said quietly. "Tell me what you found."

And the boy, God help him, told her. He laid it out plain, the cipher strip, the dates, the reports keyed to Harrow's agent in Anji, the debt papers bought and called due. His voice shook twice and steadied itself both times, and I thought: whatever else becomes of this child, he will never be afraid of a hard sentence again.

When he finished, the fire popped. Somewhere above us, snow slid off the roof and hit the porch boards with a soft dead weight.

"Maren," Odile began.

"Don't." Maren's hand was on the pistol now. Not drawing it. Resting. "My father is dead on a floor in Karawari Landing because men followed a route someone marked for them. Elias Wren walked into that town with a map and a promise and no idea that the woman piloting the boat that carried the courier had already sold the courier's heading downstream." Her voice stayed level, and that levelness was more frightening than screaming. "So don't say my name. Not yet."

"He was alive when we left the Landing," Odile said. "You know that. Whatever Harrow's clerk did—"

"He was dying when we left, and he died because of that map, and every mile we've climbed since has been paid for with blood that traces back to someone keeping Harrow informed." Maren's thumb moved on the hammer. A small motion. A surveyor's motion. "How long."

"Since the river," Odile said. "Before the gunboat. Before any of you knew there was a gunboat to fear."

"How long, Odile."

"From the first night." Odile's chin came up. I had seen her take waves that would fold a steam launch, seen her run the Kestrel through fog so thick the banks were rumors, and I had never seen fear on her—not once. I saw it now. It looked nothing like I'd imagined. It looked like pride holding a door shut against a flood. "The night you came aboard, engineer, I sent word from the Landing telegraph office. *Courier moving, four souls, Kestrel.* That was the first. There were eleven after it."

"Eleven," Maren whispered.

"Eleven." Odile spread her hands. "And here is the rest of it, since we're doing inventory. I ran syndicate cargo for six years. Contraband ore, mostly, and worse things I won't name in front of the boy. I surveyed the strike at Yalu Bend—the one Harrow burned the village over. My charts are the reason his gunboat knew those channels better than the navy does. Everything I am, everything I know how to do, I learned in their service, and when I tried to walk away they held my paper over my head, and when the paper wasn't enough anymore they held something else." Her voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture across all that pride. "They held the memory of Yalu Bend. They said: you owe us for the village too. You lit the lamp on that reef, pilot. So row."

The room was absolutely still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"So you rowed," I said.

"So I rowed." She turned to me, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Every report, every mark on the map, every time I led you toward a narrows or away from one, I was choosing which lie to tell them. Do you understand? Half the time I lied to *them*. I gave them headings that were true but useless. I told them you'd taken the south channel when we took the north. I kept Teo's name out of every report—he doesn't exist in their files, do you hear me? As far as Harrow knows, there are three of you." She was shaking now, and letting herself shake, spending it rather than hiding it. "But the last three reports were true, because the agent stopped believing my lies after Anji, and he made that clear in terms I believed. So yes. I marked this route. Yes. Harrow knows we're in this valley tonight. And yes, I am the reason your father's grave will be dug twice, Maren Wren, and I will carry that until I die, and I am asking you—" her voice broke clean through, "—I am asking you not to make me carry it alone."

Maren drew the pistol.

She didn't point it. She drew it the way a person draws breath before saying a thing they cannot unsay, and the sound of the leather was very loud, and Teo came up off the bunk onto his good foot with a cry he strangled halfway born, and I stepped between them—not bravely, not nobly, just the way a body moves when it sees a rockslide start.

"Maren."

"Move, Dominic."

"No."

"*Move.*"

"Shoot me first, then," I said, "because I'm standing in front of her, and I'm telling you the truth, and if truth is worth killing for in this room then we are already the syndicate and just haven't filed the paperwork."

That stopped her. Not the words themselves—I don't flatter myself the words were anything but a wall thrown up in front of a flood—but the shape of them. The shape of her father's argument. I watched it land on her like weather.

She kept the pistol in her hand. But the muzzle drifted toward the floorboards, degree by degree, like a compass needle finding north.

* * *

We talked until the fire burned down to coals, and what we talked was the whole ugly ledger, aloud, all four of us, because I refused—I planted my feet on that rotten floor and refused—to let us vote on Odile's exile or her death or her penance until every card was face-up on the counter.

So I went first. Not all of it—not Tunnel Nine, not yet, that confession was still coiled in my chest like a spring under load—but enough. I told them I had been a surveyor for the syndicate before I was a fugitive from it. I told them my name was on documents in their vaults, documents with numbers I had signed because the company's figures said the mountain could bear it. I told them I had spent six years under a false name repairing engines in a mud town because I could not bear to look at my own signature.

"You want to talk about betrayal?" I said. "I betrayed thirty-one men I never met, on paper, in ink, in a warm office, and I didn't even know their names. Odile betrayed us for nine days under a knife at her throat. Which of us does the mountain forgive first?"

"That's not the same," Maren said.

"No," I agreed. "Mine might be worse. At least she fought."

Maren turned on me with something blazing in her face, and I let her turn, because she needed to aim it somewhere and I was the safest target in the room.

"You defend her. You stand in front of her and make speeches, and meanwhile Harrow is on our trail *because of her*, and my father is in the ground *because of people like her*, people who worked for them and told themselves they had no choice—"

"Yes," I said. "People like her. People like me. Maren—listen to me. Listen." I crossed to her and took her by the shoulders, and she let me, trembling with fury, the pistol hanging forgotten at her side. "Your father gave me a map and a promise and died for it, and I have asked myself every night since what he actually bought with that promise. It wasn't delivery. A map can be copied; a courier can be replaced. What Elias Wren bought was a witness. Someone who would see what's buried in these passes and *refuse to look away*. And here is the thing I have learned at terrible cost, standing in front of a girl with a gun in a ruined waystation: loyalty without honesty is exactly what the syndicate sells. It's their whole product. Men who will follow a captain into fire and never once ask what's in the wagons. Crews who keep silent because silence is solidarity. That's what they call loyalty. It isn't. It's a cage with the door painted on. Your father didn't die for painted doors. He died trying to open one."

The coals ticked in the stove. Teo had lowered himself back to the bunk, and I saw that he was crying without any noise at all, the way boys cry when they've decided nobody gets to see it.

Maren looked at Odile for a long time. Odile took it. She stood with her arms at her sides and her wet eyes open and she took every second of it, and I understood that this, too, was a kind of navigation—reading the current, holding the helm, refusing to run for cover even with the reef dead ahead.

"If you ever send another word downstream," Maren said at last, very quietly, "true or false, useful or useless—if you so much as *think* in cipher—I will not shoot you. I'll give you to Harrow with a note explaining what you are. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Odile said.

"And when this is over—when the map is delivered or we're dead on the pass—you and I are going to settle what you owe my father. Not tonight. Not with bullets. But settled."

"Settled," Odile said again, and something went out of her shoulders, some tension that had been living in them for six years, and I thought: that is what honesty costs, and that is what it pays.

Maren holstered the pistol. Then she did the last thing any of us expected. She picked up the coffee pot, filled the tin cup nearest Odile, and handed it to her without a word—an act so ordinary and so enormous that Odile's hand shook taking it, and hot coffee slopped over her fingers, and she drank it anyway, scalded, blinking hard.

Teo wiped his face with his sleeve and said, in a voice still thick, "For what it's worth, I'm glad nobody shot anybody. I only know how to splice wire, and I'd hate to practice on people."

A laugh went around the room—small, broken, real. The first one since Broken Narrows. It did what laughter does in cold places: it made a pocket of warmth just big enough to sit in.

* * *

It was near midnight when I took the last watch by the north shutter, and near one when I heard the step behind me and knew it was Odile by its weight—she walked like a woman who had spent her life compensating for a deck's motion, rolling slightly even on solid ground.

"Engineer." She stood beside me at the gap between the warped planks, looking out at the valley below, where the moon had come up on new snow and turned the switchbacks into a silver ladder climbing toward the pass. "Why did you stand in front of me? You knew what I was. Teo told you on the trail. You had hours to decide, and you chose the one spot in the room where a bullet meant for me would find you first."

I considered lying to her, briefly, the way you consider a shortcut across bad ice.

"Because I've been where you're standing," I said. "On the far side of a confession, waiting to find out if the truth buys exile or a place at the fire. And I know that most people, given the choice, choose the cage. Painted door, warm inside, everyone agreeing not to mention it. Your syndicate runs on it. Mine did." I turned to look at her. In the coal-light her face was all planes and scars and exhaustion. "You opened the door yourself, Odile. Nobody pried it. That's rarer than courage. Courage is common on a river. Honesty isn't."

She was quiet a while. Below us, far down the valley, a light moved—a tiny orange spark threading the switchbacks, patient as a lighthouse walking uphill.

Then a second light joined it.

Then a third.

Odile's breath went out slow through her teeth. "They're not hurrying," she said.

"No," I said. "They think the storm will do their work for them. Harrow's plan was always the mountain." I touched her arm. "Wake the others. We climb tonight, ahead of the snow—or we learn what kind of family we really are, right here, in the dark."

Down in the valley, the lights kept coming.
