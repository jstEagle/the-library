## 13. The Ascent Begins

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years learning how to leave places, and only three days learning what it cost to be allowed to stay.

The waystation at Anji's edge sat in the gray hour before dawn, and she stood on its porch with her pack already shouldered, watching the summits north of town catch the first light. The peaks were white. Not the dirty white of river fog or the chalky smear of a late-season squall—this was clean snow, the kind that came to stay, that fell with the patience of something that had all winter ahead of it and knew it.

*First snows,* Elias Wren had said, if Teo's transcription of his dying words could be trusted. *Before the snows seal the passes.*

They had made it to the foothills with a day to spare, maybe two. It would have to be enough.

Behind her, through the thin wall of the waystation, she heard the others stirring: Maren's boots hitting the floorboards with the crisp economy of a woman who had been awake for an hour already; Dominic moving slower, the way he always did, as though he were listening to the building before trusting it to hold him; Teo's cough, still loose and wet at the bottom of his lungs but no longer the dry rattle that had frightened her on the second night out of Broken Narrows. The boy was healing. They all were, in the ways that didn't show.

She had not slept. She had sat up instead with her back against the doorpost and her rifle across her knees, keeping the watch she had assigned herself without asking anyone's permission, because asking permission implied she had a right to be refused, and she wasn't sure yet that she did.

Three days since the waystation ambush. Three days since Teo had laid the telegraph flimsies on the table between them like a man laying out cards he already knew were losing, and read aloud the ledger of her debts, bought and held by men who collected in favors. Three days since she had confessed everything—the cargo runs, the surveys, the village called Tumun whose name she had never once said aloud in eleven years—and Maren Wren had leveled a pistol at her chest with hands that shook and eyes that didn't.

And Dominic Montrose had stood between them and said the thing she kept turning over now like a stone in her pocket:

*Loyalty without honesty is just another cage. That's the syndicate's whole business model. Don't buy from them.*

He hadn't forgiven her. She'd seen that plainly enough—he was a man who understood that forgiveness was a currency you couldn't counterfeit. But he had kept her on, and keeping her on meant one thing in this country: she walked point. She led them into the sealed country, up the closed grade, through the passes the syndicate had bought and blasted and erased from every map but one.

Penance, she thought, testing the word the way you'd test a rope before trusting your weight to it. Penance wasn't kneeling. Penance was forward motion. Her grandmother on the coast had told her that once, mending nets by lamplight—a knot doesn't undo itself by being looked at; you work it loose with your fingers, strand by strand, and your hands bleed a little, and then the net holds again.

Her hands were going to bleed. She had accepted that somewhere in the dark hours, and it had let her sleep for perhaps forty minutes, and those forty minutes had been the best rest she'd had since the betrayal.

The door opened behind her. Dominic came out with two tin cups and handed her one without a word. Coffee, black as river mud, hot enough to scald. She drank it anyway.

"You've been up all night," he said.

"I've been up worse nights."

"That's not an argument against sleeping."

"No," she agreed. "It's an argument against pretending I'm someone who sleeps."

He almost smiled. He had a face built for almost-smiling, she'd noticed—all the machinery of humor present and accounted for, held back by some governor inside him that had been installed years ago and never serviced. In another life, she thought, he'd have been easy company. In this one he was better than easy: he was reliable, which was rarer and worth more.

"The grade starts above the old mill site," she said. "Two miles of switchbacks through the timber cut, then it climbs open country to the first tunnel. If the syndicate sealed the line the way Elias's map says they did, there'll be a gate at the tunnel mouth. Chain, probably. Maybe a watchman."

"And if there's a watchman?"

"Then we'll know Harrow hasn't gotten ahead of us." She turned the cup in her hands. "If there's no watchman—if the gate's been cut, or the chain's fresh-broken—I'd worry."

Dominic looked north at the white summits. "You know this country," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I ran cargo up the Anji branch for four seasons. Before they closed it." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice of testimony. "Timber, mostly, coming down. Going up it was blasting powder, lamp oil, foodstuffs for the camps." A pause she didn't want but took anyway. "Men, sometimes. Contract laborers the syndicate didn't want passing through any town with a newspaper."

Dominic said nothing. That was his gift, she was beginning to understand—he let silence do the work that other men filled with questions, and the silence asked harder questions than words would have.

"There were thirty-one of them," she said. "That's what the map says. I didn't know the number then. I don't think anyone did except the men who signed the papers." She looked at him. "Did you sign papers, Dominic?"

His jaw worked once. "Not yet," he said. "But I will. Up there, where the numbers came from." He drained his coffee. "One confession at a time. You went first."

* * *

The old mill site announced itself by smell before sight—creosote and rot and the sweet mushroom reek of timber giving itself back to the forest. The syndicate had abandoned the mill eight years ago when the branch line closed, and the forest had treated the abandonment as an invitation. Ferns grew through the loading dock. A young banyan had shouldered up through the sawpit and spread its roots across the foundation stones like fingers laced over a dead man's chest.

Odile led them along the millrace, where the footing was gravel and the sound of water covered their passage. Behind her came Maren, steady as a metronome; then Teo, favoring his healed-but-stiff ankle and refusing with tremendous dignity to admit it; then Dominic, carrying the map case strapped against his spine beneath his coat, where a knife would go.

The rail grade began where the timber cut ended, and Odile felt it under her boots before she saw it—the graded roadbed running straight and true through the trees, ballast stone still firm after eight years of neglect, the ties silvered and split but holding. There was something about a railroad grade even dead, even ruined. It had a direction. It had intent. Walking it felt like walking through somebody's unfinished sentence.

"They built this in fourteen months," she said over her shoulder. "Winter crews. The syndicate wanted ore moving before the government survey office got around to inspecting the route." She kicked a tie. "That's why the numbers were wrong. That's why everything up here is wrong. They were building faster than the truth could travel."

"Is that an excuse?" Maren asked. Her voice wasn't hostile. It was worse—it was genuinely curious, a daughter of an engineer asking an engineer's question.

"It's an explanation," Odile said. "Excuses are what you make afterward. Explanations are what you owe beforehand, and nobody up this mountain paid theirs."

The grade climbed. By midmorning the timber thinned and the world opened onto scrub slopes and granite ribs, and the Kestrel's river valley fell away below them, a green thread stitching down toward a brown ribbon of water so distant it looked painted on. Odile set a pace she hated—slow, deliberate, the pace of a woman leading people she could not afford to lose—but she set it without apology, and to their credit none of them pushed it.

Teo caught up to walk beside her in the early afternoon. The boy moved like a heron, all angles and attention, and he carried the portable telegraph key wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way other children carried talismans.

"Odile." He said her name carefully, the way you'd test ice. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything. Whether I answer is a separate negotiation."

"When you ran cargo for them. Did you ever—" He stopped. Started again. "My father worked a dredge on the Sepik. Company contract. He used to say the company was like weather—you didn't blame the rain, you just built a roof." His jaw tightened. "I used to believe that. Before the wire room. Before I read what they write to each other when they think nobody's listening."

"What's your question, Teo?"

"Did you build a roof? Or did you tell yourself the rain was somebody else's problem?"

Odile walked twenty paces before she answered. Below them the valley turned gold-green in the slanting light; above them the snowline gleamed like a promise neither mercy nor threat, just fact.

"Both," she said finally. "For years, both. And then one season the rain followed me inland, and I watched it fall on a village that had never built anything but fish racks and garden fences, and I learned that weather doesn't stay in its own country. Not when there are men selling it passage." She glanced down at him. "Your father was wrong, and he was also wise, and both things are true, and someday you'll be old enough to hold them together without dropping either one."

Teo considered this with the ferocious seriousness of fourteen. "Maren says you're buying your way back," he said. "Carrying us up the mountain. Like fare."

"Maren says a lot of true things."

"Is that one?"

Odile looked up the grade, at the switchbacks stacked above them like the vertebrae of some enormous buried animal, climbing toward the first tunnel's black mouth, invisible from here but known to her, remembered in her feet.

"No," she said. "Fare gets you a seat. This—" she gestured at the whole climbing world, the grade, the snow, the thirty-one names waiting somewhere in the rock ahead of them— "this isn't a seat. It's the boat. You don't pay for the boat. You row it."

* * *

They reached the first tunnel at dusk.

It was exactly as she remembered and nothing like she remembered, which was the trick of returning to places you'd helped ruin. The portal stood in a cutting of raw granite, the arch framed in creosoted timbers gone gray as bone. And across the mouth, just as she'd predicted, hung a gate: two steel posts sunk in concrete, a chain thick as her wrist strung between them, padlocked, furred with eight years of rust.

No watchman. No camp ash, no tracks in the dust of the approach except the small busy script of birds. The syndicate had sealed this line and then, confident in distance and winter and the short memories of the living, had simply stopped looking at it.

"Fresh-broken?" Dominic asked quietly, reading her earlier warning back to her.

She knelt at the chain and examined it. Rust had welded link to link; the padlock's shackle was scaled orange; the ground beneath showed no disturbance newer than a decade. "Old iron," she said. "Nobody's been through here in years. We're ahead of Harrow." She stood, and something in her chest loosened half a turn—not relief exactly, more like the moment when a headwind drops and you realize how hard you'd been leaning into it.

Maren had her kit out already, working on the padlock with a file and a length of hacksaw blade. "Bolt cutters would be faster."

"Bolt cutters weigh nine pounds," Odile said. "We're not carrying nine pounds of bolt cutters up a mountain to cut one lock we'll never need to cut again."

"Efficient," Maren murmured, and there was—Odile catalogued it carefully, suspiciously, hopefully—the ghost of approval in it.

The lock surrendered in twenty minutes. The chain came down with a sound like a struck bell, ringing off the granite walls of the cutting and rolling away downhill, and all four of them stood very still for a long moment, listening to whether the mountains had heard.

Only wind answered. Wind, and somewhere far below, the last birdsong of evening.

Inside, the tunnel breathed cold air at them, mineral and wet, with a faint undertone of old smoke that had soaked into the stone and never left. Odile lit a lantern and held it high. The bore ran straight into the heart of the range, rails still in place, glinting dull silver in the light. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of geology.

"Elias's map marks this as Tunnel Seven," she said. "There are nine on the original survey. The upper two were never finished—they're the ones that matter." She looked back at the others, their faces floating in lantern-light, and heard herself say the next part steadily, because steadiness was the only thing she had left to give them. "Tomorrow we climb past Seven and Eight to Nine. That's where the seam blew. That's where they're buried."

Nobody spoke. Then Dominic stepped past her into the tunnel mouth, laid his palm flat against the cold rail, and stood there a moment like a man taking a pulse.

"Thirty-one," he said softly. "We'll want their names by tomorrow night. Teo—how many pages did Elias's case hold?"

"Forty-some. Why?"

"Because a number is an indictment," Dominic said. "Names are a verdict. And I intend to see this one delivered before the snow closes over us."

Above the cutting, unseen in the gathering dark, the first flakes of the season's real snow had begun to come down—fine and dry and silent, settling on the rails, on the rusted chain, on the shoulders of four small figures at the mouth of the mountain, falling with the patience of something that had all winter ahead of it.

And far below and behind, down in the valley they had crossed that day, a point of lamplight moved along the river road where no traveler had any honest business being—moving north, unhurried, keeping pace with the storm.
