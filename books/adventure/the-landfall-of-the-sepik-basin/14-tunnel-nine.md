## 14. Tunnel Nine

The grade climbed out of the river country like a scar climbing a man's back—straight where nothing in the Sepik Basin was straight, cut and benched and ballasted by men who had believed the mountain could be argued with. Odile led them along it at first light, moving with the certainty of someone walking a road she'd helped build in another life, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline they stood before the mouth of Tunnel Nine.

Or what had been made to look like the mouth of Tunnel Nine.

Dominic knew collapse when he saw it. He had spent eleven years of his life reading rock the way other men read weather, and he knew the difference between a mountain that had failed on its own terms and one that had been *helped*. This was not help. This was surgery. The portal was choked with rubble, yes—but the rubble was too uniform, too well-sorted, too neatly feathered back into the talus slope above it. Someone had brought down the face deliberately, then spent days dressing the wreckage so that any surveyor riding the grade would see an ordinary failure, log it, and ride on.

"Thirty years of rain hasn't moved that," Maren said quietly. She crouched at the base of the pile, her gloved hand hovering over a block of shot granite still wearing its drill scars in clean parallel rows. "Rain moves everything else in this basin. It hasn't touched this."

"Because it's packed from inside," Dominic said. His voice came out flat, and he heard in it the voice of the man he used to be—the engineer who signed things. "You blast a face, you muck it into the tunnel behind it, you pack it tight so there's no void to settle. Then you dress the outside." He swallowed. "It's expensive work. Nobody pays for expensive work unless they're buying silence."

Teo stood a little apart, holding the map case against his chest like something warm he was afraid of losing. The boy had been quiet since the pass, quieter even than his usual watchful quiet, and now he said, "Elias marked it. Right here." He turned the case so the lamplight caught the wax-pencil annotation beneath the tunnel's symbol: a number, written small and pressed hard enough to tear the paper. *31.*

Not a distance. Not an elevation. A count.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. Above them the first snows of the season clung to the high shoulders of the range, thin as flour sifted over iron, and the wind came down the grade carrying the smell of stone and cold and old timber rot.

"We can't go through," Odile said at last. "Even if the bore were open, we couldn't. But—" She looked at Dominic, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen there before: not guilt exactly, and not apology, but the particular steadiness of a woman offering him a door she knew he had to walk through alone. "There'll be a ventilation adit. Every deep tunnel on these lines has one. A service heading, driven up from below or sideways off the grade, for air and drainage and hauling muck. They wouldn't have bothered sealing it pretty. Nobody rides the ventilation shaft."

She was already moving along the downhill edge of the bench, reading the slope the way she read water—for the thing underneath the thing. It took her less than twenty minutes. Below and east of the portal, half-swallowed by alder scrub and a generation of slide alder and devil's club, a timbered opening leaned out of the hillside like a drunk against a wall.

The adit was maybe five feet high, cribbed with timbers gone silver-grey and soft at the corners, and from its black throat came a smell that stopped Dominic with his boot on the threshold.

Not rot. He knew rot. This was older than rot and colder—stone dust and standing water and the faint, sweet-chemical ghost of blasting powder that had hung in dead air for years because there was no wind to carry it away. And under all of it, so faint he might have invented it, something organic. Something human.

"You don't have to go in," Maren said beside him. She had come up without him hearing her, which frightened him more than the dark did.

"Yes, I do." He got his lamp lit with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. "I drafted the survey that put this tunnel here. Whatever's in there, I signed my name next to it."

* * *

The adit ran level for forty feet, then dog-legged, then began to rise—a gentle service incline, graded just steep enough for a mule-drawn muck car, and Dominic's boots remembered the pitch of such grades before his mind did. The timbers overhead were sound. That was the first horror, though he didn't understand it yet: whoever sealed the main bore had left the ventilation heading intact, propped and maintained, right up until the day they walked away from the whole line forever. As if part of them had meant to come back.

The air grew warmer as they climbed. That was the second horror, and this one he understood immediately, and understanding it was like swallowing a stone.

A sealed tunnel should be cold. Dead rock holds the temperature of the winter above it. But this air moved—barely, sluggishly, a breath rather than a draft—and it was warm the way deep workings are warm, the way ground is warm where men have lived in it, cooked in it, sweated in it. The mountain was exhaling the memory of bodies.

"How far up?" Maren asked. Her voice had gone very controlled, the voice of a woman holding herself together with rivets.

"The adit should break into the main bore near the face," Dominic said. "Service headings always do. Maybe three hundred yards."

They went on. Teo had insisted on coming, and none of them had found the heart or the authority to order him back; he carried the spare lamp low and kept his free hand on the timber, and his breathing was the loudest thing in the world. Water dripped somewhere ahead with a patience like time itself. Once, a timber ticked as it took load, and all four of them froze, and the mountain said nothing more.

Then the incline leveled, the roof opened, and their lamps fell upon the face of Tunnel Nine.

Dominic had imagined many versions of it in the hours since Elias Wren died in his shed with a map case pressed into his hands. He had imagined rubble. He had imagined a clean bore, empty, its sins legible only in paperwork. He had imagined—God forgive him—he had imagined almost nothing at all, because some part of him had spent eleven years perfecting the art of imagining almost nothing at all.

He had not imagined a camp.

The muck train stood on the incline landing where its crew had parked it: six cars, the last one still heaped with broken granite, the others empty and waiting for a load that never came. Tools lay where they'd been set down—drill steels racked with a neatness that broke his heart, shovels leaning in a corner the way working men lean their shovels, a lunch pail sitting open on a car bed with a tin cup beside it. Blankets. A cook fire ringed with stones, long cold, but swept. A crate of candles, half full. A deck of cards spread face-down across an upturned powder keg, mid-game, abandoned mid-game, as if the players had only stepped away to check the fuse.

And on the far wall of the bore, where the live seam crossed the tunnel like a vein of lightning frozen in the rock, the syndicate's crime gleamed wet in the lamplight.

Quartz. A ribbon of white quartz and sulphide running diagonally through the granite, wide as a barrel in places, glittering with ore that had never been reported, never been assayed on any book Dominic had ever seen or signed. The face around it was shattered outward—not drilled and blasted in the careful pattern of development mining but blown, hammered, torn open in greed and haste. They had hit the seam in the drive, realized what it was worth, and instead of reporting it, instead of surveying it, instead of doing the one honest thing that would have cost them months and millions—

They had decided the seam was worth more than the men who found it.

"They weren't killed by the blast," Maren said. She had gone to the far wall, and her lamp was steady, and her voice was not. "Look at the dust. Look at—" She stopped. She was an assay woman; she had spent her life reading rock, and she read it now, and what it told her she could not finish saying aloud.

But Dominic could finish it, because he had been a railroad engineer, and engineers are taught how tunnels kill.

The seam had run through weak, fractured ground—sulphide alteration, rotten rock, the kind of ground that stands only while you leave it standing. Blowing the face open to strip the quartz had unloaded the back. Any competent miner would have known. Any competent engineer would have known. Which meant the men who ordered the stripping had known too, and had done it anyway, and had timed it for the shift change or the end of the week or whatever hour would find the most men underground, and then they had fired the main charge from the portal side and brought thirty-one men's world down on top of them while they stood in the light of their own strike.

And then, because a seam that rich could not be abandoned, could not be left for claim jumpers or inspectors or the slow honest daylight of a reopened tunnel—they had packed the bore shut behind them and written the whole thing off the books. Not as a disaster. As a *survey casualty*. A line item. Thirty-one names reduced to a rounding error in a falsified cross-section, and Dominic Montrose had drawn that cross-section with his own hand, believing the numbers they fed him, believing the ground reports, believing because believing was easier and believing was paid.

He sat down on the rail. He did not decide to; his legs simply ended their argument with him. The lamp trembled in his grip and threw his shadow huge and broken against the timber.

"Dominic." Maren's hand on his shoulder. "Breathe."

"There were thirty-one of them." His voice sounded like someone else's, gravel-deep and far away. "They came down here in the morning. Some of them complained about the pay. Some of them had letters home in their pockets. One of them was winning at cards." He looked at the spread deck on the powder keg, and something in his chest tore along a seam of its own. "They found the richest strike in the basin, and it killed them, and the men who profited wrote *unfavorable ground conditions* in a report I typed."

Teo had drifted along the landing, and now the boy made a sound—not a word, just a sound, small and strangled—and held his lamp close to the tunnel wall near the cook fire.

Names. Dozens of them, scratched and penciled and chalked into the timber lagging and the soft altered rock beside it, the way miners everywhere sign the walls of their workings: *here I am, I was here, remember me.* Some were initials only. Some were full names with dates. One hand had written, in careful schoolroom letters, *J. OKONKWO — PAYDAY TOMORROW.* Another, smaller, shakier: *tell rosa i tried.* And near them, lower, half-hidden behind a timber butt, a longer inscription that someone had taken time over, cutting each letter with a knife:

*IF ANY MAN READS THIS, WE DID NOT DIE OF BAD GROUND. THE GROUND WAS GOOD. THE GROUND WAS THE BEST ANY OF US EVER CUT. REMEMBER THAT IT WAS NOT THE MOUNTAIN.*

Below the words, a tally. Careful rows of four strokes and a fifth crossing them, counted and recounted, grouped in threes and tens, adding up with terrible bookkeeping precision to thirty-one.

"They knew," Teo whispered. "At the end. They knew what happened to them, and they knew why, and they—" The boy's voice cracked clean through. Fourteen years old, a runaway who had intercepted a wire ordering a stranger's death, and he stood in the dark with his lamp shaking and wept for men dead eleven years, and Dominic thought: *this is the only honest mourning this tunnel has ever received.*

Maren knelt and copied every name into her notebook, her pencil scratching in the silence like a small persistent animal. She worked methodically, top to bottom, refusing to hurry, and when she finished she read the list aloud—all thirty-one, each name given its full weight, its full syllables, spoken into the air of the place where those syllables had last been spoken by living mouths. It was a funeral. It was the funeral. Eleven years late and attended by four strangers and two lamps, and it was more than the men had ever gotten from the company that owned their lives and erased their deaths.

When she finished, the silence had changed quality. Dominic could not have said how. But it felt less like emptiness now, and more like attention.

"We take the list out," Maren said. She closed the notebook the way one closes a Bible. "Every name. To Port Vessel. To the gazette, to the magistrate, to every wire office between here and the coast if that's what it takes. My father didn't die carrying a map of track beds. He died carrying *them*." She touched the wall, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping man's shoulder. "They've waited long enough."

Odile spoke from the landing, where she had kept watch on the incline with her rifle across her knees. Her voice was rough. "We should move. We've burned an hour, and Harrow isn't a patient man, and this hole is the one place on the mountain he cannot afford to let us be seen leaving."

"She's right," Dominic said, and found that standing required a bargain with his body, and made it.

But at the mouth of the adit, pausing with his lamp raised for one last look back down the incline—at the parked muck cars, the open lunch pail, the card game frozen mid-hand, the wall of names catching the light like a field of struck matches—Dominic saw what he had missed, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the mountain.

Fresh air. Moving from the direction of the main bore—or what should have been the main bore, packed solid with dressed rubble eleven years ago. A thread of it, thin and cold and smelling of snow, threading through the sealed face like smoke through a keyhole.

Someone had opened Tunnel Nine. Recently. From the other side.

And below them, far down the grade they had climbed that morning, faint but unmistakable on the still air, came the sound of many boots marching in step.
