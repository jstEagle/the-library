## 15. Harrow's Trail

Maren Wren had learned to read country the way her father taught her to read ore: not by what it showed you, but by what it refused to show. A seam of quartz could lie about the gold beneath it. A switchback cut into a mountainside could lie about the men cutting it. And a silence on a slope could be the loudest thing in the world.

She heard that silence now, two days out from Tunnel Nine, as the party climbed the dead rail grade into the high passes. The mountain was talking to itself—scree settling, meltwater ticking under stone, wind combing the stunted pines—but underneath all of it was a second sound, or rather the absence of one. No birdsong above them. No pikas whistling from the talus fields. The animals had gone quiet in the way they went quiet when something with too many boots was moving through their country.

"They're behind us," she said to Odile, who stood at the edge of the grade with a hand shading her eyes, looking back down the line they'd climbed since dawn.

"I know."

"You can't know."

"Third day running the birds have shut up below us." Odile spat over the edge, a habit Maren found both vulgar and oddly steadying. "Birds don't care about weather this far up. They care about people. And somebody down there is making enough noise to empty a valley."

Maren looked back along the grade. It descended in long switchbacks through the defile called the Sawteeth, each turn of the old track bed visible as a pale scar across the gray-green flank of the mountain, like the rings of a felled tree. From here she could count six of those scars before the grade dropped into shadow and cloud. Somewhere below the sixth—or below the twelfth, or the twentieth—Harrow's column was climbing. Teo's wire intercepts had given them the man's name weeks ago; the waystation ambush had given them his patience. He did not travel fast. He traveled *certainly*, the way water travels toward a drain.

"How many?" Dominic asked, coming up the grade with the map case slung across his chest on a strap of braided rawhide. Elias's case. Maren still caught herself staring at it sometimes—the worn leather, the brass buckles her father's hands had polished without ever knowing he was polishing his own coffin lid.

"Twenty, maybe twenty-five," Odile said. "Enough to take us if we're stupid. Not enough to take us if we're smart."

"And if he doesn't want to take us?" Dominic's voice was flat. "At Tunnel Nine you saw what he did to his own wounded. He left two men in the collapse rather than slow the column."

"He didn't leave them," Odile said. "He *used* them. Dead men slow nobody. Living ones do."

Maren said nothing. She was watching Teo, forty yards up the grade where he'd scrambled ahead with the signal flags he'd salvaged from the hut, rigging a wire between two creosoted poles that had stood dead and leaning for fifteen years. The boy moved like the mountain belonged to him—quick, light, unbothered by the thinning air that made even Dominic breathe hard. Fourteen years old and already he'd been a telegrapher, a stowaway, a spy, and now, apparently, a mountaineer. When Maren had been fourteen she'd been learning assay ledgers in Anji, hating every column of numbers, never guessing that columns of numbers would be the weapon she'd carry against an empire.

The thought of the ledgers steadied her, as it always did. Thirty-one names. Payroll records of men the syndicate had written off as *survey casualties*—men who'd never existed on paper but existed in Elias's map, in the collapsed dark of Tunnel Nine, in the cairn they were still three days' climb from. Her father hadn't been a prospector. He'd been a witness who ran, and hid, and finally stopped running in a shed in Karawari Landing with a bullet in his gut and a stranger's hands pressed against the wound.

She would finish what the bullet started.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a maintenance siding—a roofless stone platform where snow sheds had once protected the track from avalanche, now just walls and memory. Odile built a fire small enough to hide in a coffee tin, and they ate salt pork and hard biscuit while the temperature fell around them like a curtain coming down.

It was Teo who heard them first. He held up a hand—not the sharp gesture of alarm but the listening one—and pointed downhill with his chin.

Through the dark came voices. Faint, distorted by the rock, but unmistakably human: the low murmur of a column on the march, the clink of gear, once a single sharp bark of command. Then, closer than the rest, a sound Maren felt in her teeth before her ears understood it.

Rockfall.

It started small—a trickle of stones somewhere above their own position, on the broken face where the grade cut through a shoulder of shattered granite—and then it gathered itself. In the firelight Maren saw Dominic's face change, saw him do the engineer's arithmetic in half a second, and then he was moving, grabbing her arm, shouting the only word that mattered:

"Down! Off the grade—*now!*"

They went over the wall of the old shed and onto the uphill side, flattening against scree, and the world above them came apart. The slide wasn't aimed at them—that was the terrible, clarifying thing about it. It was aimed at the grade below, at the switchback two turns down, where Harrow's column had reached the exposed face. Maren heard the roar build and break, heard it roll away into the gorge, and then heard what came after.

Screaming. Two voices, maybe three, rising out of the dark and then falling silent one after another, the way voices fall when the men behind them decide there is nothing left to save.

Nobody in the ruined siding spoke. The fire had died under the stamp of boots. Above them the mountain settled, stone finding stone, and the cold poured down into the gorge like water into a cup.

It was Odile who finally moved, crawling back to the tin and coaxing the coals alive again with the economy of a woman who had done harder things in darker places. "That'll slow them," she said quietly.

"It'll make them angry," Dominic said. "Angry men don't slow down. They spend."

"They spent two men," Maren said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, very calm. "You heard it. They didn't even try to dig."

"That's Harrow," Odile said, and there was something in her voice Maren had learned to recognize over the past weeks—not guilt exactly, because Odile didn't offer guilt cheaply anymore, but the older, harder cousin of it. Recognition. "I told you what he did at Kavali. Burned a village over a strike I helped survey. Eleven dead, and he wrote it up as a clearance operation. He doesn't bury his mistakes, Maren. He buries his witnesses. We're not pursuers to him. We're paperwork."

Teo had come down off his perch and sat close to the fire, knees drawn up, and Maren noticed—he was sure the boy thought no one noticed—that the boy had moved his bedroll so that it lay between Maren's and the open downhill slope. Fourteen years old, arranging himself as a shield. She reached over in the dark and squeezed his shoulder once. He didn't say anything. Neither did she. Some things said better unsaid.

* * *

Morning came white-rimed and windless, the kind of cold that makes sound carry for miles, which is how Maren heard the crying.

It came from downslope, from the tumbled face where the slide had run out—a thin, rhythmic sound, more animal than human, threading through the stillness. The others heard it too; she watched it land on each of them differently. Dominic's jaw tightened. Odile's eyes went flat and calculating. Teo simply looked at Maren, because somehow over these weeks the boy had decided that when there was a question about what was right, it was hers to answer.

She hated him a little for that. She answered it anyway.

"I'm going down."

"No," Dominic said.

"There's someone alive down there."

"There are twenty-five men down there."

"There's *one*," she said. "One is the only number I've ever known how to count."

She took the rope, took the pistol, took Odile without asking—because Odile was the only one among them who knew loose rock the way a fisherman knows water—and left Dominic holding the top of the line with a face like a man watching his own judgment walk off a cliff. Which, she supposed, he was.

The descent took an hour that felt like a day. The slide field was treacherous in the morning thaw, stones shifting underfoot with small betraying whispers, and twice Odile froze them both with a raised fist while some shelf of the slope finished deciding whether it meant to move. The crying guided them—a man's voice cracked down to almost nothing, calling words that weren't words yet, just pain shaped into rhythm.

They found him wedged in a pocket of boulders, pinned from the knee down by a slab the size of a door. Young, Maren saw immediately—younger than her, maybe, a farm-boy face gone gray with shock, lips split and crusted, one eye swollen shut. His partner lay ten feet farther down, folded wrong against a boulder, past anything. The young one kept calling anyway. Kept calling for a man who couldn't answer.

"Harrow's column?" Maren asked, crouching beside him.

His good eye found her and went wide with terror. He understood instantly what she was—who she had to be—and Maren watched the fear war with something else in his face. Hope, maybe. The animal kind, the kind that doesn't check whose hand reaches into the wreck.

"Don't," he whispered. "Don't leave me."

"I'm not going to leave you." She was surprised by how easily the words came. She'd rehearsed hatred for months—had built whole mornings out of it, waking in Anji with her father's name in her mouth and murder in her ledger. She had not rehearsed this. But here was a boy—no older than the miners in her father's payroll records, no older than the men the syndicate had erased with a stroke of ink—and the arithmetic of mercy turned out to be simpler than the arithmetic of vengeance. One was still one.

Odile was already levering at the slab with a length of grade-iron they'd carried up from Tunnel Nine. "On three," she said. "And when it moves, you drag him clear, because I won't be able to hold this twice."

The slab came off with a grinding groan, and the scream the boy made Maren would hear for a long time afterward. His leg was broken below the knee, bad, the kind of break that killed men on mountains. They splinted it with a tent pole and strips of his own shirt, packed the wound with the last of their moss dressing, and got him upright between them, and by then it was nearly noon and Maren had made her second decision without quite noticing she'd made it.

"We're taking him up," she said.

"Maren—" Odile began.

"He knows Harrow's plan. He knows the column. He knows things we can't know from up here." That was the argument she gave. It wasn't the true one, and she knew it, and she suspected Odile knew it too.

They hauled him up the rope line hand over hand, the boy biting down on a strap to keep from screaming and bringing the whole slope down on them, and when they crested the grade Dominic looked at the prisoner, then at Maren, and said nothing at all—which was its own kind of verdict, and she found she could live with it.

* * *

He gave them everything, once the morphine Odile carried for emergencies had blunted the worst of the pain. His name was Pell. He'd signed with the syndicate's protection detail eight months ago for the same reason most men sign such contracts: debt, and the promise it would be forgiven. He'd never seen the inside of a mine office. He'd never heard of Tunnel Nine until this climb. He'd been told they were pursuing *thieves carrying stolen instruments*, and he'd believed it right up until the mountain had killed the man beside him, and Harrow had ordered the column forward without stopping.

"He's not following us to catch us," Pell said, propped against the shed wall, shivering under every blanket they owned. His voice had the loose, floating honesty of a man past caring what he confessed. "You have to understand. He's not trying to catch you at all."

Dominic went very still. "Then what is he doing?"

"Boxing you." Pell's good eye tracked upward, toward the high notch of the pass, where the first snows had begun dusting the summit ridge overnight—white powdering black stone like flour sifted over iron. "There's a detachment ahead of you. Went up the eastern grade ten days ago, before you even reached the foothills. Twelve men, blasting supplies, a mountain gun disassembled on mule-back. They're sealing the passes ahead of you. Every snow shed, every tunnel mouth, every choke point on the grade. By tomorrow night the upper line will be closed behind you and ahead of you both."

"Why seal it?" Maren asked. "Why not just fight us?"

"Because fighting leaves bodies," Pell said simply. "Bodies get found when the spring surveys come through. Bodies have names." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, half fever. "You know why they call him Harrow? Because he harrows the ground smooth. Nothing planted. Nothing growing. Just—field. Ready for whatever story gets sown next."

Silence in the ruin of the siding. Maren looked at the summit ridge, at that thin white line thickening hour by hour, and understood with perfect clarity what Pell was telling them. The storm was coming early—Odile had been saying so for two days, reading the milk-white sky the way she read water. And Harrow wasn't chasing them into the high country. He was driving them there. Herding the whole party—her, Dominic, Odile, Teo, and now a wounded enemy soldier who ate their food and drank their water—into a pass that would be sealed at both ends, and letting the mountain do what the syndicate had always used it to do.

No bodies. No names. Just a field.

"He buried thirty-one men in Tunnel Nine," Pell said, quieter now, drifting. "I heard the sergeants talk. They think nobody knows. Everybody knows. Everybody just—" his eye closed, opened— "everybody just keeps walking. You keep walking long enough, you're one of them."

Maren knelt beside him. She should hate him. Part of her did hate him—for the column, for the ambush at the waystation, for the simple fact of the uniform. But kneeling there, she found the hatred wouldn't hold its shape. It kept softening into something else, something that felt less like a weapon and more like a weight.

"My father walked," she said. "For eleven years he walked, running from what he knew. And it caught him anyway, in a river town he thought was too small to matter." She pulled the blanket higher over the boy's shoulders. "So here's what happens now. You're going to live. You're going to live because I say so, and when the magistrates come through this range in the spring—when the truth comes through this range in the spring—you're going to stand up on whatever legs they've mended for you, and you're going to tell them everything you just told us. That's your debt paid. Not to me. To the thirty-one."

Pell looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded, slowly, the way men nod when they've been handed a sentence and discovered it's also a rope thrown to a drowning man.

Above them the sky was going the color of pewter, and the wind had turned, and Odile stood at the edge of the grade with her face tipped into it, reading it like a page. When she turned back, her expression had changed.

"Snow tonight," she said. "Real snow. And there's a signal hut four miles up, at the mouth of the old gallery—if it's still standing. We make it there, we might winter through the front. We miss it—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Behind them, far down the Sawteeth, the wind carried up the sound of Harrow's column beginning to move again—patient, certain, closing like a book.
