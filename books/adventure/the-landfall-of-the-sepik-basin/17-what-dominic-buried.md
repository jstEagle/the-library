## 17. What Dominic Buried

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

It had been building all day—a bruise-colored wall in the northwest that Odile watched with the flat expression of a woman reading her own death warrant—and by dusk it arrived in earnest, wind screaming down the couloirs of the closed grade with a sound like freight cars coupling at speed. The signal hut sat where Elias Wren's map said it would, third switchback above the old water tower, its timber walls silvered to bone by nine winters of neglect. Dominic got the door wedged shut with an iron spike while Maren and Odile dragged Teo inside, and then the world simply ceased to exist beyond the walls, as if the mountain had reached out and closed its hand around them.

They had food for two days if they starved themselves carefully. Teo had been burning since morning.

The boy lay on the cot nearest the stove, which was the only cot, wrapped in every blanket they owned and still shivering, his skin flushed dark and his lips cracked. His telegrapher's hands—quick, clever hands that could read a wire by feel in the dark—lay open on his chest, twitching now with the fever like birds dreaming of flight. Every few minutes he murmured something. Once it was his mother's name. Once it was a call sign from the Anji office, *K-7 answering, K-7 answering*, the litany of a boy who had spent half his life listening for voices that never came for him.

Odile fed the stove splinters of the bench she'd broken up with the back of her axe, feeding the fire the way she did everything—economically, without sentiment, as though warmth were cargo to be rationed across a long haul. Maren sat beside the boy with a cloth dipped in melted snow, pressing it to his forehead, wringing it, pressing again. She had not stopped doing this in six hours. Her sleeves were soaked to the elbow. She moved like a woman keeping a promise to someone who couldn't hear it.

Dominic stood at the window he'd cleared with his palm and watched the snow erase the world.

He had built things like this once. Signal huts, snowsheds, mile markers stamped with a company number. He had drawn them in clean ink on linen paper, and men had cut the timber and driven the spikes, and trains had run through country that had no business holding rails, and everyone had called it progress because the alternative word was too heavy to lift.

"Dominic." Maren's voice, low, without turning. "Sit down before you fall down. You've been standing there since the light went."

"I'm watching the weather."

"The weather is watching you back. Sit."

He sat. That was the thing about Maren Wren—she gave orders the way her father must have given them, quietly, with the certainty that they'd be obeyed, and Dominic, who had spent four years refusing to obey anyone, found himself obeying. He lowered himself onto the floor against the far wall, knees up, and the stove heat found him and made him drowsy, and drowsiness was dangerous, because drowsiness was where the truth lived.

* * *

He had rehearsed this confession for years without knowing it. In the false-name towns, in the locomotive sheds, in the muddy hours before dawn when Karawari Landing smelled of fish rot and diesel, he had composed the speech a hundred times—to a magistrate who never summoned him, to a jury of strangers, to Elias Wren, who was dead, to the thirty-one men whose names he had helped erase. But rehearsal is a coward's craft. It lets you believe the words are the hard part.

The hard part was the silence afterward.

"Teo's fever," Odile said into the quiet. "If it breaks tonight, we move tomorrow. If it doesn't—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Everyone in the hut understood arithmetic.

"He'll break it." Maren wrung the cloth. "He's stubborn. Nobody runs away from home twice out of weakness."

"He ran away from home once?"

"Everyone here ran away from somewhere." Maren looked up then, and her eyes were gray as the storm-light, and they settled on Dominic with a weight he felt in his chest. "Some of us are still running. Aren't we, Mr. Montrose."

Not a question. She had known his name since Anji—he'd told her himself, finally, on the trail above Broken Narrows, because it seemed obscene to carry her father's map under a lie—and hearing it spoken aloud in this small wooden room, with the wind trying to tear the roof off, did something strange to him. It didn't wound him. It *emptied* him, the way a fever breaks empty a body.

"Yes," he said. "I'm still running. And I should tell you what I'm running from, while the storm does the work of pinning us all in one place. Because if I don't say it now, I'll find a reason not to say it ever, and you deserve better than that. All three of you do."

Odile stopped feeding the stove. The axe head rested on her knee. She looked at him the way she looked at white water—reading it, waiting for the rock hidden underneath.

Maren set the cloth in the basin and folded her hands. "Say it."

* * *

"In 1889 I was the junior survey engineer on the Sepik Basin Extension," Dominic began, and his voice came out steadier than he expected, the voice of a man reading from a document, which was fitting, because that was all he had ever really been good at—documents. "Third generation railroad family. My grandfather drove grades through the Coast Range. My father laid the Vessel Junction spur. I was raised to believe that a line drawn true on paper was a kind of moral act. That the map was honest even when the men above it weren't. I believed the paper. God help me, I believed the paper more than I believed people."

The stove ticked. Wind pressed at the walls like a crowd leaning in.

"The syndicate wanted the Basin Extension finished in eighteen months. Eighteen months for ninety miles of mountain grade, three tunnels, two trestles. Any engineer worth his transit would have called it impossible and meant it. But the numbers came down from the directors' office already massaged—rock densities understated, water tables ignored, blast yields assumed generous. And my section chief, a man named Corliss, handed me the Tunnel Nine survey and told me to check it and sign it. Check it and sign it. Not survey it. There wasn't time to survey it."

"You signed," Maren said. Not cruelly. Just placing the stone.

"I checked it the way you check a thing when you already know what answer keeps your name off the trouble list. I walked the portal, I read their figures, I did enough arithmetic to make the signature defensible in a courtroom, and I signed. Dominic A. Montrose, Assistant Engineer, in ink I can still see when I close my eyes. Blue-black ink. I remember thinking the pen scratch sounded loud." He laughed, briefly, and hated the sound of it. "That's what shame sounds like, apparently. A pen."

Teo stirred on the cot and murmured, and Maren touched his wrist until he settled.

"Three weeks later they blasted Tunnel Nine through a live seam. The survey said dry ground. The seam was not dry. The charge went off, the face collapsed, and thirty-one men went into the dark—miners, muckers, a powder monkey who was sixteen years old. The syndicate sealed both portals within the week. No recovery. No inquest. The payroll ledger listed them as transferred to the coast division, and then, when the coast division asked, as survey casualties—men who had never existed, names invented to absorb the dead ones. I know because I was asked to draft the casualty report. I was asked because my signature was already on the survey, and a man who has signed one lie will sign the next one cheaper."

"And you did," Odile said.

"No." He shook his head, and the motion cost him something, like lifting a beam. "That's the part I've hidden longest, and it isn't credit. I refused. I wrote a letter to the territorial engineer's office. I put it in the mail myself, in Vessel Junction, and I watched the mail car take it south. Two days later Corliss told me the letter had never arrived, that I was mistaken about having sent anything, that I was tired, that I should take leave. And I believed—I *chose* to believe—that the letter was lost. Because believing it was lost meant I was free. Believing it was lost meant I hadn't failed thirty-one men; the mail system had."

The wind rose to a shriek and fell away, as if even the storm wanted to hear the rest.

"A month after that, a man came to my boarding house asking questions with a gun-shaped bulge under his coat. I left that night. Left my name, my license, my family's name—all of it. I buried Dominic Montrose in a river town that couldn't spell it and became whoever the next town needed me to be. And I told myself for four years that I ran because I was hunted. That's true. But it's only half true. The other half is that I ran because staying meant finding out whether my letter arrived, and if it arrived, then the deaths were mine—not the syndicate's, mine. My signature. My arithmetic. My pen scratch in blue-black ink."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls a draft whined through a knot-hole, thin as a whistle wire.

"So when Elias Wren put that map case in my hands," Dominic said, "and I opened it by lamplight and saw my own survey marks staring back at me—altered, corrected, annotated in a dead man's hand with the truth I'd signed away—every step since has been me walking toward this room. Toward this sentence. I didn't take the map to Port Vessel to save the world. I took it because the dead were in it, and I put some of them there, and a man can only run so far from a hole he dug before he finds he's carried the shovel the whole way."

* * *

He expected anger. He had earned anger; he had budgeted for it the way he budgeted rations. What he had not prepared for was Maren Wren reaching into her coat and drawing out her father's ledger—the payroll records, the phantom names, the book she had guarded harder than her own life—and laying it on the floor between them with a soft slap of leather.

"Read the casualty page," she said.

"Maren—"

"*Read it.*"

His hands were not steady. He opened the ledger to the tabbed page, and there it was in the syndicate's neat clerical hand: *Survey Casualties — Tunnel Nine Section — 31.* And beneath it, in Elias Wren's cramped, furious script, added sometime in the years after, a single line:

*None of these men exist. The men who died had names. Find the man who signed.*

Dominic stared at it. The hut seemed to tilt gently, like a boat taking water.

"He knew," Maren said quietly. "He didn't know your name then. He knew a signature—D.A.M., assistant engineer. He spent eleven years tracing it. He was in Karawari Landing looking for you when they shot him. Did you understand that? He wasn't prospecting. He was hunting the man who signed, so he could ask him one question before he died." She leaned forward, and her voice did not rise at all, which made it worse than shouting. "So answer it. Answer him. Why did you sign?"

"Because I was a coward," Dominic said, "who thought he was a careful man. Because the numbers were plausible and the deadline was real and my whole life I'd been taught that the line on paper was sacred, and I confused the paper with the people. Because it was easier to believe the syndicate's figures than to believe that I might be wrong and lose everything by saying so." He closed the ledger with care, the way you close a coffin. "There's no better answer. I've had four years to write one and there isn't one."

Odile spoke from the stove, her voice roughened by smoke. "In the delta villages, when a man drowns, they don't ask why he swam. They ask who pushed." She fed a sliver of bench into the fire. "Nobody pushed you, Montrose. You jumped. That's worse, and it's also better, because a man who jumps can choose to climb out. A man who's pushed just floats."

"That's the most forgiving thing anyone's ever said to me, and it wasn't forgiving."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." She looked at him across the small orange light. "You want absolution. There's none in this hut. There's a storm, a sick boy, two days of food, and a pass full of armed men behind us and dead men ahead of us. Absolution is a warm-climate luxury. What there is instead is the summit cairn, and thirty-one names, and a chisel in my kit. You want to pay? Pay in stone. Carve till your hands bleed. Then get up tomorrow and walk the rest of the way and testify in a courtroom under your own name, knowing what it costs, and let the cost be the payment. That's the only currency they accept up here."

Maren was crying. She did it silently, without changing her posture, tears running off her jaw onto her folded hands, and somehow that was harder to witness than any rage. "My father died carrying your name," she said. "Eleven years, and a bullet in a river town, for a signature. And I have spent the whole trail wanting to hate you, and the worst part—the truly unbearable part—is that I can't. Because you're here. You could have sold the map at the trading post for a pardon and your old life back, and you're sitting in a freezing hut telling the truth to a dead man's daughter." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears. "Hate needs a coward, and you've stopped being one. I don't know what to do with that."

"Neither do I," Dominic said. "I've never been it before."

Teo coughed then—a deep, wet sound that pulled all three of them to the cot at once—and the boy's eyes opened, glassy and unfocused, and fixed on Dominic with a flicker of the old sharpness swimming up through the fever.

"Heard you," Teo whispered. "The letter. It arrived."

The hut went very still.

"What?" Dominic said.

"At the Anji office. Before I ran. There was a—" another cough, "—a records request, old file, 1889, territorial engineer's office, marked *received, referred, no action*. Referred to the syndicate's counsel. Somebody's letter came in and they buried it in a drawer." His eyes drifted shut. "Filed under M. Montrose. I remembered because... nobody ever filed anything under a real name. Everybody lied. Yours was the only true one in the drawer."

He slept. Or sank. His breathing kept its ragged rhythm, but slower now, and Maren pressed her fingers to his throat and after a moment nodded: still with them.

Dominic sat back against the wall. The letter had arrived. It had always arrived. There had never been a lost mail car, a merciful accident, a gap in the machinery through which his conscience could slip. The syndicate had read his warning, filed it, and killed the miners anyway—or rather, had already killed them, and buried his letter alongside them, one more document in the hole. Which meant the choice had been real. Which meant the guilt had been real. Which meant—

Which meant nothing new. Only confirmation. Only the last door closing on the room he'd lived in for four years, the comfortable furnished room called *it wasn't my fault*, and outside that door the cold air of the truth, which was that he had signed, and they had died, and the letter changed nothing except his excuses.

"Montrose." Odile's voice, low. She nodded toward the shuttered window. "Listen."

He listened. Beneath the wind—far beneath it, at the edge of hearing, so faint he might have conjured it—came a sound that did not belong to storms. A rhythmic sound. Metallic. Patient.

A chisel on stone? No. Too regular. Too far below them, down the black switchbacks where the snow erased the trail.

Maren heard it too. She went to the window, cleared the frost with her sleeve, and held the lamp to the glass. The light bounced back off the white nothing—but for one instant, one heartbeat, it caught on something moving down-grade: a lantern, swinging, and the shape of a man holding it, and behind the man the suggestion of more shapes, a column of them, climbing.

"Harrow," Maren breathed. "In this. He's climbing in this."

"He wants the pass sealed with us inside it," Odile said. She was already pulling her boots on. "Storm'll cover his approach same as ours. We've got maybe till dawn."

Dominic rose. His legs ached; his hands ached worse, and he understood suddenly that they would ache from here to the coast and past it, and that this was correct, that pain was simply the interest on a debt now honestly acknowledged. He picked up the ledger and the map case and strapped them to his chest.

"Then we don't wait for dawn," he said. "We take him the summit first. Thirty-one names, and mine among the witnesses. Let him climb into the storm after the truth—it's the one thing he's never caught yet."

Outside, the wind turned, and the sound below them grew a fraction louder, patient as a pen scratch, coming on.
