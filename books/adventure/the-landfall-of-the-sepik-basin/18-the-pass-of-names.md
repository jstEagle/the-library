## 18. The Pass of Names

The storm had spent itself in the night, and what remained was a silence so complete that Maren could hear her own blood moving, a tide in her ears. She climbed out of the signal hut into a world scoured white and blue, the air thin as glass, the sun striking off the snowfield with a brightness that made her eyes water behind the smoked goggles Odile had pressed on her three days ago. The pass lay ahead—a saddle between two shoulders of black rock, wind-carved, bare of snow along its spine where the gales had licked it clean.

Elias's map called it the Pass of Names. Maren had thought it a surveyor's flourish when she first read the notation, some sentimental touch her father would never have allowed. Now, standing at the lip of the saddle with the cairn rising out of the drifts like a broken tooth, she understood.

It was not a flourish. It was an instruction.

The cairn was older than she expected—larger, too, a man-made mound of fitted stones taller than Dominic, wide at the base, built by hands that had meant it to last. Lichen had taken the lower courses, orange and grey, and ice had welded the upper stones into a single mass. But the face of it, the southern face sheltered from the worst of the weather, was smooth. Dressed stone. Cut for a purpose.

"He knew," Maren said. Her voice came out strange in the thin air, flattened, as if the mountain were refusing to carry it far. "He knew they'd never let him put up a marker down there. So he brought them up here."

Dominic stood beside her, breath smoking, the map case slung across his chest on its strap of waxed canvas. He had barely slept. None of them had. Teo was inside the hut still, fevered, wrapped in every blanket they owned with Odile sitting vigil over him, and the knowledge of that small burning body sat in all of them like a stone swallowed whole. "Thirty-one names," Dominic said quietly. "Your father carried them up here himself. That's what the map's been telling us since Karawari Landing. Not just where the lines ran. Where the dead were kept."

Maren knelt at the base of the cairn and brushed away the rime with her bare hand. The cold bit through her gloves' worn fingertips immediately, a bright animal pain, but beneath the frost her fingers found grooves. Carved letters. Shallow, weathered, half-filled with ice.

She worked at them with her knife point, chipping out the frozen grit, and letter by letter the first name emerged from the stone like something surfacing from deep water.

*JONAS ADEYEMI.*

She sat back on her heels. The name meant nothing to her and everything to her. A man who had gone underground one morning in a company town none of them could find on a map anymore, and had not come up. A man whose wife, if he'd had one, whose mother, whose children, had been told nothing—or worse, told a lie dressed in official language: *survey casualty, regrettable, compensation disbursed.* A name erased from ledgers and payroll books and finally from memory, except in the mind of one stubborn prospector who had walked up out of the basin with the truth folded against his chest.

"Bring the list," Maren said. Her voice did not shake. She was surprised by that. She had expected grief to unmake her; instead it had done something else entirely. It had hardened, the way water hardens into ice—not dead, but changed into something that could cut.

* * *

They worked through the morning.

The list came from Elias's map case, a fold of oilskin paper covered in his cramped, patient hand—thirty-one names, each one written twice, once in ink and once scored deep with a stylus so the record would survive even if the ink bled or faded. Maren read each name aloud before she carved it, because it seemed important that the names should be spoken on this ground at least once, aloud, in air, by a living voice. The mountain took the sound and gave back nothing, but she said them anyway.

*Jonas Adeyemi. Tomas Breen. Wei-Lin Chao.*

Dominic held the paper steady against the wind and read with her, taking turns, his voice low and level. He had insisted on that—insisted that this was not her burden alone, though it was hers most of all. His hands, when they weren't holding the paper, held the chisel, and he struck the letters true and deep while Maren followed with finer work, squaring the serifs, cleaning the grooves. They fell into a rhythm without discussing it. Read. Strike. Square. Breathe. The whiteout glare made everything dreamlike; the only colors in the world were the black rock, the white snow, the blue shadow, and the slow welling red of their split knuckles.

By midday they had finished eleven names. Maren's fingers had stopped feeling like her own. She looked at the growing column of letters and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since Anji, since the assay office, since the night she'd learned her father was dying in a river town four hundred miles from anywhere and had spent his last strength making her the keeper of other people's grief.

"He wasn't my father," she said suddenly. It came out before she decided to say it. "Not only mine. I mean—" She pressed her mitten against her mouth, then pulled it away. "I spent two years hating him for leaving. For sending nothing but money orders and postmarks. And the whole time he was carrying this. Thirty-one men. He couldn't tell me because telling me would have killed me, and he knew it, and he carried it alone anyway."

Dominic set down the chisel. For a long moment he said nothing, and the silence was not empty; it was the silence of a man choosing words the way you choose footholds on rotten rock.

"I signed the survey that buried them," he said. "I've been running from that signature for six years. Your father spent those same six years walking toward it." He looked at the cairn, at the eleven names catching the light. "We were both cowards, Maren. We just ran in opposite directions."

"And now?"

"Now we're standing still," he said, "in the one place they can't make us move."

* * *

Odile came up from the hut at mid-afternoon, moving carefully on the packed snow, and the look on her face reached them before her voice did.

Teo's fever had broken near dawn. He was sleeping, truly sleeping, color coming back into his face. But while she'd sat with him she had heard it—the faint, wrong note under the mountain's silence. Wind carries sound strangely in the high country, folding it around shoulders of rock, and what Odile had heard, folded and refolded until it arrived almost clean, was the ring of iron on stone. Many times. Rhythmic. Patient.

Pitons. Someone was driving pitons into the switchbacks below.

"They're coming up the old grade," Odile said. She stood with her weight on her good leg, her wounded side braced, and there was no apology left in her face anymore—only arithmetic. "Harrow doesn't travel light and he doesn't travel quiet, not after the bridge. He lost men on the ice and he knows we know the country better than he does. So now he comes loud. He wants us to hear him. He wants us to run, and break ourselves running, and be easy to finish."

"How long?" Dominic asked.

"A day. Less, if the cold holds and they push through the night." Odile spat, a habit that seemed almost ceremonial at this altitude. "Or we go down the western chute now, ahead of them, and leave this place unfinished."

Maren turned back to the cairn. Twenty names remained uncarved. Twenty men still lying under the ice like letters under snow.

"No," she said.

"Maren—"

"I said no." She heard her own voice come out flat and final, a rail spike driven home. "My father walked four hundred miles to mark these names and they shot him for it in a muddy town that forgot its own name. If we leave now, Harrow wins without firing a shot. This cairn becomes just rocks again. The syndicate erases them a second time, and this time they erase us doing it." She picked up the chisel. "We finish. Then we decide how to meet them."

Dominic met her eyes. Something passed between them—not agreement exactly, but recognition, the way one climber recognizes another's judgment on bad ground. "Then we finish," he said. "And we don't meet them at all if we can help it. There's a mine adit two hundred yards past the saddle—your father marked it. Old workings, timbered. We can hold it or lose it, but either way nobody buries us in the open."

* * *

They worked until the light went copper and then went out.

Seventeen names. Twenty-three. The cold deepened as the sun dropped behind the western wall, and their breath hung around them in clouds that froze in their eyebrows and lashes, so that they carved the last names looking at each other through masks of white. Maren's world narrowed to the width of a chisel blade. *Samuel Okonkwo. Petrus Vasseur. Anselm Krieg.* Each letter a small act of war. Each name a door opened onto a life she would never know—a man who whistled, a man who cheated at cards, a man who wrote letters home that someone, somewhere, had stopped answering years ago.

When the last name was squared and cleaned—*Aurelio Zuniga*, the thirty-first—Maren stepped back, and the four of them who could stand stood together before the cairn in the last blue light.

It was not beautiful. Grief rarely is. The letters marched down the stone in uneven rows, some deep, some shallow, the work of exhausted hands in killing cold. But it was *true*, and Maren felt the truth of it enter her body like warmth, like a coal swallowed. Thirty-one men had a monument now. The syndicate could burn the gazette offices, buy the magistrates, shoot the witnesses—but they could not uncarve stone at eight thousand feet, and everyone who ever climbed this pass, forever, would stop and read and wonder, and wondering is the one thing money cannot kill.

Her father had known that. Her father had planned for everything except a bullet in Karawari Landing, and even that he had planned around—he had found Dominic Montrose, the one man in the basin with both the skill to read the map and the shame to need redeeming.

"Dominic," she said. "Read them. All of them. Out loud."

"Why?"

"Because the dead should hear their names spoken over their own stone. And because—" she looked west, where the darkness was gathering, and beneath the darkness, faint and rhythmic, the ring of iron on stone had started again, closer now, "—because after tonight, we may be the last ones who ever will."

So Dominic read. All thirty-one names, his voice cracking on the fourth and steadying by the ninth, while the wind rose out of the west and the first flakes of the evening's snow came down soft as ash.

* * *

The shooting started an hour after full dark.

It began, as Odile had predicted it would, with light—a flare arcing up from the switchbacks below, hanging in the falling snow like a small cruel moon, turning the whole pass the color of bone. In that light Maren saw them: a column of dark figures strung along the old grade, perhaps twenty, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believed the outcome was already purchased.

"Hold your fire," Dominic breathed beside her. They had taken position among the tumbled boulders flanking the saddle, the cairn at their backs. "Let them climb. Every yard they gain costs them breath they haven't got."

The flare died. Darkness rushed back. Somewhere below, Harrow's voice carried up the mountain, amplified by the cold, smooth as oil poured on water: *"Montrose! You're standing on syndicate property! Walk down and we'll talk about your pardon again—and the girl walks free!"*

Maren felt Dominic tense beside her. She put her hand on his arm. "He's lying," she said. "You know he's lying."

"I know," Dominic said. "But thank you for saying it out loud."

The column reached the shoulder below the saddle and paused, regrouping, and in the second flare's harsh bloom Maren saw the front rank raise rifles, saw the muzzles tilt toward the boulders, toward the cairn—

Toward the names.

Something in her went very quiet and very clear, the way the air goes clear after a storm. She leveled her rifle across the boulder, found the silhouette of the nearest man against the flare's dying light, and did the arithmetic she had been dreading since Anji: that these were men sent to erase thirty-one human beings a second time, and that mercy for them might mean oblivion for the dead.

The first shot cracked the silence open, and the pass answered with thunder—echoes rolling from wall to wall, avalanche-warning thunder, and above the gunfire Maren heard Odile shouting from the rocks above the chute, voice wild and exultant:

*"You're shooting under an overhang, you fools—you'll bring the cornice down on your own heads!"*

For one heartbeat, the gunfire below faltered. Men looked up. In the flare-light Maren saw the cornice—a vast white fang of wind-packed snow, hanging over the switchbacks like the sword her father's stories used to promise—and she saw Harrow, small and distinct at the column's head, make his choice: he fired again, upward, defiant, at shadows and names and the stubborn living.

The mountain answered for the dead.

The cornice went with a sound like the world clearing its throat—a crack, then a roar, then a white wall descending the switchbacks with the indifference of a god. Maren threw herself flat behind the boulder as the blast of displaced air hit them, and when she raised her head, wiping snow-steam from her goggles, the grade below the saddle was simply *gone*—smoothed, erased, wiped clean as a ledger page. Of Harrow's column there remained only scattered figures far down-slope, dark specks crawling in the moonlight, alive by accident, leaderless, their rifles buried somewhere under forty feet of powder snow.

Silence returned, deeper than before. Snow drifted down through the beam of the dying flare like feathers.

Beside her, Dominic began, very quietly, to laugh—not with joy, but with the shaking relief of a man who has been holding his breath for six years. Odile limped down from the rocks, grinning like a wolf. And Maren Wren stood up, turned to the cairn, and laid her bare palm flat against the carved stone, against thirty-one names standing in the freezing dark.

"We're going down the western chute tonight," she said, "while they're digging themselves out. Teo first. Everyone moves." Then, softer, to the stone, to her father, to the men she would never meet: *Hold on. We're carrying you to the coast.*

Below them, far down the switchbacks, a single figure rose from the snow—alive, upright, unmistakable even at that distance by the set of his shoulders.

Harrow.

And he was not retreating. He was pointing—up the mountain, past the pass, toward the western chute and the route down—giving orders to the survivors of his buried column.

The descent, Maren understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, had only just begun.
