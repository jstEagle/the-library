## 19. The Descent

The mountain gave them one clear morning, and Odile spent it like a woman spending her last coin.

She stood at the lip of the pass while the others broke camp below, breath steaming, and looked down the long white throat of the descent. The map called this stretch the Sickle — a crescent of switchbacks cut into the eastern face of the range, rail grade abandoned before it was ever finished, the ties rotted to black lace under the snow. Beyond it, the country dropped away in terraces toward the timberline and the river country she knew, the country that had made her and unmade her and was about, if she had anything to say about it, to make her one last time.

Behind her, far below on the western switchbacks, came the sound she had been listening for since dawn: the ring of iron on stone. Crampons. Pitons. Harrow's column, climbing.

They had been behind them for a day and a half, patient as weather. Harrow did not rush. Rushing was for men who feared their quarry. Harrow simply climbed, with his thirty men and his mountain guns broken down into mule-loads, and he let the mountain do his thinking for him. Odile had watched him work before, from the deck of a cargo barge six years ago, the morning the village of Temberi burned on the far bank and the smoke stood up straight in the windless air like a black tree planted in the world. She had watched and she had said nothing and she had taken her pay, and every mile since had been interest accruing on that debt.

"Odile." Maren's voice, low, from the rocks. "Teo's asking for you."

"How is he?"

"Fever's down. He ate half a biscuit and complained about it, which I'm told is a good sign."

Odile allowed herself the ghost of a smile. The boy had nearly died in the signal hut, burning through the blizzard while the storm screamed like something with a throat, and he had come out the far side of it thin and gray and stubborn, which was to say: himself. She went down to him. He was sitting propped against a pack, wrapped in every spare layer they owned, his telegrapher's fingers — quick, clever, always moving — folded still for once in his lap.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You should see the other pilot."

He laughed, and the laugh cost him, and she saw him pay it anyway. That was the thing about Teo she had come to love and could no longer pretend she hadn't: the boy paid every cost. He had run from the wire sheds because he couldn't stomach what the wire carried, and he had stowed away on a boat full of strangers because silence felt worse than danger, and now he sat half-frozen on a mountain with a price on all their heads and he still found a joke to spend.

"Listen to me," Odile said, crouching. "Today we go down the Sickle. You stay between Maren and me. You do not leave the grade, you do not cut corners, and if I say run, you run, and if I say stop, you stop like your boots are nailed down. Do you understand me?"

"You've got a plan."

"I've got a memory."

His eyes narrowed — the telegrapher in him, reading the line beneath the line. "The bad kind or the useful kind?"

"Both," Odile said. "They're usually the same thing."

* * *

She told them at the first switchback, while the wind was still kind and the column below was still two hours back.

"There's a bridge," she said. "Not a bridge like you're thinking. A snow bridge, over a crevasse field where the glacier sheared off the old rail bed. The survey crews used it — there was a winter crossing, years back, before the syndicate sealed the grade. It's not on any map. I know because I carried the cargo across it."

Dominic looked at her the way he always did when she offered something true: like a man checking a rivet. "You're sure it's still there?"

"No," Odile said. "I'm sure it was there in the spring of the year Temberi burned, carrying forty tons of drill steel and two mules. Snow bridges either melt or they don't. This one's in shadow all day, north face, cold sink. My guess is it's holding. My guess is also that Harrow's maps show nothing there at all — just a blank, and a blank reads as safe ground to a man in a hurry."

"And you want him in a hurry," Maren said.

"I want him on it," Odile said. "There's a difference."

The wind moved through the pass and carried off the end of her sentence, and for a moment none of them spoke. Dominic studied the descent, the long white crescent of it, the crevasse field below the fourth switchback where the glacier had torn itself open like a wound that never healed. She watched him do the arithmetic she had already done — weight, load, spacing, the physics of collapse — because that was who Dominic was, a man who had once believed in numbers enough to sign his name under them, and who had spent years learning what numbers could be made to say.

"It could take us instead," he said quietly. "If we misjudge it."

"It could," Odile said. "So we won't misjudge it."

"And if it takes you?"

She had known the question was coming. She had been carrying it since the waystation, since Maren's pistol and her own confession and the long terrible night when the family she'd found had voted — not to cast her out, but to let her stay, which was harder, which was the harder mercy by far. Trust given back was a heavier load than trust never broken.

"Then it takes me," Odile said. "I've been on borrowed ground a long time, Dominic. Everyone gets a bridge that holds or one that doesn't. I'd rather mine be the kind that means something."

* * *

They went down through the morning, four small figures on a grade built for locomotives, and the mountain watched them go.

The Sickle was beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful, all clean curves and blue shadow, the snow squeaking under their boots at a pitch that meant deep cold, the air so clear it felt like a cheat. Below the third switchback Odile called the halt and showed them the line: single file, wide spacing, Maren and Teo on the inside of the grade where the snow lay thin over rock, Dominic sweeping behind to blur their tracks with a pine bough. She herself would take the outside, the thin edge, the line above the void — because someone had to test the crust, and because she had decided, in the wordless ledger she kept now instead of prayers, that it would be her.

The crevasse field opened below them at the fourth turn like the mountain exhaling. It was worse than she remembered — a chaos of blue slots and shattered ice, some of the cracks narrow as a hand, some wide enough to swallow a rail car whole, all of it stitched over by the winter's snow into a surface that looked, treacherously, like ground. And there, exactly where her memory said it would be, the bridge: a smooth white span maybe forty feet across, sagging gently in the middle, arched between two shoulders of blue ice like a held breath.

"Nobody breathes wrong," Odile murmured, and led them across.

It held. Of course it held — it had been holding for her for six years, in the back of her mind, waiting. On the far side she turned and looked back at it and felt something in her chest that was not quite fear and not quite peace.

"Now," she said. "We make ourselves worth following."

They did not hide their trail past the bridge. That was the whole design. They walked openly down the fifth and sixth switchbacks, in plain view of the climbing column, moving slow enough to be seen and fast enough to be envied. Odile even left a marker — a strip of red cloth from her pack, tied to a rock spur, the kind of trail sign a courier in a panic might leave for a follower he hoped was a friend. Harrow would read it the way she wanted it read: they're tired, they're sloppy, they're hurrying for the low country, and here is a shortcut across flat snow.

By midday, from a shoulder of rock, she watched them come.

They came exactly as she had known they would. Harrow's column moved like a machine that had learned to imitate men — thirty of them in a spaced line, the mule-loads of disassembled guns swaying, the pickets' iron ringing on the stone. And at the head of it, small with distance but unmistakable, the gray coat and the unhurried stride. Harrow did not look at the crevasse field. That was the thing she was counting on. Harrow looked at maps, and the map said nothing, and a man who has burned a village does not trouble himself with what the blank places are hiding.

"He's going for it," Dominic breathed beside her. "Straight across."

"He'll space them out," Odile said. "He's not a fool on ice. It'll hold for the first six, maybe eight." She kept her voice flat, pilot's voice, cargo-manifest voice, the voice she used for numbers that meant people. "Then the span fatigues. Then it goes."

Maren was watching through the glass, and her face was doing something complicated. "There are thirty of them," she said. "Some of those men are just — hired. They don't all know what's in the tunnel. They don't all know what Harrow is."

"No," Odile said. "They don't."

It would have been easy, right then, to take it back. To call them down off the grade, to run for the timberline and trust to distance and luck. And she understood why Maren said it, and she loved her a little for saying it, because mercy that costs nothing isn't mercy, and Maren had learned that on the mountain with a wounded hunter's life in her hands.

But Odile had watched Temberi burn. She had helped survey the strike that made the burning profitable, and she had carried the drill steel across this very bridge, and somewhere under the snow below them — not here, not in this field, but in the country this mountain guarded — thirty-one men lay in Tunnel Nine with their names scraped off the books. The syndicate had made the dead anonymous. It was the whole of their method: erase the names, and the killing becomes arithmetic.

"Harper's men chose their payroll," Odile said. "I chose mine. Mine was wrong for six years." She took the glass from Maren's hand. "This is the payment."

* * *

It happened faster than she had imagined, and she had imagined it every hour for two days.

The column reached the bridge at a little past two o'clock, when the light came flat off the western face and everything on the ice turned the same blind white. Harrow crossed first — of course he did; he would trust no span he hadn't tested with his own weight — and then the line came on behind him, eight, ten, twelve men, spaced and steady, boots crunching in the cold hush.

Thirteen was when the bridge spoke.

It was not a crack, exactly. It was a sound like a held breath finally released — a deep, structural sigh, the snow-pack settling a hand's width, and every man on it froze at once, and for one long moment the whole column stood perfectly still on the white span like figures on a Christmas card, and Odile thought, absurdly, *it will hold, it's going to hold, all this for nothing* —

Then the far shoulder sheared.

It went with a crack like a rail car coupling, and the span tilted, and the world turned to white thunder. The crevasse field opened its blue throat and the bridge poured into it — men, snow, rope, a mule-load of gun barrel, all of it sliding with a terrible smoothness into the dark, and the screams were very small under the roar of the collapse, and the powder cloud rose up the face of the mountain like a white ghost standing up.

And Teo was on the wrong side of it.

He had gone back — she saw it all in one flash, the whole catastrophe in a single frame — gone back along the grade to retrieve the dropped tin of signal mirrors, the one thing left from his old life, his telegrapher's kit, his wire-tools, and he had been fifty feet below the bridge when it went, and the slide was coming down the slope toward him now, a white river with a will, and the boy was running and the snow was faster.

Odile did not decide. That was the truth she would carry afterward, the truth she would tell in Port Vessel under oath: there was no decision, no weighing, no ledger. There was a boy in the path of an avalanche and there were her boots, and the distance between those two facts was the only honest thing she had ever measured.

She went down the grade in four falling strides, caught him at the edge of the slide's run-out, and threw them both sideways behind a rib of exposed rock as the white water broke over them. It did not bury them. It almost buried them. It filled the world with roar and cold and the weight of a mountain deciding, and then it was past, and the silence after was so total she could hear her own heart knocking like a telegraph key.

Teo was under her, alive, coughing, his hands fisted in her coat. "Odile — Odile, your leg—"

She looked down. There was a great deal of blood, which seemed, in the white world, almost decorative. A piece of ice-shelf, riding the slide, had opened her thigh from hip to knee like a knife through canvas, and the cold had done its merciful work and numbed most of it, and she understood with a pilot's calm that the numbing would not last and the bleeding did not care.

"Hold here," she said. "Press. Both hands. Don't let go even if I yell, and I will yell."

She bound it with the sash from her coat and a length of rope-line, and she did yell, and Teo did not let go, and by the time Dominic and Maren reached them — sliding down the grade on their heels, faces white as the snow — the bleeding had slowed to a stubborn seep and Odile had begun, in the way of the badly hurt, to make jokes.

"Well," she said. "That's the second time this mountain's tried to collect."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the rock rib, a fire small enough to hide, and Odile lay with her leg wrapped and propped on a pack and watched the crevasse field in the moonlight.

The bridge was gone. The field had smoothed itself over, the way glaciers do, folding its dead under the blue ice with the patience of a bookkeeper. Somewhere down there were most of what remained of Harrow's thirty. A handful had been on the near shoulder when it went — she had counted four figures scrambling back up the grade, four small dark flecks retreating westward into the passes, leaderless now or led by whoever was loudest. Harrow himself had been at the head of the line, on the far side, on ground that held.

She had watched for him. She would not pretend she hadn't. She had watched the far rim through the glass while the powder cloud settled, looking for the gray coat.

She had not found it.

"He went down with them," Dominic said quietly, beside her. He had read her face the way he read everything, slowly and completely. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure the gray coat isn't standing on the far rim," Odile said. "The mountain can keep the rest of the accounting. I've carried enough of it."

Maren sat down on her other side, and for a while none of them spoke, and the fire ticked, and far below in the dark the glacier groaned like a ship working at anchor.

"I keep thinking I should feel different," Odile said at last. "Finished. Paid up. That's not how it feels. It feels like — the tide going out. You don't feel the sea get lighter. You just notice, one morning, that more of the shore is showing than there used to be."

Teo, half-asleep against a pack, mumbled: "That's the most pilot thing you've ever said."

She laughed, and the laugh pulled at the wound, and it was worth it.

They would move at first light, Dominic said — down out of the high passes while Harrow's surviving four were still running the other way, down through the timberline, down to the river country where the Kestrel waited at a mooring Odile had not let herself think about for three weeks. Down to the coast. Down to Port Vessel, and the gazette office, and the magistrate with the tired eyes, and the thirty-one names that were going to be read aloud at last in a room with a roof on it.

Odile lay back and looked up at the stars, sharp and cold and numberless, and felt the tide going out of her, and did not fear what it would leave showing on the shore.

Below them, miles down in the black timber, a light appeared. Then a second. Then a string of them, descending the river road toward the coast — lanterns, many lanterns, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had been sent ahead to wait.

Maren saw them too. "Odile," she said. "Who waits on the river road?"

Odile watched the lights, and her blood went cold in a way the wound had nothing to do with.

"The syndicate," she said. "Somebody always waits at the landing."
