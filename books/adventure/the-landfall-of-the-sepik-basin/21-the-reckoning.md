## 21. The Reckoning

The magistrate's court in Port Vessel smelled of coal smoke and wet wool, of a hundred bodies crowded into a hall built for half that number, and beneath it all—faint but unmistakable to a man who had spent years around machinery—the cold iron tang of the harbor seeping through the floorboards. Dominic Montrose sat on the witness bench in a coat he had bought that morning from a secondhand shop on Cordage Street, and he could not stop noticing the small sounds: the scratch of the stenographer's pen, the creak of the ceiling fans turning slow against the winter damp, the murmur of the gallery like a river running under ice.

He had testified before. That was the strange thing his hands kept remembering. Years ago, in another life, in another range of mountains, he had sat in hearing rooms with polished railings and given evidence about grades and load tolerances and the bearing capacity of cut stone, and men in good coats had nodded and written down what he said as though his word were a kind of currency. He had believed then that his word was worth something. He had learned, in the cruelest way a man can learn, that a signature is only as honest as the hand that signs it.

"State your name for the record."

The clerk's voice was dry as old paper. Dominic rose. His knees ached—not from fear, though there was plenty of that, humming in him like a live wire—but from the descent, from weeks of switchbacks and scree and the long gray slide of winter into the coast. His body had not yet forgiven him for the pass.

"Dominic Elias Montrose."

The name came out of him sounding foreign and familiar at once, like a song from childhood sung in an unfamiliar key. For six years he had been other men—D. Marsh, the fitter at Karawari Landing; Dom, the quiet one who kept every door unlocked and every window shuttered; the man who answered to anything because nothing belonged to him. Now the syllables stood in the air of the courtroom, taken down by the stenographer, entered into the permanent record of the territory, and no one in the gallery laughed or pointed or sent a wire ahead of him to the next town. They simply listened.

"You understand," the magistrate said—a narrow woman named Corvus with spectacles that caught the lamplight like twin coins—"that testimony given here is given under penalty. You are not compelled to speak, Mr. Montrose. The amnesty offered to cooperative witnesses covers the map and its provenance. It does not cover your own history with the Sepik Range Survey."

"I understand."

"And you choose to speak regardless."

Dominic looked out across the room. Maren Wren sat in the front row with her father's map case open on her knees like a book of scripture, the waterproof oilcloth worn soft as skin from twenty years of a prospector's handling. Beside her, Teo sat very straight in a borrowed jacket two sizes too big, his sprained ankle propped on a crate, his eyes moving constantly between the door and the witness stand as if Harrow's ghost might still come through either. Odile was not beside them. Odile was three rows back, alone, where she had chosen to sit since they reached the coast—as if proximity were something she had to re-earn, row by row, day by day. Her arm was bound in a sling of canvas. The ice bridge had taken more than Harrow; it had nearly taken the use of her shoulder, and the surgeons at Port Vessel spoke of months, maybe a year, before she could hold a wheel again.

She caught his eye and gave him the smallest nod. *Go on,* the nod said. *Say it all.*

"I choose to speak," Dominic said.

* * *

They had been in Port Vessel eleven days, and the town had turned the trial into a season.

It was the gazette that did it, of course. Maren had walked into the offices of the *Port Vessel Shipping Gazette* on their first morning with the map case under one arm and her father's ledger under the other, and she had laid them both on the editor's desk with the deliberation of a woman setting charges, and she had said, "Print it before they buy you." The editor—a heavy, red-faced man named Quill who had spent thirty years printing tide tables and freight notices—had read until midnight with his door locked, and then he had done the bravest thing Dominic had ever seen a newspaperman do: he printed everything, uncut, including the parts that made the syndicate's directors look like men and the parts that made them look like something worse.

THE THIRTY-ONE, the headline ran, in letters tall enough to read from across the street. And beneath it, in smaller type that somehow hit harder: *Names recovered from falsified survey records. Tunnel Nine, Sepik Basin Line, sealed 1889.*

Quill's typesetters had worked through three nights reconstructing the list from Elias Wren's marginalia and Maren's payroll ledgers and the survey marks Dominic himself had drafted a lifetime ago. Thirty-one names. Some were full names, birthdates, next of kin. Some were only surnames and a village. One was a single initial and the notation *boy, mule driver, approx. 12 yrs.* When that edition came off the presses, people lined up in the snow to buy copies, and somewhere in the crowd a woman began to sing a mourning song in a language Dominic didn't know, and others took it up, and the sound moved through the street like water finding its level.

The syndicate's lawyers arrived within forty-eight hours. They arrived the way weather arrives, in force and without apology: nine of them, quartered at the Grand Meridian, filing motions before dawn. They sought injunctions against the Gazette. They sought to have the map excluded as hearsay, the ledger as forgery, the testimony of a runaway telegraph boy as inadmissible on grounds of age. They sought, above all, to make the proceedings so tedious that the public's attention would freeze over like the passes and thaw onto some newer scandal by spring.

But the syndicate had made one error, and it was the same error they had always made: they had counted the dead as nothing, and therefore they had failed to count the living who remembered them. Widows came forward from the basin towns. Old track-walkers who had been paid to keep silent came forward. A foreman from Tunnel Nine itself—an ancient, bent man carried into the courtroom on his son's back—came forward, and when the syndicate's lead counsel suggested gently that his memory might be unreliable after so many years, the old man had gripped the rail of the witness box with both hands and said, in a voice like gravel sliding, "I carried four of them out myself, sir. I remember the weight better than I remember my own wedding day." The gallery had gone so quiet that Dominic could hear the harbor ice creaking outside.

And now it was his turn. Not the map—that was Maren's inheritance, and she had surrendered it formally, page by photographed page. Not the ledger—that was hers too, her father's true profession revealed at last: not prospector but witness, a man who had spent his final decades walking closed lines and writing down what the syndicate paid to keep unwritten. This part was Dominic's alone. The survey. The numbers. The signature.

"The court has heard," Magistrate Corvus said, "that you served as assistant surveyor on the Sepik Range extension between the years '86 and '89. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"Under what name?"

"My own. Dominic Montrose." He let the silence sit for a moment, because the next words deserved their weight. "I have not used it since the spring of 1890. Until today."

"Why did you abandon it?"

The syndicate's counsel stirred, objecting to relevance, and Corvus overruled them with a flick of two fingers, and Dominic understood that she wanted this answer as much as the gallery did. Perhaps more. There was a hunger in her face that went beyond procedure.

"Because I signed a document I should have burned," Dominic said. "In the autumn of '88 I was given revised figures for the Tunnel Nine approach seam—a pressure reading, a gas analysis. The numbers said the seam was cold. Dead rock. Safe to blast through." He paused. His mouth had gone dry, and he accepted the water the bailiff offered without shame. "I checked the arithmetic. It was flawless. That should have been my first warning—field data is never flawless. But I was twenty-six years old, and the chief engineer told me the assays came from the company laboratory, and I wanted the line finished. I wanted my name on a completed railroad. So I signed the certification, and the certification authorized the charge, and the charge went off on the fourth of March, 1889, and it brought the seam down and it brought the tunnel down, and thirty-one men were inside."

Nobody in the gallery moved. Even the fans seemed to slow.

"The company reported it as a survey casualty," he went on, because he had promised himself he would not stop, would not soften, would not do what he had done for six years and let the sentence trail off into fog. "A mapping party lost in the high country. No tunnel. No seam. No names. I found the discrepancy within the month—I saw the internal reports, I knew what the numbers had been before the laboratory revised them—and I said nothing. I gathered what proof I could, and I ran. I changed my name and I hid in river towns and I repaired locomotives under false papers for six years, and I told myself that staying alive was a kind of resistance. It was not. It was a kind of cowardice with good posture." He set the glass down. "That is why I abandoned my name. And I would ask the court to note that I am not asking for it back. I am asking to be judged under it."

Corvus removed her spectacles. For a long moment she only looked at him, and the lamplight moved on her lenses like water.

"Noted," she said.

* * *

The syndicate's cross-examination lasted two days, and it was conducted with the courtesy of knives.

Their lead counsel was a pale, precise man named Aldous Renk, and he never raised his voice, which was how Dominic knew he was dangerous. Renk walked him through the survey certifications page by page, and each question landed exactly where a blade lands when the wielder knows anatomy: *Did you verify the laboratory's methods? Did you request the raw field readings? Did you, at any time prior to the fourth of March, express doubt to your superiors?* And the truthful answers—no, no, once, briefly, and I let it go—were laid out in sequence like exhibits, building, brick by brick, the portrait of a man whose negligence had killed thirty-one people just as surely as the dynamite had.

"He wants you angry," Maren had warned him, the night before. She had a way of talking strategy the way her father must have talked terrain—flat, practical, unsentimental. "Angry men defend themselves. Defending yourself looks like guilt. Give him the truth whole and don't flinch when he makes it ugly. Ugly is what happened. Let it be ugly."

So Dominic gave him the truth whole. When Renk asked whether a competent engineer ought to have detected the falsification, Dominic said, "Yes." When Renk asked whether his signature had made the blasting legally possible, he said, "Yes." When Renk, in his closing flourish, asked whether the court should weigh the testimony of a man who had fled justice for six years, Dominic leaned toward the little brass microphone and said, "Weigh it however you like, sir. I'm not going anywhere."

Something shifted in the gallery at that. He felt it rather than heard it—a settling, like ballast shifting in a hull. Later, Teo would tell him that the telegraph boys in the corridor had started repeating the line to each other, and that by evening it was all over the wire traffic on the coast, and that a printer on Anchor Street had set it in type for a broadside that was selling for two bits a copy by week's end. *I'M NOT GOING ANYWHERE.* Words traveled strangely in the world now, faster than any courier, faster than any gunboat. The syndicate had built its empire on controlling what moved along the lines—freight, ore, information, men. And now the lines themselves were carrying the thing that would undo them.

The verdicts came in pieces, the way ice goes out of a river—not all at once but in groaning shifts, leads opening, floes turning.

First the charter. A federal examiner arrived from the capital with a warrant and a team of clerks, and for nine days the syndicate's Port Vessel headquarters gave up its paper like a wounded animal giving up blood: payroll ghosts, double books, the laboratory's original gas readings preserved by a clerk with a conscience who had waited six years for someone to ask. On the twelfth day, the territorial court revoked the Consolidated Sepik Mining & Railway Company's charter, and the newsboys were shouting it before the ink dried, and in Karawari Landing—so Odile reported, having wired her old river contacts—men who had spent years looking over their shoulders walked into the company office and took down the company sign and burned it in a barrel on the landing, and nobody stopped them.

Then the arrests. Three directors taken quietly at the Grand Meridian. A fourth already gone—ship's manifest showed a berth to the southern colonies under a name that fooled no one—and warrants went out along every wire in the territory. Captain Harrow was beyond warrants. The ice bridge had settled that account, and though Dominic had watched the man die, he found he could not summon satisfaction, only a tired sorrow for the men Harrow had led onto that bridge, men who had perhaps believed, right up to the cracking of the ice, that they were on the side of order.

And last—last, because the court saved it deliberately, and everyone in the gallery knew it would come last—the matter of Dominic Montrose.

* * *

Magistrate Corvus delivered her ruling on a gray Thursday with snow ticking against the courtroom windows.

"On the matter of the falsified certification of March 1889," she read, her voice level and clear, "this court finds that the respondent acted with negligence, that the negligence contributed materially to the deaths of thirty-one persons, and that his subsequent concealment prolonged the injury done to their families and to the public record." She paused, and turned a page, and looked up over her spectacles directly at him. "This court further finds that the respondent's concealment was coerced in part by documented threats from the Consolidated Sepik Company; that his evidence has been material to the dissolution of a criminal enterprise; and that his return to open testimony, under his true name, constitutes the beginning of an accounting rather than an evasion of one."

The sentence, when it came, was neither the absolution he feared he didn't deserve nor the ruin he knew he did. Two years' suspension of his engineer's license, retroactive, with reinstatement contingent on supervised practice. Restitution of his survey earnings from the Sepik extension—every dollar—to a fund for the families of the thirty-one, administered by the court. And a public censure entered into the record, permanent, unsealable, a scar on his name that no verdict and no headline would ever fully close.

"Some will say this is too light," Corvus said, folding her pages, "and some will say it is too heavy. Both may be right. The law cannot restore what was taken in Tunnel Nine. It can only insist that what remains be dealt with honestly. Mr. Montrose—you have chosen honesty late. Late is not never. The court notes the difference, and expects you to live up to it."

He stood while she said it, because sitting felt wrong, and when she finished he said, "Thank you, Your Honor," and his voice held, mostly, and he walked out of the witness box a censured, suspended, diminished man who had not been called Dominic Montrose in six years and was now, irrevocably, Dominic Montrose again—flaws and debts and all, entered into the light.

Maren met him at the courthouse steps. Snow was coming down fine and steady, dusting the shoulders of the crowd that had waited hours in the cold to hear a verdict about a tunnel most of them had never seen. She didn't embrace him. That wasn't her way. She stood beside him instead, close enough that their sleeves touched, and watched the crowd watch him, and said, "My father spent twenty years carrying that map, waiting for someone who wouldn't sell it. I used to think he was waiting for a hero." She shook her head slowly. "He was waiting for a witness. Same as you."

Teo came barreling down the steps a moment later with a telegram flapping in his fist, breathless, cheeks red, the borrowed jacket finally fitting because Odile had taken it in herself with a sailmaker's needle, one-handed, stubborn as rope. "Wire from the capital," the boy panted. "The federal commission—they're reopening every closed line in the basin. Every one. And they want—" he glanced at the paper, wanting to get it exactly right— "*a licensed engineer of demonstrated familiarity with the Sepik surveys, to advise on safe rehabilitation of the Tunnel Nine corridor.*" He looked up, grinning. "That's you. That's got to be you."

Dominic took the telegram and read it twice. His license was suspended. His name was a byword. His hands, holding the thin paper, were shaking in a way they hadn't shaken on the switchbacks, hadn't shaken in Harrow's gunsights, hadn't shaken standing before the magistrate himself.

"They know that," Maren said quietly, watching his face. "Suspended isn't struck. Supervised practice—advising is supervised practice. Corvus wrote that clause wide enough to drive a locomotive through, and I'd wager my father's map she meant to." A rare, crooked smile crossed her face, Elias Wren's smile, resurrected. "The dead don't want your shame, Dominic. They've got their names back. What they want now is the line opened. Honest rails, honest grades, honest books. Somebody's got to survey it who knows where the lies were buried."

Behind them, at the foot of the courthouse steps, Odile Ferrant stood waiting with her sling and her river-gray eyes, and beyond her, at the harbor, the *Kestrel* rode at her moorings with fresh paint on her strakes and steam already breathing gentle from her stack—refitted, restoked, restless, as if the boat herself had heard the telegram first and was impatient to be gone.

"Well?" Odile called up to him, over the snow and the crowd and the six long years between the man he had been and the man standing on these steps. "Are you coming, engineer? Spring's coming early this year, and the river won't wait."

Dominic folded the telegram into his breast pocket, over his heart, where a dying man's promise had ridden for a thousand miles, and started down the stairs toward them.
