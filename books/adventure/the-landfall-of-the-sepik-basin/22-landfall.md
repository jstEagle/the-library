## 22. Landfall

Spring came to Port Vessel the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

One morning in late March, Dominic Montrose woke in the loft above Odile's boathouse to a sound he had not heard since he was a young man with an unbroken name: gulls arguing over something worth having. He lay still beneath the wool blanket and listened. The harbor ice had gone out overnight. He could hear it in the water's voice—the slap of open swell against pilings instead of the dull groan of floes grinding themselves to pieces. Somewhere below, Odile was already moving, her boots on the stairs announcing her mood before her mouth could: brisk, even, purposeful.

He dressed in the gray light and went down.

The Kestrel sat in the slings of the marine railway like a patient coming out of a long fever. All winter they had worked on her—Teo up in the rigging with tar on his hands and rope burns on his palms, Maren below with the ledgers and the paint scraper, Odile directing everything from the deck with the particular tyranny of a woman who loved a vessel the way other people loved children. Dominic had rebuilt her boiler himself, welding seams by feel in the dark of February when the cold made iron sing. Now she gleamed. Her hull wore fresh black; her wheelhouse windows were new glass; her name, repainted in white letters tall enough to read across the harbor, caught the first true sunlight of the year.

*KESTREL*, it said. Just that. No owner's port, no syndicate house flag. A boat belonging to no one but the people standing on her.

"You're late," Odile said, without turning around. She was coiling line at the rail, her dark hair tied back with a strip of red cloth Teo had won off a fisherman at cards. "The chandler opens at seven, and I intend to be first through his door before every other captain in this town remembers spring exists."

"The boiler gauge needed calibrating."

"The boiler gauge has been calibrated three times." She glanced at him then, one eyebrow raised, and there was something in her face that had not been there a year ago—not softness exactly, because Odile Ferrant would sooner scuttle herself than go soft, but ease. The absence of the flinch. "You sleep badly when you're happy, Dominic. It's a flaw we'll work on."

He almost laughed. A year ago he would not have recognized the sound if it had come out of him; now it came easily, worn smooth like a river stone.

* * *

The chandler's yard smelled of pitch and hemp and coffee, and by eight o'clock it was full of their cargo: flour in hundred-pound sacks stenciled with the mark of the Vessel Milling Cooperative, salt pork, lamp oil, nails, bolts of cotton, a crate of schoolbooks for Anji that Maren had argued the price down on until the bookseller nearly wept. Honest freight, honest prices, honest towns—the mining settlements upriver and over the passes, the ones the syndicate had bled dry for twenty years and abandoned when the veins thinned. The gazette's exposé had run in eleven installments through the winter. The federal magistrate's warrants had gone out in January. Two directors had surrendered; two had run; Harrow's name was on a stone cairn under forty feet of glacier meltwater, and no one mourned him except perhaps the men who had followed him onto the ice bridge and lived.

The charter had been revoked in February. Dominic had read the headline standing in the gazette office doorway, hat in hand, feeling nothing so simple as triumph. What he felt was closer to the sensation of setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten its shape.

"Montrose!" Teo came skidding around a stack of crates, fourteen going on fifteen, taller by two inches since autumn, his telegrapher's fingers now calloused into a rigger's hands. He was carrying a telegram like it might bite. "Wire came up from Karawari Landing. Old man Sefu says the landing stage is repaired—they want us to stop there on the way up. They're rebuilding the town. They want—" he squinted at the paper, grinning—"they want to know if we carry passengers."

Odile took the wire, read it twice, and handed it back. "Passengers," she said. "Freight rates I understand. Passengers mean liability. Passengers mean someone's grandmother complaining about the coffee."

"They mean fares," said Maren Wren, emerging from the office with the manifest under her arm. She had cut her hair short over the winter and it made her look more like Elias every day—the same stubborn jaw, the same eyes that weighed a man and found him lighter than he claimed. "And the basin's full of people who need to get somewhere now that the syndicate's not charging them a month's wages for the privilege. We'd be fools to say no."

"We are frequently fools," Odile observed. "It's why the boat floats. Fools ballast her nicely."

Dominic watched the four of them argue it out in the chandler's yard—Teo lobbying hard for passengers because passengers meant stories, Maren already drafting rate schedules in the margins of her manifest, Odile pretending resistance while her eyes did the arithmetic she'd already finished—and felt the thing again, the thing he had no better word for than landfall. The moment when a coastline resolves out of fog. You don't always know you've arrived until the ground is under your feet.

A year ago he had kept every door unlocked and every window shuttered. A year ago he had been a man named Sam Corliss who fixed locomotives in a muddy town that couldn't remember its own name, and invisibility had been the whole of his ambition: to pass through the world leaving no print, owing nothing, belonging nowhere, safe. He had thought safety was the same as peace. Elias Wren had died on his shed floor teaching him otherwise—that a promise carried is heavier than any cargo, and that the carrying is the point.

"Dom." Teo tugged his sleeve. "You went somewhere."

"Just checking the weather," Dominic said, which was half true. He looked south, toward the range. The summits still wore snow, but it was the clean snow of high country in spring, not the killing kind. The passes would be open within the month. Tunnel Nine stood collapsed and sealed now—sealed properly, with a federal order and a granite marker and thirty-one names cut deep, names Maren had recovered ledger by ledger while the rest of them held the lanterns. Trains would never run that grade again. But men were already walking it, surveying a wagon road along the old right-of-way, and the first wagons would need flour and lamp oil and schoolbooks too.

"When do we cast off?" he asked.

Odile smiled the way a knife smiles. "High tide," she said. "Eleven forty."

* * *

They cast off at noon anyway, because Odile refused to leave on anyone's schedule but the tide's, and the tide, she announced, was running lazy.

Port Vessel turned out to see them go—or some portion of it did: the gazette's editor, a small ferocious man named Quill who had staked his paper's survival on their story and won; the magistrate's clerk, who shook Dominic's hand with both of his and said, "We couldn't have done it without your testimony, Mr. Montrose," and Dominic had let the name sit in the open air between them like a coin placed on a table; a knot of dockworkers who cheered the Kestrel's lines off the bollards; and one old woman from the mission who pressed a bundle of dried fish into Teo's arms and told him to write to her, which he promised to do, and meant.

Teo stood at the bow as the harbor opened out, working the lead line out of habit, calling depths in the clear bright voice that had once, a lifetime ago, tapped out a warning down a telegraph wire: *intercept the courier*. The boy had intercepted instead. He had chosen a side with nothing to gain by it, and the choosing had made him theirs.

Maren was below, lashing cargo, singing something low and tuneless that might have been a hymn or might have been a mine song with the profanity sanded off. Her father's map case hung at her hip now, the waterproof leather gone soft with handling. Inside it, alongside the original map, rode copies of everything: the falsified surveys, the payroll ghosts, the list of thirty-one names, the affidavits. Elias Wren had made his daughter a witness before she ever knew what she was witnessing. Dominic understood now that the old man hadn't sent him a map at all. He had sent him a torch, and trusted a stranger to keep it lit.

Odile had the wheel. She steered the way she did everything—economically, contemptuously, perfectly—and when Dominic climbed to the wheelhouse she didn't surrender the wheel but shifted enough to make room for him at the glass.

"Course?" he asked.

"Up the coast to the delta, then the east channel to Karawari. Sefu's passengers, if the old man hasn't imagined them. Then the basin towns as far as Broken Narrows while the water's high." She glanced at him. "Then home."

Home. She said it the way she said *tide*, as though it were simply a fact of water, requiring no argument.

"I've been thinking about the winter," Dominic said. "About what you said. That I sleep badly when I'm happy."

"I say many wise things. It's exhausting."

"You asked me once why I stayed hidden so long. And I gave you an answer about fear, and it was true, but it wasn't the whole truth." He watched the coastline slide past, green and indifferent, gulls riding the Kestrel's wake. "The whole truth is that hiding was easier than being known. If no one knew my name, no one could expect anything of me. No one could be disappointed. I told myself it was safety, but it was really just a smaller cage with the door painted open."

"And now?"

"Now the door's actually open, and I find I'm not running through it." He turned to look at her—really look, the way he had learned to over a winter of shared work and shared silence and one terrible confession in a snowbound signal hut. "I'm staying on this boat. Not because I'm hiding on it. Because I chose it."

Odile studied him for a long moment, and then she did something she almost never did: she surrendered the wheel. "Hold her steady," she said. "Two points off the headland." And she crossed to the little shelf where she kept the coffee things and poured two cups, black, and handed him one, and drank hers looking at him over the rim with an expression that in any other woman might have been called tender and in Odile was called nothing at all, because it didn't need naming.

"To the Kestrel," she said. "Honest freight, honest crew, honest rates."

"Honest crew," Dominic agreed, and touched his cup to hers.

Below them the river widened, brown and glittering, carrying spring melt down from the passes where thirty-one names stood carved in granite and the snow was letting go of the high country at last. Teo's voice floated up from the bow, calling the depth, and Maren's song rose underneath it, and the engine beat its steady heart, and Dominic Montrose stood at the wheel of a boat with his own name on no papers anywhere and his true name written plainly in the affections of the three people aboard, and found that the second registration was the only one that mattered.

The headland fell away behind them. Ahead, the channel ran wide and open into the long light of the north, and the Kestrel went up it like a word finally spoken after years held in the mouth—

—and at the mouth of the east channel, where the current braided and the willows stood ankle-deep in floodwater, a figure stood on the broken staging of the old Karawari landing with a lantern raised high against the afternoon, signaling them in, and beside the figure, small and unmistakable, a boy's kite climbed the wind on a string of telegraph wire, flying at last.

*THE END*
