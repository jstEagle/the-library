## 1. Salt in the Wires

The freighter had been dead for thirty-one years, and Rafael Okafor was reasonably sure it resented him for waking it up.

He could feel its mood through his gloves — a low grumble of rust and shifting cargo as he pulled himself along the hull, forty feet down in water the color of strong tea. The *Kumusi Star* lay on her port side in the delta mud like an old man who'd lain down in a field and simply decided to stay there. Barnacles had colonized her railings. A fishing net from some long-forgotten argument hung off her propeller shaft, waving at him in the current with what Rafael chose to interpret as sarcasm.

"Easy, old girl," he murmured into his regulator, though nobody down here spoke English and the fish certainly didn't care.

Above him, filtered through the brown water, the morning sun sat on the surface like a coin at the bottom of a well. That was the thing about the Fly River delta that no brochure ever mentioned: you didn't dive in water, you dove in broth. Silt, tannin, silt again, and somewhere in it all the ghosts of every log drive and dredge barge since 1880. Visibility ran about six feet on a good day, which this was not. On a bad day you dove by braille and prayer, and Rafael had done enough of both to last several lifetimes.

He found the breach aft of the engine room where the survey chart said it would be — because the survey chart was his own work, drawn three weeks ago when the navy's money first cleared, and because Rafael Okafor did not go into wrecks blind. Not anymore. Once, long ago, he'd been the kind of diver who went in on instinct and charm, all grin and no plan. That diver had gotten Ekon killed, and Rafael had buried him along with whatever part of himself used to find any of this fun.

The hole was tight. He worked his shoulders through sideways, exhaling slow, feeling the wreck close around him like a fist deciding whether to squeeze. Inside: darkness, the tick of cooling metal that hadn't been cool for decades, and the particular silence of a ship holding its breath.

His light swept across a corridor of collapsed bunks, a coffee urn fused to a bulkhead by corrosion, a pair of rubber sandals standing neatly side by side as if their owner had just stepped out to complain about the weather. Thirty-one years, and still nobody had come back for them. Rafael made himself look away. There were things down here that wanted your attention, and attention was a currency he'd learned to spend carefully.

The crate was where the manifest said it would be — hold two, starboard side, lashed under a pallet of rusted drums. Except "crate" was doing generous work. It was a steel box the size of a coffin, stenciled in flaking white paint:

**R.N.Z.N. — PROPERTY — DO NOT OPEN — HOLD FOR COLLECTION**

And beneath that, a serial number that matched, digit for digit, the number written on the waterproof card zipped into the pocket of his buoyancy vest.

Rafael hovered over it a moment, letting his breathing settle. In through the nose, out through the mouth. The trick to salvage diving, he liked to tell the younger men at the dock, was that ninety percent of the job happened before you touched anything. You did your homework. You knew your exits. You never, ever let the wreck know you were in a hurry, because wrecks could smell hurry the way sharks could smell blood, only with less manners.

He rigged the lift bag. Checked the lashings. Found the pallet bolts sheared clean through — someone had already tried to move this box once, a long time ago, and failed, and left it here like a promise nobody kept.

*Paid not to open.* That was the actual clause. He'd read the contract four times on the flight into Port Moresby, mostly because reading it was cheaper than thinking about why he needed the money. *Contractor will recover item per manifest. Contractor will not open, inspect, or photograph item. Breach voids payment in full.*

Which meant, translated out of contract-speak: *we are paying you a truly stupid amount of money to not be curious.*

Rafael had been curious his whole life. Curiosity was how you found the good wrecks. Curiosity was also, if you asked the Okonkwo family, how their son ended up in a casket with a closed lid, and Rafael had learned — expensively, permanently — that some questions cost more than they paid.

He clipped the line to the lift bag and gave it air.

The crate rose off the pallet with a groan of protesting steel, shedding a curtain of rust that turned the water around him the color of dried blood. For one absurd second Rafael thought of the story Old Tamu told at the Kiwai landing — about the river keeping what it was given, and charging interest on anything you took back. Tamu told that story to every diver who came through, and every diver laughed, and Rafael had laughed too, once.

Now he watched the crate climb toward the light and thought: *interest.*

* * *

On the surface, the world reassembled itself in pieces. First sound — the diesel idle of his boat, the *Second Chance*, which he'd named during a period of optimism he now regarded as a youthful indiscretion. Then smell — salt, two-stroke exhaust, and the green rot of the mangroves lining the channel. Then the sky, enormous and pale, and the delta stretching out flat as spilled milk in every direction.

The crate broke the surface beside him, streaming brown water, and Rafael hauled it over the gunwale with a grunt that cost him something in his lower back he intended to invoice somebody for eventually.

It sat on the deck plating, dripping. Sealed. Silent. Weighing approximately as much as his conscience.

"You're not going to open it," he told it.

The crate said nothing, being a crate, but it managed to say nothing very loudly.

Rafael stripped his gear, wrung out his hood, and put the kettle on the single burner he kept wedged next the wheelhouse. Tea first. Decisions after tea. This was another rule, and unlike most of his rules it had never once gotten anyone killed.

While the water heated, he took out the tin that lived under the chart drawer. Inside: a compass. Brass-cased, glass scratched to frost, the needle still swinging true after everything. Ekon Okonkwo's compass. Ekon had bought it in Dar es Salaam with his first real pay, and he'd held it up to the light like a jeweler and said, *Rafael, my friend, this is the difference between being lost and being adventurous.* And then, three years later, off a rig in the Timor Sea, Ekon had gone down on a line that should have held and didn't, and Rafael had surfaced alone with the compass in his pocket because Ekon had pressed it into his hand at the last second — or because Rafael had torn it off him, depending on which version of the memory he could stand that day.

Either way, he carried it. Carried it the way you carry a debt that has stopped being financial and become architectural — load-bearing, built into the walls of you.

Because that was the other ledger. The official one. After the accident, Ekon's mother, Grace Okonkwo, had refused to let Rafael so much as apologize at the funeral without also handing her an envelope, and when he'd finally worked up the courage to visit, she'd looked at him with eyes that held no anger at all, which was infinitely worse, and said, "You were there. You came back. So you owe us the years he owed us."

She'd said it gently. She'd said it like arithmetic. And Rafael, who could argue his way out of most things, had simply nodded, because she was right. He'd been sending money home to Port Moresby ever since — irregular amounts, guilt-indexed — and the balance remaining stood at roughly the price of one sealed navy crate, recovered unopened, no questions asked.

The kettle screamed. He poured, sweetened it far past the point of dignity, and sat on the crate itself while he drank, because there was nowhere else dry to sit and because it amused him to use government property as furniture.

Through the wheelhouse window, the delta went on being beautiful in its indifferent way — egrets stalking the shallows, a dugout canoe crossing the far channel with a family and what appeared to be an entire wardrobe aboard, smoke rising from a village he couldn't name. People lived out here the way barnacles lived on the *Kumusi Star*: stubbornly, in places the maps barely bothered with.

His radio crackled. Static, then a voice, thin and clipped through the interference.

"*Okafor. Confirm recovery.*"

Rafael set down his mug. "Recovered. One item, hold two, condition as expected. She's on deck and she's ugly."

A pause. When the voice came back it had shed its military flatness by exactly one degree, which from Commander Voss was practically a hug. "*Good. Keep it sealed. Collection team meets you at Kiwai landing, oh-eight-hundred tomorrow. There'll be additional work after. Well compensated.*"

"There usually is," Rafael said. "That's generally why people do it."

Another pause, longer. Somewhere in the static, Rafael thought he caught a fragment of another transmission underneath — a woman's voice, ragged, half-swallowed by interference, saying something that might have been *—repeat, upper reaches—* before Voss's channel snapped back like a door closing.

"*Kiwai landing,*" the voice repeated. "*Oh-eight-hundred. Don't be late, Okafor.*"

The radio clicked dead.

Rafael sat with his tea going cold and looked at the crate, and then at the radio, and then at the compass tin still open on the chart table, Ekon's needle swinging lazily north as if pointing at something specific. Something upstream.

That voice under the static. He'd heard it for half a second, maybe less, and it shouldn't have meant anything — the delta was full of voices, most of them selling fish or complaining about fuel prices. But there was a cadence to it, a clipped precision even through the ruin of interference, and it reached back eleven years and tapped him on the shoulder like an old classmate.

Dr. Amara Sesay. Who had once told him, over cheap wine on a pier in Thursday Island, that he was too careful for a man whose job was swimming into wrecks. Who had left before he worked up the nerve to disagree.

"No," Rafael said aloud, to the compass, the crate, the river, and the general direction of his own common sense. "Absolutely not."

The needle kept pointing north.

He finished the tea, sealed the tin, and turned the boat toward Kiwai landing.
