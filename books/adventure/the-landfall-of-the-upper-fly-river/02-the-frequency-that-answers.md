## 2. The Frequency That Answers

The crate sat in Rafael's kitchen for six days before he opened it.

He told himself this was discipline. A professional does not open what he is paid not to open. But the truth was less flattering: he kept walking past it on his way to the kettle, and each time he walked past it, the crate seemed to grow a little larger, the way a debt grows when you're not looking at it directly. By day four it had achieved roughly the dimensions of a coffin. By day six he put a dish towel over it, which helped not at all, because now it looked like a coffin with a hat.

He was boiling water for coffee he didn't want when the knock came — three sharp raps, spaced like someone who expected doors to obey him.

The man on his veranda wore civilian clothes the way some men wear disguises: correctly, but without conviction. Khaki shirt pressed to knife-edge creases, sunglasses pushed up into grey-flecked hair, and shoes that had never once been near mud. He held out an identification wallet that said *Ministry of Fisheries* in letters that were trying very hard to be boring.

"Commander Voss," the man said. "Navy liaison. You're Okafor."

"Fisheries," Rafael said. "That's new."

"We do a great deal of work protecting fish." Voss's smile was brief and administrative. "May I come in? It's hot."

It was hot. That was the delta's one honest feature — everything else about it lied: the water looked like tea and tasted like iron, the sky looked soft and could cut you to ribbons by noon, and the river looked slow while moving faster than any current Rafael had ever fought. He stepped aside and let the navy onto his veranda, because refusing would have been more interesting than he could afford.

Voss looked at the crate under its dish towel. He did not ask about it. That, more than anything, told Rafael the man already knew exactly what it was.

"Coffee?" Rafael offered, mostly to watch him decide whether divers' kitchens met naval standards.

"Thank you, no." Voss settled into the wicker chair that creaked under anyone, a chair with opinions. He produced a folder — actual paper, actual string tie, like something from a war his grandfather had lost. "I have a contract for you. Sensitive work. Well paid."

"Everything well paid is sensitive. That's how I know to charge extra."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth that might, in a different man, have become amusement. "A scientific expedition went upriver eleven days ago. Hydrographic survey. They stopped transmitting four days ago." He laid a photograph on the table: a riverboat, white hull, banks of antennae, a name stenciled on the bow — *MV Meridian Star*. "We need our relay buoys raised and repositioned along the upper channel so we can re-establish contact. Diving work. Your kind of work."

"My kind of work is rust and silt in the delta," Rafael said. "The upper Fly is a different animal. The villages up there don't love visitors, and the river doesn't love anybody."

"The river doesn't have to love you. It only has to let you work." Voss slid the folder across. "The fee is triple your usual rate. Paid on completion. There is also a completion bonus, if the equipment comes back undamaged."

Triple. Rafael turned the number over in his mind the way he'd turn a coin found on a riverbed — checking both faces. Triple meant urgency. Triple meant somebody upstream was embarrassed, or frightened, or both. And triple meant the Okonkwo family's ledger in Port Moresby could finally be closed — every kina of the money he'd borrowed after the dive that killed Ekon, the money he'd sworn on a grave to repay even if it took him the rest of his life, which at his current rate of saving it would.

"You said 'we,'" Rafael said. "Who's we, exactly? Because the last time the navy hired me, the paperwork said fisheries too, and the crate they had me raise is currently wearing a dish towel in my kitchen."

Voss regarded the towel-covered crate with the patience of a man who had interrogated better liars in worse rooms. "Consider this job a way to make amends for whatever you imagine that crate contains."

"I don't imagine anything. I just don't open things."

"A wise policy." Voss stood, buttoned his jacket against no weather at all. "The expedition departed from Kiwai. My launch can take you as far as the old mission landing tomorrow at dawn. Think about the money, Mr. Okafor. I'm told you're a man who understands debts."

He left the folder on the table. Rafael watched the launch carry him back downriver, sitting straight-backed in the bow like a man being carried somewhere he'd rather command, and then Rafael picked up the folder and read it twice through, standing in the heat, while the river muttered its endless brown monologue below the stilts.

* * *

The recording was the third thing in the folder, after the map and the payment schedule, and it nearly didn't get played at all. An old cassette — who used cassettes anymore except navies and ghosts? — labeled in ballpoint: *MERIDIAN STAR, FINAL TRANSMISSION (PARTIAL).*

Rafael dug the player out of a box under his bed, blew dust off the heads, and pressed play mostly because the alternative was another evening negotiating with the crate.

Static first. Then a voice fighting through it, clipped and professional, the kind of voice trained to stay calm because other people's lives depended on the calm:

"—Meridian Star to base, do you copy. We've moved the second camp east of the sinkhole lake, grid reference as filed. The formation is — hold on—" A pause. Water sounds, or wind. Then the voice again, and here something changed in it, something loosened, wonder cracking through procedure like light through a door left open a hand's width. "You should see it, base. The whole hillside is hollow. There are galleries down there older than any map we have, and the water in them is — clear. After days of this river, clear water feels like a miracle. We're going down tomorrow with the sonar rig. Sesay out."

Rafael stopped the tape with his thumb.

He knew that voice.

Not from a briefing, not from a personnel file. From a lifetime ago, in another country, in a university diving club where the equipment was held together with hope and the social events were held together with rum. Amara Sesay, who could name every fish in a reef and argue politics with a professor until the professor surrendered. Amara, who had once talked him out of a dive he wasn't ready for, and thereby saved his life before she ever knew she'd done it. Amara, who had written him two letters after Ekon died and received no answer to either, because answering meant saying out loud what he'd done wrong, and he hadn't been able to afford that yet.

She'd gone quiet in his life the way good things go quiet — gradually, then completely.

And now her voice was on a navy cassette, describing a hole in the earth full of impossibly clear water, four days after her last transmission, in a folder carried by a man whose shoes had never touched mud.

Rafael played it again. And again. On the fourth pass he heard what he'd missed: beneath Sesay's voice, low in the static, a second transmission bleeding through from another frequency. Not words. A pattern — three tones, pause, three tones. Deliberate. Repeating.

Somebody else was still transmitting from up there. Somebody who didn't want the navy to hear it, or didn't know the navy was listening, or — and this was the thought that made him set down the coffee he'd finally poured — wanted whoever heard it to understand that *Meridian Star* had not simply gone silent.

He sat on his veranda until the mosquitoes came up off the water in their evening battalions, the cassette case in his hand, doing arithmetic.

Triple pay cleared the debt. The debt was the wall between him and the rest of his life; he'd built it himself, brick by brick, out of guilt and installments. One job behind that wall and he could stand in front of Mama Okonkwo with his head up. That was the offer on the table, and it was a good one, and good offers from men like Voss were exactly the kind of thing Ekon would have laughed at. *Brother,* Ekon would have said, grinning through his regulator, *when the water is too clear, it means something underneath is dead.*

Below him the Fly River rolled past, brown and patient, carrying toward the sea whatever the hills upstream chose to give it.

Rafael packed his dive bag by lamplight: mask, fins, the good torch, spare line. Last of all, from the tin on his shelf, he took Ekon's compass — brass, dented, the needle trembling north like a small stubborn heart — and dropped it into his chest pocket, where it always rode.

Then he looked at the crate under its dish towel.

He didn't open it. Discipline, he told himself. Professional discipline.

But he moved it to the far corner of the room, away from the bed, where it couldn't watch him sleep — and in the morning, when Voss's launch sounded its horn at the mission landing, Rafael Okafor was already there, bag on his shoulder, triple-rate contract signed, and a question riding in his chest pocket next to a brass compass:

What had Amara Sesay found down in the clear dark water — and why did the navy need a diver to raise its radios before it would admit she'd ever gone missing at all?

Old Tamu was waiting at the wheel of the boat, chewing betel nut, watching him come with eyes that had measured every fool who ever pointed a motor upstream.

"You're the one going after the scientists," Tamu said. It wasn't a question either.

"That's right."

Tamu spat red over the side, unhurried, and nodded at the empty seat beside him. "Sit down, then. River's long, and you look like a man with questions."
