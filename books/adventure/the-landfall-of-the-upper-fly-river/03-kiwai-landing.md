## 3. Kiwai Landing

The boat was called *Patience*, which Rafael took as either a joke or a warning, and by the second hour upstream he suspected both.

She was a long-tail with a diesel engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before every difficult decision, and she belonged to Old Tamu, who had agreed — for a sum Rafael considered generous and Tamu plainly considered insulting — to take him past the last mapped village and up into the country where maps went vague and apologetic. Tamu sat at the stern with one hand on the tiller and the other holding a thermos of tea he never seemed to drink from, and he had spoken perhaps forty words since dawn, thirty-nine of them navigational.

"Shoal," he said now, and swung the bow wide around a brown tongue of sand that hadn't existed on any chart Rafael had memorized in Port Moresby.

"Thank you," Rafael said.

Tamu grunted, which Rafael was beginning to understand was a complete sentence containing gratitude, agreement, and a mild rebuke all at once.

The Fly River delta did not look like water so much as it looked like the memory of water — braided channels splitting and rejoining, islands of cane and sago palm that appeared and dissolved depending on the light, the whole landscape breathing slowly in and out with the tide. Rafael had dived these waters for six years. He knew their moods the way you know a house you grew up in: which stair creaked, which door stuck. And he knew, the way you know when a house has been empty too long, that something about the river ahead of them was wrong.

Not dangerous-wrong. He'd have smelled danger; danger had a taste, a metallic edge on the back of the tongue. This was quieter than that. This was the wrongness of a room where people had stopped talking just before you walked in.

He'd felt it first at Sibidiri, two hours back. A village of stilt houses over the water, kids who should have swarmed the boat — kids always swarmed the boat, wanting to sell him betel nut or a carved crocodile or, once, memorably, a live cassowary chick — but today they'd watched from the railings without waving. When he'd called out asking after the expedition, the white boats with the scientific gear, an old woman had turned her face away with the deliberate slowness of someone closing a window. A boy, maybe ten, had made a small gesture Rafael didn't understand until later, when Tamu finally spent some of his word budget on it.

"They went quiet," Tamu said, meaning the expedition. Then, meaning the gesture: "River took its voice."

Which was not, Rafael reflected, the kind of thing you wanted to hear from your guide while heading directly toward wherever the river had taken it.

* * *

They stopped at midday at a place Tamu called only "the good bank," a shelf of gravel and pandanus where the current ran slow and the midges ran fast. Tamu built a fire with the efficiency of a man who had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more without ever finding it interesting, and put a tin of fish on to warm.

Rafael ate sitting on his heels, watching the water slide past. Brown, opaque, patient. Somewhere under that surface were things he understood: wrecks, cables, crates, the honest geometry of sunken machinery. He was going upstream because there was a job at the end of it, and jobs were the one language grief hadn't managed to ruin for him yet.

Except it wasn't only a job, and he knew it, and lying to himself about it had never been one of his talents. Amara Sesay's voice on that garbled recording — *relay four is down, we're moving camp, I don't like the water here, Rafa, if anyone hears this* — had reached through six years and grabbed him by the collar. She'd said *Rafa*. Nobody called him Rafa except her and Ekon, and Ekon was three years dead at the bottom of a harbor he should never have dived.

"You knew her," Tamu said. It wasn't a question. Tamu didn't seem to traffic in questions; he made statements and let you deal with them.

"The scientist? Yes. Years ago." Rafael turned the tin in his hands. "We worked a salvage season together, up in the Torres Strait. She mapped wrecks. I went inside them."

"And then?"

"And then my partner drowned, and I left, and she left, and that's generally how those stories go."

Tamu considered this with the gravity of a judge. "You are going to find her."

"I'm going to find out what happened to the expedition. That's what I'm paid for."

Tamu made the small sound that Rafael had catalogued as *doubt*, and poked the fire. "The navy pays you," he said. "The river doesn't. The river will want to know whose side you're on."

"I'll let it know when I've decided."

For the first time since dawn, Old Tamu smiled. It transformed his whole face from something weathered and closed into something weathered and open, like a door you suddenly realized was a door. "Good answer," he said. "Most men lie to the river. The river remembers liars."

* * *

By late afternoon the villages had thinned to nothing and the banks closed in, walls of green so dense they seemed poured rather than grown. Hornbills crossed overhead in pairs, wingbeats like loose shutters. Twice they passed floating logs that were, on closer inspection, crocodiles, regarding the boat with the flat professional interest of customs officers.

It was near dusk when Tamu cut the engine without warning and let *Patience* drift.

Ahead, maybe half a kilometer, a side channel opened westward, and at its mouth stood a navigation stake — a steel pole with a faded orange band, the kind the survey teams used. Except this one had been snapped off two meters above the waterline, the break clean and recent, and the top half floated nearby, tangled in cane.

"Somebody didn't want markers," Rafael said.

"Somebody didn't want anything," Tamu said. He restarted the engine and took them into the channel anyway, which told Rafael everything about how much Tamu feared whatever lay ahead versus how much he disliked leaving a question unanswered. "Camp is past the bend. First camp. I brought them here in the wet season. Good people. Loud." A pause. "Loud is good, in the bush. Quiet is when you worry."

The bend gave onto a wide oxbow lake fringed with reeds, and on the eastern shore, under a stand of karuka trees, stood the first camp.

Or what had been first camp.

Rafael saw it from two hundred meters out and his stomach began composing its report early. Tents down — not collapsed, *down*, poles snapped, guy-lines cut rather than untied. Equipment cases stacked neatly on the shore, which was somehow worse than if they'd been scattered. Neatness implied organization. Organization implied purpose. A generator sat in the mud with its fuel cap removed and its fuel gone. A solar array lay face-down like something shot.

And no people. No sign of people. No clothes, no gear abandoned in flight, no graves, no crosses. Just absence, arranged.

"They weren't robbed," Rafael said quietly, wading ashore. He crouched by the equipment stack. "Look. Radios still here. Cameras. Laptops worth more than this boat. Somebody went through everything and took nothing worth taking."

Tamu stood at the treeline, very still, watching the forest the way Rafael watched water — reading it. "What did they take?"

Rafael opened the nearest case. Sample trays, empty. Notebooks — he flipped one open — pages torn out, cleanly, in blocks. A hard drive case, open, empty. "Their records," he said. "Somebody took every scrap of paper and data and left the hardware. You rob a camp for the hardware. You strip a camp for the information."

He moved through the site with a diver's methodical calm, hands steady, mind running cold and fast. Mess tent: table set for eight, plates still on it, food long since gone to ants and birds but the plates *set*, nobody had eaten a final meal in panic. Sleeping tents: bedrolls rolled. Rolled. You didn't roll your bedroll if you left in a hurry. They had packed, calmly, and walked out — or been walked out.

At the edge of the camp, driven into the earth, stood a wooden post with a strip of survey tape fluttering from it, and beneath the tape, scratched into the wood with a knife in Amara's unmistakable hand — she drew her letters like an engineer, all right angles — a single line:

**SINKHOLE 4 KM N. WE FOUND THE SHIP. GO BACK, RAFA.**

His own name, in her handwriting, in the middle of a drowned green nowhere, hit him harder than the ransacked camp had. He stood there a long moment with his thumb on the carved letters, feeling the grooves, the way you'd feel a pulse.

"She knew I'd come," he said.

"Then she knows you better than the navy does," Tamu said behind him. "Navy sent you alone in a tin boat. She wrote *go back*."

"Yeah." Rafael looked north, where the reeds thickened and the land rose almost imperceptibly toward something the failing light wouldn't show him. Four kilometers. "She also wrote *we found the ship*, and she knew exactly what that would do to me. She's known me a long time. She knew I'd read *go back* and start walking forward."

The hornbills had gone silent. All along the oxbow, the evening noise of the bush — insects, frogs, the rustle of things feeding — continued, but around the camp itself there was a pocket of quiet, precise and unnatural, like the silence around a fresh grave.

Tamu touched his shoulder, briefly, a weight and a warning in one. "Night comes fast here. We can go back downriver, or we can go north."

Rafael looked at the carved words one more time. *Go back.* Ekon had said something like that once, laughing, on their last dive together: *if I ever tell you to turn back, Rafa, promise me you won't listen.* He'd laughed when he said it. Rafael had promised anyway. He'd kept that promise at the cost of everything, and he was tired — bone-tired, three years tired — of keeping promises to the dead that only dug the debt deeper.

"We go north," he said.

Tamu nodded once, unsurprised, and began rolling the bedrolls — rolled, Rafael noticed, calmly, the way you pack when you mean to come back — and above the treeline the first stars came out over a stretch of country no map admitted to, where a sinkhole waited with a ship inside it, and a voice that had once called him *Rafa* was four kilometers away, alive or not, loud or quiet, waiting to be found.
