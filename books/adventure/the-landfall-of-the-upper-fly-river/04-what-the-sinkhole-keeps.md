## 4. What the Sinkhole Keeps

Amara Sesay had learned, in thirty-one years of fieldwork on three continents, that fear had a texture. In the Sahara it had been dry and papery, like breathing through old paper. In the Congo it had been wet and close, a hand cupped over her mouth. Here, in the drowned country above the Fly's middle reaches, fear was green — a slow green light that came up out of the water at dusk and lay across everything like moss growing on the air itself.

She crouched in the lee of the overturned survey tent and watched the lake.

It was not supposed to be there. That was the first thing she made herself remember, every morning, every time the terror tried to rewrite the record: *it is not supposed to be there.* The satellite imagery showed karst, a limestone shelf, dry forest. The imagery was wrong by roughly four square kilometers of water so still it looked poured rather than fallen. The sinkhole — she had stopped calling it an anomaly weeks ago; it deserved its name — dropped away beneath their camp in terraces of blue-black dark, and somewhere down inside it, past the depth where light gave up, was the thing that had killed half her team.

Not the water itself. The water was only the messenger.

"Amara." Peter Anand's voice, low, from behind the collapsed mess tarp. "Boat."

Her heart did what it always did now, that ugly double-tap against her ribs. She crawled to him on her elbows through the ferns, careful of the mud, careful of the light. Down at the southern rim, where the river channel threaded back into the sinkhole's outflow, a longboat was working upstream against the current — one man poling, one man sitting very still in the bow.

"Not military," Peter murmured. He had a geologist's eyes, calibrated for distance and grain size, and he used them now on the boat the way he'd use them on a core sample. "No canopy. No radio mast. Just two men and a lot of fuel cans."

"It's the third boat this week," Amara said. "The others turned back."

"This one isn't turning back."

She watched it come. And because she was who she was — a woman who had once spent nine hours in a collapsing trench in Sulawesi taking notes because the data would be lost otherwise — she found herself cataloguing even now. Hull type: local, Kiwai-built, but with a repaired transom done in marine plywood, which meant money, or at least intent. The poling man moved like someone who knew this water the way other men knew their own kitchens. The man in the bow sat wrong for a passenger and right for a soldier pretending otherwise.

"They've sent someone," she said. "Finally. Or they've sent someone to make sure we stay missing."

"You want my professional opinion?"

"I want your honest one."

Peter smiled the small, tired smile he kept for moments when honesty was going to cost something. "Both options end with us deciding in the next ten minutes whether to run or hide. So let's do both badly and call it tactics."

* * *

They had hidden well, if Amara said so herself, and she did, because no one else was left to say it. After the second attack — after Nkem and Ruiz went into the water and did not come up, after the patrol boat with no markings swept the south shore with a searchlight like a finger running down a ledger — she and Peter had abandoned the main camp and built a ghost of themselves in the root buttress of a fallen bintangor tree, forty meters inland and invisible from the water unless you already knew. Two tarps. A stove they lit only at noon, when the smoke vanished into the heat. Their instruments wrapped in plastic and buried in a cache under a stone that weighed as much as Peter's regrets.

For eleven days they had lived like animals with doctorates. Peter had developed opinions about sago grubs that he shared freely and at length, mostly to keep them both from thinking about the graves.

That was the word she could not stop circling. *Graves.*

It had been Chidi — bright, doomed Chidi, who'd insisted on the first dive before anyone signed off on it — who had found the cave mouth. Sixty feet down the sinkhole's western wall, behind a curtain of vine-root, a gallery opening into the limestone like a throat. Chidi had gone in with a torch and come back up in eight minutes, white as bone dust, babbling about metal. *There's a ship down there. There's a whole ship.*

A munitions barge. Japanese, or Australian, or both — the war had chewed up this coast and swallowed the evidence. Forty meters of riveted steel resting on a shelf inside the flooded cavern system, her holds open, her cargo of shells and depth charges furred with sixty years of sediment. And laid out along the near bank of the cavern floor, arranged with a terrible orderliness that no shipwreck explains —

Amara pressed her palm flat against the earth until the trembling stopped. It never fully stopped anymore.

Prisoners. Laborers, conscripted, transported, worked, and when the barge was scuttled — deliberately, methodically, with the holds sealed and the hatches dogged from outside — shot or drowned or simply left below with it. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. Bones in the black water, ranked like survey stakes. And among the sediment around them, things that were not bones: crates stenciled with naval codes, a strongbox, documents in oilskin that Chidi had brought up one handful of before his air ran short.

Documents that named names. Documents that explained why, three days later, a navy patrol boat had appeared in a river the navy officially did not patrol, and why the expedition's satellite phone had begun receiving calls instead of making them, and why the calls had stopped being questions and started being instructions.

*Seal the site. Surrender the materials. Remain available for debriefing.*

Chidi had gone back down for the last crate on the fourth day. The sinkhole had kept him too.

* * *

The boat grounded at the south rim, twenty meters from their dead camp, and Amara watched the two men step ashore and felt the green fear climb another shade darker.

The big one moved first — broad, deliberate, checking the treeline with a competence that was not civilian. The other stayed with the boat a moment, and even at this distance something about the set of his shoulders snagged at her memory like a hook in cloth. Then he turned toward the ruined tents and the light caught his face, and Amara made a sound she hadn't planned.

"What?" Peter whispered.

"I know him." Her mouth had gone dry around the name. "Rafael Okafor."

"The diver? Your—"

"He dove with us in Palau. Before. With—" She stopped. Some sentences had rusted shut. "He was Ekon's partner. They were inseparable. Then the accident, and he just — vanished. Paid off everyone he owed and disappeared, Ekon's mother said. Like a man settling accounts before leaving the country."

"And now he's here, in the middle of nowhere, with a soldier."

"Yes," Amara said. "Which means either the navy found the one person alive who knows these waters and me, or Rafael Okafor sold himself to them. And I have spent eleven days hoping it's the first one, because I am not sure I can survive finding out it's the second."

Below, the big man — the soldier, clearly, whatever cover story he wore — kicked over the collapsed survey tent with the flat of his boot and said something she couldn't hear. Rafael didn't answer. He had gone still in the way divers go still, she remembered that, absolutely motionless at depth, reading the water. He was reading the camp. Reading what was missing.

Then he turned, slowly, and looked directly at the fallen bintangor tree where they were hiding.

Not at the treeline generally. At the tree. At the exact root buttress.

"Oh no," Peter breathed.

"He saw the smoke stain," Amara whispered. "Or the cache stone. Or he's just better at this than we are." Her heart was hammering out some new rhythm entirely now, fear and something dangerously adjacent to hope fighting for the same space. Eleven days of silence, of grief stacked on grief, of being hunted through a landscape that refused to hold them — and here was a face from the time before, from when diving had been joy and Ekon had been alive and the worst thing in the world had been bad weather.

Ekon. Oh, Ekon, your friend has come up my river wearing a stranger's face.

The soldier was saying something sharp. Rafael shook his head once. Then he raised his voice — carried it across the water with the ease of a man who had spent his life making himself heard through masks and depths:

"Dr. Sesay! I know you're up there. I dove with you in Palau — you called me 'the human anchor' because I sank like one!" A pause. The soldier grabbed his arm; he shook it off without looking. "I'm not with them. Ask me anything. Ask me what you spilled on my regulator in Palau!"

Amara closed her eyes. Opened them.

"Coffee," she said aloud, to the tree, to the day, to the dead. "Black, half a thermos, straight into his gear bag. He yelled for a week."

She stood up out of the ferns.

Peter hissed her name. She ignored him, hands raised, walking into the open with her heart going like a dive computer alarm, and watched Rafael Okafor's face change across the distance — watch the guardedness crack and something older and rawer come up through it, grief and relief tangled together like line in current.

"Amara," he said. Just her name. As if the river had been holding it for him all this way.

"You'd better explain the soldier," she said, when she was close enough, "and you'd better start with why the navy that's trying to erase my team paid for your boat."

His jaw tightened. Behind him, the soldier — Voss, he said the name was, Commander Voss, and the word came out of his mouth like something he'd stepped in — watched them both with patient, ledger-keeping eyes.

"Because they think I'm recovering equipment," Rafael said quietly. "And because I think I'm here for you. And because—" he glanced at Voss, then back, and lowered his voice to almost nothing, a diver's whisper, meant for her alone, "—because whatever's down in that hole, they're willing to kill to keep it there. And I've seen what that kind of secret does to the people who carry it. I need to know what you found."

Amara looked at him — at the compass hanging on a cord at his neck, scarred and familiar, Ekon's compass, of course it was — and felt the resolve settle over her horror like armor plate.

What she found, down in the green dark, was a war crime sleeping in a steel coffin, and the navy's fingerprints all over the lid.

"Come to the tree," she said. "And bring your friend only if he's ready to become a witness. Because I have documents, Rafael. I have photographs. And I am not giving them to anyone — not to you, not to Voss, not to God — until I understand exactly whose side this river thinks you're on."

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a headache, an engine began to turn.
