## 5. The Debt We Dive For

The night Ekon died, the sea off Bonny was the color of a bruise going green, and Rafael Okafor was twenty-six years old and absolutely certain of everything.

They had been working a pipeline survey for a company that paid in promises, two divers on a line in forty meters of silt-brown water, and the current had come up out of nowhere the way currents do when they've decided to take something. Ekon had been above him. Rafael remembered that with the terrible fidelity he remembered everything — the flicker of Ekon's fins against the failing light, the tug-tug-tug on the umbilical that meant *hold on*, and then the tug that didn't stop, that kept pulling, that pulled until the line went slack as a dead man's handshake.

He'd followed the slack all the way down into the dark and found nothing but a severed hose and a compass spinning loose in the water column, its needle drunk, its brass case already fogging.

Ekon Okonkwo's mother had not cried at the funeral. She had stood very straight in her good blue dress while the pastor talked about the sea receiving her son, and afterward she had taken Rafael's hands in hers — hands smaller than his, older than the river itself — and said, "You came back."

That was all. *You came back.* As though it were an accusation. As though it were a fact she intended him to live inside for the rest of his life.

And so he did. He sent money home every month, more than he could spare, because the debt wasn't written down anywhere and couldn't be settled by any amount, which is precisely what made it a debt instead of a payment plan. He carried Ekon's compass — salvaged from the wreck site by a fisherman who knew which diver hadn't come up, who'd known Rafael would want it, or need it, or be destroyed by it; with fishermen you never could tell which gift was which. The needle had been rebalanced twice. It still pulled a few degrees west of true, and Rafael had long ago decided that this was correct. A compass that pointed exactly north belonged to a man with nothing to answer for.

* * *

Presently, in the green hour before dawn at the edge of the sinkhole lake, Rafael sat with that same compass open in his palm and watched Amara Sesay sleep sitting up against a buttress root.

Anand snored with the dignity of a man whose snores had been peer-reviewed. Old Tamu was awake, of course. Tamu was always awake. He sat at the waterline sharpening a knife that was already sharp, which Rafael understood to be less maintenance than meditation.

"You keep looking at that thing," Tamu said, not looking up. "It doesn't get better if you look longer."

"It gets worse," Rafael agreed. "But honest worse."

The compass needle trembled, pulled its familiar few degrees west, and settled. West. Toward the delta. Toward the freighter he'd dived, the sealed crate he'd been paid not to open, the navy liaison with the voice like a filing cabinet closing. Toward the whole tidy architecture of silence he'd been building his life inside of, one paid-off installment at a time.

Because here was the thing he'd worked out sometime around the third watch of the night, staring at the black mirror of the lake: the crate on that freighter had been supplies. Chain, cable, demolition stores. He'd known the weight of it through his gloves before Voss ever told him what to do with it. And the expedition that had found whatever needed sealing — Amara's expedition — was now a garbled frequency, a missing persons file, an embarrassment to be managed.

And he had taken the job. He had taken the job the way he took every job, as a rung on the ladder out of debt, without once asking what the ladder was leaning against.

"Ekon would have hated me for this," he said aloud, surprising himself.

Tamu's knife paused. "The friend?"

"The friend." Rafael turned the compass over. On the back, scratched by some long-ago owner — Ekon had bought it secondhand off a dredge captain and been proud of the bargain — someone had cut a small cross, and under it initials that weren't Ekon's, and Ekon had always joked that the compass came pre-haunted, that it would steer you right but make you pay for the directions. "He hated liars. Professionally. Personally. He once made me turn the boat around because I told a client the visibility was four meters when it was three."

"A crime," Tamu said gravely.

"An atrocity. He reported me to myself. I was captain and crew both, so the tribunal took all night." Rafael found himself smiling, and the smile hurt, the way smiles do when they're carrying something heavier than they look. "I've spent eleven years paying his family because I thought that was loyalty. Money every month. Silence about how scared I was, how it should have been me up-current, how I've never once dreamed of anything but that slack line. I called it loyalty because loyalty sounds noble. But mostly it was just lying where nobody could see me doing it."

Tamu resumed sharpening. The stone whispered. "And now somebody wants you to lie where everybody can see."

"Somebody's paying well for it."

"Mm." Tamu tested the edge on his thumb, wincing theatrically at how sharp it wasn't, since it had been sharp for an hour. "In my village we say a debt to the dead is paid in truth. Money is only for the living."

"That's convenient, coming from a man I'm paying in cigarettes."

"I am extremely alive," Tamu agreed.

Behind them, in the hollow of roots where she'd finally gone down around midnight, Amara stirred. She'd slept maybe three hours in two days, and even sleeping she looked like a woman composing a report — brow furrowed, lips moving faintly, cataloguing atrocities in her dreams so they'd survive the waking. When Rafael had shown her and Anand the manifest fragment he'd memorized off the crate — *demolition stores, qty 40, consignee: hydrographic survey* — Anand had gone quiet in the way of scientists doing arithmetic, and Amara had said, "Forty charges is enough to bring the galleries down and seal the sinkhole with everyone's evidence inside. Including ours, if we're in it."

She hadn't sounded frightened. She'd sounded like a woman taking minutes.

Rafael closed the compass. The needle swung, dragged west, held.

Loyalty to the dead, he thought, cannot mean lying for them forever. Ekon hadn't died so Rafael could spend a lifetime apologizing in monthly installments. He'd died because the sea was careless and the company was cheaper, and the only thing Rafael could actually give him now was the truth, spoken out loud, in daylight, to whoever would print it. Maybe the money would stop coming. Maybe Mrs. Okonkwo would finally have her accusation confirmed — *you came back* — and slam the door. But a door slammed on truth was at least a door, and he'd been living in fog.

* * *

It was Tamu who heard the engine, because Tamu heard everything, including things that hadn't happened yet.

"Boat," he said, without urgency, the way he might announce rain or a good price on taro. "Downstream. Big engine, being quiet about it."

They killed their small fire's remains and moved back from the waterline, and Rafael glassed the channel through Anand's battered binoculars while the light came up gray and apologetic. At first there was nothing but mist peeling off the water like gauze from a wound. Then the mist shifted, and there it was: a gray hull sliding upriver with its wake folded flat behind it, patrol-boat quiet, running dark shapes along its rail.

Voss.

Even at this distance Rafael felt the man's presence like a change in pressure — that filing-cabinet voice, that offer already composed, the arithmetic of how many men it took to manage one scientist, one geologist, one guide, and one diver who'd been hired specifically because he was reachable. Reachable. That word again. Everyone in Rafael's life lately wanted him reachable, which apparently meant findable, which apparently meant controllable.

"They're stopping," Anand breathed beside him. "At the first camp. They'll see our tracks."

"They'll see everything," Amara said softly. "We left in haste. Haste is legible." She crouched beside Rafael at the root line, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and river mud on her, and studied the patrol boat with the expression of a woman reading a document she intended to quote later. "How long before they work out we went deeper into the system rather than back to the river?"

"Tamu?" Rafael asked.

Tamu considered. "They are navy. Navy reads maps. The map says the caves go nowhere." He smiled, thin and dry as old bark. "The map has never asked me."

"So we go where the map says nothing," Rafael said. "Through the flooded galleries, toward the outlet spring Tamu's grandfather drowned proving exists."

"My grandfather," Tamu said with dignity, "was a poor swimmer and an excellent direction-finder. There is a difference."

Amara laughed — a short, startled sound, the first laugh in days, and Rafael felt it land somewhere under his ribs and stay there, warm and inconvenient. He was beginning to understand that what he felt for this woman wasn't gratitude for a rescued survivor or duty to a missing expedition. It was the specific gravity of a person. She weighed something. She pulled at him, a few degrees off true, like a certain cracked compass he could name.

Below them, the patrol boat's launch put ashore at the ruined first camp. Figures spread out, methodical, patient. Somewhere a radio crackled a call sign Rafael recognized from a garbled recording he'd once been played in an office that smelled of toner.

"We can't outrun them on the water," Anand said. "And we can't fight them."

"No," Rafael agreed. He opened the compass one last time, watched the needle drag faithfully west toward everything he owed and everything he feared, and then snapped the lid shut on it. "So we go down. All the way down, and out the other side of the world if we have to. Tamu — how far to the gallery mouth?"

Tamu stood, slung his pack, and looked at the sinkhole lake as a man looks at an old enemy he has finally decided to introduce to his friends.

"Close enough," he said. "Bring your courage and your spare regulators. And leave the fire pit looking natural — navy boys hate finding evidence anyone was happy."

They broke camp in ninety seconds flat, five people's worth of fear moving with the strange calm of people who have decided. Behind them, downstream, the navy's voices carried thin across the water, calling a name into the mist.

It was Rafael's.
