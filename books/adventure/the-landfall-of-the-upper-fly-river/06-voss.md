## 6. Voss

The patrol boat came at dusk, the way bad news always does — quietly at first, then all at once.

Rafael heard it before he saw it: the low diesel mutter threading up the river through the evening birdsong. He was on the sinkhole's rim, checking the tie-off points for tomorrow's dive plan, and he went still in the way he'd learned twenty years ago on wrecks — stillness as a skill, not a virtue. Old Tamu materialized beside him without seeming to have walked there, which was Tamu's principal talent.

"Boat," Rafael said.

"I know." Tamu spat thoughtfully over the edge. "Navy one. Not the good navy. The embarrassed navy."

Rafael looked down to the second camp, where Amara sat hunched over a camp table by lamplight, copying figures from a water-stained logbook while Anand slept off his fever. The lamp made a small gold island of her. He felt something turn over in his chest that had no business turning over in the middle of tactical thinking, and he noted it the way you note a leak in a regulator: acknowledge it now, deal with it later, hope later is far away.

"Tamu. Get her lamp out. Get them under the tarp."

"And you?"

Rafael watched the boat round the far bend, running dark except for one impertinent courtesy light. "I'm going to go have a conversation."

* * *

Commander Voss climbed the sinkhole rim alone, which surprised Rafael. He had expected marines, or at least an escort with more shoulders than sense. Instead it was just Voss — tall, gray at the temples, uniform pressed despite four days on a riverboat, carrying a small case like a man visiting a difficult relative.

"You're a hard man to find," Voss said.

"You're a government," Rafael said. "You found me anyway."

Voss smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "Okafor. Rafael Okafor. You did salvage work for us out of Daru two years ago. The barge recovery off Parama Island. Your paperwork was immaculate."

"My paperwork is the only part of me anyone's ever called immaculate."

"I remember thinking so." Voss set the case on a flat rock, opened it, and produced two enamel mugs and a thermos. Coffee. He poured like a man who had never once been refused. "Dr. Sesay is here, then."

"She is."

"And Anand?"

"Alive."

"Good. That's genuinely good. I'd hoped." For a moment — just a moment — something tired moved behind Voss's eyes, some sediment of a man who had once been younger and cleaner. Then it settled again. "May I speak with her?"

"No."

Voss nodded as if this were the answer he'd come for. "Then I'll speak with you. You're the reachable one, Okafor. You've always been the reachable one. That's why I hired you, if we're being honest about it. A man with debts is a man with handles."

Rafael took the coffee, because refusing would have been theater, and theater was a luxury. "How much do you know about what they found down there?"

"I know enough." Voss looked out over the black water of the sinkhole, where the last light lay like oil. "In 1944, a munitions barge went into this system during a storm. Prison labor aboard. Local men, conscripted. The barge sank in a flooded gallery. The prisoners were never recovered because recovering them would have meant explaining why they were chained below decks." He said it flatly, without decoration, and somehow that was worse than any speech. "There are graves, Okafor. There is documentation in the barge's strongroom that names officers, names orders, names a decision made in a comfortable room. And there are two scientists down there who have spent three weeks cataloguing it with loving precision."

"They're witnesses."

"They're archivists." Voss turned back to him. "And here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, and I'll make it honestly, because you strike me as a man who can smell a lie through neoprene. The inquiry that follows this will happen regardless. Papers will be sealed. What I need is time — months, perhaps years — and the absence of photographs. Dr. Sesay's evidence, handed to me intact. In exchange: passage south for all four of you tonight. Payment of whatever you owe the Okonkwo family, wired directly, with a receipt you can frame. Plus a sum I suspect will embarrass us both to say aloud."

The number, when he said it aloud, did exactly that.

Rafael looked at the coffee in his hands. Steam curled off it, delicate as handwriting.

"Ekon Okonkwo died because I checked his gear wrong," he said. Not because Voss needed to hear it. Because he needed to hear himself say it in front of a man selling absolution. "Eleven years ago. His line fouled on a wreck off Moresby and I'd signed off on that line that morning. His family has never asked me for a single kina. The debt is mine. I invented it. I carry it because putting it down would mean admitting I put him down, eleven fathoms, and swam up without him."

Voss studied him with genuine interest. "And if someone pays it for you?"

"Then I'd owe them instead. You don't erase a debt, Commander. You just change the name on the ledger." Rafael set the untouched coffee down. "You want me to sell you silence. But silence is how Ekon died. Nobody talked about the faulty lines. Everybody knew. Nobody talked. So the next diver rigged up with the same rotten practice, and the next, until it got around to him." He shook his head slowly. "I'm not buying another one."

* * *

From the wheelhouse of the patrol boat, Voss watched Okafor's silhouette descend the far side of the rim, and allowed himself ten seconds of professional disappointment.

He had liked Okafor. That was the trouble with hiring competent men; competence came bundled with character, and character was a liability in this business. Voss had made this offer six times in his career, in various rivers, on various continents, and five times it had worked. The sixth man had also refused, and Voss had attended his funeral, and sent flowers, and slept fine.

It wasn't cruelty. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, insofar as clarity was available to a man whose job description included drowning the truth. Sesay and Anand could live — he had meant that sincerely. He was not a murderer by preference; he was a tidier. The graves were forty years cold. The dead did not vote. What mattered was the living, the alliance, the quiet arithmetic of nations, and if a few chained prisoners from 1944 had to stay unfound so that nothing larger broke, then that was the price, and history would never even send an invoice.

But Okafor had looked at the money the way a man looks at a door he already knows opens onto nothing.

That was new data.

Voss picked up the radio and keyed the channel to his boarding team, still waiting at the landing with the launch. "Stand by," he said. "We'll do this the harder way."

He told himself the harder way could still be gentle. There were procedures. Detention pending investigation. Sealed transport. Sedation, if required, administered by medical personnel, documented properly. No one needed to drown. The river, however, had its own procedures, and Voss had served on this coast long enough to know that rivers kept better secrets than navies did.

He looked across the black disc of the sinkhole, where a lamp had gone out behind a tarpaulin, and felt the familiar cold weight settle into place — the weight of a man about to do the necessary thing and call it duty.

"Signal Kiwai base," he told his radioman. "Tell them the package is confirmed at the upper site. Request authorization for containment protocol."

The word *containment* sat in the air between them, both men pretending it meant something clean.

* * *

"He offered you the debt," Amara said, when Rafael got back down to the camp. Tamu had doused the lamp, but she had heard enough through the tarp — Voss's voice carried like a man accustomed to rooms listening to him.

"He offered me everything," Rafael said. "That's how I knew it was worthless. Everything usually means someone's holding nothing."

Amara laughed — a short, exhausted sound, half disbelief — and then looked at him across the dark with an expression he couldn't file anywhere. "You could have taken it. Nobody would have blamed you. Anand wouldn't have blamed you. *I* wouldn't—"

"Yes, you would," Rafael said. "So would I. That's the problem with knowing people. They get inside your standards."

She reached across the table in the dark and found his wrist — not a romantic gesture, she'd have insisted, just locating him, confirming coordinates. But her thumb rested against his pulse, and neither of them moved for a long moment, and Rafael understood with sudden, terrifying clarity that he was past the point where he could hand her over to anyone, even to save her. Some things you couldn't raise and salvage. Some things you simply dove on and stayed.

"We leave tonight," Tamu said from the darkness, having apparently been awake the entire time, because of course he had. "Upstream. Before their boat moves."

"There is no upstream," Anand murmured, half-feverish, from his bunk. "We've been over the maps. The galleries close."

"There's always upstream," Tamu said placidly. "The river remembers where it comes from. Question is whether you do."

Rafael felt for Ekon's compass in his jacket pocket — brass, cracked crystal, needle faithful as a dog — and it pointed north, straight toward the wall of the sinkhole, straight toward the flooded galleries that every map marked with a question mark.

Toward the only door Voss hadn't thought to lock, because it wasn't a door. It was a promise the water had made and never told anyone.

Rafael pulled the compass out and held it up to the last starlight. The needle trembled, then steadied.

"Pack light," he said. "We're going diving."
