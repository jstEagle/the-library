## 7. Upstream of Every Map

Amara had spent eleven years learning to read the earth, and the earth had never once lied to her. It lied by omission, certainly — it kept its secrets in the dark and let you dig for them at your own expense — but it did not deceive. Water did. Water told you it was shallow when it was deep, calm when it was pulling you under, still when beneath that stillness an entire cathedral of stone was dissolving into nothing.

The sinkhole lake proved all of this within the first hour.

They left the second camp before dawn, moving on Tamu's instruction, because Tamu had gone quiet in a way Amara had learned to trust more than speech. He carried a canvas pack with dried fish, a coil of fishing line, and a machete worn to a crescent, and he walked ahead of them along the rim of the sinkhole as though he were strolling to market, which was either courage or a very old man's indifference to death. Rafael carried the evidence: three waterproof drums holding Sesay's survey notes, Anand's core samples, and the photographs — the photographs above all, because paper could be argued with but those images could not. Anand limped between them, his ankle wrapped in a strip of sailcloth, insisting every twenty minutes that he was fine in a tone that convinced no one, least of all himself.

"You understand," Anand said, for perhaps the fourth time since they'd set out, "that we are walking *into* the thing everyone sensible walks out of."

"That's why they won't look for us here," Rafael said.

"That is also why we will die here."

"Peter." Amara touched his arm. "Breathe."

"I am breathing. I am a geologist. Breathing in enclosed spaces is my entire profession."

It should have been funny. Some part of her registered that it was, in the way that gallows humor registers from very far away, through glass. But her mind kept returning to the barge. To the rows of shapes she had photographed in the flooded hold, arranged too neatly to be cargo, and to the navy crate Rafael had described raising from the freighter in the delta — the same supply line, the same silence, sixty years wide. The navy hadn't lost the expedition. The navy had *filed* it.

Tamu stopped.

He didn't signal; he simply became a tree, one with the trees, and Amara nearly walked into him. Below, far down the southern rim, a patrol boat sat at anchor in the main channel, its running lights off, a searchlight sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs. Voss wasn't chasing them yet. Voss was waiting for them to come down for water, for food, for the simple animal need to keep existing.

"Outlet," Tamu said, and pointed north, upriver of everything, toward a wall of limestone so dense with vine and fig root it looked like the forest had grown a cliff to hide behind. "Old men say the river goes under there and comes out past the falls. Old men say many things." He shrugged. "This one, I believe."

"How do you know?" Rafael asked.

"Because my grandfather helped hide people in there during the war." Tamu looked at Rafael with an expression Amara couldn't parse — grief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Japanese, Australians, runaways. The cave keeps what goes in. Sometimes it gives back."

* * *

The entrance was a crack in the cliff face, half-drowned, exhaling cold air that smelled of wet stone and something older — mineral, metallic, like blood diluted ten thousand times. Amara stood at the lip while Rafael rigged their single reel of guideline to a fig root thick as her waist, and she made herself think like a scientist instead of a woman whose hands were shaking.

Limestone. Karst. The whole plateau was riddled the way a honeycomb is riddled, galleries dissolved along fracture lines over millions of years, and somewhere in that maze the river found its way through and out. If the outlet existed, the airflow would tell them. Moving air meant a second opening. Dead air meant a tomb with good ventilation.

"The air's moving," she said. "Feel it? Inward, steady. There's a draw. That means another mouth somewhere."

Rafael glanced at her, and something passed across his face that she'd begun to recognize — surprise, quickly converted into respect, quickly buried again under competence. "You've done cave diving."

"I've done *science* in caves. Someone else did the diving." She held up her hands. "I can run a line, I can read a gauge, I can keep calm. That's the inventory."

"It's a better inventory than most."

Anand laughed, high and thin. "The bar is on the riverbed, then."

They went in at first light, because Tamu said the patrol boat's night glasses would be blind against the cliff shadow, and because none of them wanted to enter the dark in actual darkness. The water closed over Amara's head at knee depth, then waist, then all at once, and the world became a green-black throat swallowing them single file: Rafael first with the reel, feeding line off at a pace calibrated to the slowest swimmer, then her, then Anand, then Tamu, who swam with the unhurried economy of a man who had been doing this since before masks were invented, holding his breath in long, outrageous intervals and surfacing in air pockets with commentary.

"There is a dry ledge," his voice echoed down through black water, impossibly, when they rose into the first gallery. "There are bats. They are rude."

The gallery opened like the ribcage of something enormous. Amara's headlamp swept flowstone curtains the color of amber and rust, columns fused floor-to-ceiling, and the ceiling arched away overhead into darkness her light couldn't reach. For one full minute she forgot to be afraid, which she suspected was the cavern's oldest trick.

Then the passage narrowed, dropped, and became water again, and stayed water.

* * *

By the fourth submerged traverse, Amara had learned the grammar of black water. You did not see in it; you *listened*. You listened to your own breathing, ragged then steadying. You listened to the guideline whispering through your gloved hand — the one true sentence in a room of lies. You listened for Rafael's fins ahead, a soft double pulse, metronomic, patient. He never rushed her. When she stalled at a constriction, heart slamming, the walls pressing in like cupped hands, he would back up without a word until his shoulder brushed hers, and stay there, a warm solid fact in the liquid dark, until her body remembered itself and moved.

She fell into a rhythm and the rhythm became a strange kind of peace. Air pockets came at intervals — chambers where they surfaced, coughed, drank from seeps, ate dried fish that tasted like salted rope. Anand mapped as they went, sketching by headlamp on waterproof film, calling out bearings in a voice growing hoarser with each chamber. Tamu navigated by some sense older than compasses, occasionally disagreeing with Anand's instruments and being right.

It was in the fifth gallery — a long, low flooded corridor they'd named, in Anand's fading humor, *the Hallway of Regret* — that the mountain ended the argument.

Amara felt it before she heard it: a pressure change, a shudder through the water like the whole aquifer clearing its throat. Then sound arrived — not loud so much as *total*, a rolling concussion that turned the water to a solid thing and shoved them sideways. Silt exploded from the ceiling in boiling clouds. Her lamp became useless, a lit coin in milk.

The guideline went slack.

No — worse. It went *taut*, jerking twice, and then the vibration of someone else's movement along it simply stopped.

"Tamu?" Rafael's voice, muffled, everywhere and nowhere. "Tam— Peter! Peter, give me two pulls!"

Silence. The silt hung around them like weather. Amara pulled herself hand over hand toward the last known position of the man behind her and found the line ending in a tangle of rockfall, the fresh rubble sharp-edged, still settling. Somewhere beyond it, on the other side of however many tons of newly collapsed ceiling, was Peter Anand, with the spare reels, half the air, and the only other person who knew how to read his own maps.

"Peter!" She screamed it into her regulator, which was idiotic, and then screamed it properly when they surfaced minutes later in a pocket chamber barely bigger than a kitchen, both of them crammed onto a sloping shelf of rock, breathing hard, lamps carving cones out of fog.

Nothing answered. Water dripped. Somewhere far off, stone ticked and settled, counting.

"He's alive," Rafael said.

"You don't know that."

"He's alive," Rafael said again, and the certainty in it was so absolute that she believed him, and hating herself for how easily she believed him, believed him anyway. "The collapse cut the line, not the passage. If the gallery sealed completely, the air pressure behind it would have pushed back through the water — we'd have felt the surge from this side. We felt a shove *outward*. That means his side vented. Means there's space over there. Space means air."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong, and we dig anyway." He was already checking her gauge, then his own, doing arithmetic she could see him doing — the cold arithmetic of remaining gas versus remaining mountain. "We go back through. The fall is fresh; fresh fall is loose; loose moves. I've dug through worse with worse equipment and less reason."

"What reason did you have?"

He paused. In the lamplight, silt-streaked, breathing hard, he looked younger and older at once. "A friend who taught me that you don't leave people in the dark," he said. "Even when leaving them would be easier. Even when they'd understand."

Amara thought of Ekon Okonkwo, whose name Rafael carried like a stone in his boot, and understood that whatever debt he thought he was paying, this moment was its interest coming due.

"Then we dig," she said.

* * *

It took four hours. It took four hours that felt like a geological epoch, prying rubble in zero visibility while the water level crept up the shelf beneath them — the collapse had dammed something upstream, and the river, patient as ever, was filling its new room. Rafael worked like a machine with a soul, reading the rubble by feel, choosing each stone, passing them back to her in a chain that became its own language: heavy, heavier, careful, careful, *careful*.

When the gap finally admitted a body, it admitted Tamu first — the old man squeezing through like water finding its level, blinking at their lamps with mild reproach, as though they were the ones who'd been late. Behind him, gray-faced, ankle screaming, clutching his waterproof maps to his chest like scripture, came Anand.

"We heard digging," Anand croaked. "For a while I thought it was the mountain digesting us. Then I thought, no — mountains don't swear in three languages."

"Two," Rafael said. "I'm learning yours."

But there was no time for the relief to become anything usable, because Tamu put his ear to the water and his face changed, and Amara had spent enough weeks in this delta to know what that change meant.

"Water's rising fast now," Tamu said. "Not rain-water. *Pushed*-water."

Rafael went still. "Voss."

"Pumps," Amara whispered, and the word tasted like the photographs, like the neat rows in the flooded hold, like sixty years of official silence. "He's not searching the galleries anymore. He's flooding them. Sealing it with us inside."

Rafael looked at the rising black water, then at the three of them, and Amara watched him arrive at the same map she was already drawing in her head — the only route left, the deep one, the sump Anand's sketches showed running north under the collapsed section, unexplored, unmapped, a single line and a prayer.

"Black water," she said. "All the way."

"All the way," Rafael agreed. He clipped the reel to his harness and held out his hand to her, palm open, steady as bedrock. "Stay on me. Stay on the line. I'll get you through."

And Amara Sesay, who had trusted instruments, data, and the slow honest patience of stone her entire life, took the hand of a salvage diver and stepped off the edge of every map she knew.
