## 8. Black Water

The sump was exactly as wide as a coffin and twice as long, and Rafael had been telling himself for ten minutes that this was fine.

"Define 'fine,'" Amara said. She could read his face even in the half-dark of the cavern mouth, where the water lay like poured pewter and the air tasted of stone and old rain. She'd been reading him since Kiwai Landing — the way he checked gear three times, the way he hummed when he lied to himself. He hadn't known he hummed until she told him. Now he couldn't stop noticing it, which was its own kind of torment.

"It's a hundred and forty meters of flooded tunnel," he said. "Single line, no visibility after the first bend, one exit at the far end or none at all."

"And?"

"And I've dived worse." He paused. "Once. It didn't go well."

She didn't laugh, but something in her shoulders loosened, which was the point. Fear was a load you could redistribute if you carried some of it yourself. He'd learned that from Ekon, who'd been the funniest man ever to drown.

He clipped the reel to the primary line — Tamu's line, laid the evening before by a seventy-year-old guide who swam these galleries like a fish that had grown opinions about tourists. The knot was textbook. Of course it was. Tamu did not do anything else.

"Rules," Rafael said. "You stay on my left hand. One squeeze means go. Two squeezes means stop. Three squeezes means turn around, no questions, no arguments. If your regulator fails, you take mine. We share on every second breath. You do not let go of me, and I do not let go of you, and if either of those things happens anyway—" He stopped. "They won't."

"You're very reassuring."

"I'm told it's my best quality."

"It is not."

"No," he agreed. "I know what my best quality is." He said it lightly, but they both heard what sat underneath it, and neither of them reached for it, because there would be time later or there would be no later at all, and either way the water didn't care.

Anand had gone through an hour ago, limping, furious about limping, with Tamu leading him by the wrist like a stubborn child. The collapse behind them had taken the easy route out and nearly taken Anand's leg with it; a slab of limestone had caught him across the shin, and now every step cost him a color he couldn't afford. The outlet gallery ahead was their only road, and its middle third was drowned.

Rafael spat into his mask, rinsed it, settled it against his face. Last check: gauges, knife, light, the compass lashed to his chest strap. Ekon's compass, brass-cased, needle drunk since the delta but still swinging, still trying. He touched it the way other men touched saints' medals.

"Amara."

She looked up. In the dive light her eyes were very dark and very steady, and he thought: *if we get through this, I'm going to tell her.* Not today's lie, not tomorrow's deferral. The actual thing.

"Squeeze twice if you're ready," he said.

She squeezed twice.

* * *

The first thirty meters were honest water — green-gold, lit, almost friendly. Then the tunnel bent and swallowed the daylight whole, and the world became the size of a flashlight beam.

Rafael had a diver's relationship with darkness. He respected it the way you respect a crocodile: never surprised, never casual, always aware it was older than you and better at this. He moved down the line with long, economical kicks, feeling Amara's grip on his harness strap, her regulator's exhaust bubbles ticking past his ear like a slow clock. Squeeze. Go. Squeeze. Go.

At sixty meters the line went slack.

Not loose — *slack*, hanging, and his stomach dropped before his brain caught up. A slack guideline meant one thing: the anchor had failed. Somewhere ahead, Tamu's tie-off had pulled free, and the line that was supposed to be the difference between life and death was now just string in the dark.

He stopped. Two squeezes back along his arm — her answer came immediately, two, steady, good. He swept the light. The tunnel floor here was smooth mud, featureless, and the line drifted off into blackness like a thought losing its way.

Options. Swim the line to its end and hope the far tie-off held. Or find the wall and follow it blind, burning air, in a tunnel whose dimensions he knew only from Tamu's gestures and Tamu's laconic *"you will know when you are through."*

He chose the line. A bad line was still a line; a guess was just a way of getting lost with confidence.

Twenty meters of drifting string. Thirty. His gauge read pressure he did not love. And then the line simply ended — cut, not frayed, sheared clean where the collapse above must have pinched it against rock — and the dark pressed in like a hand.

Rafael did the arithmetic fast. Turn back: not enough air to redo the distance plus the return. Go on: no line, no map, one rumor of an outlet. Behind him, Amara squeezed once — go — because she'd understood the situation faster than he'd finished fearing it, because she was the kind of scientist who treated catastrophe as data.

He found the wall with his fingertips. Limestone, slick, ribbed like the inside of a throat. Left hand on rock, right hand holding her, and he began to swim the tunnel by touch, counting kick cycles, building the passage in his head the way Ekon used to build songs out of nothing on night watches — verse, verse, chorus, keep going, the rhythm is the rope.

Forty meters by count. Fifty. The tunnel narrowed. Sixty. The ceiling came down to meet him and he flattened, dragging himself along, and felt Amara's body press close against his back — not clinging, *aligned*, matching his movement, reading him through contact the way he'd taught her in the shallows two days ago when they'd both pretended the lesson was casual.

Seventy meters. His light caught a pale shape ahead and his heart slammed before his eyes resolved it: a root. A living root, threaded through a crack, reaching down toward the water. Roots meant soil. Soil meant surface. Somewhere above that crack, the world was still happening.

He squeezed once and kept going, and the root became roots became a curtain of them, and then the tunnel floor rose to meet his fins and his head broke water so suddenly that the noise of his own breathing seemed obscene.

* * *

They surfaced into silence so complete it rang.

Rafael pulled his mask down and the air hit him — cold, mineral, enormous. He clicked the light around and the beam just... gave up. The beam went out and out and the walls it found were fifty meters away and rising, ribbed and fluted and hung with pale flowstone, and overhead the darkness continued past any height his light cared to name.

"Cathedral," Amara whispered beside him, treading water. Her voice made the space bigger somehow. "Rafael. Look at it."

"I'm looking."

"No, you're checking your gauges. Look at *it*."

He laughed — actually laughed, the sound bouncing off stone that had never heard anything so human — and made himself lift his eyes. The cavern was vast and serene and utterly indifferent to them, and there was something in that indifference that felt less like menace than mercy. A place that had been perfect for a million years without anyone's permission.

"Anand!" he called. The echo took his voice, turned it over, handed it back.

Nothing.

Then, faintly, from somewhere along the left shore: a groan, and then Tamu's voice, dry as driftwood: "You are loud. He is bleeding. Come before he explains his injury again in detail."

They swam for the sound. Anand lay propped against a fallen stalactite the size of a church pillar, leg wrapped in a shirt that had once been white, face gray but composed in the particular way of a man who has decided pain is beneath his dignity. Tamu crouched beside him with a strip of dried fish and the expression of a man who has seen worse Tuesdays.

"The line failed," Rafael said.

"The line did not fail." Tamu pointed upstream with the fish. "The mountain moved. The line was honest. The mountain was not." He looked at Rafael's chest strap and frowned. "Your compass is broken."

It was. Somewhere in the squeeze under the ceiling, the brass case had cracked against rock, a jagged split across the crystal, and the needle inside swung loose and wild — except that as Rafael watched, it shivered, caught, and settled. North. Cracked housing, chipped card, and still pointing true, the stubborn old thing, like its owner's ghost refusing to be retired.

"Still works," Rafael said.

"Everything broken still works," Tamu said, "until it doesn't. Eat."

Rafael ate. Amara sat down against him without deciding to, her shoulder finding the hollow under his arm as though the geometry had been agreed upon years ago, and he felt the last of the black water drain out of his hands. Around them the cathedral held its breath. Somewhere below, the flooded galleries wound back toward the sinkhole and the navy and everything they were swimming away from; somewhere ahead, Tamu said, the cavern narrowed into a chimney of daylight, half a day's climb, and beyond that the outlet spring.

"We rest here," Tamu said. "Then we climb toward the sun, like reasonable people."

Anand raised one finger. "For the record, I want it noted that I suggested the flooded route was—"

"Noted," said everyone.

Amara's head tipped against Rafael's shoulder. Her breathing slowed toward sleep, that boneless trust of the genuinely exhausted, and he sat very still, guarding it, listening to the drip of the cathedral keeping time.

Then, from far below — down through the water, down through the stone, faint as a pulse — came a sound he knew from the delta, from wrecks, from the worst day of his life.

Engines. More than one. Coming up the river.

Tamu's eyes met his across the firelight. Neither of them said anything. They didn't have to.

The compass needle trembled on its cracked pivot, and held north.
