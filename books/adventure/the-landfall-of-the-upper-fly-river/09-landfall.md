## 9. Landfall

The water came for them at dawn.

Rafael heard it before he understood it — a low, patient roar somewhere below the gallery floor, like the river clearing its throat. He had spent enough years underwater to know that sound. It wasn't a collapse. It was plumbing. Somewhere down in the flooded lower galleries, Voss's men were opening the old sluice channels the wartime engineers had cut into the limestone, and the Fly River was being invited in.

"They're filling it," Anand said from where he sat propped against the wall, his bandaged leg stretched out on Tamu's folded tarp. "The whole system. They're drowning the evidence."

"Not the evidence," Rafael said. "Us."

Amara was already moving, headlamp sweeping the cathedral cavern they'd surfaced into twelve hours earlier — the one with the air bell and the slow breathing of the outlet spring somewhere above. She'd been mapping it in her notebook by lamplight all night, and now she tore out the page and held it up like a verdict.

"The spring," she said. "It has to go somewhere. Water under that much pressure doesn't just vanish — it comes up. There's an outlet. There's always an outlet."

"There is," Rafael said. "I've been listening to it since midnight."

They all looked at him.

"It breathes," he said. "Every few minutes the water level in the sump drops about a hand's width, then comes back. That's not current. That's a lung. Somewhere up there, this system exhales into open air. And when those sluices open all the way—" he nodded toward the distant roar, "—the pressure behind that breath is going to spike. The spring will blow."

Tamu spat thoughtfully into the dark. "Blow like kettle," he said. "Or blow like bomb."

"Both," Rafael admitted. "Probably both."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took twenty minutes to argue through and would take everything they had to execute.

The outlet shaft was a vertical fissure maybe forty meters up, choked with debris and old flood timber. The blowout, when it came, would drive the water column up that shaft like a cork leaving a bottle — and anything loose in the water column with it. They couldn't ride the surge itself; it would pulp them against the rock. But if they entered the shaft low, ahead of the pressure front, and let it carry them up from behind—

"You want to surf an explosion," Amara said.

"I want to be in front of one," Rafael corrected. "There's a difference. Marginally."

"And if you're wrong?"

He looked at her — mud-streaked, exhausted, three days without real sleep, holding a waterproof case full of photographs of mass graves against her chest like something newborn — and felt the strange calm that had been settling over him since Voss's patrol boat first appeared downstream. For ten years he had carried Ekon's compass and the debt it stood for, diving other people's wrecks to pay for a death that wasn't even his fault, or wasn't only his fault, or was exactly as much his fault as being alive could be. He had thought loyalty meant keeping quiet. Keeping the ledger. Diving down and bringing things up and never once saying what they were.

Ekon would have laughed at him. Ekon, who talked to fish, who named every crocodile on the river, who died because he went back for Rafael's dropped dive light — Ekon would have said: *you great idiot, the debt was never the point. The point was what you do next.*

"If I'm wrong," Rafael said, "we won't know about it for long."

"Genuinely comforting," Anand muttered. "Truly. Put that on my headstone."

Tamu grinned, which from Tamu was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

* * *

They rigged for it the way Rafael had rigged a hundred salvage lifts, except this time the cargo was people. Rope harnesses, doubled. The evidence case lashed flat to Amara's back so it couldn't tear free. Anand strapped tight across Tamu's shoulders — the old man was sixty-something and built like river teak, and when Rafael hesitated, Tamu just said, "Boy dives. Old man carries. Is fair trade." Anand's broken dive light went into Rafael's chest pocket, because someone should bring it out even if no one needed it anymore.

At the lip of the flooded shaft, Rafael checked his gauges, then did the thing he'd been avoiding for two days: he pulled out Ekon's compass.

The crack from the cavern wall still ran across the face like a lightning scar, but the needle swung steady, found its bearing, held. North. It had kept working anyway, through everything, stubborn as memory. He'd meant to leave it here, at the bottom of the world, to let the caves have it along with everything else they were about to bury. But his hand wouldn't open.

"Keep it," Amara said quietly. She'd seen. She saw most things. "You can give it away when you're ready. Not when the river tells you to."

He put it back in his pocket, over his heart, and stepped into the black water.

* * *

The roar below them grew teeth.

Rafael counted the breaths of the shaft — thirty seconds of stillness, then the shudder, then the pull — and timed their entry to the exhale. Four bodies in the water, four lines clipped to the guide rope he'd fixed up the fissure, and then the pressure front hit them from behind like the palm of God.

It was not diving. It was being fired.

The world became noise and white water and the screaming of timber somewhere overhead. Rafael's shoulder struck rock and he wrapped the line and held, feeling Amara's grip on his harness strap, iron-tight, the way she'd held on in the sump when her regulator failed and they'd shared his air mouth to mouth in the dark — the closest thing to a vow either of them had spoken yet. Above him Tamu was a shadow with a man on his back, rising like something ancient and unsinkable.

Then daylight. Actual daylight, tearing down the shaft at them, and the water column beneath them convulsed as the sinkhole's throat finally cleared itself, and Rafael had one half-second to think *now* —

— and the spring blew.

They came out of the earth in a column of white water and shattered limestone, forty feet up into the morning sun, and landed — all four of them, more or less intact, Anand hollering a word that was probably not scientific — in the pool below the outlet spring, in a tangle of limbs and rope and one very indignant freshwater turtle.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Amara started laughing, wet and wild and disbelieving, and Tamu joined in with a sound like a landslide being pleased about something, and Rafael lay on his back in the shallows looking straight up at the sky and thinking: *so this is what the surface feels like when you're not hiding anything.*

Behind and below them, the ground spoke.

A deep, rolling groan ran through the karst, and then the sinkhole began to die. They felt it through the water more than heard it — galleries folding, the drowned barge and the graves and the navy's sluice works and every secret the upper Fly had kept since 1944 going down together into crushed stone and silence. Brown dust breathed out of the spring vent. The pool shivered, settled, and went still.

Anand, flat on his back, raised one finger at the sky. "Note," he said hoarsely, "for the paper. The site is now self-sealing. Nature, apparently, takes sides."

* * *

They came out of the gorge at mid-morning, four mud ghosts limping along the river path, and found the village already gathered on the bank — because of course they were. Tamu had sent word days ago, paddling downriver in the dark to the mission station, and word travels fast in a country where the river is the road. Two journalists from Port Moresby stood among the villagers with cameras hanging forgotten at their sides. A district officer in a sweat-darkened uniform looked like a man who had been told many things and believed none of them until today.

And downriver, unmistakable, a grey patrol boat sat at anchor, its crew watching from the rail, doing nothing — because there was nothing left to do. You cannot seal a cave that has already spoken.

Amara waded to the bank, unslung the evidence case, and set it down in front of the journalists with both hands, the way you set down something you've carried a very long way.

"Everything's in there," she said. Her voice cracked and she let it. "Names. Photographs. Survey data. The barge, the munitions manifest, the graves. Forty-one men. Somebody wrote their names on the crates, and somebody else decided that was a problem." She looked up, and Rafael watched her stand straighter instead of smaller, which was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen anyone do. "Ask me anything."

The questions came like rain after drought. And Rafael stood back in the shallows, dripping, and let her answer, and understood that this — standing aside, saying nothing, letting someone else's truth take the light — was the easiest sacrifice he had ever made.

Tamu appeared at his elbow and pressed a coconut into his hands, freshly opened, as if this were an ordinary Tuesday.

"You look," Tamu observed, "like man who lost something heavy."

Rafael reached into his pocket. The compass was there, cracked face fogged with river water, needle swinging lazily, pointing nowhere in particular and everywhere at once. He turned it over in his fingers. Ten years of north. Ten years of owing.

"Yeah," he said. "Maybe I'll figure out what."

Down the bank, Amara glanced back at him through the crowd — just a glance, quick and certain, the kind you give someone you've shared air with at the bottom of the world — and Rafael found, to his considerable surprise, that he was smiling.

The patrol boat's engine turned over. It was leaving. The river stayed.

And in his pocket, the cracked compass needle trembled, steadied, and pointed — for the first time in ten years — simply, quietly, home.
