## 10. The Compass Point North

The inquiry was held in Port Moresby, in a courtroom with ceiling fans that turned with the weary patience of men who had heard everything twice. Rafael sat in the back row for three days and said almost nothing. He didn't need to. The evidence did the talking — Amara's waterlogged notebooks, Anand's core samples sealed in wax, the photographs that had survived the blowout inside a waterproof case Amara had strapped to her chest like a child.

The dead were vindicated first. Six names read aloud into the record: Dr. Lena Marsh, Tomas Reyes, the twins Okello and Okello — nobody ever seemed to know which twin was which, even their own mother, Amara said gently — and two Kiwai boatmen whose families received, at last, an official account of where their sons had gone. The graves in the sinkhole were given numbers, then names. A naval chaplain who had never met any of them read a psalm over a video link, badly, and Rafael found he didn't mind. The river had already done its own liturgy, years of it, in the dark.

Commander Voss did not appear. He sent instead a letter of resignation so terse it might have been a surrender note, and a second envelope addressed to Rafael containing no letter at all — only a bank draft covering, to the dollar, the fee for the original salvage job. No note. No apology. Rafael held it for a long moment in the courthouse corridor, feeling the weight of what it wasn't, and then he folded it into his pocket and later used it to pay for Ekon's flight north. It seemed the only honest thing the money could do now.

* * *

The Okonkwo house stood on a hill above the delta town, painted the blue of shallow water, with a veranda that caught every breeze the river offered. Rafael had stood at its gate four years ago and been unable to climb the three steps to the door. He had sent money instead, wrapped in silence, and hated himself efficiently each month when the transfer cleared.

This time he climbed the steps.

Mrs. Okonkwo opened the door before he knocked. She was smaller than he remembered, or he had grown heavier with the years between, and her eyes went straight past his face to his hands, as if checking whether they still shook.

"You look," she said, "like a man who has been swimming."

"Something like that."

"Then you'll want tea. Sit down before you fall down." She turned and called into the house, "Papa! The boy from the river is here. The one who finally came."

Rafael's throat closed around whatever he'd prepared. He had rehearsed the whole speech on the flight — the dive, the entanglement, the eleven minutes he'd searched before his air ran low, the compass he'd pulled from Ekon's jacket because someone had to carry something out. He had rehearsed it until it sounded like a confession and not like a wound.

But Mrs. Okonkwo poured the tea, and set a plate of cassava cakes within his reach without being asked, and waited, and the waiting undid the rehearsal entirely.

"I've owed you the truth for four years," he managed.

"We have known the truth for four years," she said.

He stared at her.

"Ekon wrote us a letter the week before that dive." Her voice was steady, but her hands, wrapping and unwrapping the cloth around the teapot, told another story. "He said if anything happened, it would be his own doing — he'd taken a job too deep for the money, and he knew it, and he wanted us to know he chose it. He asked us not to let you carry it. He said —" she closed her eyes briefly, finding the words the way you find footing in black water — "he said, 'Rafa will make it a debt, because that's the only way he knows how to love people. Don't let him.'"

The fan turned. Somewhere below the hill, the river kept its long gray patience.

"The money transfers," Mr. Okonkwo said from his chair by the window, where he'd been sitting all along, half-hidden by a curtain of prayer beads and afternoon light. "We never touched them. They sit in an account under your name. We couldn't work out how to give them back without making you come and hear this, and we were afraid that if we made you come, you'd drown on the doorstep rather than knock."

Rafael laughed — a cracked, surprised sound — because it was exactly what he'd have done.

"So," Mrs. Okonkwo said, and pushed the plate toward him again, an offer and an absolution in the same gesture. "Eat. And then tell us about the woman on the radio. Papa has questions. He's been composing them since the inquiry started."

* * *

He stayed two days. On the second evening, Mr. Okonkwo walked him down to the water's edge, moving slowly, leaning on a stick carved with fish. The tide was going out and the mudflats shone like hammered tin.

"Do you know why we forgave you before you ever asked?" the old man said.

"Because Ekon asked you to."

"That's the reason we gave ourselves. But no." He tapped the stick against a mangrove root. "A debt is a rope. Some men use it to pull others out of the water. Most men use it to tie themselves to the bottom. Your friend saw which kind of man you'd become if we let you. We simply agreed with him." He looked sideways, eyes bright and sly. "Also, you are a terrible correspondent, and we hoped forgiveness might improve you."

"It has," Rafael said. "I'm told I'm now excellent at letters. I write one every week to a geologist who answers maybe half of them."

"A geologist." Mr. Okonkwo considered this with the gravity of a man evaluating a bridge design. "Steady profession. Studies what holds things up. Good choice, after all your years studying what pulls them down."

They stood together while the light went copper and then violet, and Rafael felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

* * *

Tamu was mending a net on the beach at Kiwai when Rafael found him, sitting cross-legged in the shade of his boat with the patience of driftwood. The old guide looked up, took in Rafael's clean shirt and rested face, and grunted the Tamu equivalent of a warm embrace.

"They gave you a medal," Tamu said. It wasn't a question; there'd been a photograph in the paper.

"They gave me a medal and a hearing and a form asking if I intended to write a book." Rafael sat down on the sand beside him. "I said no."

"Wise. Books sink."

"I brought you something."

He took out the compass. It had traveled in his pocket through the inquiry, through the flight, through the two days on the hill — the brass casing scarred where it had struck the rock in the cathedral cavern, a hairline crack running across the crystal like a frozen lightning bolt. But when he opened the lid, the needle swung, wobbled, and settled north as faithfully as it had the day Ekon's father had given it to him, and Ekon had given it to Rafael with instructions never to lose it, and Rafael had carried it through four years of water and guilt and one very bad week in a flooded cave system.

Tamu took it in both hands. He turned it over slowly, reading the crack the way other men read scripture.

"It broke," Rafael said. "Down there. Hit the wall when I was pulling Amara through the sump. I thought that was the end of it."

"And?"

"And it kept pointing north anyway." Rafael watched the old man's thumb trace the fracture. "That's the whole lesson, I think. Took me forty years and one near-drowning to learn it. Things crack. They keep working. That's how you know they're real."

Tamu closed the lid with a click that sounded like a door being shut on a room that no longer needed tending. "Your friend's compass."

"Our friend's compass. And he'd have liked you — you're both terrible conversationalists who always know where north is." Rafael nudged the old man's shoulder. "It should stay in these waters. With someone who reads them properly. Consider it back pay for the worst guiding contract in delta history."

Tamu's mouth did the thing that was, in Tamu currency, a grin. He tucked the compass into the cloth fold at his waist, where it disappeared as though it had always lived there.

"You will stay," Tamu said. Not a question either. Old Tamu had stopped asking questions somewhere around the third day of their upstream journey and had been issuing findings ever since.

"I'm staying."

"The woman also."

"The woman is staying. She's got a research permit and a grant and strong opinions about sediment layers in the lower delta, so I suspect I'm staying partly as ballast."

Tamu nodded gravely. "Ballast is important. Boats need it. So do men." He returned to his net, conversation concluded, and Rafael sat with him a while longer in the shade, watching the tide come back in over the flats, filling in everything the water had written and erased and written again.

* * *

Amara found him at dusk on the veranda of the stilt house they'd rented — cheap, crooked, facing west so the sunsets came right into the room whether they were wanted or not. She dropped onto the bench beside him, smelling of sunscreen and river and the particular chalky dust of core samples, and put her cold feet against his leg without apology, because four weeks of shared air in a black sump will teach you things about boundaries that twenty years of courtship cannot.

"Letter from the university," she said, waving an envelope. "They want me to lead the formal survey of the outlet system. Proper funding, proper team, six months in the field." She paused. "Upstream of every map, officially this time."

"Voss's people won't like it."

"Voss's people can stand on the bank and hold the coffee." She leaned her head on his shoulder. "There's a line item in the budget for a dive safety officer. Local hire preferred. Someone who knows the currents and doesn't panic and makes decent cassava cakes, allegedly, according to a source on a hill."

"I told them the recipe under duress."

"You told them the recipe voluntarily and you know it." She tilted her face up to look at him, and in the last of the light her eyes were the exact brown-green of the river in flood season, which Rafael suspected was not a coincidence and had decided not to examine too closely. "So? Dive safety officer Okafor. Six months of mapping holes in the world. Interested?"

Rafael looked out at the water. Somewhere downstream, the sinkhole was quiet now, sealed by its own collapse, holding its dead the way rivers hold everything — closely, secretly, and forever. Somewhere upriver, Tamu was navigating home by a cracked compass that pointed true. Somewhere behind him lay four years of a debt that had never existed outside his own chest, and ahead of him lay six months of work with a woman who had shared his last regulator breath and still laughed at his jokes, which was either love or Stockholm syndrome, and he'd decided it was love.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm interested."

She kissed him, salt and river and tea, and below the stilt house the tide turned, and the needle of every compass on the water swung gently, faithfully, north.
