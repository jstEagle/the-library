## 1. Miles, Not Years

Gideon Okonkwo measured her life in distances now. Not years — years were treacherous things that pooled and stagnated and reminded you of what you'd failed to carry — but kilometers. Kilometers were honest. A kilometer was a kilometer whether you walked it in triumph or in shame, and it never asked you what you'd been doing ten years ago.

She had walked eleven thousand of them since the mountain took her name.

This morning's ledger: fourteen point two, from the tea house at Chhomrong down through the bamboo forest to the suspension bridge over the Modi Khola, then up the stone stairway on the far side until the porters' village appeared out of the mist like something remembered rather than seen. Fourteen point two kilometers of the Annapurna Sanctuary approach, the gentlest route she guided now, so well worn that the stones had been polished by a million boots into something like a riverbed. Safe ground. The kind of ground where nothing could happen to anyone.

"Madam Gideon." Tenzin, her sirdar for six seasons, fell into step beside her, his breath easy even at this grade. "The American woman is asking again about the pass above base camp."

"The pass is closed."

"You told her."

"I told her twice." Gideon kept her eyes on the trail, on the wet leaves and the small snails making their patient way across them. "Tell her a third time. Tell her the weather window is narrow and my insurance doesn't cover curiosity."

Tenzin smiled his folded-paper smile and dropped back to deal with the client, and Gideon climbed alone in the gray-green light, listening to the forest drip.

Ten years. She did not count them, but they counted themselves anyway, in small betrayals of memory — the way certain rock formations looked like the serac field below the Karakoram pass, the way a whiteout in the Annapurna fog could put her hands shaking around a thermos lid for no reason she would admit to. Ten years since three men went into a storm on her word and did not come out. Ten years since the inquiry board read its findings in a hotel conference room in Islamabad while photographers crowded the door, and the word *blacklisted* settled over her career like snowfall over a buried tent — soft, total, suffocating.

She had stopped guiding big mountains after that. Stopped guiding anything with exposure, anything with consequence. Now she led retirees and honeymooners and corporate teams on trust-building retreats along trails where the greatest danger was a sprained ankle or an ill-advised photograph near a cliff edge. She was good at it. That was the bitter joke of it — she was very good at it, because she knew exactly how people died, and therefore exactly how to keep them from it. Fear made you meticulous. Shame made you meticulous. Together they made Gideon Okonkwo the safest guide in Nepal, and the emptiest.

The rain started an hour before camp, a fine monsoon drizzle that turned the stone steps to glass. She heard it coming through the canopy before she felt it, that particular hush the forest made when it braced itself, and she called the halt early, got the clients under the tarpaulin of a teahouse veranda while the sky emptied itself. The American woman — Diane, mid-fifties, recently divorced, hiking her grief into submission — watched the clouds boil over the ridge line with an expression Gideon recognized. Longing. The look of someone who wanted the mountain to be more than scenery.

"You used to climb the big ones," Diane said, when Gideon brought her ginger tea. It wasn't a question. The other clients had talked; they always talked. Someone had found the old articles, the ones from before, or the ones from after. There were always articles.

"I did."

"Why'd you stop?"

Gideon looked out at the rain. Somewhere above them, invisible behind the cloud deck, there were passes no permit would let her cross and faces she had no business looking at anymore. "I got tired," she said, which was true in the way most lies are true.

Diane nodded slowly, accepting it or pretending to, and drank her tea, and the rain came down, and Gideon counted the day closed at twenty-six point eight kilometers.

* * *

The letter arrived in Pokhara, eleven days later, and it arrived wrong in every way a letter can be wrong.

It was waiting at the trekking agency office when she came down off the trail, a thick cream envelope in a stack of bills and permit renewals, and the first wrong thing was the handwriting. She knew it instantly, the way you know a voice across a crowded room — the sharp forward slant, the letters stabbed rather than written. Mara Voss had not written to her in ten years. Mara Voss had not spoken to her except once, through lawyers, and that had gone the way everything between them went: badly.

The second wrong thing was the stamp. Swiss. Postmarked Geneva, forwarded through Kathmandu, re-forwarded to Pokhara — someone had spent real effort finding her address, and Gideon had worked hard to make her address unfindable. She rented through a cousin of Tenzin's wife. She paid cash. Her name appeared on no manifest larger than a tea house register.

The third wrong thing was the weight. She stood in the doorway of the office with the envelope in her hand and weighed it like a guide weighs a suspect snowpack, and it was too heavy for a letter. Too heavy for good news.

"Gideon-ji," said Prakash from behind the desk, watching her face. "Bad news?"

"I don't know yet."

She carried it to the lake. This was her ritual when the world required thinking: she walked the shore path below Sarangkot where the paddleboards drifted and the kites turned circles in the thermals, and she sat on the flat rock past the last tea stall, the one tourists didn't find, and she opened whatever needed opening.

The envelope held two things. The first was a check — an actual paper check, drawn on a Zurich bank, for a number with so many zeros it looked less like money than like a typographical error. The second was four pages in Mara's stabbed handwriting, and Gideon read them twice before the words stopped swimming.

*Okonkwo.*

No greeting. Of course not.

*I'll keep this short because I know you won't read it twice. I need a crew for a crossing of the Karakoram passes — the high route, the one we both swore off, the one your name is still nailed to. Restricted territory. No permits exist. I'm not going to pretend otherwise and you're not going to pretend to be shocked.*

*I need a body recovered. A climber who died on the pass. And I need his effects — specifically a compass that belonged to his father. The family has waited long enough and the official channels are a decade of nothing. I have the money to do it properly and quietly and I need a guide who knows that ground better than any living person, which means I need you, God help me, and you need the money, whatever you tell yourself about being content with tea houses and honeymooners.*

*Half up front. Half on delivery. Name your crew, name your terms, but don't take more than a month deciding, because the weather window closes with the monsoon and it will not reopen this year or possibly ever, given how the politics are drifting.*

*You know where I am. Or you will, if you're half the navigator you used to be.*

*M.V.*

There was no signature beyond the initials. There didn't need to be. Mara Voss signed things the way glaciers calved — once, massively, and without apology.

Gideon sat on the rock while the light went gold and then copper over the lake, and she did not think about the money. The money was noise. What she thought about was the shape of the request, the geography underneath it, because Mara had been careful — Mara was always careful — but she had given away more than she knew. A climber dead on the high pass. Effects unrecovered for years. A compass belonging to the man's father.

There were only so many bodies on that stretch of ice. Only so many expeditions in the years before the closure. Gideon had spent ten years refusing to memorize the casualty lists from that season, and so of course she knew them all by heart, the way you know the names of the dead you failed: they are the only names you cannot forget.

But none of them — none — she told herself firmly, folding the letter along its existing creases — none of them was the name her mind was circling toward like a bird over carrion. That name belonged to a different disaster, an older one, a private one, a disappearance with no body and no closure and only a sister's certainty, worn smooth by a decade, that somewhere under the moving ice of that same accursed range her brother Emeka was still waiting to be found.

The check sat on her knee. The wind off the lake moved it slightly, and she pinned it down with her thumb.

A fortune. Mara hadn't exaggerated. It was enough to buy out her contracts for five years. Enough to fund the search foundation she'd once imagined and never founded, the one that would sweep the glaciers systematically instead of leaving the missing to surface on their own schedule, which was to say mostly never. Enough to matter.

Enough to buy, in other words, exactly the thing she had spent ten years learning to live without: a reason to go back.

She should burn it. She knew that with the calm professional clarity she brought to hazard assessment. The route was restricted because the last time anyone had crossed it, three men died and two reputations did. The politics were worse now than then. The monsoon was arriving early — every farmer in the valley said so, and farmers knew weather the way bankers knew interest. And Mara Voss wanted something from her, which meant Mara Voss knew something, and Mara Voss did not spend fortunes on sentiment. Whatever was on that pass, whoever had died there, Mara's interest in it was not clean.

All of this was true, and Gideon assembled the facts neatly, as she always did, and reached the conclusion that any competent guide would reach, and then she sat on the rock until dark and did not move, because beneath the facts, beneath the assessment, something older and less reasonable had lifted its head and was looking toward the northwest, toward the wall of the world, and it was not asking her permission.

* * *

That night she dreamed of white.

Not the clean white of snowfields in photographs — the other white, the blind white, the inside-of-a-lightbulb white where up and down become rumors and the only honest instrument left is the rope between you and the next human being. In the dream she was roped to someone and calling their name and the someone answered in Emeka's voice, though Emeka had not been on that mountain, Emeka had been lost two years earlier on a different face in the same range, and the dream did not care about her corrections. Dreams never do. In the dream the rope ran tight and then slack, tight and then slack, and each time it went slack her heart went with it, and she woke at three in the morning in her rented room above the shuttered pharmacy with her fists full of blanket and the ceiling fan turning its slow useless circles.

She lay awake and listened to the pre-monsoon rain starting against the clay tiles, and she did the thing she did instead of praying, which was to recite distances. Pokhara to Kathmandu, two hundred kilometers by road. Kathmandu to the border, ninety-five. Across Tibet and down into Pakistan, if the passes allowed it, better than two thousand. And then north, up the Baltoro and the glacier highways, into the country where the map ran out of confident lines. She could walk the whole route in her head. She had walked it in her head a thousand times in ten years, the way a prisoner walks a corridor, and she hated herself for knowing it and could not stop knowing it.

The rain thickened. Down in the street a dog complained about it and gave up.

Here was the truth she had built her life around avoiding: she had never believed Emeka was dead. Everyone else had accepted it — her mother first, terribly, then the consulate, then the search teams that went out for nine days and came back with photographs of empty ice. But Gideon had stood at the base of that face afterward, in the one week before the authorities sealed the area, and she had looked up at the seracs hanging over the route like broken teeth, and she had known — with the ugly animal certainty that guides are cursed with, the knowledge that has no evidence and no argument and never once has been wrong — that the mountain had him. That he was in there. That someday the ice would give him back, the way it gave everyone back eventually, and that when it did, she wanted to be standing there. Not notified. Standing there.

Ten years she had arranged her life so that she would never have to test that certainty. Low trails. Safe clients. Kilometers instead of years. And now here was Mara Voss, whom she had not spoken to in a decade, writing in her knife-slash handwriting, offering a fortune to walk Gideon directly onto the ground where both their histories were buried — and claiming there was a body on the pass, and a compass, and a family that had waited long enough.

Restless, she got up and lit the lamp and took out the letter again, and read it a third time, hunting the subtext the way she'd hunt for a hidden crevasse bridge — reading the snow, reading the lie underneath. *A climber who died on the pass.* Which pass? Mara named no peak, no sector, no date. *His father's compass.* Whose father? The dead man's, obviously — but Mara wrote like a lawyer when she wanted to, and lawyers left doors open on purpose.

And then there was the sentence she kept returning to, the one that had slid past on the first two readings and snagged on the third:

*A compass that belonged to his father.*

Not *a compass.* *The* compass, in the definite article of someone who knew exactly which object she meant. And Mara Voss knew about compasses. Mara Voss had been there, ten years ago, on the expedition before the disaster, the season when Emeka had come home for three weeks between trips with a brass compass wrapped in a sock in his rucksack and shown it to Gideon on the roof of their mother's house in Lagos, turning it in the orange evening light. Their father's. Engraved on the back, in a script too fine to read without squinting, with the childhood nickname nobody else in the world was permitted to use.

Gideon set the letter down very carefully, as if it might go off.

The lamp hissed. The rain came harder, drumming the tiles, running in ropes off the eaves, and out over the lake the lightning began its silent work behind the clouds, illuminating nothing and everything.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to the letter, to the version of herself that was already packing. "Absolutely not."

The room did not answer. Rooms never do. But the restlessness stayed, pacing the floor of her chest on soft feet, and she recognized it for what it was, because she had felt it once before, the night before the inquiry board announced its findings, and the night before she signed the contract for the expedition that ruined her. It was the feeling of the past having found her address. Not approaching — it had done that already, quietly, while she slept. Arrived. Standing outside in the rain, patient as weather, waiting to see whether she would open the door.

She did not sleep again. At dawn she boiled water, made coffee strong enough to strip paint, and sat at the little table with the check and the letter squared in front of her like instruments awaiting a decision. Outside, the clouds had lifted just enough to show the spine of the Annapurnas, white and indifferent, forty kilometers away and another world entirely.

She took out a pen. She told herself she was only going to write the questions — *which pass, whose son, what compass, why now, why me* — a clarifying reply, nothing binding, professional due diligence, the kind of correspondence any guide would send.

But her hand, moving across the page in the gray early light, wrote neither questions nor refusal.

It wrote: *Kathmandu. One week. Come alone.*
