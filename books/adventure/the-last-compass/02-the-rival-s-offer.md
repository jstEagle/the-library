## 2. The Rival's Offer

The letter had said the Hotel Yak & Yeti, four o'clock, and Gideon almost didn't go.

She told herself she went because of the money. That was a clean, defensible reason, the kind you could hold up to the light and show people. Forty thousand dollars for one job was more than she made in two seasons of leading German retirees up to Everest Base Camp and back, listening to them complain about the dal bhat and photographing yaks. One job. In and out before the permits office woke up to what they'd done.

But standing in the marble lobby of the Yak & Yeti at ten minutes to four, watching a fountain throw water into the smoky gold light of late afternoon, Gideon knew the money was only the story she'd brought with her like a cover charge. The real reason had arrived in her mailbox three days ago in an envelope with no return address, and it had been sitting on her kitchen table ever since, radiating heat like a stove left on.

*The past has found your address,* she'd written in her journal that night, and then she'd laughed at herself for writing something so melodramatic, and then she hadn't laughed at all.

A porter in a red vest approached her. "Madam? You are expected. Please."

She followed him through corridors that smelled of gardenia and old carpet, past a courtyard where a wedding party was taking photographs against the bougainvillea, bridesmaids in crimson laughing behind their hands. Somewhere a sitar played from speakers hidden in the hedges. Everything about the place was designed to make you forget there was a mountain anywhere in the world, and Gideon found herself cataloguing exits out of habit — the service gate by the kitchens, the alley behind the pool, the way the compound walls would funnel anyone leaving toward the main road.

Ten years of living small taught you things you didn't want to know.

Mara Voss was waiting on a second-floor veranda overlooking the courtyard, alone at a table set for two, and the sight of her hit Gideon like altitude — that first thinning of the air where your body knows, before your mind consents to know it, that everything from here on costs more.

She hadn't changed as much as the years demanded. Still tall, still built like the alpinists of the old school, all tendon and economy. Her hair had gone from iron to white at the temples, cut short now, practical. She wore a linen jacket over a climbing club T-shirt, the kind of thing a woman wears when she has stopped dressing for anyone's opinion but her own. Her hands were wrapped around a glass of something amber, untouched.

"Gideon." Mara stood. For a moment neither woman moved, two predators across a kill too old to fight over anymore. Then Mara extended her hand, and Gideon took it, and Mara's grip was dry and strong and held on perhaps half a second longer than courtesy required.

"Mara."

"Sit. Please." Mara sat back down, and Gideon took the chair opposite, angling it so her back wasn't to the veranda rail. Old habits. Mara noticed — of course she noticed; she'd always been able to read Gideon like a route description — and something flickered across her face that might have been respect or might have been grief wearing respect's clothes.

"You look well," Mara said.

"I look older."

"We all do. It's the trade we're in." Mara lifted her glass, considered it, set it down without drinking. "I'd have bought better whiskey if I thought you'd drink it."

"I'm working tomorrow."

"At this hour? Leading tourists?"

"A acclimatization hike. Americans. They want sunrise photos from Kala Patthar." Gideon kept her voice flat. "You know how it is."

"I know exactly how it is." Something moved behind Mara's eyes then, quick and cold, and Gideon understood suddenly that Mara Voss had spent the last decade being polite about what she really thought of Gideon Okonkwo's exile, and that the politeness was a wall, and behind the wall was a room full of things neither of them had ever said aloud. "That's why I wrote to you instead of any of a dozen guides who'd take this job and ask fewer questions."

"Then ask me nothing and tell me everything."

Mara smiled without warmth. "Still all business."

"Still all business," Gideon agreed. "It's kept me alive."

Below them, the wedding party erupted into applause. The bride threw her bouquet; it fell short, into a hedge, and everyone laughed. Gideon watched the small bright scene the way you watch weather coming in from a distance — beautiful, irrelevant, and none of it yours.

Mara reached into the leather satchel beside her chair and drew out a folder, thick, bound with a rubber band gone brittle with age. She set it on the table between them but did not push it across.

"The pass is called Shipton La. You'll remember it."

Gideon's spine went cold. Not Shipton La. Anywhere but—

"I remember it," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her something.

"It's been closed since '09. No commercial expeditions. No recovery missions. The government sealed it after—" Mara stopped herself, recalibrated. "After the season that ended both our careers. There are bodies up there, Gideon. Eleven confirmed. Probably more under the ice now. Families have petitioned for years. The ministry says the seracs are unstable, the approach too dangerous, the liability too great. And they're right. It *is* too dangerous. I've read every report. I've hired consultants. Two of them refused before they heard the fee."

"And the ones who didn't refuse?"

"They weren't good enough." Mara's gaze was steady. "I need someone who knows that mountain the way you know your own hands. Someone who can get a team in and out through terrain that has killed better-equipped parties than ours will be. And someone who understands why discretion isn't optional."

"Smuggling routes," Gideon said. It wasn't a question. "You want me to run a body recovery through territory the smugglers use, during a window when they'll be moving product, and you want it quiet enough that nobody official ever learns we were there."

"I want my brother back."

The words landed in the silence between them like a stone dropped down a crevasse — falling, falling, no bottom to the sound.

Gideon stared at her. "Your brother."

"Dominic." Mara's composure held, but her hands, wrapped around that untouched glass, had gone white at the knuckles. "Dominic Voss. He was on Shipton La in '09. His rope team was swept in the same storm that—" She stopped again. Drew breath. Started over, quieter. "His body was never recovered. Last year a Japanese survey team flying the pass photographed something in the ice near the old high camp. A figure. Preserved. Wearing gear from that season. The coordinates put him within two hundred meters of where Dominic was last seen."

The fountain below threw its water. The sitar played. Somewhere in the courtyard a child shrieked with laughter, bright as a struck bell.

"Why now?" Gideon asked. "Why after all these years?"

"Because my mother died in March." Mara's voice did not break, but it bent, the way ice bends before it fails. "Her last coherent request was that we bring Dominic home. She waited thirty years to ask. I intend to honor it while I still have the strength to lead the expedition myself, which, despite what the mirror tells me, is not forever." She finally drank, a long swallow, and set the glass down with a click. "And because the survey photographs won't keep. The glacier moves. Next season he could be under fifty meters of ice, or spat out onto the moraine where the scavengers will find him. There is a window, Gideon. Windows close."

Gideon looked at the folder. She could see the corner of an aerial photograph inside, gray and white, a smear of shadow against blue ice. She did not reach for it.

"No," she said.

Mara closed her eyes briefly, as if she'd expected exactly this and had budgeted for it. "Hear the terms first."

"There are no terms. The answer is no. That mountain took eleven people and my career and—" Gideon stopped. Careful. Careful. "I don't go back to Shipton La. Not for money. Not for anything."

"Fifty thousand."

"No."

"Sixty. Plus a full pardon-for-hire arrangement with the liaison office — your crew walks away clean regardless of what the permits say."

"No."

"Gideon." Mara leaned forward, and for the first time the mask slipped entirely, and underneath it Gideon saw not the rival, not the client, but a woman of sixty-one years who had buried her mother in spring and carried her brother's ghost for three decades and was tired, so tired, down to the marrow. "I watched you work for six years before the disaster. I know what you are. You were the best guide on two continents, and everyone knew it, including you, and the fall destroyed you more thoroughly than it destroyed the others because you survived it. I am offering you the only cure for what you've got. You've been measuring your life in safe distances for ten years. This job is the first unsafe distance anyone has offered you since. You'll say yes eventually. The only question is whether you say it today, like a professional, or at two in the morning three weeks from now, staring at your ceiling, like a haunted woman."

Every word landed. That was the worst of it. Every word found its mark, because Mara Voss had always known exactly where Gideon was soft.

But Gideon had built walls around that softness with ten years of masonry, and she knew how to stand behind them. She pushed back her chair.

"I'm sorry about your mother," she said. "I'm sorry about Dominic. But I'm not the guide you remember. That woman died on that mountain with everyone else. What's left does base camp treks and goes home." She stood. "Find someone else. Find someone who doesn't remember the sound the wind made."

She turned toward the door.

"Gideon."

She stopped. She should have kept walking. Some part of her, some deep animal part that had kept her alive through storms and rockfall and the slow suffocation of her own guilt, screamed at her to keep walking, to get out of the room, to get off the mountain, to get off the continent if necessary.

She stopped.

"The manifest," Mara said quietly, behind her. "From the '09 expedition. The one the ministry sealed along with the pass. I pulled a copy through a lawyer in Delhi. Do you want to know whose name is on it?"

Gideon turned around slowly.

Mara was watching her with an expression Gideon had never seen on her face before — not triumph, not leverage, but something rawer and more frightened. Pity, maybe. Or its twin, recognition.

"Say it," Gideon said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, as if someone else were speaking through her.

Mara opened the folder. Turned pages with deliberate care, as though handling something explosive. Found the sheet she wanted. Held it out.

Gideon crossed the veranda and took it.

The paper was photocopied twice removed, grainy, the official stamp bleeding gray across the top. A list of names in a clerk's careful hand. Rope teams, assignments, high camp rotations. Her own name was there — *Okonkwo, G., Lead Guide* — and seeing it printed in bureaucratic type made her stomach drop the way it always did, the past refusing to stay filed.

Her eyes went down the list. Bisi Adeyemi. Tomas Reyes. Names she knew, names she'd led, names of the dead and the scattered.

And there, third from the bottom, in the same clerk's hand, under *Rope Team Four*:

*Okonkwo, E.*

Emeka.

Her brother. Who had been presumed lost a decade ago somewhere below the pass, his body never found, his name spoken in her family only in the past tense, at funerals, at anniversaries, in the careful euphemisms of the bereaved. *We lost him too.* As if the mountain had taken him the way it took the others, cleanly, completely, a closed account.

But the manifest said otherwise. The manifest said Emeka Okonkwo had been assigned to Rope Team Four. High camp rotation. The storm day. The day everything fell apart.

He'd been *there*. On Shipton La. In the disaster. In the white hell she had descended through alone, calling names into the wind, counting survivors, and his name had been on her lips with all the others and she had— she had—

The veranda tilted. Gideon gripped the back of a chair. The wedding party noise swelled and receded like surf. She became distantly aware that Mara had risen, that Mara was speaking, that the words were arriving one at a time like supplies winched down to a stranded climber.

"—the ministry listed him among the missing, but the family filed differently, I checked, I checked everything, Gideon, listen to me. Your parents reported him lost on a different mountain, years earlier. They never connected him to Shipton La. Nobody did. The records were sealed. He was twenty-six years old and he walked into that storm carrying your family's name and nobody has looked for him in ten years except me, for the last eight months, and the survey photograph—"

"Stop." Gideon's voice came out cracked down the middle. "Stop talking."

She looked down at the manifest. At the name. *Okonkwo, E.*

Emeka had followed her into the mountains. Of course he had. He'd been nineteen when she'd left Lagos with her first international contract, and he'd written to her every week, and then he'd shown up in Kathmandu at twenty-two with a duffel bag and a grin and their mother's fear folded into his luggage, and he'd begged her to train him, and she had, because she'd been proud, because she'd wanted him beside her, because she had never once imagined that the thing she loved most in the world could take the thing she loved most in the world.

And then she had led him onto Shipton La.

And she had come down without him. And she had let the world believe whatever it believed, because believing he was lost elsewhere, earlier, painlessly, was easier than knowing he had died in her storm, on her route, under her leadership, calling her name maybe, into the wind that ate everything.

"Oh God," she said. It was not a prayer. It was arithmetic. Ten years of arithmetic collapsing all at once, every safe distance she'd ever measured revealing itself to have been measured from the wrong zero point.

Mara's hand settled on her arm. Light. Almost weightless. From anyone else it would have meant nothing. From Mara Voss it was an armistice.

"The photograph," Mara said gently. "The figure in the ice. It's within two hundred meters of where Rope Team Four was last accounted for. Gideon — I think it's him. I think I've known since I saw it, and I couldn't tell you in a letter, and I'm sorry, I am so sorry, but I needed you here. I needed you to hear it standing up."

Gideon sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their contract. The chair caught her. The folder lay open on the table between them, and the aerial photograph stared up out of it — blue ice, gray shadow, a dark shape curled against the white like a comma pausing mid-sentence.

She looked at it for a long time.

When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet and very steady, the voice she used above the cloud layer, the voice that had once commanded rope teams through storms that killed other people's leaders.

"What's the window?"

Mara exhaled, long and slow, ten years of exhaling compressed into a single breath. "Pre-monsoon. Six weeks. Maybe five, if the jet stream shifts early."

"Who else knows about the photograph?"

"Two surveyors. My lawyer. Now you."

"Keep it that way." Gideon picked up the manifest, folded it once, and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it lay like a coal. "I'll need three days. I'll need my own crew — mine, chosen by me, no substitutions. And Mara?" She met the other woman's eyes, and what lived in her own eyes now was not grief, not yet, grief was still somewhere below the tree line working its way up. It was something colder and more durable. "If it's him — if that shape in the ice is my brother — then this stops being your expedition. You understand me? You can come. You can pay. But I lead. All the way up and all the way down. Nobody touches that body before I do."

Mara studied her for a moment, and then she nodded, slowly, and extended her hand across the table, and this time the grip lasted a full second longer than courtesy required, and neither woman pretended it was anything but what it was: a treaty signed between two countries that had been at war for a decade.

"Three days," Mara said. "I'll wire the advance tonight."

Gideon walked out through the lobby, past the fountain, past the wedding party now dancing under strings of lights, and out into the street where Kathmandu roared and honked and smelled of dust and marigolds and diesel, and none of it touched her. She walked the twelve minutes back to her guesthouse without remembering any of them, and when she got to her room she sat on the edge of her narrow bed and unfolded the manifest and read her brother's name until the light failed and the call of the muezzin rose over the rooftops and the name was still there, patient as stone:

*Okonkwo, E.*

Ten years she had measured her life in miles. Safe distances. Controlled ascents. The long flat road of survival.

Tomorrow she would start assembling a crew. Tomorrow she would begin the impossible arithmetic of going back.

Tonight she sat in the dark with her brother's name against her heart, and understood, finally, that the past had never needed to find her address at all.

It had been living with her the whole time.
