## 3. The Name on the Manifest

The mountain did not take Emeka all at once. It took him in pieces, the way ice takes a rope — a strand at a time, patiently, until what held was no longer what had been tied.

Gideon would spend ten years learning that sentence by heart.

But that morning, ten years before Mara Voss's letter found its way to her door, there was only light. The kind of light the Karakoram gives away for free at dawn, when the peaks go from grey to gold in the space of a breath and the whole world seems briefly forgivable. Gideon stood at the mouth of her tent at Camp Two, breathing air so cold it tasted like metal, and watched her brother's silhouette against the serac field above, small and upright and laughing at something Bisi had said.

He was twenty-six years old and he moved like water finding its level — unhurried, certain, going exactly where gravity wanted him anyway. He'd been climbing four years then. She had taught him most of it herself: how to read a slope for the hum of hidden hollows, how to breathe at altitude like a man sipping rather than gulping, how to be afraid without letting fear make his decisions. The last lesson she'd taught him on this very mountain, two seasons earlier, tapping his chest with two gloved fingers: *The mountain doesn't care who you love, Eme. It only cares whether you're paying attention.*

*Then I'll pay attention,* he'd said, grinning. *You taught me that too.*

She should have turned them around that morning. She knew it now the way you know the shape of a scar — not because you can see it being made, but because your hand keeps returning to it in the dark.

* * *

The expedition had been hers from the start. Not Mara Voss's — hers. That was the part the inquiries never quite managed to hold onto, the part the newspapers got wrong in their hurry to print a story with two women's names in it and one of them already famous. *Voss-Okonkwo Expedition*, they called it, as if the hyphen divided responsibility evenly down the middle. It didn't. Mara had brought money and sponsors and a television deal; Gideon had brought the route, the permits, the roster, the weather judgment. Eleven climbers, nine support staff, a plan to put six people on the summit of an unclimbed face via a couloir she'd spent three years studying from every angle short of walking it.

Her name was on the manifest as expedition leader. Her brother's name was on it too, ninth from the top, listed as assistant guide. She had fought him about that for a month over email — *you're not ready for lead work on 7,000-meter faces* — until he wore her down the way he wore everything down, with patience and that smile and the argument that finally broke her: *If you don't let me climb beside you, I'll just keep climbing behind you, and I'd rather be where I can see you.*

So she wrote his name on the line. And when the sponsors asked her to vouch for him, she did. In ink.

That was the arithmetic she could never make come out clean afterward. Every death on that mountain traced back through decisions, and every decision traced back through her, and at the bottom of the column, under everything, was a single line item written in her own hand: *Okonkwo, Emeka — approved.*

* * *

The storm came up from the southwest on the fourth day of the summit push, which was early. Everyone agreed later that it was early. The forecasts — three separate services, paid for out of Mara's budget — had given them a five-day window. Gideon had built the entire push around that window, and the window had opened like a promise and held like one for three days while they climbed through the couloir in conditions so stable that Tomas, the team medic, joked about sunburn above eight thousand meters.

On the afternoon of the fourth day, Gideon stood at the high camp and looked west and saw the thing the forecasts had missed: a wall of cloud building over the far ridgeline, flat-topped and bruise-colored, boiling upward faster than any weather she had ever watched build. Anvil-shaped. The kind of cloud that means the atmosphere has decided something and is not interested in negotiation.

She made the call at 4:40 in the afternoon. Turnaround at dawn. Everyone down to Camp Three by midday, off the face entirely within thirty-six hours. She radioed it to all teams, she said it twice, she made each rope team leader repeat it back to her. Six voices came back across the static confirming. The sixth was Emeka's, and he signed off the way he always did, with their private shorthand instead of his name: *Copy, big sister. See you low.*

She fell asleep believing the mountain had accepted her terms.

* * *

It hadn't. At 2:15 in the morning the wind arrived like a train derailing.

There is no preparing for that sound if you haven't heard it — canvas screaming, poles flexing, the whole camp shuddering as though gripped in a fist. Gideon was out of her tent with her headlamp bouncing, shouting for everyone to stay clipped in, and the darkness answered her with snow moving horizontally, a white river running uphill past her face at a hundred kilometers an hour. Somewhere to her left a tent tore loose and went cartwheeling into the dark, and someone screamed a name after it, and the scream was swallowed whole.

By first light the storm had not broken but settled into a steady, murderous roar, and Gideon stood in the wreckage counting heads. Nine at the high camp. Three missing — swept from the tents during the worst hours, their anchors ripped from ice that had softened in the warm days before. One of the missing was found at noon, alive, wedged in a crevasse fifty meters below camp with a broken arm and frostbite creeping up his fingers. The other two were never found at all.

And the summit team — four climbers who had pushed for the top before the turnaround order reached them, or despite it; the inquiry would argue about which for months — was somewhere above, pinned near the shoulder, radio contact intermittent, food gone, oxygen nearly gone.

Mara wanted to wait. That much was true, whatever else was true about Mara Voss. She stood in the shredded remains of the communications tent with her face raw from wind and said, *We can't go up in this. We'll lose the rescue party too.* And she was right. Gideon knew she was right. Every manual ever written, every dead climber's postmortem, every hard-won law of the mountains said she was right.

But one of the four above was Emeka.

* * *

Here is what she told the inquiry, and it was true: she organized the relief ascent according to protocol, with fixed lines, with staggered teams, with turnback times written on her wrist in marker. Here is what she also told the inquiry, and it was also true: she led the first team herself, which violated her own protocol, because no rule she had ever written could survive the fact that her brother was dying on the shoulder and she was the best climber within a thousand vertical meters of him.

They left at eleven. The storm had thinned to spindrift and gusts, a liar's lull. They gained the shoulder in four hours, moving fast on the fixed ropes the summit team had placed, and found the first of them at the base of the final headwall — alive, delirious, frozen to the anchor he'd clipped himself to. Then the second, unconscious. They loaded both onto sleds improvised from pack liners and started down.

Emeka and the fourth climber were above, somewhere past the headwall, in the white.

The radio crackled once, twice. His voice came through shredded by static and wind, but it was calm, and that calm undid her more than panic would have. *We're traversing east,* he said. *Visibility's nothing. We're following the contour. Don't come up — avalanche terrain, the whole slope's loading. Stay with the wounded. We'll find the descent gully ourselves.*

*I'm coming up,* she said.

*No.* A pause full of wind. *Gideon. Listen to me. You taught me to read terrain. Let me use it. Get them down. That's the job. That's always been the job.*

She stood on the shoulder of a seven-thousand-meter face with two broken men on sleds behind her and her brother's voice in her ear, and she did the thing that leaders do, the thing she had done a hundred times, the thing that had made her famous: she chose the many over the few. She chose the lives she could hold over the life she couldn't reach.

*See you low,* she said.

*See you low,* he said. And the radio went to static, and the white took the rest.

* * *

They never found him. Not that season, not the search season after, not in any of the years of melt and refreeze that followed. The slope where he'd been traversing avalanched twice in the next seventy-two hours, burying the contour line under forty feet of consolidated snow. The fourth climber's body surfaced three summers later, carried downslope and mangled, spat out by the glacier at its own pace. Emeka stayed where the ice kept him.

The inquiry ran eleven weeks. Storm forecast error: shared. Decision to launch the summit push inside a marginal window: shared between leadership and sponsors. Failure of the summit team to observe turnaround: theirs alone. But the relief ascent — the unauthorized-in-spirit, leader-led dash up the mountain — that was hers, and the report said so in careful bureaucratic language that meant, in the language the industry actually spoke: *she let her heart drive the rope.*

Three dead. Two lost forever. The sponsors withdrew. The television deal collapsed into a lawsuit. Mara Voss gave testimony that was technically accurate and surgically incomplete, defending her own decisions by detailing Gideon's, and Gideon understood even then that she might have done the same in Mara's place, and hated her for it anyway, and hated herself more for understanding.

The blacklist wasn't formal. There was no document. It was quieter than that and total: the phone calls that stopped being returned, the permits that grew mysteriously difficult, the sponsors whose eyes slid away at dinners. Within a year the most sought-after guide on two continents couldn't get hired to walk tourists to Everest Base Camp. She went to Nepal, where the trails are long and nobody asks questions if your hands are steady, and she began measuring her life in distances covered. Kilometers were honest. Kilometers didn't have names attached.

* * *

Ten years later, in a tea house in Kathmandu, Mara Voss would say a name to her across a table, and the name would crack her open like a serac calving.

But that night, ten years ago, Gideon sat in the ruins of the high camp with the survivors huddled around a stove that ate their last fuel, and she did the arithmetic leaders do. Nine alive. Three dead. Two missing. And somewhere above her, in the loading dark, her brother moving along a contour line he could not see, trusting the terrain lessons she had given him, trusting her voice in his ear, trusting that if anything went wrong his big sister would come.

The storm returned at midnight with the full weight of the system behind it. It blew for six days. When it finally cleared, the face wore a new skin of snow fifty centimeters deep, and the slope where Emeka had walked was smooth and featureless and beautiful, the way graves are beautiful to no one.

Gideon stood looking at it for a long time. Bisi came and stood beside her and said nothing, because Bisi understood that some silences are load-bearing.

*We're going down,* Gideon said at last, and her voice sounded like someone else's, scraped hollow. *All of us. Together.*

She led them down. Of course she led them down. It was the only thing she knew how to be anymore — the one at the front, making the calls, carrying the count. She led them down through the couloir and off the face and across the glacier and into the green valleys where the air was thick enough to drown in, and every step of the way a single thought kept time with her boots like a metronome:

*He asked me to come up. No — he asked me not to. Which means the choice was mine. Which means the choice was mine.*

For ten years she would carry that knot, pulling it tighter every time she tried to untie it. Guilt does not sit on a person like a stone sits on a grave. It moves. It migrates with the pressure, the way a body moves under a glacier — surfacing here, sinking there, appearing decades later and miles downstream, worn smooth but unmistakable, still wearing the clothes you buried it in.

She thought she had left it on the mountain.

She was wrong. It had simply learned her new address.

* * *

In the morning, back in the present tense of her careful, measured, kilometer-counted life, Gideon Okonkwo would stand at her window in Kathmandu with Mara Voss's letter open in her hands, and she would begin — slowly, unwillingly, the way ice begins to move — to assemble a crew.
