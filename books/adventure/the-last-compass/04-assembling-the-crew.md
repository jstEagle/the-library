## 4. Assembling the Crew

Gideon spent two days in Kathmandu doing nothing, which was the hardest work she knew.

She sat on the roof of her guesthouse with a thermos of black tea going cold beside her and watched the city perform its morning: kites over Patan, a wedding party spilling gold into the street below, the mountains invisible behind their curtain of haze but present anyway, the way a scar is present under a sleeve. She had said yes to Mara Voss. She had not yet told herself why.

The name had done it. *Emeka Okonkwo.* Two words that reached across ten years and closed around her throat like a hand she almost welcomed.

She could not do this alone. That was the first truth, and it cost her something to admit it even privately. A crossing like this — restricted ground, monsoon season, a body to carry down — needed four at minimum. Ropes, medicine, river sense, and someone who could keep watch while the others slept. And it needed people who would not ask too many questions until they were too far in to turn back.

She made three calls from the rooftop, her phone cupped against the wind.

* * *

Bisi Adeyemi answered on the second ring, as she always did, as if she had been sitting by the phone for a decade waiting for exactly this call and wanted Gideon to believe otherwise.

"Ten years," Bisi said, without hello. "Ten years, and you call me at five in the morning."

"You're awake."

"I'm always awake. That's what happens when you stop climbing and start teaching children to tie bowlines in Pokhara. You dream about knots." A pause, and when Bisi spoke again the humor had drained out of her voice like water through scree. "It's real, then. What Mara's people have been whispering about."

Gideon looked out at the haze where the mountains should have been. "You've heard."

"Kathmandu is a village wearing a city's clothes, Gidi. Everyone hears everything eventually." Another pause, longer. "Tell me you're not doing it alone."

"Four of us. Maybe five."

"And the route?"

"The old one. Through the pass."

The silence on the line went on so long Gideon checked whether the call had dropped. When Bisi finally spoke, her voice was very quiet. "You swore an oath on that mountain. I watched you swear it. You said you'd never walk that ground again if you lived to be a hundred."

"I remember."

"Then either you've lost your mind, or someone has given you a reason worth breaking an oath for." Bisi exhaled, a sound like wind through a col. "Which is it?"

Gideon found she couldn't answer. The reason had a name and a face and a body lying somewhere under ice, and saying it aloud would make it real in a way she wasn't ready for — not yet, not before she had rope and steel and people she trusted between herself and the truth.

"Bisi," she said instead. "I need you on anchors. Nobody alive reads rock and ice the way you do. Nobody I'd trust with my life more, and I'm asking you to trust me with yours, which after everything is a monstrous thing to ask."

"It is," Bisi agreed. "It's an absolutely monstrous thing." A rustle; Gideon pictured her standing at the window of her little house above Phewa Lake, looking at the Annapurnas the way she always looked at them — like an argument she intended to win. "I'll come. But hear me, Gideon. I'm not coming back for your reputation or Mara Voss's money. I'm coming because ten years ago we left something up there that we shouldn't have left, and I have dreamed about it every winter since. If this goes wrong again—"

"It won't."

"You can't promise that. Nobody can promise that. That's the whole lesson of the mountains, and it's the one you never learned." But there was something in her voice now that hadn't been there at the start — something that might, in a better light, have been hope. "Thakurdwara in three days. I'll bring the rack. You bring the truth, whenever you're ready to carry it."

* * *

Tomas Reyes was harder.

She found him in a clinic off Chaksibari Marg, elbow-deep in the broken wrist of a trekker who had tried to descend Thorong La in sandals. He worked with the brisk economy of a man who had stopped being impressed by injuries sometime in the previous century, and he didn't look up when she came in. He simply said, "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying," and finished splinting the wrist, and sent the trekker out with instructions delivered in three languages, and only then turned to face her with his arms crossed and his gray-streaked beard set in its permanent expression of mild disappointment.

"Gideon Okonkwo walks into my clinic after six years," he said. "Either someone is bleeding out, or this is about the pass."

"Mara Voss hired me. High crossing, three weeks, remote terrain. I need a medic who can handle altitude, trauma, and cold."

"No."

"Tomas—"

"No." He said it without heat, which was worse. He began restocking the shelf behind him, ampoules clicking into rows like a man building a wall out of small glass bricks. "You know how many times I've done this dance? Chile, first. Then the Karakoram, with you, which ended with me holding a tourniquet on a man's leg while you made a decision I still think about at three in the morning. Then Nepal, smaller trips, safer trips, and I told myself: fine, this is what old guides do, they shrink their ambitions until the ambition fits inside a first-aid kit." He turned around. His eyes were tired and sharp at once. "And now you want to go back to the exact place where it all came apart, in monsoon season, on restricted ground, for a body. Do you know what we carry bodies down like that? In pieces. In bags. Do you understand what you're asking me to pack for?"

"Yes," Gideon said. "I do."

He stared at her. Outside, a scooter buzzed past, and somewhere a temple bell rang its flat bronze note, and Tomas Reyes stood in his clinic full of bandages and antiseptic and looked at her the way a man looks at a river he once nearly drowned in.

"Why?" he asked. "Not the money. Not Voss. Why you. Why now."

And because he had earned it — because he had held the tourniquet, because he had been there — Gideon gave him a piece of the truth. Not all of it. One piece, cut clean.

"The dead man is my brother."

Tomas went very still. The ampoules stopped clicking. For a long moment the only sound in the clinic was the ceiling fan turning its slow, indifferent circles.

"Emeka Okonkwo," he said at last, quietly. "Presumed lost, '09. They never found—" He stopped himself, and Gideon watched him arrive at the arithmetic, watch him understand that the body Mara Voss wanted recovered, the body waiting under the ice of a forbidden pass, belonged to the boy whose photograph Gideon had kept taped inside her expedition journal for years after everyone else had stopped mentioning him. "Christ," Tomas said softly. "Christ, Gideon."

"I need a medic who won't quit when it gets ugly. It will get ugly. I'm asking knowing what I'm asking."

Tomas rubbed his face with both hands, a gesture she remembered from a storm bivouac eleven years ago, from a tent full of wind-noise and fear. When he lowered his hands, something in his expression had shifted — not softness exactly, but a kind of grim surrender, the look of a man stepping back into cold water because someone else is already in it.

"Full kit," he said. "Blood products if I can get them. And I pick the pace on any descent with casualties. You lead the climb; I own the way down. Those are my terms."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's the minimum." He was already pulling a duffel from under the counter, already moving, because Tomas Reyes had never in his life been able to refuse a wound. "And Gideon — if you're keeping secrets from the rest of the crew the way you just kept half of one from me, tell them before the trailhead. Secrets are heavier than rope. I've carried both."

* * *

Dev she did not choose so much as notice.

She had posted word through the usual channels — discreetly, no names, no destination, only *experienced hands, hard route, good pay, silence required* — and the replies had come in the usual trickle of guides and porters and men who claimed experience the way tourists claimed altitude sickness. She interviewed four of them in the back room of a teahouse in Thamel, and three were competent and forgettable.

The fourth arrived late, wet from an afternoon shower, a lean man in his thirties with a canvas satchel and the particular stillness of someone accustomed to being overlooked. He introduced himself as Dev Bisht, from the Karnali side, and when Gideon asked about his experience he gave her a résumé written in rivers.

"Suli Gad. Mugu Karnali. The Humla gorge in high water, twice." He said it without boasting, listing rapids the way other men listed degrees. "I've run boats since I was fourteen. My father ran the ferry at the confluence before the road came. I know every eddy between Simikot and the border, and most of the ones nobody names because nobody sane runs them."

"We may need to run water that shouldn't be runnable. Early melt. Monsoon."

"Then you'll need someone who reads water the way your ropeman reads ice." He met her eyes steadily. There was a scar through one eyebrow, old and white. "I heard you're going west. High. I don't need to know where exactly. I need the work."

Everyone who came to these interviews needed the work. But there was something else underneath it with this one, some pressure behind the calm, and Gideon had spent enough years reading faces on ridgelines to know that pressure could be grief or greed or fear, and that from the outside they looked nearly identical.

"References?" she asked.

"I don't have references. I have a reputation, and it's quiet." He hesitated, and for the first time the steadiness flickered. "There was a boat. Two seasons ago. A client drowned — flash flood, middle gorge, no fault in the handling, but the family talked, and talk is enough in the valley towns. I've been taking whatever work comes since. This pays well. That's the honest answer."

Honesty was itself a reference. Gideon studied him a moment longer — the wet shoulders, the scarred brow, the hands resting flat on his knees like a man keeping himself from reaching for something — and felt the small, unreasoning click of trust that she had learned to distrust and had never once managed to ignore.

"Ropework?" she asked.

"Enough. I belay clean, I follow well, I don't lead on rock."

"Pay is half up front. You'll sign a confidentiality agreement with teeth. And if anyone asks you where we're going — anyone, at any teahouse, at any checkpoint — the answer is Upper Mustang pilgrimage trek. Say it until you believe it."

Something moved behind Dev's eyes then, quick as a fish under current, gone before she could name it. "Understood," he said. "Pilgrimage trek."

They shook hands. His grip was dry and strong and careful, the grip of a man used to holding on to things that were trying to leave.

* * *

They gathered three days later at a truck stop outside Dhading Besi, where the road gave out and the trails began: Bisi with her hair cropped short and her harness coils slung like bandoliers, Tomas with two medical duffels and a face like a forecast, Dev already checking the straps of the loads with a quiet competence that made the hired muleteer relax visibly. Rain had followed them up from the Terai, fat warm drops that fell without conviction, a monsoon clearing its throat.

Gideon stood at the tailgate of the truck and looked at the four of them — herself, Bisi, Tomas, Dev — and felt the old machinery of an expedition begin to turn inside her, gears she'd thought were rusted solid. Route cards. Load plans. Escape options. It all came back with a fluency that frightened her a little, the way a language returns to a tongue that swore it had forgotten.

She briefed them plainly. Restricted approach, night crossings, a recovery job for a private client, monsoon margins. She watched each face take it in: Bisi unsurprised, Tomas grimly cataloguing risks, Dev listening with his head tilted slightly, the way a man listens when he is memorizing rather than merely hearing.

She told them the dead man's name. She owed them that much, and Tomas's warning was still in her ears. She watched it land differently in each of them — grief in Bisi, professional dread in Tomas — and in Dev, something she couldn't read at all, a shutter drawn halfway down a window.

They walked out of Dhading Besi in the last light, single file on the stone stairway north, the terraces steaming around them, the great white walls of Ganesh Himal burning rose and copper above the haze. Gideon took the point out of habit and let the rhythm settle into her legs, mile after mile, the old measure, the only measure she had trusted for ten years.

It was Bisi, dropping back beside her at the first switchback, who said quietly, "We're being followed."

"I know."

"Since the truck stop. Half a kilometer back, maybe more. One man, good on his feet, keeps to the trees when we're in the open." Bisi glanced sideways. "Dev's been checking our backtrail every twenty minutes. He hasn't said anything either."

Gideon didn't break stride. Behind them the trail climbed into shadow, and somewhere in that shadow, patient as weather, something moved when they moved and stopped when they stopped.

"Watch him," she said. "Both of them."

"Which one?"

Gideon adjusted her pack straps and kept climbing into the dark. "That," she said, "is the question."
