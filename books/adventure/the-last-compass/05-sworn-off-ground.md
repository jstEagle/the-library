## 5. Sworn Off Ground

They crossed the boundary at 2:14 in the morning, and Gideon knew the time because she had been counting minutes since midnight, lying in her bag with her eyes open, listening to the wind comb through the dwarf juniper like fingers through old hair.

There was no fence. There was no wall. The restricted zone announced itself only by a weathered concrete pillar leaning at the angle of a drunk man against a doorway, its painted characters flaked to ghosts, and by the knowledge — carried in every one of them like a swallowed stone — that what lay beyond it was forbidden. Bisi stepped over first, planting her boot deliberately on the far side of the pillar's shadow, and looked back with an expression that dared anyone to make this solemn. Tomas crossed without ceremony, jaw tight. Dev last, pausing for half a breath with his hand resting on the cold concrete before he came over, as if paying respects.

Gideon went over last of all, after checking their backtrail three times, and felt nothing dramatic happen inside her chest. That was the strange part. Ten years she had imagined this line as something that would burn, or bless, or break her. Instead it was just ground, and her boots were just boots, and the mountain ahead was just the mountain — except that it wasn't, except that every step now fell on memory, and memory, unlike snow, did not hold a print.

They walked until the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, and then they walked through that too, up a valley that Gideon could have navigated blindfolded and had sworn, ten years ago, weeping into a satellite phone in a rescue base two thousand kilometers away, never to walk again.

*The oath didn't take,* she thought. *Oaths never do. They're just promises made to a version of yourself who doesn't know yet what you'll need.*

* * *

She led them by the old route because there was no other route, and because the new maps — the ones Mara had provided, printed fresh, expensive, wrong — showed a trail along the eastern bank that had been eaten by a rockslide sometime in the years since anyone official had bothered to look. So it was memory that chose their line: memory of a cairn shaped like a seated figure, which was still there, shouldered deeper into scree; memory of a stream that forked below a red-stained cliff, which still forked; memory of the place where the valley narrowed and the wind funneled through with a sound she had once described to Emeka as *the mountain clearing its throat*, and he had laughed and said, *More like it's about to spit.*

Emeka had been twenty-three on that expedition. She had been twenty-nine and already famous, already the guide whose name got whispered in Kathmandu tea houses like currency, and he had begged her for two years to let him come along, and she had finally said yes mostly to stop him asking, and she had spent the entire approach telling herself it would be fine, that the mountain didn't keep accounts, that being her brother meant nothing to the ice.

The ice had disagreed. The ice had taken him into white silence and given back nothing, not a glove, not a carabiner, not a sound.

Gideon pushed the thought down where it lived and kept walking, but the body keeps its own map, and hers knew exactly where they were. Every landmark was a headstone. Every headstone was familiar.

"You've gone quiet," Bisi said, falling into step beside her as the morning strengthened. "Quieter than usual, I mean. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one you get when you're arguing with a ghost." Bisi was sixty-one and built like the anchor chain of a trawler, gray at the temples, hands so scarred they looked braided. She had been on the original expedition too — roped two hundred meters behind when the whiteout took the mountain apart — and she had never once asked Gideon to talk about it, which was why Gideon could talk to her about anything. "How far to camp?"

"Six hours. Maybe seven if the moraine's moved."

"Has it?"

"It always moves." Gideon scanned the ridge above them, the long gray spine of it running north toward the pass, still days away, still hidden behind shoulders of cloud. "That's the whole argument between mountains and maps. Maps want things to stay put."

Bisi grunted. Then, lower: "You feel it too, then. Being back."

Gideon didn't answer right away. Because the honest answer was yes, and the honest answer was complicated, and Bisi deserved both halves. "It's like going back to a house you grew up in," she said finally. "You know where everything is. You know where everything *was*. And some of it you're glad to see again, and some of it you'd burn."

"And which is winning?"

"Ask me at the pass."

* * *

They made camp at midafternoon in a hollow below the terminal moraine, a place Gideon remembered as sheltered and found still sheltered, though the glacier that fed the moraine had retreated visibly, leaving a raw apron of gravel and meltwater pools the color of skimmed milk. Tomas set up the medical station with the methodical irritation that was simply how he breathed. Dev gathered dung and scrub wood for the stove, working quietly, competently, always slightly apart — and Gideon watched him the way she'd been watching him since Kathmandu, without accusation, without proof, just with the animal attention of someone who has survived being wrong about people before.

He was good on the trail. That was the thing. He read water better than anyone she'd trekked with in a decade, knew which stones held and which lied, moved through broken ground like a man greeting old neighbors. Whatever debt had brought him onto this expedition, it hadn't blunted his skill.

Skill wasn't trust, though. She'd learned that lesson too, and paid full price.

She was checking rope inventory when Bisi touched her elbow, once, hard.

"Gideon." Barely a breath. "Ridge. North. Don't turn your head."

Gideon kept her eyes on the coiled rope, counting coils she'd already counted. "What am I looking at?"

"Movement. Two figures maybe, maybe one moving twice. High on the spine, near that notch. They dropped out of sight when I looked straight at them."

"How long?"

"Saw them twice in the last hour. Thought it might be birds. It isn't birds."

Tomas glanced up from his kit, reading their faces rather than the ridge. Dev had gone very still by the stove, a kettle in his hands, steam rising past his chin.

"Could be locals," Tomas offered. "Herders cross high country this time of year."

"Not this country," Gideon said. "Not since the closure. Herders know where the lines are drawn better than the government does." She finished coiling the rope, unhurried, and only then let her gaze travel casually north, along the ridge, to the notch — a shallow saddle of gray stone against paler sky.

Empty. Of course empty. Whoever it was had watched Bisi watching them, which meant they were professionals, or desperate, and in Gideon's experience those two categories overlapped more than anyone liked to admit.

"Dev," she said. "When you scouted upstream this afternoon. Did you see anyone? Any tracks, any smoke, anything?"

A pause — half a second, no more, but she caught it, the way you catch a false note in a song you know by heart. "Nothing," Dev said. "Ground's clean on our side of the water."

*Our side.* As if the river divided the world into territories already claimed. Gideon filed the phrasing away with the pause, in the same drawer where she kept the fact that Dev photographed route notes, that he slept with his pack buckled, that he had known the name of a dead village on the approach that no living map still listed.

Maybe he was just careful. Careful men existed. She was one of them.

But she doubled the watch that night anyway, and told no one why, and lay awake listening to the wind clear its throat above the moraine, thinking: *Someone else knows this route. Someone else is walking my brother's valley.*

* * *

Dawn came up bruised and beautiful, the peaks catching fire while the valley floor still lay in blue shadow, and they broke camp fast and climbed. The trail left the river here and switchbacked up through a hanging valley of boulders the size of houses, ground so broken that progress became a conversation between four people and the terrain, hands and feet and rope, everyone breathing hard in the thinning air.

It was midmorning, crossing a slab of bare granite above the boulder field, that Gideon found the first sign.

Not a footprint. Not a wrapper. A cairn — small, knee-high, freshly stacked, three stones balanced with a precision that nature doesn't manage. It stood beside the route where a route-marker would stand, and it had not been there ten years ago, and it had not been built by wind.

She crouched beside it without touching it. The stones' undersides were dark with damp. Built within days. Maybe hours.

"Bisi."

Bisi came over, looked, and said nothing for a long moment. "Marking the way," she said at last. "For someone coming behind them. Or—" she met Gideon's eyes, "—for us. Showing us they know exactly where we're going."

Behind them, Dev had stopped on the slab, and Gideon heard his breathing change — a small hitch, quickly mastered. When she turned, his face wore the appropriate blankness of a man looking at rocks. But his eyes had flicked, just once, north along the ridge, before coming back.

*You know something,* she thought. *Or you are something. And either way, you're standing on my mountain, inside my grief, and I can't afford to be wrong about you yet.*

"Everyone drink," she said aloud, voice level, carrying. "We move in five minutes. From here on, nobody strays from the line. Nobody walks point alone. If you see anything — a cairn, a track, a flash of color on the ridges — you say it out loud, no matter how small. We're not alone up here, and I'd rather know it early than learn it late."

Tomas frowned. "Smugglers? This far in?"

"Someone's marking our route." She stood, shouldering her pack, and looked north along the valley toward the pass that waited beyond the clouds like a debt waiting beyond a horizon. "And whoever they are, they wanted us to find this."

"Why leave a marker at all?" Dev asked. Too smoothly. "If they're hostile, why announce themselves?"

"Because fear slows people down," Gideon said. "And slow people are easy people." She started walking. "Or because they're not hostile at all, and they're herding us somewhere they've chosen. Either way — we walk awake."

They climbed. The valley narrowed. Somewhere above, unseen, the wind changed direction, and Gideon felt it on the back of her neck like breath, and thought of Emeka laughing about the mountain spitting, and put one foot in front of the other on ground she had sworn off, following a trail of stones someone else had stacked, toward a cave she did not yet know existed, where something of her brother was waiting — patient as ice, patient as guilt — for her to arrive.
