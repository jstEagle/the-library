## 6. What Dev Carries

The photograph lived in the lining of Dev's jacket, sewn there by his mother's own hands with a curved needle and a thread she had waxed against the damp. He could feel its corners through the fabric when he pressed two fingers to his chest — a small rectangle of stiffness over his heart, like a second heartbeat made of paper.

In it, his brother Kiran stood on a bridge over the Trishuli, seventeen years old and laughing at something outside the frame. The river ran brown beneath him with the silt of a hundred glaciers he would never see. Three weeks after that photograph was taken, the mountain above Langtang took him — not in an avalanche, not in a storm, but in the ordinary way the mountains take porters: a slope that had held for ten thousand mornings chose the eleventh-thousandth to let go, and Kiran was under forty tons of rock before the sound reached the valley floor.

Dev had been fourteen. He had carried his brother's boots home in a rice sack, because the boots were all they let the family keep, and for years afterward he had slept with the sack beside his bed until the leather smell faded into nothing and even grief became a kind of forgetting.

He thought about this now, on the fourth night out from the border, while the others slept around the low fire and Gideon Okonkwo sat awake at the edge of the light with her back against a boulder, watching the dark the way other people watched television — steadily, without appetite, as if she expected it to show her something.

* * *

Dev had joined the crew eleven days ago, and already he understood that he had made a mistake of category, not degree. He had expected a job. What he had joined was a wound that walked.

He saw it in all of them. In Bisi Adeyemi, who checked her ropes three times where another guide would check them once, and who touched each carabiner gate with her thumb before trusting it, a small ritual of verification so constant it seemed less like caution than prayer. In Tomas Lindqvist, whose skepticism was not personality but scar tissue — you could hear it in the way he said *the last time*, whenever anyone proposed anything, as though the phrase itself were a splinter he'd never worked out. Even Gideon, most of all Gideon, who moved through the mountains like a woman revisiting the scene of a crime she couldn't remember committing.

They were all carrying someone. That was what this expedition actually was, Dev realized: five people hauling their dead up toward the ice, and one dead man's body waiting at the top of it all like a receipt.

And him? He was carrying two things. Only one of them was sewn into his jacket.

* * *

The debt was simple, which is how debts always described themselves afterward, once the telling had worn their edges smooth.

His family owed money. Not to a bank — banks had no interest in men like Dev Thapa, men whose collateral was a house of stone and tin in a valley that flooded — but to a man named Sher Bahadur Rai, who owned three jeeps, two teahouses, and, though no document said so, a long stretch of the smuggling routes between Tibet and the lowlands. Kiran had worked for Rai's cousin hauling loads across the passes. When Kiran died, the debt did not die with him; it simply changed hands, the way a load changes shoulders at a rest stop, and settled onto Dev like a pack he hadn't agreed to carry.

For nine years he had paid it down in increments — guiding treks, hauling freight, one season on a road crew in India that had nearly killed him more efficiently than any mountain ever had. And then, eight months ago, Rai had come to his mother's teahouse, drunk a cup of tea he complimented too warmly, and mentioned that a woman named Mara Voss was assembling an expedition. Illegal crossing. Restricted ground. High pay. And a need, Rai had heard, for someone who knew the river valleys — someone discreet.

*You know these gorges better than your own teeth,* Rai had said. *And discretion is a skill, Dev bahadur. Some men have it. Most don't.*

Dev had understood immediately, the way you understand the shape of a thing from its shadow. They didn't want a guide. They wanted a camera. Someone inside the crew, photographing route notes, marking camps and crossings, feeding the line of the expedition out to men who sold passage through closed country the way other men sold cigarettes — by the carton, by the carton's weight in anything worth moving.

He had said yes. He wanted to say that he had hesitated, that some better version of himself had stood up inside him and refused, but the truth was plainer and uglier: he had looked at his mother's hands, cracked from decades of washing other people's dishes in her own teahouse, and he had said yes before the sentence finished forming in Rai's mouth.

The satellite messenger had been delivered to him in Kathmandu, wrapped in cloth like a relic. *Photograph everything,* Rai's man had told him. *Route notes, maps, camp positions, the crossing points. Send at intervals. You'll know when.*

*What if I'm caught?*

The man had smiled with genuine kindness, which was somehow worse than menace. *You won't be caught. You're the kind of man nobody looks at twice. That's why we chose you.*

* * *

Now, four nights in, Dev lay on his sleeping mat and listened to the mountain breathe.

It breathed in wind — a long exhalation down the glacier's throat, steady, unhurried, the sound of air moving over ice the way blood moves over bone. Beneath it, farther off, the river muttered to itself in the gorge below, still low, still tame, though Dev had grown up on these waters and knew what the heat on the high snowfields meant. Every degree of warmth up there was a promise being written down here in water. By the time they reached the crossing, the river would have kept every promise it was making tonight.

He should sleep. Tomorrow they would move fast, Gideon had said, covering ground she clearly knew from memory so intimate it frightened her — he had watched her face that first evening when Bisi asked about the route, watched something close behind her eyes flinch away from whatever map she was reading in her head.

Instead of sleeping, Dev took out the messenger.

He did it the way he had done it the previous two nights: rolled onto his side, back to the fire's dying glow, screen brightness at its lowest, the device cupped against his stomach like something stolen from a temple. Which, in a sense, it was. It was stolen from every person sleeping within ten meters of him.

The route notes were in his breast pocket — Gideon's own hand, copied during the day's halt while she conferred with Bisi over the map. She wrote them each evening: bearings, altitudes, water sources, the character of the ground. *Scree field unstable above 4,600m — single file.* *Spring unreliable; carry extra from moraine lake.* Her handwriting was small and severe, letters leaning forward like climbers into wind.

Dev photographed the page. Four shots, overlapping, the way he'd been taught. His hands were steady. That was the part that shamed him most, later, lying awake — not the act itself but the steadiness of his hands while doing it. A man's body should object. His body had decided long ago, somewhere between the rice sack and the debt, that objection was a luxury.

He queued the images. He did not send them. Not yet.

That was the compromise he had constructed, the little architecture of self-forgiveness he built each night and watched collapse each morning: he would gather the intelligence, because refusing meant Rai's men found another way in and Dev's family paid the difference in ways he didn't want to imagine. But sending could wait. Sending was the sin; gathering was only preparation. As long as the photographs sat unsent in the messenger's memory, he told himself, no harm had actually crossed the air. The betrayal existed in potential only, like energy in a coiled spring.

He knew exactly what this reasoning was worth. He had watched his brother's boots rot in a rice sack and learned that a thing delayed is not a thing denied; it is a thing ripening.

* * *

Near dawn, he gave up on sleep and went to fetch water, and found Tomas already at the stream, crouched over it with his medic's fussiness, filtering through cloth though the water ran clear enough to drink raw.

*Trouble sleeping?* Tomas asked, without looking up.

*The altitude,* Dev said. It was the standard lie, universally accepted, and Tomas accepted it with a grunt that said he didn't believe it and didn't care enough to press.

But then, wringing the cloth, Tomas said: *You know why I signed on?*

Dev shook his head.

*Because I wasn't on the mountain last time.* He said it flatly, wringing the cloth again, harder than the cloth required. *Bisi was. Gideon was. Half the people who won't work with Gideon Okonkwo weren't there either, but they talk like they were. I've read the reports. All of them, including the ones that were buried. And I'll tell you what the reports say that the gossip doesn't.* He stood, slinging the filter bag over his shoulder. *They say she made the right call with the information she had, and the information was wrong, and three people died anyway, and she's spent ten years paying for arithmetic that wasn't hers to begin with.*

*You admire her,* Dev said.

*I trust her arithmetic,* Tomas corrected. *Admiration is for saints. We're not climbing with saints.* He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — the same flinch Dev had seen on Gideon. Everyone on this mountain was haunted by the same ghost through different windows. *I signed on because if I'm ever the one bleeding at altitude, I want the woman who checks everything twice making the call about whether we stop or go on. That's the whole of it. Don't tell her I said anything kind. It'll make her suspicious.*

He walked back toward camp. Dev stayed at the stream a moment longer, the messenger heavy in his pocket, and felt the full weight of what Tomas had just handed him without knowing it: a picture of the woman whose route notes he was photographing. *She made the right call with the information she had.* And here was Dev, deliberately corrupting her information, feeding her route to men who would use it for purposes she'd have died before permitting.

The spring water was very cold. He drank until his teeth ached.

* * *

It was Bisi who caught him — not at the messenger, not at the photographs, but at something smaller, and that was the terrifying part. On the fifth day, descending toward the river valley through forest loud with birdsong, Dev stopped at a bend in the trail to photograph what he genuinely wanted to photograph: a view of the gorge where the water ran green over white stone, the kind of view his brother would have loved, the kind of view Kiran had died one slope away from never seeing again.

Bisi appeared beside him. She moved quietly for a large woman; he was learning that about her.

*Pretty,* she said. Then, mildly, nodding at the phone in his hand: *You take many pictures.*

*My brother liked views,* Dev said. It came out before he could weigh it, and once it was out he discovered he didn't want it back.

Bisi turned to look at him fully. Up close her face was broad and calm, seamed at the eyes from squinting into thirty years of glare, and her calmness was worse than interrogation. *Your brother,* she said. *Climber?*

*Porter. Langtang. Nine years ago.* He heard his own voice go flat, recitation-flat, the tone of a man who has said these words so many times they've been machined smooth. *A slope failed. There was nothing wrong with the day.*

*There is never anything wrong with the day,* Bisi said. *That is the mountain's favorite trick.* She looked back at the gorge, at the green water and white stone. *Gideon's brother was a climber. Did you know?*

*I guessed. This trip—*

*This trip is a grave robbery,* Bisi said, without heat, as if stating the weather. *A loving one. But that is what it is. She thinks none of us knows. Tomas knows. I know. Maybe she knows we know.* She started walking again, then stopped, half-turned. *Listen to me, Dev. I am not a suspicious woman. Suspicion is heavy and I already carry enough. But I have guided twenty-two years, and in twenty-two years I have learned one thing about men who take pictures on my rope team.* Her eyes were very steady. *Some of them are keeping memories. And some of them are keeping records. And the difference matters more than anything else on this mountain.*

She walked on. Dev stood at the bend a moment longer, the phone warm in his hand, the unsent photographs sitting in the messenger like stones in a cairn — each one placed carefully, each one marking something.

That night, at the fire, Gideon announced the change of plan. The melt was running early; the mapped crossing might be gone. They would push hard tomorrow, reach the river by midday, assess with their own eyes rather than trust the notes.

*If the crossing's washed out,* Bisi said, *we lose two days going upstream to the gorge alternative.*

*Or we don't lose them,* Gideon said, and there was something in her voice Dev recognized from the reports Tomas had described — the voice of a woman who had once made a call with incomplete information and was now, forever, making every call as if the lives lost last time were standing behind her, watching her arithmetic.

Dev lay back on his mat. Below them, faint but unmistakable, the river sounded louder than it had the night before.

He took out the messenger one more time. Four new photographs waited in the queue, on top of the eleven already there. Fifteen frames of Gideon's severe handwriting, her route, her grave-robbery, her grief — all of it packed tight as powder in a charge, needing only the signal.

His thumb hovered over send. Below in the dark, the river rose and rose, keeping its promises.

He put the device away unsent, and told himself, one more night, one more night — and did not sleep at all.
