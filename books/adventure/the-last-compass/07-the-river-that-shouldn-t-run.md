## 7. The River That Shouldn't Run

The sound woke Gideon before dawn — a low, grinding roar that had no business being there.

She lay in the bivouac shelter a long moment, listening, letting the noise resolve itself into meaning the way she'd once been able to do in her sleep. Wind had a voice; rockfall had another. This was neither. This was water — deep, freighted, muscular water — and it was coming from the direction of the valley floor, where last night the river had been a silver thread you could cross on stepping stones.

She crawled out into the blue-black cold. Frost furred the guy-lines. Below, through the thinning dark, the Nubra tributary that their maps called seasonal and their route called passable was no longer either of those things. It had doubled its width overnight. Chunks of ice the size of tables rode its surface like slow white barges, and where the current hit the gorge wall it threw up spray that caught the first grey light and turned briefly, horribly beautiful.

"Glacial lake burst," Bisi said behind her. Gideon hadn't heard her come out. Ten years and the woman still moved like rope sliding through a belay device. "Somewhere upstream. You can hear it eating the bank."

Gideon nodded. She didn't trust her voice yet. The river was doing something to her chest that had nothing to do with cold — a tightening, an old alarm bell rung by a new hand. Water rising where it shouldn't. Plans dissolving overnight. She had stood at the edge of a river like this once before, ten years ago, and made a decision about crossing it, and three people had died downstream of that decision. The mountain did not repeat itself exactly. It rhymed.

Tomas emerged next, zipping his jacket to the chin, squinting at the water with clinical distaste. Dev came last, quiet as always, and stood a little apart, looking not at the river but upstream, toward the gorge, his face unreadable in the half-light.

"The crossing point's gone," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it too. "That shelf of gravel on the map — the one your contact swore was stable in June —"

"It's under four meters of meltwater." Gideon crouched, touched the ground, felt the tremor in it, faint and constant, the whole valley shivering with the river's pulse. "And it won't drop. Not for weeks. Maybe not until September."

They all did the arithmetic silently. Their window was measured in days, not weeks. The monsoon was early — everyone from Yusuf's valley to the tea stalls in Skardu had said so, and Gideon had gambled they were wrong, or early enough to be survivable. The gamble was now standing in front of her as a brown, roaring wall of snowmelt, and it wanted its payment.

"So we go around," Tomas said. "North rim, up over the shoulder, rejoin the route past the gorge."

"Four days," Bisi said flatly. "Five, with loads. And the north rim is scree the whole way. One slip with a full pack and you're in the river anyway, just slower."

"Then we wait it out here."

"On what?" Gideon asked. She kept her voice level, almost gentle, because she knew what was underneath her own words and didn't want him hearing it. "We have food for nine days if we're careful. After the river took the cache marker yesterday, we don't know the resupply point is even intact. Waiting isn't neutral, Tomas. Waiting is spending our margin at interest."

He looked at her then, and she saw it happen — the thing she'd been dreading since Kathmandu. His eyes went somewhere else, ten years back, to a different river under a different sky, and whatever he saw there hardened his jaw. He didn't say it. He didn't have to. *You've made this call before.* The accusation lived in the space between them like a fourth crew member, silent, patient, keeping pace.

Dev spoke for the first time. "There's a boat."

* * *

It was half a kilometer downstream, tucked in the lee of a collapsed moraine wall: a wooden river boat, local-built, the kind Yusuf's people used for ferrying goats and grain between banks when the water was kind. It had been abandoned — that much was clear from the weeds growing through the mooring rope and the silt line inside the hull, a pale ring showing where high water had once sat and then withdrawn. Someone had beached it in a flood year and never come back for it.

Bisi walked its length, pressing the planks with her thumbs like a doctor palpating for fractures. "Hull's split," she reported. "Port side, aft of the mid-thwart. Maybe forty centimeters. Old break, dried out, but it'll open right up under load." She straightened. "Also, it stinks of goat."

"We patch it," Gideon said.

"With what?"

She was already digging in the salvage they'd carried off the lost cache site — the tarp, the spare cord, the tin of pine pitch Tomas had insisted on bringing because *someone* on this crew should think about waterproofing, and wasn't it ironic now, Gideon thought, that his fussiness might save them. They worked through the morning while the sun climbed and the river kept up its argument below them. Bisi stitched the split closed with cord and pitch-soaked cloth, sewing the hull the way you'd sew flesh, tight and overlapping. Dev caulked the seams. Tomas watched the water level against a marked stick he'd driven into the bank, and every hour he called out the reading without being asked, and every hour the number was worse.

By noon the stick showed thirty centimeters of rise since dawn.

Gideon stood at the boat and made herself look at the thing honestly. It was nine feet of questionable carpentry held together by hope and pine pitch. The river was a mile wide in places, braided around gravel bars, then funneled into gorges where it ran green and smooth on top and murderous underneath. Running it would be the single most dangerous decision of the trip so far, and the trip had already cost them a cache and most of their luck.

Not running it meant five days on scree with failing food and a monsoon closing behind them like a door.

"You're going to say we run it," Tomas said. He'd come up beside her without her noticing, which bothered her more than she let show. "I can see it in how you're standing. You get very still when you've already decided."

"I haven't decided."

"That's not stillness, then? What is it?"

"Honesty." She turned to face him fully. Around them the wind worried the tarp, and the river filled the valley with its roar, and she understood suddenly that this conversation was going to happen in front of the others whether she staged it or not, so it might as well be staged here, in daylight, with facts. "Say what you're thinking, Tomas. All of it. I'd rather hear it ugly now than clean later."

His mouth twisted. He glanced at Bisi and Dev, who had gone carefully, conspicuously busy with the pitch tin. "Fine. Ten years ago you stood at a crossing like this one and you told everyone the window was closing and we had to move now. And maybe you were right about the weather. But you didn't leave room for anyone to tell you otherwise. You decided, and we obeyed, and Emeka—" His voice snagged on the name, and Gideon watched him wrestle it down. "Three people, Gideon. And you've spent ten years leading low-risk treks where the biggest decision is lunch. So forgive me if I want to know whether the woman making this call has actually changed, or whether she's just hungry to feel like her again."

The words landed the way well-aimed stones land — precisely, and where it hurts most because it's truest. Gideon let the silence sit. She had learned that much, at least, in ten years: that the pause was not weakness. The pause was the whole job.

"Here's what's changed," she said finally. "Ten years ago I'd have told you we run the river because I said so. Today I'm telling you the numbers, and you're going to vote, and if we run it, Bisi pilots, because she grew up on water and I didn't. If anyone says no, we take the north rim and eat less and arrive late and alive. That's the difference. Not that I'm braver or smarter. That I'm asking."

Tomas studied her for a long moment, searching for the flaw in it, the old arrogance wearing a new coat. She let him search. Behind him, Dev had stopped pretending to work and was watching her with an expression she couldn't read — something between surprise and grief, as if he'd expected a different woman and didn't know what to do with this one.

"I say run it," Bisi said, breaking the spell. "The scree kills you slow and certain. The river only kills you if you're stupid, and I'm not stupid, and I'm steering."

"I say run it," Dev said quietly. "I've seen this river in monsoon. It gets worse. Every day we wait, it gets worse."

They all looked at Tomas. He exhaled through his nose, a man conceding a battle he intended to keep fighting in some other form. "Run it," he said. "But I want it noted that the medical kit goes in a dry bag lashed to the thwart, and if we lose it, I'm done pretending this outfit knows what it's doing."

"Noted," Gideon said. "Everything important gets lashed to the thwarts. Nothing rides loose. Nobody stands up in the boat. We scout each braid from the bank before we commit, and if any of us says pull out, we pull out — no debate, no pride. Agreed?"

Three nods. It was the flimsiest constitution ever ratified, on the bank of a river that shouldn't have been running, in a valley that was filling with rain-smell from the south. But it was theirs, and Gideon felt something shift in her chest as she helped drag the boat down the shingle — not confidence, nothing so clean as that. More like weight, redistributed. A load shared is still heavy, but it moves.

* * *

The first two hours were almost kind.

The upper braids were wide and shallow, the boat hissing over gravel shoals with inches to spare, Bisi poling where the current slackened and paddling where it quickened, calling depths in a flat, unhurried voice that did more for morale than any speech could have. Gideon sat in the bow reading the water ahead — the V of a submerged rock, the glassy tongue that marked the safe channel, the boil lines where hidden currents fought each other. It was like reading a page she'd memorized as a child and hadn't opened in a decade. Her hands remembered. That frightened her a little, how easily they remembered.

They portaged twice around rapids that none of them liked, hauling the boat over shingle with the pitch-patch holding, the goat-stink baking out of the wood in the sun. Once, on a gravel bar, they flushed a flock of bar-headed geese that went up all at once like thrown confetti, and even Tomas laughed, a short surprised bark, and for twenty minutes the day was almost ordinary.

Then the valley narrowed.

The walls drew in on both sides, grey granite streaked with meltwater veins, and the river gathered itself between them and turned from brown to a deep, churning green. The roar came up off the rock and pressed against their chests. Gideon scrambled along the bank to scout and found what she'd been afraid of: a quarter mile of continuous whitewater, no eddies worth the name, no gravel bar to bail out onto. Run it all or portage around it all — and the portage route, she saw, climbing the talus with her eyes, was a knife-edge of broken stone above a sheer drop to the water. With their loads, someone would die on it. Maybe several someones.

She came back to the boat and told them the truth, all of it, because that was the deal now.

Bisi looked at the rapid, then at the sky, where the clouds had begun stacking themselves in the south like grey plates. "Rain up-valley means more water tonight," she said. "This is the lowest it will ever be." She rolled her shoulders. "Line runs left of center, then hard right past the big hole. There's a seam — see the smooth band? — we ride the seam."

"And if we miss the seam?" Tomas asked.

"Then we swim," Bisi said, "and pray the cold takes us before the rocks do."

Nobody laughed. Dev checked the lashings twice. Tomas sealed the medical kit into its dry bag and knotted it to the thwart with a fisherman's bend, testing it with a hard yank, and when he caught Gideon watching him he said, "If we lose this, we lose everything that matters," and she nodded, because he was right, and because she was learning to let people be right at her.

They pushed off into the green water.

* * *

The rapid took eleven minutes. Gideon would remember eleven minutes for the rest of her life.

It began well. The seam was there, a ribbon of smoother current threading the chaos, and Bisi found it and held it, her paddle strokes economical and absolute, the boat surging forward with a speed that made the spray fly warm against Gideon's face. For ninety seconds it was flight. Then the river bent left where the map said it bent right, a fresh collapse upstream having shoved the channel sideways, and the seam dissolved beneath them like a path in a dream.

"Rock!" Dev shouted, and there it was, a black fang dead ahead, and Bisi leaned the boat into a turn so hard the gunwale kissed the water, and they missed the fang by the width of a prayer and slammed instead into the standing wave behind it.

The wave took the bow and stood it up. Gideon felt the world tilt into vertical, felt Dev's shoulder crash into hers, heard Tomas swear with a fluency that would have been impressive in other circumstances. The boat stalled, teetered, and then the river simply picked it up and threw it, and they were surfing sideways down a staircase of water with the granite walls strobing past on both sides.

Something struck the hull — a rock, a log, the river's fist — and Gideon heard the sound she'd been listening for all day: a crack, deeper than wood, the sound of the patch giving up. Water came in over the port side in a cold, insistent sheet.

"Bail!" Bisi roared. "Bail or we sink!"

Dev bailed with the cooking pot. Tomas bailed with his cup. Gideon, wedged in the bow, bailed with her hands, scooping frantically while the rapid hammered them onward, wave after wave, each impact a question the hull answered a little less confidently than the last. The water in the bilge rose to her knees, then her calves, then found her ankles, and through all of it Bisi steered, jaw set, eyes reading the chaos ahead and choosing, choosing, choosing.

Then, as abruptly as it had begun, the valley opened. The rapid spat them out into a long, calm pool below, and the boat wallowed, sitting dangerously low, and Bisi drove it for the nearest gravel bar with the last of her strength.

They grounded with a grinding lurch. For a moment nobody moved. Then Tomas tipped over the side into ankle-deep water, hauled the medical dry bag up onto the shingle, and clutched it to his chest like a rescued child, and Bisi lay back across the thwarts and laughed — a wild, gasping, slightly unhinged laugh that Gideon joined before she could stop herself, the two of them laughing like survivors of a shipwreck, which was exactly what they were.

The hull had split again. Two new cracks, longer than the first, radiating from the old repair. The boat was finished; everyone could see it. Whatever it carried from here, it would carry leaking, and not far.

Gideon waded to shore and stood wringing out her sleeves, and that was when she counted heads and supplies and did the grim inventory. Dry bags soaked through — rice gone to porridge, flour gone to paste. Half the medical kit's outer stores ruined despite Tomas's vigilance: the tape dissolved, the blister dressings a sodden mass. The satellite messenger, thank God, had ridden in the innermost bag and still blinked its small green eye.

"We lost the food for two days," Bisi said, when the laughter had burned off. "Maybe three."

"And the boat's dying," Dev said. He was standing hip-deep beside the hull, his palm flat against the cracked planks, and his face had gone strange — not despairing, but focused, like a man remembering something. "But we don't need it to survive. We need it to float one more day. There's a village upriver — my brother used to trade there. They'll have timber, pitch, maybe a better hull entirely. Two days' push along the bank."

"You said you'd never been in this valley," Tomas said sharply.

"I said I knew the river valleys," Dev said, without turning around. "This is one of them."

Gideon filed that away — another fragment for the collection of things Dev knew too well and explained too little — and let it go, because the light was dying and the rain-smell in the south had thickened into something you could taste. She looked upriver, toward the gorge walls going purple, toward the pass that waited beyond them like a debt unpaid, and she made herself say the true thing aloud, because the crew deserved to hear their leader count costs honestly.

"Today bought us distance and cost us comfort," she said. "We're wet, we're short on food, our boat is broken, and the monsoon is coming faster than anyone promised. That's the price. It doesn't stop being paid just because we survived the afternoon." She met each of their eyes in turn. "Tomorrow we fix the hull and we keep moving, and we do it knowing the mountain is charging us by the day now."

Tomas said nothing, but he set the medical bag down gently on the driest stone he could find, and spread it open to air, and began sorting the ruined from the saved with the care of a man tending wounded. It was, Gideon understood, as close to an apology as the day would get, and she took it.

They camped above the waterline on a shelf of shingle, and the river talked all night in its new, swollen voice, and near midnight Gideon woke to a sound that froze her where she lay: a thin, high whistle from the ridge above the opposite bank — two notes, repeated, deliberate. Not wind. Not bird.

Across the black water, on the far ridge, a fire sparked briefly to life and was smothered just as fast.

Someone was marking their position.
