## 8. Leaking

The river told them before the boat did.

Gideon heard it first as a change in the water's voice — a lowening, a gathering of syllables into something older and less patient — and she knew, without seeing around the bend, that the easy water was over. The gorge ahead narrowed to a throat of black granite, and through it the glacial melt ran green-white and furious, braided with foam, carrying with it the smell of stone ground to flour.

"Everyone out," she called, and the boat scraped against a gravel bar as Bisi and Dev hauled it in.

They stood on the bank while she read the rapids, the way she had read them a hundred times on rivers in two hemispheres. The channel split around a midstream boulder the size of a bus. Left of it, the water folded into two standing waves and then a hole that swallowed its own spray; right of it, the current pressed hard against the cliff wall and shot through a gap barely wider than the boat itself. Neither line was good. Both were survivable — if the hull held.

She looked at the hull. It didn't hold her confidence. Yusuf's patched boat had carried them two days now, the resin seams sweating at every cold morning, the split plank near the bow bound with cord and hope. She put her palm flat against the wood and felt it flex under the pressure of the current tugging at the stern.

"It'll hold," Dev said beside her, too quickly.

"Everything holds," Gideon said, "until it doesn't."

Tomas stood apart, arms crossed, watching the water with the flat expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. He'd been quiet since the crossing point — since her call to run the river instead of losing three days climbing the ridge and dropping back down. Quiet in the way a glacier is quiet: moving all the time, just slowly enough that you forget it can calve.

"We lighten the load," she said. "Food stays dry-bagged and tied to the thwarts. If we swamp, the bags float, we grab them. Medical kit goes in the middle, wrapped double."

"The medical kit," Tomas repeated, not looking at her.

"Wrapped double. In the dry bag with the yellow strap."

"I know which one it is." He turned then, and his eyes were the color of the water. "I packed it."

* * *

They launched at midmorning, when the light came high enough to show the waves' true faces. Gideon took the sweep oar at the stern, Bisi forward with a paddle, Dev amidships bailing with a cut-down jerrican. Tomas sat low in the center with his arms around the medical bag, and if there was symbolism in that, nobody said it aloud.

The first rapid was a lie — big noise, soft water, the boat sliding left of the boulder with room to spare. Bisi whooped once, a short bark of joy, and for half a minute Gideon felt something loosen in her chest that hadn't loosened in ten years. The river was terrible and alive and honest in a way people weren't. It only wanted one thing from you, and it told you exactly what.

The second rapid did not lie.

It came around a blind corner where the gorge pinched and the whole river stood up on end — a single wave train, four waves deep, each taller than the last, the fourth breaking back against itself in a curtain of white. There was no line through it. There was only through it.

"Straight!" she shouted, and dug the oar, and the boat climbed.

The first wave lifted them like a hand. The second broke over the bow and filled the world white. She heard Dev gasp, heard the jerrican clatter loose, and then the third wave hit them sideways and the world tilted, and Gideon understood, in the long cold instant before impact, that they were going to surf this wave or die on it, and that the difference would be measured in inches of freeboard and seconds of decision.

The boat surfed. For three heartbeats they hung in the trough, walls of water on either side, spinning slow as a compass needle finding north. Then the fourth wave took them broadside and rolled them like a log.

Cold went through her like a blade drawn the length of her body. The Karakoram does not warm its water for anyone. She surfaced gasping, found the sweep oar floating beside her, grabbed it, grabbed the safety line trailing from the stern as the boat came upright half-swamped, riding low as a drowned animal. Bisi was on the line already, hauling herself hand over hand, teeth chattering so hard Gideon could hear them over the roar. Dev surfaced downstream, coughing, one arm hooked through a floating dry bag.

The medical bag was gone.

No — not gone. She saw it for one second, maybe two: the yellow strap bright against the green water, turning over and over in the current, Tomas's hands actually closing on it, and then the river took it the way the mountain had taken other things, without malice, without ceremony, because taking things was simply what moving water did.

"Tomas!" His head broke surface ten meters down, and he made a lunge for the bag that pulled him toward the seam of current along the cliff wall, the place where rivers drown strong swimmers.

"Let it go!" Gideon roared. "Let it GO!"

He let it go. She hauled him to the boat by his pack straps, and together they dragged him over the gunwale into the sloshing bilge, where he knelt, streaming, staring back downriver at the place where the yellow strap had vanished, as if staring could bring it back.

* * *

They grounded on a shingle bar an hour later, all of them shaking with cold, and took inventory the way survivors always do: everything laid out on the stones, counted twice, the numbers getting worse the second time.

Half their food had been in bags that floated and were recovered. Half the fuel was gone. Two sleeping bags soaked past saving. And the medical kit — Tomas unwrapped what he'd managed to snatch from the flood line downstream, a single torn pouch that had snagged on a willow — and laid out the salvage: gauze, tape, a bottle of iodine, half a blister pack of antibiotics gone soft. No sutures. No splint material beyond what they could cut from trekking poles. No altitude medications. No painkillers stronger than paracetamol.

"Six days above five thousand meters," Tomas said quietly, "with no dexamethasone, no acetazolamide, no oxygen. If anyone gets pulmonary edema up there, we carry them down or we bury them."

Nobody answered. The river filled the silence, indifferent, enormous.

Bisi wrung out her braid and spoke carefully, the way she did when she was choosing sides. "We could go back. Climb out at the last side valley, take the shepherd trail to the road. Lose the season but keep the people."

"And come back next year?" Dev said. "The pass closes after monsoon. Mara's window is now. You know that."

"Mara's window." Bisi laughed, but there was nothing in it. "Funny how the dead man's window matters more than ours."

Gideon crouched by the fire they were building, feeding it splinters of driftwood, and said nothing, because she was doing the arithmetic Tomas had already done, and arriving at the same answer, and hating it.

It was Tomas who finally turned on her.

"You knew." His voice was low, controlled, worse than shouting. "You looked at that hull for a full minute before we launched. You touched the seam. You *felt* it flex. And you put us on the water anyway, because losing three days was unacceptable, because your schedule mattered more than my kit, more than—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was somehow louder. "I've read the report, Gideon. The 2014 expedition. I've read it three times. You want to know what it says? It says the leader was advised to wait out the storm window and elected to push for the summit. Advised. Elected. Pushed." He held up the sodden antibiotic packet between two fingers, dripping. "This is you electing to push."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them a chough called, a thin rasp against the gray sky.

"That was different," Bisi began.

"Was it?" Tomas didn't look away from Gideon. "Three people died because one person couldn't stand to turn around. I wasn't there. Maybe the report is unfair. But I'm looking at the same woman making the same calculation — speed over margin, pride over prudence — and I'm asking myself whether I signed on to a rescue or a repetition."

Gideon stood. Her wet clothes steamed faintly in the fire's heat, and she was aware of the others watching her the way climbers watch a cornice: waiting to see if it holds or lets go.

"Say what you're going to say, Tomas."

"I'm saying you should have waited. I'm saying a good leader counts the cost before the river does. And I'm saying that if anyone dies above five thousand meters because we're short on medicine, that death is yours. Not the mountain's. Yours." He gathered his salvaged supplies with hands that trembled slightly, whether from cold or fury she couldn't tell, and walked to the far end of the bar to dry them alone.

Dev watched him go, then looked at Gideon, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — guilt wearing sympathy's clothing, or the reverse. "He's scared," Dev offered. "Scared men say cruel things."

"He's right," Gideon said.

Bisi's head came up sharply. "He's not—"

"He is." She fed another stick to the fire and watched the flame take it. "Ten years ago I pushed when I should have waited, and Emeka—" The name caught, as it always caught, a fishhook in her throat. She swallowed past it. "Emeka went up ahead of me because I told him the window would hold. So when Tomas stands there dripping river water and tells me I'm repeating myself, I don't get to be angry. I get to be accurate."

The silence that followed was not comfortable, but it was honest, and honesty was the only currency they had left that the river hadn't taken.

* * *

They camped that night on the bar, cold and damp, watches staggered because trust now required verification. Gideon took first watch, sitting with her back against a boulder, the sweep oar across her knees like a spear, watching the river run silver-black under the rising moon.

Her mind kept returning to the yellow strap. To the small bright violence of watching something essential leave. Ten years ago she had watched a figure in a red jacket vanish into whiteout and told herself he was dead, because the alternative — that he was alive, alone, calling for her somewhere behind the curtain of snow — was a weight no spine could carry. Now she was walking back up into that country to find out which truth she'd buried, and she had done it with half a medical kit and a crew whose faith in her was leaking faster than any hull.

A leader counts the cost before the river does.

He was right. That was the poison of it. He was right about the calculation even if he was wrong about her heart, and leadership meant living inside that distinction without letting it rot you.

Movement at the edge of camp. Dev, unable to sleep, walking down to the waterline, standing with his back to her. She saw the small glow of a screen cupped in his hands, saw him glance over his shoulder — once, twice — before he crouched, hiding the light against his body.

Gideon sat very still in the dark and told herself there were a hundred innocent reasons for a man to check his phone by a river at midnight.

She thought of none of them.
