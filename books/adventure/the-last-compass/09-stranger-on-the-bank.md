## 9. Stranger on the Bank

The river spat them out like something it had only meant to taste.

Gideon came up coughing, hands clawing at shingle, and for a long moment she simply lay there on her side, cheek pressed to wet stone, letting the current tug at her boots as if it were considering taking her back. The sky was the color of dishwater. Rain fell with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

She counted them the way she'd learned to count everything in the last ten years — by what remained. Bisi, twenty meters downstream, hauling herself onto a gravel bar with one arm wrapped around her ribs. Tomas, face-down and breathing, which after the rapids felt like a small miracle. Dev, already moving, already checking, because whatever else he was, he was not useless.

Four of them. One split boat somewhere behind them in the white water, along with half their food, the rope bag's spare coils, Tomas's second oxygen bottle, and — Gideon closed her eyes and saw it go — the satellite phone's charging kit, dead weight now that the phone itself had drowned in her chest pocket.

"Nobody's dead," she said aloud, to the rain, to herself. "Say it back."

"Nobody's dead," Bisi called back, voice cracked but steady. Then, darker: "But we're wet through, boss, and it's dropping toward four degrees."

Gideon got up. Her knees reported in. The left one had found a rock on the way down and would be filing complaints for days.

They were on the true right bank of the river, a shelf of grey shingle maybe thirty meters wide, walled in by scree above and water below. The gorge walls rose on either side like the pages of a shut book. There was no trail here — no trail was supposed to exist here at all; this valley was off every map that mattered, which was why they'd come — but rivers made their own logic, and where there was flat ground near water in these ranges, someone had usually stood on it before.

"Shelter first, then inventory," she said. "Bisi, can you walk?"

"I can walk angry, if that helps."

"It always has."

* * *

They found the overhang an hour later, or rather Dev found it, scrambling up a fold in the scree where a slab of gneiss leaned against its brothers and left a triangle of dry shadow beneath. It wasn't much. A floor of gravel and old flood debris — a bleached log, a tangle of plastic netting, the rib cage of some animal long picked clean. But it was out of the rain, and out of the wind that came funnelling down the gorge like a train through a tunnel.

Tomas was the problem. He hadn't stopped shivering since the water, and his lips had gone the blue-grey of old ice. Gideon stripped him, wrung out his layers, and got him into what dry gear they had left, which amounted to Bisi's spare fleece and Gideon's storm shell over the top, and then sat him between them with a hexamine stove hissing under the last of their fuel, melting snow into something hot.

"We lost the medical kit," Tomas said. His teeth chattered around the words. "Half of it. The antibiotics, most of the dressings, the—"

"I know what we lost."

"Do you?" He looked at her then, and there it was again, the thing that had been growing in his eyes since the boat went into the rapids. Not hatred. Something worse. Recognition. "Because I'm starting to think you don't. I'm starting to think this is familiar to you. The gamble, the river, the—"

"Tomas." Bisi's voice, low.

"No. Let me say it." He was shivering too hard to sound properly angry, which somehow made it more damning. "Ten years ago you ran a river that shouldn't have been run, and three people went into the white and didn't come out. And today I watched you make the same calculation with the same face. Calm. Like the math was just math."

Gideon held his gaze. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the scree in slow metronome ticks.

"The math is never just math," she said quietly. "That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone learns. I weighed losing two days against losing the boat anyway when the level came up overnight. I'd make it again."

"You'd better hope the mountain doesn't audit you twice."

"Tomas." Bisi again, sharper.

But Gideon raised a hand. "He's allowed. Fear talks. Let it talk." She turned back to the medic. "When you're warm, you'll tell me what we still have, and I'll build the plan around what we still have. That's the job now. Not the plan I brought. The plan this river left us."

Tomas looked away first. It wasn't surrender. It was exhaustion wearing surrender's clothes. Gideon would take it.

Dev said nothing through all of it. He sat at the mouth of the overhang, watching the river, and Gideon watched him watch it, and thought: *he knew the crossing point was gone before we got there.* Or maybe he hadn't. Maybe she was becoming the kind of leader who suspected everyone, which was another way of saying the kind who trusted no one, which was another way of saying alone.

The light died early under the rain. They ate cold — half rations, by her order, no argument even from Bisi — and took watches in pairs. Gideon took the middle of the night with Dev, and they sat shoulder to shoulder at the overhang's lip while the river talked to itself in the dark, a vast low muttering, sometimes rising into a roar when something big gave way upstream.

"My brother hated rivers," Dev said, without preamble. "Felt they were dishonest. Ice tells you what it's doing, he said. Rivers lie about their depth."

"What happened to him?"

"Avalanche. Six years ago. On a peak I won't name." Dev turned something over in his fingers — a smooth stone, worried shiny. "Everyone asks about the mountain. Nobody asks about the river."

Gideon said nothing. In the dark, the stone clicked softly against his ring.

* * *

They smelled the smoke before they saw anything else.

It came down the gorge on the morning air — woodsmoke, real smoke, the smell of a fire tended by human hands — and all four of them froze over the remains of breakfast like animals catching a scent on the wind.

"Someone lives here," Bisi breathed.

"Or someone's hunting here," Tomas said.

Gideon stood. Downstream, perhaps a kilometer where the gorge widened, a thread of grey rose from behind a spur of rock and bent sideways in the wind. Beside it, impossibly, hung a shape she recognized from a decade ago and another life: a drying rack, wooden poles cross-hatched, fish strung in silver rows.

A fisherman. In a valley that was supposed to be empty.

"Stay here," she said. "I'll go."

"Alone?" Bisi's eyebrows went up.

"If it's trouble, one person is a visitor. Four is an invasion." She pulled on her pack, checked the knife at her belt out of habit rather than intent. "If I'm not back in two hours, come down loud."

The walk took forty minutes. The gorge opened as she went, the walls leaning back to reveal a shelf of terraced gravel where someone had built — not a camp, a *place*. A stilt house of driftwood and tarpaulin and scavenged tin, squatting above the flood line on legs of black timber. Nets hung from poles. A coracle-style skin boat, patched and repatched, rested hull-up beside a stack of split wood. Smoke curled from a stovepipe made from a section of chimney pipe that must have fallen off a plane or been carried fifty miles for the privilege.

And standing in the yard, mending a net with hands that moved like they'd done nothing else for twenty years, was a man.

He was hard to age. Fifty, sixty — the mountains kept their own calendar. Broad through the shoulders, narrow through the hips, with a beard going iron-grey and eyes the color of the river in March: pale, quick, and carrying more weight than the surface showed. He watched her come the whole way without stopping his work, and when she stopped at the edge of his terrace, he spoke first.

"You're the ones who ran the gorge yesterday."

Not a question. Gideon blinked rain from her lashes. "You heard us?"

"Heard you. This valley carries sound like a drum." He set down the net. "Two boats' worth of noise, then one boat screaming, then nothing. I thought the river had taken you all. It usually does, when it takes anyone." His gaze went past her, up the gorge, then back. "You're four. You were six."

"Five," Gideon said. "And we lost gear, not people."

"Not yet." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reports weather. "You're off the maps. Restricted ground. So either you're smugglers, which means I should have buried you already, or you're fools on some errand the government doesn't want done." He tilted his head. "Which are you?"

The honest answer was *both*, or close enough. Gideon chose a third road. "We're looking for someone who died up high. We're bringing him home."

Something moved behind the man's eyes — a door opening a crack onto a lit room, then closing. "The high passes collect people," he said. "Rarely give them back."

"That's why we're doing it ourselves."

"Hm." He studied her the way he might study a knot in his netting, deciding whether to cut it out or work with it. "You're the leader. They wait for you to speak first. Even the angry one." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "You're Gideon Okonkwo."

Her whole body went still. Rain ticked on the gravel between them. "I don't know you."

"You don't. But your name walked ahead of you. It walks strange roads, that name." He wiped his hands on his thighs and extended one — huge, scarred across the palm in a pattern Gideon recognized: rope burn, old and deep, the kind that comes from holding on when every instinct screams to let go. "Yusuf. I fish this river. I guide it, in the seasons when guiding is honest work. And I know the monsoon came three weeks early, which means you're not walking out of this valley, and neither is anyone else, for eleven days at least. Maybe fourteen."

"Eleven days." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. Eleven days marooned on shingle with a quarter of their food and a quarter of their medicine and a crew coming apart at the seams. Eleven days while whoever else was on the mountain — and she no longer doubted there was someone — moved freely.

"Eleven days," Yusuf agreed. "Unless you have a boat."

"Our boat is in the next district by now. In pieces."

"Your boat was a bathtub with opinions. I watched it go by." He nodded toward the hull-up skin boat by the woodpile. "Mine has crossed this river two hundred times. She leaks where I tell her to leak."

"And you'd take us upriver." Gideon kept her voice level, but her pulse had quickened. Upriver meant the gorge, meant the route to the high country, meant days saved that they did not have to spend. "In monsoon swell."

"In monsoon swell, yes. I've run worse with less." He folded his arms. "Shelter until the river drops enough to run — three days, maybe four. Food, such as my larder offers. Dry ground for your sick one, who I could hear shivering from here, which is a bad sign even over water. And passage upriver past the gorge, to the last landing before the climb."

Gideon felt the trap of gratitude closing gently around her, and stepped into it anyway, because pride was a luxury and her crew was freezing. "What's the price?"

Yusuf looked at her for a long moment. The rain filled the silence, patient as ever.

"You couldn't pay it yet," he said finally. "Even if I named it."

"Try me."

"No." He turned back to his net, and his hands resumed their ancient rhythm, and his voice came quiet under the rain. "Rest your people. Eat my fish. When the river drops, I'll take you up. And when we get where we're going — you'll see the price yourself. Then you can decide whether to pay it."

"That's not how deals work."

"No," Yusuf agreed, not looking up. "This is how *mountain* deals work. It takes what it takes, and it shows you the bill afterward." He glanced at her once, pale eyes unreadable. "Go fetch your people, Okonkwo. Your sick one doesn't have three hours in him on that shingle. And—" the pause was small, but she caught it, the way she'd catch a hold breaking loose under her hand, "—tell the young one who photographs things to leave the camera in his pocket while he's under my roof. I count everything in this valley. Everything."

The rain kept falling. Gideon stood very still on the stranger's terrace, the word *photographs* ringing in her ears like a dropped carabiner, and understood that the man mending nets had known more about her crew in five minutes than she'd managed to learn in five days.

She turned and walked back up the gorge, fast, mind racing faster — toward her shivering crew, toward the deal she'd already swallowed, toward the price waiting unnamed at the top of the river like a serac waiting to fall.
