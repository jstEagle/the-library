## 10. Monsoon Walls

The rain began an hour before dawn, and Yusuf Karim knew before he opened his eyes that it would not stop.

He had lived forty-one years beneath these walls of water and stone, and the mountain spoke to him in a language older than words — the particular weight of cloud on the ridgeline, the smell the river gave off when it had been drinking from a glacier all night, the way his grandmother's old stilt house creaked at its joints like an old man rising from prayer. This morning the house groaned low and continuous, and the river below had changed its voice from conversation to argument.

*Chhiso,* his mother used to call such days. Cold ones. The kind that closed the valley.

He rose, fed the fire with split pine until the room filled with orange light and the smell of resin, and stood at the window watching the rain come down in ropes. Across the water, half-hidden in the gray, five figures were picking their way along the bank toward his landing — four walking, one supported between two others, and all of them moving with the stiff, deliberate care of people who had already given more to this river than they intended.

Yusuf had known they would come back. He had watched them run the gorge two days ago in a boat that was dying under them, and he had stood on this bank with his arms crossed and thought: *that woman will kill them all, or she will bring them through.* There was no middle road with people who steered like that. He had seen her kind before. He had married one, once, briefly, before the mountain took up all the room in her chest.

He put the kettle on. If they were coming to his door, they would need tea more than they needed pity.

* * *

They arrived looking like drowned birds.

The tall woman — Bisi, the one with rope-scarred hands and a laugh she kept holstered — carried the sick one, Tomas, whose lips had gone the blue-gray of old ice. Gideon Okonkwo came last, the way leaders do, watching the back trail even as she stepped onto his planks. Dev hovered near her elbow, young and watchful, carrying too much of someone else's weight in his face. And behind them all, limping, wrapped in a plastic sheet that had surrendered its fight hours ago, came the fifth — no. Four. It was four now. He counted again out of habit, the way his father had taught him to count goats before dark, and found only four where there had been five on the ridge yesterday.

Something had happened in the gorge. Something had cost them.

"You said shelter," Gideon said. Water ran off her in threads. Her eyes went past him into the house, cataloguing exits, food stores, the strength of the floor joists, and Yusuf felt the familiar irritation of a man being measured by someone who did not yet know she was measuring herself against him too.

"I said passage," he corrected. "Shelter is what the rain gives you whether you want it or not." He stepped aside. "Come in before my house decides you're firewood."

Tomas they laid on the sleeping platform nearest the stove, and Yusuf worked while Bisi held the man's head — altitude sickness blooming into something worse, fluid gathering in lungs that needed to be dry. He had medicine, the good kind, traded down-valley from an army doctor who owed him favors he no longer remembered incurring. He dosed Tomas, set a pot to boil for steam, and gave orders in the flat tone that made strangers obey him before they'd decided to. Bisi followed every one without question, which told him she had been a medic's hands long enough to know competence when it walked into a room wearing wet wool.

Gideon stood apart, dripping, watching the rain hammer the valley shut.

"The pass," she said finally. "How long does this hold?"

Yusuf poured tea and handed cups around before answering. "Three days if we're lucky. Five if we're not. After that the snowline drops and the high trails go soft as bread in milk." He looked at her over the rim of his own cup. "You knew that. You asked anyway because you wanted to hear me say it."

A flicker crossed her face — not anger. Recognition. "You've guided."

"Before your time. Before mine, almost." He sat down heavily on a stool, joints announcing themselves. "My father guided. His father poled cargo up this river when the passes still belonged to traders and not governments. I fished when the fish were worth catching and guided when they weren't, and now mostly I fish and remember."

"And the pass we're bound for?" She kept her voice level, but he heard the current underneath. "The one that's been closed ten years. You know it."

The rain filled the silence for him, drumming on the roof like fingers on a drumhead, and Yusuf found himself looking at the woman across the fire and understanding, with the slow certainty of a man watching weather build over a ridge, that she was going to ask him something he could not refuse, and that he was going to ask her something neither could she.

"I know it," he said. "I buried part of myself on it."

* * *

Her name had reached him before she did, of course. Names travel faster than people in these valleys; they ride the wind down from the tea houses and the checkposts, whispered from porter to cook to police constable, arriving ahead of their owners like birds before a storm.

*Gideon Okonkwo.* Ten years ago that name had been shouted across a different kind of silence — the silence after the mountain finished its work and the radios went quiet. He had been younger then, and angrier, and when the news came down that a foreign expedition had lost three climbers on the pass where his son —

He stopped the thought the way you stop a boat above a rapid: hard, both poles down, everything in your arms.

But the boy's face came anyway. It always came. Rahim at fourteen, all elbows and questions, standing on this same platform asking why anyone would climb a mountain when the river gave you everything. *Because some people are made for the high cold places, baba,* Rahim had said, repeating it back to him with a grin, having heard it from a trekking client, having decided it was true. Having decided it about himself.

Rahim had gone up with a commercial team the summer he turned seventeen, as a porter, because the money was real and because his father had said no and because seventeen-year-olds are built out of the exact material that turns *no* into fuel. The storm took the team on the descent. Six dead. Among them a guide whose name was never printed in the papers here but was spoken in every tea house for a season afterward, blamed, blacklisted, erased — a woman who had led survivors down through whiteout while her brother vanished somewhere above her.

Yusuf had hated that woman for years. Not because she was guilty. Because she was alive, and alive people get to keep walking away, and the dead stay exactly where the mountain puts them.

Now she sat in his house drinking his tea, and the hatred, meeting her actual face, collapsed like a snow bridge underfoot.

"You're her," he said quietly, when the others had gone out to secure the boat and check the lines. "The one from the disaster. Okonkwo."

She didn't flinch. He gave her credit for that. "I'm her."

"They say you killed three people with your pride."

"They say a lot of things." She set down her cup. "Some of them are even true. What do they say about you, Yusuf? The man on the river who won't go above the tree line?"

The question landed precisely where she meant it to, and he let out a breath that was half laugh, half surrender. "That I'm a coward."

"What do you say?"

He looked out at the rain. The valley had disappeared entirely now — wall upon wall of gray water falling through gray air, the world reduced to the width of his porch and the sound of the river swelling below. Somewhere up in that blindness, past the clouds, past the seracs, lay the pass. Lay the cave. Lay the small tin box he had left inside a dead boy's jacket eleven years ago because he could not carry it home and could not leave it anywhere the living might find it.

"I say," he answered slowly, "that some men don't climb mountains because they fear dying on them. They don't climb because they fear what they left on them."

Gideon was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the leader's armor. "There's a cave. Below a serac on the north shoulder. We're going to it."

"I know the cave."

"There's a body in it. My brother's." Her hands, wrapped around the cooling cup, had gone white at the knuckles. "And there may be something else. Something he — " She stopped. Started again. "Why do you know the cave, Yusuf?"

Because I put my son's watch in it, he did not say. Because I climbed up there with a shovel and a prayer rug and got within two hundred meters before my legs stopped being mine, and I left the tin box beside a stranger's frozen hand because I could not dig in that ground and I could not go home with the box either, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the mountain would keep it safe the way it keeps everything — selfishly, forever.

What he said was: "My son died on that pass. Six summers before your disaster. A commercial team, a storm, six dead." He watched her face change — watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes, the same arithmetic he had performed a hundred times since reading the expedition reports. "Your brother's team came later. Different route, same shoulder. The reports said a guide named Okonkwo identified the site from below. Through binoculars."

"I did." Her voice cracked on the second word. "We couldn't reach them. The serac was — "

"Active. I know. I stood where you stood." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight moved across both their faces like a slow tide. "So you see, Ms. Okonkwo, we are the same animal, you and I. Two people who looked at a mountain through glass and made our peace with never touching it. Until now."

* * *

The others returned soaked a second time, and the evening became the kind of evening that happens only in houses cut off from the world: rice and lentils and dried fish, steam on the windows, Tomas breathing easier under the influence of the drugs, Bisi telling a story about a mule in Manang with the timing of a born performer, Dev laughing a beat too late because he was listening to the rain instead, counting something in it.

Yusuf watched them all from his corner and understood that he had already decided. That was the thing about decisions in these valleys — you didn't make them so much as notice, one morning, that they had been made for you by everything that came before.

When the fire burned low and the others slept, Gideon remained awake, sitting by the window the way he did on nights the river talked too loudly. He brought her tea she hadn't asked for and sat across from her, and for a while neither spoke, and the rain spoke enough for both.

"My price," he said at last. "For the boat, the supplies, the route knowledge. You've been waiting to hear it."

"I have."

"You take me to the cave."

She turned to look at him fully, and in the dark her eyes were just two points of reflected fire. "It's dangerous. The approach is — "

"I know what the approach is. I've dreamed it eleven years." His voice stayed level, which cost him. "I left something there. Beside a body that wasn't my son's but became, in my mind, close enough. A tin box. Inside it, his watch — the cheap one I bought him in Gilgit the week before he left, the one his mother gave him money to replace and he refused to. I couldn't bury it at home. Bringing it home meant admitting the mountain had all of him and I had nothing, and I wasn't strong enough for that arithmetic." He looked at his hands, broad and scarred from rope and net and winter. "I am strong enough now. Or I am old enough, which comes to the same thing."

Gideon set down her cup. For a moment he thought she would refuse him — leaders refuse things reflexively, the way rivers refuse to run uphill — but instead she said, softly:

"He survived. Up there. Four days."

Yusuf went very still.

"We don't know yet — there's a journal, we think, if the ice kept it. But the compass he left, the way it was placed — he placed it, Yusuf. Deliberately. For me." Her breath shuddered. "Four days alone in the dark, dying, and he spent them writing to me. While I was down at base camp believing he was already gone." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Your son. Did he — was anything ever — "

"No." The word came out of him like a stone from a sling. "No note. No effects recovered. Six bodies in the spring melt, identified by clothing and teeth. That is what the mountain gave me, and I took it, because the alternative was taking nothing." He stared into the fire. "If your brother wrote four days of words before he died, then he was luckier than Rahim. And if those words forgave you — then you have something I will never have, and I confess I don't know whether to envy you or grieve for you."

"Both," Gideon said. "It's both. It's always both."

Outside, the rain intensified, hammering the valley shut for another day, another night, perhaps three, perhaps five. The river below rose and argued with its banks. Somewhere up in the blind white dark, two boys waited in the ice — one with words in his frozen hands, one with only a watch ticking in a tin box, keeping a time that no longer mattered to anyone but the fathers and sisters who would climb up through the weather to kneel beside them.

"So we have a bargain," Yusuf said.

"We have a bargain."

He rose to bank the fire, and as he stirred the coals he felt something shift in his chest — not lightness, nothing so kind as that, but a settling, the way a load settles correctly on a porter's back after a mile of adjustment. Eleven years he had lived below the tree line, telling himself the mountain had taken enough from him and owed him nothing. But the mountain owed nobody nothing. The mountain collected. It collected sons and brothers and reputations and names, and it kept them in cold storage, and once in a generation it opened its ledger and let the debtors come up to read the accounts.

The rain would break in three days. He knew it in his knees, in the creak of his grandmother's house, in the changed voice of the river.

And when it broke, Yusuf Karim would go up the pass he had sworn off, beside a woman whose name he had once cursed, to open a tin box that had waited longer than any patience should have to.

He slept that night without dreaming, which for him was itself a kind of miracle — and woke before dawn to a silence so complete it woke him faster than any storm. He crossed to the window.

The rain had stopped. Above the eastern ridge, through a tear in the clouds no wider than a blade, the first light of morning fell on the high snowfields — and on the shoulder of the pass, where a serac caught the sun and burned gold, pointing down like a finger at the cave below it.

As if the mountain, having collected long enough, had decided at last to pay out.
