## 11. The Price Named

The rain did not stop. It had not stopped in two days, and Gideon had begun to suspect it never would — that the monsoon had simply swallowed the valley whole and would keep them there, dissolved into water and woodsmoke, until the mountain decided otherwise.

Yusuf's stilt house stood above the flood line the way old things stand above new troubles: patiently, having seen this before. The river below had gone from brown to a churning gray-brown that carried whole trees past them like matchsticks. Every few hours a crack of ice or rock echoed down from the heights, and Tomas would look up from his drying socks with an expression that said, *this is what I warned you about*, without saying anything at all.

Gideon sat on the veranda under the eaves, watching the water take the world apart. Her boots were stuffed with newspaper, her jacket hung from a nail, dripping its slow metronome onto the boards. Somewhere behind her, Bisi was re-coiling rope with the particular violence of a woman who had nearly drowned that morning and intended to punish the rope for it.

Yusuf came out carrying two tin cups of tea so sweet it was almost syrup. He handed her one without asking, which she had learned in two days was his way of asking.

"You are thinking about the pass," he said.

"I'm thinking about the river."

"The river is my business. The pass is yours." He lowered himself onto the bench beside her, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. He was perhaps sixty, perhaps seventy; the mountain had rubbed the numbers off him years ago. "You will want to leave when the rain breaks. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe three days. You cannot know, and neither can I, and that is the whole of what I know."

"Then we wait."

"You will wait." He sipped his tea. "But first you will hear my price."

Gideon turned to look at him. She had been waiting for this since the riverbank, since the moment he'd waded out of the shallows with a fish trap on his shoulder and studied their bedraggled line of survivors the way a man studies weather — not with surprise, but with calculation. *Passage upriver,* he'd said. *Shelter, food, my boat.* And when Gideon had asked what it cost, he had smiled with half his mouth and said, *We will discuss it when your people stop shaking.*

They had mostly stopped shaking.

"Name it," she said. "I don't have much money left after Mara's advance, but—"

"It is not money." He set his cup down on the rail with a small ceramic click. "Money I have. Money is what the lowlands use to pretend things are simple."

* * *

He told it plainly, which was somehow worse than if he had dressed it up.

"My son," Yusuf said, looking at the river rather than at her. "Jamil. He was twenty-four. He wanted to be a guide like me, and I told him no, because I knew what the work costs, and I thought saying no was the same as protecting him." A pause long enough for the rain to fill with meaning. "So he went anyway. With another outfit, one that took clients too high too fast because the clients paid extra. There was a whiteout on the descent. The others made it to shelter. Jamil went back for a client's dropped pack, because he was the kind of boy who goes back for packs." His voice did not change at all, and that flatness was the loudest grief Gideon had ever heard. "They found the client alive in the spring. They found Jamil where the serac above the cave had come down. Mostly."

Gideon's tea had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway.

"The cave," she said quietly. She knew the cave. Of course she knew the cave. It was the reason she was sitting on this man's veranda at all.

"The cave below the serac, yes. On the route you intend to walk." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were the color of wet slate. "You see now why I stopped you on the bank. Not charity. Geography."

"What do you want me to do?"

"When we reach the cave — and we will reach it together, because you cannot find it without me and I cannot climb to it without you — I will go inside. I will be some time. What I do there is mine. But afterward—" His hands, knotted and brown as tree roots, opened slightly in his lap. "Afterward I need you to help me carry something down. Something I left there eleven years ago. I was too old to make the climb then, and every year I am older, and every year the mountain says *not yet, old man, not yet.*" He almost smiled. "Now it has sent you instead. The mountain is patient. It collects everyone eventually. It just likes to make us collect each other."

The rain drummed on the roof. Downriver, something large shifted in the current with a sound like a door closing.

Gideon set her cup beside his. "What did you leave there?"

"That is also mine." But then he exhaled, and some of the slate in his eyes softened toward something almost like shame. "A letter. And a pair of his climbing gloves. Small ones. He was always cold, even as a baby. I told myself I would bury them at the cave, where he—" He stopped. Started again. "Eleven years ago I stood at the base of the moraine and looked up and could not make my legs do it. I hired men. They came back and said the serac was unstable, that it was suicide, that they were sorry. And I paid them and I went home and I told my wife I had tried. I let other men's fear finish my grieving. That is my price, Gideon Okonkwo. Not money. I want my legs to finally do it. And I want a guide whose legs I can trust when mine fail — because they will fail. I am honest about that. Take me to the cave, help me bring my son's things home, and everything I have — the boat, the routes, my knowledge of these valleys — is yours until we cross the last pass."

* * *

She walked to the end of the veranda and stood there a long time, letting the spray off the eaves hit her shoulders, because she needed the cold to think against.

It was a fair price. That was the problem. If he had asked for money she could have haggled, resented, calculated. If he had asked for something dishonorable she could have refused cleanly. But he had asked for the one thing she could not put a number on: passage to the very place she had spent ten years not going, in the company of a man whose grief was a mirror held up to her own.

*The mountain collects everyone eventually.*

Her brother was in that cave. She had known it since Kathmandu, since Mara had said the name — *Emeka Okonkwo* — and the floor of the world had tilted. The manifest listed him as recovered remains, location approximate, high route near the old pass. Approximate. Which meant someone had to go and make it exact. Someone had to kneel in the blue dark of the ice and touch what ten years of cold had kept, and she had known, hiring Bisi, hiring Tomas, signing Dev's quiet handshake, that the someone would be her.

And now the mountain had sent her a second errand, folded inside the first, the way grief folds inside grief. A father's letter. A dead boy's gloves. Two families' worth of unfinished business stacked in one hollow of ice below a hanging serac that could come down any year, any day, any hour — had already come down once, on her brother, on this man's son, maybe in the same season, maybe within sight of each other, two bodies lying in the same cold room while the world above went on arguing about permits and blame.

She thought about refusing. She examined the refusal honestly, turning it over the way Tomas turned over a wound, looking for infection. Was she afraid? Yes. Was the price too high? No — that was the humiliating truth. The price was exactly right, priced by someone who understood that the only currency that mattered up here was what you were willing to carry.

Behind her, the door slid open. Bisi stepped out, rope still over her shoulder, and read the veranda in one glance — the two cups, Yusuf's stillness, Gideon's spine.

"We're staying longer than the weather," Bisi said. Not a question.

"A few days. Maybe more."

"And?"

"He's coming with us. To the cave."

Bisi absorbed this. She was the only member of the crew who had been with Gideon before the disaster, who remembered Emeka from base camp — a big laughing man who taught porters card games and gave away his chocolate. She didn't say his name. She just said, "Good," in a voice that meant *it's time*, and went back inside.

* * *

That evening Yusuf cooked rice and dried fish over the iron stove, and the crew ate in the lamplight while the river roared its one long note below. Tomas asked careful medical questions about altitude and Yusuf's heart, and Yusuf answered them with the unbothered honesty of a man who had already settled his accounts. Dev sat near the door as always, quiet, his eyes moving between the strangers in the room as though memorizing exits — or perhaps, Gideon thought, watching him turn his cup slowly in his fingers, as though memorizing something else entirely. She filed the thought away. Suspicion was a splinter you couldn't dig out; you just learned to grip around it.

Later, when the others slept, Yusuf brought out a wooden box no bigger than a shoebox and opened it on the table between them.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a pair of child-sized climbing gloves, leather cracked along the seams, and a letter in a plastic sleeve, the envelope soft as cloth from handling. No name on the envelope. Just a single word in careful Urdu-English script: *Jamil.*

"I wrote it the winter after they found him," Yusuf said. "Forty pages. Everything a father says when there is no one left to say it to. Then I rewrote it. One page. Forty pages is for the writer. One page is for the dead — they are busy, and they were always impatient readers." He closed the box. "In the morning, if the river drops even a hand's width, we talk about the route. If it doesn't drop, we talk about it anyway. Waiting is also a decision, and I have made enough bad ones."

Gideon reached across the table and rested her hand briefly on the lid of the box. "We'll get them down," she said. "Your son's things. I give you my word."

Yusuf looked at her hand, then at her face, and whatever he saw there seemed to settle something in him. "Careful with words like that on this side of the valley," he said gently. "The mountain hears promises and holds you to the interest." But he covered her hand with his own for a moment, calloused palm on her knuckles, brief and hard as a knot being tied.

* * *

She slept badly, and near dawn dreamed of the cave — not as she'd imagined it in ten years of not imagining it, but as it must actually be: blue light through ice like stained glass, breath hanging in the still air, and somewhere in that cold chapel, waiting with the patience of stone, a compass with her childhood nickname engraved on the case, and a voice she hadn't heard in a decade saying her name the way only a brother could.

She woke before light. The rain had thinned to a whisper. She went out to the rail and looked down, and the river had dropped — not much, a hand's width, maybe less — but the rocks below the stilt house had begun to show their backs like sleeping animals surfacing.

Yusuf was already there, of course, standing in the gray dawn with his hands behind his back, reading the water.

"Well?" Gideon asked.

He nodded slowly. "Two days. Maybe three. Then we go upriver, and then we go up." He glanced sideways at her, and in the half-light his face was unreadable and ancient and, unexpectedly, kind. "Rest now, guide. The mountain has waited eleven years for me. It can learn to wait a little longer for both of us."

Below them, the river kept running, carrying the mountain piece by piece toward the sea — and somewhere far upstream, above the clouds, the ice held its two secrets a little longer, patient as the dark, waiting to be found.
