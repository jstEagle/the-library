## 12. Dev's Signal Fire

The rain had followed them upriver for two days, thinning from monsoon violence into a gray, needling drizzle that made the world feel like it was being erased. They had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder where the gorge widened and the river ran quieter, a shelf of gravel and stunted juniper just large enough for four tents and the patched boat pulled up on its side like a beached animal.

Dev waited until the others slept.

He told himself it was habit — the discipline of a man who had spent years moving through valleys where information was currency and sleep was a luxury you paid for in mistakes. He lay in his tent listening to the river's low murmur and the tick of water off the flysheet, and he did not move for a long time. Above him, through the mesh, the clouds had torn open in one place, and a scatter of stars showed through, cold and indifferent as nailheads.

The satellite messenger sat at the bottom of his pack, wrapped in his spare shirt, exactly where it had been since Yusuf's stilt house. It was heavier than its weight. It had been heavier every day since the river.

He thought about what Bisi had said at the fire two nights ago, almost casually, passing him the tea: *You photograph everything, Dev. Even the things that don't matter.* She had smiled when she said it. Her smiles were like the mountain weather — you learned not to trust a clear patch.

He should have left the device in the river. There had been a moment, knee-deep in the shallows below the gorge, hauling the boat while the smugglers' smoke still smudged the ridge behind them, when he could have simply opened his hand. The current would have taken it. No one would have known.

Instead he had dried it by the fire, carefully, like tending something alive.

Now he unzipped the tent and stepped out into the drizzle, boots silent on the wet gravel. Gideon's tent was dark. Tomas snored behind his, the sound rhythmic and human and oddly comforting. Bisi's tent stood closest to the rock wall, and Dev gave it a wide berth, heart tapping against his ribs like a fist on a door.

He walked down to the water's edge, where the noise of the river would cover any sound, and knelt, and took out the messenger.

The screen glowed green in the dark of his cupped hands. One bar of signal. Of course there was signal — there was always signal now, the whole world wired and watched, no valley so deep that a man could disappear into it. That had been the promise they'd made him, back in Kathmandu, in a tea shop that smelled of cardamom and lies: *You won't have to do anything but walk and send. Three coordinates a day. That's all.*

Three coordinates a day. And then the men with the rifles would come up the route like water finding a crack, and take whatever the dead man's pass held, and Dev would have enough money to go home to his mother in Humla with his head held high, and his brother's debt would finally be a thing that belonged to the past.

His brother. Sanjay, who had gone onto the mountains at nineteen with a load on his back and never come down, whose body the glacier kept the way misers keep coins. Sanjay, who had taught him that porters were the ones who carried other people's dreams up the ice, and that the mountains ate porters first because they were closest to the ground.

*You won't even have to lie,* the man in the tea shop had said. *Just don't tell them what you are.*

And Dev had believed, in those first days, that he could carry both truths at once — that a man could walk beside people and betray them and still look them in the eye, because hadn't his whole life been a lesson in carrying impossible loads? But the river had changed the arithmetic. Tomas shouting at Gideon in the rapids. Bisi's hands, rope-burned and steady, pulling him out of the water when he slipped. Yusuf naming his price without flinching: *my son's grave, that is all I ask.* And Gideon herself, standing in the bow of a leaking boat with her jaw set like heredity, making the call no one else would make, taking the weight of it onto her own spine.

They were not what he had expected. That was the whole ruin of it. He had expected clients — rich ghosts playing at adventure, guides running from their reputations. Instead he had found people who bled, who grieved, who forgave in increments too small to measure but real, real as rope under tension.

He looked at the screen. The last message sat in the outbox, unsent, composed three days ago before the storm: coordinates, elevation, a note that the crew was delayed by weather. If he sent nothing tonight, the men on the ridge would know something had changed. If he sent the truth, he killed them. If he sent a lie —

If he sent a lie, he was still sending.

His thumb hovered over the button. The river talked and talked, saying nothing, saying everything.

Behind him, a boot crunched on gravel.

Dev's whole body went cold, colder than the drizzle could account for. He knew before he turned who it would be. Some instincts arrive ahead of knowledge, the way thunder arrives ahead of the storm's center.

Bisi stood ten paces away in her rain shell, hood back, hair plastered dark to her forehead. In her right hand she held her headlamp, switched off. In her left, casually, terribly, she held the coil of climbing rope she always slept with near her feet.

"I couldn't sleep either," she said. "Funny thing."

"Bisi—"

"Get up," she said quietly. "Slowly. Come to the fire."

"There's no fire."

"There will be." Her voice didn't rise, didn't sharpen. That was the worst part. Bisi angry was loud; Bisi certain was soft. "Gideon needs to see this. We all do."

* * *

They woke Gideon first. Dev watched her emerge from her tent in the gray pre-dawn, watch her face go through the stages — confusion, then attention, then a kind of stillness that frightened him more than anger would have. She listened to Bisi's few flat sentences — *found him at the waterline, satellite messenger, he was composing a transmission* — and she did not look at Dev. Not once. She looked at the device in Bisi's hand as if it were a wound on the mountain itself.

"Tomas," she said. "Wake him. Then build a small fire. Small."

Tomas came awake fast, the way medics do, and understood faster. He looked at Dev across the little flames with an expression Dev had seen before on faces in hospitals — the look of a man recalculating how much of someone he had trusted, and finding the sum short.

"Say it yourself," Gideon said. "Whatever it is. Say it yourself, Dev. All of it."

So Dev said it.

He said it badly at first, and then, as the words found their channel, he said it completely, because there was no longer any use in half-loads. The tea shop in Kathmandu. The nameless man with the soft voice and the hard eyes who had known about the expedition before Mara Voss had finished planning it. The money — enough to clear his family's debt, enough to buy back the small terraced field his father had lost, enough to stop being a man who owed everyone everything. The instructions: three transmissions a day, route notes photographed, positions logged. The promise that no one would be hurt. *We only want what's on the pass. Nobody has to die.*

"They told me nobody would get hurt," Dev repeated, and heard how it sounded, and hated himself for having ever believed it. "I know how it sounds."

"How long?" Gideon asked. Her voice was level. The firelight moved on her face and made it look carved.

"Since Kathmandu. Before we crossed the boundary line. The photographs — the route notes —"

"I know about the photographs," Bisi said. "I've known since the second valley."

Dev's head came up. "You —"

"You hold your phone like a man writing letters," Bisi said. "Everyone else holds it like a man reading them. I've run enough expeditions to know the difference." She fed a stick to the fire. "I wanted to be wrong. I've been wrong before about people I liked."

"The river," Tomas said suddenly. "The crossing point. Did they know where we'd cross?"

"They knew the route," Dev said. "The standard route. The river changing — that wasn't in anyone's plan. The early melt, the flood — the mountain did that, not me." He stopped. "But the boat. When we ran the gorge. I think — I think they were already following us by then. I think my messages brought them up the valley faster than we moved."

Silence, except the fire and the river. Somewhere upslope a stone clattered loose — a marmot, probably, probably — and every head turned toward the dark, and every face came back wearing the same new knowledge: the mountain was no longer the only thing hunting them.

"The warning shots," Yusuf said. He had been standing at the edge of the firelight, and now he came forward and crouched, old joints cracking, and looked at Dev with eyes that had seen forty years of rivers and every kind of man who came down them. "On the ridge. You think those were yours."

"Yes," Dev whispered.

"And the medical kit," Tomas said, "the supplies lost in the rapids — we're short because of what your paymasters cost us, and you took their money to make sure they found us short." His voice cracked on the last words, and Dev realized the medic was not only angry; he was afraid, and had been afraid since the shots, and fear had been looking for somewhere to put itself down.

"Yes," Dev said again. There was nothing else. The word was a stone and he laid it down each time they needed it laid.

Gideon had not spoken for a long time. She sat with her forearms on her knees, watching the fire, and when she finally lifted her head, Dev braced himself for the verdict — for the rope, for the abandonment, for being left on this gravel shelf with a quarter of his supplies and the whole monsoon coming down the valley behind him. He had seen what happened to men caught between parties on a mountain. The mountain always got custody.

"Who else knows?" she asked. "About the ring. Names. Faces. Anything."

"A name," Dev said, startled. "He called himself Dorje. I don't think it was his name. He had a scar through his left eyebrow. He wore a Kathmandu football club jacket. There was another man who never spoke, bigger, with hands like —" He made fists to show. "They paid half in advance. Cash. The rest on delivery of the final position."

"Delivery," Bisi said softly. "Like a package."

"Where's the money now?" Gideon asked.

Dev reached into his jacket and drew out a waterproof pouch, swollen and soft with river damp, and set it by the fire without being told. Half his advance. He had carried it through the boundary crossing, through the flooded valley, through the gorge under gunfire, and he understood only now, watching it sit there in the firelight like something dead, that he had been carrying it the way a drowning man carries a stone he cannot remember to drop.

"That's not all of it," Gideon said. "That's the money. Who else knows about us? Does anyone above this valley know where we are right now?"

"No one," Dev said. "Not anymore. My last transmission was three days ago, before the storm. Unsent." He nodded at the messenger in Bisi's hand. "I couldn't send it. I've been trying not to send it since the river. Tonight I took it out to —" He stopped. Every ending he tried was a lie. *To send a false position. To warn them off. To destroy it.* All of them true. None of them clean.

"To decide," Gideon said.

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long moment, and Dev felt the look go through him like a probe through snow, testing for the crevasse underneath. Then she turned to the others.

"He sold us," she said simply. "Before we ever set foot on the route, he sold our path to men with guns, and men with guns are behind us on this mountain because of it. Bisi is right that he hid it. Tomas is right that we're shorter on supplies than we should be because of what they've done." She paused. "And he pulled Bisi out of the water at the second crossing. He carried Tomas's pack for six hours above the gorge when the altitude had him. And three days ago, on this gravel, alone, with nobody watching, he chose not to press send."

"That doesn't balance," Tomas said. "That doesn't come close to balancing."

"No," Gideon agreed. "It doesn't. Nothing balances out here. That's the first thing the mountain teaches." She picked up the satellite messenger from where Bisi had set it, weighed it in her palm, and held it out to Dev, handle-first, like a tool being returned to its owner. "So here's what happens. You keep this. You keep transmitting — three times a day, same schedule, same format. But the positions you send are the ones I give you. From tonight, you're our instrument pointed the other way."

Dev stared at her. Around the fire, the others were staring too.

"You want to use him," Bisi said. It wasn't quite an objection. It was a question wearing armor.

"I want to survive," Gideon said. "A man who can talk to the hunters is worth more than a man tied to a tree. And Dev—" she turned back to him, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of old ice, "—you're going to earn whatever forgiveness you think you might want, and you're going to earn it at altitude, under load, in front of all of us. Nobody here owes you belief. You'll have to climb for it."

"Yes," Dev said. It was the third time he had said the word that night, and it was the first time it had ever felt like a door opening instead of a stone dropping.

"One more thing." Gideon leaned forward, and the fire painted her shadow huge against the rock wall behind her, a giant with open hands. "Dorje. The scar, the jacket, the voice. Everything you remember about him, you tell Bisi tonight, every detail, until there's nothing left in your head. Because when this is over — and we are going to make it be over — I'm going to find that man. And I'm going to need to know his face better than you do."

Dev nodded. Something in his chest that had been clenched since Kathmandu — some knot of dread and shame and the terrible lightness of secrets — loosened by a single turn. Not undone. Loosened. Forgiveness, he understood suddenly, was not a thing anyone would hand him at this fire. It was a slope. He would have to ascend it.

The fire burned low. Tomas went back to his tent without a word, which Dev noted was not the same as peace. Bisi began, in her flat, patient way, to question him about Dorje, pen and notebook out, and Dev answered, digging through memory like a man digging through avalanche debris, finding small warm shapes of detail he hadn't known he'd kept.

It was near dawn when Gideon stood, stretched, and looked east up the valley — and stopped.

The clouds had broken along the upper ridgeline, and the first light was coming down the glacier in a long rose-colored blade, and it lit, sixty meters above their camp, at the lip of the moraine, a thread of smoke rising straight and thin into the windless air.

Not their smoke. Not a marmot's work.

Someone had a fire on the ridge above them, and had lit it in the night, and had let it burn where they would see it.

Gideon watched it for a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "They're not chasing us anymore."

Nobody asked the obvious question. Nobody had to.

"They're waiting," she said, "to see what Dev tells them next."
