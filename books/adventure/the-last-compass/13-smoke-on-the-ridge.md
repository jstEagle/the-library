## 13. Smoke on the Ridge

The shot came out of the morning mist like a whip-crack, and for one absurd second Gideon thought it was thunder — that the mountain had simply cleared its throat, the way it had all week, muttering in the language of rockfall and groaning ice. Then a geyser of water jumped up thirty meters off their starboard bow, white and sudden, and the sound arrived late, flat, wrong — a rifle report rolling down the valley walls, doubled by echoes until it seemed the whole gorge was firing back.

"Down!" Yusuf's voice, raw as gravel. He threw his weight against the tiller and the patched boat slewed hard to port, the river grabbing them sideways.

Gideon was already moving. Ten years of instinct folded her body low against the gunwale before her mind caught up, one arm hooked through the cargo netting, the other reaching across the thwart to haul Dev down beside her. Bisi had Tomas flattened beneath her in the bilge water, shielding him with her own back, her face turned up toward the ridge with an expression Gideon had seen only once before — on another river, in another lifetime, when the world came apart in white.

*Second shot.* Higher this time. It cracked off a boulder somewhere behind them and sang away into the mist, spent.

"They're not aiming at us," Yusuf said through his teeth. The boat wallowed, shipping water over the gunwales, and he hauled it straight again with both hands. "Not yet."

"Not yet," Bisi echoed from the bottom of the boat, dry as dust.

Gideon lifted her head just enough to look back the way they'd come. The ridge above the last bend was a smear of dark green and gray stone, the monsoon clouds hanging so low they merged with the treeline, but she could see it now — or felt she could: a flicker of movement where no movement should be, men in oilskins who did not belong to any village on this river. Smoke from their cook fire had been bleeding through the trees since dawn, thin and gray and patient. They'd been watching the boat come down the current the way a hawk watches a hedgerow.

Three days ago there had been nothing on that ridge. Three days ago the smugglers had still believed Dev's signal fire had bought them time. Now they'd found the gorge, found the river, and found three men with rifles and all the patience of people being paid by the kilo.

"Yusuf." She kept her voice level, pitched under the river-noise. "How far to the narrows?"

He didn't answer at once. He was reading the water ahead, and she watched him do it — the small tilts of his head, the way his eyes moved not along the surface but *through* it, mapping what lay underneath: the drowned rocks, the seams of current, the holes where the river dropped into itself. A man who'd run this water since he was old enough to hold a pole.

"Narrows are four kilometers," he said finally. "Maybe five. After that the gorge walls close in and the river drops fast. Too fast for them to follow by land once we're inside — the banks go vertical."

"And the rapids in the narrows?"

His jaw worked. "Grade four. Maybe four-plus, in this water." The monsoon had swollen everything; what had been a grade-three chute in his father's day was now something else entirely, brown and muscular and loud enough to feel through the hull. "With this boat—" He stopped. Looked down at the patched hull between his feet, the split planks sewn shut with cord and sealed with pine pitch and a strip of Bisi's tarp, holding the way a promise holds when everyone involved knows how thin it is.

"It'll hold," Gideon said. She didn't know if it was true. She said it anyway, because someone had to say it first, and she had learned long ago — the expensive way, the way carved into her by a storm and three graves — that a crew will believe whatever its leader believes first. Fear is a contagion. So is nerve. You choose which one you carry, and you carry it visibly, or you might as well stay home.

Tomas raised his head from under Bisi's arm. His face was gray-green, whether from fear or the altitude hangover of the last week Gideon couldn't tell, and his voice came out stripped of all its usual surgical precision. "You want to run grade-four water in a boat held together with pitch and prayer, while men shoot at us."

"I want to be alive tomorrow," Gideon said. "They want us stopped tonight. Those aren't the same goal, and I know which one I'm choosing."

Another shot. This one closer — the bullet smacked into the water maybe ten meters astern, and the echo came back wrong, flatter, from a different part of the ridge. *They're moving.* Firing and walking the bank at the same time, keeping pace, herding. Not trying to kill them yet. Trying to push them toward something — a landing spot ahead, probably, some shingle bank where the gorge opened wide enough for men to wait. Smugglers who worked these valleys knew every eddy, every beach. They weren't shooting to sink a boat worth nothing to them. They were shooting to steer it.

"They're driving us," Dev said quietly. He was crouched low, and his hands were shaking — Gideon saw it, saw him see her see it, and something in his face went beyond fear into shame. "There'll be a takeout past the bend. Wide shingle, easy access from the trail. If they herd us there—"

"—we step ashore into their hands." Gideon finished it. She looked at Yusuf. "Can you beat them to the narrows? Run us in before they can get below us?"

Yusuf stared downstream for a long moment. The rain had thinned to a drifting veil, and through it the gorge ahead looked like a wound in the world — black stone rising sheer on both sides, the river vanishing into it around a shoulder of rock, the sound coming back amplified, a deep continuous roar like the mountain breathing through clenched teeth.

"If I commit," he said, "I commit. No eddies to bail out in. Once we're in the throat, the river decides."

"The river already decided," Gideon said. "It's deciding right now, back there, behind those rifles. I'd rather take my chances with water than with men."

Bisi laughed — short, sharp, no humor in it. "Same choice as always, eh, Gidi? Mountain or man."

"Mountain's honest," Gideon said. "Let's go."

* * *

They made ready the way soldiers make ready — without talk, each knowing the shape of the work. Loads were split and lashed low: the medical kit wrapped in the last dry sack and strapped beneath the forward thwart, Tomas's hands pressed flat on top of it as if he could hold it to the boat by will alone. The food sacks went amidships, double-bagged. Ropes coiled and stowed where they couldn't foul. Everything that would float was tied to something that wouldn't.

Gideon took the bow. It wasn't her boat, wasn't her water, and Yusuf was the pilot — but she'd run rivers on two continents, and bow weight mattered in heavy water, and more than that she needed to be where she could see what was coming and call it. Her body remembered things her conscience had tried to bury for a decade: the lean of a hull, the timing of a wave train, the exact moment to stop fighting and let the river have you.

"Dev." She turned and looked at him straight. He flinched from it. Whatever he'd done, whoever he'd answered to, he was here now, soaked and shaking and unable to look away. "When I call a side, you paddle. You don't think. You don't hesitate. You paddle like your life is mine to spend. Understood?"

He swallowed. Nodded. And then, unexpectedly, he said: "I'm sorry. For all of it. If we live—"

"Paddle first," Gideon said. "Apologize after."

Yusuf pushed off from the shingle with one long stroke of the pole, and the current took them.

The river in flood was not water. It was a moving country — brown and churning, ridged with standing waves, full of things unseen and half-glimpsed, whole trees traveling in its grip like spears. The noise closed over them within seconds, the roar of the gorge ahead swallowing even thought, and then the walls rose on both sides and the sky narrowed to a wet gray ribbon overhead and they were inside the mountain's throat.

The first rapid hit them like a door opening.

Gideon called it as she read it — "*Left! Hard left!*" — and the boat answered, Yusuf leaning the tiller, Dev and Bisi paddling with a desperation that looked almost like grace, and the hull slid along the seam of calm water hugging the left wall while the main current detonated past on their right, throwing spray six feet high. Rocks loomed and slid past close enough to touch. Water came aboard in cold slaps. Somewhere behind them, faint under the roar, a rifle cracked twice more — firing at a boat already gone beyond reach, the bullets wasted on stone and spray.

Then the second drop, bigger, and there was no seam.

"*Center!* Hold center!" Gideon's voice tore itself on the words. The wave train ahead stood up out of the brown like a row of hills walking upstream, and Yusuf drove the boat straight into the middle of it with the calm of a man stepping into a doorway he'd walked through a thousand times. The first wave broke over the bow and filled the world white. The second lifted the stern and dropped it. The third—

The third they hit sideways.

The hull spun. The world became rotation and noise and cold, and Gideon felt the boat flex under her like a living thing in pain, heard the patched planks groan along their seams, heard Bisi shouting something that might have been a prayer or a curse, and then the river spat them out the far end of the chute and they were through, wallowing, half-sunk, everyone hauling water with helmets and cupped hands and the cooking pot while Yusuf wrestled the tiller and laughed — actually *laughed*, a wild bark of a sound, the laugh of a man who has just been told he may live.

"Third hole on the left," he shouted over the din. "My father said it eats boats. It only chewed!"

Ahead, the gorge bent, and the light changed, and the roaring eased from a scream to a growl. They floated out of the narrows into a wider stretch where the walls fell back and the rain drifted down gentle again, as if nothing had happened, as if the mountain hadn't just rolled them in its palm and set them down unbroken.

No shots followed. Behind them, somewhere above, the smugglers' ridge was already out of sight. The river had done what rivers do: erased the road behind.

* * *

They bailed and limped two more kilometers in a stunned, wordless procession, and made camp at dusk on a gravel bar wedged between the walls — the only ground for miles, a fan of pale stones the river owned nine days out of ten. Yusuf swore the water wouldn't rise tonight; the melt came down from higher valleys and needed daylight to feed it. Everyone chose to believe him. Belief was the day's last ration.

They got a fire going under a scrap of tarp, small and shielded, more heat than light. Bisi distributed food with the economy of a quartermaster: half-rations, announced as full ones. Nobody argued. Tomas checked the surviving medical kit twice, then sat staring at the flames with his hands wrapped around his knees, and when Gideon lowered herself onto the stones beside him he spoke without looking at her.

"I owe you an apology."

"For the boat?"

"For what I said. About you repeating the old disaster." His voice was quiet, careful, a man picking his way across scree. "I've been waiting ten years for you to be arrogant. It's easier to follow someone you've already decided will fail. Then you don't have to trust them." He turned his head. In the firelight his eyes were wet — exhaustion, she told herself, though she suspected otherwise. "Today you weren't arrogant. You were just right. It's harder to forgive."

Gideon looked at the fire. Something in her chest had come loose during the rapids and hadn't settled back — not pain exactly, more like a joint finding its socket after years of disuse. "Then don't forgive me yet," she said. "Forgive me when we're down. There's still a cave above us and men with rifles below. Save your apologies for the far bank."

Bisi snorted from across the fire. "She's insufferable when she's right. Has been since '09."

"You love it," Gideon said.

"I tolerate it," Bisi said, "at altitude, under fire. Which is more than most marriages get."

And then Dev spoke, from the edge of the firelight where he'd been sitting apart, the way he'd sat apart since the night Bisi caught the satellite messenger blinking in his pack. "I'll take first watch."

"No," Gideon said, and saw him flinch. "You'll take watch with me. First shift. We'll sit together." She met his eyes across the flames and let the silence carry what it carried — not punishment, not absolution, just the plain fact of shared ground. "If they come down that trail tonight, I want four eyes on it. Yours count same as anyone's."

He came and sat beside her when the others bedded down, and for a long time neither spoke. The river ran silver-black past the gravel bar. Above the gorge walls, where the clouds had torn open for the first time in days, stars showed — hard, high, indifferent, the same stars that had hung over another storm ten years ago, over a white slope and a rope team of three and a brother who had stepped off the edge of the visible world.

"He was your brother," Dev said at last. Not a question. He'd pieced it together from fragments, the way everyone eventually did — Mara's manifest, Yusuf's questions, the compass case Gideon checked each night like a woman counting money she was afraid to spend. "The climber. On the pass."

"Yes."

"That's why you came. Not the money."

Gideon turned the compass case over in her hands, feeling the worn brass through the canvas. "I came because I owed him ten years of being looked for," she said. "The money was Mara's reason. Mine fit in a pocket."

Dev nodded slowly. Then he reached into his jacket and brought out the satellite messenger — the thing that had nearly ended them — and held it out over the fire.

"I should have thrown it in the river days ago," he said. "I told myself I kept it for safety. That was a lie I liked better than the truth." His hand trembled, but the object in it stayed steady, extended. "Take it. Or throw it. But it doesn't leave my pocket again. I'm done selling routes. I'm done being the reason smoke follows us down a valley."

Gideon looked at the device for a long moment. Taking it meant carrying his betrayal in her own pack. Throwing it meant destroying evidence that might someday matter. Instead she reached over and closed his hand back around it.

"You keep it," she said. "Dead weight teaches better than confiscated weight. And tomorrow, when we climb out of this gorge, you walk second rope behind me. Where I can see you." She paused. "That's not trust, Dev. That's arithmetic. Trust comes later, if you earn it on the moraine."

He nodded, and put it away, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch — the posture of a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

They sat together through the rest of the first watch, saying little. Around midnight the clouds closed again and the stars went out, and far upstream, faint through the layers of rain and stone, Gideon thought she heard it: a sound too regular for the river, too rhythmic for wind. Engines. More than one. Coming down the valley road on the far side of the gorge — the smugglers' route, paralleling theirs, keeping pace.

She woke Yusuf without a word. He listened for ten seconds and gave her a look that needed no translation.

"How fast can you patch a boat?" she asked.

"Fast," he said, already reaching for the pitch pot. "But not fast enough to outrun engines on a river this low." He glanced up at the walls of the gorge, black against black, where somewhere above them a trail clung to the rock — a trail he'd climbed since boyhood, a trail his son had climbed, a trail that led up and up toward the pass and the ice cave and everything waiting there. "We don't outrun them," he said. "We out-*climb* them. Tonight, before dawn, while the rain keeps their dogs stupid and their lamps dimmed. We leave the river to them."

Gideon looked up into the dark where the trail vanished, and felt the old pull in her chest — the pull she'd spent ten years drowning, the pull toward height, toward cold, toward the pass that had taken everything and was about to give some of it back.

"Then we climb," she said. "All night if we have to."

Above them, invisible in the rain, the glacier waited with its blue light and its secret. Below, the engines grew louder, mile by mile, patient as debt.
