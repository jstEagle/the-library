## 14. Above the Clouds

The river gave them one more day before it turned on them.

They had run the gorge through the night — Yusuf at the patched hull's stern with his hands reading the water like braille, Bisi bailing with a dented pot, Gideon calling the rocks ahead from memory of a route she'd never actually traveled but which the mountain seemed to have kept in her bones. By dawn they'd put eleven kilometers between themselves and the muzzle flashes on the far bank, and the smugglers' shots had stopped coming, and for two hours no one spoke because speaking would have meant admitting how close it had been.

Then the boat began to die.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Gideon noticed first, and hated: nothing on this mountain was ever dramatic when it mattered most. The split in the hull that Yusuf had sealed with pine pitch and a strip of inner tube simply opened again, quietly, an inch at a time, the way wounds reopen in people who refuse to rest. The water came in gray and patient. Bisi bailed. Tomas counted the bailings aloud like a metronome until the counting became its own kind of prayer, and then Dev took over the counting, and then even that dissolved into the sound of the river chewing at stone.

By midmorning the current changed character. It went from brown to white, from deep to shallow, braiding itself across a valley floor that rose in long granite steps. The boat scraped once, hard, and Yusuf said a word in Balti that Gideon didn't understand but whose meaning required no translation.

"Here," he said finally, in English, steering for a gravel bar the color of bone. "Here is where the river wins."

They grounded her on the bar and stood in the shallows looking up. Above them the valley walls closed into a V of black rock, and above the rock the clouds sat like a lid on a pot. Somewhere beyond that lid, three days' climb if the weather held, was the pass. Somewhere off to the northeast, half a day beyond the pass, was the serac, and beneath the serac, the ice cave, and beneath the ice—

Gideon didn't finish the thought. She hadn't finished it in ten years.

"We walk from here," she said.

* * *

They walked. The trail, if it could be called that, was a goat path stitched to the canyon wall, and by noon it had given up pretending and become merely a direction — cairns every few hundred meters, some ancient, some freshly stacked, all of them saying the same thing: someone has come this way wanting something badly enough to mark the way back out.

The cloud came down to meet them around two o'clock. Not rain at first — just a thickening, a graying of the air, the light going flat and sourceless so that the mountain lost its shadows and with them any sense of scale. Gideon had climbed in weather like this a thousand times. She knew what it did to people: it erased the horizon, and without a horizon the body forgot where it ended and the world began, and fear crept in through that gap.

She checked on each of them the way she used to check on clients, back when checking was her whole profession and her whole pride. Bisi was solid as always, moving with that economy that made rope work look like dancing. Dev climbed well, too well for a man who claimed he'd never been above four thousand meters, and Gideon noted that and filed it away beside everything else she'd filed away about Dev. Yusuf moved like the mountain was a house he'd grown up in, touching rock without hurrying, reading the wind by putting a wet finger to his lips.

Tomas was the problem.

He had been quiet since the river — quieter than accusation, quieter than anger. He carried the surviving half of the medical kit strapped to his chest like a child, and he climbed with his head down, and when Gideon fell into step beside him on a stretch of scree he answered her questions with single words.

"Headache?"

"Fine."

"How many liters today?"

"Enough."

"Tomas." She stopped him with a hand on his arm. His face was the color of old ash, and there was a tremor in his jaw she could see only because she was close enough, and she understood, with the cold certainty of long experience, exactly what she was looking at. "Take a pulse ox reading. Now."

He fumbled the clip onto his finger with hands that weren't quite his own. The little screen blinked. Eighty-one percent, and falling, at four thousand nine hundred meters, with the pass still six hundred vertical meters above them and the cave beyond that.

"Descent," Gideon said. "Now. Down to the gravel bar. I'll go on with—"

"No." His voice cracked like dry wood. "No. You are not doing it again."

The words landed in her sternum like a dropped anchor.

"You are not deciding who goes forward and who gets left," Tomas said, and now the anger was there, ten days of it, two decades of it maybe, coming up through the altitude sickness like groundwater through a fault. "I read about the expedition. Everyone read about it. Three dead and you walked down. So no. No descent. We stay together or we turn around together, and we both know you won't turn around."

Bisi had drifted close enough to hear. Dev too. Even Yusuf stood ten meters down the slope, watching, saying nothing, letting the mountain hold the silence.

Gideon looked at Tomas and saw the truth of him: a good medic, a careful man, terrified not of dying but of being *decided about*. She looked at her own hands and saw the other truth, the older one, the one she had carried down off that pass a decade ago wrapped in survivor's guilt like a second pack. She had chosen, on that expedition. She had chosen who carried loads and who turned back and who pushed for the summit window, and when the storm ate the mountain she had chosen again, over and over, hour after hour, and every choice had been right by every rule she knew, and three people had died anyway, and Emeka —

Emeka had gone into the white, and she had led the living down, because that was what leaders did, and she had spent ten years being punished by strangers for it, and worse, punished by herself.

"I'm not leaving you," she said quietly. "But I'm not carrying you up either. Here's what happens. We descend two hundred meters to the moraine camp. You get oxygen, rest, drugs. If your numbers recover by dawn, you come on slow with Bisi. If they don't—" she held his eyes, refusing to flinch, refusing to let this be the old story wearing new clothes, "—then Bisi takes you down and I go on, and that isn't me choosing the mission over you. That's arithmetic. Two people alive beats four people dead. Say you understand the arithmetic."

Tomas stared at her. The clouds pressed down. Somewhere above them thunder rolled along the ridge like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"I understand the arithmetic," he said at last. "I hate it. But I understand it."

"That's leadership," Bisi murmured. "Nobody likes it."

They descended.

* * *

The moraine camp sat in a bowl of shattered stone below a hanging glacier, and by the time the tents were up the monsoon had arrived in earnest — not the polite afternoon buildup of the low valleys but the real thing, a wall of water moving up the gorge behind them, swallowing the river they'd abandoned, swallowing the smugglers and their smoke and their rifles, swallowing everything below five thousand meters in a gray roar.

Inside the tent, Tomas breathed oxygen from the bottle they'd saved through the rapids, and his color came back by degrees, and he slept the drugged, gulping sleep of a man whose brain had been drowning in thin air and was remembering how.

Gideon sat outside under the fly with the others, watching rain hammer the glacier.

"The pass closes tomorrow," Yusuf said. "Maybe tonight. After the snow, the cornices come down. There will be no crossing for weeks. Maybe a month. Maybe the season."

"Then we cross tonight."

"In this?" Dev's voice was thin.

"In this," Gideon said, "because in this, nobody can follow us. The smugglers are pinned below the river. By morning the storm will be our wall, not theirs. And by morning, if we're through the col, we'll be above the weather entirely."

She looked up at the blackness where the pass should have been. She couldn't see it. She could feel it, though — the way you can feel a room you grew up in with the lights off.

"My brother walked into weather like this," she said. Nobody had asked. She offered it anyway, because the storm deserved honesty, and because she was tired of carrying the sentence unspoken. "Ten years ago. Right about here, give or take a kilometer. The team voted to wait. He didn't want to wait. He said the summit window was closing and waiting was just dying slower." Her voice stayed level. "I let him go. I sent him. I was the guide. It was my call, whatever the vote said, because calls were mine. And the white took him, and I brought everyone else home, and the world decided that meant I'd traded him for the rest."

Bisi reached over and gripped her wrist once, hard, and let go. It was an old gesture between them, older than the disaster.

"So tonight," Gideon said, "we go up, and we stay roped, and nobody walks anywhere alone, and if the white comes down we stop and dig in and live, because living is the whole job. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

They left at midnight, in snow that fell sideways.

Yusuf led. He claimed to know the col from his youth, from the years before the closure, when these routes belonged to traders and salt carriers and men like him who guided for grain instead of glory. Whether he truly remembered or was navigating by nerve and inheritance, Gideon couldn't tell, and after the second hour she stopped caring, because his feet never hesitated and hesitation kills in a whiteout faster than cold does.

They climbed on a single rope, four of them and Tomas — Tomas, whose numbers had recovered at two in the morning to eighty-eight percent, who had stood up, swayed, and said one word: "Together." Gideon had looked at him a long moment and then handed him the middle of the rope without comment, and something passed between them that neither of them named: not forgiveness, not yet, but a truce signed in oxygen molecules.

Above four and a half thousand meters the world simplified. There was the rope. There was the breath. There was the next step. Gideon had once loved mountaineering for its complexity — the logistics, the weather models, the chess of it. Now, climbing blind through the small hours with her dead brother's compass riding in her jacket pocket, wrapped in cloth, not yet read, not yet trusted, she loved it for this: the mercy of simplicity. One foot. Next foot. Breathe.

The storm broke apart just before dawn, torn open from above by winds that lived higher than weather. They came out the top of the clouds the way divers come up from deep water — suddenly, painfully, into a light so clean it hurt.

Below them: an ocean of cloud, white to every horizon, the monsoon buried alive beneath their boots. Above them: sky the blue of deep glacial ice, hardening toward black at the zenith, and stars still fading in it like sparks on wet iron. And ahead, catching the first sun, the pass itself — a notch in the ridge line, sharp as a wound, with the cornice above it glowing gold and terrible.

They stood on the crest and looked down the far side, and Gideon felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't pain, or wasn't only pain.

"There," she said.

Not the pass. Past the pass. Beyond the second ridge, visible now for the first time in ten years, a serac wall hung off a shoulder of ice like a frozen wave about to break. Beneath it, invisible at this distance, was a cave.

Yusuf had gone very still. He was staring at the same shoulder of ice, and Gideon realized, watching him, that his stillness was different from hers — that he was seeing something else in that ice, someone else, and that for ten years the two of them had been aimed at the same mountain from opposite directions like bullets fired at one target from either side of a wall.

"How far?" Dev asked.

Gideon judged it the way she used to judge it, before the blacklist, before the exile, with the old skill rising in her like blood returning to a numbed limb. "Half a day. Maybe less, if the snow bridges hold. We move now, while the sun's low and the cornices are still frozen."

"And Tomas?" Bisi asked softly.

Everyone looked at him. He was leaning on his axe, gray again, the ascent having taxed what the descent had spared, his pulse ox hovering at eighty-five and lying to no one.

"I can make the cave," Tomas said. "I can make it. What I can't promise is making it back fast." He met Gideon's eyes. "So here's my arithmetic. You and Yusuf go for the cave. Bisi ropes me down to the shelter of that moraine — I saw it on the approach, there's a hollow, wind-sheltered, defensible. We wait. Dev stays with us."

"No," Dev said quickly.

"Yes," Gideon said, and heard her own voice go flat and final, the voice from before, the leader's voice she'd sworn off for a decade. Because she had watched Dev photograph route notes. Because she had watched him count their campsites with his eyes. Because the storm had pinned the smugglers below the river but storms end, and men with satellite messengers do not. "Dev stays where I can see him. That's not punishment. That's the price of the last ten days. Take it or take it down the mountain alone."

Dev's face worked through several expressions — shame, anger, something that might have been relief — and settled at last on a bleak acceptance. "Together," he said, echoing Tomas, and the word sounded in his mouth like penance.

They divided the loads. Gideon took the rope, the spare oxygen, the compass. Yusuf took a shovel and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that he had carried since the stilt house and never once explained, and did not explain now.

They parted at the crest of the pass with the sun full up and the cloud ocean burning white below them, Bisi's hand on Gideon's shoulder, Tomas's nod, Dev already turning away.

Then Gideon and Yusuf were alone on the high ice, two small figures on a white immensity, roped together, walking toward the serac.

The mountain was silent in the way it is silent only above the clouds — a silence with pressure behind it, like the inside of a held breath. Their crampons squeaked. The rope swung gently between them, linking a woman running toward her grief and a man walking toward his.

Halfway across the snowfield, Yusuf spoke without stopping.

"When we reach the cave," he said, "you will want to go in alone. I know this. But you will wait for me, and we will go in together, because I have waited twenty-two years to bury what I left in that ice, and the mountain does not care whose grief arrives first. It only asks that we carry it honestly."

Gideon looked at him — this stranger the river had given her, this man with his unnamed price and his oilcloth bundle — and she found that she was not afraid of him, and not afraid for herself, and that the feeling rising in her as the serac grew against the sky was the feeling of a door she had nailed shut ten years ago beginning, at last, to open.

Fifty meters below the ice wall, in a shadow at its base barely wider than a doorway, something caught the sunlight and flashed — once, twice — deliberate, rhythmic. A signal.

Someone was already at the cave.
