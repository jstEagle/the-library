## 16. Emeka's Last Miles

The journal was wrapped in a plastic map case, the kind expedition outfitters sold in Kathmandu for a few hundred rupees, the kind every climber carried and half of them never used. It had been tucked beneath Emeka's crossed arms as if he were sleeping with it, and when Gideon lifted it free, the ice that had held it for ten years let it go with a sound like a sigh.

She did not open it in the cave.

She could not have said why, except that some rooms are too small for grief, and the cave was Emeka's room, had been his room for a decade, and she would not read her brother's last words kneeling over his body like a thief at a deathbed. She wrapped the journal against her chest beneath her down jacket, next to the compass, and Yusuf — who understood without being told — began the work of preparing the body for transport while she stood at the cave mouth and watched the clouds boil up from the valley floor like something rising from a drowned world.

They camped that night two hundred meters below the serac, on a shelf of scree flat enough for three tents. Tomas was stable, dosed and sleeping, his breathing a wet metronome Bisi checked twice an hour. Dev sat apart, hands empty for the first time since Gideon had known him, staring at nothing. The wind came down the glacier in long, patient gusts, and above them the ice groaned and settled, the mountain turning over in its sleep.

Gideon waited until the others were wrapped in their bags. Then she took out her headlamp, turned it to its lowest red glow, and unwrapped the map case with fingers that would not quite obey her.

The journal was a standard Rite-in-the-Rain notebook, green cover, spiral bound. The first pages were swollen and fused with ice-melt, illegible, a gray pulp of lost days. But the later entries — the ones that mattered — had been protected by the case, and Emeka's handwriting marched across them in his old careful print, the handwriting of a boy who'd done his homework at the kitchen table while their mother cooked, who'd labeled everything, who'd once made an annotated map of their Lagos neighborhood so Gideon wouldn't get lost coming home from school.

*Day One,* the entry began. *I don't know where I am. I don't know if anyone else is alive.*

She read the first line four times before she could read the second.

* * *

The storm had taken him on the third day of the whiteout, ten years ago. That was the official record — the one Mara Voss had spent a decade trying to overturn, the one that had buried Gideon's career alongside her brother. He had been roped to her. The cornice had given way beneath them both. She had held him — she had *held him*, she had felt the rope saw against the lip of ice, had felt the moment his weight changed, the terrible arithmetic of a falling body — and then the rope had parted, or been cut, and he had gone into the white, and she had never stopped hearing the sound he didn't make.

That was what the inquiry never understood. Not that she'd failed. That he hadn't screamed. That the silence had followed her down the mountain and into every year after, filling rooms, filling marriages, filling the long gray stretches between trekking contracts when she lay awake measuring herself against the distance between where he fell and where she stood.

She read on.

*Day Two. Found shelter in what I think is a crevasse bridge — there's a hollow under the ice, wind can't reach me. My left hand is bad. Frostbite, I think, or worse. I've got the stove but no fuel. I ate the last food yesterday. Writing this mostly to stay awake.*

*I keep thinking about the rope. Not about how it broke. About how you held it, Gidi. I was upside down and spinning and I couldn't see anything, but I could feel you holding. You didn't let go. Whatever they say happened up here, I know what I felt, and you didn't let go.*

The red light trembled across the page. Gideon realized she was making a sound, low and continuous, somewhere between breathing and weeping, and she pressed her sleeve against her mouth and kept reading because stopping now would kill her.

*Day Three. Heard aircraft this morning. Too far east. I tried to signal with the mirror but my arm won't lift right. Funny thing: I'm not scared anymore. Cold past scared. I keep thinking about the compass Dad gave us, the one he said pointed home no matter where you stood. I've been trying to remember which direction home is. West? Everything's west from here.*

*If someone finds this — I want my sister to have it. Not because it's worth anything. Because she'll understand what it means. We used to fight about who got to hold it. She always won. Let her win this one too.*

Gideon put the journal down.

She looked up through the tent's open flap at the mountain, black against a sky salted with stars, and for a long moment she could not tell whether the sound in her ears was wind or her own blood. *Let her win this one too.* Ten years. Ten years he had lain in the ice holding those words the way she had held the rope — not letting go — and she had spent all of them believing she was the one who needed forgiveness.

* * *

"Okonkwo."

Yusuf's voice, low, from outside. She wiped her face with a roughness that bordered on anger and unzipped the flap. He crouched at the edge of her vestibule, silhouetted against the snow-glow, steam rising off the tea he offered without ceremony. She took it. Her hands shook; the tea trembled in rings.

"You read it," he said. Not a question.

"He lived four days." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Four days in a crevasse with no fuel, no food, a ruined hand. And he spent them writing to me."

Yusuf said nothing. He was good at that — the silence of a man who had learned that most words arrive too early or too late, and patience sorts them.

"He forgave me, Yusuf. Before I even knew there was anything to forgive. He—" The sentence collapsed. She drank the tea, scalding, tasting nothing. "He wrote that he felt me holding the rope. That I didn't let go. The inquiry said the anchor failed. They said I cut him loose to save myself. Mara's own testimony—"

"I know what they said." His eyes went toward the dark slope below, where his son lay under ten years of his own silence. "The mountain tells everyone a different story than the people waiting at the bottom. That is its cruelest habit." He rose, knees cracking. "Read the rest. All of it. Tonight, while it's fresh. The dead speak clearest once. After that, memory edits them."

He withdrew into the dark, and she zipped the flap, and she read the rest.

* * *

*Day Four. Morning, I think. Hard to tell. I want to write down the things that matter in case the cold takes my head before it takes the rest of me.*

*Gidi. If you're reading this, then you came back. Of course you came back. You never leave anything on a mountain — that was always your problem and your gift. Mom used to say you'd carry the whole house up K2 if someone dared you.*

*I need you to hear this, so I'm going to write it plain:*

*It wasn't your fault.*

*Not the storm. Storms happen. Not the route — your route was good, better than good, and I told Voss that and she didn't listen, she wanted the summit photo more than she wanted sense. Not the rope. I checked the anchors myself that morning. Ice does what ice does.*

*And not the choice you made after. Bisi told me — well, she'll tell you, or maybe she already has — that you got six people down that mountain in conditions that should have killed all seven of you. Six people are alive because you kept walking when every part of you wanted to lie down in the snow next to where I fell. I know you, Gidi. I know that's exactly what you wanted. Don't do it. Don't lie down. Walk.*

*The compass is in my chest pocket. It was in my chest pocket the whole time. I took it without asking because I wanted a piece of home up here, and I'm sorry for that, and also I'm not, because now it's the piece of home that gets to finish the trip.*

*Take it. Point it west. Go home. Live long enough to be old and bossy and impossible, the way you were always going to be.*

*You were the best guide on this mountain. You still are. Whoever says otherwise doesn't know what you carried down.*

*Your little brother,*
*Emeka*

*P.S. Tell Mom the compass points home. She'll know what I mean.*

Their mother had been dead six years. Gideon held the journal against her sternum, both arms wrapped around it like a child with a stolen thing, and the sobs that came out of her were not graceful — they were ugly, gulping, animal, the grief of a decade leaving the body all at once through a door that had finally, finally opened. She cried until her ribs ached and the cold air burned her throat, and the mountain stood over her and did not judge, because mountains never do, and the stars wheeled slowly overhead the way they had wheeled over him on his fourth night, alone, forgiving her in careful print by the light of a dying headlamp.

When it was done, she felt lighter and emptier at once, the way a house feels after the furniture is gone. Memory as burden. Memory as gift. Both true. Both his.

* * *

Bisi found her at dawn.

Gideon heard her boots on the scree and didn't turn, didn't hide the journal or the tear-frozen tracks on her face. Some things you stop hiding when a man dies twice for you.

"His handwriting," Bisi said quietly, sitting beside her on the frozen rock. She was looking at the open page without reaching for it. "Still drew his *a*'s like little houses."

"You knew he wrote this."

"I helped him pack that notebook." Bisi's voice caught, steadied. "I didn't know what was in it. But when Mara showed me the recovery coordinates, I knew whose pocket that case came out of." She paused. "There's more, isn't there. Something he asked you to do."

"He asked me to walk." Gideon closed the journal. "Ten years ago he asked me to keep walking, and I stopped the day I got off the mountain. So." She stood, joints protesting, and looked down-valley — down the route they'd bled for, the river, the gorge, the smoke that might still be rising from a ridge behind them. "We break camp in twenty minutes. Tomas rides the sled. Dev carries the front of the litter — he owes weight, and carrying is how a man pays that back honestly. Bisi, you're on rope team lead. I'm navigating."

Bisi blinked. "You're giving away your own job."

"I'm finally doing it properly." Gideon tucked the journal inside her jacket, beside the compass, brass against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "Emeka carried this thing into the ice so it could come home. We're going to make sure everything else he wrote comes true. Including the part about living long enough to be impossible."

A sound from the tents — Tomas coughing, Dev moving, the small machinery of a camp waking. And then another sound, thinner, carried on the morning wind from the south: a whistle. Two notes, repeated. A human signal, deliberate, from the moraine below.

Bisi stiffened. "Smugglers?"

Gideon raised her binoculars. On the grey spine of rock a kilometer downslope, a figure stood in the open — no weapon visible, hands raised, waving something bright orange. A jacket. Waving it in the universal grammar of the mountains: *I am here. I am alone. Come.*

Or: *come, so I don't have to chase you.*

"They split up," Gideon murmured, lowering the glasses. "Or one of them wants to talk." She looked at Bisi, at the journal against her heart, at the pass that stood open for perhaps another thirty hours before the weather sealed it until spring. "Whatever he wants, we're not going down to him. We go up. Break camp."

Below them, the figure in orange waved again — and then, unmistakable even at a kilometer, began to climb.
