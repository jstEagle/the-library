## 17. The Smugglers' Ambush

The moraine came down out of the glacier's shoulder like a spine of broken vertebrae — a mile of grey rubble where the ice had coughed up everything the mountain had ever swallowed: boulders the size of houses, slabs split by frost into blades, and between them a maze of gullies that could hide a man or lose him forever.

Gideon stopped at its edge and let the others catch up. Below them, the valley they'd climbed out of two days ago lay drowned in cloud, and somewhere inside that cloud were men with rifles who had been patient for a very long time.

"They'll be behind us," Yusuf said quietly. He said most things quietly now, as if the mountain had taught him volume was a form of waste. "They lost us in the gorge, but not for good. A tracker reads scree like a ledger."

"Then we read it first." Gideon scanned the slope. The descent route she'd memorized on the way up cut across the moraine's flank in long switchbacks, exposed to anything above and anything below. There was no way around it. There never was. That was the cruelty of mountains — every safe place cost you an unsafe one to reach it.

Bisi crouched beside her, working her fingers through her rope coils the way she always did when she was thinking, each loop a small prayer. She'd been Gideon's anchor for twenty years, through storms and lawsuits and the long silent years after Emeka vanished. Her hair had gone iron-grey at the temples since Kathmandu. Everything else about her was exactly the same.

"I don't like the open ground," Bisi said.

"Neither do I."

"But we're crossing it anyway."

"We're crossing it anyway."

Tomas knelt over his salvaged medical kit — half of what he'd started with, the rest feeding the river in chapter eight of their disaster. He'd stopped arguing with Gideon days ago, but he hadn't stopped keeping score; she could see the tally in the set of his mouth. He looked up at her now, and whatever he meant to say, he folded away and simply nodded. Some accusations keep better than others. He was saving his.

Dev stood apart, as he always did, at the edge of the group like a man waiting to be told which direction shame pointed. Since the night of the signal fire — since Bisi had caught the blue pulse of the satellite messenger against his chest and he'd confessed everything, the money, the photographs, the name of the man in Pokhara who'd paid him — Dev had carried himself differently. Lighter and heavier at once. He'd given up his secrets but hadn't yet been forgiven, and he moved among them like a guest at a funeral for someone he'd killed.

Gideon caught his eye. "You know these valleys best. Where would you set an ambush?"

He didn't hesitate, which was itself an answer. "There." He pointed to a cluster of boulders where the trail narrowed between two fallen slabs, maybe four hundred meters down the switchbacks. "Cover on three sides. Sightline to the whole traverse. If I were selling a route, that's where I'd collect."

"You're not selling it anymore."

"No," Dev said. "I'm buying it back."

* * *

They crossed the moraine the way water crosses stone — finding the low lines, moving in bursts, freezing at each pause. The wind came off the glacier smelling of iron and old snow. Gideon counted her people the way she counted steps: Bisi ahead on point, Tomas behind her with the pack holding what remained of their food, Yusuf at the rear with the compass wrapped in cloth against his chest — no, that was wrong. The compass rode against *her* chest. It had since the cave. She felt its small brass weight with every heartbeat, Emeka's last gift, engraved with the name only he had ever called her, and she drew from it a steadiness that had nothing to do with navigation.

They were three hundred meters along the traverse when the first shot cracked the air.

It went high — a warning, or a miss, and there was no way to know which until the second shot, which was neither. It struck the slab above Bisi's head and burst a fistful of rock into shrapnel that caught her across the shoulder and cheek as she dove for cover.

"Bisi!" Gideon was moving before the echo died, low and fast, sliding into the shadow of a boulder beside her. Blood ran bright down Bisi's jaw, shocking against the grey stone. Her left arm hung wrong at the elbow, not broken — Gideon saw that in a heartbeat — but torn, a deep gouge from triceps to elbow where the rock shrapnel had opened her like a letter.

"I'm fine," Bisi said, which was how she said *I am not fine*.

"Tomas! Down here, now!"

The shooting continued, spaced and deliberate, pinning them. Gideon pressed herself flat and looked downslope through the gap beneath the boulder. Two figures moved among the rocks below — dark jackets, rifles, unhurried. They knew the crew couldn't run across open scree under fire. They were herding them, letting the altitude and the blood loss do the work bullets might miss.

Tomas arrived crawling, dragged his kit open one-handed. His face had gone the color of the moraine. "Artery's intact," he said, fingers probing the wound with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "But she's losing blood and I've got sutures and maybe enough gauze. We cannot stay here, Gideon. We cannot run either. Pick one."

Above them, Yusuf's voice came thin across the wind. "More of them on the ridge. Four. Maybe five."

*Five*, Gideon thought. *Four of us, one wounded.* The arithmetic of disasters. She'd done this math ten years ago on another mountain and gotten it wrong, and three families had learned her name from a news report. She closed her eyes for half a second — no longer, there was no time for longer — and asked herself the question she'd spent a decade refusing to ask: *what does the mountain want me to see?*

It wanted her to see the gully. Fifty meters east, a drainage channel cut down through the rubble, steep and choked with loose stone — suicide going down fast, but it bent north behind a wall of slabs, out of the shooters' sightline, and rejoined the trail below the ambush point. If they could get Bisi into it. If they could hold the shooters' attention while they did.

If somebody stayed behind to hold it.

"I need thirty seconds of noise," she said.

Yusuf understood first. He was already uncoiling the length of climbing rope they'd kept after abandoning the boat, tying stones into a sling at intervals — a poor man's thunder. "I can give you noise," he said. "I cannot give you thirty seconds of me surviving it."

"You won't be exposed. You throw from cover, move, throw again. Make them think we're coming *down* at them."

"And while they watch the wrong direction—"

"We go east. All of us."

"Not all of us." It was Dev who said it.

Everyone turned. Dev had gone very still, the way he did when something true was being born in him. He looked at Gideon, and there was no calculation in his face anymore, none of the careful blankness he'd worn from Kathmandu to the river. What was in his face instead was naked and young and terrified and certain.

"They know my voice on the radio," he said. "They know my call sign. They've been following *my* reports for two weeks." He swallowed. "Let me draw them. I can move on this ground — you've seen me. I can make them chase me east, away from the gully, and buy you the whole descent."

"And then what?" Gideon heard her own voice crack on it. "Then they catch you."

"Then they catch a man who owes more than he can pay." Dev's jaw tightened. "You took me knowing what I was. You didn't leave me on the ice when you found out. I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to be."

"It's not—" Bisi started, pressing her good hand against her wound.

"It is." Dev pulled the satellite messenger from his jacket and held it up. "And I'm taking this. They'll track me moving east and believe every step, because it's *me*. Because I'm still, as far as they know, theirs." He smiled then, a terrible crooked smile. "Let them think so one last time."

Gideon grabbed his wrist. "Dev—"

"Gideon." He said her name softly, the way you say a thing you've decided to remember. "My brother died in a whiteout on Annapurna because his team ran and no one stayed. I've thought about that every day of my life. Today I get to be the one who stays." He pried her fingers free, gently. "Get Bisi down the mountain. Get everyone down. That's your job. This is mine."

There was no time to argue and no argument that would have held. That was the worst of it — he was right, and everyone knew he was right, and the knowledge sat among them like a stone none of them could lift.

"North ridge, past the serac, there's a shepherd's hut," Yusuf said suddenly, urgently. "Abandoned. Three hours east if you're fast. Wait one night, no more. If he's alive, he'll find you. If he's alive." He gripped Dev's shoulder. "My son waited for me once. I was too late. Don't make her too late for you."

Dev nodded once. Then he turned and went — low, fluid, impossibly quick across the scree, heading east with the messenger pulsing blue against his chest, shouting in Nepali as he ran, giving the smugglers a voice to follow, a story to believe, a target made entirely of his own courage.

The shooting shifted. Followed him. Grew fainter.

"Move," Gideon said, and her voice did not shake, though everything in her did.

* * *

They got Bisi into the gully and down it in a controlled fall of dust and prayer, Gideon above her, Tomas below, Yusuf flanking with the rifle he'd taken from the smugglers' cache at the river — he'd never explained fully where he'd learned to use it, and no one had asked. The loose stone sang around them. Twice Bisi blacked out from pain and twice Gideon talked her back, using the old rope-team cadence, *breathe, weight, breathe*, words that had lived in her hands for twenty years and had never once mattered this much.

At the bottom of the gully they bound the wound properly — Tomas stitching by headlamp in the lee of a boulder, cursing steadily in Spanish, Bisi gripping Gideon's hand hard enough to grind bone. When it was done, Bisi looked up at her with blood-crusted eyes and said, "Don't you dare slow down for me."

"I wasn't going to."

"Liar. You were going to. I watched you do the math." She managed a grin through the pain. "Emeka would've done the same stupid math. It's genetic. Now stop it. We run the pass tomorrow or we die here, and I refuse to die within walking distance of a hut with a roof."

So they ran. Or what passed for running at five thousand meters with a wounded woman between them — a grinding, gasping, relentless walk-jog that ate the miles as the light failed. Gideon led from the front for the first time since the cave, and found that leading no longer felt like the thing that had ruined her. It felt like the thing she was for.

They reached the shepherd's hut as full dark fell, a dry-stone shelter with a sagging roof and a smell of ancient dung fires. Inside, out of the wind, they did what survivors do: dressed wounds, melted snow, divided the last of the food into four portions and pretended not to notice there should have been five.

Bisi slept under sedation. Tomas sat sharpening his knife with unnecessary violence. Yusuf stood at the doorway watching the eastern darkness, and Gideon stood beside him.

"He'll come," Yusuf said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But I know what it costs a man to turn back toward danger for strangers. It is the rarest currency in the world." He glanced at her. "You spent some yourself, in the cave. I watched you kneel by your brother and I understood why the mountain has been collecting debts from you for ten years. It wasn't punishing you, Gideon. It was waiting for you to come back."

She touched the compass through her jacket. Somewhere east, in the black, a man who had sold them was spending everything to buy them back, and all she could do was wait, and keep watch, and count the hours the way she used to count miles.

Near midnight, a shape detached itself from the darkness below the hut — stumbling, limping, one arm cradled against his ribs, but upright, unmistakably, impossibly upright.

Dev collapsed into the doorway, bleeding, breathless, grinning.

"They bought it," he gasped. "Every step. And I found something on the ridge you need to see." He fumbled a folded paper from inside his jacket — a map, hand-drawn, annotated in a smuggler's careful script. "Their fallback position. Their route over the pass. And Gideon—" he looked up, and the grin faded into something dead serious, "—they're not chasing us anymore. They're already *ahead* of us. Waiting at the summit cairn."
