## 18. Dry Season Running Out

The storm came down the valley behind them like a debt collector.

Gideon felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her ears, a metallic taste in the wind, the way the light went thin and bruised over the western ridgeline. She had spent twenty years learning to read mountains the way sailors read water, and every instinct she owned was screaming now: *the pass will close in thirty-six hours, maybe less.*

She stood at the edge of the moraine camp and did the arithmetic she would once have done in her head, fast and arrogant, the way her father had taught her on the slopes of the Rwenzoris when she was nine years old. Distance to the col: eleven kilometers of broken glacier, then six hundred meters of ascent through the notch they called the Needle's Eye. Food remaining: four days' rations for seven people, if Tomas counted the crushed energy bars and the tin of ghee Yusuf had produced from some bottomless pocket of his pack. Bisi's wound: a graze along the ribs from a smuggler's round that had opened skin but missed bone — lucky by millimeters, though you wouldn't know it from the gray around her mouth or the fever climbing in her by afternoon.

Eleven kilometers. Thirty-six hours. One wounded woman who could walk but shouldn't, one man with altitude sickness still ghosting his thoughts, one boy carrying the weight of his own betrayal like stones sewn into his jacket lining.

The old Gideon would have done this sum alone. The old Gideon would have announced the answer at dawn, set a pace that broke whoever couldn't hold it, and called it leadership because it got bodies to summits.

She looked at her crew instead.

Bisi sat against a boulder, arm wrapped around her ribs, cleaning her rope coil with the methodical tenderness of a woman touching something she loved. Tomas was repacking the medical kit — what remained of it, half lost to the river weeks ago, the rest sacrificed to Bisi's bandages — muttering inventory numbers under his breath like a rosary. Dev crouched apart from them, as he had since the ambush, close enough to help, far enough to be dismissed. Yusuf stood at the moraine's edge facing the storm, motionless, reading it the way Gideon read it, and when he turned his head their eyes met across the camp and he gave a single nod: *you see it too.*

"Everyone," Gideon said. "Sit. All of you. We need to talk before we move."

Tomas looked up sharply. In ten days of travel she had never once said *we need to talk.* Decisions had come from her like weather — necessary, impersonal, arriving whether you wanted them or not.

They gathered. Even Dev, hesitating at the circle's edge until Bisi patted the stone beside her, an invitation that cost her something visible.

"We have maybe a day and a half before the weather seals the Needle's Eye," Gideon said. "After that, we're wintering on this side of the pass, and we don't have the food, the fuel, or the shelter to survive it. So we go now, we go fast, and we go smart." She paused. "And I need to tell you how we're going to do it, because I'm not going to decide it alone."

Tomas blinked. Bisi's eyebrows rose above eyes still bright with fever.

"Bisi can't carry a full load. Her body is spending everything it has fighting infection, and if I push her at expedition pace she'll collapse on the glacier, and then I'll be choosing between her and the pass." She said it plainly, without apology, because Bisi deserved plainness more than kindness. "So here's what I'm thinking. We split the loads three ways instead of two. Dev takes the heaviest — he's the strongest climber among us right now and he knows it. Yusuf takes navigation on the lower glacier; he's walked this ice longer than I've been alive. Tomas sets the pace."

"Me?" Tomas's voice cracked upward. "I'm the medic, not—"

"You're the one whose judgment I trust most about what human bodies can do," Gideon said. "You've been watching all of us for ten days. You'll know before any of us do when someone's hit their wall. You call the rests. You call the stops. If you say we halt, we halt — even if it costs us the light."

Tomas stared at her. Something moved behind his face — suspicion first, the old accusation from the river still lodged there like shrapnel, *you're repeating it, you're doing it again, your arrogance killed three people* — and then, slowly, something else. Recognition, maybe. That she was handing him the one thing she had never handed anyone: the power to stop her.

"And Bisi?" Dev asked quietly.

"Bisi walks behind me," Gideon said. "Where I can see her face. And if she tells me she's done, we make camp, full stop, and I live with whatever the weather does to us." She met her oldest friend's eyes. "That's not negotiable. Not even by you."

Bisi laughed — a short, cracked sound that turned into a wince. "Ten years ago you'd have carried me over the pass on your back rather than stop."

"Ten years ago I'd have killed us both trying." Gideon shouldered her pack. "We move in ten minutes."

* * *

The lower glacier was a ruin of the summer's violence. Meltwater had undercut the medial moraine until whole hillsides of rock sat balanced on ice pillars no thicker than a man's waist, and the surface itself was a maze of melt channels, blue and treacherous, some of them wide enough to swallow a person whole. They moved in the rhythm Yusuf called out — *step where I step, breathe when I breathe* — an old man's chant that kept five exhausted bodies synchronized like one animal with five hearts.

Gideon walked point behind him and made herself do the hardest thing she had ever done on a mountain, harder than any pitch of ice, harder than the descent after the storm ten years ago.

She made herself not lead.

Not entirely. Not the way she used to, with the route locked behind her eyes and everyone else following the shape of her certainty. Instead she led the way a compass leads — pointing true, holding steady, while other hands did the turning. When the route forked at a serac field, she sent Dev up the left branch to scout and waited, chewing a strip of dried meat, while he picked his line. He came back down with his face lit in a way she hadn't seen since before the signal fire, before the confession, before everything — the face of a climber who had been trusted with something that mattered.

"Left side's cleaner," he reported. "Right side's got a crevasse bridge that won't hold a cat."

"Then we go left," Gideon said, and meant: *because you read it right,* and watched him understand that she meant it.

Behind her, Bisi climbed with her arm pressed to her ribs and her jaw set in the old stubborn line Gideon knew better than her own heartbeat. Twice Gideon slowed without appearing to slow, drifting back in the column until she was beside her friend, matching her stride, saying nothing, because Bisi would rather die than be pitied and Gideon had finally learned the difference between pity and company.

"How bad?" she asked once, low.

"Bad enough that I'm glad you're not asking me to be brave about it," Bisi said. "Good enough that I'll curse your name properly if you leave me behind."

"Noted."

At midday Tomas called the first rest, unprompted, and Gideon felt the strange vertigo of obedience — sitting down because someone else had judged the moment, trusting a judgment she hadn't made. They ate cold rations in the shadow of an ice block the size of a cathedral. Above them, the clouds were assembling themselves into the thing mountaineers called the plume, the white banner streaming off the summit that meant the jet stream had dropped and the mountain had closed its hand.

Yusuf studied it and spoke for the first time in hours. "By tomorrow noon, the eye of the needle fills with cloud. After that, a week minimum. Maybe the season."

"Then we sleep high tonight," Gideon said. "Camp at the base of the headwall. Summit push at first light."

Tomas frowned. "Sleeping high with a fevered patient and a man who vomited twice yesterday—"

"I know." Gideon held his gaze. "Your call, Tomas. Can they do it?"

He looked at Bisi. He looked at Dev. He took Bisi's pulse with two fingers, checked the color of her lips, made her stand and walk ten steps and watched her feet. Then he sat back on his heels and rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them his expression had changed — the skepticism burned away, leaving only the raw honesty underneath.

"She can make the pass if nothing goes wrong," he said. "Dev's fine. I'm fine. And you—" he pointed at Gideon, and there was almost a smile in it, "—are the healthiest person on this mountain, which frightens me more than the weather."

"Take that as a compliment," Yusuf said gravely, and for the first time in ten days, someone laughed.

* * *

They made camp at five thousand meters as the light died, in a hollow the wind had carved between two ice blocks. Two tents. Four people in one, three in the other, bodies packed together for warmth because fuel was too precious to melt snow for hot water bottles. Gideon gave her sleeping bag to Bisi and took the bivi sack, and when Bisi protested she said, "You're carrying an infection. I'm carrying a grudge against the cold. We each fight our own war," and zipped the tent flap on her friend's objection.

She lay awake in the blue dark listening to the mountain breathe.

It sounded different than it had ten years ago. Or perhaps she heard it differently now. There had been a time when every groan of settling ice had sounded to her like accusation, like the voices of the dead asking why she had lived. Tonight the glacier groaned and settled and breathed, and it sounded like what it was: an enormous living thing indifferent to her, older than grief, honest in a way people rarely managed. It did not hate her. It did not forgive her. It simply was, and she was inside it, small and warm-blooded and determined, and somehow that indifference had become a kind of mercy.

In the other tent she could hear Yusuf's voice, low, speaking Urdu or Balti or some third language she couldn't place, and Dev answering in the same tongue. Prayers, maybe. Or stories. A man teaching a boy the names of things. She found she didn't need to know which. Some inheritances passed sideways, between strangers, in the dark, and needed no bloodline at all.

Sleep took her like a tide.

* * *

They rose at three. By headlamp, in air so cold the metal of their buckles burned, they broke camp and stood at the base of the headwall where the Needle's Eye cut its notch through the ridge — a slot of sky barely two body-widths wide, glowing faintly with starlight, six hundred meters above.

Gideon looked up at it and felt the old pull, the hunger that had made her great and nearly destroyed her: *go fast, go hard, be the one who gets them there.* She let the feeling rise, named it, and set it down like a heavy pack she no longer chose to carry.

"Tomas sets the pace," she reminded them. "Rope teams of two. Bisi with me. Dev with Yusuf. Tomas floats."

The climb was slow and it was perfect. Tomas called rests at intervals that seemed maddening and were exactly right; Bisi climbed with a ferocity that had nothing to do with strength and everything to do with will, her breath sawing, her boots finding holds by faith; Dev moved above them like water finding its level, placing protection for the whole team without being asked, and when a rockfall rattled down the couloir at dawn he was the one who shouted the warning that put them all under the overhang with seconds to spare.

Gideon climbed last, watching all of it, and understood — with a clarity that arrived not as revelation but as simple fact, the way you notice you've stopped limping — that this was what her father had actually been trying to teach her on the Rwenzori slopes all those years ago. Not speed. Not certainty. Not the compass in his hand, brass and true north and inheritance.

Judgment. The patience to wait for the right line. The humility to let others find it. The courage to spend yourself last.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge as they reached the notch, and light poured through the Needle's Eye like something poured through a keyhole — gold, blinding, total. Beyond it the range fell away southward, peak after peak dissolving into a sea of cloud, and beyond the clouds, invisible but utterly certain, Nepal. Home. Testimony. The end of running.

Bisi pulled herself over the lip and stood swaying in the wind, and Gideon caught her elbow and held it, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the light wasn't already saying.

They crossed the pass at 7:14 in the morning, all six of them, alive.

And it was Dev, standing at the far edge of the col, looking down the southern descent, who went rigid and grabbed Gideon's arm.

"Gideon," he said. "Look. On the trail below."

Far down the switchbacks, tiny with distance but unmistakable, figures were climbing toward them. Uniformed. Organized. Moving with the deliberate pace of people who had come looking for someone specific.

Gideon's hand went to the compass in her pocket — Emeka's compass, her name in his handwriting inside the lid — and closed around it like a hand closing around another hand.

"They're not smugglers," Dev said slowly, squinting. "Those are... those are police. Nepali police. Someone told them we were coming."

Gideon stared down at the approaching figures, and the first name that rose in her mind was Mara Voss — and with it, unbidden, a question she could not yet answer:

*Who else knew we'd come out this way?*
