## 19. Burial at the Pass

The wind at the top of the world was not a sound so much as a condition of being. It came off the ice fields to the north in long, patient gusts, the kind that did not howl but simply pressed, as if the mountain were leaning its whole gray weight against them and asking, quietly, whether they intended to stay.

Gideon stood at the cairn with her hood cinched tight, her breath rasping through the scarf across her mouth, and looked down at what the last nineteen days had made of them all. Bisi's arm hung in a sling Tomas had fashioned from a torn sleeping bag liner and two trekking poles sawn short. Dev's face was scoured raw by windburn, his eyes hollowed out by whatever he'd done on the moraine — by the shots he'd fired into the air, by the smugglers he'd led away into the white on foot, alone, and by the three days it had taken him to circle back around to them, frostbitten and half-starved, walking into their camp at dawn like a man returning from the dead. Yusuf stood closest to the stones, motionless, his weathered hands cupped around something small.

They had buried Bisi's blood loss and Tomas's pride and Dev's old self somewhere below the ice line. What remained at the summit was thinner than that. Cleaner. Four people and one dead man's pages and one father's grief, all of them carried this far on legs that had no business still working.

"The weather window," Tomas said behind her, not unkindly. He had stopped arguing with her somewhere above six thousand meters; now he only stated facts, like a man reading instruments. "We have maybe four hours before that cloud deck climbs the valley. Maybe less."

"Then we won't waste them standing here," Gideon said, though she made no move to leave. She turned to Yusuf. "It's yours first."

The old guide nodded once. He had not spoken much since the moraine, since the ambush, since the moment Bisi went down with a bullet torn through the meat of her shoulder and Gideon had knelt in the scree pressing cloth against the wound while Yusuf covered them with a borrowed rifle and a calm that seemed older than fear itself. He spoke the way the mountain spoke now: rarely, and only what needed saying.

He walked to the cairn.

It was an old structure, older than any of them — a shoulder-height stack of flat stones mortared with generations of prayer flags gone gray and threadless, their colors bleached to ghosts. Pilgrims had built it, perhaps, or shepherds, or some expedition whose name had dissolved into the same wind that now tested every joint of Gideon's body. The stones were furred with lichen the color of old brass. At its base, someone had wedged a yak skull, its horns curved like the handles of two doors opening onto nothing.

Yusuf knelt. The motion cost him — Gideon saw the hitch in his back, the way his breath caught and held — but he knelt anyway, the way men do when the ground beneath them is holy whether or not anyone has ever consecrated it.

What he held in his hands was small. From where she stood, ten feet away with the wind trying to push her over, Gideon could see only that it was wooden, dark, and worn smooth in places where a child's fingers might have worried it for years. A toy. A carving. Something a boy had loved until the day the mountain took the loving hands away.

She knew the shape of that story. She had been carrying its twin against her own chest for eleven days.

Yusuf did not pray aloud, or if he did, the wind ate the words before they crossed the distance between them. He worked instead with his hands, levering loose one of the foundation stones at the cairn's northern face, the side that faced the pass they had bled to reach. Beneath it there was a hollow, dry and dark, lined with fine glacial dust. He set the little wooden thing inside it with a tenderness that made Gideon's throat close. Then he paused, his hand still in the hollow, and stayed that way for a long time — long enough that Tomas shifted his weight, long enough that Bisi, sitting propped against a boulder with her good arm wrapped around her knees, bowed her head.

When Yusuf finally withdrew his hand, he spoke. His voice came out roughened, barely louder than the wind, and he spoke in English, carefully, as if each word had been packed for a long journey.

"His name was Ilyas." The stone hovered above the hollow. "He was nine years old. He wanted to see the high country, and I told him — I told him the mountain was no place for children." A dry, terrible sound that might have been a laugh moved through him once and was gone. "So I left him home. And the flood took the bridge, and the river took the bus, and the mountain took everything else." He set the stone back into place with both palms, seating it exactly as it had sat for years, so that no one passing would ever know the cairn held anything at all. "I kept this because I could not keep him. Now the mountain keeps us both."

He stood. He did not look at them. He faced north, toward the pass, toward the wall of cloud building along the far ridgeline like a slow blue tide, and Gideon understood that he was not looking at weather. He was looking at a direction. At the place where a boy had gone, and where a father had finally followed, and where nothing more remained to carry.

"Thank you for bringing me," he said, and turned, and his eyes were clear in a way Gideon had never seen them, not on the river, not in the stilt house with the rain hammering the walls, not even in the cave below the serac when he had stood aside and let her go in first. Clear, and empty of debt. "Now it is finished."

* * *

But it was not finished, because it was her turn.

Gideon had known this moment was coming since the ice cave — had felt it waiting inside her pack like a second heartbeat, heavier than rope, heavier than fuel, heavier than the rifle they'd abandoned at the gorge. Emeka's journal. Forty-odd pages written in pencil on waterproof paper, the letters growing looser and fainter as the days of his survival ran out, the last entries nearly illegible, written by fingers that could no longer feel the pencil. She had read them aloud in the cave by headlamp while Yusuf stood watch at the mouth and the glacier ticked and settled around them like a house at night. She had read them again on the climb, alone in her tent, tracing the words her brother had chosen for her with a fingertip, as though touch could reach backward through ten years of ice.

*I'm not angry,* he had written on the third day, when the storm had pinned him in the cave and the radio was dead and the compass was the only thing besides himself still working. *I need you to know that. Whatever they tell you up top, whatever you decide it meant — I'm not angry, Gidi. You got the others down. That's what guides do. That's what Okonkwos do.*

And then, near the end, when the cold had come into the cave to stay: *Take the compass. Not to remember me. To remember which way you're going.*

She had promised herself she would leave the pages here. She had decided it on the descent, somewhere in the white hours before dawn, with Bisi feverish and Dev limping and the pass still a full day's climb away. The journal belonged to the mountain the way Ilyas's carving belonged to the cairn. Grief you carried forever became a stone in your own pack; grief you gave to the mountain became part of the mountain, and mountains are patient, and mountains do not blame.

She knelt at the base of the cairn, opposite Yusuf's stone, and opened her pack with numb fingers.

The wind rose as she worked, as though the summit had noticed her intention and leaned in to watch. Cloud shadow slid across the snowfield below them, then away, then back. Somewhere under that white, three days' march to the south, the smugglers were either licking their wounds or calling down the valley for reinforcements; somewhere below that, the river they had run was running on without them, indifferent, brown with melt. None of it mattered here. Here there was only the cairn, and the wind, and a sister kneeling with her dead brother's last words in her hands.

She unwrapped the journal from its oilcloth. The pages were stiff with cold. She did not read them again — she had read them until she could recite them — but she held them a moment, all forty-odd leaves of them, feeling their lightness, their terrible lightness, the way a life reduced to pencil marks weighs almost nothing at all.

"I brought you home," she said quietly. Her voice came out cracked, half-strangled by the wind, and she found she didn't care who heard. "Eleven days late. Ten years late. But I brought you home, Emeka."

She thought about keeping one page. Just one — the page with her childhood nickname in it, *Gidi*, the way he'd spelled it at eight years old, the way he'd spelled it at twenty-six with a dying hand, some things constant across a whole life. She thought about it the way a drowning woman thinks about air.

Then she looked at the compass.

It sat in the inner pocket of her jacket, against her breastbone, where she had carried it since the cave. Brass, dented at one corner where it had struck rock in the fall, the needle cage scratched nearly opaque. On its case back, in engraving worn shallow by ten years of ice, the word *GIDI*. Her brother's hands had held this last. Her brother's last deliberate act — she believed this now, wholly, the way she believed in gravity — had been to point it toward home and leave it where only she would find it.

The pages were the story. The compass was the direction. A person could not walk forward carrying a whole story; but a direction fits in a pocket.

She folded the journal closed. She slid it into the hollow beneath the stone, into the same dry dust that held a boy's carved toy on the other side of the cairn, so that two fathers of the mountain — one a boy of nine, one a man of thirty-one — would keep each other company in the dark. She seated the stone with both palms, pressing it firm, and her hands stayed on it longer than they needed to, and when she lifted them the lichen had printed its pale map briefly against her skin, and faded.

Bisi was watching her. So was Tomas. Even Dev, who had no right to be watching anything after what he'd done, watched her with an expression Gideon could not name and did not try to.

"Say something for him," Bisi said. Her voice was thin — blood loss and altitude — but steady underneath, the way it had always been, since the first rope team they'd ever run together, fifteen years ago, on a mountain that had never tried to kill either of them. "He'd want it said out loud. You know how he was. Never could keep quiet."

Gideon laughed — a broken, startled sound, the first laugh in ten years that had grief and love in equal measure and neither one winning. Because it was true. Emeka had talked constantly, relentlessly, through base camp and breakfast and blizzards, narrating everything, joking at everything, until the silence of these last ten years had felt less like absence than like a room where the radio had died.

"He'd have hated this ceremony," she said. "He'd have wanted music. He'd have wanted everyone eating." She wiped her face with the back of her glove — she hadn't realized she was crying until the wet froze at her jaw. "So. When we get down, we eat. All of us. That's the service. That's the whole eulogy. Emeka Okonkwo, who never stopped talking, and never once stopped believing his sister knew the way."

The wind took her words north, over the pass, into the country beyond the clouds where no one living would ever hear them. She found that she was glad. Some words should go where only the mountain can file them.

Tomas cleared his throat. "Cloud deck's moving."

"I see it." She stood. Her knees cracked; her shoulders screamed; she stood anyway, and found that standing upright felt different now, felt lighter, as though something had been removed from her spine rather than added to it. She checked the sky with the old habit — the cloud deck climbing the southern valley in stacked gray shelves, the barometric weight she could feel in her healed fracture, in her teeth, in the pressure behind her eyes. Four hours. Three, if the mountain was feeling honest. Two, if it wasn't.

"Everyone drink," she said. "Bisi, can you walk?"

"I can walk," Bisi said, "if you stop asking."

"Dev?"

"I'm here," Dev said simply. It was more than he'd earned and exactly what he owed, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

Gideon pulled the compass from her jacket. She held it flat on her palm, shielding it from the wind with her other hand, and waited for the needle to settle. It swung, hesitated, steadied. North. True north, minus the few degrees of declination her father had taught her to correct for when she was seven years old, kneeling beside him on a hillside in another country, another lifetime, learning the family trade: *the needle doesn't lie, Gidi. People lie. Maps get old. The needle just points.*

The route down lay southeast, through the col, along the ridge Tomas had spotted yesterday, then the long traverse to the border country before the weather sealed the passes for the season. Dry season running out. Food for five days, maybe six, stretched across four people and one descent that should take three. Bisi's wound needing cleaning, needing antibiotics, needing a hospital that existed a very long way below them.

She looked at the needle. She looked at her people.

"Southeast," she said. "Single file. Bisi behind me, Tomas on her rope side. Dev, you've got the sharpest eyes left — rear guard, and you sing out if anything moves on our backtrack." She clipped the compass to her chest strap where she could read it without stopping. "We move fast and we don't talk about it. We'll talk at the bottom. We'll talk for a week at the bottom if you want."

Yusuf fell in beside her at the front, unasked, unneeded, and she didn't send him away. He had earned the front of the line the way they all had: by paying. He glanced at her once, and what passed between them needed no translation, and then he pointed his boots down the col and started walking.

The last thing Gideon did, before she followed, was turn back once toward the cairn.

The wind had already begun its work — scouring their tracks, softening the edges of the stones, erasing the evidence that anyone had ever climbed this high for any reason except the reason mountains exist. Two hollows under two stones, holding a toy and a journal, a boy and a man, a father's grief and a sister's absolution. The mountain would keep them. The mountain kept everything eventually, and it kept nothing cruelly; it simply held what was given, patiently, at the temperature of forever.

She had given it what belonged to it.

She kept the direction.

Gideon turned southeast and walked, and the first flakes of the coming storm came down around them like slow white ash, fine and dry and almost gentle, and the compass needle trembled once against its cage and settled true, and pointed the way down, and home.
