## 20. Descent

The sky broke at dawn the way ice breaks — all at once, and then in pieces.

Gideon stood at the edge of the last moraine with her face turned east, watching the monsoon peel itself away from the peaks like a bandage coming off a wound. For nine days the clouds had owned everything above four thousand meters. Now, ridge by ridge, the light came down: first the summit of the unnamed peak they'd been navigating by sound alone, then the glacier's blue spine, then the long brown shoulder of the pass behind them where Yusuf had knelt in the snow and buried his son's keepsake under a cairn he built with hands that shook only once.

The air after rain was cold enough to bite. Gideon breathed it anyway, deep, until her lungs ached.

Behind her, Bisi slept in the shelter of the rocks, propped against Tomas's pack, her bandaged thigh stretched out straight as a fallen log. The wound was ugly — a smuggler's bullet had torn through the muscle of her outer leg two days ago on the moraine — but it had missed the bone and missed the artery, and Tomas had cleaned and packed it with what remained of their medical kit, which was not much: half the supplies sat at the bottom of the river now, along with most of Gideon's certainty about who she used to be.

"She slept through the night," Tomas said, coming up beside her. He handed her a mug of melted snow water with a pinch of tea leaves floating in it, the last of the tea, hoarded for exactly this kind of morning. "Pulse is steady. Fever's down to almost nothing." He said it flatly, the way he said everything, but Gideon heard what lived underneath it: *she's going to live.*

"Good."

"Don't make it a habit, carrying people off mountains," he added.

"No promises."

He almost smiled. It was the closest he'd come in eleven days, and Gideon took it the way she'd learned to take everything lately — gratefully, and without asking for more than it was.

Below them, the trail dropped into green. Real green, the soaked emerald of Nepal's low valleys after weeks of rain, terraced fields shining like hammered copper in the new sun. Somewhere down there was a checkpoint, a police post, a jeep track, a world with rules in it again. Gideon felt the strangeness of that like a second language remembered mid-sentence.

Yusuf came up from the stream with two full waterskins and set them down without ceremony, the way he did everything. He looked older in the clear light — or perhaps just finally visible, the way a mountain looks different once the cloud lifts off it. His son's name was Rahim. She knew that now. She knew about the tin box he'd left in the ice cave seven years ago, and the letter inside it he'd never been able to say aloud, and why he'd needed the mountain's permission, in whatever way mountains grant such things, before he could go home to his wife.

"Clear skies," he said, nodding at the horizon as if reporting weather to a client. "Three days of them, maybe four. Then the next system comes up from the south."

"Then we walk today."

"We walk today," he agreed, and there was something between them that hadn't been there when he'd named his price back in the stilt house — not friendship exactly, not yet, maybe not ever, but the particular bond of two people who had each carried a body's worth of grief up the same slope and come down lighter for it.

* * *

They made eight kilometers before noon, which was slow by every measure Gideon had ever used, and fast enough that she found herself calling rest stops before anyone asked.

Bisi walked. Of course she walked; Bisi had been walking since before Gideon met her twenty years ago on a rope team in the Khumbu, when they were both young enough to think altitude was a personality trait. But she walked differently now, favoring the leg, one hand on Tomas's shoulder on the steep sections, and every time Gideon looked back and saw that hand, saw her oldest friend leaning on the man who had accused her of arrogance in the rapids and then carried her pack for six hours without being asked, something loosened in her chest that she hadn't known was tight.

"You're hovering," Bisi said during the third rest stop, lowering herself onto a boulder with a grunt. Sweat ran clean lines through the dust on her face. "You've checked my bandage four times this morning. I can count, Gideon. Rope-team brain, remember?"

"I know you can count."

"So hover less."

Gideon crouched anyway and looked at the dressing, because some things didn't change, and Bisi let her, rolling her eyes at the ceiling of the sky. The wound was clean. No spreading redness, no heat. Tomas's work.

"I keep thinking about the moraine," Bisi said while Gideon re-taped the edge of the gauze. Her voice was quiet, pitched under Dev's hearing, though Dev was thirty meters down the trail pretending to study his boots. "When the shots came. You put yourself between me and the ridgeline. You didn't even think about it."

"You'd have done the same."

"I know. That's not the point." She looked at her own leg, then up, and her eyes were the old sharp brown ones, unfooled by anything. "Ten years ago you'd have thought about it. Ten years ago you were so busy proving the mountain couldn't beat you that you'd have calculated the angle, weighed the odds, done the arithmetic of who was worth the exposure. I watched you do that arithmetic on the descent from the pass, back then. I watched it eat you alive afterward." She tapped Gideon's knee once, hard, the way she used to tap her harness to check it. "This week you didn't do any math at all. You just moved."

Gideon didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wouldn't have cracked her open on a trailside boulder in front of everyone, and the day was too long, and the miles were too many, and some inheritances you carry silently or not at all.

But she thought about it all afternoon, walking, the compass riding in her jacket pocket against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Emeka's compass. Engraved with *Little Bird* in her father's handwriting, the nickname only two people alive had ever known, waiting ten years in the dark of an ice cave for a sister who had stopped believing she deserved to find it. He had survived four days. He had written to her. *It was the storm, not you. Lead again.* She had read those words aloud in the cave with tears freezing on her cheeks, and she would read them again tonight, and tomorrow, and probably for the rest of her life, the way other people read scripture.

The trail dropped through rhododendron scrub gone wild with late blooms, scarlet and white, and the smell of wet earth rose around them like something exhaled after long holding of breath. Gideon led, but loosely now, the way she'd begun leading somewhere on the high pass — delegating the pace to Tomas, the route-finding at the river junctions to Yusuf, trusting the crew the way she'd trusted no one since the disaster. It should have felt like losing control. It felt like setting down weight.

* * *

They reached the police post at Salleri an hour before dusk.

It was nothing — a corrugated roof, a flagpole with the line snapped, a young officer in a uniform too crisp for the mud of the yard, a dog that stood up to bark and then thought better of it. But to Gideon it looked like the border between countries she'd been living in: the country of the mountain, where the only law was judgment and weather, and the country of paper, where names went into files and files went into drawers and sometimes, if you were lucky, drawers got opened again.

Dev stopped walking fifty meters out.

Gideon watched him stand there in the last light, pack on his back, camera bag over one shoulder, looking at the police post the way a man looks at water he knows is deep. He was twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven. His brother had died on Manaslu five years ago, carried off by an avalanche whose warning signs everyone in base camp had seen and no one in authority had acted on. Dev had joined the expedition because Gideon's route passed within a day of the place his brother fell. He'd told her that on the stilt house floor, in Yusuf's lamplight, voice cracking only once. What he hadn't told her then was that he'd already sold their route.

She knew now. They all knew. Bisi had caught him at 3 a.m. on the eighth night, hunched over the satellite messenger with its little screen glowing like a held match, transmitting waypoints to men with guns. The confrontation had lasted forty minutes and contained, as far as Gideon could tell, every emotion a human being is issued at birth. Dev had wept. Dev had explained: debts, a mother's medical bills, men who had approached him in Kathmandu with photographs of his brother's memorial and a number attached to a promise. And then Dev had said the thing that mattered, the thing Gideon kept turning over like a stone in her pocket: *I changed my mind on the river. When you decided to run it. I watched you decide, and I thought — she's betting all our lives on her own judgment, and I'm selling hers. I've been sending updates ever since. False ones. I sent them north when we went south.*

And then, on the moraine, when the smugglers' ambush broke over them like the storm ten years ago, Dev had run *toward* the gunfire, shouting, throwing stones, drawing three men off the flank while Bisi bled — and none of them knew until later whether he'd survive doing it. He had. Barely. A graze across his forearm that he hadn't mentioned until Tomas noticed the blood drying black.

Now here he was, standing in the road, refusing to take the last step toward the police post.

"You don't have to," Gideon started.

"Yes, I do." His voice was steadier than she'd expected. "That was always the point. I didn't sell the route and then feel bad. I sold it, and then I fought the people I sold it to, and both things are true, and neither one cancels the other out. If I just walk past this post and go home, then the version of me that took their money gets to hide inside the version that took the bullets. I'm not letting him hide." He looked at her, and his jaw was set in a way that reminded her painfully of Emeka at twenty-five, of a stubbornness she recognized from the inside. "They need my statement. Names, numbers, the account the payments came from, the photographs they showed me. I'm the only witness who was inside it. If I don't testify, they buy another guide next season, and someone else's sister finds out the way you found out."

Gideon stood beside him in the road for a moment. She thought about telling him it would be easy, and decided against lying. She thought about telling him the authorities would protect him, and decided against worse lying. Instead she said: "Whatever happens in that room, you walk out and we're still a crew. That part doesn't depend on them."

Dev nodded once, swallowed hard, and stepped forward.

What happened inside took ninety minutes and happened mostly without them. Gideon waited in the yard with the others, sitting on a bench beneath the flagpole, drinking tea a constable's wife brought out without being asked, sweet and milky and impossibly good. Through the open window of the post they heard fragments — Dev's voice rising and falling, the scratch of a pen, the officer saying *again, slowly, the name* — and the evening birds restarting their noise in the trees whenever the talking paused.

When Dev came out, he looked lighter and heavier at once, the way people look after confession, after funerals, after childbirth — after all the transactions where you pay with something internal and receive nothing you can hold. He sat down on the bench next to Bisi, who studied him for a long moment and then said, gruffly, "You owe me a new carabiner. The one I clipped your pack with when I dragged you off the moraine got shot through."

"It did not."

"Hole's right through the gate. Look." She tossed it to him, and he caught it, and turned it over in his fingers, and laughed — a wet, broken, genuine laugh — and Gideon understood that this was how forgiveness actually worked. Not a speech. A carabiner with a hole in it, thrown across a bench, caught.

* * *

They spent the night in the constable's storeroom, on sacks of rice that smelled of the earth they'd grown in, and Gideon slept harder than she had in ten years.

In the morning the skies were still clear — Yusuf's three days, ticking — and they walked out of the hills together, six of them now moving at the speed of their slowest member, which was Bisi, which was fine, which was the whole point of everything. The trail followed a river running bright and loud with snowmelt, and the terraces rose golden on either side, and porters passed them heading up with loads of roofing tin, and one of them, a boy of maybe fifteen, fell in beside Gideon for a kilometer and asked her, in careful English, whether the mountain ahead was dangerous.

"All mountains are dangerous," she said. "That's not the question."

"What is the question?"

"Whether you're paying attention." She dug into her jacket pocket as they walked and showed him the compass, brass worn bright at the edges, *Little Bird* catching the sun. "My father taught my brother and me on a trail not unlike this one. Needle points north, but the mountain doesn't care where north is. You have to hold both truths at once."

The boy considered this with enormous seriousness. "Did your brother become a guide also?"

"He became something better," Gideon said. "He forgave me."

The boy didn't understand, and she didn't expect him to, and she smiled anyway, and he grinned back, and peeled off toward his village at the next bend with a wave.

That night at a tea house in the valley, with Bisi's fever finally gone and Dev asleep with his arm bandaged and Tomas writing up medical notes by lamplight and Yusuf smoking one thin cigarette on the step like a man easing out of a long-held breath, Gideon sat with her back against the wall and felt the exhaustion arrive all the way down, the true exhaustion, the kind that comes after fear has finished with you rather than before it starts.

There was a message waiting on the tea house owner's phone, relayed from Kathmandu, from Mara Voss.

*Heard you came out,* it said. *Heard what you brought back. Get to the city when you can. There are people who need to hear your testimony — and Gideon: what they did to your name ten years ago, I found the manifest discrepancies. The load counts don't add up. It wasn't the storm that put you over that serac. Someone falsified the route report. Come testify, and we burn the blacklist down together.*

Gideon read it twice.

Outside, the first stars were coming out over the ridgeline, hard and clean, and the river went on talking to itself in the dark, and the compass lay warm against her ribs, pointing — as it always had, as it always would — not toward safety, but toward home.
