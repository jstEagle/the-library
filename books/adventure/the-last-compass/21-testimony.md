## 21. Testimony

The courthouse in Kathmandu smelled of dust and old paper and, faintly, of rain that had not yet arrived. Gideon sat on a wooden bench outside the hearing room with her hands flat on her knees, the way she used to sit at base camp before a rotation, breathing slow, letting the body believe it was calm even when the mind was still climbing.

She had worn the only clothes she owned that were not made for mountains: a dark blouse Bisi had pressed in the hotel bathroom with wet hands and a borrowed iron, and trousers that still carried a faint ghost of river silt in the seams no amount of washing had touched. She had thought about leaving them dirty on purpose. She had thought about a great many small acts of defiance on the flight down from the pass, and then, somewhere over the Terai, she had let all of them go.

The truth did not need costume. That was the whole point of the truth.

Bisi sat beside her, her left arm in a sling, the bullet having gone through the meat of her shoulder and out the other side as cleanly as a rope through a carabiner. She had refused a wheelchair at the airport with such ferocity that a customs officer had actually apologized. Now she sat with her good hand wrapped around a thermos of tea she kept offering to Gideon and Gideon kept declining, a small liturgy they had performed a hundred times on the trail and were performing now, here, in this unlikely cathedral of paperwork.

"Your knee is bouncing," Bisi said.

"My knee is fine."

"Your knee is trying to leave the country." She pressed the thermos into Gideon's hands. "Drink. You can't testify on an empty engine."

Across the corridor, behind a partition of frosted glass, Dev sat with two officers from the tourism ministry's investigation unit. He had turned himself in the morning they crossed into Nepal — walked into the checkpost at Rasuwagadhi before anyone could decide what to do with him, put the satellite messenger and the photographs of the route notes on the desk, and said, in his quiet river-valley voice, "I'll tell you everything, but you'll need more paper than that." He had been talking for six days now. Names, dates, payments, the chain of handlers who had recruited him in the lowlands with promises wrapped around his grief like a bandage around an infected wound. He had looked at Gideon only once, at the checkpost, and she had given him nothing — not forgiveness, not condemnation, just the same nod she would give any climber starting a pitch he might not survive. It was the only honest thing she had to give him.

The doors opened. A clerk with spectacles and an air of profound fatigue said her name, mispronounced it, and did not wait for correction.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than she had expected, and colder. A ceiling fan turned with the lazy indifference of something that had outlived its convictions. Three officials sat behind a long table stacked with files, and to one side, at a smaller table, sat a lawyer from the ministry — and beside him, immaculate in a charcoal suit that had probably cost more than Gideon's entire gear locker, Mara Voss.

Mara looked like a woman who had slept. That was the thing that struck Gideon hardest. Ten years of carrying the same disaster, and Mara had somehow slept, and dressed, and built a life with corners sharp enough to cut, and now here she was, spending a fortune and a reputation to unspend it. She caught Gideon's eye and gave the smallest tilt of her head — not a smile, nothing so warm, but an acknowledgment. *We're on the same rope now. Don't fall.*

The testimony took the better part of two days, though it felt to Gideon like one long pitch with no anchors.

She told it in order, because order was the only mercy she trusted: the letter, the offer, the name on the manifest that had cracked her open like river ice in spring. The crew. The night crossing. The river that should not have been running. The boat. The loss of the medical kit. Yusuf. The monsoon walls. Dev's signal fire, and the confession, and the smoke on the ridge. She spoke of Bisi's wound without dramatizing it, of Tomas's sickness at altitude, of the gorge at night with smugglers' bullets cracking off stone like the mountain itself snapping its fingers.

The officials were less interested in the adventure than in the machinery behind it. Who paid Dev. Who the smugglers were moving — not just route information, she testified, but artifacts, remains, anything of value that could be pried out of the high passes and sold to collectors who would never smell the thin air where those things were taken. She named the names Dev had given. She described the man who had fired across the river, the voice on the satellite calls, the ledger page Yusuf had found tucked in the patched hull of the boat — a page that had traveled with them all the way to the pass and back, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting.

And then, on the second day, the lawyer turned to the older matter. The original expedition. The one that had ended three lives and, in the eyes of every licensing board on two continents, one career.

"Tell us about the fall," he said.

Gideon had rehearsed this a thousand times in a thousand tents and hotel rooms and sleepless dawns. She had rehearsed it as defense, as confession, as curse. What she had never done was tell it as evidence — stripped of guilt's editorial hand, just the facts, in sequence, the way she'd read a route.

She told it plainly. The storm cell that the forecast had missed by six hours. The fixed lines on the traverse. The decision, hers, to push for the col before the window closed — and the second decision, also hers, to send Emeka's rope team ahead while she stayed with the slower clients. The serac. The white. The way the mountain had simply erased a section of the world, and when the world came back, her brother was gone, and two others with him, and the survivors were looking at her with the terrible question in their eyes that she would spend ten years answering wrong.

"I led them down," she said. "That part is true. What the record says about the rest — that the route was mine, that the risk was mine, that I ignored conditions — that part was never true. But I didn't fight it. That's on me. I let the report stand because I believed what it said about me. Grief makes a very convincing witness against yourself."

The room was quiet. The fan turned. Somewhere outside, a motorcycle argued with traffic.

Then the ministry lawyer slid a folder across the table and said, "Before we close today, there is material the board has not previously seen."

Mara stood.

She did not perform it. That was the surprise — Gideon had braced for theater, for Mara's famous precision turned into a courtroom aria. Instead she spoke the way she climbed: efficiently, without wasted movement, each fact placed like a foothold she had tested before trusting it with weight.

"Fourteen months ago," Mara said, "I began purchasing expedition archives from the outfitter that supplied the original expedition. Not to reopen the case. At first, frankly, to bury my own part in it more deeply — I had commissioned the route, and I wanted the paperwork that proved I had pressured the schedule." A pause, and Gideon watched her rival pay out the rope on her own guilt, hand over hand. "What I found instead was the outfitter's internal weather correspondence. The forecast they received and did not forward to the expedition lead. Six hours of warning. A storm cell the regional office had flagged and the outfitter's owner chose to sit on, because a delay would have cost him a bonded shipment window and a client bonus clause."

She let the folder fall open on the table. Telexes. Timestamps. A signature.

"He chose cargo over climbers," Mara said. "And when the disaster came, he helped write the report that blamed the guide, because a dead guide is cheaper than a live lawsuit. Gideon Okonkwo was not reckless. She was the only person on that mountain who did not know what was coming — because someone made sure she wouldn't know, and then made sure she'd be blamed for not knowing."

The fan turned. Gideon sat very still, the way you sit when the ground that has been shaking for ten years finally stops, and you don't yet trust it.

She thought of Emeka's journal, read aloud by headlamp in a cave below a serac, his handwriting steady even at the end: *It wasn't your fault, Gidi. I knew the mountain. I chose it. Forgive the mountain, and forgive yourself, in that order.*

She had forgiven the mountain first. It had taken the law a little longer.

* * *

The ruling came three days later, and it took four minutes to read.

The blacklist was dissolved. The original report was vacated and referred for criminal review — the outfitter's owner, it turned out, was already in custody in a neighboring jurisdiction, the smuggling ring's ledger having reached him like water reaching a low room. The restrictions on the pass would be reviewed. Dev's cooperation was noted; his sentencing would be separate, and lighter, and he would carry the rest of it himself, which was its own sentence, and perhaps the right one.

Gideon heard it standing in the corridor, because the clerk had mispronounced her name again and she had stepped out to breathe, and so she learned her own vindication through a half-open door, in the flat voice of an official reading findings into a record.

It was quieter than she had expected. That was the thing no one warned you about. Ten years she had imagined this moment as a door bursting open, as air after altitude, as a shout. Instead it was a fan turning. It was a clerk's tired voice. It was Bisi gripping her good shoulder and saying, "Well. There it is," as if they'd just topped out on an ordinary hill in ordinary weather.

Vindication, it turned out, was not a summit. It was the walk down, when you realize your legs still work.

* * *

They ate that night on the rooftop of a restaurant in Thamel that Bisi chose because the momos were criminal and the beer was cold, and because it had chairs, which Tomas — recovered, sunburned, and insufferably opinionated again — declared the true mark of civilization.

Yusuf came late, still in his river clothes because he owned no others and had refused, with quiet finality, every offer of shopping. He sat down heavily, accepted a beer he would not finish, and looked out over the neon churn of the street below as if it were a river he was reading.

"They gave my son's name to the record today too," he said. "In the findings. The cave, the artifacts, the men who were selling what the mountain keeps." He turned the bottle in his hands. "Ten years I thought no one would ever write his name anywhere but in my heart. And it took smugglers and a broken woman and a leaking boat to do it." He almost smiled. "The mountain collects everyone. But sometimes," he added, looking at Gideon, "it gives a little back."

Bisi raised her glass with her good arm. "To the ones who came back."

"To the ones we carried down," Gideon said, and they drank, and no one said Emeka's name, and everyone heard it.

Later, when the others had gone up to their rooms, Mara appeared at the bottom of the stairs with her coat over her arm, looking for once like a person rather than a force of nature. She stopped one step below Gideon, so that for the first time in their long, grinding history they were nearly eye to eye.

"I'm not going to apologize," Mara said. "You'd know it was a lie, and I'd know it, and we'd both be poorer for it. But I'll say this. I hired you because you were the best, and I told myself it was for the body and the compass. It wasn't. It was because I couldn't carry my half of that mountain alone anymore, and I knew — I *knew* — you were the only one strong enough to carry both halves, and too stubborn to let me help." She held out her hand. "The pass will reopen. There will be an inquiry, and permits, and a proper memorial expedition. I'd like it led by a guide with a clean record and a compass."

Gideon looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she took it.

"Don't make me carry your half again," she said. "Bring your own rope."

Mara's laugh was short and real, the first one Gideon had ever heard from her, and it sounded strange and human and a little rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut.

At the bottom of the stairs, Mara paused. "One more thing. The outfitter's archives — there was a box I couldn't catalog. Expedition effects, unclaimed. It's in a storage room in my office, and there's a name on it I think you should see before you decide anything about tomorrow." She said it lightly, but her eyes were not light at all. "Come by in the morning. Whenever you're ready."

She was gone before Gideon could ask.

* * *

Gideon did not sleep. She sat by the hotel window with the brass compass in her palm, its lid open, the needle settling again and again to the same unwavering point. *GIDI* on the inside of the cover, in her brother's careful engraving, worn soft by a decade of ice and four days of a dying man's hands.

*Whenever you're ready.* Mara had left the door open on purpose, the way you leave a rope hanging for the next climber. A box with a name on it. Emeka's effects, perhaps — the things he'd checked at the outfitter's before the expedition, the things that had sat in a storeroom while the world decided his sister was a killer. His boots. His letters. Maybe something he'd written before he left, something she'd never had because the report and the blacklist and the years had swallowed everything else.

Or maybe something else entirely. Mara's face had said *maybe something else entirely.*

Outside, the monsoon was ending. She could feel it in the air the way she'd learned to feel it on Yusuf's river — a loosening, a first dry note under the wet, the season turning over in its sleep. In a week the skies would break open into that impossible Himalayan clarity, and the passes would stand up sharp and white against a blue that made pilgrims of everyone who saw it.

Gideon closed the compass. She thought of the epilogue of every expedition — the part no one writes, the part where you unpack, and the mountain lets you go, and you have to decide who you'll be in the flat world below.

Tomorrow there was a box with a name on it.

She set the compass on the windowsill, needle north, and for the first time in ten years, Gideon Okonkwo slept without measuring anything at all.
