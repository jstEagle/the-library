## 1. Salt in the Wires

The sea off Cape Verren had a way of pretending to be empty.

Yara Halloran had learned that lesson young, and she'd learned it the way divers learned everything—by nearly dying. The water here looked like nothing: a flat pewter plain stitched with the rusting hulks of a decommissioned buoy field, buoys the navy had moored forty years ago and then simply forgotten, the way governments forgot things, the way fathers forgot to come home, the way mothers—

She stopped the thought where it stood. Twenty years of practice had made her good at that. Not good enough, but good.

Below her, the Thessaly's shadow lay on the seafloor like a sleeping animal, her running lights a soft green smear in the particulate. Forty feet up, the morning sun came down in columns, and Yara hung in the middle of it, neutral, weightless, her breath rasping through the regulator in the slow rhythm Dez called *the monk's breathing*—four counts in, six counts out, because panic was just impatience with a snorkel.

The buoy field was a graveyard of small failures. Chain, corroded to lace. Concrete sinkers furred with anemones. One buoy had broken loose years back and lay on its side in the sand, its steel skin colonized by plumose anemones that opened and closed in the current like a field of pale, hungry flowers. Routine salvage. Strip the copper, cut the chain, log the coordinates, move on. The kind of work that paid for diesel and beans and kept the crew one season ahead of broke.

Yara had been at Cape Verren eleven weeks. She knew this the way she knew everything—precisely, and against her will. Eleven weeks was already too long. The record was fourteen, in a harbor town in the Azores where the wine was cheap and the harbor master didn't ask questions. She'd left that place in the night with everything she owned in two duffels and a dive bag, and she'd never once looked back, because looking back was a habit, and habits were how people found you.

*Found you.* She turned the phrase over in her head, watching her exhaled bubbles climb and shatter against the surface far above. Found you. As if she were lost. As if there were somewhere to be found.

Her mother's voice, twenty years old and worn smooth by repetition, rose in her memory the way it always did when the water went quiet: *Yara, love, if you ever get lost, stay still. Things find you when you stop moving.*

She'd spent her whole adult life proving the woman wrong.

* * *

The work went easy for two hours. Dez worked the far sinker line, his cutting torch a stutter of blue-green light in the gloom, and Odile's voice came down the comm line every few minutes in her flat harbor French, all business: *depth, bottom time, pressure, current.* Odile ran a dive boat the way a nun ran a chapel—no wasted motion, no unsanctioned joy. Yara trusted her more than she'd trusted anyone in a decade, which was exactly why she'd have to leave her soon.

It was on the third sinker, a block of poured concrete half-swallowed by the sand, that Yara saw the wreckage that shouldn't exist.

Not the buoy hardware—she'd catalogued all of that in her head by now, the way she catalogued everything, exits and inventory and the faces of strangers. This was something else. A section of hull plating, maybe two meters square, lying flat in the sand fifty meters east of the buoy line, half-buried, its edges torn outward in a way that spoke of violence rather than decay. Marine growth covered it thick as carpet, which meant it had been down here a long time. Decades. And it was naval steel. She knew naval steel the way other people knew their mother's handwriting—riveted seams, the particular gray-green of old warship plate, the stenciled fragment still legible under the fouling where the paint had held:

*...RIDIAN...*

She knelt in the sand beside it, knees sinking, and felt the cold come up through her suit in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

There was no wreck charted here. She'd read the charts herself, all of them, because reading charts was the first thing she did in every new port—every hazard, every obstruction, every forbidden zone in a fifty-mile radius. It was how you stayed alive in this business, and how you stayed free: know where the edges were. The buoy field was listed. The old cable run was listed. Nothing else. Nothing that tore.

She worked her glove along the plate's buried edge and pulled. The sand gave reluctantly, in slow gray breaths, and what came up with the plate was a tangle of cable and conduit, fused—actually *fused*, the metals run together like candle wax, which meant heat, which meant fire, which meant a ship had died here, violently, and no one had ever bothered to say so.

And threaded through the fused cable like a bone through meat, a transponder housing. Military stamp. She scraped the fouling away with her dive knife, gentle as skinning a fruit, and the numbers emerged one by one from their coat of twenty years of sea:

*MERIDIAN — NAVRES 7741 — PROPERTY OF THE DEPARTMENT OF THE NAVY. RECOVERY MANDATORY.*

The current pushed against her, patient and indifferent, and Yara Halloran hung there in the cold green light with a dead ship's name in her hands and her heart going like a fist on a door.

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to bring it up. That was the thing about the deep parts of herself—she'd mapped them the way she mapped the sea, and there were still trenches down there no line had ever sounded. One moment she was kneeling in the sand; the next she was ascending with the transponder clamped to her harness like a thing she'd stolen, which, in a way, she had. Salvage law was clear: recovery mandatory meant the navy wanted it back, which meant the navy had lost it, which meant the navy had something to lose.

Dez was waiting at the ladder, big hands out to take her gear, his face already reading hers the way it always did—Dez missed nothing, that was his curse. He was the only person on the crew who'd been with her more than three years, and he'd never once asked why she never stayed anywhere past a season. He just watched, and waited, and carried things.

"You found something," he said. Not a question.

"Steel. Old." She set the transponder on the deck between them, seawater pooling around it, and watched Dez go still in the particular way he went still when the world tilted.

"Yara." He said her name the way a man says the name of a storm. He crouched, didn't touch it, read the stencil upside down. His jaw worked. "That's a naval transponder."

"It's a naval transponder fused into hull plate that isn't on any chart, fifty meters off a buoy field that's been dead for forty years." She pulled her hood back. Her hair came out wet and heavy, and the wind off the water was cold enough to make her grateful for it. Cold was good. Cold kept a person sharp. "That plate tore outward, Dez. From the inside."

Dez looked at the transponder for a long time. Then he looked at her, and what was in his face wasn't fear exactly—it was the tired recognition of a man watching someone walk toward a thing he'd watched her walk toward before.

"You know what I'm going to say."

"Drop it."

"Drop it." He nodded at the sea as if the whole gray expanse of it were the object in question. "Throw it back. Log the dive as unremarkable. We strip the copper, we take the pay, we move on to the next harbor, and in ten years nobody remembers Cape Verren or the buoy field or the woman who couldn't leave well enough alone." He stood, joints cracking, a big man made deliberately slow by decades of heavy work. "I've watched you do this for six years, Yara. You get a smell of something, and you follow it, and every time, *every time*, it costs us. The Marquesas job. The ferry off Sable. You don't find trouble, you *dig* for it, and then when it comes up, we're the ones holding the shovel."

Yara crouched and wiped the transponder dry with the heel of her hand. The stencil stared up at her. *MERIDIAN.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She knew the name. Of course she knew the name. It was a word from the other room of her childhood, a word spoken in her father's voice, low and final, the way he'd spoken it the night he'd made her swear: *Some ships sink, Yara. Some ships are sunk. You don't go looking under either one.* She'd been nine years old. She'd sworn on a Bible neither of them believed in.

Her father had died still keeping that oath. She'd spent twenty years keeping it too, the way you keep a scar—without thinking, without choosing, just carrying it.

"I'm not digging," she said.

"You're holding a navy transponder."

"I'm holding a question." She looked up at him, and she knew what he saw in her face, because she'd seen it in mirrors her whole life: her mother's face, her mother's hunger, her mother's restlessness wearing her daughter like a borrowed coat. "There's a difference."

"No," Dez said quietly. "There isn't. Not with you."

* * *

They sat in the wheelhouse that evening while Odile cooked, the transponder sitting on the chart table between them like a fourth crew member nobody wanted to acknowledge. The Thessaly rolled gently at anchor. Outside, Cape Verren's harbor lights smeared across the water, and somewhere past the breakwater the navy patrol boat that had been idling offshore for three days—three days, Dez had counted—showed its lights in slow rotation, a patient eye.

Odile set down three bowls of stew and looked at the transponder, and Yara watched her first mate do the arithmetic she'd already done: salvage rights, finder's claims, the navy's recovery-mandatory stamp and what it implied about what else might be down there, fused and waiting.

"Recovery mandatory," Odile said at last, in her accented, unhurried English. "That is a word with money in it. Or with prison in it. Usually both."

"It's a word with a ship in it," Yara said. "A ship that isn't supposed to be here."

"Ships are always where they're not supposed to be. That is most of what a ship is." Odile sat, spooned her stew, ate with the calm of a woman who had survived worse than mystery. "The question is not what it is. The question is who is coming to ask us about it. And when."

As if the sea had been listening, the patrol boat's searchlight swept the anchorage—once, lazily, the way a man checks a lock he's already sure of—and moved on.

Dez pushed his bowl away untouched. "Give it back," he said. "Drop it over the side tonight, deep hole past the shelf, and tomorrow we're three strangers who found nothing. Yara. *Boss.*" He almost never called her that. "I've been on this boat six years because you're the best diver I've ever seen and the only captain I've ever trusted. Don't make me be on it for the thing that comes next."

Yara looked at the transponder. Under the chart light the naval stamp gleamed faintly, wet and patient, and beneath the stencil of the name her thumb found a second line of smaller text she hadn't noticed before, half obliterated by corrosion, and her blood went cold and then colder:

*...CREW EFFECTS — UNIT 7 — E. HALLORAN...*

The wheelhouse went very quiet. The sea made its small sounds against the hull. Somewhere below the waterline, in the dark, the current that had carried her whole life along—out of harbors, out of relationships, out of every place that had ever tried to hold her—seemed to slow, to gather, to turn.

Her mother's initials. Her mother's unit. Stamped on wreckage that had no business lying in forty meters of water off a buoy field that had been dead for twenty years before the *Meridian* ever went down.

"Yara," Dez said softly. "What is it?"

She couldn't answer. Her hands had gone steady in the way they only went steady when everything else in her was coming apart—the diver's paradox, the body's oldest trick. Twenty years of moving. Twenty years of one season, one harbor, one name on a lease never signed. Twenty years of proving her mother wrong about staying still, because staying still was where the grief could catch you, and she had run the length of the world's coastlines from a single word spoken in a dead man's voice.

And now the word had found her anyway. In the sand. In the cold. Fused into cable, patient as the sea itself.

"Nothing," Yara said, and closed her hand over the stencil, and the lie sat in the wheelhouse with them like a third person. "It's nothing. Get some sleep. We dive the east sinkers at first light."

Dez looked at her for a long moment, and then at the transponder, and she watched him decide—not to believe her, but to stay anyway. That was Dez. He'd follow her into anything. It was the thing she loved about him and the thing that made leaving him, when the season turned, the hardest part of every year.

Outside, the patrol boat's light swept the anchorage again. Closer, this time.

And Yara Halloran sat in the wheelhouse of the only home she'd ever kept longer than a season, with her mother's name under her hand and the current finally, finally turning against her, and understood that the running was over—not because she'd chosen to stop, but because something twenty years patient had just reached up out of the sand and taken hold.
