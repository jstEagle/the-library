## 2. The Man Who Kept Score

The rain came in off the harbor the way it always did in March—sideways, mean, and without any intention of stopping. Yara Halloran stood at the window of the Thessaly's wheelhouse and watched it stitch silver threads across the water, and she told herself the itch between her shoulder blades was just the weather.

It wasn't. It never was.

Three days since the transponder. Three days since Dez had pried that navy serial number out of wreckage that had no business sitting in a decommissioned buoy field forty miles from anything worth a patrol route. Three days of Yara turning the thing over in her hands at night like a stone with a fossil inside, feeling the shape of something buried underneath her own life pressing up against the soles of her feet.

She'd wired the transponder into the shop computer on Tuesday, told herself she was only checking whether it was stolen property—due diligence, salvage law, nothing more. The registry hit came back in four seconds flat. *Vessel class: research support. Assignment: Meridian program. Status: decomm—* and then a wall, a classified flag thrown across the record like a tarp over a body.

She hadn't slept well since. She'd slept badly for twenty years, but this was a different flavor of badly, one with coordinates in it.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said from the galley doorway, a mug of coffee in each hand. She was sixty-one and built like a bollard, gray hair cropped to stubble, forearms mapped with rope burns old enough to be topographical features. She'd been Yara's first mate for six years, which made her the longest relationship of Yara's adult life, a fact neither of them ever said out loud.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're standing at the window looking at water like it owes you money." Odile handed her a mug. "That's the thing."

Yara took the coffee because refusing Odile's coffee was a war nobody won. "We finished the buoy contract."

"We did. Which means next month we're finished too." Odile leaned against the bulkhead, unhurried, watching her the way she watched weather fronts. "Fuel's paid through April. Insurance lapsed in February because someone—" she let the pause do the work, "—prioritized engine parts over paperwork. And Renn's dive computer is held together with epoxy and prayer."

"We'll find work."

"Yara." Odile said her name the way other people set down heavy tools. "I've been running boats thirty years. I know what a dying season looks like. This is one."

Yara drank her coffee and looked at the rain and didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer that wasn't either a lie or a surrender.

* * *

The Salt Line sat at the crooked end of Harbor Street, wedged between a chandlery that smelled permanently of tar and a net-loft that had been "closing soon" for a decade. It was the kind of bar where the floorboards had absorbed forty years of diesel, beer, and arguments, and where the jukebox only worked if you kicked it in exactly the right place—a piece of local knowledge Yara possessed and used sparingly, as a form of civic duty.

She came in at seven with Dez and Renn flanking her, salt-crusted and hungry, expecting nothing more than chowder and the cheap domestic draft the owner poured for working crews.

Cassian Voss was already there.

He'd taken the corner booth—the good one, under the brass lamp, with sightlines to both doors—and he sat in it like he owned the building, the street, and possibly the tide itself. He wore a charcoal coat that cost more than the Thessaly's winch, unbuttoned over a sweater that probably had a name Yara couldn't pronounce. His hair was going silver at the temples in a way that looked engineered rather than earned. He was reading a paperback, of all things, and he closed it with one finger marking his place when they walked in, as though he'd been waiting precisely long enough for the waiting to register.

"No," Yara said.

He smiled. He had a good smile; he always had. It was the smile of a man who had learned early that charm was cheaper than honesty and lasted longer. "You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. Whatever it is, no." She turned toward the bar.

"Triple your annual take."

The word *annual* did its work. She felt it land in the room behind her—in Renn's sharp intake of breath, in Dez going very still by the door—and she hated Cassian Voss, in that moment, with a clean and clarifying hatred, for knowing exactly how broke they were.

"Triple," she repeated, not turning around. "Of what? You don't even know our take."

"Forty-two thousand last year, after expenses. I keep score, Halloran. You know that about me." A pause, silk over steel. "One hundred twenty-six thousand. For six weeks' work. Plus per diem, plus equipment allowance, plus a completion bonus that would put Odile's boat back in black ink."

Yara turned around slowly. Voss hadn't moved except to fold his hands on the table, and there was something almost gentle in his expression, which was the most dangerous thing about him. Twenty years ago they'd been two kids on rival salvage skiffs out of the same marina, racing each other to whatever the sea dropped, and he'd beaten her exactly once—by cutting inside a channel marker in fog, a move that should have killed them both and instead got him a contract with a survey company before he turned twenty-five. He'd been climbing ever since: salvage, then recovery, then the gray-market jobs that paid in figures with commas. She'd stayed small on purpose. Staying small meant staying invisible, and staying invisible meant staying free.

"Who told you our numbers?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously, Cassian. Because it means somebody's been talking about my crew, and I want to know who."

"Your reputation does the talking. You're the best shallow-water salvager on this coast and everyone knows it, including the people who matter to me." He gestured at the bench across from him. "Sit down. Ten minutes. Then tell me no again, and I'll buy the whole bar a round and leave you alone forever."

Renn was already sliding into the booth. Renn was twenty-six, all elbows and appetite, and had the financial caution of a man who'd once bet his truck on a coin flip. Dez remained standing, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who'd seen this movie and remembered how it ended. Dez had been navy—eight years, discharged with a commendation he never discussed—and he had a fixer's instinct for when a deal smelled wrong.

Yara didn't sit. She stood at the end of the table like a woman at a graveside, which, given what she suspected was coming, was appropriate.

"Talk," she said.

Voss opened the leather folder he'd placed beside his beer. Inside were documents—real ones, not printouts, on paper heavy enough to have weight in the hand. He turned the top sheet toward her.

"Salvage rights. Exclusive. To a wreck designated MV-*"—he paused, savoring it, the way he savored everything—"—Meridian. Naval research vessel. Sank in '04. Coordinates included." He tapped the page. "Full rights of recovery. Anything we bring up is ours, minus my finder's percentage. The navy has formally abandoned the site. There's a court order. It's clean, Yara. Cleaner than anything you've touched in years."

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

*Meridian.*

She kept her face still. She'd had twenty years of practice keeping her face still, and she spent all of it now, every hour of discipline, holding the name behind her eyes where none of them could see it land.

"The Meridian's a myth," Dez said quietly. "Half the fleet swears it doesn't exist. Other half swears it sank with everybody aboard and the navy bought the silence."

"The navy bought the silence," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Which is why the rights are available. Nobody official wants to go near it. But the wreck is real, it's sitting at ninety meters in water I can describe to you down to the thermocline, and it is full of material worth recovering." He leaned back. "Research-grade electronics. Alloy stock. And one item of particular interest—a sealed archive vault amidships, pressure-rated, likely intact. That's the prize."

"What's in the vault?" Yara asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Data. Research data." Voss shrugged, an elegant, empty gesture. "Twenty-year-old naval research. Probably worthless. Probably corrupted beyond recovery. But my client collects complete sets."

"Your client."

"My client." He met her eyes, and for half a second the charm thinned and she saw the thing underneath, the thing she'd glimpsed only twice in twenty years: a hunger so patient it looked like calm. "You don't need to know the name. You need to know the money is real and the job is legal and the crew gets paid. Everything else is decoration."

Yara picked up the contract. The paper was cool and smooth. The coordinates printed in the header meant nothing to her consciously—but her body knew them, some animal ledger deep in her chest flipping open to a page she'd spent two decades gluing shut. Ninety meters. The trench shelf east of the Corvus Bank. Her father had sailed past those waters his whole remaining life with the wheel held hard over, jaw set, saying nothing, saying everything.

Her mother had gone down with the Meridian—or hadn't come home when it did; the navy's version changed depending on who was asking and how much they'd had to drink. Yara had been nine. Her father had gathered whatever crew would listen into their kitchen, smelling of diesel and grief, and made them swear: *nobody touches that site. Not for money, not for closure, not for anything. Some wrecks are load-bearing. You pull one brick and the whole house comes down.* They'd sworn. Most of them had kept it. Her father had died keeping it, in a hospital bed, gripping her wrist with a strength that surprised them both, saying *promise me* one more time as if the first eleven times hadn't taken.

She set the contract down on the table, face up, and slid it back across the wood.

"No," she said, and this time it was quiet, and it was final, and it cost her more than the first one. "Find another diver, Cassian. One who doesn't ask questions and doesn't have reasons."

Voss studied her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise; he'd expected the refusal, maybe wanted it, wanted the chase. "You know," he said softly, "most people would at least ask why me. Why now. Why a wreck the navy spent twenty years pretending was a fuel barge."

"Most people are smarter than me."

"They're not. They're just less curious." He closed the folder. "Offer stands for seventy-two hours. After that, I take it to the Marlowe brothers, and you can watch their rust-bucket drag your mother's ship apart with a grapple hook."

He said it lightly. He said it like a man flicking ash off a sleeve. But the word *mother* went into the room like a blade between ribs, and Yara understood, with total clarity, that he knew. He'd known before he walked in. The contract, the timing, the corner booth—all of it built around the one person on this coast who could do the job and the one person on this coast who'd refuse it for reasons she'd never say aloud.

He stood, dropped bills on the table that covered his tab three times over, buttoned his coat. At the door he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "I'm sorry about the grapple hook line. It was inelegant. But you left me no elegant options, and I've never been able to afford elegance." The bell above the door chimed. The rain took him.

Nobody spoke for a while. Renn stared at the door. Dez stared at his beer. The jukebox, unprompted, began playing something mournful and country, and nobody kicked it.

"He knows about Mom," Yara said finally. Flat. Factual. Saying it out loud so it couldn't grow teeth in the dark.

"Yeah," Dez said. "Yeah, he does."

"That's not a negotiation. That's a leash."

"Then we cut the leash." Renn, leaning forward, urgent. "Yara, listen—one hundred twenty-six thousand. We could refit the Thessaly top to bottom. New compressor. New insurance. Odile could stop eating instant noodles and pretending it's a cuisine."

"It's not about the money."

"It's a little about the money."

"It's not about the money, Renn." She heard the edge in her own voice and didn't file it down. "That wreck isn't a payday. It's a grave, and worse than a grave, and the navy didn't bury it because it's embarrassing—they buried it because of what's *in* it. You heard him. An archive vault. Sealed. Pressure-rated. You don't pressure-rate a vault on a research ship unless what's inside matters enough to survive the sinking. And whatever it is, Cassian Voss wants to sell it to somebody whose name he won't say, and people like that don't collect complete sets out of nostalgia."

Dez nodded slowly. "And if we say no, he takes the Marlowe brothers out there anyway, and they'll strip it blind and probably die trying, and the navy will come asking everyone in this harbor who talked." He rubbed his jaw. "There's no clean exit from this room, Yara. There's just exits."

"There's always an exit. It's called leaving." She drained her beer. "We finish the season. We take the coastal contracts. We stay away from the Corvus Bank, and in a year nobody remembers Voss ever darkened this bar."

She believed it when she said it. That was the trick of belief—it arrived fully formed, right up until the moment it didn't.

* * *

She found out at midnight.

She'd walked back down to the dock to check the bilge pumps—the Thessaly worried about her even when Yara pretended not to worry about anything—and saw light burning in Odile's cabin, low and yellow, and the hatch cracked to the night air. Through the gap: the glow of a screen, and Odile's broad back hunched over the little desk, and her hand moving across a document with the slow, deliberate pressure of a signature.

Yara didn't knock. She opened the hatch.

Odile didn't startle. She'd heard her boots on the dock planks, obviously; she'd had time to hide the papers and hadn't, which was its own kind of message. She sat there in the lamplight with the contract spread before her, signed, dated, Voss's finder's-percentage clause initialed in the margin in her blunt carpenter's handwriting. On the desk beside it lay a bank statement and a letter from a marine lender, and Yara's eyes went to the statement before she could stop them, and the numbers on it explained everything: the lapsed insurance, the epoxy, the instant noodles, six years of Odile quietly absorbing costs that should have sunk them all.

The second document was a mortgage notice. Against *her own boat*—the *Petrel*, the little trawler Odile had owned since before Yara was born, the boat she'd named after the bird that flies farthest from land and always comes back. Mortgaged. Six weeks ago. To fund the Thessaly's refit, to keep the crew afloat through a season that had gone thin.

"Odile." Yara's voice came out strange. Younger. "What did you do."

"What needed doing." Odile capped the pen. She didn't look ashamed. She looked tired in the way that bedrock is tired—uncomplaining, permanent, worn down by forces too large to argue with. "Voss's runner came by the boat this afternoon. Second copy of the contract. Said the offer was open to the crew collectively, but any qualified signatory could execute it." She folded her hands. "So I executed it."

"You had no right."

"I had every right. I'm part-owner of this operation, and I'm the reason it's still operating." She finally looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were steady and old and unbearably kind. "You think I signed because of the money? I signed because you've been standing at that wheelhouse window for three days looking like a woman being pulled apart by two currents. And I signed because if we don't take this job, Voss takes it to someone worse, and that wreck gets torn open by idiots, and whatever's down there—and I've watched you carry whatever it is for six years, girl, don't tell me there's nothing—gets sold to strangers anyway, without you anywhere in the room."

"My father swore us off that site."

"Your father's been dead nine years. And the oath was his grief talking, and grief makes bad law." Odile rose, joints cracking, and put her hand on Yara's shoulder—the same hand that had taught her splice knots, that had pulled her out of a rip current off Cape Ferro when Yara was nineteen and stupid and immortal. "You've spent twenty years running from that wreck. Running is expensive, Yara. It's cost you every port, every lease, every maybe. Voss just made running more expensive than stopping."

Yara stared at the signature. Odile's name, in ink, binding the Thessaly and everyone aboard it to the Meridian, to ninety meters of black water, to a vault stamped with her mother's unit designation. The rain ticked against the cabin roof. Somewhere out in the harbor, a halyard clinked against a mast, keeping time like a patient clock.

"If you dive it," Odile said quietly, "you dive it with your crew. With family. Not alone in the dark with a stranger's agenda. That's the difference between what Voss is offering and what I just did." She squeezed Yara's shoulder once, hard, and let go. "Seventy-two hours. Sleep on it. Or don't sleep—that's traditional too."

She climbed out past Yara and went up the dock toward her truck, unhurried, a woman who had made her decision and would live inside it comfortably for the rest of her life.

Yara stood alone in the lamplight with the contract. Outside, the storm was easing at last, the rain thinning to a fine salt haze, and through the cabin's porthole she could see the harbor lights doubled and trembling on the black water—every light a boat, every boat a choice, and hers narrowing by the hour to a single set of coordinates she'd spent half her life sailing away from.

She picked up the pen.

She put it down.

She picked it up again, and her hand was steady, and that frightened her more than anything else—the steadiness, the terrible calm of a current that has finally stopped fighting the shore and begun, instead, to turn.
