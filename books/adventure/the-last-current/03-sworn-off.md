## 3. Sworn Off

The night before the Thessaly was to make way, Yara did not sleep. She sat in the wheelhouse with the logbook from the first dive wrapped in a towel on her knees like something feverish, and the harbor lights smeared themselves across the glass, and she let the past come up the way bodies come up—slowly, bloated with water, refusing to be turned away.

She had been nine years old the last time she saw her mother's face in anything other than a photograph.

* * *

It was a Tuesday. She remembered that because her father had said it over and over in the weeks after, as if the ordinariness of the day were itself an accusation. *A Tuesday, Yara. Who loses anyone on a Tuesday.* He'd been mending a net on the porch of their house at Cape Ferrow, and Yara had been drawing in the dust with a stick, and her mother had come out of the kitchen still wearing her shore-leave clothes—civilian clothes, which was strange, because Mara Halloran almost never took them off when she had leave; she wore her uniform even to church, and the neighbors had opinions about it that they kept behind their teeth.

"I'll be back before the equinox," her mother had said, crouching down so their eyes were level. Her hands smelled of coffee and engine grease. They always did. "You keep the lighthouse lamp for me."

"It's not a real lighthouse," Yara had said, because it wasn't—it was a yard light on a pole, wired by her grandfather out of salvage, and it burned orange and attracted moths in biblical numbers.

"Doesn't matter what it is. It matters that you keep it." Her mother had tapped her once on the sternum, two fingers, the way she did when she meant something down to the bone. "You keep it lit, I'll find my way home. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked."

Then she'd kissed the top of Yara's head, said something low to her father that Yara hadn't caught and would spend twenty years trying to reconstruct, and walked down to the lane where the navy car idled with its government plates and its driver who never got out.

Yara had kept the lamp lit. Every night. Even after the news came, even after the navy men came with their hats held to their chests and their voices sanded smooth with practiced regret—even then, especially then, she'd gone out into the cold and switched it on, because no one had told her the contract was void. No one had told her that some ships don't find their way home no matter how many lamps you burn.

The Meridian went down off the Corvane Shelf in October, ninety meters down, in water so cold the search divers could only work in forty-minute rotations. The official account was a hull failure during a storm. The unofficial account—the one the fishermen traded like contraband—was that there had been no storm worth the name, that the navy cordoned thirty square miles of open ocean for eleven days, that helicopters came and went at hours helicopters shouldn't fly, and that when the cordon finally lifted, the sea gave back exactly nothing. No raft. No body. No debris field worth the word. A three-hundred-foot research vessel does not die politely, and yet the Meridian had died like a secret: quietly, completely, on purpose, if you believed the men at the Anchor Line who'd had too much rum and not enough sense.

Her mother's service number was M-1147-K. Yara knew it the way other children knew their prayers.

* * *

Her father changed after that, the way wood changes when it takes salt. Not rotten, exactly. Hardened. Darkened. He stopped taking contracts south of the shelf, and when crewmen asked why, he said the currents were bad there, which was true the way most of his lies were true—they just weren't the *reason*.

Yara was fourteen the first time she found the chart. It was folded into the back of his copy of the coast pilot, an old admiralty chart of the Corvane approaches, and someone—her father, in pencil, in his small furious hand—had drawn a circle around a featureless stretch of blue and written across it in letters pressed hard enough to tear paper:

**SWORN OFF.**

Below it, smaller: *M.H. — let her rest.*

She hadn't asked him about it. Asking would have made it a conversation, and conversations about her mother in that house were like opening a pressure hatch at depth—everything inside came out at once and killed you. So she'd folded the chart back along its worn creases and put it away, and she'd started diving anyway, at sixteen, against his explicit wishes, because the water was the only place the silence didn't follow her.

He found out, of course. Found her coming up the harbor ladder at dusk with a bag of scallops she'd taken from a bed she had no license for, her wetsuit steaming in the cold air, and he stood at the top of the ladder with his arms crossed and looked at her the way you look at a hull breach.

"You swear it off," he said. "That water. That ground. You swear it now, girl, or you and me are done."

"She's down there."

"The sea keeps what it keeps."

"That's a coward's proverb."

His face went through several weathers at once—rage, grief, something older than both—and then he came down the ladder, which he hadn't done in years, his knees bad since the trawler accident, and he gripped her shoulders with hands that shook, and he said, very quietly, very close, so the gulls couldn't hear: "I made a promise on her name that none of us would go looking. Because the last man who went looking came back wrong, Yara. He came back *wrong*, and he wouldn't say why, and six months later he walked into the channel at slack tide and didn't walk out. You want to know what's down there? That's what's down there. Not your mother. Something that eats the reason to come home."

And Yara, fifteen days shy of seventeen, hard as barnacle and twice as stubborn, had said: "Then I'll bring a lamp."

He hadn't spoken to her for a month. But the night before she left for her first deep-water certification, she'd found a new dive knife on her bunk, honed mirror-bright, with a small brass plate soldered to the grip. On the plate, stamped in a jeweler's careful hand: **M-1147-K**.

They never spoke of it. That was the Halloran way. Love expressed exclusively through hardware.

* * *

He died in February, eight years ago now, of the thing in his lungs that the shipyard doctors had been warning him about since before Yara could read. The end was slow and undignified and mostly consisted of him losing arguments with an oxygen concentrator, and in the last week, when he knew, when the knowing settled on him like snow, he'd asked her to open the window even though it was freezing, and he'd looked out at the harbor for a long time and said:

"You'll want to go down there someday. To the Meridian. I know you will. You have her compass in you, God help us both."

"Don't talk like that."

"Hear me out, because I haven't got the breath to say it twice." His hand found hers on the blanket, weightless as kelp. "I swore us off that ground because I was afraid. That's the truth of it. I wasn't protecting a grave. I was protecting myself—from knowing. There's a difference, and it took me twenty years and a tube in my throat to learn it. Fear makes a fine anchor but a poor compass." He coughed, wet and long, and waited it out. "If you ever do go—if the day comes when staying away costs more than going—then you go prepared, and you go with people who'll pull you out, and you come back up, Yara. Whatever you find down there. However it ends. You come back up. Promise me the coming back. I can't ask more than that. I never could."

She promised. He died at dawn, which the nurses said was common, though Yara chose to believe he'd simply waited for the light the way sailors do.

They buried him beside the empty plot they'd bought for her mother—a stone with both names on it, one with dates, one without, and under her mother's name the single word the navy had authorized: *LOST*. Not dead. Lost. As if she were a pair of gloves, a set of charts, a thing mislaid rather than taken.

Yara had left Cape Ferrow eleven days after the funeral. Sold the house, kept the yard light—dismantled it herself, packed it in the truck bed like cargo—and pointed herself north, then west, then wherever the work was. One season here, one season there. Never a lease. Never a forwarding address longer than a month. People called it wanderlust, and she let them, because the truth was uglier: she ran because every harbor she stayed in long enough became a place her mother might have known, and every place her mother might have known became a wound with a street address. Stillness was where the grief lived. So she moved. For twenty years she moved, and the moving worked the way anesthesia works—it didn't heal anything, it just postponed the screaming.

Until Cassian Voss had walked into the Anchor Line with a contract and coordinates, and the coordinates had landed on the table like a dropped engine block, and Yara had understood, with the flat clarity of a depth gauge hitting red, that the running was over. Not because she'd chosen to stop. Because the thing she'd been running from had finally gotten tired of waiting and come to meet her.

* * *

Now she stood at the rail of the Thessaly at 0300, the harbor black and breathing beneath her, and unwrapped the logbook again. Renn's near-miss today—the way he'd come up the ladder gray as ash, lips the color of drowned things, the chamber tech shaking his head at their profiles—should have been enough. Any sane person would call it enough. Two dives, one man bent nearly to death, a wreck that breathed wrong, a vault door with her mother's unit designation stamped into steel that had spent two decades at ninety meters and still showed tool marks fresh as yesterday.

But sanity had never been the Halloran inheritance. Compass was.

She opened the logbook to the page she'd copied by headlamp on the swim step, salt-stiff, her own handwriting shaky from cold. Her mother's script was compact and slanted, engineer's hand, the letters of someone who wrote fast because there was always something else to do. The entry read, in part:

*Day 41. The quieting works better than the protocol intended. Dr. Ehrenfeld says this is success. I say a door that won't open from either side isn't a door anymore. Requesting transfer of trial records to my personal effects. If I'm right about reversibility, someone will need to prove it, and if I'm wrong, someone will need to know I tried.*

Someone will need to know I tried.

Yara closed the book. Below her, the water slapped the hull in its patient rhythm, and somewhere out past the breakwater, past the buoy field, past the patrol boat that pretended to be idle, the Meridian lay on its shelf of cold mud with its breached hull and its scorched corridors and its sealed door, holding whatever was left of Day 41 and all the days after.

Her father had sworn them off that ground out of fear, and had confessed it with his last breath, and had asked only one thing in exchange: *come back up.*

She could keep that promise. She could keep it and still go. Those were not, she understood now, contradictory instructions. They were the whole instruction. Go down. Come back up. Everything between those two points was just weather.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Dez stood in the wheelhouse doorway, coffee mug steaming, face half-lit by the binnacle glow. He'd been watching her, she realized. Probably had been for an hour.

"No," she said.

"You're going to do it. One more dive. Vault or nothing." It wasn't a question. Dez had known her eleven years; he'd stopped asking questions he already had answers to around year four.

"My mother's voice is in a locker on that wreck, Dez. Maybe. Maybe it's rust and seawater and nothing. But maybe—" She stopped. The word *maybe* had carried her whole life; it deserved better than to crack now.

"Maybe," Dez agreed quietly. He came to stand beside her at the rail. For a while neither of them said anything, and the harbor breathed, and the mooring lines creaked their old complaints. Then he said, "Your dad. Would he have stopped you?"

Yara thought about the dive knife with the brass plate. About the window opened in February. About a dying man trading his fear for her freedom in the space of a single sentence.

"He'd have hated it," she said. "And then he'd have checked my gear himself, twice, and stood on this rail until I was back aboard."

Dez nodded slowly, as if this settled something. "Weather window closes tomorrow evening. Voss wants a briefing at oh-six-hundred. And Yara—" he paused at the wheelhouse door, silhouetted against the instruments, "—the patrol boat moved again tonight. Quarter mile closer. Somebody out there is counting our dives."

Yara looked east, where the dark was thickest, where a ship without running lights sat on black water pretending to be nothing.

"Let them count," she said, and went below to check her gear.
