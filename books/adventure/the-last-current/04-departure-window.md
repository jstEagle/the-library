## 4. Departure Window

Dez Okafor counted the ways a boat could kill you the way other men counted sheep. It was a habit from his navy years, and it had never left him: every hull a ledger of failures waiting to happen, every weld a promise someone might have broken. He walked the Thessaly's deck at first light with a coffee going cold in his fist and ran his palm along the rail, feeling for the soft spots rust had chewed thin over the winter.

The boat was forty years old and honest about it. She was a converted trawler with a dive gantry bolted where the nets used to run, a compression chamber crammed into what had been the fish hold, and a list to port that Odile swore was character and Dez swore was an argument with gravity they were slowly losing. She smelled of diesel and salt and the particular staleness of a galley that had fed the same six people for too many seasons. He loved her the way you love a thing that has carried you through weather you had no business surviving.

Below him, already, the sounds of preparation. Renn in the engine room, cursing at the portside injector in a voice that carried up through the deck plates like heat. Odile on the radio, her voice low and clipped, arranging fuel at a price that made Dez wince just hearing half the conversation. And Yara up on the flybridge before anyone else, doing what she always did—checking the sky like it owed her something.

He climbed the ladder and joined her. The harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under a lid of cloud, the fishing fleet out early, gulls working the gutting tables on the pier. And beyond the breakwater, past the channel markers, sitting exactly where it had sat yesterday and the day before—

"There she is again," Yara said, without turning.

"I see her."

The patrol boat hung offshore like a sentence nobody had finished. Gray hull, no name visible at this distance, no wake. Just idling on station four miles out, close enough to be seen, far enough to claim coincidence. Navy pennant at the mast. Dez had watched her through the binoculars three times since Voss's contract landed on their table, and each time he'd found the same thing: nothing. No patrol pattern, no interest in the fishing lanes, no reason at all to be there except them.

"Coast guard runs this sector," Yara said. "Not navy."

"Maybe they're lost."

"Four days lost?"

She lowered the binoculars and looked at him, and Dez saw what he always saw in her face when she talked about the job—the hunger, banked but burning, the look of a woman standing downwind of a fire only she could feel. He'd known Yara Halloran for eleven years. He'd pulled her out of water twice, taped her ribs once, and lied to harbor officials on her behalf more times than he could count on both hands. He knew her better than most people knew anyone. And he still couldn't tell whether what drove her was courage or a wound that had never learned to close.

"Voss says the site's clean," Dez said. "Says the navy wrote it off decades ago."

"You believe him?"

"I believe Voss believes whatever version of the truth gets him what he wants." He drank the cold coffee and grimaced. "That's not the same as clean."

Yara grunted—agreement or dismissal, impossible to say—and went back to the glass. Dez left her to it. There was no point arguing with her before breakfast; the words just slid off. She'd been like that since the contract was signed, wound tight as new line, running checklists in her head at all hours. He'd seen her like this before, on jobs that paid well and ended badly, and he'd learned that the only thing you could do for Yara Halloran when she got like this was make sure the equipment worked and stand between her and the worst of herself when the moment came.

He just hadn't expected the moment to come so soon.

* * *

By midmorning the Thessaly wore the organized chaos of departure. Crates of mixed gas stacked and lashed on the aft deck. The recompression chamber pressure-tested, Renn crawling halfway inside it with a flashlight clenched in his teeth because the interior light had failed and nobody had ordered a replacement. Dez spent two hours on the winch, greasing the cable link by link, because a frayed lift cable at ninety meters wasn't a malfunction, it was a funeral with extra steps.

It was while he was elbow-deep in the winch housing that Odile came looking for him.

He heard her boots on the deck plates and knew something was wrong by the rhythm of them. Odile Marchand walked like a metronome—steady, unhurried, the walk of a woman who'd skippered boats through two decades of North Atlantic weather and had never once been surprised by the sea. This morning her steps were short. Careful. Like someone carrying something breakable.

"Don't stop on my account," she said, when he surfaced from the housing with grease to his elbows. "I need to talk while your hands are busy. You think better when you're fixing something."

"That's not true."

"It's absolutely true. You once explained your entire divorce to me mid-rebuild of a bilge pump." She leaned against the rail beside him, arms folded, watching the harbor instead of him. That was Odile's way: hard conversations delivered sideways, to the horizon.

"So talk."

She was quiet long enough that he started to worry. Then she said, "You've been asking around about the money. I know. Marisol told me you went to her brother at the chandlery, asking if we could get better terms on the gas order."

"I wanted to know how much slack we had."

"We don't have any." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "We have none. The crew hasn't been paid properly since the Kestrel job went sideways in the spring. The insurance payout covered the boat damage and nothing else. I've been covering fuel and food out of my own account since June, and my own account—" She stopped. Started again. "I mortgaged the *Cormorant*."

Dez sat back on his heels and forgot the winch entirely.

Odile's boat. The *Cormorant*, the little longliner she'd bought with her first salvage money twenty-two years ago, the boat she kept moored across the harbor and polished by hand every Sunday morning rain or shine, the boat she'd refused to sell when her marriage ended, when her daughter's tuition came due, when the doctor bills came after that. The *Cormorant* wasn't a boat. It was Odile's proof that she'd built something in this life that couldn't be taken by weather or bad luck or men.

"You did what?"

"The bank gave me enough to cover the charter costs, the gas, the insurance rider, and six weeks of wages. Which is what we need to finish the Meridian job if everything goes right." Her jaw tightened. "And I'm telling you now, Dez, because you're going to find out anyway—you always do—and because I'd rather you hear it from me than from the loan officer."

"Why?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Why would you put that boat on the line? For Voss? For a wreck the navy's been sitting on for twenty years?"

"For us." Now she turned and looked at him straight on, and her eyes were dry and furious and frightened all at once, and Dez understood he was seeing something almost nobody ever saw: Odile Marchand without her armor. "Look at them. Look at the crew. Renn's sleeping in the workshop because he can't afford the room above the chandlery anymore. Marisol's been sending money home to her mother instead of paying off her cert renewals. You've got—" she gestured at him, at the whole of him, "—whatever you've got, which is less than you let on. And Yara would leave tomorrow rather than ask any of us for help, and we'd all pretend we didn't notice her go, and then there wouldn't be a crew anymore. There'd just be people who used to work together."

Dez wiped his hands uselessly on a rag. "So the answer was the *Cormorant*."

"The answer was the only thing I have that a bank wants." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her; he saw it land somewhere behind her ribs. "Yara signed because she needs this. Voss signed because he wants something—I don't fully buy his reasons, and neither do you. But I signed because if this crew breaks up, there's nothing left to hold together. We're not colleagues, Dez. We haven't been for years. We're the closest thing to family any of us has managed to keep, and families don't scatter just because the money's gone. They find money."

He looked at her for a long moment. Out on the water, the gray patrol boat sat motionless against the gray sky, patient as a headstone.

"If this goes wrong," Dez said finally, "you lose the boat."

"If this goes wrong," Odile said, "we'll have bigger problems than the boat."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the work was good for all of them, the way physical labor is good when everyone is carrying something invisible. Marisol calibrated the sonar and re-ran the survey grid until the numbers matched to the meter. Renn emerged from the engine room at three o'clock with a rebuilt injector, a bleeding knuckle, and the satisfied scowl of a man who had beaten a machine into agreement. Dez tested the chamber, charged the banks, checked the emergency bottles twice, then a third time because the patrol boat was still out there and paranoia, in his experience, was just foresight with bad manners.

At dusk they gathered in the galley for the pre-departure brief. Six of them around a table built for four, the overhead light buzzing, a chart of the survey area spread between the coffee mugs. Voss sat apart, as he always did—not excluded, exactly, but occupying space the way a man does when he knows he's tolerated rather than welcomed. He wore his competence like cologne. Dez distrusted it.

"Departure window opens at 0400," Odile said, tapping the chart. "We ride the ebb out of the channel, make the site by noon tomorrow. Weather window looks solid for five days, maybe six. After that, a low comes up from the southwest and I don't intend to be anywhere near ninety meters of water when it arrives."

"Five days is enough," Yara said. "Three dives, maybe four. We know the target."

"We know part of the target," Dez said.

Every head turned. He hadn't planned to speak—he'd promised himself he'd keep his doubts below decks where they belonged—but the words were out before the better instinct caught them.

"The navy's had four days to write us off and hasn't done it," he said. "That boat out there isn't a coincidence. Somebody knows what we're after, or knows what's down there, and they're watching to see if we're stupid enough to go get it. I'd like to hear, one more time, why we think this is a salvage job and not a trespassing charge with a body count."

Silence. Renn looked at his hands. Marisol looked at Odile. Yara looked at the chart like it might rearrange itself into an answer.

"The Meridian was decommissioned and struck from the register," Voss said smoothly. "Salvage rights were auctioned through a maritime court in a jurisdiction the navy doesn't control. I purchased them legally, with documentation I'll happily show any official who asks. If a patrol vessel takes an interest in our operations, we are a licensed survey team conducting archaeological recovery. Nothing more."

"And if they board us?"

"Then we show them paper and smile." Voss smiled as he said it, demonstrating. It was a good smile. Dez hated it.

"And the vault?" Dez pressed. "The thing you actually want. You still haven't told us what's in it."

"Data," Voss said. "Research archives. Twenty years old, sealed, probably degraded. Worth something to collectors, worth less to anyone else. That's all you need to know to do your jobs, and your jobs are diving, not diplomacy." He spread his hands, reasonable as daylight. "Mr. Okafor. You've been checking my gear all day. Have you found anything on it that suggests I'm lying to you?"

Dez held his gaze. "Your gear's clean. People usually aren't."

Voss laughed—a real laugh, warm, disarming—and some of the tension in the galley broke with it. That was the trick of Cassian Voss, Dez thought. He made suspicion feel like bad manners.

Odile cleared the chart away and stood. "0400. Sleep while you can. Dez has the first watch."

The crew filed out. Yara lingered last, gathering the coffee mugs the way she always did, never letting anyone wait on her, never quite able to accept being taken care of. At the door she paused.

"Dez."

He looked up.

"You checked the lift cable," she said. Not a question.

"Link by link."

"Good." Something moved behind her eyes—gratitude, maybe, or its difficult cousin. Then she was gone, and he heard her boots climb toward the bow cabin where she slept alone even when the boat was full.

* * *

First watch. Midnight water, the harbor breathing against the pilings, the Thessaly riding her lines with small patient sounds. Dez stood in the wheelhouse with the lights off and let his eyes adjust to the dark, and the dark gave him the fleet at anchor, the shore lights, the slow blink of the channel marker, and—far out, unchanged, unmoving—the gray shape of the patrol boat.

He raised the binoculars. Deck lights on her now, faint. A figure moving along her rail. Doing circles, maybe. Standing watch, the way he was standing watch. Two vessels keeping vigil on each other across four miles of black water, pretending not to.

Odile appeared beside him with two cups of tea, having materialized the way she always did when the boat needed her, silent as ballast. She handed him one and they drank together without speaking for a while.

"She mortgaged the Cormorant," Dez said at last. "Did you know?"

"I helped her carry the paperwork." Odile sipped her tea. "She asked me not to tell you until she'd told you herself."

"Why?"

"Because you'd have tried to talk her out of it. And she didn't want to be talked out of it." Odile set her cup on the console. "You know what she said to me at the bank? She said, 'A boat is just steel and wood until you give it to somebody.' Twenty-two years she kept that boat for herself. First thing she's ever spent it on is us."

Dez looked at the old woman beside him—at the deep seams of her face, the hands scarred from a lifetime of rope and rust—and felt something shift in his chest, heavy and warm, like ballast settling right.

On the horizon, the patrol boat's deck lights went out, one by one, until only her silhouette remained. Then, slowly, deliberately, she turned her bow toward the open sea—and did not leave. She simply changed the angle of her waiting.

"Dez," Odile said quietly. "What do you suppose they know that we don't?"

He lowered the binoculars. In the wheelhouse mirror he caught his own reflection, and behind it, faint through the aft windows, the shape of Yara's cabin light burning long past midnight—because of course she wasn't sleeping either, none of them were, the whole boat humming with the same held breath.

"They know enough to wait," he said. "That's what worries me. Waiting means they think we'll come to them."

At 0400, with the tide turning and the sky still black, the Thessaly slipped her lines and crept out past the breakwater into open water—toward the wreck, toward the watchers, toward whatever the Meridian had kept sealed for twenty years.
