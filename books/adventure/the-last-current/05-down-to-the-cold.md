## 5. Down to the Cold

The sea off the Kestrel Shoals was the color of hammered tin that morning, and it stayed that way all day, as if the water itself knew what lay beneath it and had gone dull with the knowledge.

Yara Halloran stood at the rail of the *Thessaly* in her dive skin, watching the sonar feed flicker on Dez's laptop through the wheelhouse window. The image was old news by now—they'd run the survey three times since anchoring—but she looked anyway, the way a person looks at a scar they've decided to stop pretending isn't there. Ninety meters down, lying on her port side in a shelf of grey silt, the hull of the *Meridian* made a shadow long enough to swallow their whole boat twice over.

"Water's four degrees at depth," Renn said behind her, checking his gauges for the third time in ten minutes. He was a careful diver, methodical to the point of ritual, and today his rituals were running long. "Bottom time's going to be brutal. Twenty minutes working, then an hour and change hanging on the line like bait."

"I know the tables, Renn."

"You know the tables." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "I just like saying them out loud. Helps me believe we're actually doing this."

Below them, ninety meters of cold held its breath.

* * *

They'd anchored over the site at dawn, and Voss had come up on deck before anyone else, standing at the bow in a coat too good for salt air, looking down at the water as though he could see straight through it. He hadn't said much. He never said much when there was something he wanted them not to ask about. He'd handed Yara the site chart himself—real paper, real ink, none of his usual digital sleight of hand—and pointed to a mark amidships.

"The vault is here," he'd said. "Or close to here. Everything else is yours. Anything you bring up outside that compartment, you keep or sell, I don't care. But the core comes to me intact."

"And if the core's not there?"

Voss had smiled the way a man smiles at a card game he's already won. "It's there."

Now Yara pulled her hood up and let Odile check her seals, the older woman's hands quick and impersonal, tugging the drysuit cuffs, testing the inflator hose, pressing two fingers hard against the valve at her shoulder the way she'd done before every dive for six years.

"Breathe," Odile said.

Yara breathed. Regulator clean, air tasting of rubber and cold.

"Heart?"

"Fine."

"Liar." Odile looked at her then, really looked, her face creased into something between a scowl and a blessing. "Twenty years you've kept away from this wreck. Your father made us all swear on his own deathbed, and now here we are, dropping a line straight onto its spine. If you want to turn this boat around, Yara, say it now. Nobody on this crew will think less of you."

For a moment Yara let herself feel the weight of it—the oath, the years, the shape of her father's hand raised over a kitchen table while rain came down sideways on the harbor, all of them with their palms flat on the wood because grief makes children of everyone. *We don't go near the Meridian,* he'd said. *Not for salvage, not for curiosity, not for anything. Some doors are closed for a reason.* She'd been nineteen. She'd sworn loudest of all.

"No," she said. "Take me down."

Odile nodded once, and that was that. On this boat, one nod carried more weight than any contract.

Dez clipped the descent line to the anchor chain and gave it three sharp tugs, testing. "Line's set. Bottom's at ninety-one meters on my mark, current's running half a knot south-southwest, visibility unknown." He paused, and behind the fogged glass of his mask his eyes found hers. "Comms will be garbage past forty meters. You're on your own down there, boss. You know that."

"That's why it's me going down."

Renn would be her backup, hanging at the thirty-meter stop, ready to descend if her line went slack or her lights went dark. It wasn't the buddy system anyone taught in certification courses. It was the system you used when the job mattered more than the safety manual, and Yara hated it, and she'd designed it herself.

She swung her legs over the gunwale, spat in her mask out of habit though the anti-fog gel had already done its work, and dropped backward into the sea.

* * *

The surface took her the way cold water always did first—a fist of chill against her face, then the muffled thunder of her own breathing, then nothing but blue-green light falling away beneath her fins.

She equalized at five meters, ten, fifteen, pinching her nose, feeling the pressure fold itself around her eardrums like a closing hand. The descent line ran straight down into a darkness that thickened by the meter. At twenty meters the sunlight above her turned from gold to pewter. At thirty, Renn's silhouette hung beside the line, arms crossed, a lantern in the gloom, and he gave her a slow thumbs-up that she returned without slowing.

At forty meters the color left the world entirely.

This was the part most divers feared, the moment the ocean stopped pretending to be a place for people. No blue, no green, just a grey-black murk that pressed in from all sides, and her breath loud as surf in her ears, and the only truth left the line in her fist and the numbers climbing on her console. Sixty meters. Seventy. Her suit squeezed her like a second skeleton. The helium mix in her tanks made her voice sound like a stranger's when she murmured her checks aloud, a habit she couldn't break: *gas, depth, time.* Gas, depth, time. A litany against the dark.

At eighty-five meters her light found the bottom.

And then it found the *Meridian*.

Yara had seen wrecks her whole life. She'd crawled through freighters torn open like tin cans, ferries resting on their bellies in fields of white sand, trawlers folded in half by storms. Wrecks had a grammar to them—tear, twist, settle, bury—and after enough dives you could read the story of a ship's dying in the way its bones lay scattered across the seafloor.

The *Meridian* broke every rule she knew.

She hovered above the hull, her exhaled bubbles streaming upward in silver ropes, and felt awe arrive first, dread sliding in behind it like a thief through an unlocked door.

The ship lay on her side, yes, settled into the silt the way great things settle, slowly and without apology. But the breach in her hull—there should have been a breach in her hull, some wound to explain the sinking, a reef gash or a torpedo seam or the ragged blossom of an internal explosion. Instead the plating was *opened*, peeled outward in long curled petals of steel, as if something inside the ship had wanted out. Not blown. Not torn. Opened. The edges of the metal were smooth where they curled back, almost delicate, and scorched black along the inner faces in patterns that didn't spread the way fire spreads. Fire goes up and out, hungry and random. These burns ran in lines. In rows. As if the flames had been organized.

Yara drifted closer, her light playing along the flank of the dead ship, and saw that the cargo cranes still stood rigged, the deck hatches still dogged shut, the mooring lines still coiled on their reels. A research vessel caught mid-voyage, preserved like a fly in amber—except for those opened seams, except for the scorch.

Her mother had walked these decks. Had slept in a bunk somewhere behind that plating, had eaten in a mess hall now crushed under ninety meters of ocean, had maybe stood at a porthole—she could see one, small and round, its glass clouded with twenty years of marine snow—and looked out at water exactly like this water, at light exactly like this failing light.

Yara's chest tightened around her regulator. She made herself breathe. Slow. Four counts in, six counts out. CO2 buildup made divers stupid, and stupid divers died, and she refused to die here, refused to give the *Meridian* anything else.

She swam to the breach.

* * *

Inside, the corridors were a cathedral of wrongness.

Her lights cut cones through water so still it seemed solid, and the beams fell on bulkheads warped and blackened, overhead cables drooping like burnt hair, insulation foam slumped in melted drifts against the deck plates. Every surface within reach of her torch wore the same organized scorching, the same impossible neatness. And yet—she swept her light down a passageway toward what had been the science labs, and there sat a rack of sample containers, glass still sealed, labels still legible under a skin of pale silt. There lay a toolbox, latched. There leaned a bicycle, of all absurd things, propped against a bulkhead as if its owner had only stepped away to check a gauge.

Fire had walked through this ship and taken nothing.

Yara had salvaged burned vessels before. Fire was a glutton; it consumed cloth and paper and plastic and left behind a landscape of ash and slag. Here, the paper charts in their wall tubes were intact. The corkboard in the passage junction still held photographs, curling but whole, faces of men and women smiling on docks and in mess halls, frozen mid-laugh under her beam. One of the faces snagged at her memory like a hook—a young woman, dark-haired, squinting into sun—and Yara's heart slammed once, hard, before she made herself look closer. Not her mother. Just a stranger. Just another ghost.

But her hands were shaking now, fine tremors in her fingertips that the cold couldn't fully explain.

She checked her console. Eleven minutes of bottom time spent. Nine remaining before she needed to start up the line. The vault compartment was amidships, two decks below the main corridor according to Voss's chart, and the chart had been right about everything else.

She finned down the sloping passageway, following the list of the ship, her bubbles rising crooked against the overhead, and the deeper she went into the vessel the more the silence changed. It wasn't empty silence. It was the silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking. She'd felt it before on wrecks—the presence of the drowned, call it what you liked, the weight of unfinished business soaked into steel—and she'd always been able to shake it off, to tell herself it was nitrogen narcosis, imagination, the mind's hunger for ghosts.

Not tonight. Tonight the silence had her name in it.

The corridor ended at a junction, and at the junction stood the door.

* * *

It was nothing like the rest of the ship.

Everything else aboard the *Meridian* bore the marks of catastrophe—scorch, warp, collapse, the honest chaos of disaster. This door was pristine. A circular pressure hatch, naval pattern, its paint unchipped, its wheel bright under her light as if it had been polished yesterday. The surrounding frame sat flush in the bulkhead with no gap, no corrosion bloom, no rust weeping from the hinges. Twenty years underwater, and the door looked *maintained*.

Stamped across its face, in stenciled block letters the navy used for unit designations, was a code Yara had known since before she could read properly. She'd seen it on her mother's uniform patches, on the letters that came home in government envelopes, on the single photograph her father kept in a drawer he never opened. She'd traced the characters with her finger a thousand times as a child, learning them the way other children learned their own names.

**MER-RD / UNIT 7 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Her mother's unit. Her mother's designation, stamped on a sealed vault at the bottom of the sea, on a ship the navy had spent two decades pretending had never mattered.

Yara hung motionless in the black water, her bubbles the only movement in the world, and felt the last of her awe curdle into something colder and older. Dread, yes—but underneath the dread, a terrible clarity, the kind that arrives when a suspicion you've carried so long it's become part of your body finally puts on a face.

Her father hadn't sworn them off this wreck because it was cursed. Curses were for sailors who needed stories instead of answers. He'd sworn them off because he'd known—or suspected, or been told in words he couldn't repeat—that what sank with the *Meridian* wasn't cargo. It was proof. And whatever Unit 7 had done behind this door, it had been worth killing a crew, worth burning a ship from the inside out, worth twenty years of navy silence and a widow's grave and a daughter who couldn't stay anywhere longer than a season.

The door's wheel gleamed. Beyond it, she knew without knowing how, waited everything she'd been running from.

Her console chirped the low-tone warning: bottom time expiring. Nine minutes gone. The ascent would take over an hour, hanging in the cold at staged depths, her blood slowly giving back the pressure it had borrowed. She had no cutting gear rated for this hatch, no time to work it even if she had. Voss's plan called for the heavy lift gear tomorrow, the hydraulic ram, the controlled entry.

But her hand rose anyway, gloved fingers settling on the wheel's cold spokes, and for one long breath she imagined turning it. Just to see. Just to know.

From somewhere beyond the door—impossibly, through steel and silt and twenty years of dark—came a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. A soft metallic tapping, three beats, pause, three beats again, like a signal, like a greeting, like a knock.

Yara snatched her hand back. Her light swept the hatch, the frame, the flawless painted steel. Nothing moved. The tapping did not come again.

Above her, far above, the surface waited with its questions, and Renn waited at his stop with his lantern, and the *Thessaly* rode at anchor over the spine of the dead ship while somewhere beyond the horizon a navy patrol boat turned its bow toward waters it had no business patrolling.

Yara backed away from the door, fin-stroke by fin-stroke, keeping her light on it until the last possible moment, until the darkness folded over the stamped letters of her mother's unit like a hand closing a book.

Then she turned for the line, and began the long climb back into the world of the living—carrying with her, in the flooded cathedral of her chest, the echo of three taps, and the certainty that whatever waited behind that door had been waiting for her.
