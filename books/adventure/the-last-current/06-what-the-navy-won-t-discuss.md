## 6. What the Navy Won't Discuss

Cassian Voss had given a great many briefings in his life, and he had learned long ago that the secret to lying well was not to lie at all. It was to tell the truth in the wrong order, to arrange facts like ballast stones so that the vessel of the story sat low and steady in the water, riding exactly where you wanted it. Truth was cheap. He had crates of it. What cost money was knowing which truths to stack where.

He stood at the head of the Thessaly's mess table with his hands flat on the scarred wood and let them look at him.

They were a good audience, he'd give them that much. Odile Marchand with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a cleat taking strain. Dez Okafor perched on the bench nearest the door, because Dez always sat nearest the door, a habit Voss recognized and respected—men who watched exits were men who had needed them. Renn slouched against the bulkhead with an ice pack wrapped around one knee, watching Voss the way a man watches weather coming over a headland. And Yara Halloran at the far end of the table, apart from the others, not quite touching her coffee, looking at Voss with those grey eyes that had never once in twenty years given him anything for free.

The mess smelled of diesel and old coffee and the particular salt-stiffness of canvas that had been wet too many times. Through the porthole behind him, the harbor lay flat as poured pewter under a high white sky. Somewhere out past the breakwater, he knew, a patrol boat idled with its engines warm. He was counting on it being there. He just wasn't going to mention it yet.

"Thank you all for coming," he said, and Odile snorted.

"We're on your boat's payroll, Voss. We didn't come. We were contracted."

"You came to hear why the contract tripled." He smiled, thin and professional. "So I'll tell you."

He opened the waterproof case he'd carried aboard and turned it toward them. Inside, nested in cut foam, lay a facsimile of the object: a cylinder the size of a wine bottle, matte black, unmarked except for a stenciled serial number and the small cold authority of a government stamp. Not the real thing. The real thing sat ninety meters down inside a vault on a dead ship, and Voss intended it to stay there until it was in his hands and no one else's.

"This is a hardened data archive," he said. "Naval designation, but the navy will deny it exists, which is why they've spent twenty years pretending the Meridian sank empty." He tapped the foam beside the replica. "Research vessels carry black boxes the same way aircraft do. Everything. Sensor logs, communications, internal recordings, experimental data. When she went down, the archive went down with her. Sealed. Rated to survive pressure, fire, seawater, and time."

Dez leaned forward. "And the navy's spent two decades not recovering it?"

"The navy has spent two decades recovering it," Voss said pleasantly. "Unsuccessfully. The vault sits in a section of the hull that collapsed during sinking. Every approach they've tried has failed. They've burned through three salvage contracts and buried every report." He let that hang in the air a moment. "I have the survey work from the last attempt. Their divers got within four meters of the vault door before a structural shift nearly killed two of them. The program was shelved. Officially, the Meridian carries nothing worth the risk."

"And unofficially?" Yara asked.

Her voice did something to the room. It always had. Voss felt the old familiar irritation rise in him—the way she could ask a single question and make everyone else feel like they'd been talking too loud—and he mastered it, the way he always did, and turned it into charm.

"Unofficially," he said, "the archive contains research data from a classified materials program. Alloy testing. Hull compounds. Deep-pressure metallurgy." He watched their faces as he laid it out. "That kind of technology doesn't age. A breakthrough from twenty years ago is still a breakthrough. There are buyers—legitimate ones, industrial concerns—who would pay a great deal for it, and more importantly, there are licensing structures that make recovery legal if the material is classified as abandoned commercial property rather than military asset. My lawyers have been very thorough."

It was a beautiful lie. Ninety percent true, which was the correct ratio. The archive did exist. The vault was real. The navy really had failed to reach it and really had buried the attempts. The metallurgy program was even real—it simply wasn't the *point* of the program, any more than a scalpel is the point of surgery. But the crew didn't need the point. They needed the shape of a reason that would hold their weight, and this one would.

Renn shifted against the bulkhead. "You're saying we dive into a collapsed wreck section the navy itself gave up on."

"I'm saying the navy gave up because they sent contractors. Contractors follow procedures. Procedures are written by people who've never been inside that hull." He looked around the table. "Your people have been inside wrecks the navy wouldn't chart. That's what I'm paying for. Not muscle. Judgment."

Odile uncrossed her arms slowly. "And the buyer. This industrial concern. They know the site coordinates?"

"They'll know the product when I deliver it. Not before." He met her eyes steadily. "I don't sell locations, Captain. I sell results. If this goes wrong, no one traces back to your boat through me. I've structured it that way deliberately."

*Deliberately,* he thought, and enjoyed the word. He had structured everything deliberately. The shell company in Malta. The insurance filings. The survey permits purchased through a marine archaeology consultancy that existed mostly on paper and entirely on his signature. Twenty years of patience, of building credit and cover and quiet influence, all of it converging now on one sealed cylinder in one drowned corridor. People called him ruthless. He found that lazy. Ruthless men broke things. Voss preferred to *arrange* things, patiently, until breaking became unnecessary.

What he did not say—what he would never say at this table—was that the archive contained no metallurgy worth the name. It contained the Meridian's own record of what had been done to its crew. Every session. Every subject. Every result, logged in the dispassionate language of men who had learned to write human beings down as line items. Including, though Voss had only confirmed this recently, through channels that had cost him more than money—

Including Subject 14.

He kept his face still. Across the table, Yara Halloran was studying him with the particular attention she reserved for things she intended to take apart later. She had that look even at nineteen, he remembered, the first time they'd competed for the same wreck off Cape Serrat, when she'd beaten him by three hours and then had the decency to look sorry about it. He had never decided whether the decency made it better or worse.

"You knew my mother," Yara said suddenly.

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone drops something glass.

Voss let the silence sit for exactly as long as it served him. "Everyone in that fleet knew of Dr. Elin Halloran. Her name was on half the published papers in deep-sea sensor design." He shrugged, easy. "I never met her. I was a teenager when the Meridian went down."

All true. All beside the point. He watched Yara watch him, and he understood that she did not believe him, and he understood further that it did not matter whether she believed him, because she would dive anyway. Grief was the strongest mooring line there was. You could anchor anything to it.

"Then why hire *me*?" she said. "Of every salvage diver on this coast. Why the one woman with a personal connection to the wreck?"

"Because you're the best," Voss said. "And because I need someone who won't quit at eighty meters when it gets ugly. Sentiment makes divers careless, Ms. Halloran. I'm counting on yours staying exactly where it belongs—ashore." He closed the case with a soft, expensive click. "We dive in forty-eight hours. Weather window holds for six days after that. Questions can come to Odile; she's coordinating."

* * *

He stayed aboard through the afternoon, ostensibly reviewing the survey grid with Odile in the wheelhouse, actually doing what he always did: listening. Ships talked. Crews talked. A man who kept his mouth shut and his ears open could learn more in one galley conversation than in a week of contracts.

He learned that Dez didn't trust him and had told Renn so within earshot of the companionway. He learned that Renn's knee was worse than the ice pack admitted and that Renn had said nothing because the crew couldn't afford another body off the roster. He learned that Odile had mortgaged something—her tone when she mentioned the bank had the specific flatness of a woman discussing a wound—and that she hadn't told Yara yet.

And he learned, standing in the wheelhouse doorway while Odile plotted waypoints, that Yara Halloran slept badly and walked the deck at night, and that when she passed the chart of the Meridian site pinned above the nav station, she stopped. Every time. Just for a second. Just long enough.

Voss filed all of it away. Information was ballast too.

At 1612, the helicopter came.

It came in low off the water from the northeast, a navy grey bird with its running lights off in daylight, and it crossed the Thessaly's stern at perhaps two hundred meters altitude—close enough that the rotor wash shivered the awning over the aft deck, close enough that Voss, standing at the rail, could see the observer's helmet turn toward the ship. Then it climbed, banked wide, and pulled away south without transmitting a word.

Nobody aboard said anything for a full minute. Then Dez said something profane and short, and Odile picked up the radio and called the harbor office, and the harbor office said, yes, they'd seen it, no, they had no flight plan on file, probably a routine transit.

"Routine transits don't fly dark over working vessels," Dez said.

"No," Voss agreed mildly. "They don't."

He felt it the way he imagined other men felt music—a clean rising note of satisfaction. The helicopter meant the navy had noticed the Thessaly's survey permit. Noticed, assessed, and chosen to *watch* rather than act, which meant they were not certain what he had, which meant his cover was holding. A second overflight at dusk confirmed it: same approach, same altitude, slower this time, lingering. Counting antennas. Photographing the deck. Building a file.

Let them build it. Files were how bureaucracies thought, and bureaucracies thought in quarters and fiscal years. By the time anyone upstairs connected Voss Marine Recovery to the Meridian's coordinates, the archive would be in a crate with a Maltese shipping label, and the truth would be somebody else's problem at somebody else's price.

He stood at the rail after dark and watched the Thessaly's riding lights double themselves in the black water, and he allowed himself, briefly, the pleasure of the whole design laid out in his mind: the vault, the core, the buyer in Zurich who believed he was purchasing a miracle of materials science; the fixers ashore who believed they were managing a nuisance; the crew of this battered little ship who believed they were diving for alloy data and a payday. Everyone holding a piece of the board, and only Voss seeing the whole of it.

Only—not quite only. He thought of Yara asleep somewhere below decks, or not asleep, walking the deck past that chart. He thought of Subject 14, whose service number he had memorized the way other men memorize the dates of their worst days. He thought of his brother, and put the thought away in the room inside himself where he kept such things, locked, unlit, visited never.

Almost never.

Tomorrow they would run the first descent. Survey pass only, hull exterior, no penetration—he'd insisted on it, both for safety theater and because he wanted the navy's next overflight to see a cautious, legitimate operation. Caution was camouflage. He'd built a career on it.

Below him, faintly, through the deck plates, he heard footsteps stop. He didn't need to look at the chart to know where.

Yara Halloran was going to be a problem. He had known it since the harbor bar, since the moment she'd refused his contract and meant it, which almost nobody did. Problems were manageable. What interested him was that she might also be useful—because a woman diving for her mother's ghost would go places procedure would never permit, and Voss intended to be standing behind her when she did.

He finished his drink, set the glass down without a sound, and went below to sleep the sleep of a man whose ledger, at last, was about to balance.
