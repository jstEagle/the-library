## 7. The Inside Job

The sea at ninety meters did not forgive you for coming back.

Yara felt that truth in her teeth before she felt it anywhere else—the pressure of the second descent pressing down through the Thessaly's hull, through her suit, into the marrow of her, as if the water remembered her from yesterday and resented the return. She hung on the descent line and watched the Meridian rise out of the dark to meet her, gray-green and vast, her superstructure furred with twenty years of sediment, her broken decks holding their secrets the way a drowned man holds his last breath.

"Bottom in four minutes," Renn said over the comm, his voice flattened by the helium mix into something almost childlike. "Stay with me, Halloran."

"I'm not the one who wanders," she said.

"You wandered yesterday. Right off my shoulder light. I counted."

He was right, and they both knew it. Yesterday, on the exterior survey, she had drifted eleven meters off the transect line without noticing—drawn along the hull like something on a wire—until her own dive computer's alarm had shocked her back. Dez had logged it as narcosis. Yara knew better. The wreck pulled. It pulled the way a wound pulls a tongue.

* * *

They entered through the breach.

It was the thing about the Meridian no one who hadn't seen it could understand: the hole in her side was not blast damage. Blast tore outward, peeled metal like fruit skin, left petals of steel bent toward the sea. This breach opened inward. The edges of the plating curled into the ship, smooth as if the explosion had been poured rather than detonated, and the corridor beyond was scorched in a way that made no chemical sense—burn patterns running against the airflow, flame shadows printed on bulkheads where no fire should have reached.

Yara moved through it with her lights cutting twin cones through the silt-hung water, and the ship received them the way a throat receives a swallowed thing.

The corridors were narrow. Naval research vessels were built for function, not comfort, and the Meridian's interior passages were barely wide enough for two divers abreast with gear. Their exhaled bubbles ran along the overhead in silver rivers, seeking escape, finding none. Somewhere forward, the ship groaned—a long, structural complaint, the sound of a body shifting in sleep.

"Deck plan says lab section is two compartments port of the central stair," Renn said. "Voss wants anything paper. Logs, drives, hard copy."

"Voss wants what Voss paid for."

"And what we want?"

Yara didn't answer. What she wanted was a word she hadn't let herself say in twenty years, and saying it inside this ship felt like speaking it into an ear.

* * *

The lab compartment had been sealed by its own destruction. A pressure door stood half-closed across the passage, jammed on its track, and beyond it the water was darker—not just unlit but *thick*, the silt so fine and settled that their lights seemed to lose conviction a meter out. Yara went through first, sideways, the door frame scraping her tank, and for one disorienting moment she was weightless in a black room full of floating ghosts: sheets of paper suspended everywhere, hundreds of them, turning slowly in the still water like a snowfall that had forgotten how to land.

"Oh," Renn said softly. "Oh, that's not right."

Paper didn't survive twenty years submerged unless it was preserved. Unless it had been kept dry, sealed, protected—and then released all at once. Yara swept her light across the room and understood: the compartment had been flooded recently. Days? Weeks? Someone had vented it deliberately, or a seal had failed on its own schedule, and everything the navy had locked away in here was now drifting free for anyone to read.

She caught a page out of the water. Heavy stock, laser-printed, the ink barely faded. A duty roster. Names, watch rotations, a date twenty years old. She let it go and took another. Equipment calibration records. Another. A memorandum marked with a classification banner she recognized and had spent her whole adult life pretending she didn't: the same designation stamped on the vault door below, the same unit code her mother had served under.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, something was screaming in a register too high to hear.

"Renn. Bag everything that isn't silted down. I'm taking the office."

The compartment's small administrative alcove held a desk bolted to the deck, and on the desk, weighted under a glass paperweight furred with bacterial mat, lay a logbook. Not a printout. A real book, canvas-bound, the kind naval officers still kept because regulations demanded redundancy. Yara lifted it with both hands, gentle as lifting a bird, and worked it into the sample bag Dez had rigged with a waterproof liner.

Through the bag's plastic she could see the cover stamp. *MERIDIAN — OFFICER OF THE DAY LOG.* And beneath it, in faded ink, a service number.

She knew that number.

Not because she'd memorized it. Because she'd grown up hearing her father say it—muttering it into his beer, writing it on the backs of envelopes, saying it to men on porches who came asking questions and got doors closed in their faces. Her mother's service number had been the only headstone the family ever had.

"Yara." Renn's voice, careful. "You've gone quiet."

"I found something."

"How something?"

"The kind we don't open down here."

* * *

They were forty minutes into a planned seventy-minute bottom time when Renn stopped answering properly.

It started small. His responses stretched half a second too long, then a full second, the helium voice lagging like a bad phone connection. Then he said, "Hey, Yara, did you ever notice how the bubbles go up but we don't?" and laughed at nothing, and her stomach dropped through the deck plates.

"Renn. Say your gauge readings."

"Gauges are—" A pause. Water moving. "Gauges are fine. Everything's fine. It's so quiet in here. Isn't it quiet? Like the ship turned the volume down."

*The quieting.*

The words arrived in her mind fully formed, in her mother's handwriting she hadn't even seen yet, and she would remember afterward how that felt—to be haunted by a sentence before she'd read it.

"Listen to me." She grabbed his harness, turned him, put her light directly in his mask so the beam filled his vision. "You're narced. We're going up. Now. You follow my line, you do exactly what I do, and you don't stop swimming. Nod if you copy."

He nodded. His eyes behind the mask were wide and pleased, a man delighted by a wonderful secret, and that frightened her more than panic would have. Panic fights you. Euphoria lets go.

The ascent was the longest hour of her life.

They climbed the line through water that darkened and warmed by degrees, and Renn swam when she pulled him and stopped when she stopped and once, horribly, began to ascend past her, fast, arms windmilling, bubbles streaming, until she caught his ankle and hauled him down and wrapped the guide line around his wrist and held it there. At the thirty-meter mark he started humming. At twenty he tried to remove his regulator to say something, and she pinned his hand to his chest harness and held it through three full minutes of decompression while he looked at her with mild, drunken reproach.

At ten meters, he convulsed.

Not narcosis now—the deep hangover arriving late, nitrogen boiling out of his blood in the wrong places, in the joints and the spine and maybe, God forbid, the brain. He jackknifed around the line, knees to chest, and his scream came through the comm as pure distortion, a sound with no words in it, and Yara held him against the water's insistence and screamed into her own mouthpiece for the surface crew and counted seconds and hated herself with a completeness she had not felt since the day her father died.

Because she had pushed. Because she'd said *one more compartment, five more minutes,* and Renn—who never argued with anyone, who folded himself around other people's needs like a man making his body smaller in a crowded room—had said okay.

The safety diver met them at six meters. Hands took Renn from her. The world above resolved into the Thessaly's shadow, the ladder, the deck, the rain-soft gray sky, and then Renn was on the deck convulsing while Odile knelt on him with the oxygen mask and Dez shouted into the radio for the chamber coordinates and Voss stood apart with his hands in his pockets, watching, his face doing nothing at all.

"He'll live," the medic said later, or someone said it; Yara couldn't remember who. Renn was in the portable chamber by then, cycling through a treatment table, and through the little viewport his face looked bruised and ancient and fifteen years old all at once.

Yara sat on the deck beside the chamber with her suit half-peeled and her hands finally, violently shaking, and pressed the sample bag between her palms like something that might need CPR.

Inside, through the plastic, her mother's service number looked back at her.

* * *

They opened the logbook that night in the galley, because waiting was impossible, because some doors only get heavier.

Odile cleared the table. Dez taped plastic sheeting over the porthole so no lens, no drone, no passing trawler could catch the light of what they were doing. Voss watched from the doorway with his arms crossed, and Yara let him watch, because the book belonged to the dead and the dead deserved witnesses.

The early entries were ordinary. Meals served, drills run, a card game dispute arbitrated. The Meridian's voice came off the pages—dry, dutiful, occasionally funny in the way institutional humor always is, jokes that only make sense to people trapped together. Yara read names. She made herself read every name, because those men and women had been somebody's whole world, and the sea had taken their story and the navy had taken their file, and someone aboard this boat owed them readers.

Then, six weeks before the sinking, the handwriting changed.

It loosened. Sped up. The entries grew longer, private, written in the margins where the official form ended—as if the officer of the day had begun using the log as a diary, regulations be damned. Yara knew the handwriting the way you know a face in a crowd you've been avoiding for years. She had seen it on grocery lists, on birthday cards, on the chalkboard menu of a kitchen that smelled of garlic and salt. Twenty years had not worn it away. Twenty years had only made it a language she could still read but no longer speak.

She read aloud, because her voice was the steadiest thing available, and because Odile's hand had found her shoulder and stayed there.

*"Day 41. Third session today. They call it 'the quieting,' which is the kindest name anyone's put on it yet. The subjects come out calm. That's the word everyone uses—calm, calm, calm—as if calm were the goal and not the symptom. Marlow came out of the tank smiling and didn't know his daughter's name. Didn't know he had a daughter. He wasn't distressed about it. That's the part I can't write fast enough: he wasn't distressed. They took a man's whole life and left him comfortable in the ruins."*

Yara's voice held. Barely.

*"Day 44. Command is pleased. The trial works better than designed—that phrase keeps appearing in the briefings, 'better than designed,' and no one asks the obvious question, which is: better for whom? The erasure doesn't fade. Day one subjects are identical to day nine subjects. Whatever the process takes, it takes permanently, unless—"*

The entry broke off there. The next day's entry resumed in the same hand but different ink, hurried:

*"Day 45. I volunteered. If it can rewrite a person, it can rewrite a person back. Someone has to prove the damage reverses. I told Reyes I'd keep a record in my head either way—if I forget myself, this book will remember for me. If you're reading this and I don't know your face, I was still me. Remember that. I was still me the whole time."*

The galley was silent except for the tick of the cooling engine and the sea working the hull.

Yara set the book down with both hands, carefully, the way you set down something sleeping. Her mother had volunteered. Had walked into the tank on purpose, to prove it could be undone, and had kept a diary against the day she might not know her own name—and the last entry cut off mid-sentence, mid-word, the ink dragging away in a thinning line like a pulse flattening out.

Below the final entry, stamped in red across the page, was a single word in a clerk's efficient hand:

*TRANSFERRED.*

"Yara." Voss, from the doorway. His voice had lost its performance. What remained was quieter and worse. "There's a page missing. Torn out at the binding. Whoever pulled it wanted it gone before the ship went down."

Odile looked at Dez. Dez looked at the plastic-sheeted porthole, beyond which the navy patrol boat had been idling, visible and patient, since morning.

"They know we have it," Dez said. "They've known since we surfaced."

Yara touched the torn stub of the missing page, and beneath her fingers the paper felt warm, though she knew it was her own heat she was feeling, her own blood arriving at last in her hands.

Somewhere out in the dark, a helicopter passed low over the water, its rotor beat arriving a heartbeat after its running lights, and nobody aboard the Thessaly said a word.
