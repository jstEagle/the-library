## 8. Logbook of the Quieting

The galley smelled of burnt coffee and salt-damp wool, and Odile Marchand had been awake long enough to forget which night it was.

She sat at the chart table with the logbook open in front of her, a lamp pulled low, the Thessaly creaking around her like an old man turning in his sleep. Outside, the sea worked against the hull in slow, patient shoves. Somewhere above, Dez was on watch, his boots thudding across the deck every few minutes—a rhythm she'd stopped hearing hours ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Yara had gone down at 0200. Odile had heard her go—no pretense of sleep, just the soft click of the cabin door and then nothing. That was Yara. She moved through the world like someone who expected to be followed.

The logbook did not move at all. It sat on the table like something dead that hadn't finished dying.

It had come up in Renn's mesh bag along with a corroded flashlight and a fistful of silt, and when they'd opened it on deck, seawater had run out of the binding like blood from a wound. The pages were fused in places, welded into brick by two decades of pressure and brine. But not all of them. Some had survived, and those pages were written in a small, disciplined hand that Odile now knew better than she knew her own mother's—which was to say, not at all. Her own mother had died when she was nine, and what remained was a photograph and a smell she couldn't name anymore.

She understood, then, why Yara had looked the way she'd looked on deck. Why she'd handed the book over without a word and walked to the rail and stood there until dawn started thinking about arriving.

Because this handwriting belonged to a woman named Maren Halloran, service number 4471-KD, and Odile had known that number since she was nineteen years old, since the first night Yara ever got drunk on the Thessaly and said it out loud like a confession: *My mother's number. If anyone ever asks, that's how you'll know her.*

* * *

Odile poured more coffee and bent back over the pages.

Deciphering was slow work. The ink had faded unevenly—some entries legible as print, others reduced to ghosts, brown shadows of words that had once been certain of themselves. She used a jeweler's loupe Dez kept for inspecting salvage marks, tilting each page under the lamp to catch the light where the pen had pressed hardest. Where the nib had dug in, the impression survived even when the ink didn't. She learned to read by touch as much as sight, running a fingertip over the grooves like a blind woman reading a face.

The early entries were ordinary. That was the thing that unsettled her most—not horror, but the absence of it. Supply requisitions. Equipment checks. A complaint about the mess cook's fish stew. A note that Ensign Duval had sprained his wrist in the gym and was on light duty. The Meridian, in these pages, was just a ship: ninety meters of navy steel doing navy things, its crew bored and human and alive.

*Day 41. Morale holding. Dr. Ashe's team has taken over Compartment C-3 for "acoustic calibration." Loud all night. Duval says he can feel it in his teeth.*

*Day 44. Calibration continues. Men complaining of headaches. Ashe says it's normal, adjustment period.*

*Day 47. Requested clarification on scope of trials from Command. Response: proceed as authorized.*

Odile read that last line three times. *Proceed as authorized.* Four words carrying the weight of everything that came after, the way a single rivet carries the weight of a hull plate.

She turned the page with a pair of tweezers. The paper crackled, dry as insect wings.

*Day 52. First full trial today. Twelve volunteers from engineering. They were told it was a sleep study. I watched through the observation glass. There was no pain. That is the worst part. There was no pain at all. They laughed afterward. Asked when they could do it again.*

Beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to score the next page:

*I asked Pvt. Okafor what he remembered of the morning. He smiled at me and said, "What morning?"*

The galley seemed to get colder. Odile realized she'd stopped breathing somewhere in the last sentence and made herself start again. On watch above, Dez's boots crossed the deck. The sea shoved the hull. The lamp hummed.

She thought about waking Yara. Her hand actually went to the table edge, ready to push herself up—and stopped. Because what would she say? *Come look at what your mother wrote? Come see the moment she watched twelve men lose themselves and called it the worst part, meaning the mercy of it, meaning she already knew which side of the glass she'd end up on?*

No. Some things you had to read alone before you could survive reading them twice.

* * *

Toward the middle of the book, the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically—the same slant, the same economy—but tighter, faster, the letters leaning forward like a runner. Whatever discipline had governed the early entries was eroding, page by page, the way a coastline erodes: imperceptibly day to day, catastrophically across a season.

*Day 63. Third trial group. Six men this time. One of them, Lt. Ferris, began crying during the procedure and could not say why. Afterward he asked me if his wife had been to visit. He has no wife. He invented one between the chair and the corridor. Ashe calls this "confabulation." He says it's temporary. He has no data to support this. He has no data at all. We are all of us flying blind into weather we cannot see.*

*Day 66. I have begun keeping this record in duplicate. This copy stays with my personal effects. The other goes where they will never think to look. If you are reading this, then either I am ashore or I am not, and either way, the numbers matter more than the words. Count the men. Count the days. Count who signed the authorizations. Numbers cannot be quieted.*

*Numbers cannot be quieted.* Odile mouthed the phrase silently, testing it. It sounded like something Maren Halloran would have said. It sounded like something Yara would have said, if Yara ever said anything at full volume instead of in glances and departures.

Because that was the thing Odile had understood about her captain years ago, back when the Thessaly was young and so were they: Yara Halloran didn't run from places. She ran from being *known*. Every port was a fresh set of strangers who couldn't connect her to the last one. Every season a new ledger with no history in it. Odile had always assumed it was temperament. Restlessness, wanderlust, whatever pretty word you wanted to put on it.

Now, holding a dead woman's handwriting in a cold galley, she understood it was inheritance. Yara wasn't running from being found. She was running from being *erased*—from waking up one morning as someone else's invention, a woman with a husband who didn't exist, asking strangers what morning they meant.

And she'd been doing it for twenty years without knowing why.

* * *

The last third of the logbook was the worst, because it was the most coherent.

Whatever chaos had gripped the middle weeks—the headaches, the confabulations, the men who cried without knowing why—had been resolved. Not ended. *Resolved*, the way a problem gets resolved in a report. The entries became shorter, cleaner, almost serene. Odile found herself missing the desperate scrawl. At least desperation was honest.

*Day 78. Trial parameters revised. Subject retention window extended to 90 days post-procedure. Full identity rewrite confirmed stable. Subjects report high life satisfaction. Two have requested reassignment to new ports under new names. Request granted. They shook my hand. One of them thanked me.*

*Day 81. I have requested to be added to the trial roster. Command is pleased. I told them I wanted to understand the procedure from inside. That is part of the truth. The other part is that I need to know whether it can be undone. Someone has to prove reversibility or every man on this ship is lost forever, and I will not be the one who let that happen while writing warnings in a book nobody will read.*

There it was. Odile set down the tweezers and pressed both palms flat against the chart table, grounding herself against the swell of nausea and something else, something hotter underneath it.

Hope. That was the hot thing. Hope arriving disguised as horror, wearing horror's clothes, speaking in horror's voice—because the only proof that Maren Halloran might have survived as *Maren Halloran* was buried in the same pages that proved she'd volunteered to have herself unmade.

Yara had spent twenty years grieving a body that was never recovered. And the body had never been recovered because there might have been nothing left to recover. Not death. Worse. A woman walking around somewhere with a new name and borrowed memories, laughing at jokes she didn't remember learning, maybe inventing a husband between the chair and the corridor.

Or—God, or—fighting her way back. Day 81 said it plain: *I need to know whether it can be undone.* Which meant Maren had believed it could be. Had bet her self on it.

*Day 84. The quieting works. God forgive us, it works beautifully. Ashe's team is calling it a success. I am calling it what it is: we have built a machine that murders people and leaves the bodies walking around, happy.*

*Final entry. Transfer list approved tonight. Surviving subjects to be relocated ashore under new identities, records sealed under Directive 9-C. My number is on the list. So are eleven others. If I do not reverse this within the year, burn this book. If I do—then whoever finds this, find me. I'll be the one who remembers.*

The entry stopped there. No signature flourish, no date beyond the ship's count. Just *I'll be the one who remembers*, and then blank paper, and then the fused brick of pages that had been underwater for twenty years, holding whatever came next the way the sea holds everything—tightly, and without any intention of giving it up.

Odile sat back. The coffee had gone cold hours ago; she drank it anyway, tasting nothing.

*Find me. I'll be the one who remembers.*

Eleven others on the transfer list. Her mother's number among them. And somewhere in the vault below ninety meters of cold water, stamped with unit designation 4471-KD, a sealed door that the navy had spent two decades pretending didn't exist.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin through the galley porthole, the color of old gauze. Odile heard the deck hatch open and close, boots on the ladder, and then Yara filled the doorway—hair still damp from a shower she'd clearly taken to avoid sleeping, eyes red-rimmed but steady.

She looked at the logbook. Then at Odile. Then at the loupe, the tweezers, the cold coffee, the whole archaeology of the night laid out on the table.

"You read it," Yara said. Not a question.

"Every word I could lift off the pages." Odile turned the book around slowly, so it faced her captain, and tapped the final entry with one finger. "All of it."

Yara didn't sit. She stood in the doorway with the gray light behind her, and Odile watched her read—or pretend to read, or simply rest her eyes on the place where her mother's handwriting said *find me*. The Thessaly rolled on a long swell. Somewhere below them, ninety meters down, the Meridian held its silence.

"Her number's on the transfer list," Odile said quietly. "Survivors relocated ashore. New identities. Records sealed." She paused. "Yara. Your mother may have walked off that program alive."

Something moved through Yara's face—too fast and too many things at once to name. Grief and hope tearing at each other like currents meeting, neither willing to yield. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman handling live ordnance.

"The vault," she said. "Unit designation 4471-KD. That's her personal effects locker. Her duplicate records. Whatever she hid where they'd never think to look."

"And Voss wants the core inside it," Odile said. "To sell."

"To sell." Yara's jaw tightened. "Her testimony. Her proof. Eleven survivors' names. Everything she bled onto these pages to preserve—and he wants to price it by the kilo."

Odile rose, joints stiff from the night's vigil. "Then we have a decision to make before he makes it for us. Because there's something else." She hesitated, hating the words even as she lined them up. "Dez checked the radar at 0400. There's a navy vessel holding station forty kilometers east. Been there since midnight. Not moving. Not transmitting. Just... waiting."

Yara looked at the logbook a long moment. Then she reached out and closed it, gently, the way you close a coffin—or a door you intend to come back to.

"Wake the crew," she said. "We dive again tomorrow. And this time, we're not coming up empty-handed."

Behind her, through the porthole, the eastern horizon stayed empty and patient, the way a held breath is empty and patient—and forty kilometers out, something waited with them.
