## 9. Visitors

I heard the engine before I saw the boat—a flat, patient thrum coming out of the northeast, the kind of sound that doesn't belong to anyone in a hurry. I was on deck with a mug of coffee going cold in my hands, running the morning's sonar sweep through my head for the fourth time, and when I turned toward the sound I felt it settle into my chest like a swallowed stone.

Gray hull. No markings I could read at that distance. Running lights off in full daylight, which meant either sloppiness or confidence, and boats like that one were never sloppy.

"Odile," I called down the companionway, keeping my voice level the way you do when you don't want to spook anything—fish, crew, or yourself. "Company."

She came up the ladder fast, hair still wet from the head, and stood beside me at the rail squinting into the glare. For a long moment she said nothing. Then: "Coast guard?"

"That's what they'll say."

The Thessaly swung at anchor over the survey grid, cables humming, the Meridian lying ninety meters beneath our keel like a held breath. We'd been on station six days. Six days of careful, methodical work, of Voss pacing the aft deck with his charts and his quiet arithmetic, of Renn's shoulder still wrapped from the bends hit, of Yara sleeping four hours a night and diving like a woman paying off a debt. Six days of feeling watched without being able to prove it.

Now we were being watched up close.

The boat closed the distance without hurry. Two men aboard, maybe three—I counted twice and got two both times, which bothered me more than three would have. They wore civilian windbreakers over what I'd have bet my share of the salvage was military-issue everything else. The man at the helm brought them alongside with an ease that said he'd done it a thousand times in worse water than this.

"Thessaly," he called across the gap. Not a question. "Coast guard. Routine inspection. Requesting permission to come aboard."

Odile looked at me. I looked at Odile. Somewhere below us, sealed in a steel sphere at the end of a cable, Yara was descending toward a vault the United States Navy had spent twenty years pretending didn't exist.

"Permission granted," Odile said, because there was no honest answer that wasn't also a bad one.

* * *

They came up the pilot ladder like they owned it. The talker was tall, fortyish, sandy-haired gone gray at the temples, with a smile that reached his mouth and stopped well short of his eyes. His partner was shorter, darker, silent, and spent his first minute aboard doing something that made the skin crawl on the back of my neck: he looked at everything and touched nothing, cataloguing. Winch. Dive shack. Compressor lines. The decompression chamber bolted to the deckhouse like a scar. He looked at Renn's shoulder brace longer than was polite and wrote nothing down, which somehow made it worse.

"Cutter's tied up at Port Anselm," the tall one said, producing credentials from an inside pocket so smoothly I never actually saw them. "Delgado. This is Marsh. We're doing compliance checks on commercial survey operations in the corridor." He smiled again. "You're the Thessaly out of Harrow Point. Odile Marchand, master. Crew of five including your salvage consultant, Mr. Voss."

Not a question. None of it was questions yet.

"That's us," Odile said. She had her captain's voice on, the flat one she used in weather. "What can I do for you, Inspector Delgado?"

"Well." He strolled the deck the way a man strolls a garden he's thinking of buying. "We've been reviewing survey activity in this sector. There's been some interest in your grid pattern." He stopped at the chart table under the awning, where last night's plotting still lay pinned under brass weights—the Meridian's coordinates ringed in red grease pencil, the search lanes fanning out from her like spokes. "Interesting methodology. Concentric expansion from a fixed datum point. Most salvage outfits work a lane-and-drift pattern. You're working inward. Like you already know exactly what you're looking for."

The silence lasted maybe two seconds. It felt like a season.

"We had partial historical data," Voss said.

He came out of the deckhouse the way he always did—unhurried, immaculate even in salt-stained canvas, carrying two mugs of coffee as if he'd been waiting all morning for company. I'd seen Cassian Voss lie before. I'd watched him do it to harbormasters and insurance men and once, memorably, to a customs officer in Valletta while a crate of undeclared dive gear sat ten feet behind him. He lied the way other men breathed, without effort or apology, and I had never once seen him sweat.

But watching him now, I realized I'd been wrong about the mechanics of it. Voss didn't lie by concealing things. He lied by giving you so much true thing that the false part went down smooth as bait in chum.

"Mr. Cassian Voss," he said, extending a hand. "Expedition consultant. And yes—you've caught us out, Inspector. We're not working blind." He handed Delgado a coffee, uninvited, and the man took it, and just like that the geometry of the conversation changed. "The Meridian's loss position has been the subject of private speculation for years. We acquired a fragmentary record—a declassified hydrographic survey from '04, portions of a fishing vessel's log. Enough to fix a search datum. Not enough to be certain. So we work inward, concentric, until the wreck confirms or refutes the data." He sipped his own coffee. "It's slower. It's also cheaper than dragging lanes across open ocean for a month."

Delgado listened with his head tilted, a man tasting soup. "And what is it you expect to find, Mr. Voss?"

"Wreckage," Voss said pleasantly. "A naval research vessel that sank twenty years ago with considerable equipment aboard. Equipment with scrap value, if any of it survived. The navy declined to conduct its own recovery—which, given the depth, I understand—and the site sat unclaimed. We filed for salvage rights through the admiralty court in March. You're welcome to verify; the filing is public record." He gestured at the chart table, magnanimous. "In fact, Inspector, if your office has better positional data than ours, I'd genuinely welcome it. Every meter of error costs us dive time."

It was beautiful. I mean that professionally. He'd taken the one thing we couldn't hide—the grid, the datum, the red circle—and reframed it as evidence of diligence rather than intent. He'd cited paperwork that existed. He'd offered cooperation no suspicious man would offer. And underneath all of it ran the implication, gentle as a current, that the coast guard inspector ought to know his own service's business better than to ask.

Delgado smiled his short smile. "The navy doesn't share much with us, Mr. Voss. You might be surprised how little we get told."

"I'm never surprised," Voss said, "by how little anyone gets told."

Marsh, the quiet one, finally spoke, and his voice was exactly what I expected: soft, unhurried, the voice of a man who'd learned that quiet makes people fill silence against their will. "Your diver," he said. "She's down now?"

"She is," Odile said.

"Ninety meters on mixed gas. That's serious work." He turned his head slowly, taking in the winch, the umbilical fairlead, the chamber. "Long bottom times?"

"Short ones," Odile said. "Careful ones."

"Mm." He looked at the chart table again—at the red circle, at the fan of search lanes—and then, deliberately, at me. I don't know what he saw. I tried to give him nothing: a deckhand's bored face, coffee, weather. But I've spent enough years around dangerous men to know when one is memorizing you, and Marsh was memorizing all of us, item by item, the way I'd catalogue a wreck before cutting into her.

"How long are you planning to remain on station?" Delgado asked.

"Depends on the sea state and the findings," Voss said. "Two weeks. Three, if the equipment's worth recovering."

"And after?"

"After?" Voss raised his eyebrows. "After, we go home, we sell what's sellable, we pay our crew, and we file our reports with whoever requires them. Is there someone who requires them?"

Delgado laughed—a real laugh, warm, easy, and completely unconvincing. "No. No one requires anything, Mr. Voss. This is a courtesy call. Compliance check. We like to know who's working the water." He drained his coffee and set the mug down on the chart table with a small precise click, directly on top of the red circle, covering it. Whether he knew what he'd covered, I couldn't say. I suspected he knew exactly. "Mind the forecast, Captain. Weather's turning midweek. These waters get ugly fast this time of year."

"So I'm told," Odile said.

They went down the ladder the way they'd come up it, unhurried, entitled. Their engine faded to the northeast, back the way it had come, and the sea closed behind them like water over a dropped stone, and for a long moment nobody on the Thessaly moved.

Then Dez—that's me, that's the name on my papers and the name I answer to—Dez exhaled about a minute's worth of held breath and said, "Well. That was fun."

Nobody laughed.

* * *

Voss collected the mugs, rinsed them at the deck tap, and set them upside down to dry with the same care he gave everything, and I understood suddenly that the tidiness was the performance too—that every gesture of the man was load-bearing.

"They weren't coast guard," Odile said. It wasn't a question either. Nobody was asking questions anymore; we'd moved past questions into the country beyond them.

"No," Voss said. "They weren't."

"You knew that before you opened your mouth."

"I knew it when their boat cleared the horizon. Coast guard cutters run transponders, Captain. That vessel was dark. Coast guard inspectors carry standardized kit—" he nodded toward the ladder where Delgado had stood— "camera, tablet, citation book. Those two carried nothing but their eyes. And coast guard personnel introduce themselves with rank. He gave me a surname only. Men who give you only a surname are men whose ranks would tell you too much."

Renn had come up during the tail end of it, moving carefully, favoring the shoulder the way he still did. He leaned on the rail and stared at the empty northeast horizon. "Navy," he said. "Those were navy."

"Office of Naval Intelligence, most likely," Voss said, "or whatever they're calling the people who clean up after ONI these days. Call them fixers. It's accurate." He folded his arms and regarded the chart table, the weights, the grease-pencil circle now smudged faintly where the mug had rested. "They boarded us to accomplish three things. One: confirm we've found the wreck. Two: assess how much we know. Three: put us on notice without putting anything on record. They succeeded at all three, largely because I let them."

"You *let* them," Renn repeated flatly.

"If Delgado leaves believing we're scrap salvagers chasing a rumor, we buy time. If he leaves uncertain, we buy less. If he leaves certain—" Voss shrugged, a small economical motion— "we'd already be under way with an escort. So yes. I managed what he walked away with. It's the only currency we have left to spend."

Yara surfaced from below then—not from the water; she'd scrubbed the second dive that morning, bottom conditions fouled by a silt surge—but from the forward hold where she'd been cleaning her regulator stack, and I watched her take in the scene the way she took in everything: fast, whole, missing nothing. She looked at the wet mugs drying upside down. She looked at Voss. She looked at the smudged circle on the chart.

"Who was here?" she asked.

"Inspectors," Voss said.

"Real ones?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes," Yara said, and the word came out with a weight that made even Voss pause. She crossed to the chart table and stood over the red circle, hands flat on the wood, and I remembered what Odile had told me once, drunk, in Harrow Point, about why Yara Halloran never signed a lease anywhere, never kept a berth past a season, never let a port learn her face: because somewhere behind her was a thing that had already taken her mother, and she'd decided the only defense was motion. Twenty years of motion. And now here she stood, anchored over the exact spot where that motion had started, with the navy's dogs sniffing at our wake, and there was nowhere left to move to except down.

"The next time someone boards this vessel," she said quietly, "I want to be on deck. Whatever story you're telling them, Voss, I want to hear it while you're telling it. Because I'm starting to think the person on this boat who knows least about what's happening is me."

Voss held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever passed between them, neither of them shared it with the rest of us.

"Fair," he said finally. "Fair enough."

That frightened me more than the visitors had. Cassian Voss did not concede things. Concession, from a man like him, was a coin spent in expectation of return.

* * *

We worked the rest of the day in a silence that had a new texture to it. Before the boarding, the quiet on the Thessaly had been the good kind—focused, purposeful, the hum of a crew pulling in one direction. Now the quiet had eyes. Every radio transmission got logged and timed. Every distant engine got identified before it got ignored. When the supply tender came out from Port Anselm at dusk with our fuel drums, Odie met it at the rail herself and checked the manifest line by line, and I caught her counting the tender's crew twice, the same way I'd counted the visitors' boat.

At 1900 I climbed to the flying bridge with the evening sweep running and found Voss already up there, alone, studying the northeast quadrant through binoculars though there was nothing out there but the last copper light on the water.

"They'll be back," I said.

"Almost certainly."

"Not with a boarding party next time."

"No." He lowered the glasses. "Next time it will be less polite. A patrol boat standing off our position, day and night, close enough to read our flag. A notice-to-mariners closing the sector for 'unexploded ordnance survey.' Paper, Dez. The navy's favorite weapon is paper. They can make this square mile of ocean illegal for us to occupy without ever firing a shot, and they can have the paperwork ready in forty-eight hours."

"Then we finish before the paperwork does."

He glanced at me sidelong, and for half a second something moved behind his composure—something that wasn't calculation. Something almost like respect, or maybe just recognition. "You've changed since Harrow Point," he said. "You used to be the one arguing for caution."

"I'm still arguing for caution. Caution says we finish fast and leave clean." I looked at the water, at the place where the Meridian waited under ninety meters of cold and dark, holding whatever it held. "What I can't figure is what *you* think we're finishing. Because scrap value doesn't bring intelligence officers onto a salvage boat, Voss. Scrap value doesn't make a man rehearse his lies. Whatever's down there, you knew about it before you ever handed Odile those coordinates. And I'm starting to count the days until you decide we know too much."

The light was nearly gone. Out along the eastern horizon, right at the edge of seeing, a shape resolved and unresolved—low, gray, stationary, there or not there depending on the swell. I pointed. Voss looked, and didn't pretend he hadn't seen it.

"There," he said quietly. "That's your answer to the first part. As for the rest—" he turned back toward me, and the last light caught his face and made it hard to read, which I suspect was the point— "ask me again tomorrow, Dez. After tomorrow, I suspect we'll all know each other considerably better."

Below us, the dive lamp flared on at the stern, and Yara's voice came up the tube from the shack, calm and clipped, calling the descent: *"Thessaly, diver one, going down."*

And out there in the dark, the gray shape turned—slowly, deliberately—until its bow pointed at us like a sentence waiting to be finished.
