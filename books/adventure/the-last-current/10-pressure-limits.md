## 10. Pressure Limits

The pain woke him before the pain had a name.

Renn Okonkwo came up out of a dream of falling—endless falling through green water with no bottom and no surface—and his left shoulder was on fire. Not the dull ache of an old injury, not the bruise he'd earned hauling anchor line off the Thessaly's rail two seasons back. This was deeper. This was inside the joint itself, inside the bone, a hot wire pulled taut from shoulder to elbow and humming.

He lay still in his bunk in the dark of the forward cabin and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since they'd hauled him out of the water six hours ago: ninety meters down, forty-one minutes on the wreck, a missed decompression stop at nine meters because Yara had waved him toward the vault corridor one more time and he'd gone, because he always went, because that was what crew meant. Ninety meters. Forty-one minutes. Nine meters, missed.

The numbers added up to bubbles in his blood, and the bubbles had found his shoulder and were setting up housekeeping.

He breathed through it. In through the nose, four counts. Out through the mouth, six. The way the dive manuals said, the way he'd taught three generations of green salvage hands, the way he'd promised himself he'd never need again because he was fifty-one years old and careful, careful being the whole architecture of his life. Careful was why he was still alive when better divers than him were names on memorial walls. Careful was supposed to be enough.

The wire in his shoulder hummed and tightened.

He got up without turning on the light. He knew the Thessaly's passages by feel now, eleven days aboard—the bulkhead frame at hip height, the cable run along the deckhead, the galley door that stuck unless you lifted as you pushed. He stood in the galley in the dark drinking water straight from the jug, because hydration was the first commandment after a hit, and because his throat felt like he'd been breathing sand.

"You should be flat on your back."

Odile's voice from the doorway. She had a mug in her hand and the ship's only functioning flashlight clipped to her collar, angled down so it lit nothing but her own chin, which made her look like a storyteller around a campfire telling something terrible.

"I'm hydrating," Renn said.

"You're standing. That's already a violation." She came in and sat across the table from him, and in the dark her face was mostly shadow and one bright wedge of cheekbone. "How bad?"

"Three out of ten."

"Liar."

"Four," he admitted. "It'll hold till morning. If it doesn't—" He shrugged with the good arm. "You know the protocol. Back in the water on oxygen, shallow recompression, or we run for Port Meridian and I spend a week in a chamber listening to a man named Doug tell me about his divorce."

Odile didn't laugh. That was how he knew it was bad. Odile laughed at everything—that was her armor, same as Yara's restlessness, same as Dez's jokes, same as Voss's ledger-book smile. When Odile went quiet, the ship itself seemed to list.

"We can't make Port Meridian," she said. "Weather window closes tomorrow night. And Voss says—"

"Voss says," Renn repeated. He set the water jug down harder than he meant to. It knocked against the bulkhead and the sound went through the whole hull like a knuckle rapping a coffin lid. "What does Voss say, exactly? Word for word. I want to hear it."

"He says we're close. He says one more dive gets us into the vault proper, and then we're done, and everyone gets paid triple."

"And you believe him?"

"I believe the triple," Odile said quietly. "Renn. I mortgaged my boat for this job. My *boat*. Twenty-two years I've owned the *Maren Sky*, and she's sitting under a bank's thumb in Port Callas right now because I signed a paper Cassian Voss put in front of me while I was drunk on his expensive rum." She turned the mug in her hands. "So yes. I believe the money. The money is real even if nothing else is."

Renn looked at her across the dark table and felt the old grief move through him, the one that lived in every working diver past fifty: the grief of watching people you loved walk toward a door you'd seen other people walk through and not come back from.

"Odile," he said. "I've been doing this thirty years. You want to know what I know about ninety meters?"

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then let me say it anyway, because apparently nobody listens to me until I'm dead." His shoulder pulsed, and he rode it, breath slow, and kept talking. "At ninety meters you're narced. Everyone is. Doesn't matter how many dives you've got logged, doesn't matter how calm you are—the nitrogen gets in your head and the world goes soft at the edges, and soft edges are where people die. At ninety meters your deco obligation is longer than your bottom time. You spend more of the dive hanging in cold black water paying off debt than actually working. And every minute of that hang, you're a target—for current, for boats running over you, for your own equipment, for your own tired stupid brain. We've done three dives to that wreck in five days. Three. My tissue saturation is a bank account and we keep overdrawing it, and today the bank came collecting." He lifted his bad arm slightly, let it drop. "This is what the interest looks like."

Odile was silent a long moment. Then: "Yara thinks the vault's the answer. To everything. To her mother, to the navy, to all of it."

"I know what Yara thinks."

"She's not wrong that there's something down there worth finding."

"I never said she was wrong." Renn rubbed his eyes with his good hand. "That's the hell of it, Odile. She might be completely right. Right and dead are not mutually exclusive. I've buried men who were right."

* * *

Morning came gray and hard, wind out of the southeast with a smell in it like iron and old rain. Renn sat propped in the wheelhouse with a blanket over his legs like an invalid grandfather, which he resented, and watched the survey plotter track their position over the Meridian's grave, which he resented more.

His shoulder was a five now. He'd taken the ship's ibuprofen supply down to its last four tablets and was rationing them like wartime chocolate. Every swell that rolled under the hull sent a small white spark of pain from shoulder to fingertips. He kept his breathing even and his face neutral, because the first rule of a hit was don't scare the new divers, and on this boat, this week, everyone was a new diver.

Yara came in at seven with salt dried white on her shoulders and that look on her face. Renn had spent eleven days learning to read her looks the way sailors read weather. There was the focused look, the angry look, the look she wore when someone mentioned her father. And there was this one—the vault look, the hungry look, the look of a woman standing at the edge of a hole in the world and hearing something call up out of it.

"How's the shoulder," she said. Not a question. A formality before business.

"Fine."

"Renn."

"It's a five. Maybe a six. It'll dive or it won't, but either way I'm not the problem." He turned the plotter screen toward her. "We are the problem. Look at this. Bottom time yesterday, forty-one minutes. Day before, thirty-eight. Total accumulated deco debt across the expedition—" he tapped the figures, "—is beyond anything any commercial operation would sign off on. Any of them. We are so far outside safe margins that 'safe margin' isn't a phrase anymore. It's a memory. Like the Meridian."

Yara crossed her arms. "One more dive."

"One more dive," Renn repeated. "You said that yesterday. And the day before. Do you know what the definition of insanity is in my trade? It's 'one more dive.' Every fatality report I've ever read has those words in it somewhere. *Diver elected one additional descent.* It's practically a genre."

"This isn't a treasure run." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "There's a vault down there with my mother's unit stamp on the door. There's a logbook describing an experiment that erased twenty-six people. The navy has been sitting on this for twenty years, and Voss wants to sell it, and the fixers are circling, and every hour we sit here arguing about margins is an hour closer to a patrol boat deciding we've seen too much. One more dive, Renn. Into the vault, core out, done. I'll take the deepest leg myself. You ride the surface station."

"And if you don't come back up?"

The question landed in the wheelhouse and just sat there. Through the windows, the sea ran gray-green to the horizon, and somewhere under all that gray-green lay a scorch-marked hull with a sealed door, patient as a held breath.

"I've been diving since I was nineteen," Yara said. "I know my limits."

"No," Renn said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, because maybe cracking was the only language left that she'd hear. "You know your *depths*. Limits are different. A limit is the thing you respect because it will kill you without caring that you're special. Your mother probably knew her depths too. And the sea took her anyway, and instead of teaching you fear it taught you arithmetic—you figure if you just get deep enough, fast enough, far enough into it, you can go back and undo it. But you can't dive to twenty years ago, Yara. Nobody's built a rig that reaches."

Her face went very still. Out of the corner of his eye Renn saw Dez appear in the wheelhouse doorway and stop, sensing the pressure the way fish sense a change in the water.

"My mother didn't die diving," Yara said finally, each word placed like a foot on loose rock. "She died because of what people did to her. Down there. In that ship. And whatever's in that vault is the proof, and proof is the only weapon anyone's ever had against people like the ones who did it. So yes. One more dive. And I'm asking you—not ordering, asking—as crew, as family, as the man who taught me how to clear a flooded mask when I was panicking at fifteen feet: help me do it right. Because I'm going either way."

"There it is," Renn said softly. "There's the knife under the request."

Dez stepped fully into the wheelhouse. "Okay, everybody breathe—"

"Stay out of it, Dez."

"With respect, boss, no." Dez leaned against the chart table, arms folded, and Renn watched the youngest of them plant his feet like a man taking a stance in weather. "Renn's right. I've been running the numbers too. We're past every envelope. My computer's been flagging our profiles in red since dive two. Red means stop. It doesn't mean red-ish, it doesn't mean red-with-exceptions. And now Renn's taken a hit, the weather's turning, and there's a navy frigate that's adjusted course twice toward us since dawn—I checked the AIS feed, it's not subtle. Everything about this says stop."

"Everything except the vault," Yara said.

"The vault's been down there twenty years! It'll be down there another twenty!"

"No," Yara said. "Not once Voss sells what we've already pulled. Not once the navy decides to bury the site properly. Windows close, Dez. That's the one thing the sea teaches you that land never does."

The wheelhouse door banged and Odile came in with the wind behind her, hair whipping, and Renn saw by her face that she'd already decided. He'd known her twenty years; he could read her decisions forming like weather fronts.

"Vote," Odile said. "Right now, before Voss sticks his oar in. Captain's privilege. One more dive, yes or no. I say yes. We finish this clean, we get paid, the *Maren Sky* comes home, and Yara gets whatever truth is down there before somebody else takes it away forever."

"I say no," Renn said. His shoulder screamed as he raised his hand, and he raised it anyway, slow and deliberate, an old man's flag planted in bad ground. "No. Not because I don't care about her mother. Because I care about *her*, and about you, and about every name I've ever had to write in a memorial book, and I will not help add one."

"Dez?" Odile asked.

Dez looked at Yara. Renn watched the kid do it—watched loyalty and sense fight it out behind his eyes like two currents meeting, choppy water, no clear line.

"I'm with Renn," Dez said quietly. "Red means stop."

Yara stood in the middle of the wheelhouse with the vote two and two against her, and Renn braced for the explosion, the captain's override, the fury. Instead she just nodded slowly, once, like a woman confirming a coordinate.

"Two and two," she said. "And Voss makes five. And Voss owns the contract." She turned toward the aft deck, where Cassian Voss stood at the rail in his immaculate jacket, watching the horizon where the navy frigate was now visible to the naked eye—a gray shape growing out of the gray water, coming on at steady, unhurried speed, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Through the glass, they all heard Voss say, pleasantly, to no one and everyone:

"Well. It seems we have a quorum after all."
