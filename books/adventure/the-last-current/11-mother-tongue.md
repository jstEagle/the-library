## 11. Mother Tongue

The water at ninety meters was not blue. It was the color of a held breath, of iron filings suspended in old glass, and Yara moved through it the way a thought moves through grief—slowly, against its own weight.

She had made this descent twice before. The first time had been discovery; the second, confirmation. This was the third, and third times were for taking things. Her headlamp cut a cone through the murk that seemed to end too soon, as if the sea itself were rationing what she was allowed to see. Below her boots—she wore fins, but her mind insisted on boots—the Meridian lay on the seafloor like a cathedral that had been argued over by giants and then abandoned mid-quarrel. Her hull plating peeled back in petals. Her corridors gaped. Twenty years of cold current had polished every edge to a dull shine, so that the wreck looked less ruined than *finished*, the way driftwood is finished, the way bones are finished.

Yara checked her gauges out of habit rather than need. Bottom time: forty minutes planned, thirty-eight usable if she wanted a margin Renn would approve of. Renn did not approve of this dive at all. He'd said so with his hands braced on the Thessaly's rail, his voice still rough from the chamber, saying *Yara, the tables don't care about your mother*, and she had heard him, truly heard him, and gone down anyway. That was the kind of person she was. She had known it since she was nine years old and first understood that her mother had stepped onto a ship and become a hole in the world.

*The vault is aft,* she reminded herself. *Aft, below the waterline, behind the scorched section. Don't wander. Wandering is how people die down here.*

But wandering was also how people found things, and the Meridian knew it. As she swam the exterior toward the stern, her light swept across the breach they'd catalogued on dive one—that impossible tear in the plating, edges bent *outward*, metal that had been pushed from inside by something with no shape the navy's reports would admit to. She kept her eyes off it. Looking at it too long made the water feel occupied.

* * *

The vault door waited where the plans Voss had bought said it would wait: recessed into a bulkhead that should have been a galley, stamped with a designation she had read three times now and still read a fourth, because reading it was a small violence she couldn't stop committing.

**MERIDIAN — RESEARCH UNIT 7 — PERSONNEL ACCESS ONLY — K. HALLORAN, CHIEF ARCHIVIST**

Her mother's initials. Her mother's name. Not a coincidence, because nothing aboard this ship was a coincidence; the navy had built a machine for making coincidences and then sunk the evidence.

The outer seal was a wheel-lock, old-fashioned, mechanical, chosen precisely because mechanical things survived EMPs and purges and twenty years of salt. Yara wedged herself against the frame, planted her feet on a stanchion, and took hold of the wheel with both gloved hands. It didn't move. Of course it didn't move. Salt had been welding this ship shut since before she'd learned to drive a car.

She worked it the way her father had taught her to work seized fittings on their little boat, back when he was still talking, back when the swearing-off was fresh and holy: not with force, but with patience. Nudge. Rest. Nudge. Let the metal remember it had once been flexible. Penetrating oil was useless at this depth, so she used leverage instead—a pry bar braced against the frame, applied in increments, each one accompanied by a sound like a whale clearing its throat somewhere far away.

On the fourth increment, something gave. Not the wheel—the hinge-side seal, a hairline surrender of corrosion, and then a whisper of silt escaping the gap like the ship exhaling after two decades of holding it.

Yara stopped. Listened. The ocean offered her its usual answer: pressure, current, the tick of her own regulator, the distant groan of a hull settling by millimeters per year. Nothing else. No footsteps. No voices. Just the deep being the deep.

She turned the wheel.

It came around with a shriek she felt in her teeth, and the door swung inward six inches and stopped, blocked by something soft. Silt had drifted in over the years, or—her light showed her—papers. Hundreds of them, sodden pulp fused into a gray mattress against the door, as though someone on the last day had tried to shove an archive out into the corridor and been interrupted.

She pulled the door wide enough to enter and slipped through sideways, into a room the size of a walk-in closet. Her lamp found shelving bolted to three walls, and on the shelves, sealed cases—aluminum, military-gray, latched with rubber gaskets that had no business still being intact after twenty years underwater. But they were intact. Whatever the navy had built here, they had built it to survive its own sinking. That fact sat in Yara's chest like a swallowed stone. You only waterproof your sins if you intend to come back for them.

One case stood apart from the others, centered on the middle shelf, marked with a strip of reflective tape that still caught her light and threw it back, greedy and bright. Beside it, smaller, a personal effects locker—standard issue, the kind every sailor kept under a bunk, dented, its latch hanging open as if someone had opened it in a hurry and never closed it again.

Yara took the taped case first. DATA CORE — UNIT 7 — DO NOT DUPLICATE, the stencil read, and beneath it, handwritten in grease pencil, in handwriting she would have known anywhere, in any century, at any depth:

*If you're reading this, I'm sorry.*

Her mother's hand. Slanted, hurried, the crossbar of the T trailing off like a woman writing while walking. Yara held the case against her chest plate and felt nothing through the neoprene, which was a mercy, because if she could have felt it she might have stayed there until her air ran out, hugging an aluminum box in the dark like a child with a radio.

Then the effects locker. She hesitated—some part of her understood that whatever was in it would be worse than data, more personal, less deniable—and then opened it the rest of the way.

Clothes, rotted to lace. A photograph fused into a single gray rectangle. A hairbrush with a few strands still caught in it, dark, going silver at the temple. And at the bottom, wrapped in a wool sock that had somehow held together, a voice recorder. Military-issue, olive drab, the size of a paperback. Its battery compartment was sealed with wax—her mother had waxed it shut, deliberately, before the sinking or during it, preparing it for exactly this journey, this depth, these twenty years.

*Kay.* Yara said it aloud and heard her own voice turn strange in her helmet, flattened by the comm, unrecognizable. *Okay. Okay. Take it and go.*

She clipped the core to her harness's hard point, stowed the recorder in the dry box strapped to her chest, and gave the room one last sweep of light. On the wall above the shelves, someone had scratched words into the paint with something sharp. She leaned close. The letters were small, neat, patient:

*We were the experiment. Remember us correctly or not at all.*

Yara backed out of the vault, closed the door as gently as a thing that heavy could be closed, and did not lock it. Let the sea have it. Let the sea keep what she couldn't carry.

* * *

The ascent was supposed to be routine. Routine meant discipline: rate, stops, breathing like a metronome, no celebrating at eighty meters, no crying at sixty, no doing anything at all except rising through the water column like a slow bubble with a job to do.

At fifty meters, on her second required stop, hanging in the featureless green-dark with the core tugging at her harness and the dry box pressed to her sternum, Yara let herself think about the recorder. About the wax seal. About the fact that somewhere above her, in a bunk or a cabin or the galley of the Thessaly, there was a charging cable and a speaker and twenty years of silence waiting to end.

Her mother's voice. She realized, with a lurch, that she couldn't fully reconstruct it anymore. She had the timbre the way you have a word on the tip of your tongue—present, load-bearing, inaccessible. She remembered that her mother had laughed with her whole torso. She remembered singing in the kitchen, off-key, some folk song about the sea giving back what it takes. But the voice itself, the actual grain of it, had eroded the way all true things erode when you handle them too often or not enough. Twenty years of running, and the one thing she'd run out of was the sound.

*Hold on,* she told the dry box silently. *Almost up. Hold on.*

At thirty meters, Dez's voice crackled in her earpiece, and it was wrong. Dez's voice was always flat, always bored, always pitched at the exact frequency of a man who refused to be surprised by anything the ocean did. This voice was tight.

*"Thessaly to Yara. Come up slow but come up now. We've got company."*

Yara's heart did something complicated. *"Define company."*

*"Navy corvette, bearing northeast, call sign matches the patrol boat that's been loitering all week. Except it's not loitering anymore. It just came about."*

*"Came about how?"*

A pause. Through her mask, Yara watched her own exhaled bubbles climb away from her, silver coins spent into the dark, and she hated them a little for being able to leave so easily.

*"Came about like it's coming here," Dez said. "Like it knows exactly where we are. Which it shouldn't, unless—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Unless someone aboard the Thessaly was telling them. Unless the contract had teeth they hadn't seen yet. Unless Cassian Voss, who smiled like a man sharing a joke only he understood, had sold them all before he'd even bought them.*

*"Time to surface?" Yara asked.*

*"At your current rate? Eleven minutes. They'll be inside five miles in eight."*

Eleven minutes. Yara looked up. The surface was a rumor this deep, a faint bright smudge, impossibly far. Between her and it hung thirty meters of water, three decompression stops, and the entire weight of everything she'd just taken from the vault. She could blow the stops. Divers did it sometimes, in emergencies, and paid for it on deck or in a chamber or in a churchyard. Renn had already paid once on this expedition. If she blew hers, she might pay in the worst currency there was: the ability to ever hear that recording at all, brain scrambled, ears ruined, the wax-sealed voice locked forever in a box her own body could no longer survive opening.

Or she could rise at the correct speed, minute by obedient minute, while a warship closed the distance, and trust Odile's hands on the throttle and Dez's mouth on the radio and whatever scraps of luck the Hallorans hadn't yet spent.

*"Dez," she said. "Tell Odile to get the engines warm. Don't move yet—moving looks guilty—but be ready to look guilty fast."*

*"Copy. Yara?"*

*"Yeah."*

*"What'd you find down there?"*

Yara pressed her palm flat against the dry box, against the wool-wrapped shape inside it, and for one second she let herself feel the whole absurd arc of it: the oath, the buoy field, the bar, the contract, the breach in the hull bent outward, the scratched words on the vault wall. *Remember us correctly or not at all.*

*"My mother," she said. "I found my mother."*

She kicked upward, metronome-steady, and began the long climb toward a surface that was no longer empty.
