## 12. The Recording

The Thessaly rocked like a cradle someone had forgotten to stop pushing.

Yara waited until the generator cycled down for the night watch, until the only sounds left were the sea working the hull and Dez's snore rolling through the bulkhead like distant surf. She sat on her bunk with the towel wrapped around the voice recorder, as if it were something alive that might startle, and in a way she supposed it was. It was the closest thing to a heartbeat she'd held in twenty years.

She had not opened the personal effects locker on the boat. Not during ascent, not during the frantic hour when Odile plotted their drift and Renn lay gray-lipped in his bunk breathing pure oxygen, not even when Voss stood over the recovered core turning it in his hands like a man appraising a jewel he hadn't yet decided how to fence. She'd taken the locker below decks without a word, and no one had stopped her, because everyone understood there were things you didn't ask about on this crew. Everyone carried one sealed box or another.

Now, alone, she unwrapped the towel.

The recorder was a navy-issue field unit, olive composite casing gone soft with age, the kind of plastic that sweated even in cold water. Salt had crept into the seams. The lanyard ring was still attached, threaded with a frayed cord that had once been red—she could tell by the deeper dye at the knot—and Yara's thumb found the knot before she knew what her hands were doing, worrying it the way she'd worried the drawstrings of her school bag while waiting for her mother to come up the walk.

*Stop,* she told herself. *You're forty-one years old. Act like it.*

Her hands did not stop.

The battery compartment was fused with corrosion, but Renn had rigged a bypass lead that afternoon without asking why—he'd just looked at the locker's stenciled designation, then at her face, and said nothing except, "It'll hold charge for maybe an hour. Don't waste it." An hour of her mother's voice. Twenty years of silence measured against sixty minutes of borrowed electricity. Yara had done the arithmetic a hundred times since and it never came out kind.

She pressed play.

Static first—a long breath of it, the sound of the sea translated into noise, which felt appropriate, almost ceremonial. Then a click. Then a woman's voice, close to the microphone, warm and slightly rough at the edges, the way voices get when they've spent years giving orders over engine rooms.

"—testing, day one. If anyone ever hears this, I apologize in advance for the boredom."

Yara's whole body went rigid.

It wasn't that she'd forgotten the voice. It was worse than that: she'd never known she remembered it. It arrived whole and immediate, bypassing every defense she'd built, and for a moment she was eight years old again, sitting cross-legged on the kitchen floor while her mother talked on the phone with her back turned, that same cadence, that same half-smile you could hear in the vowels. Her mother had laughed from somewhere deep, like the laugh cost her something and she paid gladly.

"Day one," the voice said again, brisker now, professional. "Log entry, Lieutenant Commander Maren Halloran, Meridian, research complement. Recording these entries on personal equipment because I don't trust the ship's systems, and I don't trust—" a pause, a breath, "—I don't trust that everything we're being told is true. Which is not an accusation. Yet."

Yara realized she was holding her breath and made herself breathe.

The recording moved through days. Her mother's voice catalogued the routine of a research vessel with the dry humor of someone who'd learned to survive institutions by narrating them: the coffee that tasted like bilge, the chief engineer who sang sea shanties off-key at 0400, the endless calibration runs. Ordinary life, recorded in extraordinary detail, as if Maren Halloran had already sensed that ordinary was a finite resource.

Then, on what the timestamps said was day nineteen, the tone changed.

"They've briefed us on the full protocol now. Officially it's called Cognitive Baseline Restoration. Unofficially, in the corridors, people are starting to call it the quieting." A pause long enough that Yara checked the display to make sure it was still playing. "Here's what they told us, and here's what I think, and I'm going to keep those two things separate so that someday, if anyone listens to this, they can hear exactly where the divergence began."

Yara pulled her knees up to her chest. The bunk was narrow; the sea kept its rhythm against the hull; and her mother's voice filled the small dark cabin the way water fills a hold—not violently, just completely.

"The literature says the compound induces targeted retrograde amnesia. Selective erasure. They frame it as mercy medicine—trauma patients, witnesses to atrocities, soldiers who can't sleep for what they've seen. Wipe the wound, leave the person. That's the pitch." Another pause. "But I've read the trial design, and you don't need this many control groups to erase a memory. You need them if you're building a new one."

The words landed in Yara's chest like a hook finding flesh.

"Not erasure," her mother said, and her voice had gone quiet in a new way—the quiet of someone standing at the edge of something. "Rewriting. That's the actual program. They're not deleting who these people were. They're installing who they want them to be. Memory is just the substrate. Identity is the target. And the horrifying thing—the thing I keep turning over at three in the morning—is how *clean* it looks from the outside. The subjects are calm. Cooperative. Grateful, even. Because whatever they become, they believe they chose it."

Yara thought of the wreck. Of corridors scorched from the inside out. Of a hull breached where no mine would have struck. Of twenty years of navy silence wrapped around a question nobody outside had known to ask. *What do you do with two hundred people whose selves have been replaced?* You sink the evidence. You sink the evidence and the witnesses, and you spend two decades making sure no one goes looking.

Except one witness had recorded everything.

"I volunteered," her mother said, and Yara's blood went cold and hot at once. "Day twenty-two. I want this on the record clearly, because if it works the way I suspect, I may not be able to say it later with any conviction: I volunteered. No coercion. My idea, argued for over four days, over the explicit objection of Dr. Osei, who is the only person aboard I'd call honest." A short, unsteady laugh. "He says I'm being reckless. He's right. But listen—if this thing works, if they can rewrite a person wholesale, then somewhere down the line they will do it whether it's reversible or not. Governments don't shelve weapons because they're dangerous. They shelve them when they're *unreliable*. So I'm going to make it unreliable. I'm going to prove it reverses. Me. On myself. Because I have something worth coming back to."

A pause, and when the voice resumed it was softer, closer to the microphone, as if she'd cupped her hand around it.

"I have a daughter. Yara. She's eight. She has my stubbornness and her father's patience, which means she'll outlast everyone. If I go under and don't come back up—if the rewriting takes and the reversal fails—I need whoever finds this to understand: the woman they made me into isn't me. The real one is in here. In these recordings. Every day I logged before the trial, every stupid detail about bad coffee and singing engineers—that's the anchor. That's the map home. You don't erase a person all at once. You erase the details first. So I'm leaving the details everywhere I can."

Yara pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth.

Twenty years. Twenty years of running—from harbors, from leases, from crews who started feeling like family, from any life solid enough to be taken away. She'd told herself it was temperament. A salt-water heart. Some people weren't built for staying. She'd worn the restlessness like armor and called it freedom, and all along it had been this: an eight-year-old's logic, fossilized into a grown woman's habits. *If I don't stay anywhere, nothing I love can be erased.* Her mother had gone under to prove a memory could be rebuilt from its details, and Yara had spent her whole adult life refusing to accumulate any.

The recorder hissed between entries. Outside, the wind had begun to work itself up—Odile had called the front hours ago, a low system dragging in behind them, and the Thessaly's rigging had started its low moan. Yara barely heard it. She was ninety meters down in a dead ship, walking beside a ghost who sounded infuriatingly, unbearably alive.

* * *

She played the middle entries twice.

Not because she missed anything the first time—because she couldn't help it. Day thirty: the first trial cohort, twelve volunteers, "calm as milk," her mother wrote in the air between them. Day thirty-four: the reversal protocol drafted, argued, approved over Osei's written dissent. Day thirty-seven: "They moved up the timeline. Command wants results before the oversight review. Science doesn't like deadlines. Deadlines are how science becomes surgery without anesthesia."

And on day thirty-nine, the last full entry, her mother's voice had changed again—thinner now, frayed at the edges, the roughness in it no longer warmth but exhaustion.

"This is it. Injection in six hours. I've hidden the original recordings where they won't look—in the effects locker, under the inventory seal, because nobody inventories grief. If the reversal works, I'll record the proof myself. If it doesn't—" a breath, steady, deliberate, the breath of a woman making herself be brave in a specific direction, "—then whoever's listening needs to know the sequence. The anchor protocol runs on emotional specificity. Not facts—facts are cheap, they rewrite facts. It's the small things. A nickname. A song sung wrong. The weight of a hand on your head. They can't fabricate what they don't know to look for, and they never bothered learning the small things, because to them the small things are disposable."

Her mother laughed then, quietly, and the laugh cracked in the middle.

"She calls the tide 'the coming-in,' did you know that? Since she was four. We'd stand on the seawall and she'd announce it like a royal procession. *Mama, the coming-in.* God, I could live a hundred years on that sentence alone. If I lose everything else, let me keep—"

The recording cut.

Mid-word. Mid-breath. The tape—or whatever medium it was—simply ended, the way a life ends, without ceremony, without finishing the sentence. One second her mother was reaching for the most precious thing she owned, and the next there was static, and then the flat digital silence of a file that contained nothing more.

Yara sat in the dark and shook.

Not sobbing—something less generous than that, more physical, a tremor that started in her sternum and worked outward until her teeth chattered and she had to set the recorder on the blanket because her hands couldn't be trusted with it. Twenty years of imagining had prepared her for a body, for a grave, for a name on a memorial wall. Nothing had prepared her for a sentence without an ending. Her mother had been stolen mid-thought, mid-love, and the theft was still in progress; it was happening right now, in this bunk, in this breath; it would happen every time the recording reached that seam and fell off the edge of the world.

*Let me keep—*

Keep what? The word after *keep* was gone. Somewhere in a flooded vault at the bottom of the Sargasso Shelf, in a data core locked in Voss's cabin, in the drowned corridors of the Meridian, the rest of that sentence existed. Or it didn't. Either way, Yara would never know unless—

Unless what?

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and stared at the recorder's dim display. The core in Voss's possession held the archive. The archive held the trial data, the subject files, the reversal protocols—everything her mother had died trying to prove. Everything the navy had killed a ship to bury. And Voss wanted to sell it. Sell it to some private buyer who would do with it exactly what institutions always did with weapons: improve them, deploy them, and call it security.

And if Yara took the core back—if she destroyed it, sank it, burned it—she would destroy the only complete copy of the science that might explain what had happened to Maren Halloran. Whether the reversal failed. Whether her mother had surfaced somewhere with a stranger's identity, alive and unknowing, walking around inside a rewritten life. Whether the woman in these recordings had ever come home.

The wind rose another notch. Above her, through the deck plating, she heard footsteps—Voss, pacing, the way he paced when he was calculating. He'd been different since the vault came up. Lighter. A man carrying a winning hand tries to look casual about it, and failing.

Yara looked down at the recorder in her lap, at the corroded casing that had survived a sinking, two decades of salt, and a salvage dive, because her mother had hidden it under an inventory seal knowing—*knowing*—that grief is the one thing no bureaucrat catalogs.

"You hid the details everywhere," Yara whispered to the dark. "Okay. Okay, Mama. I'm listening."

She played the final entry one more time, letting it cut off at the same place, letting the wound stay open, because closing it felt like a lie. Then she wrapped the recorder back in the towel and held it against her chest, and for the first time in twenty years she slept without dreaming of running.

She woke to fists on her cabin door and Dez's voice, tight and wrong: "Yara. Get up. You need to see what Voss is transmitting."
