## 13. Voss's Ledger

Cassian Voss had always believed that a man's worst confessions came out not in the dark but in the gray hours, when the sea went the color of unpolished pewter and the body, exhausted past its defenses, forgot how to lie.

It was 0400, and the Thessaly sat on a swell that moved like slow breathing. He stood at the stern rail with a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking. The galley machine aboard made something that tasted of rust and apology, and he'd carried it up anyway because his hands needed occupation. Below his shoes, through forty meters of black water, the Meridian waited with its scorched corridors and its sealed vault, and inside the vault, presumably, waited the thing he had spent two decades and most of his personal fortune arranging to touch.

The data core was already aboard. Yara had brought it up three days ago along with the personal effects locker, and since then the ship had acquired the atmosphere of a held breath. The navy ship had altered course. He'd seen it on the radar repeater at 0300, a green smear turning toward them with the unhurried certainty of a thing that had never once needed to hurry.

He heard her footsteps before he saw her. Yara Halloran moved on a boat the way water moved through a hull breach—silently, finding every gap, present everywhere before you registered her. She came to the rail two meters down from him, wrapped in a fleece gone gray with salt, her hair still damp from a shower she'd taken in water that barely qualified as warm.

"You're up early," she said.

"I don't sleep well on ships."

"Liar. You sleep on ships better than you do anywhere. You told me that yourself, eleven years ago, in Suva, after you bought me that drink I never thanked you for."

Voss smiled at the rail. "You never thanked me for a great many things."

"I'm about to thank you for even fewer." She turned to face him, and in the low glow of the deck floods her eyes had the flat shine of coins at the bottom of a fountain. "The core's encrypted. Military-grade, which means we're not cracking it on this boat, which means the only value it has is whatever you say it has. So say it. Why does a man pay triple rate for a sealed box he can't open?"

He had rehearsed this conversation for twenty years. He had rehearsed it in hotel bars in Singapore, in the passenger seat of a Land Rover crossing the Nullarbor, in the dry dock at Kaohsiung while welders stitched new plate into a hull he'd named after a woman who never learned his brother's name. And now, with the moment actually arrived, every rehearsal deserted him, and what remained was just the truth, heavy and unlovely, like a diver's body coming up from depth.

"Because I know what's in it," he said. "Not the contents. The *function*. And I know because I was there when they buried the evidence the first time."

Yara didn't move. He'd expected flinching, or the quick animal stillness she used instead of flinching. Instead she simply watched him, patient as pressure.

"Twenty-two years ago," he said, "I was nineteen. My father had pulled strings to get me a berth on the *Corvus*—the support ship. Meridian's tender and supply vessel. We'd been on station eleven days. I mostly moved crates and logged fuel transfers and got seasick in ways that still embarrass me. My brother Tomas was the opposite. Tomas belonged to the water the way you do. He was a research diver with the Meridian's oceanographic unit. Third of his class at the naval academy, first in every waterborne qualification they offered. My parents had a wall of his certificates." His thumb traced the rim of the cup. "I had a wall of nothing, so I used to stand in front of his."

"Voss—"

"Let me finish. I've been finishing this story in my head for two decades and I'd like to do it out loud, once, to someone who was also there, in the way that matters." He set the cup on the rail's flat cap. "The night it happened, the *Corvus* was anchored nine kilometers off. I was on deck because I couldn't sleep—same as now, same coffee, same rail, different ocean. And I saw the Meridian's lights go out. Not flicker. *Go out*. All of them, deck floods, navigation lights, the glow from the portholes, in the space of about four seconds. Like someone had drawn a curtain over the whole ship. And then the emergency generator came up, and the ship just sat there in the dark, silent, for almost an hour. No flares. No radio traffic that we heard. Then the lights came back on and everything looked normal, and by morning we were ordered to a new anchorage forty kilometers away, and by the following evening the Meridian was gone from the surface and the navy was telling us a flooding casualty in the engineering spaces, all hands lost, condolences, stand by for transport home."

"You saw the lights go out," Yara said. "And you never said anything."

"I was nineteen and my father signed a nondisclosure before I'd finished my first week. Everyone aboard the *Corvus* did. And when I did start asking questions—years later, when I'd inherited enough money and enough spite to make questions expensive—the answers came back in forms I didn't like. A settlement. A sealed record. A navy lawyer who looked at me with genuine pity and said, *Mr. Voss, some ships are lost to the sea and some are lost to the ledger, and it's better for everyone which one yours was*." He laughed, short and without humor. "Ledger. That word has followed me my whole life. My family made its money keeping other people's accounts. I thought I'd escaped it. Instead I just found a bigger ledger."

Yara's hands had curled around the rail. "Your brother was Meridian crew," she said. Not a question. She'd read the crew manifest; he'd made sure it was in the dossier, buried in an appendix, because some part of him had needed her to find it and some other part had needed her not to.

"Oceanographic unit. Same unit designation as the vault door you found." He watched her absorb that—the vault stamped with her mother's unit, and now his brother's. "Tomas was one of the volunteers. I didn't know that until four years ago, when I bought a copy of the trial's administrative file from a records clerk in Manila who had debts and no imagination. The Meridian wasn't a research vessel, Yara. It was a stage. The navy was running a trial on memory—selective erasure, targeted rewriting, call it what the file calls it: *the quieting*. And they weren't testing it on prisoners or volunteers from the ranks. They were testing it on their own crew. Men and women who'd signed what they were told was a routine extended-deployment waiver. Tomas signed it in March of that year. By June he was writing letters home that used my childhood nickname for our dog and got our mother's birthday wrong. By August he was gone, and the ship was gone, and the sea had a story it was told to keep."

The wind had come around, carrying the iodine smell of kelp off the shallows to the east. Somewhere below them the wreck sat in its cold cathedral, and Voss thought of the scorched corridors, the breach from the inside, and felt the old vertigo of a man standing at the edge of an answer.

"So that's the confession," he said. "Here's the part you'll hate."

"I already hate all of it."

"No. You hate the parts that hurt you. This is the part that will make you hate *me*." He turned to face her fully, because he'd decided he owed her that much—his face, unshielded, in the deck floods. "I don't want to expose this. I've had twenty years to want that, and I did, for a while, in the way you want things at nineteen—loudly, and without a plan. But exposure is a door that opens once and then the world walks through and the world forgets. A scandal runs for a news cycle. An inquiry runs for a year. And the people who built the quieting don't go to prison, Yara. They go to other programs. Better-funded ones, with the lessons learned from this one. The only way to make sure this technology never surfaces again is to make sure it never *circulates* again. And the only way I can guarantee that—me, one man with a salvage license and a family name—is to control it myself."

"Control it," she repeated. "By selling it."

"By selling it *back*." He held her gaze. "There's a buyer. Private. I won't name them yet, but they're not a government and they're not a defense contractor. They're a foundation—a research trust with a charter in bioethics, funding, and enough legal muscle to make the acquisition airtight. They've spent six years documenting the Meridian program from the outside. They want the primary data. The core. And they've agreed to terms I dictated: the data gets sealed under their custody, never weaponized, never licensed, and the existence of the trial becomes part of a public archive *they* control, on *their* timeline. Not the navy's. Not buried in a sealed record that some lawyer calls a ledger. Held. Accounted for. With my name on the deed of custody."

"You're selling your brother's memory to a stranger and calling it a rescue."

"I'm selling it to the only people who can keep it safe, because I can't." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated that, and let it stand. "You think I haven't run this every way? I've spent twenty years and eleven million dollars getting to this rail, this night, this conversation. I've imagined giving it to journalists—journalists have editors, and editors have lawyers, and lawyers have clients. I've imagined giving it to the navy itself, and you've seen how the navy handles its own history. I've imagined dumping it on the open internet, and then it's in the hands of every government and every startup with a server farm and a dream, and within five years someone builds a better quieting, and somewhere there's a ship full of people like Tomas who sign a waiver they don't understand. The buyer is the least-bad option. The least-bad option is what adulthood is, Yara. Nobody tells you that. Nobody tells you that growing up is just a long process of learning to accept the least-bad option."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. The swell lifted the Thessaly and set it down, lifted it, set it down, patient as a metronome.

"You said you'd dictate terms," she said at last. "You said the archive becomes public on *their* timeline. Whose timeline is that, exactly? Ten years? Twenty? Long enough that everyone who remembers the Meridian is dead of old age and the release is a historical footnote?"

"Seven years," he said. "Contractual."

"Contractual." She said the word the way another woman might say a curse. "My mother spent twenty years as a hole in the world. I spent twenty years running from a grief I couldn't even name. And you're going to put the truth about that in a *trust*, with a *timeline*, and call it justice because the paperwork is clean."

"I'm going to put it somewhere it can't be erased again," Voss said. "That's not justice. I know it's not justice. Justice would be the admirals in the dock and the program directors in front of cameras and Tomas's name read aloud in a parliament. I've wanted that since I was nineteen. But I can't have justice. I can have custody. And custody is the thing I know how to do, because keeping accounts is the only inheritance my family ever gave me that was worth anything." He picked up the coffee, considered it, and poured it over the rail into the dark water. "You want to know the worst part? The truly unforgivable part? I understand the navy. I understand why they did it and why they buried it. Because if I were them—if I had a technology that could unmake a man's grief, his guilt, his *love*, and put something quieter in its place—I'd want it too. Not to use. Just to know it exists. Just to know the human mind has a floor you can cut through. That knowledge is worth more than gold and more dangerous than plutonium, and it cannot be unknowable, because it's already been known, once, on a ship at the bottom of this sea."

"So what do you want from me?" Yara asked. "Why tell me this at four in the morning instead of just taking the core?"

"Because the core is encrypted, and the encryption key isn't on the core." He saw her stillness sharpen into attention. "The navy wasn't stupid. The archive is split. The core holds the trial data. The key is in the vault's secondary compartment—the one behind the outer seal you cracked, the one you didn't open because your dive computer was screaming and Renn was already bent and the navy was turning toward us on the sonar. I know you didn't open it. I've been watching the dive footage."

Yara's jaw tightened. "You want me to go back down."

"I want the *key*, Yara. The core without the key is a paperweight. The key without the core is a curiosity. Together they're the only complete record of what happened to your mother and my brother that will ever exist, and right now they're sitting in a vault under ninety meters of water while a navy ship steams toward us at twelve knots." He let that sit. "I'm offering you a choice, and I want you to understand that I hate offering it, because I know what it costs you. Go down one more time. Open the secondary compartment. Bring up the key. And then the buyer's vessel arrives at dawn tomorrow, and this ends, and Tomas and your mother go into a trust with a seven-year clock instead of a grave with a navy lock on it."

"And if I refuse?"

He looked at her for a long moment. The deck floods hummed. The radar repeater's green smear had crept closer, and he thought of the *Corvus*, and the lights going out, and the hour of silence, and how he'd stood at a rail very like this one and watched a ship die by inches in the dark.

"If you refuse," he said quietly, "then I'll do it myself, and I'll regret the way I did it for the rest of my life, which is a sentence I've apparently been writing my whole career." He turned back to the rail. "But I'd rather not. You're the better diver, and the vault knows your mother's name, and God help me, Yara, I think the vault's been waiting for you specifically."

She didn't answer. She stood at the rail beside him, and the sea breathed, and the green smear on the radar crept closer, and somewhere below them, in the cold and the dark, his brother's ship held its dead and its secrets with the patience of things that have all the time in the world and no time at all.

Voss went below at 0500 and did not sleep. At 0530 he heard Yara's boots on the deck above, crossing to the dive station, and the sound of the compressor cycling up, and he understood that she had made her choice, and that whatever it was, it would not be the one he had planned for.

* * *

The buyer's trawler appeared on the horizon at 0740, exactly on schedule, and Voss stood at the rail and watched it come, and felt the ledger closing around him like a hand.

Behind him, on the deck, Yara was checking her kit with the unhurried precision of a woman who had already stopped listening to him.

And the navy ship, patient as pressure, kept coming.
