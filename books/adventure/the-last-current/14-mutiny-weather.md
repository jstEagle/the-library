## 14. Mutiny Weather

The barometer began to lie to us on the third night.

I noticed it first as a wrongness in the way the Thessaly rode her anchor—too lively, too eager, like a horse that smells rain coming over a ridge long before its rider does. I'd owned boats for thirty years, mortgaged this one down to her rivets, and a hull will tell you things before any instrument does. She was telling me to leave.

The forecast said otherwise. The forecast said a low-pressure cell sitting four hundred miles southwest, drifting east at eight knots, nothing to worry about until Thursday. But the Thessaly's old brass barometer had been falling since dusk—slowly at first, then with the steady, committed drop of a man walking off a cliff—and by midnight it had fallen through two millibars an hour and kept going, and I stood in the wheelhouse with my coffee going cold and watched the needle and thought: *the navy has better forecasts than we do. The navy knew.*

That was the thought I couldn't shake. Not the storm itself. Storms are honest. Storms come and break over you and pass, and afterward you count what's left. It was the *timing* that bothered me—the way the patrol boat had been idling at the edge of our survey grid for six days without ever quite approaching, the way the helicopter had stopped flying over, as if it no longer needed to look. You don't stop watching something unless you already know where it is. Or unless you know it isn't going anywhere.

I went out onto the deck to check the anchor rode. The wind had backed around to the northeast, which was wrong too—it should have been southeast ahead of a front from the southwest—and it carried a smell I didn't like, a green metallic smell like copper coins and cut kelp, the smell of deep water being dragged up toward the surface by pressure changes. Far off to the west, under the last band of daylight, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise going bad: yellow-green at the bottom line, then slate, then a hard black seam where the cloud deck met the sea.

Behind me, in the saloon, Voss was talking. He'd been talking for an hour. I could hear him through the glass—his voice had a particular register he used when he was managing people, warm and reasonable and slightly amused, a voice like a hand on your shoulder guiding you somewhere you hadn't decided to go. Renn was in there, still gray-faced from his hit, propped in the corner booth with his legs elevated. Dez sat across from him, arms folded, saying almost nothing, which for Dez meant a great deal.

I came inside because it was starting to spit rain and because I wanted to hear what Voss was saying when he thought the wheelhouse was empty.

"—and frankly, the weather window works in our favor," he was saying, tapping one finger on the chart table where the survey grid was marked in grease pencil. "Nobody else can operate in these conditions. No civilian traffic, no fishing fleet, no curious eyes. We have the site to ourselves for another forty-eight hours at least. One more dive cycle and we're done."

"One more dive cycle," Renn said flatly. "At ninety meters. In a dropping barometer. With a surface swell building." He shook his head slowly. "You've never actually been in water, have you? Real water. Open water when it turns."

"I've been in plenty of water, Mr. Okonkwo."

"Pool water," Dez said quietly. "Yacht water. Water with staff."

Voss smiled at that, unbothered, the smile of a man who has been insulted by professionals before and found it merely confirming. "The point stands. The conditions isolate us. That's not a liability, it's cover."

I hung my wet jacket on the hook by the door and said, "Cover from who?"

The saloon went quiet in a particular way. Not the silence of surprise—the silence of three people who had each been waiting for someone else to ask the question first. I felt it settle over the room like the pressure change itself.

"From whoever's interested," Voss said smoothly. "The navy has made its interest clear. A quiet grid means a clean operation."

"A quiet grid," I repeated. I walked to the chart table and looked down at it. Our position marks were there, the dive sites, the transit lines, all of it neat in Yara's precise hand. And then I looked at Voss, really looked at him, the way you look at a chart when you suspect the soundings on it are wrong. "Cassian. Who are you transmitting to?"

And there it was—that half-second hitch. Most people would never have seen it. I'd spent twenty years reading skippers in bars, reading buyers on docks, reading men deciding how much truth they could afford. I saw it.

"Transmitting," he said. "Position reports to my insurer. Standard practice for a vessel operating outside normal lanes—"

"We're not outside normal lanes," I said. "We're inside a restricted survey zone you bought access to. Your insurer knows exactly where we are because you filed the coordinates with them before we left harbor. So either you're lying about the insurance traffic, or—" I let it hang. "Or there's other traffic."

Dez moved. That was the thing about Dez—he didn't argue, didn't posture, just acted, quiet as bilge water. He'd been sitting with his tablet face-down on the bench beside him the whole conversation, and now he picked it up, thumbed it awake, and turned it toward the radio rack without a word. He'd built a little spectrum logger three days ago, back when the helicopter overflights started, mostly out of boredom and a technician's itch. Passive sweep. It recorded every transmission the Thessaly's own systems made, timestamped, frequency-tagged. He hadn't shown anyone. He hadn't needed to. Until now.

He set the tablet on the chart table next to the grid.

"There's a burst transmitter active on this boat," he said. His voice was level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Short-burst encrypted packets, every six hours, on a schedule. It's not the satphone—I checked the logs against the satphone's power draw, and the draws don't match. Something else is drawing current. Something small." He looked up at Voss. "I traced it yesterday. It's in your cabin. Hardwired into your berth's 12-volt line, behind the panel. It's got its own antenna run up the flag halyard. Very tidy work. Professional."

Voss didn't deny it.

I want to be fair to him here, because fairness matters to me even now, writing this. He didn't deny it, and he didn't bluster, and he didn't reach for some smooth explanation, because there wasn't one and he knew we'd smell it. What he did was worse, or braver, depending on where you stood. He folded his hands on the chart table and looked at each of us in turn, and he said:

"Yes. I'm transmitting our position. Every six hours, to a private party. A buyer. Someone who wants what's in that vault as much as the navy does, and who can pay enough to make this whole expedition worth the risk you're all taking." He paused. "You were going to find out eventually. I preferred you find out after the job was done, when it couldn't matter anymore."

The rain picked up against the windows. Somewhere below decks, the generator changed pitch as the load shifted, and the lights dimmed and steadied.

"You sold us," Renn said. Not loudly. Almost wonderingly. "You sold us. Our position. To armed strangers. While we sleep on this boat."

"I sold *access*," Voss said. "Which is the only thing I had to sell. Your positions, yes. Fine. Guilty. But understand what you're actually angry about—you signed on to recover data worth more than this entire fleet combined, and none of you asked who'd want it. None of you asked what happens after. You were happy to take my money and keep your eyes shut as long as the shutting was free." He stood, unhurried, and buttoned his jacket. "I'm not the reason the navy is coming. I'm not the reason the storm is coming. I'm just the only man aboard who planned for both."

* * *

Yara came on deck during the worst of it.

She'd been below since the argument started—I don't know if she heard it through the hull or if Dez told her after, and honestly it doesn't matter, because the result was the same. She came up the companionway at nearly eleven o'clock, soaked before she cleared the hatch, and stood at the wheelhouse windows looking west at the lightning that was now strobing inside the cloud deck, silent heat-lightning flickering like something trying to get out.

The sea had come up fast once the front's leading edge reached us. Not the waves themselves—not yet—but the *swell*, the long deep pulse that comes ahead of weather, rolling under us from the southwest in sets of ten, twelve seconds, lifting the Thessaly and setting her down again with a creak that ran the length of her keel like a word being pronounced very slowly. The anchor rode groaned at each set. I'd put out extra scope at dusk; I was glad of it now.

She didn't say anything for a long time. She just watched the water. Then she said, "How far out is the navy ship?"

"Forty miles," I said. "Maybe less. They went dark on AIS at sunset."

"And the buyer?"

"Unknown. If Voss has been feeding them our position for days, they could be anywhere inside a hundred-mile circle. Could already be closer than the navy." I wiped condensation off the glass with the heel of my hand. "Yara. We need to talk about what happens tomorrow. All of us. Tonight."

She nodded. She'd already gotten there herself; I could see it in the set of her shoulders. There's a moment in every crisis when a crew stops being a collection of people and becomes a single organism or doesn't, and you can feel the moment coming the way you feel the swell before the wind. This was it.

We gathered in the saloon at midnight, all five of us, while the barometer needle lay flat against the pin at the bottom of its arc and the wind found its true direction and began to build in earnest. The Thessaly swung on her rode, bow quartering into the seas, and the rain came across the deck in horizontal sheets that sounded like gravel thrown against the hull. Inside, the saloon light was warm and yellow and completely inadequate to what we were doing, which was deciding whether to trust each other.

Voss sat apart. Dez had removed the burst transmitter from his cabin—carried it up on deck and put it in a bucket of seawater, which was Dez's version of a speech—and Voss hadn't protested. He sat with his hands wrapped around a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking, and for the first time since the harbor bar, he looked like a man waiting for a verdict rather than arranging one.

I laid it out, because laying things out is what I do; it's why Yara keeps me, though she'd never say so.

"Here's where we are," I said. "One: the storm pins us here. We cannot run. Even if we cut and ran tonight, we'd be taking nine meters of swell on the beam by dawn, and this hull is sound but she's not young, and I won't kill this crew to save a schedule. Two: the navy ship is closing, dark, and they know where we are whether or not Cassian told them, because they've known all along. Three: there is a third party out there—armed, private, paid—who also knows where we are, thanks to Cassian." I looked at him. He held my gaze. Good. Let him hold it. "Four: we have the core aboard, and the recorder, and the logbook, and whatever's left in that vault. And five—" I stopped, because five was the hard one, and I wanted everyone to arrive at it themselves.

Renn arrived at it first. "Five," he said, "is that everything we do from here costs somebody something, and we have to choose whose bill we pay."

"So we vote," Yara said.

Everyone looked at her.

"That's what Odile was building toward, right?" She glanced at me; I nodded. "A vote. Finish it or scuttle it. Those are the options. Nobody leaves this boat in this weather, nobody calls anybody, nobody sells anything between now and morning. We decide as a crew what the Thessaly does next, and we all live with it. Including you, Cassian." Her voice didn't rise. It never rises. That's what makes it land. "You fed our position to strangers while we slept. You don't get a veto. You get a vote. One vote. Same as the cook."

For a moment I thought he'd refuse—that he'd invoke the contract, the salvage rights, the money, all the paper architecture he'd built around himself. Instead he was quiet for a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice had lost its management register entirely. What was underneath was older and tireder.

"My brother was on that ship," he said. "Twenty years ago. I was on the support tender the night it went down. I was nineteen, and I stood on the deck and listened to the radio traffic while they lost contact, and I have spent every year since learning exactly what happened to him and exactly who decided it would stay secret. Do you imagine I give a damn about your vote?" He laughed, short and bitter. "But fine. One vote. Same as the cook. Ask your questions."

So we asked.

Dez voted first, because Dez always goes first when it matters—he says it's so the others can react to something concrete instead of to each other. "Scuttle the sale," he said. "Not the job. The core stays aboard, the buyer gets nothing, and if the navy takes us, they take us with empty hands. I'm not delivering anything to a trawler full of mercenaries because Cassian promised it to them." He looked at Voss. "No offense."

"Some taken," Voss said dryly.

Renn voted second. His hands were still trembling faintly from the decompression hit—a fine persistent tremor in the left one, the kind that might resolve and might not, and we both knew it, and neither of us said it. "Finish the job," he said, and the room shifted. "Hear me out. If we scuttle everything—if we dump the core, flood the vault, run home empty—the navy wins by default. The record stays buried. Whatever happened to those people, happened, and stays a rumor forever. I nearly died down there and I'm telling you: the only thing worse than finishing this is finishing it for nothing. We get the rest of the archive. Then we decide who sees it. Us. Not the buyer. Not the fixers. Us."

I voted third. I'll admit what I voted and why. I voted to finish—not for the money, though God knows the mortgage on this boat hangs off me like a stone, and not for the truth in the abstract, though I believe in it. I voted to finish because twenty years ago I watched a woman stand on a dock in Ponta Delgada holding a photograph of her daughter and asking every captain in the harbor if anyone would take her out to the exclusion zone, and every captain said no, including mine, including me. I was younger then and scared and I had a living to make, and I said no, and she stood there in the rain until the harbor lights came on, and then she left, and I have thought about her maybe ten thousand times since. Her daughter was standing in front of me now, voting fourth.

Yara voted last, of course. She took her time.

"The vault gets emptied," she said finally. "Everything recoverable comes up. The data core, the backup cores, the paper records, all of it. And then—" she looked at Voss, and something passed between them that I didn't fully follow, some arithmetic of damage done and damage owed— "and then we decide together, all five of us, what happens to it. Nobody sells. Nobody surrenders. Nobody destroys without a vote. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Renn said.

"Agreed," Dez said.

"Agreed," I said.

Voss was slower. He looked at Yara for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in his own. "Agreed," he said. "Though I'd caution the room—the buyer expects delivery at dawn. When it doesn't come, they won't assume weather. They'll assume betrayal. And they know where we are."

"Then they can come explain themselves to the storm," Dez said.

It wasn't funny. Nobody laughed. Outside, the wind hit seventy knots for the first time and the Thessaly leaned her shoulder into it and the whole boat shuddered like a living thing, and the barometer needle lay dead on the pin, and forty miles away—or less—a warship without lights was steaming toward us through the same black water.

* * *

I had the middle watch, which meant I didn't really sleep, which meant I was in the wheelhouse at 0400 when the radar painted the contact.

The storm had eased to a hard blow, the worst cells passing north of us, leaving behind a ragged sea and rain in torn curtains. Visibility was nothing. The radar was everything. And at 0407, out of the clutter on the southwestern edge of the screen, a target resolved: slow, steady, fourteen knots, heading straight up the bearing toward our position. Too big for a fishing boat. Too slow for a warship running dark. The signature read like a converted trawler—high freeboard, heavy superstructure, the kind of platform that gets bought by people who need to carry things and men and not appear on any registry.

I called Dez. He was up in ninety seconds, hair flattened on one side, eyes instantly awake, and he stood at the radar with me while the contact crawled across the screen, mile by mile, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"Fourteen knots," he said finally. "They slowed down."

"Why would they slow down?"

"Because they're not in a hurry anymore." He tapped the screen. "Because they already know where we're going to be. Because somebody told them we'd be here, anchored, in this exact spot, and there's nowhere we can go." He straightened up and rubbed his face with both hands. "Odile. That's not a ship coming to negotiate. That's a ship coming to collect."

Below us, in the saloon, I could hear Yara moving—she hadn't slept either, none of us had—and above the wind, faint and getting clearer, carried down from the northwest where the navy ship must be, came the sound I'd been dreading all week: a helicopter, running without lights, coming low over the water.

Dawn was two hours out. The buyer was one hour out. The navy was somewhere in between.

I rang the general alarm—two bells, soft, the way you wake a crew when you don't want panic—and went below to tell them the vote had just become a countdown.
