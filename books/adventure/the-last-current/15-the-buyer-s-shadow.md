## 15. The Buyer's Shadow

I saw it first as a smudge, the way you see everything out here—a gray suggestion on a gray horizon, too far to name, too steady to ignore. I'd been on the bow of the *Thessaly* for an hour, drinking coffee that had gone bitter in the thermos, watching the storm front build itself in the west like a wall being raised by patient hands. The clouds had that bruised, layered look of weather that intended to stay. And beneath them, six miles out, the smudge.

I put the glasses on it.

A trawler. Or something wearing a trawler's clothes. The hull sat wrong in the water—too low at the stern, too clean along the rail. Fishing boats carry their work on the outside: nets, gaffs, crates, the rust-orange stains of a hundred hauls. This one carried nothing. Its deck was empty. Its windows were dark. And on the roof of the wheelhouse, where a working boat would have a radar dome and a couple of antennas bent from years of hard use, it had a communications array that looked like it had been installed by someone who cared very much about installation.

"Dez." Odile's voice behind me, low. She'd come up without making sound, which she could do when she wanted, even on a deck that creaked for everyone else. "How long?"

"Maybe twenty minutes since it cleared the horizon. It's holding station. Not closing."

"Not closing is worse."

I knew what she meant. A boat that runs at you is a boat with a schedule. A boat that sits six miles off and watches is a boat with instructions.

Below us, through the deck plates, I could hear the argument still going—Yara and Voss, their voices worn down to a dull friction, like a rope sawing against a cleat. They'd been at it since the vote. Since Dez—since *I*—had stood in the wheelhouse with the printout of Voss's transmissions in my hand and said the word *buyer* out loud, and watched the room change temperature.

I hadn't slept. None of us had, really, but I'd slept least, because I was the one who'd found it. Voss's burst transmitter, keyed to a schedule, spitting our position into the dark every six hours like a man whistling for a dog. I'd traced the frequency, decoded the header, and there it was: a contact code, a handshake protocol, and a payment confirmation from an account with a name so bland it had to be fake—*Meridian Salvage Partners LLC*. Voss hadn't even bothered to hide well. He'd hidden just enough, the way you do when you assume no one aboard will ever know what to look for.

I'd assumed that too, once. That was the thing about Cassian Voss—he made you complicit by degrees, each one small enough to swallow, until you looked down and realized you were standing on the ocean floor.

The trawler sat out there and did not come closer, and I understood that it was waiting for dawn the way Voss was waiting for dawn, and that dawn was coming whether any of us wanted it or not.

* * *

The storm reached us first.

It came in around midnight, not the full wall but the leading edge—wind that sang through the rigging in a minor key, rain that came sideways and then vertical and then sideways again, as if it couldn't decide which way to hurt us. Odile took the first watch because she was Odile and because the *Thessaly* was hers, mortgaged to the waterline, and she would sooner have cut off her own hand than let anyone else hold her wheel in bad weather.

I found Yara in the galley at two in the morning, sitting at the table with her mother's voice recorder in front of her, not playing it, just looking at it, the way you look at a door you're afraid to open twice.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you."

"Neither of us is going to. Want tea?"

She shook her head. Her eyes had that flat shine they got when she was running calculations she didn't want to finish. "It's a converted longliner," she said. "The trawler. I've seen the class before, off West Africa. They put a crane on the stern, a boarding team below, and enough weapons to make any conversation very short. That's not a salvage buyer, Dez. That's a private navy."

"Voss says they're just security."

"Voss says a lot of things." She turned the recorder over in her hands. It was small, scuffed, older than either of us. "I keep thinking about the vault. About what's still down there. The rest of the cores, the files, whatever else they sealed behind that door. If that trawler takes the core and the navy takes the wreck, then everything my mother—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Everything she did in her last months, everything she tried to fix, it just gets filed. Classified. Buried under a different set of numbers, in a different building, by different men. Twenty more years of silence. Thirty. And nobody who was on that ship ever gets their names back."

I sat down across from her. The galley light was the color of old butter, and it made us both look ill, or maybe that was just accurate.

"You could give it to the press," I said. "The core. Before the handoff."

"To who? We're forty miles from anywhere, a navy destroyer is coming from the east, a private army is coming from the west, and our employer has been selling our coordinates since the day we left harbor." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as salt. "There's no press out here, Dez. There's just us and the water."

"Then we don't hand it over."

"And then what? They board us. They're not going to take no for an answer from a salvage crew with two dive tanks and a deck winch." She looked up at me, and for a moment the flatness cracked and I saw what was underneath, which was fear, plain and enormous, held in place by will the way a hull is held in place by frames. "I'm not afraid of dying out here. I've made peace with the water a long time ago. I'm afraid of it meaning nothing. I'm afraid of surfacing from all of this—everything we've been through, Renn's blood, the vault, the whole goddamn ocean—and having it count for nothing except making some company's quarterly filing."

The wind slammed the hull and the whole boat leaned, and the coffee mugs in the rack chimed against each other like small nervous bells.

"Then we make it count," I said. I didn't have a plan yet. I want to be honest about that. I said it because she needed to hear a voice that wasn't hers, and because sometimes a promise comes before the plan and drags the plan into existence behind it. "Somehow. We make it count."

She held my eyes for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and picked up the recorder, and carried it out of the galley like a woman carrying an ember through wind.

* * *

Dawn came the color of a healing bruise.

The storm's leading edge had passed in the night, leaving behind a sea that was still working itself out—long confused swells, a sky torn into moving rags—but the light came through anyway, thin and gray-gold, and under that light the trawler was finally moving. Toward us. Steady, unhurried, a kilometer of water closing every few minutes. I stood at the rail and watched it resolve from a smudge into a fact: forty meters of converted longliner, hull painted a gray so neutral it was almost an apology, no name visible, no flag, a stern crane folded like a sleeping insect. Men on her deck. I counted six before I stopped counting, because the number kept being worse than the last number.

Voss came out to meet them.

He'd dressed for it—I'll give him that. He had a way of putting on a fresh shirt and a certain expression that made negotiation look like a natural weather system rather than a choice. He stood at the rail with his hands folded, watching the trawler come, and when Yara stepped up beside him he didn't flinch, which I think took effort.

"You should be below," he said.

"You should be in prison," she said. "We're both disappointed."

The trawler hove to a hundred meters off our port beam. A rigid inflatable dropped from her side with four men in it and crossed the water toward us with the flat, mechanical confidence of a thing that had done this many times and expected to do it many more. They came up our hull on a boarding ladder without asking, which answered the question of whether this was going to be a conversation.

The one in front was fifty-ish, silver at the temples, with the build of a man who'd been military and then something after military, and a voice that never went above a certain volume because it had never needed to. He introduced himself as Mr. Halden, and he said it the way you'd read a serial number off a part.

"Mr. Voss. We're here to complete the transaction."

"You're early," Voss said pleasantly.

"We're punctual. There's a difference." Halden's eyes moved across the deck—the winch, the dive gear, Renn standing by the wheelhouse door with his arm still strapped from the bends hit, Odile at the rail with her arms crossed, me by the crane controls doing my best impression of a man with no opinions. "The storm compressed the timeline. Our principals want the item in hand today. You'll transfer the core, we'll transfer the funds, and everyone's morning improves."

"And if the item's not for sale anymore?" Yara said.

Halden looked at her the way you look at weather. "Everything's for sale. The only question is the price, and the price is always lower after an unpleasant morning." He smiled without engaging any of the muscles around his eyes. "I'd rather we all had a pleasant morning."

Voss raised a hand, the diplomat. "The core is aboard and intact. My crew has had—feelings. You know how creative people are. But the contract is mine, and the contract will be honored." He said it clearly, loudly, for the deck to hear, and I watched Yara's jaw set like concrete curing.

"Where's the item now?" Halden said.

"Secured."

"I'd like to see it."

"You'd like a lot of things," Yara said. "It stays in my hands until the money clears. That was the term."

"It was *my* term," Voss said, "and I'm amending it—"

"You don't amend terms in front of armed men," Yara said, not raising her voice, which was somehow louder. "You amend them in front of lawyers. You don't have lawyers out here. You have *them*." She nodded at the trawler, at the shapes behind its dark windows. "So here's how this goes. The core comes up from the hold when I see the transfer confirmed. Not before. If your friends have a problem with that, they can swim down and negotiate with the vault themselves. It's only ninety meters. Ask Renn how that went."

Renn, bless him, lifted his bandaged arm in a small, bitter salute.

Halden regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere behind his face a calculation finished, and I had the distinct feeling the result had been *acceptable, for now*. "The transfer will be initiated on confirmation of the item," he said. "A good-faith gesture, Mr. Voss, since your crew seems to require one. We'll hold station. You have one hour." He turned to go, then paused at the rail, and his eyes found Yara's with something that was almost, almost respect. "For what it's worth—whatever's on that core, our principals have waited twenty years for it. They don't intend to wait a day longer. And they are not the government. There will be no inquiry, no scandal, no light. It will simply go into a room, and the room will be locked, and the world will go on not knowing. That's the product. Silence. It's the most valuable commodity there is."

He went down the ladder. The inflatable went back across the water. The trawler held station, patient as a debt.

* * *

We gathered in the wheelhouse, the five of us, and the air was so tight I could hear the compass ticking.

"One hour," Odile said. "So we decide in fifty-five minutes or they decide for us."

"Then we decide now," Yara said. She set the core on the chart table—that dull metal cylinder, heavier than its size, holding a dead ship's last secrets and her mother's voice in its circuitry. She kept one hand flat on it, the way you'd keep a hand on a chest to feel breathing. "Once that thing crosses to their deck, it's gone. Not sold—*gone*. Into a room. Into silence. Twenty years from now some archivist with a clearance will read my mother's voice off a transcript and feel nothing. That's the future they're offering. That's the future Voss signed us up for."

Voss stood by the door with his back to the frame, and to his credit—or whatever you'd call the thing that lived where his credit used to be—he didn't look away from her. "You think exposure saves her? The navy buries its own, Yara. You detonate this in public and they'll spend a decade proving you a liar, a thief, an unstable woman with a dead mother's obsessions. They have practice. I've watched them do it." His voice dropped, and for one moment the salesman's polish fell away and something raw showed through, something that had been a boy on a support ship once, watching a horizon swallow his brother. "I watched them erase a whole ship's worth of people. Not their bodies. Their *having-been*. I am not going to hand them the chance to do it again. I'm going to make them buy their own sin back at retail."

"And the money buys what?" Odile said quietly. "Your brother back?"

The wheelhouse went very still.

Voss looked at her, and I saw the wound take the hit, and I saw him decide, as he'd evidently decided twenty years ago, to keep standing. "The money buys leverage," he said. "Leverage is the only language they speak. I'm sorry none of you learned it earlier."

Yara turned from him. She looked down at the core, at her own hand on it, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—emptied out, the way the sea goes calm before it decides something. "I was eleven when she disappeared," she said. "I spent twenty years moving because staying felt like agreeing to the lie. Every harbor, every season, every crew—gone before anyone could ask me why my eyes looked like that. And I told myself it was freedom. It was just fear with a passport." She lifted her head. "I'm done being afraid in motion. If her story dies, it dies because I chose to end it, not because I sold it to a man who calls silence a product."

"So what's the play?" I asked. "We've got one core, four boarders, a navy ship coming, and a storm that isn't finished with us. We can't outrun anyone. We can't outgun anyone."

"No," Yara said. And then she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman who has finally found the exact shape of the problem and discovered it has a door in it. "But we don't have to. They all want the same thing—the navy, the buyer, Voss. They want the *vault* to stay a vault. Every one of them is betting that the truth is a thing you can own." Her hand came off the core. "So we make it a thing nobody can own."

I looked at Renn. Renn looked at Odile. Odile looked out the wheelhouse window at the gray water, at the trawler waiting on its money, at the smudge on the eastern horizon that was a destroyer coming to collect its government's shame, and she said the thing we were all already thinking:

"You're going back down."

"Ninety meters," Yara said. "In deteriorating weather. Against a clock." She was already reaching for her logbook, already the diver again, already moving toward the water the way she'd been moving away from everything else her whole life. "The vault has a main seal and a flood vent. I've seen both. If I can't take the truth with me, and I can't give it away—" she snapped the logbook shut, "—I'll drown it. All of it. Every file. Every name they buried."

"And the core?" I asked. "The recording?"

She looked at me, and her eyes were the exact color of deep water, which is the color of things that have decided.

"That part," she said, "is already mine."

Outside, the trawler's crane began to unfold.
