## 16. Last Dive

The storm arrived the way bad news always did—first as a rumor on the horizon, then all at once, everywhere.

Yara stood at the Thessaly's stern rail and watched it come, a wall of bruised cloud shouldering across the sea from the southwest, dragging its skirt of white water behind it. The wind had already turned. It came off the water smelling of iron and cold, and it pushed against her face like a hand that wanted her to step back.

Behind her, the ship was a lit box of argument. She could hear Odile's voice through the wheelhouse glass, low and hard, and Dez answering in the clipped rhythm he used when he was frightened and refusing to say so. Renn sat on the deck bench with his arm wrapped in compression bandages, his skin still gray at the edges from the hit he'd taken two days ago, watching her with the patient look of a man who had already made up his mind about something and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

The navy ship hung off their port quarter, four kilometers out, running lights burning through the rain's first ragged sheets. Beyond it, farther south, the buyer's trawler sat dark and low in the water like a thing pretending to be a boat. Two predators circling the same kill, and between them, ninety meters down, the vault with everything inside it—her mother's handwriting, the experiment, twenty years of buried truth rotting slowly in the cold.

And Voss below decks with his satellite phone, selling pieces of it to whoever paid.

Yara turned away from the rail.

* * *

She'd spent the evening doing arithmetic no one asked her to do. The core was aboard—that much they'd won—but the vault itself was still intact down there, sealed behind its blast door stamped with her mother's unit designation, holding whatever records the navy hadn't recovered, whatever backups existed of the quieting. As long as it stayed whole, it was leverage. It was merchandise. It was a wound someone else could reopen and sell tickets to.

Voss wanted to sell it. The navy wanted to bury it again, deeper this time. The buyer wanted to own it. Nobody—not one soul on this stretch of ocean—wanted what Yara wanted, which was for it to simply stop existing.

So she'd gone through the magazine locker while Dez slept and Odile stood her watch, and she'd counted out the cutting charges herself: four shaped slabs of C-4, detonator cord, timers rated to depth. Salvage tools. Perfectly legal in a working diver's kit, if anyone ever asked, which no one would, because by morning there would be nothing left down there to ask about.

She packed them into a dive bag with the calm hands of a woman defusing her own life. She wrote coordinates on her slate. She checked her gas mix twice, then a third time, because fear makes you careful or careless and she refused to let it choose for her.

Then Renn was standing in the doorway of the gear room, and she hadn't heard him come in, which frightened her more than anything else that night.

"No," he said.

"You don't know what I was going to ask."

"I know what you were packing." He leaned against the doorframe, favoring his left side where the bubble had lodged in his shoulder joint, and crossed his good arm over his chest. "You're going down alone, in building seas, at night, to rig explosives on a structure the navy has been trying to keep people out of for twenty years. And you're going to tell me I'm not coming."

"Renn—"

"My shoulder works." He rolled it as he said it, and she saw him pay for the motion through his teeth. "My ears cleared on the surface interval. My brain is fine, which is more than I can say for yours right now."

"It's not fine down there. You took a hit at sixty meters. Another dive could put you in a wheelchair."

"Another dive could put me in a wheelchair," he agreed. "Staying on this boat puts me in a different kind of chair—the kind where I sit and watch you not come back up." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You taught me the first rule of salvage, Yara. Never send anyone into a wreck you wouldn't enter yourself. Well. Same rule applies in reverse."

She looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the porthole. Somewhere aft, the generator coughed and steadied.

"If you come," she said finally, "you follow my count. Every meter, every minute. If I say surface, we surface, even if the job isn't done."

"And if you say surface when the job isn't done?"

"Then we live with it."

He nodded once, slow, like a man signing something. "Then I'm your backup."

* * *

They went over the side at 0300, in the black hour before the storm's leading edge, when the sea had gone strangely, treacherously calm—as if the ocean were holding its breath too.

The descent line ran off the stern into water so dark it seemed solid. Above them the Thessaly's lights shivered and smeared in the chop; below, nothing but the green-black throat of the deep, and somewhere at the bottom of it, the Meridian, waiting the way it had waited for twenty years.

Yara went down the line hand over hand, feeling the pressure close around her like a fist tightening by degrees. At thirty meters the last warmth left the water. At fifty, her light began to fail against the murk kicked up by the storm swell above. At seventy, the wreck rose toward her out of the gloom—a vast darker darkness, the hull's broken spine silhouetted against the faint phosphorescence stirred by the surge.

Wrong, still wrong, after three dives. Breached from the inside. Scorched corridors. Cargo cradled intact in the holds as though the ship had died around its secret rather than because of it.

Renn's light found hers beside her. He gave the okay signal, and she returned it, and they dropped the last twenty meters together into the cold that lived at the bottom of the world.

The vault door stood where it always stood, amidships on the second deck, and seeing it again did to her what it always did: stopped her breath for a beat, made the regulator feel suddenly loud in her ears. Her mother's unit designation, stenciled in faded yellow paint. MERIDIAN EXPEDITIONARY UNIT 7 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Twenty years of silt had softened the letters but not erased them. Nothing down here got erased. That was the whole problem.

She unslung the charge bag and went to work.

Her hands knew this part. Cutting charges were honest things—they did exactly what you told them, no more, no less—and there was comfort in that, in a night full of lies. She pressed the first slab flat against the hinge assembly, molded it to the steel with her palms, seated the det cord in its channel. The second went high on the frame. The third and fourth she placed at the base, angled inward, so the blast would drive the door's own mass back into the corridor beyond, collapsing the entryway under tons of shifted hull plating.

Not blown open. Buried. There was a difference, and the difference was the point. Anyone who came after—navy divers, salvage crews, buyers with deep pockets and longer arms—would find a tomb, not a treasure. They'd have to move half a deck of the Meridian to reach what was behind that door, and by the time they did, the inquiry Yara intended to force into existence would be sitting on top of the site like a lid.

Assuming she surfaced to start it.

The thought arrived without permission, the way the worst thoughts do, in her mother's voice: *what if the charges go early?*

She killed it. Fear was just another current; you didn't fight it, you read it, and you moved with it until it passed. Her father had taught her that on her first open-water dive, when she was nine and panicking in a kelp forest off Point Reyes, and he'd held her shoulders underwater and looked at her until she breathed. *The sea doesn't want you dead, little fish. It just doesn't care. Your job is to care enough for both of you.*

She cared enough. She set the timers for forty minutes—enough to clear the wreck, complete a hang at fifteen meters, and be aboard before the blast wave came looking for them. She armed each unit with a twist of the firing pin that felt, absurdly, like cocking a gun. Then she backed away into the corridor, and her light swept the vault door one last time.

*Goodbye,* she thought, and didn't know if she meant the door, or the woman whose name was painted on it, or the version of herself who'd spent twenty years diving away from both.

Renn touched her arm. He pointed aft—their exit route along the second-deck passageway—and she nodded, and they swam out of the Meridian together, leaving the charges ticking silently behind them in the dark.

* * *

The trouble started at the deco stop.

Fifteen meters, hanging on the ascent line, breathing off the stage bottle while the storm's first real punch landed overhead. Yara felt it through the water as a rhythmic shoving, the whole ocean leaning on them, and when she looked up she couldn't see the Thessaly anymore—only a smeared yellow glow where her lights should have been, wavering like something drowning.

Then a new light. White, hard, raking down through the water column from the north.

Renn grabbed her arm and pointed. Through the murk, maybe sixty meters distant, shapes were descending—divers, multiple, on a drop line of their own, their torch beams cutting frantic cones through the soup. Navy. It had to be. Coming down to secure the vault ahead of the storm, ahead of the buyer, ahead of sense.

Yara's heart slammed against her ribs so hard she felt it in her mask. Forty minutes until the charges fired. The navy divers were descending directly toward a wreck rigged with four slabs of military-grade explosive, and they didn't know, and there was no way to warn them except—

Except there wasn't. No radio that reached. No slate big enough. No time.

She looked at Renn. His eyes behind his mask were wide, and he shook his head slowly—not *no*, she realized, but *I don't know either.*

The navy divers sank past them at a distance, oblivious, their bubbles streaming upward through her light like a handful of thrown pearls. One of them glanced toward the ascent line, and for one suspended second Yara looked straight into a stranger's face behind glass—another diver, another child of somebody, hanging in the same cold water for reasons neither of them chose.

Then he was gone, down into the dark, toward the door she had just wired to kill.

Yara pulled herself up the line. There was nothing else to do. The math was merciless: stay and try to signal and die of hypothermia or bends or worse, or ascend and pray the navy team cleared the wreck before her timers ran out.

She climbed. Renn climbed beside her. And at ten meters, with the storm hammering the surface into white chaos above them and the seconds bleeding away below, Yara Halloran made the only choice left that was actually hers.

She let go of the line and finned hard for the Thessaly's shadow, knowing that whatever happened next, the vault was going to speak tonight—one way or another—and every soul on this water was going to hear it.
