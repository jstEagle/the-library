## 17. Ninety Meters Down

The storm had not broken yet. It hung over the water like a held breath, and Yara went down through it anyway.

She rolled off the Thessaly's rail at 0340, in the black hour between the buyer's trawler anchoring to the east and the navy ship closing from the south, and the sea took her the way it always did—completely, without negotiation. The water was warmer than the air, which felt like a betrayal. Above her, the storm clouds ate the last of the moon. Below her, ninety meters of nothing.

Renn splashed in beside her thirty seconds later. She heard him before she saw him: the double-tap of his fins, the rasp of his breathing through the regulator he'd insisted was fine, even though the doctor aboard the buyer's vessel—of all people—had told him another dive could stop his heart. He'd looked at her when he said it, not at the doctor.

"You didn't have to come," she'd said.

"Yeah, I did." That was all. Some debts don't get argued about.

They dropped together through the thermocline at fifteen meters, where the temperature fell away beneath them like a floor giving out, and then they were in the cold country, the deep green-black where the Meridian lived. Yara ran her lights low. A full beam at this depth was an invitation; every predator and every man with a rifle understood light the same way. She kept it narrowed to a pencil-thin spill across her gauge console and let the wreck announce itself the way it always did—not by sight but by pressure, by the way the water seemed to lean around something enormous that wasn't moving.

The hull came up out of the dark at sixty meters like a coastline.

Even now, after four descents, it stopped her breath for a second. Two hundred meters of naval research vessel lying on its starboard side in the silt, its superstructure crushed into the seabed, its flanks furred with twenty years of cold-water coral that made the steel look almost soft, almost alive. The breach gaped amidships—the wound torn from the inside, edges bent outward like petals—and beyond it the corridors ran back into the dark, scorched black, cargo holds still sealed, still intact, still holding whatever the navy had spent two decades pretending never existed.

And at the end of corridor C, behind three bulkheads and a flooded lab, the vault.

Her mother's unit designation stamped into the door plate. *MERIDIAN TRIAL — PERSONNEL ONLY.* The quieting. The experiment that had reached into two hundred minds and wiped them clean, and then reached further, rewriting what grew back. Her mother had volunteered to prove it could be undone. Her mother's voice lived now on a recorder in a dry bag against Yara's chest, and the data core sat in a case clipped to her harness, and both of those things were why there were two ships full of armed men converging on this patch of empty ocean tonight.

The core was bait now. Everyone wanted it. So Yara was going to make sure no one got it.

* * *

The charges rode in a mesh bag slung under her left arm: six shaped charges, marine-grade, wired to a receiver that would listen for a coded acoustic pulse from the surface. Dez had built them out of cutting charges and prayer. "You put these on right," he'd said, pressing them into her hands on the deck, "and that vault becomes part of the geology. Nobody opens it. Not with a drill, not with God."

"And if they find them before you send the signal?"

"Then you swim faster than bullets." He hadn't smiled. Neither had she.

Now she swam along the Meridian's flank, following the guide line Odile had rigged on the first dive, gloved hand over gloved hand, the line humming faintly with current. Ninety meters meant eleven minutes of bottom time on their tables, twelve if they lied to themselves. Nitrogen was already sitting down inside her skull like a guest who intended to stay, making the dark feel warm and reasonable, making her thoughts arrive slightly delayed, like sound across a valley. She counted her breathing. Four counts in, six counts out. Her father's voice, or the memory of it: *the sea doesn't care how brave you are, girl, only how careful.*

Her father, who had made the whole crew swear off this site on his deathbed. Her father, whose oath she'd broken on the first descent, and who, she suspected, would have broken it too, if it had been her mother down here instead of his wife's ghost.

The breach opened ahead, a ragged arch of torn steel. She crossed the threshold into the wreck.

Inside, the Meridian was a throat. Their lights found scorch marks fanning across the overhead, decades-old fire frozen mid-scream, and the silt lay thick as snowfall over everything—over overturned chairs, over a coffee cup fused to a desk by heat, over a child's photograph in a cracked frame that Yara refused to look at directly because she knew what happened to her heart when she looked at things like that at ninety meters. Renn's light swept wide behind her, covering her six the way they'd drilled it on the surface, wordless, because talking at depth wasted air and nitrogen made words slippery anyway.

Corridor C. Bulkhead one, hatch open, rusted on its track. Bulkhead two, half-collapsed, a gap they'd widened with a hydraulic spreader on the third dive. She squeezed through sideways, the mesh bag scraping steel, and felt the vault before she saw it—the way you feel a wall in a pitch-dark room.

There it was. Round, massive, bolted into the frame of the old secure archive, its face furred white with bacterial mat that glowed faintly under her light like frost. The stamp above the wheel: her mother's designation. *LT. E. HALLORAN — TRIAL AUTHORIZED.*

Yara pressed her glove flat against the cold metal, just for a second. Just long enough to feel stupid and human and nine years old again, waiting at a harbor gate for a woman who never walked back through it.

Then she got to work.

* * *

The first charge went on at the wheel housing, magnetic base clamped, strap cinched. The second at the hinge assembly. The third and fourth flanking the door plate itself, angled inward, so the blast would drive the door *into* the mechanism and fuse the whole assembly into a single welded mass. Dez's geometry. She checked each seat twice, ran her thumb along the contact surfaces, cleared away the bacterial mat so the magnets bit bare steel. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and was grateful, because somewhere below the level of thought her body understood what her mind was refusing to calculate: that she was rigging explosives onto the only door between herself and her mother's last work, and that once it blew, there would be no going back, ever, for anyone.

Good, said some voice in her that sounded like Odile. Doors are how they get you.

Bulkhead two boomed.

Not a sound—sound traveled fine down here—but a pressure wave, a soft fist against her chest and ears, and her light snapped toward the gap they'd widened and found nothing but silt drifting through it in slow coils. Something heavy had shifted somewhere in the wreck. Or something heavy had entered it.

Renn's hand closed on her shoulder. Squeezed once. *Heard it too.*

She keyed her slate and wrote in the glow: **TIME?**

**7 MIN BOTTOM.** He held it up. His eyes behind the mask were calm in the way that scared her more than panic would have. Renn calm meant Renn running on stubbornness and damaged tissue and loyalty, and none of those kept good time.

Fifth charge. Sixth. She wired the receiver to the lead line, taped the antenna clear of the hull, and was reaching for the firing check when her light caught movement in the floodwater beyond the collapsed section—a shimmer, wrong, rhythmic. Bubbles. A thin silver rope of them rising through the drowned corridor, steady as a metronome.

Divers. Someone else's divers.

* * *

They came through the collapse in a wedge, three of them, and their lights hit the vault chamber like searchlights. Rebreathers—no bubble trails of their own, which meant military, which meant the navy had stopped pretending to be a patrol boat and started being what it had been all along. Their suits were black. Their movements were economical and fast, and the lead diver's light found Yara's face and stayed there, and even at ninety meters, even through a mask, she recognized the expression. She'd seen it on harbor cops and customs men and the two coast guard liars who'd boarded the Thessaly. It was the look of a man doing a job that required him to forget you were a person.

The lead diver pointed at the charges. Then at Yara. Then made a flat, chopping gesture: *stop.*

Yara pulled the firing check from the receiver and held it up between two fingers, where he could see it, and let him understand the arithmetic. Six charges. One dead-man protocol—if the receiver lost its acoustic link for more than ten minutes, Dez's failsafe armed the lot automatically. She didn't know if the diver could read the hardware at a glance. She banked on him assuming the worst, because men like him were paid to assume the worst.

It bought her eight seconds.

The second diver moved. Not at her—at Renn, cutting the angle through the flooded lab, trying to split them, and Renn turned to meet him and the chamber dissolved into light and silt. Fins churned the twenty-year sediment off the deck and the world went white-brown, zero visibility, a blind room full of tanks and steel and strangers. Yara's own light became a weapon turned against her, scattering off the cloud, showing her nothing but her own hands and the receiver box and the vault wheel looming out of the murk like a moon.

She couldn't see Renn. She couldn't see any of them. She could hear them—regulators rasping, a tank banging steel, someone's boot striking the deck plates—and she could feel the water moving in currents that weren't currents, displaced bodies pushing water in pulses, close, too close.

The slate was useless. Her knife came out instead.

Something grabbed the mesh bag. Empty now, but still slung under her arm, still tethered to her harness by habit, and the pull yanked her sideways off the vault face. She slashed down and back without aiming, felt the blade skip off something hard—tank, probably, God let it be a tank—and the grip released. Then hands on her regulator hose, hauling, and she was spinning, the silt swallowing everything, her gauge console cracking against the door plate, and through the roar in her ears she heard it:

A gunshot. Underwater. Muffled into a *whump* she felt in her teeth more than heard, and the water carried the shockwave through her chest like a struck bell.

No. No, no—

"RENN!" She screamed it into her regulator, useless, wasting air, wasting everything, and clawed her way through the murk toward where he'd been, following the memory of his position because there was nothing else to follow. Her light found a fin. Found a leg. Found Renn braced against the vault itself, one arm wrapped around the charge line, his other hand pressed flat against his outer thigh, and even in the brown blindness she could see the blood—not gushing, not arterial, but threading out of him in a slow ribbon that the silt drank immediately. A graze. The round had skipped off the door plate or the deck and opened his thigh on its way to nowhere, and he had not let go of the line. He had taken the shot standing between the bullet and the wire, and he was still holding on.

His eyes found hers through the fogged masks. He gave a thumbs-up. Then he pointed past her, urgent, and she turned.

The lead navy diver was coming around the vault's far curve, and he had a cutting tool in his hand, and he was going for the charge line.

Later—in the inquiry, in the reports, in the version of events the navy would spend years laundering—this moment would be described in careful passive language. But there was nothing passive in it. There was Yara Halloran at ninety meters, narced and furious and out of time, doing the only thing left that was hers to do.

She hit the receiver's manual arm switch, then ripped the acoustic link module free of its socket and drove it into the silt at the base of the wall, ten meters from the charges, buried where no cutter would find it in a day of searching. The failsafe clock started. Ten minutes. When it expired, the link loss would arm the firing circuit, and the circuit would sit there live and patient until Dez sent the pulse from the surface—or until someone touched the wrong thing.

The navy diver froze, his light sweeping the silt where the module had vanished, and Yara watched him do the same arithmetic she'd offered him earlier, except now the numbers were worse. Cut the line and maybe trigger a live armed circuit you can't see. Leave it and report that the target rigged the vault with anti-tamper charges. Either way, his tidy salvage operation was now a bomb-disposal problem, and bombs were not his job.

He looked at her one more time. And in the churn of silt and blood and failing light, Yara saw something pass across his face that she chose to believe was respect, though it might only have been exhaustion. He chopped the signal again—*surface, now*—and this time it wasn't an order. It was advice. Then he and his two divers folded backward into the murk and were gone, bubbles rising in a fading column like a signature on a document neither of them would ever admit to signing.

* * *

Getting Renn out took everything she had.

He could kick, barely. The graze had opened a furrow along the outside of his thigh, ugly but survivable, and the cold had done what cold does—slowed the bleeding, slowed him, slowed the world. She got his arm over her shoulder and swam them both out through bulkhead two, through bulkhead one, through the breach, and the Meridian let them go the way it had let her go every time: reluctantly, its corridors pulling at her fins like fingers, its darkness full of names.

At fifty meters she hung on the ascent line and ran the stops with Renn's weight dragging at her and her computer blinking warnings she chose to interpret as suggestions. At thirty meters the silt below them lit up—floodlights from the navy ship, stabbing down through the water column in great trembling pillars, turning the sea into a cathedral of dust and glare. Somewhere above, engines. Somewhere above, the buyer's trawler answering with lights of its own, and between them, small and battered and stubborn, the Thessaly, riding her anchor with her running lights burning like a lit candle in a windstorm.

At ten meters, Renn tapped her arm and pointed up, and she followed his finger to the surface, which was no longer a surface at all but a ceiling of storm-light and search-beams and rain beginning to fall in silver needles, and she thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with nitrogen:

Everything that happens next happens up there.

She kicked for it, one arm locked around Renn, the vault armed and ticking behind her in the dark, and broke through into the noise.
