## 20. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and old paper, and the light through its tall windows was the flat gray of a sea that has decided not to decide anything yet.

Yara Halloran had spent twenty years learning to read water—the way it darkened before weather, the way a current lied about where it was going by showing you only where it had been. She had never learned to read rooms like this one. The long table of officers in dress whites and civilian suits. The stenographer with her fingers poised above keys like a diver hovering at the edge of a drop-off. The microphone in front of Yara's chair, small and black as a piece of salvage, waiting to be raised from whatever silence it had been resting in.

They had given her water in a glass pitcher. They had given her a lawyer she hadn't asked for, a thin man named Corrigan who kept whispering that she was entitled to say less. They had given her a chair bolted too close to the table so that her knees nearly touched the wood, and Yara thought: they do this on purpose. Make you feel like a child called before the headmaster. Make you want to shrink.

She did not shrink. Shrinking was for people who planned to stay.

"State your name for the record," said the officer presiding—Rear Admiral something-or-other, his voice the color of wet gravel.

"Yara Nadezhda Halloran." Her mother's name in the middle of hers, spoken aloud in this room for the first time. It tasted like salt.

The admiral's pen paused for half a second. He'd read the file. He knew whose name that was.

* * *

They began, as she'd known they would, with the salvage. The Thessaly's survey permits. The coordinates. Whether Cassian Voss had held legitimate rights to the Meridian site or merely purchased the appearance of them. Question after question laid down like fathoms on a chart, each one designed to keep her in safe water, away from the reef.

Yara answered what was true and let the rest lie on the bottom.

Yes, Voss had hired the crew. Yes, Odile Marchand had signed the contract without Yara's initial consent. Yes, they had made four dives to the wreck in conditions that exceeded recommended safety margins; yes, Renn Okonkwo had suffered decompression sickness on the second descent; yes, he had taken a graze from a navy diver's sidearm ninety meters down, and yes, she understood how that sentence sounded when spoken into a microphone in a room with no water in it at all.

"And the charges you detonated," said a woman in a charcoal suit who sat slightly apart from the uniformed officers, taking notes in a hand so small Yara couldn't read it from six feet away. "You destroyed a sealed archive aboard a naval vessel. Do you understand what may have been lost?"

"I understand exactly what was lost," Yara said. "That's why I did it."

Corrigan made a sound beside her like a man swallowing his own tongue.

The woman in charcoal looked up. "Would you care to elaborate?"

Here it was. The fork in the current. Yara could feel it the way she felt an undertow—not visible, but pulling. She could tell them about the vault door and stop there. She could describe the data core in technical terms, the experiment in euphemism, the recording in generalities. She could give them enough truth to satisfy the letter of their questions and keep the heart of it locked behind her ribs, the way she'd kept everything locked behind her ribs for twenty years. Corrigan wanted her to. The admiral, she suspected, wanted her to even more. A narrow testimony served everyone in this room except the dead.

But her mother had volunteered to prove the quieting reversible. Had walked into the erasure of her own mind believing memory could be found again if someone went looking honestly enough. And here was Yara, being offered the same choice in a different shape: say less, stay safe, keep moving—or go all the way down and see what surfaces.

"The Meridian wasn't a research vessel," Yara said. "Not in any sense that word is meant to carry. It was a trial site. The navy tested a procedure on its own crew—a method for rewriting identity. Not erasing memory. Rewriting it. Making people into whoever the procedure needed them to be." She heard her own voice come out level, almost gentle, and hated how gentle it sounded, because there was nothing gentle underneath it. "My mother was one of the volunteers. Dr. Ilse Halloran, biopsychology unit. She believed it could be undone. The last entry in her log says she intended to prove it."

The room went very still. Even the stenographer's hands stopped.

"You're claiming," the admiral said slowly, "that this operation was conducted on serving personnel. Without consent."

"With consent," Yara said. "Consent obtained from people who were told it was a sleep-study protocol. My mother figured out what it actually was because she helped build it. That's in the logbook. I can tell you where to find the logbook, because unlike some parties in this room, I have no interest in it disappearing."

The woman in charcoal wrote something. The admiral's jaw worked. Somewhere behind Yara, a door opened and closed softly, and she thought of pressure equalizing.

"Why destroy it, then?" the woman asked. "If the evidence was so valuable. If it proved what you claim. Why flood the vault?"

Because Voss wanted to sell it. Because the buyer's trawler was sitting offshore with armed men drinking bad coffee waiting for dawn. Because the navy ship was closing at flank speed with its searchlights already warming up. Because once her mother's voice left the Thessaly it would die in a private vault, priced and owned, and Yara would spend the rest of her life running from the knowledge of what she'd let happen.

But that wasn't the whole answer either, and Yara was done with half-answers. Half-answers were just lies wearing wetsuits.

"Because a secret like that doesn't set anyone free," she said. "It just changes who owns you. I've been owned by it for twenty years without ever seeing the paperwork. So I gave it away. All of it. To every person standing on that deck, on every ship in that anchorage, over loudspeakers, in my mother's own voice." She met the admiral's eyes. "You can charge me with destruction of naval property. You can take my certifications, my licenses, my right to work these waters. But you cannot put that recording back in a box, and neither can I, and that is the only justice available to anyone in this story."

* * *

They recessed for forty minutes. Corrigan spent thirty of them explaining, in a low urgent monotone, the many ways she had just complicated her life, and Yara spent them looking out the window at the harbor, where the Thessaly sat at anchor with a scar along her hull plating and a new coat of primer over the welds. Dez was up in the rigging doing something to the forestay that probably violated three regulations. Renn sat on the rail with his legs dangling, watching the water the way a man watches water who has recently been shown what it can do to him and has decided to watch anyway.

Odile stood on the foredeck, arms crossed, talking to no one. Odile always talked to no one when she was counting money in her head. Yara wondered what the numbers looked like now. Bad, still. The mortgage on Odile's boat hadn't drowned, whatever else had.

"They'll offer you a deal," Corrigan was saying. "Cooperation in exchange for leniency. Limited disclosure. If you'd let me frame your earlier statement—"

"No."

"Ms. Halloran—"

"My mother spent her last known hours writing the truth down in a logbook on a ship full of people who were having their minds rewritten," Yara said. "I'm not going to testify in fragments. Fragments are how they got away with it the first time."

Corrigan looked at her for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, he nodded—once, slow, like a man conceding a point to the sea. "All right," he said. "Then we do it your way. God help us both."

* * *

The afternoon session brought the woman in charcoal forward again, and this time she came with a folder.

Yara knew folders. In twenty years of harbors and hiring halls and back offices, she had learned that a folder produced mid-hearing was either a weapon or an offering, and the difference was usually in how the person holding it tilted the cover toward the light.

"We've completed a preliminary records cross-reference," the woman said. "Regarding personnel transferred ashore from the Meridian program in the months preceding the sinking. You should be aware that much of this remains classified, and that what I'm about to share does not constitute—" she paused, choosing the word carefully, "—an apology. Or an admission. Yet."

"Just say it," Yara said.

The woman opened the folder. "Dr. Ilse Halloran was listed among eleven program participants relocated under new identities following the termination of the trial. Standard practice, we believe, for subjects whose memories of the program posed a security risk. Relocation was handled by an office that—" another pause, another careful word, "—no longer exists in its former form."

The room receded. Yara heard the words arrive one at a time, like objects coming up on a lift bag, breaking the surface one after another after twenty years underwater.

"She survived," Yara said. "The sinking."

"There is no record of Dr. Halloran aboard the Meridian at the time of its loss. Transfer documentation places her ashore nine days prior, under the name—" the woman turned a page, and Yara watched her lips move around the syllables before she spoke them, "—Ilse Marren. Resident subsequently of a town called Greywater Point, on the coast north of here. Records indicate she lived there three years."

Three years. The number went through Yara like a hook through the hand. Twenty years she had carried the drowning as fact, had built her whole restless life on top of it the way you build a house on a fault line and refuse to look at the crack. Her mother had not gone down with the ship. Her mother had been moved off it like cargo, renamed, resettled—and had lived three entire years within a day's drive of the harbor Yara had fled from, while Yara ran the other direction, season after season, port after port, running from a ghost that had been breathing the same air.

"Did she know?" Yara asked. Her voice came out strange, stripped. "When she was relocated. Did she know who she was?"

The woman in charcoal hesitated, and Yara understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the ears, that the hesitation was the answer.

"The procedure was not fully reversed," the woman said quietly. "Partial restoration was documented in follow-up evaluations. She retained procedural memory. Language. Skills." A pause. "There are letters in the file. Unsent. Addressed to a daughter."

The room tilted. Yara gripped the edge of the table and the table held, bolted down, indifferent, and she breathed the way Renn had taught her to breathe at depth—in for four, hold, out for six—because the alternative was to come apart in front of twelve strangers and a stenographer.

Unsent letters. Three years of unsent letters from a woman who remembered how to write but perhaps not entirely why, addressed to a daughter who had spent those same three years moving farther down the coast with every season, running from a disappearance that had never fully happened.

"Are they in the file," Yara said. "The letters."

"They are sealed pending the inquiry's determination. But." The woman closed the folder and slid it halfway across the table, and her small precise handwriting suddenly made sense to Yara—this was a person who had spent her career making tiny marks inside large machines, hoping someday one of them would matter. "I am permitted to note that the presiding officer has discretion regarding materials relevant to a witness's personal history. And that several members of this panel," her eyes flicked, briefly, to the admiral, "believe the Meridian families have waited long enough."

The admiral did not object. That, more than anything, told Yara how deep the scandal already ran, how far the recording had traveled in the weeks since it had played over the Thessaly's loudspeakers into every listening ear in that anchorage. Truth, given away freely, was the one thing that couldn't be recalled or reclassified. It just kept spreading, current-like, finding every crack.

"Take the time you need," the admiral said, and for the first time his gravel voice held something that might, in better light, have been human. "We'll resume tomorrow morning. Ms. Halloran—" he stopped himself, then continued anyway. "For what it's worth. The panel has reviewed the dive logs. Whatever else comes of this, the record will show that your crew dove beyond every safe margin and brought each other home. That counts for something in this service. It should count for something everywhere."

* * *

Evening came down over the harbor like a slow tide. Yara walked the length of the pier with the folder under her arm—she had signed for it, Corrigan hovering, the seal broken in a side office with a paper knife that trembled slightly in her grip, though whether hers or the clerk's she couldn't say.

She didn't open it on the pier. Some things you don't read with gulls screaming and diesel in the air and the whole world watching. Some things you take below, into the quiet, the way you take a body up from depth—slowly, in stages, letting the pressure change gently or it kills you.

Dez was in the galley when she came aboard, and he took one look at her face and put down the knife he'd been using on onions and said nothing at all, which was Dez's particular genius. He poured coffee instead, black, and set it in front of her, and sat across the table with his big hands wrapped around his own mug, waiting.

"She lived," Yara said. "Three years. Greywater Point. Two hundred miles from where I was born and I never—" Her throat closed. She drank the coffee too hot and let it burn, because the burn was simpler. "There are letters, Dez. She wrote me letters and never sent them, and I don't know if that's because she couldn't remember me well enough or because she remembered too well and couldn't stand it."

Dez turned his mug slowly on the table, thinking, steam rising between them.

"Either way," he said finally, "she was reaching for you. That's the part you keep."

From topside came the sound of Renn laughing at something Odile had said, and the creak of the Thessaly riding the evening swell, and beneath it all the endless patient voice of the water against the hull. Yara looked down at the folder, at her mother's handwriting visible through the translucent cover of the first page—*To my daughter, if there is ever a way*—and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd mistaken it for bone.

Tomorrow she would finish the testimony. She would tell them everything, all of it, down to the last fathom, and let the record sink or swim.

Tonight, she untied the string on the folder, and began to read.
