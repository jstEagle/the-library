## 21. Anchorage

The storm had taken the Thessaly's port rail and given back a lesson. That was how Dez thought of it, standing in the boatyard at first light with coffee going cold in his hands, looking at the long gouge where the hull had kissed the navy ship's fender line, the torn stanchions, the hatch cover warped like something that had tried to scream. A lesson written in fiberglass and steel: you can survive anything once, but you'll pay for the reading of it.

Three weeks since the standoff. Three weeks since the searchlights had swept the water and Yara's mother's voice had come out of the loudspeakers like weather, and everyone on every deck had stood very still, listening to a dead woman describe the unmaking of her own mind. Dez still heard it sometimes at night—not the words, just the cadence, calm as a tide table—and he'd stopped fighting it. Some sounds you let through.

The yard smelled of epoxy and salt rot and the particular sweetness of sawdust cut from wet wood. Gulls argued over the scrap bins. Somewhere behind him, Renn was already at work, because Renn was always already at work now, hammering before Dez finished his coffee, as if the man were trying to outbuild his own memory of ninety meters down.

"You're staring," Odile said, coming up beside him with a clipboard she didn't need. She kept the clipboard anyway. It was a captain thing, Dez supposed—a talisman against chaos, a piece of paper that said someone was keeping track.

"I'm assessing."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Brooding doesn't sand anything." She tapped the hull with two knuckles, the way a doctor taps a chest. "Port side stringers are sound. The transom took more than I liked, but Marisol says we can sister it rather than replace. Two weeks if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't."

"And if the money runs out?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. In the three weeks Dez had known her—which was to say, in the eleven years Dez had known her—he'd learned that her silences came in flavors. This one tasted like pride being swallowed.

"The money won't run out," she said.

"Odile."

"It won't run out." She turned the clipboard toward him. On it, in her square handwriting, was an invoice from the yard—paid, stamped, dated nine days ago. "A settlement came through. Anonymous. Covered the mortgage arrears, covered the repairs, covered six months of moorage besides."

Dez looked at the stamp. He thought about whistleblower provisions and naval inquiries and a woman who signed leases the way other people committed crimes—reluctantly, and only after reading everything twice.

"She used her real name?"

"On the testimony, yes. On the settlement—" Odile's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "There isn't a name on it at all. Just a trust account and a note that said *for the Thessaly, with thanks*. Which is the most Yara Halloran sentence ever written, if you ask me. Gratitude with the serial numbers filed off."

Dez laughed despite himself. It felt strange in his chest, laughing in the boatyard, with the gouge in the hull right there like a scar neither of them pretended not to see. But that was the thing about scars—you could look straight at them and still laugh. Maybe especially then.

"She could have kept it," he said. "The settlement. Started over somewhere new. God knows she's had practice."

"She almost did." Odile said it lightly, but Dez caught the weight under it. "I found her at the harbor office at two in the morning, week after we got back, reading bus schedules. Not because she wanted to go. Because she didn't know how to stay. Twenty years of leaving—it's not a habit anymore, Dez. It's a skeleton. You build your whole body around it."

"What changed?"

Odile looked out at the water, at the gray morning light lying flat on the harbor like a sheet pulled up to the chin. "She stayed for the inquiry. Then she stayed for the yard work. Then one day I realized she'd been here a month and hadn't once asked me when we'd be done." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "You don't notice the current stop until you try to swim against it and find there's nothing pushing back."

* * *

They worked through the mornings and most of the afternoons, all five of them, though Voss's absence hung in the rigging like a dropped halyard nobody would climb to fix.

He was still detained. The navy had taken him off the buyer's trawler in handcuffs, and the last anyone had heard, he was cooperating extensively with investigators—cooperating, Dez suspected, the way a shark cooperates with the ocean, taking what it needs and giving nothing back. Odile got letters sometimes. Typed, no return address, each one a masterpiece of self-justification that somehow also contained, buried in paragraph four, genuinely useful intelligence about which naval offices were sitting on which documents. Dez hated that he read them. He hated more that they helped.

"He loved his brother," Yara had said once, when Dez brought it up, scraping old antifouling off the starboard side with the grim rhythm of a woman punishing the boat for existing. "That's the worst part. Everything he did—the sale, the lies, handing us to that trawler—he did it grieving. Grief makes terrible accountants. It balances everything wrong."

"And you forgive him?"

The scraper paused. "No," she said. "But I understand the arithmetic. That's different." And she went back to scraping, and Dez understood that the conversation was over, the way tides are over—they don't end, they just recede beyond your reach.

Marisol came most days, welding the new stanchions, teaching Renn the torch because Renn asked to learn everything now with the hunger of a man who'd been told his blood was against him and decided to argue. The bends had scared him—that was no secret. What surprised Dez was what the fear had made of him. Not cautious exactly. Deliberate. He checked his gauges twice, then a third time, then handed them to whoever was nearest and asked them to check too, and there was no shame in it anymore, none of the old bravado flinching. He'd gone down into the cold and come back up missing something, and instead of pretending otherwise he'd built the checking into himself, like a new bone.

It was Marisol who told Dez, on a Thursday, watching Renn come up the ladder from the slip with paint flecks in his hair: "He asked me about a refresher course. Commercial cert renewal. Deep-water endorsement."

Dez blinked. "Renn? After—" He gestured vaguely downward, at all that pressure, all those meters.

"After," Marisol agreed. "He says the water owes him one. Says he means to collect." She shrugged, sparks catching in her dark eyes. "Me, I think it's simpler. I think he misses it. You can miss a thing that hurt you. Ask any sailor."

* * *

The anonymous settlement bought more than lumber. It bought time, and time, Dez discovered, was the material the Thessaly had really been built from all along.

They fell into rhythms without deciding to. Odile ran the budget and the yard schedule and pretended she wasn't relieved to have both. Renn sanded and welded and quizzed Marisol on metallurgy until she threatened to charge tuition. Dez handled the engine refit—his hands knew that work the way his mother's had known bread dough, without consulting him—and in the evenings he cooked, because a crew that eats together argues less, and this crew had plenty to argue about and needed the handicap.

Yara did whatever was hardest that day. That was her role, Dez realized somewhere in the second week. Whatever task everyone else had been avoiding—the wiring loom fused with corrosion, the bilge pump that needed replacing in a space barely wider than her shoulders—Yara volunteered for it, cheerfully, and disappeared into the boat's guts for hours, and emerged smelling of diesel and satisfied. She was exorcising the ship compartment by compartment. Or maybe herself. With Yara the two projects had always shared a hull.

On the seventeenth day, they raised the repaired rail together—all five of them lifting, Marisol calling the welds, Odile swearing beautifully when it jammed, Renn laughing so hard he nearly dropped his end—and when it seated true, when the bolt slid home and the rail stood whole against the sky, nobody cheered. They just stood there a moment, breathing hard, hands still on the metal, feeling it hold.

"Huh," said Renn.

"Yeah," said Dez.

"That's it? No ceremony?"

"We're salvage divers," Odile said, climbing down. "We fix things. We don't celebrate them. Celebrating implies surprise."

But that night Dez found Yara on the foredeck after everyone else had turned in, sitting with her legs through the lifelines, watching the anchor light double itself in the black water. He sat down beside her without asking. That was their arrangement, unspoken since the beginning: proximity without interrogation.

"The mortgage is paid," he said.

"I know."

"The settlement. Your name nowhere on it. Very mysterious. Very you."

"Mm."

"Yara." He watched the water. "Thank you."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Odile mortgaged her boat for a job I refused to take. Renn nearly died at ninety meters because I pushed one dive too many. You slept in the engine room for a week because you wouldn't leave the pumps alone during the storm." Her voice was even, unhurried, the voice she used for facts. "I didn't pay a debt, Dez. I just balanced the ledger. There's a difference, and it matters."

"How so?"

"A debt means someone's above you. A ledger just means you've been paying attention." She tipped her head back, looking at stars blurred by harbor haze. "Voss taught me that, actually. The one useful thing he ever gave me. He kept score his whole life and never understood that the scorekeeping was the cage. So I'm keeping score too—but mine has an exit clause."

"Which is?"

"When it balances, you stop counting." She smiled, small and real, the first one Dez had seen reach her eyes since the recording. "I think I might be close."

* * *

The refit finished on a Tuesday in late autumn, earlier than Odile's estimate, because the weather held and because five people working a boat they love move faster than any schedule predicts. They took her out for sea trials on a Wednesday, just past the breakwater, engines humming through their new mounts, and Dez stood in the cockpit listening to the Thessaly breathe and felt something in his chest unknot that he hadn't known was tied.

She rode clean. She rode like herself. Ninety meters of cold water and a navy frigate and a storm front full of armed men, and she came back from all of it riding like herself.

Renn dove the check on the running gear himself—shallow, ten meters, warm water, textbook conditions, every gauge triple-checked by Odile on the surface. When he came up, grinning through his regulator, giving the thumbs-up with both hands like a man greeting a crowd, Dez felt his own throat go tight and had no intention of examining why.

"Deep-water endorsement," Odile said later, watching Renn tow himself along the hull, checking the anodes. "He's really going to do it."

"He already did it," Dez said. "The paperwork's just catching up."

That evening they ate on deck—Marisol had joined them, and brought her cousin who was thinking of signing on for the survey season—and the talk was small and easy: fuel prices, a rumor of a wreck off the shoals worth surveying, whether the galley stove could be convinced to burn clean. Ordinary talk. Talk that assumed tomorrow. Dez listened to it the way you listen to rain on a roof you own, and understood, finally, what Odile had meant about home.

He'd spent his whole life thinking home was a place you found or failed to find, a harbor you reached or missed. Sitting there with grease still under his nails and Renn arguing amiably with Marisol's cousin about knot strength, he understood it was smaller and stranger than that. Home was whoever showed up with sandpaper when your hull was torn. Home was a ledger that balanced. Home was people who stayed—not because staying was easy, but because they'd all, at some point, chosen it out loud, even if only by picking up a hammer the next morning.

Yara caught his eye across the deck. She raised her mug an inch—an inch being, for Yara Halloran, a toast of tremendous extravagance—and Dez raised his back.

Later, hauling the cooler below, he passed the chart table and noticed something pinned above it, where Odile kept the invoices and the tide tables. A photograph, old and sun-bleached: the Thessaly younger, brighter, a crew on her deck squinting into some vanished summer. And beneath it, in fresh ink in Odile's square hand, a single line that hadn't been there yesterday:

*Survey season opens spring. Destination pending. All hands willing.*

Dez stood looking at it for a long moment. Then he took the pen from the cup, and beneath Odile's line, in his own careful hand, he wrote:

*All hands.*

And outside, the tide came in, quiet and certain, filling the harbor the way it always had—as if it, too, had finally decided to stay.
