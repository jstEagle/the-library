## 22. The Current That Stays

The cemetery sat on a hill above the harbor, the kind of municipal graveyard that smelled of cut grass and salt and old rain, and I had walked past its gate a hundred times in my life without once going in. That was the trick of running, I understood now. You didn't have to avoid the places that hurt you. You just had to keep moving fast enough that they never caught up.

Today I stopped moving.

The grass was wet from a morning squall, and it soaked through the knees of my trousers as I worked my way down the rows, reading the small brass plaques, most of them green with age, some of them blank. That was the thing about an unmarked grave. It wasn't that there was no marker. It was that the marker had been removed, or never installed, or filed away in some navy archive between a requisition form and a lie. The records Odile had pulled—legitimately, mostly—said that a woman named Maren Halloran, service number 4471-C, had been interred here in the spring, twenty years ago, in a plot the navy had paid for and then declined to visit.

I found the plot by counting. Ninth row, fourteenth space. The ground was flat, unremarkable, indistinguishable from the ground on either side of it. No stone. No name. Just grass growing the way grass grows over anything, indifferent and thorough, the way the sea grows over a wreck.

I stood there for a long time with the recorder in my jacket pocket, and the wind came up the hill from the water and moved through the cypress trees with a sound like breathing.

"I found you," I said, finally. It came out smaller than I'd meant it to. "It took me twenty years and a hundred harbors, but I found you."

The grass didn't answer. It never had. All those years I'd told myself that was why I kept moving—because the dead don't talk, because there was nothing left to find, because the only version of my mother that still existed was the one in my head, and the one in my head was a woman at a kitchen table teaching me knot names, bowline, cleat hitch, sheet bend, her hands moving mine through the loops. I had kept her there, in that kitchen, frozen at the age I was when she left, because going back to the grave meant admitting she'd been somewhere else all along. Somewhere I couldn't follow. Somewhere the navy had built around her like water closing over a hull.

She had lived three more years. That was the number I kept turning over, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath. Three years under a new name—Elena Marsh, of all the flat, forgettable names—in a coastal town I'd actually been to once, when I was nineteen, working a salvage job out of the marina there. I had eaten fish and chips four hundred meters from wherever she was living. I might have passed her on the street. I had spent my whole adult life running from the thing I was looking for, and for three of those years it had been within reach, breathing, buying groceries, probably watching the same water I watched.

Dez would have told me not to do that math. Dez was good at a lot of things, and one of them was knowing which sums ruined you. But I'd had weeks now, weeks of testimony and waiting and sleeping in a real bed, and the math had settled in me like ballast. Three years. It wasn't the number I wanted. It wasn't nothing, either. She had woken up every one of those mornings with a head full of someone else's life, and somewhere in there—this was the part I held onto, the part I'd copied out and kept—somewhere in there was a fragment of me.

I sat down on the wet grass, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me, and I took the recorder out of my pocket.

It was a small thing, a navy-issue field unit, scuffed grey plastic, the kind of object you'd step over on any pier in the world. The casing was scored from twenty years in the effects locker, and the battery compartment had corroded, and I had rebuilt it twice on Odile's galley table with parts from three different radios. Renn had helped with the last repair, his hands steady now, the tremor from the bends finally gone. He'd handed it back to me and said, "You know you don't have to listen to the whole thing again," and I'd said, "I know," and we'd both understood that I would.

I had copied one fragment. That had taken me four nights, because every time I got to it I had to stop, and walk out onto the deck, and breathe the cold air until my eyes stopped burning. But it was done. It was on a chip the size of my thumbnail, and the chip was in my breast pocket, over my heart, which was where I intended it to stay for the rest of my life.

The rest of the recording—the whole of it, her voice moving through those months aboard the Meridian, her voice changing as the experiment changed her, her voice in the last entry going somewhere I couldn't follow and cutting off mid-word—that was going in the ground.

I pressed play one last time.

*—day forty-one. The treatments are holding. I can't remember my sister's face today, but I can remember the boat, and I can remember—*

I stopped it there. Not at the cut-off. I'd learned where to stop it, the way you learn the exact depth at which a wreck stops being a wreck and starts being a grave. The fragment I'd kept was from earlier, from the first week, before the quieting took its toll. Her voice had been clear then, and warm, and a little amused, the way it always was when she talked about me.

*—and Yara, my little anchor, she tied her first bowline today and she wouldn't stop showing everyone on the dock. She's going to be better at this than I ever was. She's going to go further.*

My little anchor.

I sat on her grave and played it twice more, and then I turned the recorder off, and the hill was very quiet, just the wind and the far-off clank of a halyard against a mast down in the harbor, a sound I had heard in a hundred ports and never once tired of.

"I went further," I told her. "You were right. I went too far. But I came back."

I dug with my hands. The ground was soft from the squall, and it came away in dark wet clumps, and I made a hole about the length of the recorder and twice as deep, because I wanted it well under, where no fixer's sonar and no salvage magnet and no ambitious man with a contract would ever find it. The truth about the Meridian was already loose in the world—the inquiry had seen to that, the recording over the loudspeakers had seen to that, the scandal was unfolding in hearing rooms I would never have to enter again. The data didn't need burying. This was something else. This was the part of her that belonged to no one else, the part that wasn't evidence, the part that had never been anyone's to buy.

I wrapped the recorder in oilcloth, the way we wrapped anything we meant to survive the sea, three turns and a hitch, and I held it over the hole for a moment.

"Rest," I said. "I mean it this time. I'm going to rest too."

I put it in. I filled the hole. I pressed the ground down flat with my palms, and the grass closed over it, indifferent and thorough, and there was no marker, and that was right. She'd had twenty years of being a file number and a redaction. She could have twenty more of being just the hill, just the wind, just the water sound. I knew where she was now. That was the whole point. You can run from a place you don't know. You can't run from one you do.

I stayed until the light went long and gold, until the shadows of the cypresses lay across the rows like mooring lines, and then I got up, and my knees were stiff from the wet, and I walked down the hill toward the harbor without looking back. Not because looking back was forbidden. Because I didn't need to. That was the difference. All my life I'd left places without looking back because looking back would have caught me, would have hooked me like a grapnel and held me to a dock I'd already decided to abandon. This time I didn't look back because there was nothing behind me I was trying to escape.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of coffee and old paperwork, and the harbormaster himself—a wide, slow-moving man named Teodor who had watched me come and go through this port for eleven seasons without once learning more than my first name—had the lease spread out on his counter when I walked in.

"Thought you'd want the one with the winter moorage," he said, tapping the page. "It's the same rate year-round if you sign for twelve months. Nobody signs for twelve months."

"I'm signing for twelve months."

He looked up at me over his reading glasses, and I watched him decide whether to say what he was obviously thinking, and I watched him decide to say it anyway. "Eleven seasons, Halloran. You've never booked more than ninety days. I've had a pool going."

"Who's winning?"

"Odile. She bet on this spring." He slid the pen across the counter. "She said, and I quote, 'The girl's going to stop running when she finds what she's looking for, and God help us all, because she'll have to learn how to stay.' I told her you'd sooner sign a lease with the devil."

"Odile knows me." I signed. My handwriting looked strange to me on a document with a full year printed on it—Yara Halloran, moorage 14, term: twelve months. It looked like someone else's name. It looked, I decided, like mine. "Tell the pool I want a cut."

"Pool's closed. Odile collected this morning. She bought a round for the whole yard." He took the lease back, and his voice gentled in the way men's voices do when they're embarrassed to be gentle. "Your mother's name was on the news, you know. The inquiry released the crew list. Proper names, proper honors." He cleared his throat. "There's talk of a memorial. On the water, in the spring. The kind where they drop a wreath."

"I'll be here for it," I said, and the words came out easy, and that was the strangest thing of all. "That's the idea."

* * *

Moorage 14 was at the far end of the guest dock, and the Thessaly sat in her slip looking better than she had any right to, her hull patched and repainted, her rails sanded down to bright bare wood, her name picked out fresh in white against the dark blue. Dez had done the lettering himself, freehand, and had argued for a week about whether to add a second name beneath the first, the way some boats carry a dedication. In the end he'd painted a single small word on the transom, low, where the water would kiss it on a heavy day:

*Stay.*

"It's a superstition thing," he'd said, defensive, when I'd asked. "Boats come home to their names. I figured she should know what we're calling her home to."

The deck was cluttered with the ordinary archaeology of a working vessel in port—coils of line drying, a paint tin with a stir stick standing in it like a flag, Renn's dive gear hung to air along the rail, Odile's charts weighted down with a coffee mug in the wheelhouse. Dez was in the engine room, or under it; I could hear him talking to the main engine in the low, coaxing voice he used on machinery and on frightened animals and, occasionally, on me.

I stepped aboard, and the Thessaly took my weight the way she always had, with a small settling, a small acknowledgment, a boat's way of saying *I know you.*

"Lease is signed," I called down the engine hatch.

A clatter, a curse, and then Dez's head appeared in the opening, upside down, grease on his cheekbone, grinning. "Twelve months?"

"Twelve months."

"Ha!" The shout went up the dock, and Odile's head appeared out of the wheelhouse, and Renn's out of the fo'c'sle where he'd apparently been sleeping off a morning dive, and for a moment the whole boat was faces and noise, my crew, my family on paper and off it, all of them pretending they hadn't been waiting for the news all day.

"Drinks at the Anchor tonight," Odile declared. "Teodor's buying. He doesn't know it yet, but he's buying."

"Voss's lawyer sent a letter," Renn said, more quietly, once the noise had settled. He handed me an envelope, official cream stock, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than most people's dinner. "Forwarded through the inquiry. He's pleading out. Cooperation agreement. He named the buyer, the fixers, the whole chain." Renn's mouth twisted. "He also asked—his lawyer asked—if you'd visit. He says there are things he wants to say to you that won't fit in a deposition."

I looked at the envelope for a long time. I thought about a man on a support ship twenty years ago, a boy watching a research vessel sink below the horizon and being told it was a training accident. I thought about a man who had spent his whole life keeping score because the game had taken his brother and never explained itself. I thought about the difference between us, which had turned out to be almost nothing, and the difference that mattered, which had turned out to be everything: what you do with the wound. Whether you sell it, or bury it, or carry it up the hill and set it down in the wet grass and say *rest.*

"Not yet," I said. "Maybe someday. Tell him—" I stopped. There wasn't anything to tell him yet. "Tell him I read the letter. That's true, anyway."

Renn nodded and tucked the envelope away, and nobody pushed, because that was the other thing about a crew that had been to the bottom together: they knew which hatches stayed shut, and for how long, and that shut was not the same as sealed.

* * *

Evening came on soft, the way it does in that harbor in the late season, the light going amber and then rose and then a deep bruised violet, and the wind dropping with the sun until the water in the basin lay flat as poured glass. I made a pot of coffee in the Thessaly's little galley and carried the mug out to the dock, and I sat down at the end of the finger pier with my legs hanging over the black water, the way I'd sat at the ends of piers in a hundred harbors. But it wasn't the same, and I knew it wasn't the same, and for once the difference didn't frighten me.

Down the dock, lights were coming on in the wheelhouses of the working boats. Somewhere a radio was playing, too far away to make out the song, close enough to make out the warmth. The harbor bar would be filling up—Odile there already, holding court, telling the story of the mutiny vote with herself as the reasonable party; Dez letting her, because Dez understood that a story belongs to whoever tells it best; Renn arriving late and damp, because some people go back in the water the way other people go back to church. I would join them. There was time. That was the whole revelation of the lease, the thing I kept turning over like a coin found on a beach: there was time. Nothing was leaving. Nothing had to be seized tonight, or lost forever, or run from before it ran from me.

The tide was coming in. I could see it at the pilings—the old high-water line dark on the barnacled wood, and the water climbing toward it, patient, certain, the way it had climbed for longer than there had been anyone to watch it. All my life I'd thought of the current as the thing that carried you away. That was the current you felt when you were moving, when you were running, when every harbor was a waiting room and every season a countdown. But there was another current, the one nobody talks about because you can only see it when you stop: the tide that comes back. The water that leaves at dawn and returns at dusk, that goes out to the open sea and turns around and comes home, twice a day, forever, without once asking permission or apologizing or explaining itself.

I had spent twenty years being the current that leaves.

I sat on the end of the dock and watched the tide come in, and I did not check the time. There was no watch on my wrist. There was no departure window in my head, no countdown, no next port already half-packed in my mind. There was a mug of coffee going cool in my hands, and a chip the size of my thumbnail over my heart with two words on it—*my little anchor*—and a boat at anchor behind me with my name on her papers for a full turn of the year, and a grave on the hill with nothing on it that I could find in the dark with my eyes closed, and that was enough. That was, I understood at last, more than most people ever get.

The water reached the old high-water line and slid up over it, quiet as a secret, and kept coming.

Behind me, up the hill, the cypress trees moved in the dark like breathing. Out at the harbor mouth, the first star came out over the water, and the Thessaly's anchor light answered it, one small steady burn, holding.

I stayed.
