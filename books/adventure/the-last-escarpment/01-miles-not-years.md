## 1. Miles, Not Years

The escarpment measured itself in miles, and so did Lena Delacroix. He had stopped counting years somewhere around the fourth spring—years were a currency the lowlands used, a way of keeping score, of stacking up debts and birthdays and sentences. Miles were honest. A mile was a mile whether you were running from something or walking toward it, and in twenty years he had walked enough of them to circle the world twice if the world had been shaped like the rim of the Redstone Escarpment, which it wasn't, which was part of why he loved it.

He rose before the light did, as he always did, in the one-room stone cabin he'd built himself against the base of a shelf cliff forty miles from the nearest road and ninety from the nearest town that mattered. The cabin had a stove, a bunk, a table, three chairs he'd never needed more than one of, and a window that framed a mile of broken country the way other men framed photographs of their families. He boiled water for coffee he drank black because sugar was a thing you had to ride to town for, and town was a thing he did twice a year, in the shoulder seasons, when the passes were open and the weather was indifferent rather than hostile.

He called himself a range steward when anyone asked, which almost no one did. He fixed fences for ranchers who paid him in salt pork and didn't look too closely at his face. He reported range fires to the district office by wire from the relay station at Dry Fork, and once, memorably, he'd pulled a calf out of a wash after a flash flood, and the rancher's wife had pressed five dollars into his hand and told him God would remember his kindness, and he had said nothing, because God remembering him was precisely what he could not afford.

His name in the lowlands was still attached to a story. Not a famous story—famous stories got retried, revisited, printed in the papers until the truth wore smooth as river stone. His story had simply hardened into a fact, the way a bone hardens into fossil, and everyone in three counties knew the fact: Lena Delacroix, the engineer's assistant whose negligence had killed nine people when the Calloway Reservoir dam let go in the spring of his twenty-sixth year. The trial had been quick. The conviction had been certain. He had jumped bond before sentencing, which was the only crime anyone could prove he'd actually committed, and he had come out here, where the land kept no records and the sky asked no questions.

He had been nineteen years old when the dam failed. The trial made him twenty-six, the way trials do—aging a man in the space between accusation and verdict. He'd had a lawyer for six days. The company had had a firm from the capital for six months before the water ever rose.

But that was years, and he did not deal in years.

This morning the miles were these: four miles along the rim to check the rain gauge he maintained for no one, a service he performed because it gave his walking a shape; then down the goat track into Juniper Draw, six miles of switchback through country so broken that a man could stand on one ledge and drop a stone onto another ledge a hundred feet below and a quarter mile distant, and hear it strike a full second later; then the long traverse south along the base of the cliffs, where the water seeps kept the grass green in a land the government maps colored the same dun brown as everything else.

He walked the way he did everything—economically, quietly, with his weight over his feet rather than ahead of them. Twenty years had burned every wasted motion out of him the way a kiln burns water out of clay. He carried a rifle he'd cleaned that morning out of habit rather than need, a canteen, a strip of dried meat, and a notebook in which he recorded, in a small precise hand, the things he saw: bighorn sign on the rim, three days old; a lion kill under the junipers at the draw's mouth; the seep at mile eleven running a finger's width lower than last month. He did not record his thoughts. Thoughts were years. Thoughts were the lowlands catching up with you.

The country was doing its October work around him. The cottonwoods in the draw had gone the color of old coins. The light came in low and long and turned every rock to something carved and deliberate. He saw no one, expected no one, and the not-expecting was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing, holding up everything else.

He was eleven miles out, crouched at the seep with his fingers in the wet sand, reading the tracks of a band of desert bighorn like a man reading a letter from an old friend, when he heard the mule.

That was wrong in three ways. First, nobody came out here in October. The ranchers were shipping cattle, the hunters hadn't drawn tags for this unit, and the government men who occasionally came to count things had finished counting in September. Second, nobody came out here on a mule; the country ate mules. Third, he knew the mule. It belonged to Tull Hargrove, who ran the relay station at Dry Fork, forty miles north, and Tull Hargrove had not ridden south in the six years Lena had been taking his wire, because Tull Hargrove was sixty years old and weighed as much as the mule did.

Lena stood up slowly and put the seep between himself and the sound, an old reflex, the reflex of a man whose face was a fact in three counties.

Tull came around the bend of the draw at a pace that could not be called riding. He was red-faced and sweating despite the cold, and when he saw Lena he did not wave or call out. He just reined in and sat there breathing, looking at Lena with an expression Lena had never seen on him before, and Lena had seen Tull drunk, Tull grieving, Tull the day the relay station's dog got snakebit and died.

"You're a hard man to find," Tull said.

"I'm not hiding," Lena said, which was the most elaborate lie he had told in years, and they both knew it, and Tull let it pass, which was its own kind of message.

"There's a wire come for you."

"I don't get wires."

"You do now." Tull worked something out of his breast pocket—a folded telegram, soft at the creases, as if it had been carried a long way and read more than once. "Come from the district land office. Fella there said you were the only man who'd know this country well enough." He paused. "Said you'd know it better than the maps do."

Lena didn't move. The wind moved, coming down the draw, carrying the smell of sage and cold stone and, faintly, the mule.

"I don't work for the land office."

"I told them that." Tull held out the telegram. "I told them you didn't work for anybody. Fella said that was the point."

Lena took it. His hands were steady. He noticed his hands being steady and was grateful for the small professionalism of it.

The telegram was brief. He read it twice, because the first time the words refused to arrange themselves into sense, and the second time they arranged themselves into something worse than nonsense.

GOVERNMENT SURVEY PARTY SIX MEN LAST REPORTED ENTERING REDSTONE QUADRANT OCTOBER NINE STOP NO CONTACT ELEVEN DAYS STOP WEATHER WINDOW CLOSING STOP DISTRICT REQUIRES EXPERIENCED LOCAL GUIDE STOP COMPENSATION GENEROUS STOP REPLY THROUGH THIS OFFICE STOP.

He read it a third time. Eleven days. Six men. The Redstone Quadrant was the country under his feet, all of it, the rim and the draw and the seeps and the goat tracks, the whole broken shelf of it he had spent two decades learning the way other men learned the faces of their children.

"Survey party," Tull said. "Six of them. Government men. Went up into the escarpment country and just—" He made a gesture with one hand, opening it, letting the nothing fall out of it. "Nobody's heard a word. And now there's weather coming, early this year, and the district office is in a state."

Lena looked up from the paper and out across the country he knew, and felt something he had not felt in twenty years and did not have a name for, because he had never needed one. It was not fear. It was the opposite of the thing he had built his life out of. For twenty years the country had needed nothing from him and he had needed nothing from it, and that mutual indifference had been the closest thing to peace he had ever owned.

Now a piece of paper in his hand was asking him to be found.

"Six men," he said. "Armed?"

Tull shrugged. "Government men. Probably carried rifles the way government men do, to feel official." He shifted in the saddle. "There's something else. The fella at the land office—Verrin's his name, some kind of company man attached to the survey, or attached to the land office, I couldn't tell you which, and he had a way of talking that made me not want to ask. He asked about you, Lena. Not just would you take the job. He asked where you lived. How long you'd been out here. Whether you went by any other name."

The wind came down the draw again. Somewhere above them, on the rim, a raven said the one thing ravens say.

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him you were a range steward and I only knew the relay address." Tull's eyes were steady on him, and in them was the question of a man who had carried a telegram forty miles on a mule and had all forty miles to think. "Lena. I've known you six years. You fix fences. You read sign better than any man I ever saw, and I've seen them all. And once, four years back, a deputy from Harrow Bend came through asking after a fugitive, and you were gone eleven days, and when you came back you'd shaved your beard and you looked different and you never said a word about it, and I never asked. That's the deal we've had, and I've kept it, and I'm keeping it now. But I'll say this much for free. A man who's spent twenty years making sure nobody needs him doesn't get a wire like that by accident. Somebody went looking for you. Somebody wanted you. And in my experience, when a thing wants you, it's because it knows what you can do—and it's worth asking yourself how it knows."

Lena folded the telegram along its soft creases and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where the paper lay like a coal.

"Eleven days," he said. "In that country, in October, six men don't just vanish. There's water enough if you know it, and they'd have had maps, even if the maps are bad. They'd have left a trail a blind man could follow—six men make a road wherever they walk."

"That's what I figured."

"Unless somebody didn't want a trail."

Tull was quiet a moment. "Or unless," he said, "they walked somewhere the maps don't show."

Lena looked south, past the draw, past the traverse, to where the high range stood up against the sky with the first hard light of the season on its shoulders, white already in the couloirs, weeks early. The range that the maps rendered as blank space. The range that no survey party had any honest business entering, because there was nothing in it to survey—no timber worth cutting, no ore worth digging, no grass worth grazing, nothing, nothing, nothing, which was exactly what the maps said, and exactly why he was alive.

"Tell the land office I'll take the contract," he heard himself say, and the voice was his own but it sounded like a man he used to know, a man who signed things, a man whose name went on documents. "Tell them I'll start from the south and work north, and I'll wire them when I have something."

Tull nodded slowly. He didn't ask why a man who had refused every job, every kindness, every offered hand for twenty years would take this one. He gathered his reins, and the mule turned its tired head for home, and then Tull paused, one boot in the stirrup, and looked back.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the fella Verrin smiled when I said you'd take it. And I didn't like his smile."

The mule's hoofbeats faded up the draw, and then the draw was empty, and then the whole country was empty, the way it always was, the way he had made it.

But it didn't feel empty now. It felt watched.

Lena stood a long time at the seep with his hand flat against his chest, over the folded paper, feeling the coal of it burn. Then he turned south, toward the high range and the early snow, and began to measure the miles.
