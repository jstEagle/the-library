## 2. The Contract

Harrow Bend announced itself the way it always did: a smear of dust on the horizon that resolved, over the last three miles, into a water tower, a grain elevator, and a main street built for a future that never came. Lena rode in with his collar up against the grit and let the town do what towns did—look at him sideways and decide he was nobody worth remembering.

That was the arrangement. Twenty years of it. He'd learned to wear anonymity like the coat he wore over it: patched, unremarkable, wrong size on purpose. A man who wanted to be forgotten had to give people something forgettable. He gave them a lean ranger with a wind-burned face and no opinions, who bought flour and lamp oil and cartridges, paid cash, tipped nothing, and left before anyone could learn the shape of his silences.

The telegram had been waiting at the depot for six days, folded twice, the paper gone soft at the creases from being handled by everyone in the building before it reached him.

*TEAM MISSING STOP FOUR MEN STOP LAST REPORTED QUADRANT SEVEN-DELTA STOP ADVISE IMMEDIATE SEARCH STOP CONTACT LAND COMMISSIONER HARROW BEND*

He'd read it standing in the shade of the depot awning while a station agent watched him with the frank boredom of a man who read other people's business all day and found none of it interesting, including his own.

Quadrant Seven-Delta. Lena had turned the designation over in his mind the whole ride down off the escarpment, the way you'd turn a stone looking for what lived under it. He knew every quadrant of that country the way he knew the lines of his own hands—not from maps but from miles. And Seven-Delta was not empty country, whatever the official surveys claimed. It was country with a seep in it, and a spring, and the kind of shaded north-facing draws where snow held late and water held longer.

Nobody surveyed worthless land four times.

Now he sat in the outer office of the Land Commission, on a chair built for shorter men, waiting to be offered a job he already suspected was a lie with paperwork attached.

* * *

Commissioner Aldous Verrin's office smelled of ink and floor wax and the particular staleness of a room where important decisions were made by men who never sweated in them. Verrin himself was younger than Lena expected—fifty at most—with the smooth hands and careful tan of a man who owned a horse but didn't ride one anywhere that mattered.

"Mr. Delacroix." Verrin didn't offer his own name first; he assumed Lena would know it, or wouldn't care. "You come recommended."

"By whom?"

"A man whose judgment I trust." Verrin smiled without engaging anything above his mouth. "Your references are unusual. Mostly they're absences. No fixed address. No bank account. No family listed. In my experience, men with no attachments are either running from something or suited to exactly this kind of work."

"And which do you figure?"

"I don't figure. I pay." Verrin slid a paper across the desk. Lena didn't touch it. "Four hundred dollars. Half now, half on recovery of the survey team or definitive evidence of their fate."

Lena looked at the number and felt the hair on his arms lift. Four hundred dollars was more than a season of guiding. It was more than most men in Harrow Bend cleared in two years. For finding four missing government men in open desert country—a search any competent tracker could run in a week—it was an insult to arithmetic.

"That's too much," Lena said.

Verrin's smile stayed where it was. "Generosity isn't a crime yet, Mr. Delacroix."

"It will be." He leaned back. "Who else did you ask?"

"You were fifth."

"Fifth. So four men said no."

"Two said no. Two wanted more than I was willing to pay." Verrin's voice flattened out. "The others lacked your familiarity with the high range."

"The high range is closed this time of year. Weather comes early up there. Any man who knows the escarpment knows you don't send searchers into it in October unless you've stopped caring whether they come back."

"Then it's fortunate we found a man who knows it well enough to survive it." Verrin laced his fingers together on the blotter. His hands really were very clean. "Mr. Delacroix. Four federal surveyors entered the range on the fifteenth of September. They carried instruments, provisions for thirty days, and a mandate from the Reclamation Office to complete a topographic and hydrographic assessment of Quadrants Six through Eight. On the third of October they sent their last wire from a relay station at Two Buttes. Since then, nothing. No sign at the supply cache. No signal fires. The Reclamation Office wants answers before the fiscal quarter closes and before winter makes those answers permanently unavailable."

"All true things," Lena agreed. "None of them explain the price."

Verrin studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, not quite. Recalculation. The look of a man discovering that the tool he'd ordered had edges.

"You have questions," Verrin said.

"A few."

"Ask them."

"What was the team's final assignment? Before they went dark."

Something happened in the room then. Nothing visible. Just a stillness, like the air pressure changing before weather. Verrin's hands didn't move. His expression didn't change. But the office got quieter around him, the way a camp gets quieter when a stranger walks in.

"Why does that matter?"

"Because searches start where the trail starts," Lena said. "And trails end where the work ended. If I'm going to find these men, I need to know what they were doing when they stopped being findable."

"They were conducting a routine assessment."

"Of what?"

"Terrain. Water resources. Standard."

"In Seven-Delta."

"In the general region."

Lena nodded slowly, as if satisfied, and watched Verrin watch him right back. Neither man blinked much. Outside, someone drove past with an unloaded wagon, the boards clattering on the hardpan.

"There's a spring in Seven-Delta," Lena said conversationally. "Comes up out of a fault line under the red shale. Runs maybe eight gallons a minute year-round, more in spring melt. There are three more like it in the quadrants you named. None of them appear on any map I've ever seen, including yours." He paused. "I know because I've drunk from all four."

For the first time, Commissioner Verrin looked genuinely interested, and the interest had teeth in it.

"That's local knowledge," he said carefully. "Valuable. Not relevant."

"Not relevant," Lena repeated.

"The team's findings are sealed pending review. That's normal procedure. What matters to me—" Verrin leaned forward slightly, "—is location. Where are the men. What happened to them. Anything beyond that is the government's business, not a contractor's."

*There it is*, Lena thought. *There's the line drawn in the sand, and here's me standing on the wrong side of it asking why.*

"Sealed findings," he said aloud. "From a routine assessment."

"These things take time to verify."

"Sure." Lena stood. The chair scraped. "Here's how it'll be, Commissioner. I'll take the contract. Two hundred now, like you say. I'll go up there and I'll find your survey team or I'll find what's left of them, and I'll bring you a report saying so. But I'm not going to pretend I didn't notice that a routine job pays four hundred dollars, that a routine quadrant has four unmapped springs in it, and that a routine commissioner gets quiet when asked what his missing men were measuring."

He took the paper off the desk and signed it against his knee because signing it on the desk would have meant sitting back down.

Verrin counted out two hundred dollars in bills so crisp they could have been printed that morning—they probably had been—and slid them across with two fingers.

"One condition," Verrin said as Lena pocketed the money. "If you find documents out there—field notes, instruments, anything with government markings—you bring them to me. Unread. Sealed. That's part of the contract."

Lena looked at him.

"Unread," he repeated.

"Standard chain-of-custody requirement."

"For a search contract." Lena shook his head once, slow. "You keep using that word. Routine. Like if you say it enough times it'll be true."

"Is that a problem?"

"No," Lena said. "It's a map."

He left the money where it was, took the contract copy, and walked out into the white heat of Harrow Bend at noon, feeling the town's eyes slide over him and away, feeling—for the first time in twenty years—the strange, unwelcome sensation of being needed, and the older, colder sensation underneath it, familiar as an old scar:

The feeling of walking into something that had been arranged for him before he arrived.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he always did in Harrow Bend, which was nothing that could be remembered. He resoled a boot at the cobbler's. He bought salt pork and coffee at the mercantile, letting the clerk chatter about the weather and the price of wool while he listened to the store instead—to the rhythm of the town's talk, the way sound moves through a place and tells you what's living in it.

He heard the survey team's names three times. Once from a freight driver who'd hauled their crates up to Two Buttes and described them as "government men, polite enough, kept to themselves." Once from a saloon girl complaining that they'd tipped in scrip. And once, and this was the one that mattered, from two men at the corner table of the Silver Dollar, speaking low over beer, in the tone of men discussing something they'd been told not to discuss.

"—told him straight out, you don't want to be running lines up there. That country's not as empty as the maps say."

"Who told him?"

"Doesn't matter who. Point is, the survey lead laughed about it. Said every rock between here and the divide was already catalogued." A pause. The scrape of a chair. "Well. Somebody should tell that to whoever's paying for the search."

Lena nursed his coffee at the bar and didn't turn around. He filed the words where he filed everything—under distance, under later—and finished his drink, and went out into the street where the light was turning the color of old brass.

One stop remained. The livery, where he boarded his horse when he came down off the mountain, and where the stableman, a half-Arapaho old man named Tomaso who'd never once asked Lena a direct question in fifteen years, kept a feed bill current and a mouth shut.

Tomaso saddled the bay without being asked and handed the reins over the rail.

"Heard you're going up after the survey outfit," the old man said. Not a question. Tomaso never asked questions; he made statements and let you decide whether to answer them.

"News travels."

"News sits." Tomaso checked the cinch with two practiced pulls. "Man comes down off the escarpment, signs a paper in Verrin's office, buys a week of salt pork—news doesn't travel, news just stands still till somebody looks at it." He glanced up, and for once his eyes held something more than professional indifference. "You want to know what I heard?"

"I want to know what you know."

"Same thing." Tomaso led the horse to the street and stood holding its head, keeping his body between Lena and the sightlines from Verrin's window across the square. "Freight man who hauled for the survey crew drinks at my cousin's place. Says the crew wasn't mapping terrain. Says they had a instrument case chained to a wrist the whole time, and whatever was in it, the lead man slept with it under his bed. Says they asked questions in the valley settlements—asked about people. Who lived where. Who'd been there how long." He paused. "Says they asked about you."

The street noise seemed to recede. Lena stood very still, one hand on the saddle horn.

"About me," he said.

"Ranger named Delacroix. Knows the high country. Where does he go. How often. Alone?" Tomaso shrugged. "Might mean nothing. Might mean they were mapping men instead of mountains. Either way—" he patted the bay's neck, "—you'll want to ride careful. Not because of weather."

"Because of what the weather covers," Lena said.

Tomaso smiled, showing old yellow teeth. "There's hope for you yet."

* * *

He rode out at dusk, taking the long way around the south edge of town so he wouldn't pass the Land Commission windows, though he caught himself glancing back anyway. Verrin's office light burned behind its glass, and a silhouette crossed it once—Verrin or another man, bent over papers, working late on a Friday night in a town that rolled up its sidewalks at sundown.

Working late on something.

Lena put his back to the light and climbed into the broken country north of the river, where the road gave out and the tracks began. The evening cooled fast. Stars came out in the hard, cold quantity that meant the high passes were already wearing their first white, and somewhere up in all that darkness, four men were missing, or dead, or hiding, or erased—and a fifth possibility, the one he didn't name even to himself, that they had found something in Seven-Delta worth making disappear.

Two hundred dollars in his coat. A contract in his pocket that required him to hand over anything he found unread. Questions about himself in the mouths of men who should never have known his name.

He thought about turning back. He actually considered it, honestly, for the better part of a mile—the old reflex, the deep groove worn by twenty years of choosing distance. Disappear. Let the government hire its sixth tracker. Let the missing stay missing. Nobody had ever thanked him for curiosity; plenty had punished him for it.

But the trail went north, and so did he. Not for Verrin's money. Not entirely for the missing men, either, though some stubborn part of him—the part that had once believed in courts and water rights and the difference between guilt and blame—wanted them found alive.

He rode because the job was wrong in ways that only getting closer would make right. Because somebody in that country might need finding who hadn't been mentioned in any contract. Because a man can spend twenty years measuring his life in miles and wake up one morning understanding that the miles were never the point—that they were just how far you can get from a thing before you have to decide what you believe.

The escarpment rose ahead of him, black against the stars, its rim cut sharp as a blade laid on the sky.

Somewhere up there was Quadrant Seven-Delta, and a spring running eight gallons a minute into country that officially contained no water, and whatever truth the government had sealed before it was even written.

Lena touched his heels to the bay and started up.
