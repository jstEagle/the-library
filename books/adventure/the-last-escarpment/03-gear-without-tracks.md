## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The survey camp sat in a shallow bowl below a rim of red sandstone, and from the ridge above it looked almost ordinary — four tents in a neat line, a cook fire ring, the geometry of men who expected to be found.

Lena did not go down right away. Twenty years of habit made him circle first, wide, reading the ground before he trusted it. He came down off the rim through a drainage of broken shale where his own passage would leave nothing worth reading, worked his way around the camp's eastern flank at two hundred yards, then cut back along the wash that fed into the bowl from the north. Only when he had seen the camp from three angles and found no movement, no sound, no sign of anything alive but a pair of ravens working something behind the latrine trench, did he let himself walk in.

The wind had been out of the southwest all morning, dry and warm for the season, carrying the smell of sun-warmed stone and old dust. It moved the tent flaps gently, like breath.

He stopped at the edge of the camp and stood there longer than he needed to.

Four tents, all staked, all zipped or tied shut against weather that hadn't come. A canvas mess fly strung between two poles over the fire ring, still rigged. A stack of instrument crates near the largest tent, roped down under a tarp weighted with stones — packed as if for transport, not storage. The fire pit held ash gone gray at its edges, cold for days by the look of it, but the woodpile beside it was full: split juniper stacked in a careful crib, enough to burn a week. Two canteens hung on a tent pole. A tin plate sat on the mess table with a fork laid across it, and the food on the plate had dried to a brown crust — beans, maybe, or stew, whatever they'd eaten last, abandoned mid-meal.

Nobody eats half a meal and packs their instruments and leaves their canteens. Nobody does it four times over.

Lena had walked into a lot of empty camps in twenty years. Most of them told you a story fast: panic, or haste, or accident. Gear scattered. A snapped strap. Blood, sometimes, or the absence of blood where there should have been some. Camps emptied in a hurry looked emptied in a hurry. Camps hit by trouble looked like crime scenes. Camps hit by weather looked wrecked.

This one looked *tidy*. And tidy was wrong.

He set his pack down inside the mess fly, deliberately, so he'd stop drifting, and began the work.

* * *

He started with the tents because tents lie least.

The first one — small, single-occupant, probably the junior man's — was zipped shut from the outside. Inside, a bedroll rolled tight, a spare shirt folded, boots placed together under the cot with the laces tucked in. A journal lay on the cot, closed. Lena didn't touch it yet. He crouched and looked at everything first, the way you look at a room before you move anything in it, because the moment your hands start moving, the room starts telling you what you expect to hear instead of what happened.

Nothing was missing that he could see. Nothing was ransacked. If a man had walked out of this tent, he'd done it slowly, in order, folding his shirt first.

The second tent held the same discipline. Bedroll rolled. Boots paired. A water bag hanging, three-quarters full.

The third was the surveyor's own — larger, with a folding table, maps in a tube, a lantern with a cracked globe. On the table, a ledger open to a page of figures. Lena read them without meaning to: elevation readings, bearings, a column of numbers that stopped mid-line. Not crossed out. Stopped. As if the hand writing them had simply lifted off the page.

The fourth tent was storage. Rope, spare stakes, a tool roll, canned goods in a wooden crate with the lid pried loose and set neatly beside it. He counted the cans without opening anything: thirty-one. He'd count again later. Numbers were how you caught a camp lying to you.

He went back outside and stood in the middle of the bowl and turned a slow circle, letting the whole picture assemble itself.

Instruments: packed and roped. Food: untouched. Water: full. Firewood: stocked. Boots: inside the tents. Canteens: hanging on poles. Journal: closed mid-entry.

Every instinct he owned said one word, and the word was *order*. Men had left this camp on purpose, unhurried, taking nothing, leaving everything ready to be resumed. As if they'd stepped away for an hour.

But the ash in the fire pit said days. Rain — one brief shower, by the spatter pattern in the dust under the mess fly — said at least four, maybe five. The crust on the tin plate agreed with the rain.

So: five days ago, give or take, four men ate a meal, and somewhere between the last bite and the next morning, every one of them decided to fold his shirt, roll his bed, tuck his bootlaces, and vanish. Without instruments. Without water. Without boots, if they'd gone far barefoot — and nobody goes far barefoot off the escarpment in November.

Lena crouched at the edge of the fire ring and looked at the ground, and that was when the morning stopped being strange and started being bad.

* * *

Ground is a book. He'd learned that before he learned much else worth knowing, back when he was still a boy running cattle lines with a grandfather who read sign the way other men read scripture. Ground holds what passes over it — weight, direction, hesitation, fear — and holds it for days if the weather lets it. Sand holds best. This bowl was floored in fine red sand and silt washed down from the rim, the kind of surface that takes a print like wax takes a seal and keeps it for a week in dry air.

There should have been prints everywhere.

Five days of occupation meant hundreds of passages: to the latrine, to the woodpile, to the horses, to the creek seep a quarter mile east. Even careful men stop caring after a day or two. Even disciplined men leave a beaten path between tent and fire.

He found the beaten paths. That was the thing. Tent to fire ring: a faint darkening of the sand, a hundred overlapping impressions compressed into a smoothness. Fire ring to latrine: same. Fire to horse lines: same. The camp had lived exactly the way a living camp lives, right up until it didn't.

And then it ended.

Not faded. Ended. He walked the perimeter twice, then a third time on his knees in places, nose close to the sand, the way he'd done ten thousand times, the way he could do blindfolded. Outside a rough boundary — call it forty feet out from the outermost tent — the traffic stopped clean. No scuffs. No drag marks. No partial heel prints trailing off, which even the most deliberate walker leaves, because a deliberate man is only deliberate until he isn't. No sign of the horses leaving either; the picket line area showed a week of hoofwork churned into mud from some earlier rain, and then that too simply stopped, hooves and boots together, like a sentence cut off by a censor's pen.

Inside the boundary: a living camp. Outside it: nothing.

Lena sat back on his heels and stared at the sand and felt something he hadn't felt since he was young enough to admit to feelings like this. His logic had failed. Not bent. Not stretched. Failed — flatly, completely, the way a bridge fails, all at once and underneath you.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't close: four men cannot leave a camp without crossing forty feet of fine sand. Four men cannot leave it without leaving prints, unless they left on boards, or on wind, or in pieces small enough to blow away. And none of those things happen.

Which left the answer he didn't want, sitting there patient and obvious: they hadn't left at all. They'd been *removed*. Removed carefully, quietly, without struggle — no overturned gear, no spent brass, no blood in the sand that he could find, though he'd look harder for that before he left — and removed by someone who understood ground well enough to erase his own approach and exit both.

That took skill. That took more than skill. That took a professional.

Or it took several men and a long night and a shovel, and the graves were somewhere he hadn't looked yet. He made himself hold that thought without flinching. Contract work meant accounting for bodies, not just absences. He'd find the graves if there were graves. But his gut said no — gut said the removal had been clean because it had been *agreed to*, somehow, and that thought was somehow worse than murder. Murder he understood. Four grown men walking out of a camp in the dark, barefoot or carried, without one of them breaking pattern, without one of them dropping a matchbook or a button or a curse in the sand — that required either terror so complete it froze discipline into them, or a reason so strong they cooperated in their own disappearance.

He got up and went to work on the details, because details were all he had left now that the big story refused to exist.

* * *

The horses were gone entirely — not dead, not loosed. Loosed horses stay. Horses are creatures of habit and hay; turn four of them out and within a day they'll be standing at the picket line looking aggrieved, or making for the nearest water, and water leaves a trail a child could follow. These had been led out. Led out quietly, shod — he found one clear shoe print in the hardened mud by the picket, a front left, a commercial shoe with a slightly worn toe caulk — and taken somewhere. Taken *before* the men vanished, or with them, or after; the mud had dried hard enough that the chronology blurred. But someone had ridden four horses out of this camp, and that someone had known what he was doing with them, because there wasn't a second line of tracks anywhere. One pass, one direction, one rider or one leading string, and then erased.

Erased how? He worked that question along the likely exit routes for an hour. South, toward the flats: sand too soft, any passage would show, and didn't. East, toward the seep: same. North, into the rim country: rock, mostly, and rock keeps nothing — but rock also forces routes, and routes funnel sign onto the few sandy shelves between stone, and he walked every shelf within a half mile and found wind and lizard tracks and nothing else.

West, then. West was the high range. West was the quadrant on the map that the men in Harrow Bend had gone quiet about when they thought he wasn't listening. West was where the contract paperwork said the team had been headed next anyway.

He found the exit at last, and finding it made him feel briefly competent again, which he should have known to distrust.

A quarter mile west of camp, a shallow drainage cut up through the rimrock toward the high country — steep, ugly, the kind of route a mule would argue about. At its mouth, the sand firmed into gravel, and gravel holds less, and whoever had planned this exit had planned around exactly that. In the gravel he found what the sand wouldn't show: a scuff on a buried stone where a shod hoof had slipped and struck edge-on, fresh enough that the bruise in the lichen hadn't oxidized. Higher up, a sagebrush broken outward at knee height, sap still tacky at the break. Someone had come through here in the last five days, uphill, with stock, in a hurry or under load or both.

One pass. Same as the camp. One party, going west, and nothing coming back.

He stood in the drainage with the wind pulling at his collar and let himself say it plainly, because plain words were how he kept from spinning: somebody had marched these men out of their own camp and up into the high range, and had cleaned the ground behind them like a housekeeper, and had done it well enough to beat him — beat *him*, twenty years of reading this country, the man whose name was whispered around Harrow Bend campfires as the one who never lost a trail. His pride sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He noticed it. He set it aside. Pride was a luxury for trackers who still believed the ground was honest.

The ground wasn't honest anymore. That was the real discovery of the morning, bigger than the empty camp. Somewhere out here was a person who treated ground the way he did — as a thing to be managed, lied to, kept silent. He had met maybe three such people in his life and had disliked every one of them on sight.

* * *

Back in camp, afternoon light slanting long and amber through the notch in the western rim, he allowed himself the journals.

The surveyor's ledger first. The figures were good work — tight columns, corrections made honestly, margins annotated in a small crabbed hand. The final entry, five days old by the ink, recorded a bearing shot on a peak he knew by sight: the twin-crowned butte at the head of the western range. Beneath the numbers, in the same hand, a single unnumbered line:

*"Quadrant 7 tomorrow. R. says save the water notes for the sealed annex."*

Sealed annex. Water notes. Lena read the line three times and made himself think about what a government survey crew did and did not do. They mapped. They measured. They filed. Nothing about a routine cadastral survey needed sealing, and nothing about it needed the word *water* handled like contraband. Unless the water was the point. Unless somebody paying for this survey cared less about contour lines than about what lay underneath them.

He checked the junior man's journal. Less disciplined, more human — complaints about the coffee, a sketch of a horned lizard, a line about a letter home he hadn't finished writing. And on the last written page, undated, wedged between a note about instrument calibration and a grocery list of wants for the resupply run:

*"R. had us strike the seep readings from the daily log. Said orders. T. argued. R. told him to want his pension more than he wants to be right."*

Two journals, two hands, one pressure. Somebody above them — R., the senior man, taking orders from further up — was handling information about water the way you handle a live grenade. And then the whole camp had evaporated off the face of the earth, westward, upward, into Quadrant 7, into the high range, into the one direction in this entire basin that the men in Harrow Bend had flinched away from naming.

Lena closed the journals, wrapped them in oilcloth, and put them in his pack. Evidence traveled with the finder. He'd learned that lesson in another life, in a courtroom where evidence had a way of changing shape between the field and the stand.

He made camp in theirs — why waste a good windbreak — and while the light died he did the things the vanished men hadn't gotten to do. Fed a small fire. Boiled water. Ate standing up, watching the rim darken. The ravens had gone. The silence out here had layers, he'd always thought: wind-silence, cold-silence, and then the deep kind, the kind that means the country is holding its breath around something. He had that kind now.

Before sleep, he walked the perimeter one last time, lamp low. Habit. And it was habit, not insight, that made him pause at the latrine trench at the camp's edge — the one place men go alone, the one place a frightened man might scratch something no commander would see.

The trench was properly dug, properly filled-in at the far end, maintained. Beside it, driven into the sand, a thin stake — the kind used for marking instrument stations — and on the stake, scratched deep with a knife point in a hurried vertical scrawl, letters he had to tilt the lamp twice to read:

**Q7 WTR**

Quadrant seven. Water.

It wasn't a message left for searchers. It was too cryptic for that. It was a man marking a spot the way you'd mark a cache — telling *himself*, or telling whoever came after who already knew the code, where the true record lived. Or where it was hidden. Or where they'd all been taken, if taken they'd been, so that anyone with sense enough to read a stake would know the direction to grieve in.

West. Up. Into the high range, ahead of the snows, toward a valley that appeared on no map Lena had ever been allowed to see.

He banked the fire, rolled into his bedroll, and lay awake a long time listening to the country. Somewhere up in that black rampart of rock, four men were dead or complicit or captive, and someone with a broom made of sand and skill had swept the trail behind them clean.

Almost clean.

Because there was one thing the sweeper hadn't counted on, one thread left dangling in all that tidy erasure, and Lena had been worrying it for weeks without knowing what it attached to. All season, off and on, he'd been crossing a single set of boot prints — small, light-soled, a slightly rolling right foot — moving through country no lone traveler had business in. Moving, always, in the same general bearing. West. Toward the high range. He'd assumed a poacher, a prospector, a runaway. He'd tracked the stranger idly, the way a man hums a tune, without ever deciding to close the distance.

Tomorrow he would decide.

Because a camp doesn't erase itself, and a thief who moves like that — careful, skilled, alone, heading where the vanished men had gone — might be the sweeper. Or might be the only other soul alive who knew what Quadrant 7 was hiding.

Either way, come first light, Lena Delacroix was going to go find out.
