## 4. The Thief on the Flats

The flats began where the escarpment's shadow ended, a hundred square miles of hardpan and saltbush so level that the horizon looked machined. Lena Delacroix had crossed them in every season for twenty years, and he had learned their one law: the ground remembers everything, but only briefly. Wind erased a track in an hour out here. Rain erased it in minutes. A man could walk the flats and be gone from the earth's ledger before noon.

So when he saw a track that hadn't been erased, he stopped his horse and sat very still, the way he'd learned to sit when the desert offered him something it hadn't meant to.

The print was small. A woman's boot, or a man's—narrow, light-soled, the heel worn on the outside edge by someone who favored that foot. It pressed into a skin of old rain crust that had frozen two nights back and hadn't thawed since. The crust would hold a print for days in the cold. That was luck, or timing. Lena didn't believe in luck. He believed in timing, and timing was something a careful person controlled.

He dismounted without tying the horse—Copper would stand, Copper always stood, a gelding with more patience than most men—and knelt beside the print with his hat brim shading it. He didn't touch it. First rule, older than any he'd learned from a book: never touch the thing that's telling you the story.

One set of prints. Not two. Not a line of them, not a patrol, not a family moving with the slow chaos of children and stock. One person, walking with a stride that said *I know where I'm going and I don't want to talk about it*. Long steps for a small boot. No rest stops. No place where the walker had turned to look back.

Lena straightened and looked where the track pointed.

North. Toward the Vermilion Teeth, the sawtooth spine of the high range that no map in Harrow Bend bothered to draw accurately, because no one with a map had any business there. The Teeth belonged to the Ute and to the weather, in roughly that order, and the government had drawn a boundary line around them forty years ago with a red pencil and a shrug. *Closed range. Restricted survey area.* Men had lost grazing permits for less than pointing a horse that direction.

The track walked straight at that line like it was a fence with a gate in it.

Lena stood there a long moment, one hand on Copper's cheek strap, feeling the cold come off the north the way you feel a stove's heat from across a room. Then he swung up and followed.

* * *

He'd been tracking this boot for most of a season, though *tracking* was too strong a word for what he'd actually done. What he'd done was collect. A print in the dried mud of a stock tank east of Harrow Bend in late summer, narrow-soled, outside heel worn. The same print, months later, in the ash outside a line camp that had been raided for flour and matches—taken neat, not much, left the rest. A thread of blue wool snagged on greasewood at the mouth of a canyon no trail led to. A cold fire ring, stones stacked with a care that struck him even then as unusual, because outlaws don't stack stones carefully. Outlaws are in a hurry.

He'd never reported any of it. That was the part he couldn't have explained to the rangers' office in Harrow Bend, if he'd had a rangers' office, which he didn't, not officially. He was a contract man. He rode a circuit, checked water sources, counted stock for ranchers who paid him in cash and asked no questions, and every few months a man named Tuller handed him a list of names—men wanted on warrants, mostly small, mostly sad—and Lena kept his eyes open the way you keep your eyes open for weather. He brought back two or three a year. It kept his paper clean and his cabin stocked.

The boot prints he'd never brought to Tuller. He'd told himself it was because there was no warrant attached, no crime worth the ride. But that was a lie with good posture, and Lena knew his own lies the way he knew his own tracks. The truth was that whoever wore that boot was careful in a way that reminded him of himself. Careful the way a person is careful when they're not running *from* something so much as running *toward* a place they can't afford to be seen reaching. He'd wanted to know where that place was. He'd told himself he was curious. Curiosity was the respectable word for what it actually was, which was recognition.

Now the survey team was missing—four men with government instruments and a government mandate, gone from a camp that looked like a photograph of itself, no tracks out—and here was the one set of prints he'd been collecting all season, walking north toward the one direction in the territory that made no sense for a thief, a fugitive, or anyone who wanted to stay alive through the winter.

Two mysteries, and here they were, braided.

He'd felt it coming for days, the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge. The camp at Dry Fork had been wrong in ways that still itched at him. Tents pegged. Instruments crated and lashed. The cook's skillet hung on its hook. Four men did not vanish from a tidy camp; tidy camps belonged to men who expected to come back to them. And the quadrant maps Tuller had shown him—the ones the team had been working—had that last survey block inked in red, the one up against the Teeth's southern flank, and Tuller had touched that block on the map twice while talking about something else. Lena had counted the touches. People touch what they're afraid of.

He rode the flats with his eyes down and his mind working the problem like a man working a stone in his pocket.

If the thief had taken something from the survey camp, the tracks would tell him. Thieves travel heavy or travel fast; they don't travel both. These prints were light. The stride was long but the depth was shallow—a person carrying weight distributed well, or not carrying much at all. A satchel, maybe. Papers. Something that weighed less than flour and was worth more than gold, which narrowed the field to exactly one category of thing in this territory: documents.

He thought about the red-inked quadrant. He thought about Tuller's fingers.

The flats changed under him as he rode north. Hardpan gave way to a thin skin of sand over clay, then to the first stubborn grasses that marked the edge of the high country's influence. The tracks held. Whoever wore the boot had walked through the night—there was a place where the stride shortened for a hundred yards and then lengthened again, the signature of a person who'd fought off sleep and lost and won—and the cold had been kind enough, or cruel enough, to keep every step.

Near midday he found where she'd rested. A shallow scrape behind a rock outcrop, body-sized, the ground compressed in a way that said she'd sat with her back to the stone and her face to the south. Watching her backtrail. She'd built no fire. There was a spot of grease on a flat rock where something had been eaten—jerky, from the look of it—and beside it, half-buried in the sand, a button.

Lena didn't pick it up. He crouched and looked at it the way you look at a word in a language you almost speak. Bone button, small, the kind that came on government-issue clerks' coats. The kind that came on the coats the survey team had been wearing in the photograph Tuller had shown him, four sunburned men squinting into the sun, and the youngest of them—

He went still.

The youngest of them had been a woman. He'd barely registered it at the time, a clerk's name on the roster, *S. Voss*, and a face in the photograph that he'd filed without thinking under *junior*, under *assistant*, under *not important*. Tuller hadn't said *her*. Tuller had said *the team*, *the men*, *the party*, four times running, and Lena had let the grammar do his thinking for him, and grammar was a liar the same as anyone.

He picked up the button now. Turned it in his fingers. Bone, government-issue, thread still in the shank where it had been torn free—torn, not cut, torn in a hurry or by accident, snagged on something.

The thief on the flats was the survey team's own clerk.

He sat back on his heels and let that rearrange the furniture of the whole case. She hadn't stumbled onto the camp after the vanishing. She'd walked out of it. She was the vanishing—one fifth of it, the fifth that had left on her own feet in her own boots, heading not toward Harrow Bend and help but toward the closed range, carrying something light and worth everything, and the other four—

The other four were still unaccounted for. And the camp had been tidy. And Tuller's fingers had found the red quadrant twice.

Lena put the button in his shirt pocket, over his heart, which was not where a man carried evidence. It was where a man carried things he hadn't finished thinking about.

* * *

By late afternoon the flats began to tilt, so gently at first that the horse felt it before he did, and the tracks turned with the land, angling toward a low pass between two of the outer Teeth that Lena knew by the old name the Ute stockmen used: the Door. Beyond it the country went vertical. Beyond it, in three weeks, maybe four, the snows would close everything above eight thousand feet until April.

The prints went through the Door like it was any other gap in any other fence.

He found her last camp of the trip at the pass mouth—a cold ring again, stones stacked, ash scattered with deliberate care so the wind would finish the job, a latrine trench filled and flattened. She'd camped here a full night; the ground told him that much. And here she'd done something he'd never seen a fugitive do.

She'd left a marker. Small, deliberate: three stones set upright in a row on a flat rock, each no bigger than a fist, spaced with the evenness of a fence line. Not a cairn. Not a trail sign any tracker he knew would read. Something private, or something meant for eyes that knew a particular code—or meant for no one at all, a habit carried from somewhere, a way of saying *I was here, I passed through, I existed*, left by a person who had spent long enough being nobody that she needed to say it to the rocks.

Lena stood over the three stones for a long time.

He thought: *She's not running blind. She knows where she's going.* Nobody walked into the Vermilion Teeth in November on a guess. She had a destination. She had, maybe, people waiting. Which meant the closed range—the forbidden country, the blank space on the map—wasn't blank at all. It held something. It held someone. And a government survey team had been sent, with red ink on their maps, to the edge of it, and then the government had lost them, and then the government had offered a contract with too much money in it to a man with no history, and somewhere under all of it was a reason, and reasons in this territory were made of water and land and money, in that order, always in that order.

He thought: *I could turn back.* Ride to Harrow Bend, tell Tuller he'd found the clerk's track heading into the closed range, let the government's own men ride in after her with warrants and rifles and the particular efficiency of men who've been paid to find a thing. That was the job. That was the paper he'd signed. And it would be over in a week, and he'd be paid, and the flats would erase everything, the way they always did.

He thought: *And whatever she's carrying, whatever she saw in that tidy camp, whatever made her tear a button off her own coat and walk into the Teeth alone—Tuller's men won't ask. They'll retrieve. There's a difference, and I've been on the wrong end of it.*

Twenty years ago, men had retrieved things about Lena Delacroix. Testimony, shaped. Reports, filed. A verdict, delivered. Nobody had asked. Asking was the difference between justice and paperwork, and he had learned which one the territory ran on.

Copper shifted, blowing warm breath into the cold. The light was going amber and long across the flats behind them, and ahead the Door stood open and dark, and somewhere past it, a day's walk or two, a woman with a satchel and a torn coat was climbing into country that ate the unprepared, and behind him, somewhere south, men were being paid to make all of it disappear.

Lena looked at the three small stones one more time.

Then he did the thing he'd been not-doing all season. He stopped collecting and started following. He swung onto Copper, took the pass at a walk, and let the flats close behind him like a book he didn't intend to open again until he knew how the story ended.

The Door was narrow, and cold fell through it like water. On the far side the trail climbed, and the prints in the old rain crust climbed with it, smaller-looking now in the deepening shadow, a woman's boot going up into the forbidden country, one step at a time, and Lena Delacroix followed her into the red-inked blank on the map, and the snow, when it came, would come down on both of them.
