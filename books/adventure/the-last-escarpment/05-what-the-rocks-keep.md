## 5. What the Rocks Keep

The slot canyon swallowed her by inches.

She had learned its moods over six days now—the way the light came down in a single blade at noon and vanished by two, the way the sandstone sweated cold even in October, the way her own breathing came back to her off the walls like another person following. She moved through it the way she'd learned to move through the survey camp: quietly, usefully, without taking up more space than the work required.

The satchel rode against her chest, strapped tight under her coat, and every few hundred yards she stopped and pressed her palm flat against it. Not checking the straps. Checking that it was still there. That was a habit she couldn't break and didn't want to. A person might stop being afraid of losing a thing if they stopped touching it, and she wasn't ready to stop being afraid. The fear was doing something for her. It kept her moving.

Inside were forty-one pages. She knew the number the way other people knew prayers. Forty-one pages of original quadrant reports in the government's own hand, with the red ink annotations, with the initials, with the dates that proved what the company had paid for and what the company had gotten. Somewhere behind her, in a locked drawer in Harrow Bend, sat the copies—clean, typed, innocent, wrong. She had carried the originals out of the camp on the night everything happened, and she had not slept a whole night since.

She climbed a chokestone where the canyon narrowed to a shoulder-width crack, wedged the satchel first, then herself, then stood a moment at the top listening. Wind above. Water somewhere below, a seep talking to itself in the dark of the rock. Nothing else.

That was the thing people wouldn't understand, if anyone ever found her. She wasn't afraid of the desert. The desert had never once lied to her.

* * *

She thought about the camp a great deal now. More than she wanted to. Memory worked on her like weather, coming in fronts she couldn't schedule.

She'd been there eleven weeks. Junior clerk, youngest on the team, the one who ran the transit notes and inked the fair copies and made the coffee nobody drank because everyone was too busy pretending the job was routine. Eleven weeks of watching five men do careful work in bad faith. That was the phrase she'd finally arrived at, alone in the supply tent one evening, copying figures she already knew were false: *careful work in bad faith.* They measured everything twice. They recorded nothing honestly.

She had liked them. That was the worst part. Dr. Emmett Voss, the party chief, taught her to read strike and dip off the exposed strata and told her she had a surveyor's eye. Hollis, the instrument man, hummed hymns off-key. The cook, Ansel, saved her the ends of the bread because he'd noticed she ate them. They were good men doing an evil errand, or maybe they weren't good men, maybe that was just what she'd needed to believe so she could sit across the fire from them every night. She still hadn't decided. She suspected she never would.

The falsification had been elegant, which was how she'd caught it. You don't fake a desert by leaving it blank—you fake it by filling it in. Every quadrant report described water: springs, seeps, seasonal flows, all of them marked *ephemeral,* *unreliable,* *insufficient for development.* Page after page of water that wasn't there, described in enough loving detail that no one would ever ask why the descriptions were all identical. Meanwhile the actual field notes—the ones Voss dictated to her at the transit, the ones with the real numbers—told a different story. A deep aquifer under the escarpment's eastern flank. Artesian pressure. Flow rates that would make a river jealous.

Someone wanted the land certified worthless. And someone else, downstream, wanted it certified worthless *in writing,* so that when the water was taken anyway, no record would exist of who had known.

She'd understood it fully on a Tuesday. By Friday she'd copied what she needed. By Sunday night the storm came down off the high range and everything happened at once, and she stopped understanding anything at all.

* * *

The canyon bent east and she followed it, boots whispering on slickrock. Twice a day she allowed herself food. Once a day she allowed herself fire, and only in places where the smoke would die against stone before it reached open sky—a habit she'd learned from her father, who had spent his whole life on land other people held the paper to. He used to say the desert keeps you honest because it won't keep you otherwise. He died owing four years of back taxes on a quarter-section that had been in the family sixty years, and the county sold it out from under his grave. She had been nineteen. She had gone to work for the government because the government, at least, published its rules.

She almost laughed, thinking of it now, there in the cold throat of the canyon. She had believed in the rules the way some people believed in saints.

* * *

On the fourth day in the slots she heard the horse.

Not close. Miles off, maybe, up on the flats where the canyon country broke into open ground—a single set of hooves moving at a walk, the unhurried gait of someone reading ground instead of crossing it. She stood pressed into a recess in the wall for the better part of an hour while the sound faded and returned and faded again, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost calm, that this was the ranger.

She'd heard the stories even at the camp. The men talked about him around the fire once, early on, before they got careful about what they said in front of her. A desert ranger out of Harrow Bend who could read a track three days old. Who'd gone into the rocks twenty years ago after some trouble and never come out of them, and who took contracts the way winter took the high passes—eventually, and completely. Hollis had said he was probably dead. Voss had said men like that don't die; they just get harder to find than whatever they're looking for.

Now he was looking for her. Or for the camp, which amounted to the same thing, since she was the only thing the camp had produced that walked away under its own power.

She should have been terrified. She registered the fact that she should have been terrified the way you register a signpost. But standing in the dark of the rock with the horse-sound dying away, what she actually felt was something closer to relief. Because a ranger was a person, and persons followed rules, and she had spent eleven weeks learning exactly how the rules got bent. If he found her, she would know what to say. She'd rehearsed it forty-one pages' worth.

It was the others she feared. The ones who weren't bound by anything. The company man who'd visited the camp twice—Verrin, though she hadn't known his name then, only his manner: soft shoes, soft voice, eyes that inventoried people like stock. He'd asked Voss, in her hearing, when the final certification would be filed, and Voss had said after the last quadrant, and Verrin had said *good,* in a tone that meant something she hadn't understood until later. Until the storm. Until the argument she'd heard through the tent canvas, Voss shouting that he would not put his name to what happened next, and the other voice—Verrin's voice, patient as dripping water—saying that what happened next had already been arranged, and Voss's signature was a formality the company could obtain one way or another.

Three days later the camp was gone. Not abandoned—*gone.* Tents struck, instruments crated, the cook's stove cold, and in the churned earth around the site, tracks going in every direction except out. She'd stood at the edge of it with her satchel against her chest and understood that she was looking at the answer to a question she'd been afraid to ask: what does a company do with five witnesses it can't trust?

She had run. She wasn't ashamed of running. Running was the only honest thing available to her.

But she knew what the empty camp would look like to whoever found it. She knew it would look like a mystery. And she knew that mysteries, in this country, got solved by rangers.

* * *

She made her noon camp in a chamber where the canyon opened into a room the size of a chapel, light falling through a crack in the roof onto a floor of white sand. There was a pool there, fed by the seep, rimmed with green. She filled her bottle, gave the satchel its inspection—straps, buckles, oilcloth, dry—and ate half a handful of dried apricots, chewing each one longer than food deserved.

Then she did the thing she did every day, the thing that had become the spine of her routine: she took out one page. Just one. She read it standing, the way you'd read a warrant, and then she put it back.

Today's page was the seventh. Quadrant report, eastern escarpment flank. In the government's printed language it described a basin of *negligible hydrological interest.* In the margin, in Voss's hand, small as an apology: *flow test 340 gpm, artesian, sustained 72 hrs.* Three hundred and forty gallons a minute, rising out of the rock on its own pressure, and the official record called it negligible. Somebody downstream was going to fence that water and sell it back to the people who'd lived above it for a hundred years, and the paperwork would all be correct.

She folded the page along its old creases and slid it home, and her hand stayed on the oilcloth a moment longer than the inspection required.

Her father again. He kept arriving uninvited. *Paper is how they take things,* he'd told her once, sitting on the porch of the house that wasn't theirs anymore, watching the new owners' truck kick dust up the county road. *And paper is the only thing that gives it back. Remember that, girl. When it comes time, it'll be paper or nothing.*

She'd been twenty-two and hadn't listened. She was listening now.

* * *

Toward evening she climbed out of the slot system through a drainage only visible from inside, and came up onto a bench of broken rock below the first black ramparts of the high range. The air up here had a different smell—snow in it, faint but unmistakable, weeks ahead of any season she'd planned for. She looked at the peaks and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding: the passes would close early this year. Whatever she was going to do, she would have to do it soon, and she still didn't know what it was. Take the pages to a federal office? To a newspaper? To whom, exactly, when the company's fingerprints were on every institution she'd ever trusted?

Below her, far out on the dusk-colored flats, she saw it: a single rider, tiny with distance, moving along a line no road justified. Not toward her. Not away. Traversing, the way a man moves when he's cutting a contour, reading the ground, letting the country tell him where things went.

She watched until the light failed and the rider dissolved into the general dark.

He was good. She could tell that much from a mile away and a lifetime of watching men move through country. He wasn't blundering. He was *listening.*

She pulled her coat tighter and turned her back on the flats, on the rider, on the lowlands and their law, and faced the high range instead—black ramparts, white crowns, the forbidden country where the maps went blank and, if the old stories were true, where blankness still meant safety.

Tomorrow she'd start up. The snow would be early, and hard, and maybe it would kill her. But snow covered tracks, and snow closed passes, and snow was the one witness in this whole affair that had never been bought.

Behind her, miles down in the dark, she imagined the ranger making his own cold camp, reading her yesterday's sign by the last of the light, and she felt the strangest thing—not fear, exactly, and not hope either, but the recognition of a shape. Two people climbing toward each other through the same broken country, carrying between them, whether they knew it yet or not, the same forty-one pages of truth.

She shouldered the satchel, found her line up through the broken rock by starlight, and began to climb.
