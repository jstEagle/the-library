## 6. Water Sign

The seep was three days out from the abandonment site, and Lena reached it on the morning of the fourth, when the light came up gray and flat over the eastern rim and the cold had that particular bite that meant the season was turning its back on him.

He'd been following a thread. That was all he had—a thread so fine most men would have called it nothing. A scuffed stone on the flats where the wind shouldn't have bothered. A single strand of coarse wool snagged on greasewood, gray with road dust but not desert-colored, the kind of wool they wove in Harrow Bend and nowhere else for two hundred miles. A place where a horse had stood too long, shifting weight, bored or worried, its hooves printing a little circle of churned ground that said *somebody sat here deciding something*.

The thread bent south after the second day, away from the high range, and Lena had let it bend because the land told him to. The escarpment country didn't forgive a man who argued with it. Water decided the routes here, same as it always had, and if his walker was bending south, then his walker knew something about water that the maps didn't.

Which was how he came to be standing at the seep at first light, hat in hand, looking down at sign so fresh it made the hair on his arms stand up under his coat.

* * *

There were seeps in this country the way there were secrets—unlisted, unmarked, and known only to the people who needed them. This one lay in a fold of limestone below a shelf of red sandstone, where snowmelt from the high country percolated down through forty feet of rock and wept out of the cliff face in a dark stain no wider than a man's shoulders. In a dry year it barely ran. In a wet year it made a pool the size of a wagon bed, fringed with watercress, and every living thing within ten miles knew about it: the bighorn, the mule deer, the coyotes, and the handful of people scattered through these breaks who'd learned its location the way you learned anything worth keeping—by nearly dying without it.

Lena had found it nineteen years ago, half-dead himself, his horse gone lame, his canteen dry, three days from anywhere. He'd crawled into that fold of limestone and drunk from a puddle the size of his palm, and he'd sworn then that he'd never tell another soul where it was. He hadn't. In twenty years of ranging the escarpment, he could count on one hand the people who knew this water existed, and he'd have trusted any of them with his life.

So when he topped the rise above the fold and saw what was down there, he stopped moving entirely.

Someone had camped at the seep. Recently.

Not long ago—the grass around the pool wasn't trampled flat, wasn't browned by fire rings or fouled by stock—but recently enough that the signs hadn't weathered. A patch of flattened bunchgrass where a bedroll had been laid. A small ring of stones, cold now, built tight and economical by someone who understood that smoke carried and fuel was scarce. And beside the pool itself, pressed into the damp clay at the water's edge, boot prints.

Small ones. Narrow. A woman's boots, or a man's of slight build, size seven maybe, eight at the outside. The tread was worn nearly smooth at the ball of the foot, which meant distance, which meant somebody who walked for their living or had been walking long enough that the difference had stopped mattering.

Lena stood on the rim for a long time before he went down.

Habit held him still. Twenty years of moving through this country alone had taught him that the first man to arrive at water owned the ground until the second man showed himself, and he meant to own it. He read the camp from above the way other men read a newspaper: one sleeper, maybe two, arrived on foot—no hoofprints anywhere in the soft ground, which was strange, because nobody crossed the southern breaks on foot unless they had no choice—and stayed less than a day. No game hung from the rocks. No refuse beyond a fish skeleton picked clean and left where the birds would find it, tidy as a table setting. Whoever camped here had been careful. Careful the way he was careful. Careful the way only certain kinds of people learned to be.

People running from something, or toward it.

He went down at last, slow, angled across the slope so his approach wouldn't throw his shadow against the cliff face, and knelt at the pool's edge where the clay held print like fresh bread holds a thumb.

The prints circled the water once. Stood a while facing east—weight settled deep in the heels, the way a person stands when they're watching something a long way off. Then they climbed the talus below the sandstone shelf and stopped again at a flat slab near the base of the seep itself, where the wet rock kept everything, and it was there, wedged in a crack where the slab met the cliff, that Lena found the paper.

* * *

It was a fragment. Torn, not cut—a ragged quarter-sheet, heavy stock, the kind government offices used for survey plats, gone soft at the edges from damp but still legible where it mattered. Someone had tried to burn it and failed; one corner was charred black and crumbled when he touched it, and the rest had been stuffed into the crack in haste or in panic, or maybe just dropped, because the wind here funneled up the fold and would have taken it east into the talus field within an hour of letting go.

Lena smoothed it flat on his knee.

Half a map. The torn edge ran diagonally through a grid of surveyed lines, each square labeled in a fine draftsman's hand with township and range numbers, and across the top, in letters that had faded from black to the brown of old tea, someone had printed: **QUADRANT 7 — HYDROLOGIC SUPPLEMENT — CONFIDENTIAL**.

But it was the ink that stopped his breath.

Red ink. Government vermillion, the shade they used for designations that mattered—reservations, withdrawals, restricted zones. It marked a single quadrant on the fragment, a rough oval centered on the grid lines, and beneath the oval, in the same red, a notation in a cramped official hand:

**ARTESIAN INDICATIONS — EST. FLOW SUFFICIENT FOR IRRIGATION OF 40,000 ACRES — DO NOT PUBLISH**

*Do not publish.*

Lena sat down on the slab without meaning to. His knees did it for him.

He knew this country. He knew it the way a man knows his own hands, and the oval on that map sat over terrain he had walked in every season for twenty years—the southern breaks, the folded limestone, the drainage that ran down past this very seep and lost itself in the gravel sinks below the rim. Dry country. Marginal country. Country that appeared on territorial maps as scrub and badland, good for nothing but grazing a few half-starved cattle and hiding from whatever you were hiding from.

And the government had surveyed it for water. Had found it—or suspected it, or been told—and had written *do not publish* in red ink, and had filed the finding somewhere dark.

Forty thousand acres of irrigation. Out here, water wasn't a resource. It was destiny. Water meant towns instead of homesteads, railroads instead of wagon roads, titles instead of squatter's rights. Whoever controlled a flow sufficient for forty thousand acres didn't own land—they owned the future of everyone who'd ever try to live on it.

And someone had come out here, to a seep that existed on no map, and camped beside it, and burned a page of the proof.

Lena looked at the boot prints again. Small. Worn. Alone.

The thief. It had to be—the tracks matched what he'd read at the abandoned camp, the same narrow tread, the same long stride, the same economy of movement. She had come here. To this water. Which meant she knew about the seep, which meant she was local-born or had been taught by someone who was, and either answer raised questions he couldn't yet frame.

Or—his mind turned it over, slow and cold—she hadn't known about the seep at all. She'd followed the map. The red oval sat close enough to this fold of limestone that a person reading the hydrologic supplement might reason her way to the water, walking the drainage, reading the rock the way a surveyor reads it, and found it because the government's secret had led her straight to it.

Either way, she'd stood where he was standing, holding a piece of the truth, and she'd tried to destroy it and failed.

Why fail? Why tear it and burn it and then stuff the remainder in a crack? Unless the burning was interrupted. Unless she'd heard something—riders, maybe, or his own distant sign on the trail—and run, and the wind or the crack or plain luck had saved the page.

Unless she wanted it found.

No. He shook that off. Too clever. People fleeing didn't leave breadcrumbs; they left absence, and she'd left plenty of that everywhere else. But the fragment's survival gnawed at him anyway, the way a loose tooth gnaws at the tongue.

He turned the paper over. On the reverse, in pencil, faint almost past reading, someone had written a string of figures and a word. The figures meant nothing to him—columns of numbers, some crossed out, a fraction at the end. The word was a name, or part of one, written and half-erased and written again, as though the hand couldn't decide whether to keep it or kill it:

**Verr—**

Nothing after it. Just the dash, trailing off into blank paper like a road ending at a washout.

Lena sat with the fragment in both hands for a long time. The seep wept behind him, drop by drop, into the pool, and the sound was the oldest sound in the world, older than governments, older than names, and it made everything written on the paper seem briefly, absurdly small—except that it wasn't small, and he knew it, and the knowing settled over him like a change in the weather.

A missing survey team was one kind of trouble. Men lost in bad country, families grieving, a contract paid out and closed. He could solve that kind of trouble. He'd solved it before, in another life, with another name.

This was different. This was a secret with a body count waiting inside it. Somebody had surveyed water that wasn't supposed to exist, buried the finding, and then lost six men and all their instruments into the escarpment—and the company initials stamped faintly in the corner of the fragment, a watermark he recognized from freight crates and land-office filings, belonged to interests that had never in their history lost anything they intended to keep.

He thought about Harrow Bend. About the contract money, too generous by half. About the officials' careful faces and the one question they'd asked twice, in slightly different words, both times: *You'll wire us directly when you find them, Delacroix. Not the newspapers. Not anyone else. Directly.*

He'd taken it for bureaucratic caution. Now it sounded like what it probably was: a man making sure the evidence came home to the right hands.

Lena folded the fragment along its surviving creases and put it inside his shirt, against his chest, where the important things went. Then he filled his canteen, ate a strip of dried meat standing up, and studied the ground one more time.

The boot prints left the seep heading southwest. Toward the high range. Toward the forbidden country, the broken uplift where the maps went blank and the passes were already thinking about snow.

She was carrying the rest of it. He was sure of that now—not guessing, sure, the way you were sure about weather coming. A junior clerk or a hired hand or whoever she was had looked at those red-inked quadrants and understood what they meant, and had stolen the truth out from under the men who buried it, and had run for the hills with it hugged to her chest like the last warm thing in the world.

And the men who buried it would come for her. Not with a search party—with something else. With quiet men and generous offers and, if those failed, methods that didn't get written down. They'd already erased a survey team. One woman more or less wouldn't trouble them.

Lena stood at the edge of the pool and felt the shape of his own situation settle around him, familiar and unwelcome as an old coat. He'd spent twenty years learning exactly one trade: how to disappear. How to move through country without leaving a story in it. How to be no one, from nowhere, going nowhere, so thoroughly that even his own reflection seemed like a rumor.

Now he was holding a government secret in his shirt and following a fugitive into mountains that were about to close for the winter, employed by men he no longer trusted, carrying a name—*Delacroix*—that he'd buried so deep he sometimes forgot it was his.

The stakes had changed. He understood that plainly, standing over water that officially didn't exist. This wasn't a search anymore. It was a race between him and whoever had written *do not publish*, and the prize wasn't six missing men.

It was the truth, and the valley of lives that truth would either save or bury.

He should turn back. Every prudent instinct he owned said so—take the fragment to Harrow Bend, collect his fee, wire the officials, let the law do what law did. That was the job. That was the sane arithmetic.

But he kept seeing the boot prints at the water's edge, small and worn and alone, circling the pool once and then standing a long time facing east, watching something a long way off. He knew that stance. He'd held it himself, a hundred times, on a hundred rims, a man alone with a decision too big for one set of shoulders.

She was afraid. She was careful. And she was heading into the high country ahead of the snows, which meant she was desperate enough to gamble everything on ground nobody sane entered in November.

Lena cinched his pack, took one last look at the seep—he always did this, always, a habit from the years when he'd believed he might die out here and wanted the water to be the last thing he saw—and started southwest, climbing.

By midday he was in the foothills. By evening the high range stood full across the western sky, blue-black and white-capped, wearing its winter already, and somewhere up in all that silence a woman was climbing too, carrying papers that could remake the territory or get everyone who touched them killed.

That night, camped cold below the tree line with the fragment's pencil scratch burning in his memory—*Verr—*—he took it out one more time and held it to the last of the light.

He didn't know the name. But he knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a face seen once across a crowded room twenty years ago. Somewhere in the wreckage of his old life, in the depositions and the newspapers and the trial he'd fled rather than finish, that name had passed close enough to leave a shadow.

Tomorrow he'd climb into the clouds after a stranger who might be the only honest witness in four hundred miles.

Tonight he sat with the fireless dark and admitted what he'd been refusing to admit since Harrow Bend: the survey team was never the point. They were the excuse. The trail he was on didn't lead to six lost men.

It led to whoever had decided, in some carpeted office a thousand miles from this water, that the desert's one great secret was worth killing for—and Lena Delacroix, who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being no one, was about to become the most visible man in the territory.
