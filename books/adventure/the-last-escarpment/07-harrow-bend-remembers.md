## 7. Harrow Bend Remembers

Harrow Bend smelled the same as it always had: dust, diesel, and the sweet rot of overripe melons from Delgado's cart on the corner of Main and First. Twenty years since I'd walked its streets in daylight, and the town had changed less than I had. Same sagging awning over the hardware store. Same water tower with the rust stain running down its flank like an old wound that never healed. Same church bell that rang six times at six o'clock whether anyone listened or not.

I came in at dusk, which was habit more than caution now. Habit, and the small animal part of me that still counted exits. There were three ways out of Harrow Bend by road and four more that weren't roads, and I'd catalogued them all before I was twenty-five, back when cataloguing them had been a matter of survival rather than sentiment.

The survey contract money sat heavy in my coat pocket. Four hundred dollars, paid half up front, which was double what a missing-persons job should have drawn even for a team of five government men. I'd told myself on the ride down from the escarpment that I'd taken it because a man doesn't leave five people to die in broken country, whatever else he is. That was true. It just wasn't all of it.

I put my horse in the livery behind the hotel — old Vasquez still ran it, or his son did now, a broad quiet man who took my coin without looking at my face longer than he needed to. Either he didn't know me or he'd decided not to. In Harrow Bend those were the same courtesy.

* * *

The hotel dining room served until eight, and I ate late on purpose: mutton, beans, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. The room emptied around me the way rooms do in towns like this — families first, then the drummers and freight men, then the single men drifting toward the saloon where the light was warmer and nobody asked questions. By seven-thirty I had the place to myself except for the girl clearing plates and one figure at the far table by the window, hunched over a cup he wasn't drinking.

I knew him before I knew him. The shoulders did it. You can change a man's hair and bend his back and put twenty years of weather on his face, but shoulders carry their history the way rock carries strata. Hollis Greer had been the biggest man in three counties when I was young, a rancher whose spread ran up against the escarpment's southern foot, and those shoulders had once filled a courtroom doorway while he swore under oath that Lena Delacroix's negligence had killed nine people when the Cane Creek reservoir let go.

He hadn't lied, exactly. That was the thing I'd chewed on for two decades. He'd testified to what he believed, and what he believed had been built for him, plank by plank, by men who needed a body to hang the disaster on — and I was convenient, being the junior engineer with no family and no money and a signature on the wrong inspection report. Greer had lost neighbors in that flood. Grief makes honest men into reliable instruments.

I could have left through the kitchen. Some version of me, some older version, would have.

Instead I picked up my cup and crossed the room.

"Hollis Greer."

He looked up slow, the way old men do, giving nothing away until they've finished measuring you. His eyes went from my face to my hands and back, and something moved behind them — not recognition exactly, not yet. Then it landed.

For a long moment neither of us breathed. Outside, the church bell began ringing six, though it was past seven; the clock in the tower had been wrong since before either of us could remember, and there was a strange comfort in that, in a town so set in its ways it couldn't be bothered to correct time itself.

"Delacroix." He said it quietly, testing the weight of it. "Lord. They said you were dead."

"People say a lot of things."

"They do." He gestured at the chair across from him. His hand wasn't steady. "Sit down, then. If you're going to haunt me, sit where I can see you."

I sat. The girl came over and refilled both cups without being asked, and I noticed she kept her eyes down and moved quick, the way people move around things they've been told not to look at. Small towns teach their children early.

"You don't look like a dead man," Greer said.

"Neither do you. Last I heard your heart was bad."

"Last you heard right." He drank, grimaced at it. "Bad heart, bad hip, bad luck. I'm mostly held together by stubbornness and spite." He set the cup down and looked at me straight, and I'll give him this: he didn't flinch from it. "I owe you an apology, Delacroix. I've known that a long time. I rehearsed saying it about a thousand times and now that you're sitting here I find I can't make it come out any fancier than that. I'm sorry. What I said in that courtroom helped bury you, and I said it because I was drowning in my own grief and they handed me something to hold onto. It took me years to see the shape of what they'd done. By then you were gone."

I hadn't expected it. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I've got left. I had expected anger, or fear, or the flat hostility of a man defending old ground. I had not expected an apology delivered plain, without asking anything in return.

"Nine people died," I said. "That part was real. The rest of it was theater."

"The rest of it was Meridian Water and Power," Greer said, "and every man they owned in this territory, which in those days was most of them." He rubbed his jaw. "You know I wrote to the governor about it? Twice, maybe ten years back. Got a nice letter back from an assistant thanking me for my concern. That was the whole of it."

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody knows it. That's rather the point of men like them." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his face, and I saw how old he truly was, older than his years, the way men get who've spent their lives fighting wind and price and distance. "Now. You want to tell me why a dead man walks into the Bend Hotel and sits down across from me, or am I meant to guess?"

I weighed it. Telling him anything was a risk; telling him nothing was a waste. A man who'd written twice to a governor about an old injustice might be worth something now.

"There was a government survey team," I said. "Five men, working the high country north of the escarpment. They stopped reporting three weeks ago. Somebody's paying good money to find them, and the money's too good, and the questions are wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong direction. They wanted to know where the team had been, not where they'd gone. And they were nervous about one particular quadrant. High country. Empty country. Country that isn't supposed to have anything in it worth surveying."

Greer was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, and he glanced once toward the kitchen door before continuing. "You know what's funny, Delacroix? I've been chewing on something for near a month now, waiting for somebody to ask me about it, and nobody has. So I'll give it to you free, since I owe you."

"Give me what?"

"Before they went up," he said, "the survey team came through here. Officially they were resupplying. Unofficially, they were asking questions. One of them — the junior one, a young fellow, clerkish, wore his hat like he hadn't grown into it — he came out to my place. Said he was doing background on land use in the escarpment region. Asked me about grazing rights, water sources, seasonal range." He paused. "And then he asked me about the hidden valley."

The word went through me like cold water through a crack in stone.

"He used that word?" I said. "Hidden?"

"That's what stuck with me, yes. Most folks would say the back country, or the high meadows, or some such. This boy said hidden valley, like it was a phrase off a document somewhere." Greer turned his cup slowly on the table. "I told him I didn't know of any valley. Which was true, as far as what I'd swear to in daylight. But I'll tell you what I didn't tell him, and what I haven't told anybody in twenty years. My father ran sheep up in those breaks when I was a boy, before the escarpment was fenced and posted. And twice — twice — we came down out of a storm into country that shouldn't have existed. Terraced ground. Old stonework. Smoke rising off a canyon floor where no map showed a canyon. My father saw it too, and my father was not a man who imagined things, and my father also never spoke of it again, not once, not to my mother, not on his deathbed. He just stopped taking the herd up that way, and when I asked him why, he looked at me like I'd asked him to open a grave."

"And you told the survey clerk none of this."

"I told him there was nothing up there but rock and thirst." Greer's mouth twisted. "Because I'm an old man and I've learned what happens to things that get found. But here's the part that's been keeping me up nights, Delacroix. The boy thanked me. Polite as you please. And then, on his way out the door, he turned around and asked me one more question. Asked it casual, like an afterthought, but I've been around enough lawyers and company men to know an afterthought that's been rehearsed."

"What did he ask?"

"He asked if I remembered you." Greer met my eyes. "By name. Asked if I recalled the engineer from the Cane Creek business, and whether I thought he might still be living in the territory. Whether he'd ever been known to run stock or guide hunters in the escarpment country. Whether—" He stopped himself, and his hand flattened on the table. "Whether I'd know him if I saw him."

The dining room was very quiet. The girl had gone into the kitchen. Somewhere outside a dog was barking at nothing, and the wind had come up, rattling the window in its frame the way it does in October when the weather is deciding what kind of winter to be.

"Why would a government surveyor ask after me?" I said. It came out level. I was proud of that later.

"Why would a government surveyor ask about a hidden valley?" Greer answered. "Delacroix, I've had a month to turn this over, and I keep arriving at the same place. Those weren't surveyors' questions. Surveyors ask about elevation and access and forage. Those were a searcher's questions. Or a hunter's." He leaned closer. "And now a search contract goes out, paying double rate, and the man who takes it is supposed to be anybody willing to ride, and instead—"

"Instead it found its way to me," I said. "Through channels I didn't ask for."

"Who brought you the contract?"

"A lawyer in from the territorial capital. Working for the families, he said."

"Families." Greer snorted softly. "Government teams don't have families hiring private trackers inside three weeks. Government teams have departments, and departments have procedures, and procedures take months. Unless somebody wants it fast and quiet, and is willing to pay for fast and quiet, and knows exactly which name to whisper into which ear." He sat back. "Somebody who knew you were alive hired you, Delacroix. Think about that. Somebody who knew where you were, or close enough, and wanted you moving toward that quadrant with your name signed to paper."

I thought about it. I thought about it hard, sitting in that lamplit room with twenty years of careful invisibility stacking themselves up behind me like cordwood, and I felt the shape of the trap — if it was a trap — closing around the one thing I'd never offered anyone: my name, in ink, attached to that country. Whoever held that contract held a thread tied to me. And threads get pulled.

Or it was exactly what it looked like. Five men dead or dying in the high country, and a grieving clerk of a lawyer casting a wide net, and my name surfacing because names do, eventually, the way bodies do.

Both things could be true. That was the worst of it. Both things could be true at once, and I wouldn't know which until I was too deep to turn back cheaply.

"You could be wrong," I said. "Could be simple."

"Could be. Things are rarely simple, and they're never simple when money's involved." Greer reached into his coat and laid something on the table between us. A folded square of paper, soft with handling. "Boy left this. Said it was a regional base map, standard issue, in case I thought of anything worth marking. I never marked it. But I looked at it. Look close at the northeast corner, along the escarpment rim, and tell me what you see."

I unfolded it. Base map, quarter-inch scale, the escarpment rendered in the usual brown hachures, the high plateau beyond it blank white — un surveyed, the legend said, pending field verification. Except that wasn't quite what I saw. Along the rim, in the northeast quadrant, someone had penciled annotations so faint I had to tilt the page to catch them. Grid references. Elevations. And beside one of them, in that same clerkish hand, a single word:

SEEP?

I knew the seep. There was only one up there that mattered, a cold spring bleeding out of the limestone where the rim broke toward the interior, known to maybe a dozen living people, myself among them. It was the door to everything. It was the trailhead to the country beyond the maps, and if a government surveyor had stood at my table in this town and penciled a question mark next to it, then the government already suspected what the seep fed.

What the seep fed was a valley. Green, watered, terraced, inhabited — though I hadn't confirmed the last part in years, and had worked hard not to. A valley full of people who had gone to enormous trouble to be nowhere, and whose entire existence rested on the proposition that no document in any office described them.

A proposition that a pencil mark on a base map had just quietly ended.

"Where did you get this back?" I asked.

"Never gave it back. He didn't ask." Greer watched my face. "You see it too. Good. I was starting to think I was an old fool seeing ghosts." He tapped the map. "That boy was scared, Delacroix. I didn't say that before because I was still sorting it myself, but now I will. When he asked about you — that last question — his voice changed. Went careful. Like a man walking on ice he'd been told was solid. And when I said I'd heard you were dead, he looked relieved. Actually relieved. And then he looked over his shoulder at the road like he expected somebody to be on it, and left at a trot." Greer shook his head slowly. "Scared of his own team, if you ask me. Or of whoever sent the team."

I folded the map and put it inside my coat, next to the contract money. Evidence, or bait. Possibly both.

"Hollis," I said. "If anyone comes asking — company men, lawmen, anyone — and asks whether you've seen me—"

"I'll tell them a dead man bought me dinner and talked about cattle prices." He almost smiled. "It's even mostly true. Go on, Delacroix. Whatever you're riding toward, ride tonight. Weather's turning. My hip's been singing since noon, and my hip's never wrong about snow. It's going to come early this year, and hard, and when it comes, those passes will shut like a book."

Outside, the wind had swung around to the north and carried a smell I knew in my bones: that mineral coldness that comes off high country ahead of a storm, the smell of the mountain making its intentions known. The stars over the escarpment's black silhouette were hard and close and few, the way they get when cloud is coming down behind them like a lid.

I stood in the street a long minute, weighing it. Every instinct I'd honed for twenty years said the same thing: walk away. Let the contract lapse. Disappear deeper. The smart animal survives by leaving.

But the survey team had asked about the valley. And the valley — whatever it was, whoever lived there — had spent decades buying its life with silence, and silence was a currency the government had just started printing. Five men had gone up that mountain carrying questions about a hidden valley, and five men had vanished, and somebody with money wanted the matter closed fast and quiet, and the only person standing at the intersection of all of it was a dead man named Delacroix who happened to know every goat track on that rim.

Purpose is a strange thing. It had been so long since I'd felt it that I almost didn't recognize it — this settling, this hardening, like water going to ice in a crack and splitting the stone. Fear was in it, plenty of fear. But underneath the fear, for the first time in twenty years, something was building instead of eroding.

I went to the livery, saddled in the dark, and rode out of Harrow Bend on the north road with the first flakes coming down fine and dry as salt, and the escarpment waiting ahead of me like a question I'd spent my whole life avoiding.

The snow meant days, not weeks. Maybe less. Cross now, or lose the high country until spring — and spring belonged to whoever got there first.

I turned my horse toward the pass and gave him his head.
