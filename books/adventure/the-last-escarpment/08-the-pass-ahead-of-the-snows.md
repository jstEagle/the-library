## 8. The Pass Ahead of the Snows

The wind changed in the night, and Lena Delacroix woke knowing what it meant.

He'd been sleeping rough under an overhang three miles below the seep, rolled in his blanket with his boots on, the way he'd slept for twenty years. The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones. Above him the sky was still dark, but the stars over the high range had gone soft and smeared, like breath on glass. And the wind—that was the thing. It had come around to the north, and it carried a smell he knew better than his own name.

Snow.

Not the dry dusting that came and went in October, melting off the rocks by noon. This was the wet, heavy kind, the kind that fell once and stayed, that filled the passes and closed them like a door. He'd read it coming for two days in smaller signs—the geese going south low and silent, the pines hoarding their cones, the way the light had turned thin and metallic at midday. But signs could be argued with. Wind couldn't.

He sat up and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the map fragment at the seep.

The thief's tracks ran toward the high range. Toward the passes. If she crossed before the snows, her trail would be buried by morning—every print, every broken twig, every sign of a fire or a camp erased under two feet of white. He would stand at the foot of the mountains with nothing but a direction, and he would wait five months for spring to tell him what winter already knew.

And if she hadn't crossed—if she'd gone to ground somewhere on this side, in some canyon he hadn't found—then the snow would close around her too, and whatever she carried in that satchel she guarded like a child would sit frozen until the thaw, along with whatever truth had put a government quadrant in red ink on a torn map.

Either way, the mountain was about to swallow everything.

Unless he went up ahead of it.

Lena fed the last of his jerky into his mouth and chewed while he looked at the range. It was black against the graying east, jagged as a saw blade, and somewhere up there was a pass the old sheepherders called the Notch—a route no ranger used because it wasn't on any map worth trusting, a route he himself had found eleven years ago chasing a horse thief and had never told another living soul about. It was steep. It was exposed above the tree line for six miles. And it was crossable, if you were light and fast and lucky, for maybe another two days.

After that, not until April.

He'd spent twenty years learning to wait. Waiting was the escarpment's first lesson and its deepest: wait out the storm, wait out the posse, wait out the seasons, wait out your own name. Patience had kept him alive when speed would have killed him. Every instinct he owned said stay low, let the weather decide, come back when the world was readable again.

But patience was also how men lost things. He'd watched it happen from a distance all these years—watched courts forget, watched witnesses die of old age, watched the truth about a reservoir failure settle into the sediment where nobody dredged. Some doors only stood open for a little while. You either walked through them or you spent the rest of your life telling yourself there'd be another one.

He got up and kicked dirt over the fire.

* * *

By full light he was at the place where the trail left the flats and started climbing, and there he stopped and did the hardest part.

His mare, Juniper, stood hipshot in the frost, breathing steam, watching him with the flat patience of an animal who had carried him through nineteen winters and understood more of his moods than most people ever had. She was fourteen hands of dun muscle and good sense, and she had never once put him wrong.

She was also, as of this morning, a liability.

Above the tree line there was no feed, no water he could count on, no shelter wide enough for a horse in a whiteout. A horse needed a track; he intended to leave none. And if the storm caught them both up high, he'd have to choose between her and himself, and he knew which choice he'd make, and he knew what it would cost him, and he didn't intend to find out.

"Come here, girl," he said, and she came, because she always did.

He stripped her tack and packed it tight against a juniper, oilcloth over the saddle, grain in a tin he wired shut against the squirrels. Then he took a length of rope from his kit and knotted it around her neck—not a halter, just a loop she could shed if she fought it—and pointed her back down the trail the way they'd come.

"Harrow Bend," he said. "You know the way."

Juniper didn't move. She stood looking at him with one ear swiveled back, and for a long moment neither of them did anything, and Lena felt the silence stretch into something that had words in it whether he spoke them or not. Twenty years of miles, and most of them he'd measured against the rhythm of her feet. There had been nights in canyons so deep the stars didn't reach the bottom when the only warm thing in the world was her shoulder against his back.

"Go on," he said, rougher than he meant to. "I'll come find you."

She blew through her nostrils, once, and then she turned and went, picking down the trail at her own unhurried pace, and Lena stood and watched her until the frost-light took her, a dun smudge shrinking along the base of the escarpment, and then he was alone in a way he hadn't been alone in twenty years. No horse. No pack animal. No second set of lungs on the climb.

Just him, forty pounds of gear, and a mountain deciding whether to open or close.

He shouldered his rucksack and started up.

* * *

He went light the way he'd learned to go light, which meant ruthlessly. Blanket, oilskin, tin cup, knife, flint, four days of food compressed into a sack that weighed almost nothing because hunger was a tool he'd learned to use rather than fear. Rope. The map fragment wrapped in waxed cloth against his chest, next to his heartbeat, where the damp wouldn't get it. His rifle he considered for a long moment at the trailhead and finally left strapped to the juniper beside the saddle—he'd be hunting nothing up there, and every pound was a decision about how far he'd get.

What he kept, without letting himself examine why, was the memory of the tracks. He'd studied them for an hour at the seep before the wind wiped the edges soft: small boots, a long stride, the left heel worn on the outside edge the way a walker's wears when she's spent years on sidehill ground. Someone sure-footed. Someone moving with purpose and afraid of being followed, because she'd walked the shale margins where sign dies fastest even though the solid rock was easier.

Someone who deserved to be caught by someone patient enough to understand her.

He climbed through the morning with the sun on his back and the wind at his neck, and the country changed around him the way it always did, in stages. First the sage gave out and the piñon took over, squat and twisted, holding the slopes together with root and stubbornness. Then the piñon thinned and the ponderosa rose around him, tall and straight, and the air went cool and resinous, and he passed through stands where the snow already lay in the shadows in patches that crunched under his boots. Then the ponderosa quit too, and there was only spruce, dark and dense and dripping, and the light came down green and cold as water.

At midday he stopped in a spruce hollow and ate half his remaining bread and drank from a stream that was skinning over at the edges, breaking the ice with his knuckle. The water tasted of stone and coming winter. While he drank he checked the sky, and what he saw made him eat faster.

The clouds weren't building anymore. They were arriving.

They came up from the northwest in a wall with a gray underside, organized, deliberate, the kind of front that didn't drift across a range but committed to it. Behind the leading edge the air had that bruised, pressed-down look, and the birds had gone entirely quiet. Somewhere below and behind him, he knew, the temperature was already falling, and the snow line was walking downhill to meet him.

He had maybe six hours of margin. Maybe less. The Notch was eight miles up and two thousand feet higher, and the last three miles were scree and open face where a man moved at the mercy of the wind.

He got up and climbed faster.

* * *

The exhilaration found him at the tree line, and it frightened him more than the storm did.

It came on him gradually and then all at once, somewhere in the transition between the last twisted spruce and the bare gray world above, where the trees gave up in a ragged line like soldiers abandoning a position. Below him the whole escarpment country unrolled—canyons he'd threaded, mesas he'd slept on, the faint silver thread of the river he'd drunk from for two decades—all of it laid out under the advancing cloud deck like a map he'd memorized and never been allowed to own. And above him the Notch cut its dark V into the ridge, still clear, still open, still waiting.

And Lena Delacroix, forty-six years old, wanted to run.

Not away. That was the strange thing, the thing he stood in the wind turning over like a stone in his hand. For twenty years running had been the shape of his life, but it had always been running *from*—from Harrow Bend, from the courthouse, from the version of his name that appeared in newspapers next to the word *negligent*. This was different. This was his body remembering something older than exile, something from before the reservoir, before the trial, before he'd learned to make himself small: the plain animal joy of moving hard uphill through wild country with the weather coming down and no one in the world who knew where he was.

No one to report to. No one to fail. Just the mountain, the storm, and the woman's tracks somewhere ahead, going cold by the hour.

He laughed out loud, once, and the wind took it, and the sound startled him so badly he checked his own stride. When had he last made a sound like that? When had he last felt the fear and the joy sitting in the same chest without one canceling the other?

Because the fear was there too. He made himself look at it honestly, the way he looked at tracks. The fear said: *you are forty-six and alone and above the tree line and the storm is early and strong, and men have died in that Notch in good weather.* The fear said: *if you fall on the scree there is no one to find you until spring, and spring will find a story, not a man.* The fear said, quieter, in the voice he trusted least: *whatever is in that satchel, whatever truth she's carrying, it has already killed three surveyors once, and it will not hesitate to kill the fool chasing it.*

He heard all of it. He weighed all of it. And then he kept climbing, because underneath the fear, steadier than either of them, was the thing he'd finally admitted to himself two nights ago at the fire: he wasn't doing this for the contract. The contract money was still folded in his coat, untouched, and he suspected now it always would be. He was doing it because for the first time in twenty years, a trail had led somewhere that mattered—mattered beyond his own survival, beyond his own hiding. A missing team. A falsified map. Red ink on land that held water someone powerful wanted buried.

And a young woman running through the desert carrying the proof, alone, hunted by people with more resources than she could imagine.

He knew what it was to be hunted by people like that. He'd had twenty years to study the experience from the inside.

"Whoever you are," he said to the wind, "hold on. I'm coming."

* * *

The scree field took the rest of the afternoon.

It was a mile of loose gray stone angled at thirty degrees, the kind of slope where each step slid back half of what it gained, where the rock sang under your boots with a sound like distant china breaking. Lena moved through it the way he'd learned to move through bad ground: short steps, weight centered, reading the slope for the lines of stability the way other men read pages. Twice the surface shifted under him and ran in small avalanches of its own, rattling down into the fog that was beginning to rise from the valley behind, and twice he froze and let it finish before continuing.

Halfway across, the first flakes came.

They arrived without drama—one, then three, then a dozen, fat and slow and spinning, landing on his sleeves and staying. He stopped and watched one melt on the back of his glove and felt the temperature of the whole day change around him, the way a room changes when a lamp goes out. The light flattened. The shadows died. The ridge above him, which had been sharp against the sky an hour ago, dissolved upward into a whiteness that had no top.

He checked the Notch. Still visible—or the idea of it was, a darker seam in the general gray, perhaps a mile and a half on. Perhaps ninety minutes at the pace the scree allowed. Perhaps three hours, if the slope iced.

Behind him, the valley was already gone.

That was the moment, standing on the scree with the snow thickening around him and the retreat dissolving at his back, that the point of no return announced itself—not as a thought but as a fact, physical and complete. The trail down was vanishing under new snow faster than he could descend it. In an hour it would be impassable to anything but a desperate man, and by dark it would be impassable to him. Whatever happened above—storm, injury, the pass closing behind him like a trap—his road back had ended here, on this slope, at this hour, and every step forward was now taken on the mountain's terms.

A younger man might have panicked. Lena found, examining himself with the detached interest of long habit, that what he felt instead was calm—flat, clean, almost disappointing in its simplicity. The decision had been made days ago, really, back at the seep when he'd picked up the map fragment, maybe further back than that, maybe the day a telegram had reached a man who'd spent two decades making sure no one could find him. Everything since had just been the body catching up to the mind.

He pulled his hat down, turned his collar up, and climbed.

* * *

He reached the mouth of the Notch at last light, and it was worse than he remembered and better than he feared.

The pass was a slot between two walls of granite, narrow enough to shout across, floored with old snowpack that had survived a dozen summers in the shadow of the southern wall. His boots broke through its crust with every step, punching through to the ice beneath, and he learned within fifty yards to plant his feet with care, testing each hold before committing his weight. The wind funneled through the gap with a steady roar, driving the snow horizontally, filling the air with a white static that erased everything past ten feet.

But it was passable. That was the word he held onto. Passable, if it stayed passable, if the storm didn't double its effort overnight, if the drifts in the lee of the walls hadn't grown past wading depth in the miles ahead.

He found a wind pocket at the base of the northern wall, a shallow alcove where the granite had spalled away centuries ago, and he wedged himself into it with his back to the rock and his blanket around him and his oilskin over that, and he ate the last of the bread and half the jerky and made himself chew slowly, deliberately, because tomorrow's rationing started now and the body believed what the mouth told it.

Through the mouth of the alcove he could see a sliver of the eastern sky, and it was the color of lead.

Somewhere beyond that wall, on the far side of the range, the country dropped away into drainages he knew only from a distance—from ridgelines glimpsed across sixty miles of desert, from stories traded at water holes, from the kind of knowledge a man accumulates about a place he's never been. Down there, if the tracks told true, was a young woman with a satchel full of dangerous paper, and beyond her, whatever refuge she'd been running toward for a hundred miles.

And somewhere behind him, under six inches of new snow and falling, was the last of his own trail.

Lena pulled the blanket to his chin and listened to the storm build against the granite, and he thought about the strangeness of what he'd done. Twenty years spent teaching the world to lose him, and in three days he'd sent his horse away, abandoned his rifle, and climbed into a blizzard after a stranger, on the strength of a worn boot heel and a scrap of red-inked map. If Amara—no. He didn't know anyone down there yet. If anyone from his old life could see him, they'd say he'd finally lost the plot entirely.

But that was just it. He hadn't lost the plot. For the first time in twenty years, he'd found one.

Sleep came slowly, in pieces, between the gusts. Once, deep in the night, he woke to a silence so total he thought the storm had ended—but it was only the lull between fronts, the mountain drawing breath, and when he pushed his head out of the alcove the snow was falling thicker than ever, straight down now, windless, burying the world with a patience that made the wind seem merciful.

In that silence he heard it: far off, downslope on the eastern side, a sound too regular for rockfall, too heavy for an animal.

Footsteps. Or the ghost of footsteps—echoed off the granite, distorted by snow, impossible to place.

Or maybe nothing. Maybe the mountain talking to itself, the way it did in cold.

Lena lay back against the stone and stared into the dark and made himself catalog the possibilities the way he'd catalog everything for twenty years, and the last one—the one he kept returning to, the one that put a slow heat under his ribs that had nothing to do with fear—was this:

He wasn't the only one crossing tonight.

Somebody else was in the Notch ahead of him, moving through the same storm, and by first light he would know whether the mountain had given him a trail to follow or a rival to meet.
