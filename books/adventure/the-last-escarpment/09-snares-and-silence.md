## 9. Snares and Silence

The thief worked by feel, the way she'd learned to do everything in the last eleven days.

The slot canyon narrowed to a shoulder's width, then opened into a pocket of gravel and twisted juniper where the walls leaned back just enough to let a person stand upright. A good place to rest. Which meant it was a good place for whoever followed her to rest too—if they were still following. She had stopped believing she'd lost him somewhere on the third day, when the crows had started up ahead of her, twice, at nothing she could see. Birds didn't spook at wind. They spooked at men.

So: snares.

She knelt in the gravel and unpacked the coil of brass wire she'd taken from the survey camp's equipment stores—her one theft that had nothing to do with documents—and began to work. The first snare went across the pocket's only entrance, a running loop anchored to a juniper root, set at knee height. Not a killing snare. She'd thought about that for a long time in the dark of the first night, lying awake with the satchel pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, deciding what kind of person she was going to be out here. A leg-hold would stop a man. A throat snare would end him. She set the loop high enough to catch a thigh, low enough to trip, and left the anchor loose so it would hold but not drag.

The second went at the pocket's far side, where the canyon pinched again—a deadfall trigger propped on a flat stone, holding up a slab of sandstone no bigger than a bread box. It would bruise. It would warn. That was all she wanted from it. Warning, and time, and the sound of it going off carrying down the canyon like a bell.

She tested each one with a stick, adjusted the tension until the wire sang faintly under her fingers, then swept her own tracks with a branch of greasewood. The gravel took the brushing well. By the time she climbed out of the pocket through a crack in the east wall—a route she'd found by lying on her belly and studying the rock for an hour—the camp looked untouched, innocent, waiting.

She sat above it on a ledge with her back against warm stone and ate a handful of the survey team's dried apples and did not think about the taste of them, which was the taste of the last meal she'd eaten beside people who were now either dead or scattered or hunted, depending on which version of the story you believed.

*They'll say we got lost,* she thought. *That's the kind thing. Lost in an early storm, all eight of them, instruments packed, food untouched.* She almost laughed. Even the lie they'd build around the disappearance would be sloppy. Nobody packs their instruments before getting lost.

Below her, the pocket lay quiet in the afternoon light. Somewhere south and west, days behind her now, there was a ranger tracking her. She knew it the way you know weather coming—not from evidence exactly, but from the shape of things. The valley folk had told her about him. *There's a man works the escarpment,* Amara had said, the one time Sera had come down for supplies, two years ago, a nervous girl with a forged pass and a satchel of nothing. *Fair, they say. Fairer than the law deserves. If he ever finds this place, don't run from him. Run near him.*

She hadn't understood that advice then. Lying on the ledge now, watching her own snares glint faintly in the gravel below, she thought she might be starting to.

* * *

Lena found the first snare forty minutes after noon, and stopped so abruptly that his horse bumped his shoulder.

He'd been reading sign since dawn—the thief's tracks were three days old here, crisp-edged in the dry wash sand, unhurried, economical. No wasted steps. No circling. Whoever she was, she moved like water finding its level, and every mile of trail had been teaching him something he hadn't wanted to learn: that he wasn't chasing a panicked criminal anymore. Panicked people wandered. This person walked a line.

But the snare was different. The snare made him dismount and stand very still for a long time.

It was beautiful work. He meant that professionally. The anchor was set behind the root collar where the pull wouldn't tear loose; the loop height said *stop a man*, not *kill him*; the drag tension was tuned so the wire would sing rather than snap. And the sweep of the approach track was done with greasewood, in long even strokes, by someone who understood that a brushed trail reads as a blank page only to a man in a hurry.

Lena was not in a hurry. Lena had spent twenty years learning to read blank pages.

He circled wide, found the second trap—the deadfall, its trigger stone balanced with a delicacy that made him think less of a poacher and more of a watchmaker—and then stood in the middle of the pocket looking at both of them, and felt something shift in his chest that he hadn't felt in years.

Fear. Not his own.

"You're scared," he said aloud, to nobody, to the empty air of the pocket. "You've been scared since the camp."

Because that was what the snares told him, once he stopped admiring the craft and started listening to it. A criminal sets traps to protect loot. A killer sets traps to kill. These snares were set by someone protecting *time*—buying hours, buying warning, building a wall of small noises between herself and the thing behind her. And the thing behind her wasn't him. He'd read that too, in the way the snares faced. Both of them covered the southern approaches. Both of them watched the direction of Harrow Bend, of the government road, of the world with badges in it.

She wasn't running from a tracker. She was running from whatever had happened at that camp, and she expected it to follow her.

Lena crouched by the first snare and did something he would have called foolish a week ago: he left it standing. Then he took a strip of jerky from his pack, wrapped it around a stone, and set the stone inside the loop where she'd find it if she doubled back—because she would double back, a person this careful always checked her work—and on the flat face of the stone he scratched, with his knife, a single word:

*ALONE.*

Then he stood, dusted his knees, and looked south toward the high range, where the peaks had put on their first white caps overnight, early, weeks early, the mountains keeping their own counsel about the season.

"Run near me," he murmured, not knowing why the words came, and swung back into the saddle.

* * *

She found the stone at dusk.

She'd doubled back along the ledge route as the light failed, checking her snares the way she checked everything now—twice, three times, compulsively—and the moment she saw the loop disturbed she went flat behind the rimrock and stayed there through a full quarter hour of dying light, watching, listening, cataloguing every sound the canyon made and what each one meant.

Nothing came. Nothing moved. Finally, moving like smoke, she went down.

The jerky was good—better than hers, salted harder, cut by someone who'd done it ten thousand times. She ate it sitting cross-legged in the gravel with the stone in her lap, and it took her four full minutes to turn the stone over and see the word.

*ALONE.*

Her first instinct was that it was a trick, some new species of trap, bait for the mind instead of the body. Her second instinct, arriving slower and settling deeper, was that no trick would bother being this honest. A liar writes more than one word. A liar writes *help* or *friend* or *I know about the valley*. One word, scratched in haste with a knife point, is either true or it is a very sophisticated cruelty, and she had met enough of the company's men to know they weren't capable of sophistication. They were capable of money and menace and nothing else.

She sat in the dark pocket with the stone warming in her hands and thought about Amara's voice: *run near him.*

"Alone," she whispered, testing it.

She wasn't alone. That was the whole point of everything—the satchel, the flight, the eleven days of fear. She carried eight men's worth of testimony in a canvas bag against her ribs, and the dead ones couldn't speak, and the living ones were scattered like seed, and the truth weighed maybe six pounds and felt like six hundred.

She looked up the canyon, north, toward the high range and the passes that were already whitening, and then south, toward the darkness where a man had passed through her traps without springing them, without breaking them, without even disturbing the sweep of the gravel beyond what his own careful feet required.

He'd read them. That was the thing that frightened her most, when she finally let herself feel it. He'd read them like a letter. And he'd answered.

Sera wrapped the stone in her bandana, tucked it into the satchel beside the documents—absurd, sentimental, the first irrational thing she'd done in eleven days—and climbed back to her ledge.

In the morning she broke camp and moved north, and for the first time since the camp, she did not brush her tracks.

* * *

Lena read the unbrushed trail the next afternoon and sat down on a rock to think about it.

Three days of discipline, broken in a single morning. Not carelessness—he'd seen her work; she didn't have a careless bone in her body. A message. The same message he'd sent, sent back: *I know you're there. I'm letting you see me.*

Or a trap. He considered that honestly, turning it over the way he turned over everything, because hope was the one snare he'd never learned to disarm. Twenty years ago another man's signature on another man's report had taught him what trusting cost. He kept the consideration, weighed it, and set it aside. If she wanted him dead, the deadfall yesterday had been her chance, and she'd built it to bruise.

He followed the open trail north through the afternoon, and the country rose around him, and the air began to smell of snow.

By evening he could see the pass itself—a black notch in the rampart of the range, already dusted, already narrowing—and below it, half-hidden in the blue shadow of the foothills, a thread of woodsmoke rising from a fold in the land where no map showed anything but bare rock.

One fire. Small. Careful. Set in a hollow that would hide it from anyone who didn't climb to look.

Lena sat his horse on the ridge and watched that thin gray thread stand up straight in the still cold air, and understood that tomorrow, or the day after, the trail would end—at a person, at a confrontation, at whatever truth she carried in that guarded satchel of hers. And beyond her, if the smoke meant what he suspected it meant, there was something larger. Something that appeared on no map and burned no visible fires and had survived out here for years, invisible, the way he had survived. The way he had *chosen* to survive.

The first flakes came down while he watched, fat and slow, melting on his sleeve.

He thought about the word he'd scratched on the stone. He'd meant it as a fact. Standing there in the beginning snow, with the smoke thread rising and the pass closing like a door, he realized it had also been a question—one he'd been asking himself for twenty years without knowing it.

He turned his horse downslope, toward the smoke.
