## 10. First Snow

The weather had been lying to him for two days, and Lena Delacroix had let himself believe it.

That was the first mistake. Twenty years on the escarpment and he'd made the oldest mistake there was: he'd read the sky as a friend instead of an adversary. The morning had come up clear and hard, blue as fired enamel, the kind of morning that promised three more days of the same. But the wind had been out of the north since noon, and it carried a smell he should have trusted over his own eyes—the smell of nothing, of air scrubbed clean of dust, of cold moving down from somewhere with more cold behind it.

By mid-afternoon the light had gone strange. Not the amber of evening but a flat, sourceless white, as if the sun were being filtered through something vast and slow that hadn't arrived yet. He stopped on the shoulder of the ridge above the tree line, one hand on a granite fin worn smooth by ten thousand winters, and did what he always did: he counted.

Distance was how he kept time. Miles, not years. He'd told himself that so long it had become true. And the count now said this: eleven miles to the pass mouth, four to the cave country below the snowline if he turned back, six days at best to the valley if the trail held, and no trail at all if the snow came before he crossed.

He looked north. The horizon had thickened. Where the desert had been sharp-edged all week—a line you could have cut yourself on—there was now a softness, a gray blur like breath on glass. It was coming up the flats faster than anything weather had a right to move.

"Damn," he said, to no one, because there was no one.

He thought about turning back. He actually stood there and weighed it, the way he weighed everything: water remaining, food remaining, daylight remaining, the arithmetic of his own body. Turning back meant losing the trail until spring. Losing the trail until spring meant the thief vanished into whatever country she'd come from, and the survey team stayed vanished too, and Harrow Bend's nervous officials got their answer or didn't, and none of it was his problem anymore.

Except that it was. He couldn't have said why. That bothered him more than the weather.

He went up instead.

* * *

The tree line fell away beneath him within the hour, the last stunted pines giving up in ones and twos, then all together, until there was nothing above him but rock and lichen and the first flakes coming down out of the white.

They came small and dry at first, almost polite, ticking against the stone like sand. He'd seen snow do this a hundred times—arrive as a rumor, testing the ground, deciding whether the country would take it. He used the grace period. He moved fast and low across the shoulders of scree, following the line he'd mapped from below: a shelf of harder rock that ran east along the face, cutting the angle of the climb, leading toward a notch he could see between two peaks where the range dipped like a saddle.

The wind found him at four thousand feet. It came off the northern faces with a sound like tearing cloth, and it took the heat off him in seconds, straight through the wool, straight through the oilskin. He pulled his neckerchief up over his nose and breathed through it and kept counting steps, because counting was what he had. Four hundred steps to the shelf. Rest thirty. Four hundred more.

The snow stopped being polite around five thousand feet.

It came then in earnest, fat and wet and horizontal, filling the air so completely that up and down became suggestions. His world shrank to a circle ten feet across, lit gray, bounded by falling white. The shelf vanished under an inch of new snow, then two, and his own tracks filled behind him as fast as he made them, which meant that if he fell or wandered, there would be nothing left of his passing. No sign. No story. A man erased from the mountain inside an afternoon.

He knew men it had happened to. He'd found two of them, over the years, when spring uncovered what winter had hidden. They always looked surprised.

"Not me," he said into the wind, and the wind took the words and gave him nothing back.

The notch was gone. He knew roughly where it should be—he'd been reading the terrain all day, building the crossing in his head the way he built everything—but roughly wasn't good enough anymore. In whiteout, roughly killed. He needed shelter within the hour or he needed to be a luckier man than he'd ever been.

He remembered a shadow. That was all. Somewhere on this face, years ago, hunting a horse thief whose name he'd never learned, he'd noticed a dark seam in the rock below a leaning slab—a shadow too regular to be natural, holding its shape at noon when every other shadow moved. He'd noted it the way he noted everything, filed it in the ledger he kept behind his eyes, and ridden on. He'd never needed it. Nobody had ever needed it.

Now he needed it, and he had to find it in a world with no edges.

He traversed by feel. Hand on the rock, boot testing each hold before committing weight, moving the way water moves—always downhill-ish, always patient, never fighting. The snow came down his collar and melted and ran cold along his spine. His fingers went from aching to burning to nothing at all, and he worked them inside his coat at every rest, flexing them against his ribs, keeping them honest.

Twice he stopped and simply listened, because sight was finished. Under the wind's roar there were other sounds, if you knew to sort for them: the tick of spindrift off stone, the groan of the range settling under cold, and once—faint, maybe imagined—the hollow note of wind crossing an opening. Air moving through a gap makes a sound. Caves breathe.

He followed the breathing.

It took him forty minutes he would later not be able to account for, minutes lost to the white like coins through a torn pocket. Then his hand, sweeping the rock face ahead of him, passed through a curtain of blowing snow into stillness, and he stepped forward into a darkness that smelled of dry stone and old smoke, and the mountain closed its door behind him.

* * *

He gave himself ten minutes of doing absolutely nothing.

This was discipline, not weakness. Every year the mountain took someone who'd survived the approach and then died twenty feet inside shelter because they panicked—stripped wet clothes in a freezing cave, or fell asleep on bare stone, or burned everything they had in the first hour and froze in the third. Lena sat just inside the entrance, out of the wind but not deep, and let his breathing slow, and took inventory the way he'd taken inventory ten thousand times.

One bedroll, half-damp. One oilskin, holding. One tin of biscuit, most of a tin. Dried meat, eight strips. Water in the canteen, two-thirds, and snow outside for as long as the storm lasted, which meant water forever, which was the only mercy on offer. Matches, waterproofed, nineteen. A knife. A coil of snare wire. A pistol with twelve rounds he'd never once fired at a living thing in twenty years of carrying it.

And beyond that: nothing. No rope worth trusting. No tent. No partner. No one who knew where he was.

That last one he set aside carefully, the way you set aside a thought that could hurt you, and got to work.

He explored the cave on hands and knees first, feeling the floor, because floors told the truth. It rose gently toward the back—good, cold air drains downhill and out—and it was dry gravel over solid stone, no drip line, no seep. The ceiling was high enough to sit under without hunching except near the entrance. There was a chamber deeper in, past a waist-high rib of rock, and when he felt his way around the rib and struck a match, the light showed him something that stopped his hand mid-motion.

Smoke stains.

Not fresh. Not old, either. Black fans spread across the ceiling above a ring of stones, layered, many fires' worth, the soot thick as felt in the center and feathering out at the edges. Someone had burned a great deal of wood in this cave, over a great deal of time. And around the fire ring, when he brought the match lower, he saw the rest: a flat stone worn smooth in the middle, polished by sitting. A niche cut into the wall, deliberate, chisel-marked, holding nothing now. Two forked sticks leaned in a corner, soot-darkened, the kind used for hanging meat over flame.

Lena knelt there a long time, the match burning down toward his fingers, looking at the workmanship of it. This wasn't a prospector's overnight hole. Prospectors didn't carve niches. Trappers didn't polish sitting stones. This was a place someone came back to. This was a place someone had *lived*—season after season, maybe year after year, high above the tree line, higher than any sane reason to stay.

He thought of the tracks on the flats. Single set, light, long-strided, heading into the forbidden range. He thought of the snares he'd read two days ago, tied with knots no trapper used, set by hands that were skilled and desperate at once. He thought of the torn map fragment at the seep, quadrant lines in government red ink, land marked worthless that drank from an aquifer nobody official was supposed to know about.

And he thought: *someone winters here.*

The match died. In the dark he sat back on his heels and felt the shape of the thought settle over him, and it was not fear exactly. It was the feeling of a map redrawing itself. He had spent twenty years believing he was the only person alive who had chosen these mountains over the world—that his exile was a singular condition, a private country of one. Now the smoke on the ceiling told him otherwise. Somebody else had erased themselves up here, thoroughly enough to build a home of it. Somebody else had looked at the same empty ranges he'd looked at and seen the same thing: room.

The valley. It had to lead to the valley. The thief's tracks ran this way; the map fragment pointed this way; and now a furnished cave, a wintered-in cave, sat on the route like a signpost written in soot. Everything braided together, running toward some center he couldn't see yet.

He got the fire going small—one handful of dead scrub he'd stuffed in his pack below the tree line, fed stick by stick, the flames tucked low behind the stone ring so their light wouldn't leak out the entrance. Heat was life, but light was death up here; anyone watching the face in this weather would see a glow in a second. He cooked nothing. He ate two strips of meat and a biscuit, chewing slowly, making the food last the way he made everything last, and he melted snow in the tin for water, drinking it warm, and the warmth of it nearly undid him. That was the thing nobody warned you about. Cold you could fight. Warmth, when it came unexpected, broke something open.

He sat against the wall with the fire at his feet and let the mountain have its way with him.

His hands wouldn't stop shaking for an hour. Not from the cold alone. The body keeps its own books, and his body was presenting him with the bill for the day: the traverse, the whiteout, the terror he hadn't let himself feel while it was happening because feeling it would have cost him the precision he needed to survive it. Now the precision was spent and the fear came due, all at once, and he shook with it in the firelight like a man in a fever, teeth clicking, jaw aching with the effort of staying quiet.

Twenty years, he thought. Twenty years of miles, and the mountain had stripped him down in one afternoon to what he'd been at the beginning: an animal in a hole, grateful for fire, listening to the storm.

There was no competence left to hide behind. Up here his reputation meant nothing—there was no one to know it, and the storm didn't care anyway. The escarpment had never cared whether he was fair or unfair, guilty or innocent, tracker or fugitive. It only asked the one question, over and over, every day: *can you endure?* And today the honest answer was: barely. Today, barely.

He laughed once, quietly, at the absurdity of it. Nine days ago he'd been a professional. He'd read sign that stumped younger men; he'd walked into Harrow Bend and taken a contract off officials who watched him with nervous eyes; he'd tracked a ghost across open desert with nothing but patience. And now here he sat, owned entirely by weather, in a stranger's house, warming his hands at a stranger's fire, waiting for morning the way children wait for morning.

But underneath the shaking, underneath the humbling, something else was happening. Something quieter. For the first time in twenty years, he was not alone in these mountains. The evidence was all around him—soot and polished stone and carved niches—and evidence was the one language he'd never stopped speaking. Someone lived here. Someone endured here. Which meant endurance was not the solitary art he'd practiced. Which meant—

He didn't finish the thought. Sleep took him mid-sentence, sitting up, fire banked to coals, one hand on the knife.

* * *

He woke to silence, and silence above the tree line in winter is worse than noise.

Storm-silence means the storm has either ended or thickened past the point of wind, and you can't tell which without looking, and looking costs. He lay still and listened. No wind at the entrance. The particular muffled pressure of heavy snowfall, the kind that comes down without conviction and buries everything anyway. Snow still falling, then. Deepening.

He crawled to the entrance and looked out at a world remade. The scree slopes were gone. The shelf was gone. The whole face of the mountain had been smoothed into one continuous white curve, featureless, directionless, beautiful the way a trap is beautiful. His tracks from yesterday: erased. Any route he might have chosen: buried. The notch he'd aimed for: invisible. If he stepped out into that, he'd walk in circles until he dropped, and the mountain would keep him, and no one would ever know enough to look.

So he stayed. There was nothing else. He fed the fire from a cache of scrub wood he found stacked in the inner chamber—stacked, deliberately, cut to length, seasoned dry, a supply laid in by whoever wintered here, enough for perhaps a week of careful burning—and the discovery of it settled the last question. This was a home. You don't cache wood in a place you mean to leave.

He spent the day the way the mountain demanded: minimally. He ate one strip of meat. He melted snow. He exercised his fingers and toes by the fire, working blood back into them, checking them for the white patches that meant frostbite setting its hooks. He examined the cave further and found more of the resident's leavings: a clay pot, cracked, repaired with pine pitch. A bundle of dried herbs hung from a peg, wild sage and something resinous he didn't know. And in the carved niche, when he finally reached into it, his fingers found scratches in the stone floor of it—tallies, dozens of them, grouped in fives, cut deep enough to last.

Counting days. Someone had sat in this exact spot, season after season, cutting marks into stone to prove the time was passing. He understood that completely. He measured his own life in miles because miles could be walked alone; this stranger measured theirs in scratched tallies because days could be survived alone. Two systems, one loneliness.

He put his hand flat over the tallies and left them there.

Near dusk the storm thickened again, wind returning with a rising moan, and he sealed the entrance as best he could with his oilskin and snow blocks, and lay in the bedroll watching coals, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

Food: four days, careful. The pass: closed, certainly, for however long this system lasted—days, possibly weeks. Behind him: the descent, buried, lethal. Ahead of him: the valley, wherever it was, unreachable until the mountain allowed it. And somewhere below or beyond, a young woman with a satchel full of stolen truth, moving through country she knew better than he did, and a vanished survey team, and nervous men in Harrow Bend paying too much for answers, and all of it waiting on him while he sat in a hole in the rock eating one strip of meat a day.

Vulnerability, he decided, tasted like old smoke and cold iron.

He slept in patches. Once, deep in the night, he woke certain he'd heard something—footsteps, or the crunch of a boot on the crusted drift outside the entrance—and he lay with the knife loose in his hand for an hour, listening, while the storm said nothing. It might have been the mountain settling. It might have been a fox. It might have been the person who cached this wood and cut these tallies, standing out in the dark ten feet away, deciding what to do about the stranger in their house.

If it was, they'd seen his fire. They'd know he was here. And they hadn't come in, and they hadn't led trouble to him, and they hadn't taken the wood.

In the morning the storm broke as suddenly as it had gathered, the clouds ripping apart on a wind out of the west, and sunlight came down the mountainside like something poured. He dug out the entrance and stood blinking in the glare, snow-blindness risk forgotten for one full minute, staring at a landscape transformed. Three feet of new snow, level and blue-shadowed, unbroken in every direction.

Unbroken except once.

Fifty yards below the cave, crossing the open slope in a single purposeful line, ran a set of tracks. Fresh—made in the last hours, edges still crisp, before the wind could soften them. Light. Long-strided. Small-footed.

Heading uphill. Toward the cave. And then, ten yards short of the entrance, turning aside, circling wide, and continuing on toward the notch in the ridge—the pass he'd failed to find in the whiteout—marked now, unmistakably, by a path of footprints through three feet of new snow.

Whoever wintered here had come in the storm's last gasp, looked at his smoke, judged him, and left him a road.

Lena stood in the sun with the cold pouring off the snowfield and felt something turn over in his chest that he refused to name. Then he went back inside, kicked apart the fire, packed his gear, and started climbing in another person's footsteps, toward the notch, toward whatever waited on the other side of the pass.

The tracks never once looked back at him. But they waited, he noticed, at every place the route grew doubtful—pausing at the top of each pitch, a small dark interruption in the white, as if whoever made them had stopped to make sure he was still following.
