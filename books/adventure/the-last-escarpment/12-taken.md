## 12. Taken

They brought him in at dusk, and the whole valley knew it before the scouts reached the gate.

Word traveled the way it always did here—by children running the terraces, by a bell that wasn't a bell but an old surveyor's triangle struck with a hammer, three notes that meant *stranger*. Sera heard it from the drying shed where she'd been hanging peppers, and her hands went still on the twine before her mind caught up to why.

Three notes. A stranger, taken alive.

She had been in the valley eleven days. Long enough to learn the bell codes, the rotation of watch fires, which of Amara's granddaughters was allowed to carry a knife and which only pretended to. Long enough to know that strangers did not come down off the escarpment in November by accident, and that the last stranger who tried had been turned back at the fog line with a broken wrist and a warning he'd be lucky to survive repeating.

She set down the twine and walked toward the gate because she could not make herself do anything else.

* * *

The scouts had him kneeling in the trampled snow of the lower yard, hands bound behind him with the same waxed cord they used for snares, two men flanking him and a third holding a rifle that was more threat than intention—Doss held it, and Doss always held things like they were borrowed. The stranger knelt like a man who had knelt before and hadn't minded much either time. Snow in his beard. Frost in the seams of his coat. Eyes that were doing something no captive's eyes should do: counting.

Sera watched him count. The walls. The men. The distances between. The rooflines. She knew that arithmetic; she'd done it herself for three weeks on the flats, alone, with a satchel full of papers that could hang a dozen men. It was the arithmetic of someone deciding whether a place was worth dying in or worth escaping from, and this man was doing it calmly, almost politely, the way other people checked the weather.

"Found him at the fog line," one of the scouts was saying to Amara, who had come down from the long house with her shawl pinned wrong and her face already set. "No horse. He sent it back—that's the truth of it, we read his backtrack for half a mile. He came down the scree on foot, alone, in weather no sane man crosses."

"He read our snares," the second scout said. There was something almost offended in it. "All four. Didn't spring one. Stepped over the deadfall like he'd set it himself."

Amara looked at the kneeling man for a long moment. "Name," she said.

He lifted his head. Up close, Sera could see the years on him—not just the gray in the beard but the particular worn smoothness of a man who had spent two decades making sure nothing about him was memorable, and had failed at it in every way that mattered.

"Lena Delacroix," he said.

The yard went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone drops glass.

* * *

Sera had never seen his face before. That was the strange thing she would remember afterward—the first time she saw the man whose trail she'd been fleeing all season, whose reputation had reached her months before his tracks did, she learned it standing in falling snow while other people decided whether he should live.

But she knew the name the way you know a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten.

The survey camp had been full of talk, that last week before everything went wrong. Men around the stove trading stories to keep the wind out, and the senior surveyor, Halloran—a decent man, a tired man, a man who had already begun to suspect what the red ink on his quadrants really meant—had told the story of the ranger who tracked a murderer across forty miles of salt flat with no water and brought him back alive. Not dead. Alive, through country that killed grown horses, carrying a man who'd earned killing twice over.

"Why?" the junior man had asked. "Why not just end it out there? No witnesses."

And Halloran had shrugged and said the thing Sera had carried with her ever since, the thing she'd tested against every hard choice since the night she ran: *"Because Delacroix doesn't decide what a man deserves. He decides what the evidence says. Everything else is somebody else's job."*

An outlaw's fairy tale, some would say. But the men in that camp weren't outlaws yet. They were government men, and even among them the story circulated like contraband—the ranger who was fair when fairness cost him. Who testified once, they said, against his own side. Who got destroyed for it, though the details shifted depending on who told them. Who lived out past the escarpment now, alone, wanting nothing from anyone.

Wanting nothing from anyone. And here he was, having crossed the high range ahead of the snows, alone, on foot, reading their snares like scripture, kneeling in their yard.

He wasn't here by accident. He was here because of her. Because of the tracks she'd left on the flats like a signature, because of the map fragment at the seep, because twenty years of solitary craft had finally snagged on the one thread she couldn't cut loose: a satchel full of paper that proved the survey team hadn't vanished. They'd been erased. On purpose. For water.

And if she could follow that thread to its meaning, so could the company. So could Verrin's kind. Which meant every hour this man knelt in their yard was an hour the valley's location sat in a head that might be made to give it up.

She understood, suddenly and completely, why half the faces around her were hard as the rocks above the tree line.

* * *

"Delacroix." Amara said the name like she was weighing it. "The ranger."

"I was a ranger," he said. His voice was roughened by cold but level. "Twenty years ago. Now I'm a man who lost a contract."

"You were hired," Amara said. "In Harrow Bend. To find the survey team." It wasn't a question. Word moved between the valley and the low towns the way water moved under rock—slowly, invisibly, but it moved. "You found us instead."

"I found tracks," he said. "The rest I followed."

"The rest being us."

"The rest being the truth," he said, "whatever shape it turns out to be. I don't know yours yet. I know theirs is rotten—I've known since the seep. Red ink on a quadrant nobody should have surveyed. Men paid too well to look for other men too well equipped to get lost. Somebody wanted that team gone, and somebody paid me to be the eraser's broom." He paused. "I didn't take the job to help them. I took it because I've spent twenty years learning what it looks like when powerful men bury something, and this smelled exactly like that."

"And now you've found us," Amara said. "A valley that exists because no one knows it does. You understand our problem."

"I understand it completely," Delacroix said. "I'd have the same problem."

Doss shifted, and the rifle came up a few degrees. "Then you understand why we can't let you walk out of here."

"Doss." Amara didn't raise her voice. She rarely needed to. But Doss settled anyway, unhappy, the rifle dropping to a ready-carry that fooled nobody.

It was then that Sera stepped forward.

She hadn't decided to. Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one—just her boots moving through the snow and her voice arriving before her courage did.

"Ask him about the salt flat," she said.

Every head turned. Amara's eyes found hers, patient and terrible in their patience. "You have something to say, Sera?"

"Ask him," Sera said, "why the story is that he carried a murderer out alive."

* * *

The council met in the long house within the hour, because Amara was not a woman who let decisions cool overnight when they could curdle into factions instead. Sera was invited—or summoned; the distinction felt thin—and stood near the door with her satchel against her hip, that old reflex, while the argument went around the fire like a storm cell.

Kill him. That was the first voice, and it belonged to reason as much as fear. He was a tracker without peer; he'd crossed their outer defenses like a man walking home; he'd come hunting the thief who stole from the company, and where the thief went, the company's attention followed. Every day he breathed inside the valley was a day the valley's secret weighed more. A grave above the tree line. The snow would keep until spring. Cruel, yes—but the valley had survived on cruelty rationed carefully, mostly aimed inward, at comfort, at risk, at hope.

Turn him out blindfolded. That was the second voice, softer, and it died quickly under its own objections: he'd memorized the approach. Blindfolds forgot. A man like that could walk the route in his sleep, and would, because someone in Harrow Bend had paid him, and men who were paid answered to whoever held the next payment.

Keep him. Third voice, almost hopeful, immediately shouted down—keep him how? A prisoner eats. A prisoner watches. A prisoner is a hole in a boat.

Through all of it, Delacroix knelt where they'd left him—no, Sera corrected herself, watching through the doorway; he'd been moved to sit against the wall, hands still bound, and he sat the way water sits, taking the shape of his container without surrendering anything essential. Listening. Not pleading. Not performing innocence. Just listening to men debate his life with the same calm attention he'd given the snares.

That, more than anything, frightened her. Not because it was cold, but because it was honest. He genuinely believed they had the right to decide. Or he genuinely didn't care. With him, she suspected, the two had worn down to the same surface, like stone in a riverbed.

"Enough." Amara's voice closed the storm. "Sera. You called for this question. Ask it."

Sera's mouth was dry. She had argued against killing him in the yard, briefly, badly, drowned out by the practical voices. Here, in the firelight, with the satchel heavy against her ribs, she understood that she was arguing for more than one life.

"You all know what I am," she began. "Junior clerk, Meridian Water and Land Consortium survey, fourth quadrant expedition. I know what's in my satchel, and I know what it costs if it reaches the wrong hands, and I know what it's worth if it reaches the right ones. And I'm telling you that the man tied to your wall is the single most valuable thing that has walked into this valley since I did."

Murmurs. Doss's jaw tightened.

"Not because of what he can do," she said. "Because of what he won't do. There are stories about him. I heard them in a government camp, from government men, who had no reason to lie in his favor. He tracked a killer across the salt and carried him out alive because he doesn't decide what people deserve—he decides what the evidence says. I have spent three months betting my life that the world still contained one person like that. I ran toward the escarpment because of those stories. And the tracks I've been following all season—" she stopped, steadied herself, "—the tracks I was running from turned out to belong to the one man in these mountains whose fairness has been tested at a price none of us has paid."

"You don't know that," Doss said. "Stories are bait. Maybe he's good. Good trackers make the best hunters."

"Yes," Sera said. "Maybe. So test him. Don't kill him—killing him proves nothing except that we're what the company says we are. Fugitives. Dangerous. Worth erasing. Test him. Give him the truth and see what he does with it. If I'm wrong—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—if I'm wrong, then I'll take responsibility. I brought the danger. I'll carry it."

"That's generous," someone muttered, "spending other people's blood on your faith."

"It's not faith," Sera said. "It's evidence. I'm a clerk. I don't deal in faith."

* * *

They asked him one question in the end. Amara crouched in front of him, firelight between them, and put it plainly:

"The company erased a survey team to steal water from land they believe is empty. We are the emptiness they're wrong about. Tomorrow we may show you documents that prove it—documents that could save us or destroy us, depending entirely on what you do with them. Why should we show you?"

Delacroix was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Because I was buried once myself," he said. "Twenty years ago. A reservoir failed and nine people died, and the men who built it cheap needed someone guilty, and I was convenient. I never served a day—they didn't need me in a cell. They needed me gone, and I went. Everything I've done since has been one long lesson in what buried men look like." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were not hard the way she'd imagined from the stories; they were tired, and clear, and very old. "I know what your valley is. I've been living in one for twenty years—it just didn't have gardens."

Amara rose. The council looked at her. Sera realized she was holding her breath and made herself stop.

"We'll decide by morning," Amara said. "Someone bring him food. Untie his hands to eat—Doss, you'll sit with him." A pause, dry as the flats. "And Doss. He untied you once, by all accounts, a murderer on a salt flat. Try to be worthy of the comparison."

Laughter broke somewhere in the room, small and startled, the way laughter does when fear cracks. Even Doss almost smiled.

Sera stayed by the door while the council emptied. Delacroix ate slowly, watching the fire, and when her shadow fell across him he looked up without surprise, as if he'd known she was there the whole time—as of course he had.

"You're the thief," he said. Not unkindly.

"I'm the witness," she said.

Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, maybe, of the distinction, of how much it mattered. "The map fragment at the seep," he said. "Red ink. You wanted to be followed."

"I wanted to be found," Sera said, "by the right person. I just didn't know it would take a blizzard to arrange."

He almost smiled. It transformed his face and then retreated, like sun through cloud. "Get some sleep," he said. "If your council shows me those documents tomorrow, I'll need to read fast. Weather's turning again, and I don't think any of us has until spring."

Outside, the snow kept falling on the terraces, softening every edge, erasing every track—the valley's oldest ally, working as it always worked, silently, and for free.

But Sera stood in the doorway a long time, watching it come down, and for the first time since she'd run, she let herself think the dangerous thought:

What if the right person had finally arrived—and what if keeping him meant the valley could no longer stay hidden at all?

Tomorrow, the documents. Tomorrow, the vote she could already hear forming in the firelit dark.

Tonight, the snow, falling on everything equally, hiding everything, promising nothing.
