## 13. People Without Papers

They took his boots.

Not as theft—Lena understood that within the first hour—but as policy. A man without boots could still walk, but not far, and not fast, and everyone in this valley seemed to agree on one thing above all others: nobody left quickly. The woman who'd taken them, a broad-shouldered gray-haired settler named Ilsa who moved like she'd spent a lifetime hauling water, had set them by her stove to dry and told him, in an accent from somewhere east of anywhere he'd ever been, "You'll have them back when you're ready to be reasonable."

"I'm always reasonable," Lena said.

"Then you'll be wanting them back soon," she said, and smiled, and it was not entirely unkind.

So he sat in a stone house with walls two feet thick and a roof of split cedar, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and sheep lanolin, and watched the valley through a window cut deep into the rock so that no light escaped at night. That was the first lesson of the place, he realized. Everything here was built twice: once for living and once for not being seen. Window recesses. Chimneys that vented through rock scree so the smoke dispersed before it rose. Garden terraces stepped into the hillside in the shape of natural talus slopes, because from above, a garden that looks like geology is invisible, but a garden that looks like a garden is a confession.

He'd spent twenty years learning how to disappear into country like this. These people had built disappearing into their foundations.

The house belonged to a family named Corrigan—a father with a miller's forearms, a mother who said almost nothing and missed nothing, three children who stared at him from doorways with the frank appraisal of kids raised around strangers who might be trouble. The youngest, a girl of maybe seven, finally worked up the nerve to ask him the question the adults were too polite for.

"Why's your face all cut up?"

"Sera," the father said warningly.

"It's all right." Lena touched his cheek where the slot canyon had opened it. "I fell down a canyon chasing somebody."

"Did you catch them?"

"Not yet."

"Are you going to?"

"That depends on what they did."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Papa says everybody here did something or they wouldn't be here."

"Tomás," the mother said quietly, and the father went silent, but the damage was done, and Lena filed it away with everything else. Everybody here did something. He looked out the window at the terraces, the stone houses tucked against the cliff line, the thread of irrigation ditch running silver through the middle distance, and understood that he wasn't looking at a hideout. He was looking at a town. A real one, with a history long enough to have children born in it who'd never seen a government stamp on anything.

They fed him mutton stew and flatbread and a tea brewed from something piney, and nobody asked him a single question, which unsettled him more than interrogation would have. In Harrow Bend, every question came with a price tag attached. Here, questions seemed to be considered rude—not because answers were dangerous, but because a man's business was his own until he decided otherwise. It was, he thought, the first place he'd ever been where his silence was treated as a courtesy instead of a suspicion.

* * *

Amara came at dusk.

He heard her before he saw her—the particular hush that falls over a room when someone important enters without announcing herself—and turned to find a small woman in the doorway, sixty or older, silver-braided hair, hands folded in front of her like a schoolteacher's. Her eyes did the opposite of what her posture promised. They went over him the way he went over sign: boots first, then hands, then the set of the shoulders, then the eyes themselves. Reading him. Cataloguing threat.

"You're the ranger," she said.

"Lena Delacroix."

"I know your name. It arrived ahead of you, the way names do." She crossed the room and sat in the chair across from him without asking permission, which told him something about how permission worked here. "Sera argued for you. Doss wanted you dead. The council split down the middle and gave you to me, which means either they trust my judgment or they didn't want the responsibility. Possibly both."

"And your judgment is?"

"That you walked past three snares you could have sprung and disarmed a fourth rather than leave it killing some animal. Sera told us. She watches how people move through country. It tells her more than their mouths do." Amara leaned back. "So. You found us. That's a problem. What happens next depends on things you don't know yet, so I'm going to tell you some of them, and then we'll see what kind of man walks out of this room."

Lena set down the tea. "I'm listening."

"Good. Rangers usually are. It's the talking afterward that worries me."

She talked for a long time, and he let her, because everything she said rearranged the picture he'd been building since the empty survey camp, and he needed the new picture whole before he trusted himself to speak.

There were forty-one people in the valley. Forty-three counting the two babies born last spring. Some had come twenty years ago, some five, the newest arrivals a family of three who'd walked in over the southern saddle eighteen months earlier after the bank took their farm in a foreclosure so crooked even the county clerk had apologized while processing it. A deserter from the territorial militia who'd refused to burn a squatter camp and run rather than do it. Two brothers who'd jumped a mining claim dispute that was going to end with one of them hanged on thin evidence. A widow whose husband's debts had been inherited, by law, by her, and who'd chosen the mountains over the debtors' colony. Families fleeing land seizures up and down the eastern valleys, wherever the big companies decided a holding was worth more than the people on it.

"No papers," Amara said. "That's the word we use. People without papers. Not criminals—most of us never broke a law a decent person would recognize. We just stopped existing on paper. And once you stop existing on paper, you discover something interesting." She held up one finger. "The government cannot persecute what it cannot see. But—" a second finger, "—it also cannot protect what it cannot see. We chose invisibility knowing both halves of the bargain. Twenty years we've kept our side of it. We farm. We raise children. We bury our dead under cairns with no names carved, because a name carved in stone is a document, and documents get read."

"The survey team," Lena said.

Her face changed. It was small, the change—a tightening around the mouth, a stillness in the hands—but he'd spent two decades reading faces in country where reading them wrong got you killed, and he saw grief there, and under the grief something harder.

"Yes," she said. "The survey team."

"They came armed. Eviction orders. I've seen the quadrant maps—they marked this valley red."

"They came in the first week of autumn. Six men. A Mr. Halloran leading them, a junior clerk, four hired chainmen. They came down the same draw you came down, and they stood where you're standing now, and Halloran read out a notice to the whole valley assembled—every soul of us standing right here in this room and the yard outside—as if we were a fence line to be disputed." Amara's voice stayed level, but Lena heard the effort of the levelness, the way you hear wind working against a door. "He informed us that this valley had been assessed, that its water rights had been allocated to the Meridian Consolidated Water and Power Company, and that we had thirty days to vacate. All forty-one of us. Every terrace, every house, every grave."

"Where would you go?"

"Nowhere. That was the point. There is nowhere for people without papers to go. That's why we're here." She looked at her hands. "We sent elders to talk. I went myself. Halloran was polite the way ice is polite. He explained that the company had paid for assessments and assessments don't lie, and that if we resisted, well, the territory had ways of resolving occupation disputes. He used the word *squatters*. After twenty years. As if we were drifters sleeping in a culvert."

"What happened?"

"That," Amara said, "is a longer story, and part of it isn't mine to tell. But you should know this much tonight: three of those six men are dead, and the other three ran, and none of them reported what they found here, and the reason for all of that is sitting somewhere in this valley refusing to eat until she decides whether to trust you."

"Sera."

"Sera." Amara studied him. "You've been tracking her since summer. Don't look surprised—she recognized your sign weeks ago. A tracker who disarms snares leaves a signature. She said you hunt like a man looking for a conversation, not a capture. She wanted to know if that was true before you got here. Now she can find out herself."

Lena sat with that. Outside, the light was failing behind the western rim, and somewhere a child was laughing, and the smell of bread came off the evening fires, and it struck him with physical force—like a hand flat against the chest—that this was a community. Not a camp, not a hideout. A town that had never existed and therefore could never be destroyed on paper, except that now, apparently, it could be destroyed in every other way.

"Why tell me any of this?" he asked. "You could keep me ignorant. Ignorant men are safer to release."

"Because ignorance is only safe until it isn't, and you're the kind of man who doesn't stop walking until ignorance runs out." Amara rose, smoothing her skirt, suddenly every inch the elder again. "Also because I'm old enough to have learned that you can't choose what a man does with the truth. You can only choose whether you insult him by withholding it. I won't insult you, Ranger. Whatever else you are, you earned that much on the mountain."

"One more question."

"If it's quick."

"The government thinks this valley is worthless. That's why you survived. Dry, remote, unsurveyed—worthless on paper." He nodded toward the window, the terraces, the silver ditch. "But it isn't worthless. There's water under it. Good water—I found the seep, I've seen the aquifer sign in the rock. So the survey wasn't a mistake. Somebody knew."

For the first time, Amara smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because there was no comfort in it at all.

"Now you're asking the right question," she said. "Yes. Somebody knew. Somebody has known for years, and the survey wasn't discovery—it was confirmation. Paperwork for a theft already planned. And the man who signed the assessment reports, the man whose name is on the eviction order Halloran read to us like a death sentence—" she paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the firelight of the passage beyond, "—you'll want to sit down for that name, Ranger. Because I'd wager everything in this valley you already know it. I'd wager it's the same name that's been following you for twenty years, the way yours has been following you."

She left him alone with the fire and the sound of children being called in to supper, and Lena Delacroix sat very still in a house full of people without papers, feeling—for the first time in twenty years—like a man with somewhere to belong and no idea whether he'd live long enough to deserve it.

In the morning, he decided, he would find Sera. And whatever she carried in that satchel she guarded like a child, he would ask to see it.

Because Amara hadn't given him the name yet. But she'd been right about the wager. He could feel it coming like weather over the rim—and he already knew whose name it would be.
