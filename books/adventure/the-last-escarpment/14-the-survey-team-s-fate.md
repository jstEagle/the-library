## 14. The Survey Team's Fate

Amara had buried enough people to know that the dead do not tell their stories. The living have to, and the living are cowards about it.

She sat in the long house with the fire banked low, the way she liked it—enough heat to hold a room, not enough to make anyone comfortable enough to lie. Lena Delacroix sat across from her on the stone bench where they seated strangers and suspects alike, though she was no longer sure which he was. Sera stood by the door, arms crossed over the satchel she still would not set down, watching Amara the way a hawk watches a snare line.

"You want to know what happened to them," Amara said. "The survey men."

"I want to know what I'm standing in," Lena said. "I've spent twenty years reading country. I can read a room too."

"Then read this one." She let the silence sit. Outside, the wind worked at the eaves of the long house, worrying the cedar shakes the way it did every autumn, and someone's child laughed down near the granary, quick and bright and gone. "Six men came up the draw on the ninth of October. Two years ago now. You'll want the date because you're a man who keeps dates like other men keep debts."

Lena said nothing. Good. She'd known rangers who talked too much and trackers who talked too little, and the ones who talked too little were the ones you could tell things to.

"They came in the rain season's first storm," she went on. "Wet through, half-lost, following a map that didn't show the valley because no map shows the valley. We found them at the mouth of the south draw, huddled under an overhang, arguing about whether they'd crossed into some kind of government reserve. One of them thought we might be a survey camp ourselves." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Imagine that. Six men looking for a valley full of nothing, finding forty people who weren't there."

"And you took them in."

"We fed them. It was raining like the end of the world and they were shivering like wet dogs, and whatever else we are, we don't leave men to die of cold when carrying them inside costs us nothing." She looked at the fire. "That was the mistake. Or the test. Depends which day you ask me."

* * *

She told it the way she always told it, beginning three days before the storm, because the truth of any death starts earlier than the dying.

The first man to notice anything wrong was Toma, her sister's boy, who ran the lookout on the eastern rim. He came down at dusk saying there were riders on the flats below the pass—not coming toward the valley, but circling, taking measurements, driving stakes with little red flags. Toma had watched them for two hours through the spyglass. He counted six. He counted instruments on tripods. And he counted, twice, a leather document case that one of them kept unlocking to consult, and locking again, careful as a man handling money.

"We knew what a survey meant," Amara said. "Everyone here knows what a survey means. A survey is how the government learns your name before it takes your land. My grandmother saw it done to her people. My mother saw it done to the dryland farmers east of here. A man with a theodolite is more dangerous than a man with a gun, because the man with the gun only kills you once."

"So you went up to talk to them."

"I went up to watch them," Amara corrected. "Talking is what you do after you know what a man wants. I sent two of our best—quiet people, people who could cross open ground without raising dust—and they got close enough to hear. What they heard was Hargrove."

"Hargrove?"

"The team lead. Willem Hargrove, senior surveyor, twenty-two years with the Department. A precise man. A tidy man. Our listeners heard him telling his crew that the quadrant was confirmed, that the aquifer readings exceeded projections, and that the eviction schedules would proceed as planned come spring." She watched Lena's face as she said it. She had watched many faces while telling this story—the council's, the young ones', Sera's—and she had learned that the story landed differently depending on what a man had already lost. "Eviction schedules. For land those men believed empty. They had orders in that document case signed by a land office in Harrow Bend, declaring the entire escarpment interior unclaimed waste ground, suitable for appropriation under the water reclamation act. There were names on the schedule. Blank ones. To be filled in later, when the population was determined."

Lena's jaw tightened. "There is no population determination. There's never been a census out here."

"No," Amara agreed. "There hasn't. That was rather the point of us."

* * *

The storm came in on the third night, early and hard, the kind of storm that makes its own decisions. And it was in that storm that everything went wrong, and Amara had learned to be honest about the shape of the going-wrong, because a community that lies to itself about its dead will rot from the inside.

"The surveyors broke camp when the storm hit," she said. "They should have stayed put—any mountain man knows you sit out a blow—but Hargrove wanted to reach the shelter of the south rim before dark, and his junior clerk argued with him. A girl. Young. We learned her name later." She glanced toward the door, and Sera's chin lifted slightly, but she said nothing, so Amara continued. "Hargrove overruled her. They moved in the whiteout, lost the trail, and came down the wrong drainage—straight toward the valley floor, straight toward the terraces, where thirty of our people were working in the storm trying to save the late plantings from the washout."

"What happened on the terraces?"

Amara took a breath. This was the part she never shortened, because shortening it was how lies started.

"Our people saw armed men coming out of the fog. Armed—that matters. The surveyors carried rifles because of the cats and the bears, but our people couldn't know that. What they knew was: strangers with guns, descending on the valley, during the same season the council had been arguing about whether the outside world had finally found us. And one of ours—a man named Corren, who had run from soldiers once, years back, and never entirely stopped running—panicked. He fired a warning shot. In the storm, in the fog, nobody heard it as a warning."

Lena had gone very still. Trackers went still like that, she'd noticed, when the trail turned bad.

"Hargrove's men returned fire," she said. "Blind fire, into fog, at shapes. Corren was hit—grazed, he lived. But our people on the terraces scattered, and in the scattering, three of the surveyors went off the edge of the upper flume path. The old flood channel. It was running full with storm water. The drop isn't far but the water goes under the rock shelf at the bottom, and—" She spread her hands. "The mountain took them. Not us. But also, if you press me, us. Because we were the shapes in the fog. Because we built the terraces they fell from. Because Corren fired first, or thought he did, or needed to have fired first. Every version of that night is true somewhere."

"And the other three?"

"Ran. Hargrove and two others made it back up the drainage in the storm and kept going. We tracked them to the pass before the snow closed it. They didn't stop at Harrow Bend—they passed through, according to what we heard later, without reporting. Hargrove filed nothing. Filed no report, no claim, no request for a search party for his own missing men. Do you understand what that means, ranger? A twenty-two-year man left three of his crew unaccounted for in the high country and told no one. There's only one reason a man does that."

Lena spoke quietly. "Because reporting means explaining what his crew was doing up there. And explaining means producing the eviction orders. And the orders mean the valley exists."

"And the valley existing means the money behind the survey has a problem," Amara finished. "So Hargrove chose to bury his own dead men in silence. Chose it over their families, over their names, over whatever small decency might have driven him to say: there are people in that valley, and we were sent to erase them, and three of mine died doing it. He went home and became a man who had never been to the escarpment. And the department marked his whole team missing, presumed lost to weather, and paid out the widows' claims, and closed the file." She poked the fire, sending up a knot of sparks. "We know because Sera was there. Sera kept the file. Sera kept everything."

* * *

The fire settled. Somewhere in the village a dog barked twice and quit. Lena hadn't moved, but something in him had shifted—she could see it in the set of his shoulders, the way a man sits differently when a story stops being about strangers.

"You blame yourselves," he said.

"I blame accurately," Amara said. "That's different and harder. Listen to me, ranger, because this is the part that matters, the part your courts will never have patience for. Those six men were not villains. Hargrove was a company instrument, yes—he knew what the survey was for, and he took the money, and when it cost lives he chose his pension over the truth. I'll call him what he is. But the others? The two who died in the flume—one of them, Ostrander, had spent the evening before the storm teaching Toma's kids card games and asking honest questions about how we grew greens at this altitude. He told Sera, quietly, that the aquifer numbers felt wrong to him, too good, like the survey had been told its answer before it asked its question. He wasn't a monster. He was a man with a job and a family in some flat green place, doing harm he probably never fully understood, the way most harm gets done." 

"And the third?"

"Died in the water with the other two. Pell. Youngest of them. Twenty-six." She said the number and let it stand. "I keep their names. Not out of guilt—out of arithmetic. If we ever stand in front of judges and magistrates and men with warrants, someone will say: these valley people murdered a government survey team. And the answer cannot be 'no we didn't.' The answer has to be the whole night, every piece of it, the fear and the fog and the firing and the fall. The truth doesn't need us to be innocent. It needs us to be exact."

Lena was quiet a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice had the gravel of a man choosing words carefully. "The survivors. Hargrove and the two who fled. Are they alive?"

"Two are. We've had whispers over the years—a man answering to Hargrove's description working a rail office two territories east, keeping his head down. The third, a man named Voss, dropped out of sight entirely." She paused. "Until recently. Sera believes one of them may be moving again. Looking over his shoulder. Men who bury things sometimes go back to check the grave."

"Why would he come back now?"

"Because the company is coming back," Amara said simply. "You found the fresh stakes yourself, or you wouldn't be sitting in my house. The water reclamation act didn't die with that survey—it waited. And whoever is funding this new push knows exactly what happened to the last team, which means they know there are witnesses. Living ones. Hargrove, if he's alive, is either a witness against them or a loose end. Loose ends get tidied, ranger. You of all people should know how that work is done."

Something moved behind Lena's eyes—old knowledge, old scar tissue—and Amara recognized it for what it was, because she had seen that look on fugitives for thirty years. He wasn't hearing a story about surveyors anymore. He was hearing his own biography told back to him in other men's names.

* * *

Sera spoke from the doorway for the first time. "Hargrove's signature is on the falsified quadrant reports. All of them. I watched him initial pages he knew were wrong—I brought it to him, and he told me to initial them myself or find another posting." Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the satchel strap. "He's not just a witness. He's evidence."

"He's also a man who let three of his crew die unnamed," Amara said. "Don't romanticize him, girl. Fear makes men do small cowardly things for years. That's not innocence. It's just the ordinary size of most men's courage."

Lena leaned forward, elbows on knees, and Amara saw the tracker surface fully now—the man who measured the world in sign and distance, assembling a picture out of pieces. "Three dead in the flume. Three fled. One junior clerk who stole the originals and ran." He glanced at Sera. "That's seven. The team was six."

"Sera wasn't counted in the official roster," Amara said. "A clerk, a woman, hired seasonal, paid out of a discretionary fund. On paper, that team never had her. Which means when the file closed—six missing, presumed dead—she was already invisible. The company erased her before she ever stole anything." She smiled thinly. "Though I suppose she repaid the favor."

"I didn't steal anything," Sera said. "I rescued it."

"There," Amara said softly. "That sentence. That's the whole war, right there. What they call theft, we call rescue. What they call a worthless wasteland, we call home. Every word in this fight depends on whose mouth it comes out of." She rose, joints cracking, and went to the window slit where the last grey light was failing over the western rim. "You came here hunting a vanished survey team, ranger. Now you've found them—or their ends. Three dead by accident and fear. One fled into silence. One maybe hiding, maybe hunted. And one girl with a satchel full of paper that nine people in this valley have already voted on whether to burn."

"And you?" Lena asked. "Where do you stand?"

Amara watched the dark come down over the escarpment, over the terraces and the flume path and the unmarked graves of three men who had come to erase a valley and been erased instead by a mountain that didn't care whose side it was on.

"I stand with exact," she said. "And exact says the passes close soon, and the company's new survey won't wait for spring the way the last one did. Something has changed. Someone is pushing harder this time." She turned back to face him. "Which brings me to the question I've been saving, ranger, the one I've been circling all evening. Your telegram in Harrow Bend. Who hired you to find these men—and why did they pay you too well to find men everyone official already believes are dead?"

Lena's face went carefully blank—the blankness of a man who has just realized he walked into a valley carrying someone else's questions in his pocket.

"That," he said slowly, "is a very good question. And I'm starting to think I was never supposed to be the one asking it."
