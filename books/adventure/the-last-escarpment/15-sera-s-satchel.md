## 15. Sera's Satchel

The satchel weighed four pounds and eleven ounces. Sera knew because she had weighed it on the survey team's brass scale the night before she ran, when everyone else was asleep and the wind was doing its work on the tent canvas, and she had needed to know exactly what she was carrying out of that camp. Four pounds and eleven ounces of paper. Nine years of her life fit in a smaller weight than a rifle.

She had thought about it every day since. Not the papers themselves—she knew those by heart now, had read them so many times she could recite the quadrant numbers in her sleep—but the weight. The way a thing could be so light in your hands and so heavy everywhere else.

The slot canyon narrowed ahead of her and she stopped, as she always stopped, and listened first. Wind through stone made a sound like breathing. Water somewhere below the floor of the world, moving through cracks no one had ever mapped, except now someone had, and that was the whole reason she was here.

* * *

Her name was Sera Voss then, junior clerk, third assistant to the assistant surveyor, which meant she kept the field books in order and transcribed the instrument readings and fetched coffee at six in the morning from a pot that always tasted like tin. She was twenty-four years old and she had taken the job because it came with a government stipend and a promise of a permanent posting, and because her mother had died in the spring and left her nothing but a trunk of clothes and a hunger to be somewhere the grief couldn't find her.

The survey team was nine men and herself. They were mapping the eastern escarpment country—the high range nobody wanted, the broken terrain above the flats where the maps still said *unexplored* in letters that hadn't been updated in forty years. Officially they were doing triangulation work for a boundary revision. Unofficially, Sera understood within the first month, they were looking for water.

She hadn't minded. Water was interesting. She'd grown up in irrigation country and she knew what water meant to land, how a section with a spring on it was worth ten sections without. She transcribed the readings faithfully: barometric pressures, flow estimates at the seeps, the depth soundings where the geologist lowered his instruments into fissures and pulled up numbers that made him whistle under his breath.

It was the numbers that started it. That was the thing she would tell later, if she ever got the chance to tell anyone—that there was no single moment of discovery, no dramatic midnight scene with a lantern. There was just arithmetic that didn't add up.

In October the reports began going back to Harrow Bend, and Sera copied them fair for the file, and the numbers in the fair copies did not match the numbers in the field books.

Not by much. A seep measured at forty gallons an hour became twelve. A fracture zone showing artesian pressure became dry rock. The big one—the reading the geologist had whistled at, the deep aquifer under the escarpment's spine, enough water to green a county—became, in the report, *no significant groundwater indicated.*

Sera sat with the two sets of figures for three days before she let herself believe it. She checked her own transcription twice, assuming she'd made the error. She hadn't. The falsification went upward from her desk. It went upward all the way to the chief surveyor, a gray careful man named Aldous Renk who signed everything in violet ink and never raised his voice, and who had looked at her exactly once, early on, and said: *You have neat handwriting, Miss Voss. Neat handwriting is a service to this expedition.*

Neat handwriting is a service. She heard it in her head for weeks afterward, hearing finally what it meant: your job is to make the lie legible.

* * *

She should have gone to Renk. That was the procedure—discrepancies went up the chain, and the chain decided. But she had watched the chain work already, in small ways. A teamster who complained about pay got reassigned to worse duty. The cook who asked too many questions about their supply requisitions found his contract not renewed at the half-year mark. The chain didn't decide things; the chain *conveyed* decisions, and the decisions came from somewhere outside the camp, from Harrow Bend, from further than Harrow Bend, from men whose names appeared nowhere in any document Sera handled and therefore, she understood, were the only names that mattered.

So she did what a clerk does. She watched. She learned the shape of the operation the way she'd learned the shape of the terrain—from the paperwork.

The requisitions told her most of it. They were buying fence wire. Thousands of feet of it, coded as *survey line markers.* They were buying deeds-of-access forms, blank ones, pre-signed by a land office she traced through its filing number to a holding company called Meridian Consolidated. And in November a rider came up from Harrow Bend with a sealed order that Renk read alone in his tent and burned in the stove, and after that day the team's schedule changed—they stopped surveying the northern quadrants entirely and concentrated on the escarpment's hidden interior, the folded country behind the wall of the range, where the maps showed nothing at all.

Sera had grown up on stories about that country. Everyone on the flats had. Runaways went up there. Debt-fugitives, deserters, families the banks had stripped. Her own grandfather had claimed to have traded once, decades ago, with people who lived *behind the rocks,* though he'd been a liar about most things and she'd filed the story away with his other ones. But the government didn't send survey teams into places that held nothing, and the government especially didn't falsify water readings to make a place look worthless unless—

Unless the place wasn't worthless. Unless it was worth hiding.

She lay awake in her cot the night that understanding arrived, listening to the wind work the canvas, and felt the ground tilt under her. Because there was another way to read the falsified reports, and once she saw it she couldn't unsee it. You didn't just erase water from a map to keep it secret. You erased it to clear the way. A quadrant marked *no significant groundwater* was a quadrant no one would contest. No ranchers filing claims. No towns petitioning. No courts. Just a land office transferring title quietly to Meridian Consolidated, and fences going up across country that already belonged—to whoever lived back there, and to the water itself, which belonged to no one, or to everyone, depending on which law you believed in, and Sera was learning that the difference between those two laws was the whole history of the territory.

* * *

What she took, in the end, was everything original. The field books—all seven of them, the raw instrument readings in the geologist's hand. The seep logs. The flow measurements. Two pages of Renk's own working notes, which he'd left in the file box one careless evening, comparing the true aquifer figures against projected yield tables for an irrigation concern, with marginal calculations in violet ink that read like a man counting a harvest before the planting. And the master quadrant map, the one with the red ink, the one that showed the aquifer's extent drawn in confident strokes beneath a stamp that said *NO SIGNIFICANT GROUNDWATER INDICATED — FOR FILE ONLY.*

Four pounds and eleven ounces. She wrapped each item in oilcloth, sewed them into a leather satchel she'd bought in Harrow Bend for carrying instruments, and wore it against her body for two weeks before she ran, sleeping with it, eating with it, letting it become part of her silhouette so that when she finally walked out of camp no one would register anything unusual about the shape of her.

She told herself she'd decide later what to do with it. Take it to a newspaper, maybe. To a federal marshal. To anyone with more power than a twenty-four-year-old clerk with neat handwriting. But she knew, even then, in the honest part of herself she tried not to visit at night, that she was afraid of exactly that. Papers could be dismissed. A clerk's word could be buried under a company's lawyers the way the aquifer was being buried under false readings. She had seen what these men did to numbers. What would they do to her?

So she ran first and planned second, which was backwards, and she knew it was backwards, and she ran anyway.

* * *

They discovered the theft on a Tuesday morning and she was thirty miles into the badlands by Tuesday night, moving only at dusk and dawn, lying up through the heat in the shade of overhangs, following the old trick of walking in each other's footsteps—except she had no other, so she walked in washes and on bare stone and let the wind do the erasing that she couldn't.

Renk sent riders after her. She counted three separate parties over the first week, and she understood something in watching them that hardened her permanently: they weren't chasing a thief. Thieves get pursued for a few days and written off. These men rode like the papers were worth killing for, and after the second week they stopped riding like searchers and started riding like hunters, cutting sign methodically, working the water sources, doing everything right—and that frightened her more than the guns, because it meant orders had come down from somewhere, and the orders were thorough.

She survived the first month on nerve and luck and the last of her rations, and then she nearly died of thirst crossing a stretch of alkali flat, and was saved—or condemned, she still wasn't sure which—by finding a seep that wasn't on any map. It was tucked under a shelf of limestone, barely a wet thread in the rock, and someone had been there before her. There was a stone basin built to catch the drip. A tin cup hanging on a peg. A stick of kindling wrapped in oilcloth under a cairn, dry despite everything, waiting.

Nobody leaves a cup for strangers in empty country. Nobody leaves kindling.

She drank, filled her skins, took nothing else, and sat for a long time with her back against the warm stone, looking at the little basin, and understood that she had crossed some border that wasn't on any map either. The country behind the rocks was real. The people her grandfather lied about were real. And they had left a cup out for whoever came next, which was a kind of law, maybe the oldest one, and it was operating out here in the dark while the lit world down on the flats ran on violet ink and falsified quadrants.

She left the kindling. She left the cup. She left, scratched inside the cairn's base stone where rain wouldn't reach, a mark she invented—a small spiral, meaning nothing to anyone but her, meaning *I was here and I am not your enemy.* Then she moved on, deeper into the high country, toward the wall of the escarpment itself, because if there were people behind the rocks, they were the only people in the territory who might have a reason to help her that was stronger than their fear.

* * *

Winter caught her in the middle ranges, earlier than it should have come, and she sheltered three days in a cave with smoke stains older than she was, and ate the last of her food, and talked to the satchel because there was no one else to talk to. Not aloud—she'd lost the habit of her own voice, went days without using it—but in her head, a running argument.

*Nobody will believe you.* / *Then I'll find somebody who will.* / *You're a thief. In their eyes you'll always be the thief.* / *Then I'll be the witness too. Both. Whatever it takes.* / *You're going to die up here and the papers will rot in a cave and the valley—if there is a valley—will get fenced in spring, and it will all have been for nothing.* / *Then die moving.*

She was still losing the argument when she heard the bell.

A goat bell, far off, wrong for the country—there was no open range up here, no herders licensed anywhere on the high slopes. She went toward it the way thirst goes toward water, without deciding to, and found a terrace cut into the mountainside with winter greens growing under glass cloches, and beyond it, through fog, the smell of woodsmoke, and below that—

Below that, the fog opened for ten seconds, the way fog does, and she saw the valley.

Terraced gardens falling away in steps. Stone houses with slate roofs. A mill race. Smoke rising from fifty chimneys into the white afternoon. Children's voices, thin with distance, doing something ordinary—counting, arguing, singing something with a chorus. An entire town living inside a place that every map in the territory called nothing at all.

She stood in the fog with the satchel pressed against her ribs and cried for the first time since her mother's funeral, silently, uselessly, the tears freezing at her jaw. Because it was beautiful, and because it was exactly what the red-inked map was drawn to destroy, and because she had carried four pounds and eleven ounces of paper through hell to reach a place that might shoot her on sight.

* * *

They found her the next morning, or rather she woke to find them already there—two figures at the mouth of her shelter, wrapped in grey wool, standing with the patience of people who had done this many times. They took her knife and her rifle without hostility, the way you'd take matches from a child near a fire, and they looked at the satchel straps across her chest and did not take that, and something in the care of that omission told her they already knew what she was carrying, or at least what she was.

One of them spoke. "You've been followed."

"I know."

"Three riders, working the lower drainage. They turned back yesterday." A pause. "You're the one from the survey camp."

It wasn't a question, and the fact that it wasn't a question went through her like cold water. They had known. They had been watching her for days, maybe longer, letting her come, deciding.

"The papers are real," she said. Her voice cracked from disuse. "The originals. Everything they falsified. I can prove what they're doing."

The two scouts exchanged a look that contained an entire committee meeting.

"That's not yours to prove," the first one said—not cruelly, just factually, the way you'd tell someone the weather. "That's ours to survive. Come on. Amara will want to hear it, and then the council will argue until spring about what to do with you."

"With me," Sera said, "or with the satchel?"

The scout almost smiled. Almost.

"Girl," he said, turning downhill into the fog, "around here we've learned it's the same question."

She put her hand flat against the oilcloth packets, feeling the edges of the field books through the layers, the raw numbers sleeping in their stitching, and followed him down toward the smoke.
