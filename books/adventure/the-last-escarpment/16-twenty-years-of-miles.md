## 16. Twenty Years of Miles

The fire in Amara's longhouse was built the way everything in the valley was built—carefully, with an eye toward what it would need to be tomorrow. Green aspen on the bottom to hold coals, dry pine stacked above it, a flat stone set near the edge where a kettle could sit without tipping. Nothing wasted. Nothing accidental. Lena had watched her build it that evening without seeming to think about it, and he understood that he was looking at twenty years of practice compressed into ten minutes of ordinary work.

The longhouse was the largest structure in the valley, which meant it was still smaller than the bunkhouse at the Harrow Bend depot. Stone footings, timber frame, a roof of split cedar shakes weighted down with more stone. Benches along the walls. A loom in one corner with half a blanket on it, indigo and rust. Children's chalk marks on one floorboard, scrubbed but not quite gone.

Eleven people slept here. He'd counted the bedrolls.

Amara sent the others out after supper—not cruelly, just firmly, the way she did everything. The last of them was Sera, who paused at the door with her satchel strap in her fist and looked at him like she wanted to say something, then didn't, and went out into the dark.

Now it was just the two of them and the fire, and the wind working at the eaves, and the particular silence of a valley that had learned to listen for footsteps.

Amara poured something from a clay jug into two cups. Not whiskey. Something darker, thicker, made from berries he didn't recognize and probably shouldn't have asked about.

"Drink," she said. "It'll warm you twice."

He drank. It did.

She settled onto the bench across from him, cup in both hands, and looked at him over the rim with those gray eyes that missed nothing. She'd been doing that all week—looking at him the way a surveyor looks at terrain, measuring, noting the places where the ground might give way.

"You've been here nine days," she said. "You've fixed our gate hinge, re-shod two horses, taught Doss how to read sign in wet sand, and repaired the flume above the terraces without being asked." She took a sip. "You've also told us nothing about yourself except your name, and I'm not entirely sure that's your name."

"It's my name."

"Your *whole* name?"

"Lena Delacroix." He turned the cup in his hands. "My mother was French-Canadian. My father worked the rail lines. She named me after her brother, who died at Wounded Knee before I was born." A pause. "So yes. It's my whole name."

"And the rest? Twenty years of rest?"

He looked at the fire. Outside, somewhere up on the rimrock, a coyote started up and was answered by another, lower, closer to the valley floor. The sound moved through the stone houses and the terraces and the dark rows of winter wheat like water through a channel.

"You know what I was," he said. "Sera told you. A ranger."

"I know what you were. I don't know why you stopped being it."

"That's a long story."

"I'm seventy-one years old," Amara said. "I buried a husband and two sons. I've got time."

* * *

He told it the way he'd never told it, because he'd never had anyone to tell it to and no reason to trust anyone enough to try. Slowly at first, in pieces, testing the weight of each word. Then faster, because once the first stones were moving the whole slide came down at once.

"I grew up on water projects," he said. "My father laid pipe for the irrigation companies. I learned surveying from a man named Ochsner who'd worked the big dams on the Colorado. By the time I was twenty-five I could read a watershed the way a priest reads scripture. That's not a boast. It's just what I was for."

"You were good at it."

"I was *useful* at it. There's a difference, and the difference is what ruined me." He drank again. The berry stuff had a bite underneath the sweetness, like a warning. "There was a company then—the Meridian Water and Power Company. You'll not have heard of them out here, but they owned half the filings in three territories. Reservoirs, canals, diversion rights. They were building a dam on the Aldous Fork, up north, and they needed a man to certify the site. The geology was wrong. Everyone knew the geology was wrong. Ochsner himself had said so, years before, in a report nobody published."

"What was wrong with it?"

"The substrate. Water-saturated bentonite under the abutment. Clay that swells when it wets and slides when it swells. You can build on it if you know what you're doing—you drain it, you key into bedrock, you spend the money. But spending money wasn't the Meridian way. So they hired a young ranger with a clean record and a reputation for precision, and they paid him to say the site was sound."

Amara didn't move. "And you said it."

"I refused." The word came out harder than he expected, twenty years old and still sharp. "I wrote a report saying the site was unsound. I filed it. I thought that was the end of it—that I'd done my job, that the paper trail would protect everyone, including me." He laughed, short and ugly. "I was twenty-six. I believed in paper."

"And then?"

"And then the dam failed."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"Not right away," he said. "They finished it. They filled it. They operated it for fourteen months with my report sitting in a drawer in the district office—and then on the fourteenth of March, at two in the morning, the west abutment let go, and forty million gallons came down the Aldous Fork at forty miles an hour, and it took out the town of Cedar Hollow."

He'd never said the town's name aloud in twenty years. Saying it now felt like setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Nine dead," he said. "The Kettering family, all five. Old Tom Vasquez, who ran the ferry crossing. A schoolteacher named Ruth Ellery. Two brothers, the Ashfords, who'd stayed behind to move their stock and waited too long." His voice didn't break. He'd practiced this silence too long for it to break. "Ruth Ellery was thirty-one. She used to bring her students down to the depot to watch the trains. I knew her face. I couldn't tell you her voice anymore, and that's the part that shames me most—not the dying, but the forgetting."

"You didn't kill them."

"The state of—" He stopped. Started again. "The territory prosecuted a negligence charge. Criminal negligence, contributing to the failure of a certified structure. And the man they prosecuted was me. Not the engineers who overrode the geology. Not the directors who ordered the fill schedule accelerated. Not the company that owned every judge in the district. Me—the junior ranger whose report had said *don't*. Because my signature was on the certification. They'd taken my refusal report, altered it, and filed the altered version. My words, changed, certifying the site as sound. My handwriting forged on the amendment page. And when I produced the original, they said the original was the forgery."

Amara set her cup down slowly. "Who judged it?"

"A judge named Corliss. Later indicted—much later, after I was already gone—for taking Meridian money. Too late to matter. A prosecutor named Hask, who went on to be general counsel for Meridian itself. You see the shape of it."

"I see the shape of it."

"They took my license, my commission, my name. I wasn't sentenced to prison—the scandal was getting attention by then, and a trial with testimony might have unraveled things, so they gave me a suspended sentence and permanent disbarment from public service instead. Which sounds merciful." He looked at her. "It was designed to make me disappear. A man with a struck record can't testify, can't file, can't be heard. They didn't want me hanged. They wanted me *unreliable*. And then they wanted me gone, because as long as I was alive and anywhere nearby, I was a loose thread."

"So you went."

"So I went." He stared into the fire. "First south, then west, then here. I learned that the escarpment country doesn't ask questions. I learned to measure my life in miles instead of years, because miles don't accuse you. Twenty years of patrol routes. Twenty years of knowing every seep and slot canyon and winter camp between here and the Salt Fork, and never staying anywhere longer than a season." He shook his head. "I told myself I was free. What I actually was, was buried. Same as my report. Same as Ruth Ellery's name in the company ledger, filed under *losses, non-operational*."

The wind rose against the north wall and fell away again. Amara refilled his cup without asking.

"Sera's documents," she said. "The falsified quadrant reports. The aquifer surveys marked worthless. The land-grab filings."

"What about them?"

"Same hand?"

He was quiet for a moment. He'd been turning that question over since the night she'd spread the papers on the table in the council house, and he hadn't let himself finish turning it, because finishing it meant something.

"Meridian collapsed in 'ninety-one," he said. "Reorganized. New charter, new directors, new name—Consolidated Basin Development, or CBD, or whatever they're calling themselves this decade. But reorganization isn't repentance. The same families. The same attorneys. Hask's protégés running their legal shop. And the man in Harrow Bend who signed my contract—" he'd checked the letterhead, the first night, by firelight, unable to stop himself— "is a former Meridian land agent named Greer. He was a junior clerk in the claims office the year they buried me. I recognized the name. I've been carrying it around for twenty years like a stone in my boot, and I only figured out tonight, saying it out loud, that I never once asked myself why a man like that would pay triple rate for a search contract he didn't expect to survive scrutiny."

Amara watched him across the fire. "Say it plainly."

"They're not hunting a missing survey team," Lena said. "They're hunting witnesses. The team saw the aquifer. The reports were falsified to hide it. Sera stole the originals. And the last loose thread from the reservoir—the man whose forged signature started all of it—is standing in their valley drinking berry wine." He set the cup down. "I'm not the hunter anymore, Amara. I'm the other thing they're erasing. I always was."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight moved on her face like water.

"When my sons left," she said, "the oldest one told me the world outside was a machine for grinding up people like us. People without papers, without filings, without the right kind of names. He said, *Mama, we can't win, we can only not be found.* And for thirty years that's been the law of this valley. Not be found. Stay small. Stay worthless on paper." She looked at him. "But you're telling me the paper was never honest to begin with. That the men who write it will forge it, bury it, burn it, and call the ash truth."

"I'm telling you I watched them do it."

"Then hiding isn't safety. It's just a slower version of the same grave." She sat back. "That boy of yours—Doss—he says you tracked the thief across the flats alone, in the dry season, off sign a magpie couldn't have followed. He says you read snares like scripture. He says the mountain nearly killed you and you walked out anyway." Her eyes glinted. "Is any of that true?"

"Some of it."

"Which parts aren't?"

"The flattering ones are true. The modest ones I invented."

Something moved across her face that on a younger woman would have been a laugh. "Twenty years of miles," she said quietly. "And every mile of it walking away from a piece of paper with your name forged on it." She stood, joints cracking, and gathered the cups. "You sleep here tonight. The longhouse is warm and I'm an old woman who likes company, even the silent kind."

At the doorway, with the jug in hand, she paused.

"The council meets day after tomorrow," she said. "About the documents. Whether they go out or burn. You'll be there."

"I'm not valley folk."

"No," she agreed. "You're something rarer. You're a man who knows exactly what the outside world does to people like us, because it already did it to you." She looked back at him, gray eyes steady in the dark. "And day after tomorrow, we're going to need a man who knows how the erasing gets done—so we can decide, together, whether to let it happen one more time."

She went out. The door closed. The fire settled, and Lena sat alone with the heat and the coyote-song fading on the rimrock, and for the first time in twenty years he did not reach for the go-bag he kept packed beneath every bed he'd ever slept in.

It was still packed. But he didn't reach for it.

Outside, far off toward the pass, thunder rolled in a sky with no clouds—a sound he knew too well, the deep rockfall rumble of the high country shifting under early snowload, the mountains closing their doors ahead of schedule.

Winter was coming down fast. Whatever the council decided, the deciding window was shrinking by the hour.
