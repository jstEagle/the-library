## 17. The Same Hand

The council house smelled of woodsmoke and dried sage, and it was warmer than any room Lena had sat in for twenty years. That fact alone put him off balance. He had learned to read cold rooms, empty rooms, rooms where he was tolerated until he could be shown the door. This one had a table in the center, scarred and scrubbed, with a lamp on it that someone had trimmed and filled without being asked. Two chairs. Sera took one. He took the other.

Amara set a third object between them before she left: a flat stone, dark and smooth, worn at the edges like something handled often. "For when the talk gets hard," she said. "Hold it. It keeps hands honest." Then she pulled the door shut behind her, and the latch fell with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Sera unlaced the satchel's flap with the care of a woman opening a wound she'd been keeping clean. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the documents he'd heard about but never seen: survey quadrant reports, field notes in three different hands, a ledger of disbursements, and a set of maps so fresh the ink still had a faint chemical smell. She laid them out in rows, squaring each edge, and he understood she wasn't being fussy. Order was how she kept her nerve.

"Start anywhere," she said. "But if you start at the beginning, start here." She tapped a letterhead at the top of the ledger. *Meridian Consolidated Water & Power.*

Lena didn't touch it. He looked at it the way you look at a snake you've stepped over once already in your life.

"Say the name out loud," he said.

"Meridian Consolidated."

He made himself say it too. It came out of his mouth like a bone he'd swallowed long ago and never passed. "Twenty-two years ago they were called Meridian Development. Same offices in the same building in the capital. They changed the name after the reservoir."

Sera went very still. "What reservoir?"

"Calder Fork." He said it flat, because if he gave it any shape at all it would cut him. "A dam and holding basin up in the northern ranges. Built fast, inspected by men who were paid to inspect nothing. It failed in the spring melt. Took out a company town below it. Nine dead. I was the junior engineer on the inspection crew."

The lamp hissed. Outside, wind moved through the terraces with a sound like water running where no water ran.

"You were convicted," Sera said. Not a question. The stories traded among outlaws had reached even her, apparently, though he wondered now how much of what she'd heard was true.

"Negligence. Falsified inspection records—mine, supposedly. My signature on clearance reports I never signed. The handwriting expert was retained by Meridian's counsel. The judge had shares in the company through his brother-in-law, which came out years later, quietly, in a newspaper nobody reads. By then I was gone." He turned the smooth stone over in his hand. It was warm already. "I never served a day. I ran before sentencing. Which meant everything I said afterward sounded like what it was—a guilty man talking. So I stopped saying anything. Twenty years of miles instead."

Sera was watching him with an expression he couldn't file under anything he knew. Pity would have been easier to bear than what was actually there, which was recognition.

"Read this," she said, and slid the ledger toward him.

* * *

The ledger was a thing of beauty, if you liked that kind of beauty. Columns of figures, dates, initials. Payments to surveyors, to packers, to a man listed only as *H.B. liaison*—Harrow Bend liaison, Lena thought, and filed the name away like a round chambered into a gun. And there, recurring every few weeks through the whole season, a line item that made his jaw tighten:

*Disbursement — M.C.W.P. — special appropriation — aquifer confirmation — do not enter in public record.*

"They weren't surveying to find water," Sera said. "They already knew. The original geological work was done eleven years ago—there's a reference to it here, a report number, but the report itself doesn't exist in any archive. It was buried. This year's survey existed for one purpose: to produce official paper saying the escarpment country is worthless except for the water under it, so the government can declare the surface valueless, condemn it cheap, and hand the aquifer to Meridian under a rights lease nobody will read."

"And the survey team?"

"Was paid to find exactly what they found and no more. Except—" She pulled a set of field notes, pages soft from handling. "Except the senior surveyor, a man named Ostrander, started asking questions. He cross-checked the pre-existing data against their own readings and found the numbers didn't match unless somebody had adjusted them. He wrote a memo. The memo is supposed to be in this satchel. It isn't. It's in the fire back at the camp, or in Verrin's pocket—I don't know which. What I have is his draft, the one he threw away, and my own copies of the falsified quadrants."

"Verrin," Lena said. "The H.B. liaison."

"He came to camp twice while I was there. Never introduced himself to me. Wore a good coat and bad boots—boots made for looking at, not walking in. The senior men treated him like weather. You don't argue with it, you just watch which way it's coming from." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I stole the originals the night after Ostrander disappeared. Not ran—stole. There's a difference. I walked out of that camp with the truth under my arm and I've been walking ever since."

Lena spread the falsified quadrant sheets beside the ledger and began, slowly, to match figures. He had spent two decades reading ground—the lie in a trail, the story under a track—and it turned out paper lied the same way. A number smoothed where it should have been rough. A measurement repeated too perfectly, the way a witness repeats a story too perfectly. Water tables recorded as marginal in one column while the drill logs in another showed seeps at depths that could only mean one thing: an aquifer the size of a small sea, sitting under land the government was about to be told was desert.

"This is the same hand," he said finally.

"What?"

"The same hand that forged my signature twenty-two years ago. Not literally—not the same man, he'll be dead or rich somewhere—but the same method. Adjust the record. Bury the real one. Buy the experts. Let the paperwork kill whoever's in the way. Back then the paperwork killed nine people and ruined me. Now it's going to erase a valley." He looked up at her. "And everyone in it."

Sera held his gaze. Up close, in lamplight, he could see how tired she was—not the tiredness of the trail, which he knew intimately, but the older kind, the kind that comes from carrying something alone too long. He recognized that too.

"I found your name in the ledger," she said quietly.

The room got smaller.

"Page fourteen. A disbursement dated the year of the reservoir failure. *Legal services rendered — Delacroix matter.* Paid to a firm in the capital. It's not much—a single line. But it means the conviction wasn't just tolerated by Meridian. It was purchased."

"I know," Lena said. "I've known since the third year of my exile, when a clerk who owed me a debt sent me a copy of the firm's client list. Knowing has never been worth anything. Knowing without proof is just a wound you keep re-opening to check if it still bleeds."

"And now?" She touched the ledger. "Is this proof?"

"It's a start. Ledgers can be explained away. Forged signatures can be disputed. But you—" he pointed at her, then at the papers, then back at her, drawing the line between them the way he'd draw a route on a map, "—you are a living witness who worked inside the survey. Ostrander, if he's alive, is another. The surviving team members know what happened in that storm. Paper plus people. That's a case. Paper alone is a curiosity."

"So we take it to the courts." She said it carefully, testing the weight of it.

"We take it to the courts," he agreed, and then heard himself say it, and laughed—a short, rusty sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing his own trap spring. "Listen to me. Twenty years telling myself the law was a dog that bit only on command, and here I am planning to fetch it a stick."

"Then don't." Sera leaned forward. "You owe them nothing. Walk away. Stay in the valley. Let someone else carry it."

"And who? You? Alone, through passes that'll be snowed shut inside a month, with every man Meridian can hire looking for a girl with a satchel?" He shook his head. "No. If it goes out, it goes out carried by someone who knows the ground, and there is exactly one person in this valley who does."

"That's not fair to you."

"Nothing about this has ever been fair. That's rather the point." He gathered the quadrant sheets into a stack, squared the edges the way she did—it felt like learning a language—and pushed them back across the table. "Here's what matters tonight. Not my grievance, not yours. The overlap." He laid his palm flat on the ledger. "This company broke the world I lived in. It is about to break the world you built. Same hand. Different decades. If we fight it separately, it wins separately. If we fight it together—"

"Together we're a witness and a fugitive with no standing in any court," she finished. "Forgive me. I'm a clerk. I finish sentences; it's a professional habit."

"A witness and a guide," he corrected. "And whatever else the valley decides to send with us. Amara told me there's a journalist in Harrow Bend who owes me a favor from a life I barely remember. Federal investigators come through ahead of the thaw some years. There are doors. I stopped knocking on them because every door I'd ever knocked on opened onto a cell. But doors aren't walls, Sera. I forgot that. Living here, I'd have gone on forgetting it forever."

She was quiet a long moment. The lamp burned steady. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and quit, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"When I ran from the camp," she said at last, "I told myself I was saving the documents. That was a lie I could live with. The truth is I was saving myself, and the documents were the reason I permitted myself to run." She looked down at her hands—clerk's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, cracked now from the cold. "You're offering me something I haven't had since that night. A way to save both."

"Not offered," Lena said. "Agreed. There's a difference, and it matters. I'm not doing this for you and you're not doing it for me. We're doing it because the same hand wrote both our names in its book, and neither of us intends to let it write the ending."

Sera extended her hand across the table—clerk-fashion, formal, almost comically solemn—and he took it. Her grip was stronger than he expected. Ink and iron.

"Allies, then," she said.

"Allies."

She held on one second longer than the bargain required, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

They worked until the lamp needed filling a second time. She taught him the structure of the evidence—which documents corroborated which, which numbers matched which drill logs, where the memo draft fit, why the disbursement page mattered. He taught her what he knew of courts and investigators and the difference between evidence that convinces and evidence that survives cross-examination. Twice she caught him staring at the name *Delacroix* in the ledger, and twice she said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

Near the end, she pulled out one last sheet—a routing slip, internal, stamped with a date six weeks past.

"Conveyance order," she said. "For the survey equipment. Note the destination."

He read it. Read it again. The destination was not Harrow Bend, not the capital, not any depot. It was a rail siding called Kestrel Junction, and attached to it a note in a neat bureaucratic hand: *Equipment to be held pending arrival of company security personnel. Do not release to territorial authorities.*

"They're not done," Lena said. "The survey was phase one. Confirmation of the asset. Phase two comes in spring—security personnel, enforcement, probably a legal action filed in absentia against trespassers nobody can serve because officially nobody's there." He looked up. "How many people live in this valley, Sera?"

"Forty-one. Forty-three counting you and me."

"Forty-three against a company that bought a judge once and hasn't gotten less ambitious since." He rubbed his eyes. "Then the evidence isn't just justice anymore. It's a shield. Get it into federal hands before thaw, and Meridian spends the next year answering subpoenas instead of sending gunmen. Every week those papers sit in a cache is a week they're free to move."

"Which means winter isn't our enemy," Sera said slowly. "It's our deadline. Both ways. Too late and the passes seal with the truth still inside. Too early and—" She stopped.

"And Verrin knows it too," Lena finished. "Whatever he is, he's not stupid. He'll be watching the high routes. Counting days. Same arithmetic, opposite hopes."

Sera began repacking the satchel, each document going home to its place in the oilcloth. When she reached the bottom she paused, then drew out a single loose page and set it apart from the rest.

"What's that one?"

"My copy of the client list," she said. "The one the clerk sent you, third year of exile. Your name led me to it—or rather, the stories about you did, and then the list confirmed them. I've been carrying it since the flats." She slid it toward him. "It belongs with the rest. One conspiracy, one file."

Lena looked at the page a long time. Twenty years he'd carried that knowledge alone, unwitnessed, useless as a stone in the boot. Now it lay on a scarred table under a trimmed lamp, next to a girl's careful copies, part of a row, part of a case, part of something larger than one man's ruin.

He picked it up and added it to the stack.

Outside, the wind had changed. He felt it before he heard it—that faint pressure shift, the dry bite in the air that every creature on the escarpment understood. Snow weather. Early, and heavy.

Sera noticed his face change. "What is it?"

Lena stood and went to the window. Far above the valley, beyond the black teeth of the ridgeline, the stars were going out one by one, swallowed from the north like lamps being carried away down a long corridor.

"The sky's closing," he said. "Whatever we're going to do with these papers—we just ran out of time to do it slowly."
