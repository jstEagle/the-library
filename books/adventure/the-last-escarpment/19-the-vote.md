## 19. The Vote

The council met in the long house at dusk, when the terraces below had gone blue and the first wood smoke of evening rose straight up in the windless cold. Lena had been in the valley eleven days. He had learned the names of maybe forty of the hundred and sixty people who lived there, learned which stone steps were slick with seep ice, learned that the bread was baked on Tuesdays and that children here had never seen a train.

He had not yet learned whether he was a guest or a prisoner, and the council had not yet learned either, which was why they had asked him to this meeting instead of simply telling him its outcome.

The long house had been a survey shed once, before the surveyors came and before they left, and it still carried the smell of pine pitch and lamp oil. Benches had been dragged into a rough semicircle. A stove ticked in the corner. Amara sat at the center, not at a head—there was no head, he'd noticed; the valley did not seem to believe in heads—and around her sat eleven others: the orchard man Pell, whose hands were stained permanently with walnut hulls; the twins Ivo and Rass, scouts both, still snow-dusted from the high watch; a midwife named Odele; a former cavalry farrier called Brack who never sat all the way down, as if ready to leave any room he was in; and others whose faces Lena had seen at the bread ovens and the water cisterns and the evening gun that wasn't a gun, just the sound of the mill gate closing.

Sera sat apart from the semicircle, near the door, with the satchel on her knees. She had not let it out of arm's reach since he'd known her. Tonight it sat between her boots like a thing being tried.

Amara opened without ceremony. "You've all read what could be read. Sera has explained the rest. The survey quadrant, the aquifer, the falsified reports. The company that paid for the erasure is the same company that built the reservoir that failed twenty years ago and buried the blame on a ranger who never served a day because they didn't need him to—they just needed him gone." She said it plainly, without looking at Lena, which he understood as a kindness. "The question is not what the papers say. We believe the papers. The question is what we do with them."

"Nothing," said Brack. "We burn them. That's what I've said since the ranger walked in with them under his arm. Paper is a door, and doors open both ways. Right now nobody knows we exist. That's not weakness. That's the whole architecture."

"Nobody knows we exist," Ivo said, "except a government survey team was sent to find us, and a company posse is being paid to erase what the government couldn't. The architecture already has a hole in it."

"The surveyors are gone," Brack said. "The posse doesn't exist except in Sera's fear and the ranger's guesses."

"My guesses," Lena said, "are twenty years of knowing how men like Verrin are paid."

He hadn't meant to speak first. But the room turned toward him and he understood that this was the thing he had been brought in for—not his vote, not yet, but his voice. The outsider's account of the outside.

"Tell it," Amara said.

He stood, because sitting felt wrong for what he had to say, and because his knee ached from the crossing and standing distributed the pain more honestly. He kept his voice low. The long house was small enough that low carried.

"Companies like Meridian Water and Power don't spend money on a survey to lose the report. They don't send a fixer into the mountains in winter on a rumor. Everything you've told me—the eviction orders the surveyors carried, the falsified quadrant, the junior clerk they didn't count on—all of it says the same thing. They already know the valley is here. The survey wasn't to find you. It was to document you, so the erasure would have a paper trail of its own. Legal, tidy, deniable." He let that sit. "Burning the papers doesn't unsend the survey. It just leaves you with no record that you were ever documented. You'd be fighting for your existence with nothing in your hands while they hold everything in theirs."

"And taking the papers out?" Pell asked. "To courts. To the same government that sent armed men with eviction orders. You want us to hand our only weapon to the people who aimed it at us."

"I want you to stop thinking of the papers as a weapon," Lena said. "They're a witness. There's a difference. A weapon you fire once. A witness keeps talking."

Odele, the midwife, spoke for the first time. She had a way of talking that made Lena think of water finding its level. "My grandmother came into this valley in a wagon with the wheels burned off behind her, so no one could follow the ruts. She lived forty years here and never once went down to Harrow Bend, and she died in her bed, and her grave is up on the south terrace, and I have never once regretted her choice. Hiding worked for her." She looked at Lena. "But she hid from a sheriff and a land baron. Not from a company with a survey department. The scale's changed. I don't know if my grandmother's arithmetic still works."

"That's not arithmetic," Brack said. "That's a eulogy waiting to happen."

"It's a question," Odele said.

Amara raised a hand and the room settled. "We'll come to the vote. But first I want the ranger's proposal on the table in full, because there are versions of it and they are not equal. Lena. Say it plainly."

Lena had been turning the sentence over in his head for two days, walking the terraces, testing it the way he tested a route—looking for the place it would break. Now he said it out loud and found it held.

"Send the papers out. Not a courier from among you—every one of you is known to someone below, and a stranger moving fast in winter draws every eye. Send me. I've crossed that range in every month it can be crossed. I know the passes, the wind patterns, where the drifts build and where the scoured ridgeline holds bare. I can be in Harrow Bend in six days if the weather gives me three of them clean. There's a federal investigator—Corrigan—already sniffing at the water-rights filings. I know a journalist in Harrow Bend, Teale, who's been waiting twenty years for exactly this kind of paper. The documents go to both, the same day, so that no one person's disappearance ends it. Then I come back." He paused. "Or I don't. And either way, the valley's name is never in my mouth. The papers show the aquifer and the fraud. They don't show a road in. I've looked. Sera has looked. There is no map of this valley in that satchel, and there won't be."

"And if they take you," Rass said, "they take you apart until you give up the direction you came from. Everyone talks eventually. Everyone."

"Not everyone," Ivo said.

"Everyone," Rass insisted.

"I won't dispute it," Lena said. "So we plan for it. I go out with a false origin. I'll come in from the northeast, from the Salt Fork country, a route that's real and walkable and leads nowhere near you. If I'm taken, I'll have been tracking the survey team from the wrong side of the range entirely. It's not a perfect lie. But I've had twenty years of practice living inside one."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog—there were three dogs in the valley, all descended from one bitch who'd come over the pass with a debtor in the year Lena had been convicted—barked twice at nothing and stopped.

Sera spoke from the door, and the room went quiet for her, because the room had learned in eleven days that when Sera spoke it was usually something no one else could say.

"I was the one who ran with these papers. I've carried them through slot canyons and over scree and across the flats with my water gone, and every mile I told myself the same thing: get them out, get them to someone, make it mean something. And then I got here, and there were people, and the papers stopped being a mission and started being a threat. Because that's what they are, to all of you. I've heard the arguments for burning them and I won't pretend I don't feel the pull of it. Burn them and maybe we're safe. Maybe." She stood, and the satchel stayed on the bench, which Lena noted was the first time he had ever seen her set it down. "But I read the original quadrant reports. I know what Meridian paid for. They didn't pay to find out if there's water under this valley. They paid to make sure no one else ever found out first. Burning the papers doesn't make us worthless to them. It makes us undefended. There's a version of next spring where their posse comes back with better maps and worse intentions, and we're sitting here with ashes and no story to tell anyone, because we burned our own witness. I'd rather bet on the truth. I'd rather bet on him." She nodded at Lena, and did not look at him while she did it, which he understood was its own kind of care. "I'll copy every page first. The copies stay here. If he doesn't come back, we still know what we know, and we can decide again with the originals safe in the cache. Burning is permanent. Sending is only a risk."

"A risk with all of us on the other end of it," Brack said.

"Every choice in this room is," Sera said.

* * *

They argued for another hour, and Lena let them, because he had learned in twenty years of working alone that the worst thing a man could do with a room full of frightened people was fill the silence before the silence had done its work.

Pell worried about the orchards—if the valley's name went to the courts, even in a sealed filing, even in a journalist's notebook, it was a name that could leak. Odele worried about the children, and what a winter of watchfulness would do to them, and whether the watchfulness would ever end. The twins argued between themselves, Ivo for sending, Rass against, with the fluency of people who had been finishing each other's arguments since the womb. Brack did not soften. Brack did not move an inch, and Lena respected him for it, because Brack's position was the honest one: that safety and survival were not the same thing, and that the valley had been built on choosing the first over the second, and that every person in this room was alive because somebody before them had refused a chance to be brave.

"You all keep talking about the valley," Lena finally said, when the arguments had circled twice and started wearing grooves. "The valley. The valley's name. The valley's safety. I've been listening for an hour and I haven't heard anyone say the word *people* except Odele." He looked around the semicircle. "So let me say what I think the valley is. It's a hundred and sixty people who did the one thing this country never forgives, which is to stop asking permission. You built terraces out of a scree slope. You built a mill without a license and a school without a charter. You did it because every one of you was told, somewhere else, that you were a problem to be managed. And you know what I've learned about problems that get managed?" He let it hang. "Eventually somebody comes with a budget line."

Silence. The stove ticked.

"A valley that hides forever has already lost," Lena said. "Not this spring, maybe not for ten years. But hiding is a position, and every position gets flanked. The only question is whether you're still hiding when it happens, alone, with nothing in your hands—or whether by then you've made yourself into something the world has to negotiate with. These papers are the difference between those two futures. Not because a court loves you. Courts don't love anybody. Because a company that's been indicted can't get a posse deputized. Because a journalist who prints the aquifer story makes the valley famous, and famous is a kind of wall. You've spent eighty years being invisible, and invisibility has been a gift, and I'm the last man alive who'll tell you to give it up lightly. But the survey already ended it. The only choice left is whether the world learns your name from Meridian's lawyers or from your own witness. Those are the options. I wish there were others. There aren't."

He sat down. His knee throbbed. He realized his hands had been flat on his thighs the whole time, the way they went flat when he was holding something down.

Amara looked around the semicircle. "We vote. Papers go out, by the ranger's hand, before the passes seal. Copies stay. Yes or no."

He counted. He was good at counting—twenty years of counting sheep on the flats, of counting tracks, of counting the days since a telegram had ended his life as it was. Seven hands went up. Four stayed down. Brack's among them, arms crossed, jaw set, but he nodded once at Lena across the room, a small, grudging, honest nod that said: *I lost, and I'll keep watch anyway, because that's who I am regardless of what we decide.*

"Barely," Amara said. "It carries. Seven to four." She turned to Lena. "You understand what you've volunteered for. Not just the crossing. If you don't come back, we'll assume the worst, and the worst has a name, and the name is Meridian, and this valley will spend the spring preparing for a war it voted against being able to avoid."

"I understand," Lena said.

"And the passes," Amara said. "Say the truth about the passes."

He took a breath. "The high saddle was passable four days ago. It may be passable now. The storm line is moving down from the north—I've watched it build for two days from the ridge. I have maybe a three-day window, and the window is closing from both ends. If I don't leave the day after tomorrow, I don't leave until April."

"Then you leave the day after tomorrow," Amara said.

The council broke apart the way water breaks around a stone, people peeling off into small knots, and the argument continuing in lowered voices, because a seven-to-four vote does not end a disagreement, it only ends a meeting. Lena stood by the stove a moment. Sera came to him, and picked up the satchel, and held it against her chest, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"Day after tomorrow," she said. "You'll come to the mill at first light. I'll show you the document order, the codes in the margins, what Corrigan will need to verify and what Teale can just print. And I'm sewing the copies of the key pages into your coat lining, so even if the satchel is taken, you're not empty."

"I know," Lena said. "I've watched you sew. You stitch like you track—like the thread owes you money."

The almost-smile crossed her face and was gone. Then, quieter: "Come back. Seven to four isn't much of a margin, and I'm not sure I'm a seven if you're not standing in the room."

Outside, the wind had begun to rise, and Lena stood in the cold a long moment, looking up at the notch of the high saddle, black against the last violet of the sky, where the storm line was already eating the first stars.

Three days. Maybe.
