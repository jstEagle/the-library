## 20. Preparing the Crossing

The valley had a room for paper. That was the first thing that surprised me about it — not the terraces, not the stone houses fitted into the cliff like swallows' nests, but a dry room with a cedar door and a shelf of oilcloth-wrapped bundles, kept by a woman named Odile who had been a county recorder's clerk before she stopped existing on any government roll. Twenty years of deeds, birth records, letters from the outside, all of it catalogued in a hand so small it looked like it was trying to take up less space than it did.

"You'll work here," Amara said, and it wasn't a suggestion.

So Lena Delacroix sat across a plank table from me for three days while I taught him my satchel.

He learned fast. Faster than he pretended to. He'd sit with his hands flat on the table like a man at a hearing, listening, and then he'd ask one question — always one, never two — and the question would be the exact seam in the thing I'd just explained, the place where my knowledge was stitched together over a hole. *Who countersigned the quadrant reports?* *If the red-ink overlay is dated after the field notes, what proves which came first?* *How many people besides you have seen the originals?*

I hated him a little for those questions. Then I stopped hating him, because they were the questions I'd have asked, if I'd had anyone to ask them of.

* * *

"Start again," he said on the second morning. "From the top. Like I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid."

"I'm twenty years out of paper. Start again."

I laid the documents out in the order I'd come to think of them as a body. The field notes were the bones — eleven days of readings from Quadrant Seven, water table depths, seep locations, flow estimates, all in the surveyors' own hands, dated and initialed. The transmittal summaries were the flesh wrapped around the bones — the versions sent down to Harrow Bend, where every number that meant water had been changed or buried. And the correspondence was the nervous system: six letters between the survey lead and a man named Corliss Hale, whose name appeared nowhere on the public contracts but whose company letterhead appeared twice, folded inside an engineering firm's envelope like a knife inside a sheath.

"The aquifer runs under the escarpment," I said, tracing the fault line on the geological sheet. "Under this valley. Under land nobody with money thought was worth anything — until these numbers said otherwise. Hale's company can't file a claim on water nobody knows is there. So the survey had to say there was nothing. The summary reports say nothing. The field notes say everything. That gap between them is the whole crime."

"And the eviction orders?"

"Were written before the survey was finished." I let that sit. It was the fact that had turned my stomach in the camp at night, turning it still. "You don't order people off land you believe is worthless. The orders were the point. The survey was the receipt."

Lena looked at the papers for a long time. His face didn't do much — I'd noticed that about him, how a man could spend two decades alone and wear his expressions somewhere below the skin — but his thumb moved along the edge of the table, back and forth, like a man counting something.

"My reservoir," he said finally. "The failure report was rewritten too. Field findings versus what got filed. Same shape."

"Same hand," I said. "Hale's firm bought the engineering review in '03. It's in the correspondence — a retainer reference, page four."

"I know," he said quietly. "I read page four last night."

* * *

That afternoon we started on the codes.

The valley didn't trust any single hiding place, so the evidence had to be split three ways: the originals would stay cached in the valley, sewn into oilcloth and sealed in a tin Odile buried beneath her floor; a full copy would go out with Lena, disguised; and a second copy, partial, would be memorized — by me, later, when I took the southern route. But the copy that traveled couldn't look like evidence. It had to look like nothing.

"It has to survive being found," Lena said. "Assume you're stopped. Assume they open your coat."

"So we make the coat boring."

We made the coat boring. Odile produced a ranger's canvas coat, older than mine, salt-stiff at the cuffs, and we cut the lining open along seams that already existed — no new stitching, nothing a tailor's eye would catch. The pages went in folded to the width of a finger, interleaved with the horsehair padding that was already there. Then Odile sewed it closed with thread pulled from the original hem, and her stitches were so fine and so mean that I put my fingers over the seam afterward and felt nothing but coat.

"There," she said, biting the thread. "Now it's just a poor man's jacket. Nobody robs a poor man."

Lena put it on. He rolled his shoulders once, testing, and I watched his face for the flinch of a man wearing something valuable against his skin. There wasn't one. That frightened me more than anything else that week — how easily he could carry a thing like that, how practiced he was at carrying dangerous weight without letting it show.

"You should test it," I said. "Wear it tomorrow. All day. If a seam rides wrong, we fix it now, not on the pass."

He nodded. Then he took the coat off, folded it, and set it on the shelf with a care that contradicted everything else about him.

* * *

The provisioning happened in the evenings, and it happened everywhere at once, because the valley provisioned the way a family feeds a traveler — by ambush.

A boy named Teo appeared at my door with a sack of dried apricots and vanished before I could thank him. Two sisters I only knew by sight left jerky on the windowsill, elk meat cured with juniper, wrapped in cloth that smelled of smoke. An old man called Ferro — deaf in one ear, opinionated in both — intercepted Lena on the terrace path and pressed a tin of rendered fat into his hands, shouting instructions about chilblains that half the valley could hear.

By the fourth day there was a pile at the foot of my bed that hadn't existed five days earlier: pemmican in waxed cloth, pinole meal in a flour sack, salt pork in a crock, dried apples, a brick of tea, matches in a sealed jar, a spare pair of wool socks so dense they could have stopped an arrow. Somebody had oiled a set of snowshoes. Somebody had sharpened an ice axe and wrapped the head in leather. None of it had been discussed in council. None of it had been voted on. The vote had been for the plan; the provisioning was just what people did when the plan involved sending someone they'd decided to keep.

"They're feeding you like it's a wedding," I told Lena.

He was checking the ice axe's wrap, turning it over in his big careful hands. "They're feeding me like it's a funeral they're hoping to cancel."

"That's not what I—"

"I know what it is." He set the axe down. "I've been on the other end of it. Twenty years ago a town fed me off like this too. Different reason." He picked up a strip of the juniper jerky, smelled it, and something crossed his face — quick, gone. "This is good jerky. Tell whoever made it."

"You can tell them yourself. You're coming back."

He looked at me then, straight on, and I understood that he was deciding whether to lie to me. I watched him decide not to.

"I'm coming back," he said. "But everybody in this valley knows the passes don't promise anything. That's why they're feeding me now. Salt keeps. Promises don't."

* * *

On the fifth night we worked late, and the lamp burned low, and I caught myself watching his hands instead of the documents.

He handled paper like it might bruise. Every page he touched, he touched at the edges. When he copied figures into his memory — and he was copying them, I realized, building the same backup I was meant to build, silently, without telling anyone — he did it by reading each line once, closing his eyes, reciting it back, opening his eyes to check. He never wrote anything down. A man who had spent twenty years leaving no tracks had apparently also spent them leaving no notes.

"Say the transmittal dates," I said.

"March ninth, March twenty-second, April fourth. The April fourth summary omits the seep readings entirely. The field notes for April second and third are the ones that matter most — highest flows, deepest aquifer confirmation, initialialed by Voss and the junior hydrologist, Petraglia."

"Petraglia." I said the name carefully. "He's one of the ones who ran."

"Amara thinks so. The tracks went south."

"If he's alive, he's a witness."

"If he's alive, he's a witness Verrin hasn't caught yet." Lena closed the folder. "Which means he's a clock, same as the weather."

I blew out the lamp because my eyes hurt, and in the dark the cedar room smelled of oilcloth and old ink and, faintly, of the juniper smoke that lived in everything here. I heard Lena stand, heard him find the door by memory — three days and he already knew the room in the dark — and pause with his hand on the latch.

"Sera."

"Yes."

"When I'm gone — if the posse comes early, before spring — you don't negotiate. You run the southern route even if it's not ready. Even if it kills you slower than staying would."

"That's not your decision."

"No," he agreed. "It's yours. I'm asking anyway."

And he was gone, boots soft on the stone, and I sat in the dark being angry at him for asking, and being more angry at how much I wanted to say yes.

* * *

The last day, we drilled.

Odile played the part of a company man stopping a rider on the flats — she was terrifyingly good at it, having been interrogated once, in another life, by men with badges and ledgers. She'd stop Lena in the yard, demand his name, his business, his destination, and he'd give her a story we'd built together: a trapper out of the northern range, crossing ahead of the snows to sell pelts in Harrow Bend, coat too worn to bother searching, papers forged in a name that existed in exactly one county record Odile had manufactured herself.

First run, he was flawless and I told him so.

Second run, Odile searched the coat anyway, and found nothing, and Lena stood through it with his arms slightly away from his sides, patient as a fence post, while her hands walked every seam.

Third run, Odile changed the game. She asked him his mother's name. He hesitated — half a heartbeat, no more — and gave the forged name's forged mother, and it was perfect, and afterward he stood in the yard looking at the mountains with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could read it, and what it said was: *that name used to belong to somebody.*

I went and stood next to him. We watched the peaks where the storm clouds were already stacking up, white and gray, organized, patient.

"How much snow up there?" I asked.

"Enough to matter. Not enough to stop me yet." He glanced sideways at me. "You scouted the southern route as a clerk. How much of it did you actually walk?"

"All of it on paper. Half of it on foot. The frozen river section—" I stopped. "I read the river section in a report."

"So you'll read it again before you go."

"Yes."

"Good." He picked up the coat from the fence rail where he'd draped it and held it out to me. "Sew in one more thing."

"What?"

"The list of names. The nine from the reservoir. I know them by heart, but hearts stop." His voice didn't change at all, which was how I knew what it cost him. "Put them in the lining. If I don't get through, the names still do."

I took the coat. My hands were steady until the third stitch, and then they weren't, and Odile came and finished the seam for me without saying a word about it, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

That evening the council met briefly — routes, signals, the word that would send me south if the main pass failed — and afterward Amara kept Lena behind, and I pretended not to wait outside in the cold, and waited anyway. When he came out, the lamplight behind him, he carried a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth that I hadn't seen him carry in.

"What's that?" I asked.

He considered lying. I watched him skip it this time, entirely, like a man learning.

"Twenty years ago I packed to run," he said. "Everything I owned fit in this. I've repacked it maybe forty times since. Different towns, different years. Always ready to leave in ten minutes." He turned the bundle over in his hands. "It's the only habit I ever kept."

"And now?"

"Now it goes in the cache with the originals. Under Odile's floor." He almost smiled — the first whole one I'd seen from him, lopsided and unpracticed. "Can't flee with your whole life buried under a floor you're defending. Figure I ought to commit to something."

"You're committing to a valley."

"I'm committing to a floor," he said. "The rest follows."

He walked me as far as the terrace above my house, because that was the kind of thing he did now, small courtesies performed like route-finding, carefully, one step chosen at a time. At the corner he stopped.

"First light," he said. "Teo's bringing the horse around. Don't come down to see me off."

"Why not?"

"Because if you're standing there, I'll look back. And a man who looks back on the flats leaves a trail longer than his feet do." He said it lightly, but neither of us laughed. "When the passes open in spring, I'll come down the north draw. Watch for three fires on the ridge, one night apart. That's me."

"And if there aren't three fires?"

He was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence, moving down off the escarpment, carrying the first smell of snow.

"Then you run south," he said, "and you don't look back either."

He went down the terrace steps and I stood in the dark listening to his boots fade on the stone, and behind me, in the cedar room, the copies lay stacked and the coat hung on its peg with nine dead men's names sleeping in its lining, and somewhere below us, far beyond the valley's walls, a man named Doss was already riding toward Harrow Bend with betrayal in his saddlebags — though none of us knew it yet, and wouldn't, until the horse was missed at dawn and the clock began to scream.
