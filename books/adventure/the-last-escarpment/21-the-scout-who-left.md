## 21. The Scout Who Left

Doss had been the first to find Lena's tracks on the flats, three seasons back, and he liked to remind people of it. He was a narrow man with a narrow face and hands that were never still, and in the valley he'd made himself useful the way restless men do—running scout lines, reading weather off the peaks, carrying messages between the stone houses faster than anyone. He was good at it. That was the trouble with Doss: he was good at everything except being content.

Lena had watched him for weeks without saying anything. Twenty years alone taught a man to watch. He saw how Doss lingered at the council meetings after the others drifted out, how his eyes went to the satchel when Sera carried it, how he asked Amara questions that sounded like concern and landed like measurement. *How many people could make the south route in winter? How long before the company men could ride from Harrow Bend? What exactly did the papers prove?* Concern, or cartography.

On the night it happened, Lena was in the long house going over the documents one last time with Sera, her finger tracing the coded columns, his memory doing the work of holding them. The originals were cached—Amara had insisted on that much—in a dry cleft behind the third waterfall on the north fork, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed juniper trunk. Only five people knew the spot. Lena. Sera. Amara. Old Tam, who kept the goats. And Doss, because a cache no one else could reach in an emergency was a coffin, not a safeguard.

The storm came up from the southwest around midnight, the kind that made the whole valley groan like a ship. Nobody would travel in it. Nobody but a man who wanted to be missed.

* * *

Sera noticed the horse first. She'd come down to the stable at dawn to check the bay gelding she'd been promised for the southern run, and the bay was there—but the dun, the fastest animal in the valley, was gone, and the far gate stood open with snow drifting through it unbroken.

"Doss," she said, and the word came out flat, already knowing.

They found his bunk stripped. His boots gone, his heavy coat gone, and on the table where he slept, nothing. No note. A note would have been something—a reason, a grievance they could have answered. The absence of a note was worse. It meant he hadn't left in anger. He'd left in arithmetic.

Amara stood in the doorway of his quarters for a long time, her breath smoking, her face unreadable as the escarpment itself. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet enough that Lena had to lean in to hear it over the wind.

"He asked me Tuesday where the survey survivors had gone after they fled. I told him I didn't know." She paused. "I lied. He knew I lied."

"Then he knows more than the cache," Lena said. "He knows we're hiding something beyond the valley itself."

"He knows what we all know." Amara turned. "That the truth is worth more than our lives to some men, and worth less than nothing to others."

Sera had gone pale in the way she did—not dramatic, just drained, color leaving her face like water through sand. "He'll go to Harrow Bend. To Verrin's people. He'll trade the cache location for amnesty."

"He'll try," Lena said. "The question is what he actually knows. Did you ever tell him the exact spot?"

"No one told him the exact spot. But he helped carry the juniper trunk up the north fork." Sera's jaw tightened. "He was there when we chose the falls. He can stand at the third waterfall and look at the cleft and know."

Lena was already moving toward the door, pulling his coat. "Then I ride now."

"In this?" Amara gestured at the white roar beyond the walls. "You'll be dead by noon, and he'll still be ahead of you."

"He's riding blind into a storm he doesn't know, on a horse he doesn't deserve, toward men who will use him and lose him." Lena cinched the coat belt. "I've spent twenty years learning every fold of this range. He's spent twenty years learning to run messages. It's not even a race. But every hour I wait is an hour he gains."

Sera caught his arm at the door. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "If Verrin gets those originals—"

"He won't." He said it the way he said most things, flat and certain, and then, because she deserved more than certainty, he added: "And if I don't come back, send someone to the southern route anyway. Don't wait for me. The copies are sewn in my coat, but the testimony—" he nodded at her, at the clerk who had become a witness, "—the testimony walks on two legs. You're not backup, Sera. You're the second plan. Remember that."

She held his eyes for a moment longer than was comfortable, which was its own kind of message, and let him go.

* * *

The storm fought him the whole way up the north fork, and Lena let it. A man fighting weather makes noise and breaks trail; a man moving with it leaves almost nothing. He read the wind the way Doss never could, cut the switchbacks where the drifts firmed overnight, and by midmorning he stood at the third waterfall, half-frozen, listening to the water thunder under its lace of ice.

The cleft behind the falls was empty.

Not disturbed—empty. The juniper trunk was gone entirely, hauled out and carried away, and the only sign was a single boot print in the ice-melt at the lip, pressed deep by a man bearing weight on one shoulder. Doss had come through two days ago. Maybe three. Before the storm. Before anyone thought to count horses.

Lena stood in the falling white and felt the cold move through him that had nothing to do with temperature. Two days. At a hard walk, two days put Doss past the high flats, maybe into the low country, maybe within sight of the telegraph line that ran along the Harrow Bend road. Within reach of a wire that could summon Verrin's posse before the week was out.

He turned back down the valley, and the storm seemed to close behind him like a door.

* * *

By the time he reached the long house, word had spread, and the valley had gathered—not the formal council, just people, standing in the snow outside with their breath rising, the way crowds form when something has broken. Old Tam was there, wringing his hat. The Herrera brothers. Mara Voss with her two girls pressed against her coat.

"The trunk," Amara said when Lena reached her. Not a question.

"Gone. Taken clean, days ago. He planned this before the vote ever happened." Lena looked at the faces around him, and he understood suddenly what he was looking at: not fear of the company, but fear of each other. Five people had known the cache. Four remained. Every secret now had a price on it, and everyone in the circle was doing the same terrible math about their neighbors.

"We should burn the copies," someone said—it was one of the Herreras, his voice cracking. "Burn everything. If there's nothing to find, there's nothing to sell."

"And then what?" Sera stepped forward, and her voice was different than Lena had ever heard it—not the frightened clerk from the slot canyons, not the careful witness by the fire. Something harder. "Then we're exactly what they say we are. Worthless people hiding worthless land. They take the aquifer, they take the valley, and nobody ever knows why. Doss sold a location. He couldn't sell the truth, because the truth isn't in a trunk. It's in me. Are you going to burn me too?"

Silence, except the wind.

Amara raised one hand, and the crowd stilled the way water stills. "No one burns anything," she said. "No one runs. We finish the fortifications, we finish the training, and we assume the company knows everything Doss knew—which is the cache is empty and the valley exists. That's all he had. That's all he could carry." Her eyes found Lena's. "How long?"

"A posse could assemble in Harrow Bend in four days. Could reach the lower passes in six, if the weather holds. If it doesn't hold, they wait for thaw—and so does the law." Lena pulled off his gloves, flexing stiff fingers. "Which means the clock isn't the storm anymore. The clock is Doss's mouth. Everything we planned to do in spring, we do now."

Mara Voss spoke up, quiet, her daughters' mittens clutched in her fist. "Doss carried my boy down from the scree when his leg broke. Two years ago. Carried him six miles." She said it not as defense, exactly. As accounting. As if the ledger of a man might balance somewhere between betrayal and that six miles, and none of them would ever know which way it tipped.

Nobody answered her. There wasn't an answer. Lena thought of the reservoir, of the nine names he carried like stones, of the men who had engineered the collapse and slept fine afterward. Men were rarely one thing. That was the whole horror of it.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the dark. False trails re-cut where the snow had filled them. Decoy firewood stacked in the draws. The rockfall chokes above the main draw checked and re-checked, levers tested, warning signals strung. Lena moved among them like a man possessed, and he was—the clock in his head had changed sound. It had been ticking for weeks, patient as a drip of water. Now it rang.

Near midnight, Sera found him at the map table, tracing routes with a charred stick.

"You haven't slept."

"Neither have you."

"I'm not the one who has to outrun a blizzard tomorrow." She sat across from him. For a while neither spoke. Then: "Do you think he'll tell them about the south route?"

Lena set down the stick. "Does he know it?"

"He knows I scouted something. He doesn't know where."

"Then Verrin will guard the roads he knows and miss the one he doesn't." Lena looked at her across the map, at this woman who had walked out of a survey camp with a satchel and a conscience and hadn't stopped walking since. "Which means your route just became the most important piece of ground in this territory. And you'll cross it alone, in winter, with a rider possibly behind you and a blizzard certainly ahead."

"Yes."

"You understand I'm supposed to argue with that."

"You understand I'm not going to listen." Almost a smile. Gone again. "Lena. He sold us. Whatever happens next happens fast, and it happens whether we're ready or not. The only choice we get is who carries the truth out. You voted yourself the mountains. Let me vote myself the south."

He wanted to argue. He heard himself not arguing.

"First light," he said instead. "We plan your route. And Sera—if Doss reaches Verrin tonight, then somewhere on your southern line there may already be a man watching for a woman with a satchel. You don't outrun that by being brave. You outrun it by being smarter than whoever's hunting you. Promise me you'll be smarter."

"I promise to try," she said, which from Sera was a vow.

Outside, the storm was breaking apart, stars cutting through in ragged patches over the escarpment. Somewhere out in all that darkness, a narrow man on a stolen dun was riding toward Harrow Bend with his mouth full of secrets, and every mile under him was a mile off the valley's life.

Lena stood in the cold and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. Six days for a posse. Seven for the thaw courts. One chance, already moving, on a southern route no map showed.

The clock wasn't ticking anymore.

It was running.
