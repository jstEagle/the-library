## 22. Pursuit

The horse was gone before I reached the corrals.

I stood in the gray half-light with my hand still on the gate rail and read the story in the dirt like a man reading his own death warrant. Doss had taken the bay mare—Amara's best animal, the one with the deep chest and the lungs to run all day—and he'd taken her at a walk for the first quarter mile, which told me he'd been careful, which told me he'd planned this longer than one bad night. Past the first bend in the creek he'd swung up into a gallop and stopped pretending.

Behind him, in the soft ground near the corral's far corner, were the prints of his own boots from earlier that evening. He'd walked the fence line twice. Checking the footing. Choosing his moment.

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. The bay could hold eight miles an hour over broken country for as long as Doss had the nerve to ask it. On foot, carrying what I carried, I couldn't match that. Not straight-line. But Doss didn't know this country, not the way I knew it, and he was riding toward the pass mouth on the route any sensible man would take—the route a map would show him, if he'd bothered to steal a map along with everything else.

Sensible men ride maps. That's how you catch them.

* * *

They gave me twenty minutes and a horse they could spare—a rangy dun gelding named for no one, fifteen years old, half wild, kept at the valley's edge because nobody trusted him and nobody wanted to admit why. He was the fastest thing in the valley when he decided to be. The problem was always getting him to decide.

Sera saddled him while I filled the water skins. Her hands moved fast and sure, and she didn't waste breath on anything soft.

"He'll head for the low saddle first," she said. "It's the obvious line south. But if he's smart he'll swing east after the second ridge and take the stone terraces down toward the dry riverbed. That's where the cache is."

"You know the cache location," I said. It wasn't a question. She'd sewn the copies into my coat herself; she knew every stitch of the plan.

"I know it because Amara trusted me with it." She cinched the last strap and looked up at me, and her face in the lantern light was the face of a woman doing sums she hated. "Which means Doss knows it too, or half of it. We told six people. Six."

"Five now."

"Don't make it sound better than it is." She handed me the reins. "Lena. If he reaches Harrow Bend—"

"He doesn't reach Harrow Bend."

"That's not a plan. That's a wish."

"It's both." I swung up. The dun sidestepped, tested the bit, found my hands quiet, and settled. "Sera. How long before the company man expects word?"

"Doss said a week. A rider at the trading post, signals on the ridge. If nothing comes by then—" She stopped. Neither of us needed the rest of it. Verrin would come looking, and he wouldn't come alone, and he wouldn't come slow.

"Then I have less than a week to catch a man on a fresh horse," I said, "in country he can see and I can't be seen in."

"Yes."

"Good. I work better with deadlines." I turned the dun's head toward the pass, and the last thing Sera said came at my back like a thrown stone:

"Bring him back alive. We're not killers. Don't let him make us into them."

I raised one hand so she'd know I heard, and rode out into the dark.

* * *

The high flats at night are a different country than the high flats by day. By day they're open, honest, brutal—every rock and rut visible, nowhere to hide. By night they compress. The horizon pulls in close enough to touch, and distance stops being something you see and becomes something you count: strides, breaths, the rhythm of hooves on stone. Twenty years of walking this escarpment had taught me to navigate by feel, by the slope of the ground underfoot, by the way cold air pooled in the draws and spilled over the saddles like water finding its level.

Doss had none of that. Doss had moonlight, and the moon was three days past full and already sinking behind the western wall of the range, which meant he had maybe two hours of good light and then nothing but star-shine and guesswork.

I gave him his two hours.

While he burned the moon, I climbed. There's a route off the eastern shoulder of the flats that exists on no map because I invented it, or rather the mountain did and I was the first fool patient enough to find it—a goat track above a shelf of broken limestone that lets you bypass four miles of rolling ground by paying for it in terror and loose rock. No horse should have attempted it. The dun attempted it because I asked him to and because horses, like men, will do remarkable things for someone who never gives them reason to doubt. Twice the shelf crumbled under his hindquarters and twice he gathered himself and pushed through it while I lay flat against his neck and let him solve it. He was smarter about the mountain than I was. I made a note of that. Good partners get remembered.

By the time the moon went down I was four miles ahead of Doss's line of travel and a thousand feet above it, and I stopped in the black among the boulders and listened.

Wind in the scree. Wind in the bunchgrass. And underneath, faint, arrhythmic: shod hooves on stone, coming on faster than wisdom allowed.

He was running blind now. Running hard. A man who runs hard at night in strange country is a man who has already fallen once and is afraid to fall again, and fear makes riders lean forward, and leaning forward makes horses shorten their stride, and shortened strides on broken ground make lame horses. I'd seen it a hundred times chasing men who thought speed was the same thing as escape.

Speed is just a way of arriving somewhere tired.

I let him pass below me. In the starlight I could make out his shape hunched over the bay's neck, and the bay's stride—short, choppy, already favoring her left forehand just slightly, or maybe I was imagining what I wanted to imagine. Either way, he was burning her. Another hour of this pace and she'd be done, and he'd be on foot in the flats with the temperature falling toward something that killed unprepared men as casually as weather kills flies.

I could have taken him there. Slid down the slope, cut him off at the narrows below, ended it in the dark with rope and weight advantage and surprise.

I didn't. Two reasons. First, Sera's voice: *we're not killers*, and a takedown in the dark goes wrong in ways that end with somebody dead, and dead was not the outcome I wanted—not for the valley's soul, and not, though I hated admitting it, for Doss's. Second, colder and more honest: I wanted to know where he was going. If he turned east toward the cache, that told me he'd been given the full location, which meant the betrayal ran deeper than one frightened man's panic. If he stayed on the southern line, he was running blind, selling what he actually knew—which might be nothing but names and faces.

A man's route tells you the size of his secrets. I followed to read it.

* * *

He turned east at the second ridge.

I felt it before I confirmed it—that shift in the hoofbeats' bearing, the sound swinging away from the saddle and toward the terraces—and I sat very still on the cold rock and let the disappointment settle into my bones like sleet. So they'd told him. Amara had trusted six people with the valley's future and one of those six had carried the whole weight of it away on a stolen horse, and now the cache—the originals, the documents that proved the aquifer existed and that men had died burying the proof—was a bargaining chip riding east through the dark on a left-forehand lameness.

Unless.

I made myself sit another ten minutes and think it through, because twenty years of tracking had taught me the most dangerous moment in any pursuit is the moment you believe you understand your quarry. Understanding is where carelessness lives.

Doss turning east meant either he knew the cache or he guessed at it. Those were different animals. If he knew, he'd ride straight to it, confident, economical. If he guessed—if someone had mentioned the eastern terraces in his hearing without ever drawing him a line—he'd cast back and forth, searching, wasting the hours he couldn't afford.

I went down off the shelf and picked up his trail where it crossed a patch of old snow in the shadow of the ridge, and I read it the way other men read letters.

Straight line. Confident. Economical.

He knew.

* * *

The cache sat in a cleft of granite above the dry riverbed, behind a slab I'd helped position myself, in a hollow lined with oilcloth and sealed with wax against the wet. Getting there required descending a fan of talus that announced every footstep for half a mile in any direction. There was no quiet approach. There was only fast or slow.

I chose fast.

The dun took the talus at a controlled scramble, ears pinned, picking his own line while I gave him his head, and we came down onto the riverbed gravel with a clatter that must have sounded like a rockslide to anyone listening. Which was the point. I wanted Doss listening. I wanted him spending his attention on the noise behind him instead of the silence ahead.

Ahead, at the base of the granite cleft, a shape detached itself from the darkness and became a man with a rifle.

"Lena." His voice cracked on the second syllable. Young. Terrified. Trying to sound like neither. "Stop right there. I mean it. I'll shoot."

I reined in thirty feet out and sat easy, hands wide of the gun, letting the dun breathe. The bay stood beside Doss, sides heaving, head hanging, her left foreleg cocked in the posture of an animal that had stopped being able to lie about its pain. Doss held the rifle in both hands and the rifle shook, and the shaking told me more than his words ever could. Men who mean to shoot don't shake. Men who mean to shoot are calm, because the decision is already made and calm is what a made decision feels like.

"You won't shoot me, Doss."

"You don't know that."

"I know you argued against killing me when the scouts dragged me in. I know you voted to send the evidence out. I know you cried at Halloway's funeral—I was standing behind you." I dismounted slowly, keeping my hands where he could see them. "You're not a traitor, son. You're a scared boy who got offered a door out of a burning house and took it without asking whose house it was."

"They promised me amnesty." His voice climbed. "Papers. My name cleared. I've been running since I was nineteen—do you know what that's like? Never sleeping whole? Never—"

"I know exactly what it's like." I took one step forward. "I've been running twenty years. And here's what I learned that you haven't yet: the people offering you that door, they don't clear names. They collect them. You're not buying amnesty, Doss. You're selling yourself, and the price they pay for frightened men is the same price they've always paid."

"Shut up."

"The same company that erased the survey team. The same company that buried nine people under a reservoir failure and called it negligence and put it on my back. You think a man like Verrin writes pardons? Verrin writes lists. And you're on one now, boy. You've been on it since the moment you mounted that horse."

The rifle dipped. An inch. Maybe two.

And then, from the dark of the riverbed behind me, a sound that froze the blood in my throat: hoofbeats. More than one set. Coming up the dry channel fast, muffled by the sand, closing.

Doss heard them too. His face went through something complicated—triumph curdling instantly into a new and different fear.

"That's them," he whispered. "That's—they said they'd send—"

"Who did you signal?" I was moving now, low, closing the gap while his attention split. "Doss. Who's coming?"

"The trading post. I sent word from the ridge yesterday, before I—before I decided. A rider was supposed to carry it down. They said if I brought the papers they'd meet me partway, they'd—" He was backing toward the cleft, toward the cache, the rifle swinging between me and the dark. "They weren't supposed to come all the way up. They said partway. They said—"

"How many?"

"I don't know. Four? Five?" His voice broke apart entirely. "Lena, I didn't know they'd come into the mountains. I swear it. I thought—I thought I'd be gone before they—"

The hoofbeats rounded the bend in the riverbed, and torchlight came with them, throwing long shadows up the granite walls, and there was no more time for anything except arithmetic. Five riders, maybe six. Between them and me, one terrified boy holding the only rifle, guarding a cache full of the truth, with the bay mare too spent to carry anyone anywhere.

I'd spent the whole night telling myself I was hunting Doss.

Standing in the torchlight rising up the dry riverbed, watching five armed men come for the papers and the valley and everything Sera had nearly died carrying, I understood I'd had it backwards from the start.

I hadn't been hunting Doss at all.

I'd been racing him—to see which of us would reach the cache first when the real wolves arrived. And now the wolves were here, and the only thing standing between them and everything the valley had built was a shaking boy with a rifle and a tired ranger with a choice to make in the next ten seconds.

I stepped toward Doss, hands open, and said the only thing left to say.

"Give me the gun, son. Whatever happens next—we do it together."
