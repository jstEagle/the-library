## 23. The Narrows

The flats ended where the earth split open.

Lena had known the Narrows existed for twenty years and had entered them exactly twice, both times in summer, both times with the water down to a whisper. In flood season the slot canyon ran brown and violent, a throat swallowing everything the high country shed. Now, in the first hard cold of early winter, it ran thin and clear over slick red stone, and the walls rose on either side of him like the pages of a closed book.

He was gaining on Doss. He could feel it the way he felt weather coming—not evidence yet, but certainty gathering in his bones. The stolen horse was valley-bred, tough but heavy, built for gardens and short patrols, not for two days of hard country at a killing pace. Lena's own mount had been left behind at the pass mouth; he'd come after Doss on foot, light, cutting corners no map showed because he'd made the map himself, one season at a time, one mistake at a time.

The tracks told the story plainly enough. Doss had ridden hard through the night, then harder in the morning, and the horse was paying for it. There were stumbles in the print pattern now—little drags, a splayed hind hoof, places where the animal had stopped and stood too long, weight shifting. A man in a hurry stops listening to his horse. That was always how it went. That was how men died out here, more often than from cold or thirst: they stopped listening.

Lena listened. He moved along the canyon rim instead of dropping into the wash, keeping the water's sound on his left, reading the country ahead the way other men read a page. The Narrows bent twice before it opened onto the lower scree fields, and the second bend was the dangerous one—a flood-cut chute where the walls pinched to less than six feet and the floor dropped in a series of wet ledges that iced over first in any cold snap. Twenty years ago he'd watched a bighorn ewe go down there and break her neck on stone that looked like every other stone around it.

Doss would take the shortcut. Of course he would. A man running toward money runs the straight line, and the straight line through this country was a lie the escarpment told to fools.

* * *

He heard the fall before he saw it.

Not a scream—the horse didn't scream—but a clatter of shod hoof on stone, then a long sliding crash, then a sound Lena knew from reservoir work, from flood years, from all the ways water kills: the flat slap of a body hitting current.

He came over the rim at a run and looked down into the chute.

The horse was already gone—down and sideways in the narrow water, legs folded wrong against a ledge, the current pushing its head under and holding it there with the patience of anything that has forever. And downstream of it, maybe thirty yards, Doss was in the water too, clawing at rock that gave him nothing to hold, his coat already black with wet, his boots finding purchase and losing it, finding it and losing it.

The narrows amplified everything. His own breathing sounded enormous. The water sounded like a crowd.

"Doss!" The name bounced off the walls and came back at him doubled.

Doss's head came up. For one second their eyes met across thirty yards of moving water, and Lena saw the exact moment the man understood who had found him—not Verrin's people, not rescue, but the tracker he'd stolen from, the man whose knowledge of these routes he'd been counting on to carry him out. Understanding crossed Doss's face and behind it something worse than fear: shame, arriving late, like a guest who'd missed the whole dinner.

Then his feet went out from under him again and the current took him toward the second pinch.

Lena went down the inside of the bend on the old game trail, the one cut into the wall itself, barely wide enough for a boot heel, the one he'd found by following bighorn sign his first winter alone. It let him descend faster than the water carried a drowning man, which was not fast enough, which was never fast enough, so he did the thing he'd sworn off doing twenty years ago when his body was younger and his reasons were worse: he jumped.

The landing drove the breath out of him. Stone met his boots and his knees took the rest, and he was up and running on wet rock with the water at his calves, cold enough to burn, and ahead of him Doss's hand slapped against the base of the pinch where the walls narrowed and the current funneled and a man could be held under by nothing more than the shape of the canyon itself.

There was a root. An old juniper root, grown out of a crack in the east wall, dead above ground but alive below, thick as a wrist where it arched out over the water. Lena had noticed it an hour ago without knowing why, the way he noticed everything, filing it in the part of his mind that kept the desert's inventory. Now it was the only sentence in the language.

He got a hand around it and leaned out over the channel as Doss came through the pinch.

"Take my arm." The words tore out of him half-swallowed by the water's noise. "Take it or drown."

Doss took it. His grip was terrible—weak fingers, panicked strength that pulled in the wrong directions—and for a long ugly stretch of seconds Lena held the full weight of a grown man plus the river's pull on that man, anchored by one dead root and forty-nine years of stubbornness, and the root creaked, and the water climbed his forearms, and somewhere in his chest a voice that sounded like his father said *you are too old for this*, and he ignored it the way he'd ignored that voice his entire life.

Then his boot found a ledge, and he walked himself backward up the rock hand over hand, and Doss came out of the water the way drowned things come out of water—limp, streaming, coughing, alive.

They lay on the stone shelf above the channel and breathed.

* * *

For a while neither of them said anything. The canyon talked instead—the water, the wind working the rim far overhead, the small ticking sounds of cold stone warming wherever the sun reached down between the walls.

Doss rolled onto his side and vomited river water, then lay shaking. He was forty maybe, lean in the way of men who'd done hard seasons, with a face that had been ordinary until today and would never be ordinary again. His hands wouldn't close. Lena watched him try and fail twice, then stop trying.

"You broke your horse," Lena said.

"I know."

"It was a good horse."

"I know." Doss's voice came out scraped. "I know what I did."

"Do you?" Lena sat up. His arms were trembling now that they'd been given permission, fine tremors running from shoulder to wrist, and he let them run because hiding them cost more than showing them. "You stole from people who fed you. You sold a road to men who will burn what's at the end of it. And you did it for—" He stopped. He'd been about to say *money*, but looking at Doss, soaked and gray-lipped and hollowed out on the stone, he understood that wasn't the whole of it, and lying to a man at the bottom of a hole was a waste of a good hole. "Why?"

Doss laughed. It was a bad sound, one note, no humor in it.

"Amnesty," he said. "That's what they offered. Papers. A clean name." He pushed himself up against the wall, wincing. "I came over the range eleven years ago. Before that I had a spread outside of Cimarron. Lost it the slow way—bank, then drought, then bank again—and then I lost my temper worse than I lost the spread. Put a man in the hospital. A land agent's man, one of the ones who came with the seizure order." He looked at his ruined hands. "I've been a ghost ever since. Eleven years of being nobody. And a man comes up the draw last month, polite as a deacon, and says: help us find the valley and we'll make you somebody again. Legal. Signed. Notarized." He spat water. "I believed him. That's the worst part. Eleven years smart and I believed him."

"The company's man," Lena said. "In Harrow Bend."

Doss nodded slowly. Then he stopped nodding, and something moved across his face—shame turning over, becoming something else. Something with edges.

"He has a name," Doss said. "Corliss. Aldous Corliss. Works out of the land office, but he doesn't answer to the land office. He answers to Meridian Consolidated—that's the company, the one behind the survey, the one behind—" He faltered.

"Behind what?"

Doss looked at him for a long moment. Water dripped off his chin. Downstream, the horse shifted once in the current and was still.

"They're not waiting for spring," he said. "That's what I was supposed to tell Corliss when I got out. The posse's assembling now. Verrin's already in the foothills—I passed their camp the night I took the horse, moon was up, I counted fires. Thirty men, maybe more. Pack strings, wire, dynamite." He swallowed. "And it's not just the valley they want. Corliss told me—they're hunting the survey survivors too. The ones who ran. Two of them, still loose in the high country somewhere. Verrin's got a separate contract on them. Dead preferred. Dead men can't testify."

The canyon carried the words away and brought back nothing.

Lena stood. His legs held. He looked down at Doss, at the man who had betrayed everyone who'd ever fed him, and felt the strange arithmetic of mercy settle in his chest—heavy, inconvenient, unasked-for. He'd saved this man's life without deciding to. The body had decided, some older body, some self from before the conviction, before the exile, before twenty years of practicing not caring. And now the life he'd saved was going to have to mean something, because otherwise the saving was just another mile walked for nothing.

"Get up," Lena said.

Doss blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. Up. You're coming back with me."

"They'll kill me. Amara's council—"

"Maybe. That's their right, and I won't stand in the way of it." Lena turned and started up the shelf toward the game trail, then paused, not looking back. "But hear me, Doss. You know Corliss's face. You know Verrin's numbers, his gear, his timetable. Right now you're a traitor. Come back and testify, and you're a witness. Those aren't the same man, and only one of them gets to sleep indoors ever again."

Silence behind him. The water talking. Then gravel shifting—Doss getting to his feet, unsteady, alive.

"And if I run anyway?" Doss said quietly. "Right now, while your back's turned? I could still make the low country."

Lena considered the question honestly, because the man deserved honesty even now. He thought about the scree fields, the cold coming down, the thirty miles of empty country between here and anywhere, and a man with no horse, no coat worth the name, and hands that couldn't close.

"You could try," he said. "But the escarpment keeps what I give it, and I'm giving it nothing today."

A long breath behind him. Then footsteps, unsteady, following.

* * *

They climbed out of the Narrows as the light went long and copper, and camped that night in a shallow overhang Lena had used a dozen winters ago, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide in a hat. Doss slept like a man who'd been forgiven by exhaustion if not by anyone else. Lena sat up and watched the dark and did the arithmetic he hated.

Thirty men in the foothills. Dynamite. Wire. A fixer named Verrin with a contract on witnesses. And passes that would seal within days, maybe hours, sealing the valley in with its enemies—or sealing the enemies out, depending on which way the storm broke, and storms never broke the way you asked them to.

One thing was certain, and it settled over him with the cold: the valley could not simply hide anymore. Hiding had been a strategy when no one was looking. Now someone was looking, and they were coming armed, and they were coming soon.

Beside him, Doss stirred in his sleep and murmured something—half a word, a name maybe. Lena leaned closer.

"...Corliss," Doss whispered. "He keeps a ledger. Everything's in the ledger..."

His eyes opened then, suddenly, fully awake, and in the firelight they fixed on Lena with a clarity that hadn't been there in the Narrows.

"The ledger," Doss said. "I've seen it. Payments, dates, names—including names from twenty years back. Including, I'd swear it, names connected to the reservoir." He sat up slowly. "Ranger—if we're going back to that valley to save it, we should bring back something that burns hotter than we do."
