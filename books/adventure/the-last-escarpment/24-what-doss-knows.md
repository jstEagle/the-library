## 24. What Doss Knows

The storm found them an hour after dark, and by then Lena had already made the only decision available: they weren't crossing anything tonight.

He'd spotted the overhang from a half mile out—a shelf of limestone leaning off the flank of a ridge like a brim pulled low, with a dry floor of packed sand and enough depth to keep two men and a dying fire out of the wind. It wasn't shelter so much as an argument against the weather, but in the high country in November you took what the rock offered and you didn't complain.

Doss sat against the back wall with his wrists still bound in front of him, watching Lena work. The kid—man, really, twenty-five at most, though he carried himself like something younger and more breakable—hadn't said a word since the narrows. Since Lena had gone into the water after him.

Lena built the fire small. Dry sage from under the ledge, a few sticks of dead juniper he'd cached in his mind on the way through, back when this was all hypothetical. He fed it stick by stick until it threw just enough heat for two bodies sitting close, and no light that would carry past the mouth of the overhang. Twenty years of practice lived in that smallness. You didn't build a fire to be warm. You built a fire to not be seen.

He put a tin of water near the flames to take the chill off, then dug in his pack and came out with the last of the dried mutton and a heel of valley bread, hard as shingle. He cut the bindings on Doss's wrists with his belt knife—not a question, not a ceremony, just done—and set the food between them.

"Eat," he said. "You'll be no use to anyone frozen or starved, and I'm not carrying you."

Doss looked at the bread like it might be a trick. Then he took it and ate the way hungry men eat when they've been ashamed of being hungry—fast, hunched, eyes down.

Lena ate slower. He watched the storm come down the flats below them, a gray-white wall moving with terrible patience, erasing the world behind it in long horizontal strokes. Somewhere under that white was the pass. Somewhere beyond the pass was Harrow Bend, and the courts, and the truth sewn into his coat lining in Sera's careful stitches. And somewhere south of here, if Doss was telling even half of what he knew—

"You said there's more," Lena said. "In the narrows. You started to say something before your horse went down. I want it now. All of it."

Doss kept chewing. Swallowed. Wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, which trembled slightly, whether from cold or something else Lena couldn't say.

"You'll wish I hadn't told you."

"I've wished worse things."

* * *

Doss told it in pieces, the way men tell things they've been turning over alone in the dark for too long. He stared at the fire while he talked, and Lena let him stare, because it was easier to confess to a flame than to a face.

"I wasn't born in the valley," Doss said. "I came up out of the territorial prison at Kestrel—served eighteen months for stealing horses I mostly didn't steal. There was nowhere for me after. A man without papers is a man without ground to stand on anywhere in this territory. Amara's people found me half-dead on the southern flats my first winter. They didn't ask questions. That was the whole miracle of it, right there—they didn't ask." He turned the bread heel over in his fingers. "So don't think I don't know what I almost sold."

"I know what you almost sold," Lena said. "I watched you ride out with it. Tell me who bought."

"Verrin." Doss said the name carefully, like testing ice. "That's what he called himself. Company man out of Harrow Bend. Gray coat, gray eyes, talks soft like everything's already decided. He came to me in Harrow Bend three weeks ago—I'd gone down for salt and news, same as every fall. He knew my name, Lena. Knew I'd done time. Knew I wintered somewhere in the high range and wouldn't say where. He laid money on the table and amnesty papers beside it, real ones, stamped, and he said: bring me the location of the settlement and any documents its people are holding, and you walk free with a clean name and five hundred dollars."

"And you took it."

"I took it." Doss's jaw worked. "I told myself it was just a location. Just words on paper. Nobody gets hurt pointing at a map." He laughed, short and ugly. "I believed that for almost a day."

"What changed it?"

"The thing he said when I gave him the cache site." Doss finally looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were wet and furious at themselves. "I told him where the originals were hidden—the stone cairn above the south terraces. And he wrote it down and thanked me, very polite, and then he said, 'And the woman? The clerk?' Like she was a line item. And I said I didn't know anything about a woman, and he smiled at me, this thin smile, and said, 'Doesn't matter. She won't reach the river.' Just like that. Like telling me the weather."

Lena felt something go cold in his chest that had nothing to do with the storm outside.

"He has riders on her route."

"He has riders everywhere." Doss leaned forward, elbows on knees, and now the words came faster, spilling, as if he'd been carrying them too long and his back had finally given. "Listen to me. This isn't a search anymore. It never was. Verrin's got a posse assembling in Harrow Bend right now—paid men, not deputies, company men, maybe thirty of them, maybe more coming. Provisioned for a long stay. Pack strings with wire and dynamite and repeating rifles. They're waiting on one thing: the passes. Verrin's got a weather-man, some old prospector on his payroll, and the man says the snows close the high range by midwinter and open nothing before April. So Verrin's plan is simple. Go in early, hard, before the valley knows winter's even started. Take the documents. Take the leaders. Burn what can't be taken."

"They'd never get a force that size up the range in November."

"That's what I thought too." Doss shook his head. "But it's not one force. It's three columns, moving separate, converging on coordinates I gave them. Verrin split the knowledge so no single man holds the whole picture—including me. All I ever had was the cache site and the main draw. But the other columns have guides, Lena. Men who know the escarpment. One of them is a surveyor."

The word landed in the overhang like a dropped stone.

"One of the missing team," Lena said.

"Two of them, far as I heard names. The survivors didn't all run blind. Some of them went straight to the company and traded what they knew for protection. Verrin's been hunting the others since autumn—the ones who wouldn't play. There was a man named Okafor, the senior instrument tech. Verrin's riders caught him at a line camp on the eastern flats in October." Doss's voice flattened. "He didn't make it to trial. Officially he drowned. Unofficially—" He shrugged, and the shrug said everything the law would never record.

Lena sat with that. Outside, the storm pressed against the ledge and the fire leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered.

"How many survivors are there?"

"Five walked out of the mountains after the storm that killed the three. Two went to the company. Three ran." Doss held up fingers. "Okafor's dead. That leaves two loose, and Verrin wants both. Not because they know anything dangerous—he's got the falsified reports, he wrote half of them himself—but because living witnesses are loose threads, and Verrin doesn't leave threads. He told me once, talking around the fire like we were friends, 'A scandal isn't a document, Mr. Doss. A scandal is a person who remembers.' I've thought about that sentence every night since."

*A scandal is a person who remembers.* Lena turned it over and felt it fit a groove worn twenty years deep. He thought of nine names on a reservoir casualty list, ruled negligence—his negligence—while the inspection reports that would have cleared him went into a company archive and stayed there. He thought of how long a memory could be hunted before it learned to hide.

"The water rights," Lena said. "The aquifer under the escarpment. That's what all this is."

"That's what all this is." Doss nodded grimly. "The territory's drying, everybody knows it. Whoever holds the escarpment aquifer holds the next fifty years of the whole region—towns, ranches, rail lines, all of it drinking from under that valley. The company's been buying water rights across three counties for years, quiet, through front men. The survey wasn't mapping the escarpment, Lena. It was appraising it. And when the junior clerk found the quadrant reports had been doctored—found the real flow measurements showing an aquifer bigger than anything in the territory—she didn't just steal papers. She stole the company's entire play. Those documents prove they knew the value, buried it, filed falsified surveys to declare the land worthless, and started buying it up for pennies from desperate ranchers. Including—" he hesitated, then finished it— "including the land assessments that were used in court. Twenty years ago."

Lena didn't move. The fire popped. Somewhere above the overhang, wind worried at the ridge rocks with a sound like slow grinding teeth.

"You knew," Doss said quietly. "About the connection."

"I suspected it two nights ago, comparing documents with the woman who stole them. Now I know it." He looked at the kid. "Why are you telling me this? You could still be halfway to Harrow Bend collecting your amnesty. I fished you out of a river. That's a debt, not a conversion."

Doss was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its flinch.

"Because I saw his face when he said *she won't reach the river*. And because I sat in that camp eating their food while Verrin's men talked about the valley like it was a problem to be cleaned out before spring planting. There are children up there, Lena. I ate bread baked by a girl maybe ten years old who asked me if I'd ever seen the ocean." His hands closed into fists on his knees. "I told myself a location was just words. But words are the whole weapon. Words got those survey reports falsified. Words got you convicted, if half the stories are true. Words sent me to Kestrel. And my words were going to kill everyone who ever fed me." He looked up. "You want to know why? Because for one hour in that gray coat's presence I forgot that I was a man, and let myself be a price. And I'd rather freeze in this hole than be a price again."

Lena studied him. Twenty years of reading men in bad light, and everything in him said the confession was real—not clean, nothing human was clean, but real. Doss had sold them and hated himself for it, and hatred aimed inward at that intensity either curdles into treachery twice over or burns down to something usable. Lena had bet on men before with less to read.

"All right," he said. "Then here's what your debt costs. Everything. Every detail. Columns, numbers, routes, timetables, supply points, faces. Start with Verrin's timetable."

* * *

It took the better part of two hours, and Lena committed it to memory the way he committed water sources and escape routes—by walking it, in his mind, over and over until the terrain of the information was as fixed as the terrain of the escarpment itself.

Three columns. The first, twelve men under a company captain named Brandt, would come up the main draw—the route Doss had sold, the obvious road, and therefore the one the valley's defenses would watch. Lena allowed himself a thin satisfaction at that; the false trails and rockfall chutes he'd spent days designing were built precisely for men coming up the main draw believing it unguarded.

The second column, eight men, would swing wide west through the red rock maze, guided by one of the turncoat surveyors—a nervous, precise man named Ellery, who knew the escarpment from the government maps and nothing else. Government maps, Lena thought, were a particular kind of blindness. They showed the terrain but not the country.

The third column was the one that mattered. Six picked riders under Verrin himself, with the second guide—a hard, silent ex-ranger named Cade whom Doss described with something like fear—and a single objective that had nothing to do with the valley's main approaches. Their target was the cache. The cairn above the south terraces. Verrin intended to take the original documents first, burn them in front of witnesses if he could manage it, and then negotiate—or worse—from a position where the valley's only proof of its own right to exist was ash.

"When?" Lena said.

"That's the part that should scare you." Doss fed a stick to the fire. "Verrin's not waiting for midwinter. The weather-man read the signs—early snows, but a dry spell behind this front, maybe eight days, maybe ten, high pressure sitting on the basin. Verrin's moving during the dry window. First column crosses the lower flats within the week." He met Lena's eyes. "Not spring. Not midwinter. Days."

Lena did the arithmetic and didn't like any of the sums. Eight days to cross a pass that was closing behind him. Eight days to reach Harrow Bend, find a federal marshal or a judge willing to listen, get papers moving—all while Verrin's people owned the town, owned the telegraph line, owned every ear between here and the territorial capital. Even if he moved like the wind and the storm relented, he'd arrive with evidence into a county where the enemy had three weeks' head start on shaping the story. And behind him, the valley would face three converging columns with one lame-duck defense planner gone and no warning of the western route or the strike at the cache.

Or he turned back. Warned them. Fought.

And the documents—the truth, the only leverage that mattered, the thing that could end this permanently instead of just surviving it—stayed buried in a cairn while the company strangled the territory's courts one more season. Maybe forever. Verrin didn't need to win in the mountains. He only needed to win in Harrow Bend, and he'd already won there.

Both roads cost something irreplaceable. That was the shape of it, plain as the storm wall outside. Lena had spent twenty years learning that the desert rarely offered good choices, only honest ones.

"There's something else," Doss said. "You need to hear it before you decide whatever you're deciding, because I can see you deciding." He hesitated. "The survivors Verrin's hunting. The two still loose. One of them's been seen. South of the range, on the old clerk's route—the southern route, the one the woman would take if she ran the papers out herself. Verrin's got riders sweeping it. If she's out there, and one of those survivors is out there, they're on the same ground, and neither of them knows it."

Lena looked at the fire for a long time.

Sera, alone on the southern route with copies sewn into her coat. A hunted witness wandering the same broken country. Riders sweeping both. And behind all of it, three columns converging on a valley full of children who baked bread for strangers and asked about the ocean.

The storm howled against the ledge, patient as arithmetic.

"Doss," Lena said quietly, "tell me everything you know about the southern sweep. Names. Numbers. Where they're staging." He reached over and broke the last of the bread in two, and handed the boy his half. "Because I think the deadline just moved again."

Outside, the snow fell on the pass that stood open behind them—for now, for days, for perhaps the last time until spring—and began, with the terrible patience of all weather and all companies, to close it.
