## 26. Homecoming in White

**Sera**

She saw him first from the wall above the eastern terraces—a dark smudge against the white slope, moving the way nothing alive should move. Not walking. Lurching. Falling and rising, falling and rising, like something the storm kept throwing back at itself.

"Someone's coming up the draw," she called down to Toma, who was stacking firewood with his mittened hands.

He squinted. "Company?"

"No." Sera's stomach dropped even as she said it. "It's Lena."

They went down together, fast, boots punching through the crust. By the time they reached him he had stopped trying to stand. He was on his hands and knees in the snow, and there was frost in his eyebrows and beard, white feathers of it, and when Sera turned him over his eyes were open but looking at nothing, or looking through her, or looking at something twenty years away.

His lips were moving. She bent close.

"Couldn't get through," he said. The words came out slurred, half-frozen, each one costing something. "Passes closed. Couldn't—"

"I know," she said. "I know. You came back."

"He's coming back," Lena said, as if correcting her, as if this distinction mattered more than his own heartbeat.

Then his eyes rolled and Sera caught his full weight against her chest, and he was so cold it burned, like holding a stone that winter had been sharpening for days.

"Toma! Get Amara. Get blankets. *Move.*"

They carried him between them, sliding on the terraced ice, and Sera thought about the satchel sewn into his coat—the copies, weeks of her careful transcription, salt-stiff thread she'd stitched herself—and how none of it mattered right now, none of it, because the man inside the coat was dying by inches and the whole valley was watching them come up the hill.

Amara met them at the door of the long house. The old woman didn't ask questions. She put her palm flat against Lena's cheek, then his chest, then took his wrist and held it for five seconds with her eyes shut, reading him like weather.

"Frozen deep but not dead-deep," she said. "Strip him. Warm water, not hot. Skin to skin, three of you, rotate every hour. And nobody lets him sleep until he can tell me what day it is."

* * *

**Amara**

The old woman had pulled men back from cold before. It was one of the valley's oldest skills, older than the gardens, older than the stone houses—because the valley had always taken in people who arrived broken, and broken people arrived mostly in winter.

But this one frightened her, and Amara did not frighten easily.

Lena Delacroix shivered violently for two hours—that was good, that was the body fighting—and then around midnight the shivering stopped, and that was bad, and she sat up all night with her hand on his sternum counting breaths while young Sera knelt on his other side doing the same, and neither woman spoke, because speaking would have meant admitting how narrow the margin was.

Near dawn he surfaced. His eyes found Amara first, then the roof beams, then the fire, then Sera. He blinked twice.

"What day is it?" Amara asked.

His lips worked. "Third," he rasped. "Storm started third day out."

"What year?"

He told her. Correctly. Then: "Doss."

"We know," Amara said gently. "Rest now."

"Doss took—" His voice cracked apart. He tried again. "Cache. South fork. Under the split rock. He knows where it's under."

Sera went very still beside him.

"The copies are safe," Amara lied smoothly, pressing his shoulder down as he tried to rise. "Everything's safe. Sleep, ranger. That's an order from your elder."

He fought it for perhaps ten seconds—he was a man who had spent twenty years sleeping with one ear open, she could see that in the architecture of his face—then went under like a stone into deep water.

Amara stayed by the fire long after, feeding it, thinking.

So the boy Doss had run after all. She'd seen it building in him for months, the hunger for a life with papers and wages and a name spoken without a lowered voice. She'd hoped the snow would hold him past his own bitterness. Snow hadn't held anything this year.

And now the valley knew where its dead man lay buried—knew it in the shape of a half-frozen ranger who had turned back at the pass mouth rather than abandon them, which was either the bravest thing she'd ever heard of or the most foolish, and knowing Lena Delacroix, both.

Outside, the storm had eased to fat slow flakes, the kind that meant the sky was emptying itself. Whatever window the weather had given them was closing.

She woke the council at first light.

* * *

**Petra**

Petra Osei had been a corporal once, before the border war went rotten and she walked away from it, before the word *deserter* became the truest thing anyone could call her. Twenty-two years in the valley had softened nothing in her except her hands.

She stood at the meeting-house window with eleven other heads of household and watched smoke rise from the long house chimney, and behind her the room argued.

"Burn the cache," said Hollis, the miller, a cautious man shaped like his own grain sacks. "If Doss knows where it is, the originals are bait. Bait draws wolves. We've survived thirty years by never being worth the trip."

"The originals aren't just documents anymore," Petra said without turning around. "They're the only reason anyone outside these walls will ever leave us alone. Burn them and we're hiding forever, and hiding forever means waiting forever for the next survey team, the next eviction order, the next Verrin."

"You don't know there'll be a next one."

"There's always a next one," said old Maret from her corner. Maret had been in the valley longer than anyone; she remembered the first cabins. "My father ran from land seizure in '09. My husband ran from debt. I ran from a man. There is always a next one. The question is whether we meet them running or standing."

"And standing," Hollis shot back, "gets us killed. We're forty-one adults and some children against whatever the company hires. Petra, you were a soldier. Tell them the arithmetic."

Petra finally turned. She let the silence do some work first.

"The arithmetic says we lose any open fight," she agreed. "Open fight. But we're not going to fight open, are we? We've got a man in that bed who spent twenty years learning every fold of this range the way you learn your own hands. Rockfall chokes. False trails. Decoy fires. Ground that swallows horses. If we're going to be found anyway—and we will be, Doss saw to that—then I'd rather be found difficult."

"Difficult," Hollis repeated sourly. "That's your plan. Be difficult."

"It's the best plan small people ever had." Petra looked around the room. "Ask the empire that broke its teeth on the mountain tribes. Ask anyone. Small and difficult beats large and comfortable every time the ground is ours."

Silence again, but a different silence. Petra had learned to hear the difference between a room refusing an idea and a room swallowing one.

From the doorway, a small voice: "Is the ranger going to die?"

Everyone turned. It was Nils, the ferryman's boy, eight years old, barefoot when he shouldn't be, as usual.

"No," Petra said.

"How do you know?"

"Because men who turn around and come back don't die in their beds. They haven't earned it yet."

Nils considered this with the gravity of a judge, nodded once, and left.

Hollis sighed like a man surrendering a fortress. "Put it to the vote," he said. "But someone tell me why I'm losing arguments to a woman who deserted her own army."

"Because," Petra said, "I deserted a bad cause and stayed for a good one. That's the whole difference between me and the company's men. And it's about to be the whole difference between us and everyone they send."

**Sera**

On the second day Lena's fever came, and Sera sat with him through the worst of it, spooning melted snow-water and broth into him while he fought enemies who weren't in the room.

Mostly he fought the reservoir.

She learned things in those fever hours that no testimony would ever capture. He talked to the nine dead by name—she counted them later, nine names, repeated in rotation like a rosary—apologizing to each one in turn, explaining, arguing, pleading with people twenty years gone. He talked about a courtroom where the water company's lawyers sat on the same side of the aisle as the judge's clerk. He talked about a train ticket he'd bought and never used, and a town called Harrow Bend that had watched him leave with a paper saying *negligent*, and how he'd walked into the escarpment the same week and simply... not come out.

At the worst of it, near midnight of the second night, he grabbed her wrist with shocking strength and said, very clearly:

"They'll ask you if you're sure. Don't be sure. Be *right*. Sure is for people who haven't checked."

Then he collapsed back and slept like an honest man, and Sera sat in the firelight with her own pulse hammering and realized her cheeks were wet.

She had known him for less than a season. She had tracked him across the flats believing he was her hunter, been captured by his fairness, argued for his life in front of armed scouts, taught him her cipher codes with their heads bent over the same page by lamplight. Somewhere in all of it—she couldn't name the exact hour—the thief and the ranger had become simply two people keeping the same secret warm between them.

Amara appeared in the doorway with fresh bandages and read her face in one glance, the way she read everything.

"Don't decide anything while he's sick," the old woman said, sitting down across from her. "Sick men make saints of themselves, and women watching sick men make promises the healthy man has to live up to. Wait until he can carry wood again. Then see if he still looks like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a door you want to walk through."

Sera laughed despite herself, wiped her face, and took the basin to change the water. Outside, the last of the storm clouds were tearing apart over the western rim, showing hard blue sky, and the whole valley glittered under new snow, beautiful and exposed, a secret no longer kept by anything except distance.

Distance, she thought, that Doss was spending right now, at a gallop, selling them by the mile.

* * *

**Toma**

Toma Ferro was fifteen and had lived his whole life in the valley, which meant he had lived his whole life inside a rumor the world believed was empty rock. He had never seen a government man. He had never seen a gun pointed in anger. Until this winter, the word *outside* had meant Harrow Bend, where they traded wool for salt twice a year, and even Harrow Bend had seemed like the edge of the world.

Now he stood watch on the north rim with his grandfather's old rifle he barely knew how to load, scanning the white flats, and the word *outside* meant something else entirely.

Below him, the valley went about its business with a discipline that would have surprised anyone who thought fugitives were soft. The autumn harvest stores were being inventoried and re-inventoried. Petra had the younger men cutting sightlines through the timber, opening lanes and closing others, rearranging the forest into a sentence the company's men wouldn't know how to read. Smoke from the houses was being routed through the kilns so it rose cold and thin, almost invisible against the cliffs. Even the children had jobs—Nils and the other kids were learning which boulders, tipped with iron bars, would start slides.

Nobody said the word *war*. They said *preparations*, they said *the season*, they said *if visitors come*. But Toma carried the rifle and knew what it weighed.

On his third day of watch, the fever broke in the long house below, and word came up the line like wind through grass: *he's awake, he ate, he asked for a map.*

Toma grinned at the empty flats like an idiot, alone on his rock, and felt—for the first time in his life, though he couldn't have explained it—like the valley had a spine.

* * *

**Amara**

On the fourth morning Lena Delacroix walked out of the long house under his own power, gray-faced and thinner, leaning once on the doorframe and once on Sera's offered arm, refusing everything else.

Amara met him in the yard, where the council had gathered without arranging to, the way councils do.

He looked at their faces and understood immediately—she could see the understanding arrive, the way it must have arrived at the pass mouth when he turned back. These people had voted on something. These people had decided something. And they were standing here looking at him.

"You should still be flat on your back," Amara said.

"I've been flat for four days. The flats and I have an understanding." His voice was gravel, but steady. "Tell me what I walked back into."

Amara let the pause stretch, because elders are permitted pauses.

"Doss reached the company before the storm sealed the south routes—or we have to assume so. He sold them the cache location and what he knows of our numbers. Verrin will come early, while the passes are merely terrible instead of impossible. Petra thinks within the fortnight." She watched him absorb it without flinching. "The council voted to prepare. All of it—trails, traps, decoys. Petra's leading the ground craft. What the council could not decide, ranger, was who leads the rest of it. Who decides where we stand and where we bleed."

Lena looked around the yard—at Petra with her soldier's stillness, at Hollis already shaking his head, at Sera with her arms crossed against the cold and her eyes not leaving him, at the children pretending not to listen from behind the woodpile.

"I'm not one of you," he said. "Twenty days ago you captured me at the gate."

"And argued over killing you," Amara agreed. "Democracy is ugly. Vote's a vote. So answer the question, Delacroix. The passes are closed behind you. There is no mission left to fail. There's only us." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to the register she used for truths. "You turned around. Now find out what you turned around *for*."

The wind moved through the terraces. Somewhere below, a child laughed, sharp and bright in the frozen air.

Lena exhaled, long and slow, a man setting down weight.

"Show me your maps," he said. "All of them. Especially the ones that are wrong."

And that was how the valley's war began—not with a shot, but with a half-frozen stranger asking to see the mistakes.
