## 27. Fortifying the Escarpment

The morning after Verrin's scouts were first sighted on the rim, Lena stood at the valley gate with a stick of charcoal and a sheet of tanned hide pinned to the stone wall, and for the first time in twenty years he drew maps for other people to read.

It felt strange in his hands. He had spent two decades unmaking himself from every record, every register, every memory that might reach back to Harrow Bend. A man who wants to disappear does not draw. A man who wants to disappear does not stand in front of thirty faces and say *here is where we bleed them, here is where we turn them, here is where we let them go*. And yet here he was, charcoal squeaking on hide, and nobody in the crowd looked at him like a stranger. They looked at him like a man who knew the ground.

Which, he supposed, he did. He knew this escarpment better than the company men who'd paid for its surveys and better than the government clerks who'd filed those surveys in drawers marked WORTHLESS. He'd walked every drainage and every goat track from the rim rock to the snow line, mostly to make sure no one was following him, sometimes just because the walking was the only honest thing in his life. Now all those miles were going to work. It was like discovering that a scar could be a tool.

"All right," he said. "First rule. We are not fighting a war. We are running a deception that occasionally involves rocks."

Amara stood at the edge of the gathering with her arm bound and slung, her face still gray from the fever that had followed the wound, but her eyes sharp. Sera sat on the wall beside the map, the satchel — or its empty twin, the originals were cached — across her knees, watching him the way she watched weather.

"Verrin has maybe forty men," Lena went on. "Doss gave us partial truth, which means Verrin knows the valley exists and roughly where, but not how to get in. That's our whole advantage. He's got numbers. We've got ground. Ground beats numbers if you're patient and you're willing to be cruel to his boots before you're cruel to anything else."

A laugh went through the crowd, tight and nervous. Good. Nervous laughter meant they were listening.

* * *

They started with the false trails.

There were three ways into the valley that a stranger could plausibly find: the main draw, the scree ramp on the north shoulder, and the slot canyon that the valley folk called the Throat. There was also a fourth way — a shelf of stone above the main draw that looked, from below, like solid wall and was, if you knew the two handholds and the ledge behind the fallen slab, a stair. Lena had found it in his third year out here, when he'd been running from a posse that never existed, and had kept it to himself ever since. He drew it on the hide now, and watched the valley folk exchange glances — their hidden door, given away — and watched Amara nod once, deliberately, permission granted. The hiding was over. That was the price and the point.

"Verrin will come up the main draw," Lena said. "It's the obvious route, and Doss only ever traveled it. So we make the draw *more* obvious. We cut sign on it. Hoofprints, drag marks, a wagon rut where no wagon could go — cut it shallow, cut it old-looking. A trail that's been used for years."

"You want him to trust it," Sera said.

"I want him to *want* to trust it. Men who've marched a week in snow want the easy answer. You give a tired man an easy answer and he'll defend it with his life." He tapped the draw on the map. "Two miles up, the draw forks at the hoodoos. Left fork climbs to a box canyon with no way out. We make the left fork look *more* used — more sign, a cold fire ring, maybe a tin cup left where a scout will find it. Right fork is the real approach, and it gets nothing. No sign at all. Bare rock. Men trust a trail that's been swept clean less than they trust one that's been faked, but they trust a blank wall least of all, so we don't blank the right fork entirely. One set of old tracks, weathered, going nowhere. Enough to say *someone passes here sometimes*. Not enough to say *this is the way*."

Doss stood at the back of the crowd. He'd barely spoken since the narrows, since Lena had hauled him out of the flood with a rope and both their lives hanging on it. He was thinner now, quieter, doing the work nobody assigned him — carrying water, splitting wood — the penance of a man who'd sold his people and been forgiven too fast for it to feel real. When Lena glanced at him, Doss said, "He'll send scouts up all three routes first. Verrin's not the kind to commit blind."

"No," Lena agreed. "He'll probe. That's fine. That's what the probes are for." He drew a small circle above the left fork. "Every probe he sends up the false fork costs him a day and a little faith. Every probe that comes up the real route, we let see exactly what we want seen: a few defenders, badly placed, a camp that looks undermanned. Then we pull back and let him build the picture we've drawn for him. By the time he commits his main force, he'll be marching into a story we wrote."

"And the Throat?" asked a woman named Pell, one of the scouts, a former army farrier with forearms like cable.

"The Throat is our gift to him." Lena let that sit. "If he's smart, he'll find it eventually — it's the only other route a mountaineer would rate. So we let him find it. We let it look like our back door, lightly watched, because that's what a back door is. And the Throat has one property the draw doesn't." He looked at Pell, who was already smiling.

"Flood-carved," she said. "Walls sixty feet. One man with a pry bar and the right boulder can close it like a gate."

"One boulder, wedged above the narrows pinch, one rope, one man who knows the timing. We let a column get inside the pinch and we drop the wall behind them. Then they're not an invading force anymore. They're a line of men in a stone corridor with one exit — the one we're standing at." He didn't say *and then we decide what mercy looks like*. He didn't have to. The valley folk had argued mercy and killing around a hundred fires since the vote, and what they'd settled on, uneasily, was Lena's version: bleed them, blind them, terrify them, and send them home alive to tell a story so bad that no company man wants to hear the valley's name again. Fear was a weapon that didn't need burying after.

* * *

The rockfall traps took four days to build, and they were the work Lena loved best, because they were the work he'd been doing alone for twenty years without knowing it.

He'd spent two decades reading slopes. Which faces shed their frost in the morning sun and which held it. Which talus fields sang before they moved — that dry, grinding whisper, like a river deciding — and which let go without a word. Which overhangs were limestone lies and which were honest. He'd crossed the escarpment by reading it, and now he taught a dozen valley folk to write on it instead.

The principle was simple: the mountains here wanted to fall down. Gravity had been working on this escarpment for a million years, and all the company's money and all Verrin's rifles were nothing next to that. You didn't have to fight forty men. You had to help the mountain do what it already intended.

The first trap went above the real fork of the draw — a perched slab the size of a cabin, held in place by a wedge of shattered rock that Lena judged, after two days of watching it in sun and shadow, to be carrying maybe a winter's worth of trust. They didn't blast it. Blasting told the whole canyon you were there. They picked the wedge apart by hand, a little each day, with hammers wrapped in hide to muffle the sound, and propped what was left with a dry juniper trunk cut to fail. When the time came, one man with an axe would take out the prop and the mountain would take out the road.

The second was cruder and crueler: a snow slope above the Throat's approach, loaded by wind all season, with a cornice at the top like a drawn bow. Pell and her crew cut a track to the cornice's root so a single runner with a shovel could trigger the slide. "Avalanches don't kill many," Lena told them, "if you know what you're doing. But they bury trails for a week, and they make every man under that slope believe the mountain is on the other side. That's worth more than bodies."

The third was the one he was proudest of, and the simplest. The scree field on the north shoulder — half a mile of loose stone, angled at thirty-five degrees, the kind of slope that took a man an hour to climb and gave him away with every step. You couldn't trap it. But you could *tune* it. Lena walked it with the young scouts — Ferro, who was sixteen and all elbows, and a quiet girl called Wren who could move over talus without a whisper — and showed them where to kick loose the key stones, the ones that sat on top of the slope's long argument with itself. "One stone here starts ten stones. Ten start a hundred. By the time it reaches the bottom it's a rockfall, and every man on that slope will be flat on his belly listening to the mountain come apart above him. Nobody charges uphill after that. Nobody charges uphill *anywhere* after that, that day. Fear travels."

Ferro asked him, breathless, whether he'd ever set a trap like this before, back in his ranger days, and Lena thought about the reservoir, and the nine, and the hearing where the company's engineers had explained with charts how the failure was negligence, his negligence, the ranger's negligence, and how he had sat there with no charts of his own and no words that could stand against theirs.

"Once," he said. "The wrong way." And he didn't explain, and Ferro had the sense not to ask, and they went back to kicking stones loose in the long cold afternoon, and the mountain hummed its low grinding song, and Lena felt something he had no better word for than purpose. For twenty years his knowledge had been a wall he hid behind. Now it was a roof over other people. The same skill. Different direction. It was astonishing, really, how little had to change — just the question of who the miles were for.

* * *

The decoy fires were Sera's idea first, and Lena made them better, and the two of them worked on it three nights running at the map wall while the valley slept, and neither of them mentioned the other thing that was building between them, the thing they'd agreed to postpone until after the company men came and went — if they came and went.

"You can't hide a valley this size in winter," Sera said, the first night. "Smoke gives you away. Forty hearths, and the air inversion in the draw lays the smoke down flat, you can see it from the rim for miles. Doss will have told them about the smoke."

"So we don't hide it," Lena said. "We *advertise* it — wrong." He took the charcoal and marked six points on the hide: two in the main draw, one in the false box canyon, one on a bench above the Throat, and two in country where nobody lived at all, on the far side of the north shoulder, in a basin of dead timber. "A settlement of this size, burning this much wood, should show six or seven smoke columns. So we show six or seven. But only two of them are real."

The decoy fires were engineering, not theater. Green aspen cut and stacked on dry spruce gave a heavy white smoke that looked, at three miles, exactly like a hearth burning wet winter wood. They built stone fireboxes at the decoy points, big enough to burn a day on one loading, and ran the timing so the columns rose and fell the way real fires did — dying toward morning, building at the cook hours, flaring at dusk. Wren, who turned out to have a pyromaniac's patience and a poet's eye for smoke, took charge of the far basin and treated her two decoys like pets.

The real trick was the cold fire ring in the false box canyon. "Verrin's scouts will find that fire ring," Lena said, "and it will be *warm*. Not hot — warm, like a fire an hour dead. And they'll think: someone was here this morning, this camp is live, the valley's heart is up this fork. And while they're standing there warming their hands on our lie—" he tapped the perched slab above the fork "—the mountain will be having its say."

Sera looked at the map for a long time. "You've done this before," she said. "Not the traps. The — making people see what isn't there."

"I did it for one person for twenty years," Lena said. "Me. Turns out the skills scale."

She laughed — a short, real laugh, the first he'd heard from her since the scouts had brought word of riders on the rim. Then she went serious in the way she did, all at once, like weather. "Lena. When this is over. If it goes wrong — the satchel, the cache, you know where—"

"I know where."

"Then it isn't over," she said. "Whatever happens to us. It isn't over."

He looked at her, at the young woman who had run through a blizzard with a company's crimes sewn against her ribs, and he said, "It'll go right," and neither of them quite believed him, and both of them pretended to, and that was its own kind of contract, sealed and witnessed by the map.

* * *

On the sixth day they ran the whole valley through it like a play, and it went badly, and that was the point of the sixth day.

The signal system failed first — mirror flashes from the rim watchers arrived at the gate after the mock column had already reached the hoodoos, because the relay man had frozen his hands and couldn't work the glass. The Throat crew dropped their practice boulder — a small one, a rehearsal — and it bounced wrong and crushed the ladder instead of closing the pinch, and Pell spent an hour cursing in two languages before they rigged a better cradle. The decoy fires, lit on schedule, made five columns instead of six because Wren's far-basin fire caught the dead timber around it and threatened to burn a quarter mile of the basin, which at least proved the smoke worked.

Lena stood at the gate and watched it all fail and felt — not despair. Something adjacent to joy. Because the failures were happening now, with charcoal and rope and rehearsal boulders, instead of in three weeks with Verrin's column in the draw and no second chances.

"Again," he said, and the valley groaned, and they ran it again, and it went less badly. And again. By the fourth rehearsal the mirrors spoke in seconds, the Throat closed like a jaw, the false fork swallowed a mock scout whole, and the smoke columns rose and fell over the valley like breathing, six of them, steady and false as a company ledger.

That evening Amara found him at the map wall, rolling up the hide. Her arm was healing; she carried it easier now. She stood beside him a while without speaking, looking at the charcoal lines — her valley, the place that had survived by being nothing on paper, now defended in ink by a man who'd spent his life being nothing on paper too.

"You know what you've done," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've made us visible," Lena said. "That's what all this is. False trails, false fires — you can't lie to a man until he's looking at you."

"Yes." She touched the map, lightly, at the valley's blank center. "And I'd have voted against it a year ago. Maybe a month ago." She looked at him. "But hiding was always a loan, not a home. The survey found us. Doss left us. The world was always going to come up that draw eventually, wanting what's under our feet. Better it comes now, when we're ready, than in twenty years when we've forgotten we were ever ready for anything." She paused. "You've given them that. Readiness. They walk taller. Did you notice?"

He had. Ferro had stopped slouching. Pell's crew moved like they owned the mountain, because now, in a way, they did. Even Doss had straightened somewhere in the last week, as if the work of defending the people he'd betrayed was the only rope strong enough to haul him out of what he'd done.

"They gave me twenty years of ground," Lena said. "I'm just reading it back to them."

Amara snorted — a fine, unimpressed elder's sound — and left him with the map.

He stayed a while after, alone at the wall in the blue dusk, looking at the routes he'd drawn and the traps he'd set and the six columns of smoke he'd invented, and he let himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. Verrin had forty men, maybe more, and a company behind him with deeper pockets and longer reach, and a fixer's patience. The valley had eighty-three souls, a handful of rifles, and a mountain that was, as of this week, on their side. It was not good arithmetic. It was, however, for the first time in twenty years, *his* arithmetic — a problem with his name on it, chosen, not imposed.

He was rolling the hide when the rim mirror flashed from the north shoulder — three quick flashes, the signal they'd drilled, the one that meant *riders sighted, not the main column, small party, moving fast*.

Lena stood very still, counting the flashes again as they repeated. Small party. Moving fast. Coming down the north shoulder, the one route he'd left almost bare — the one with nothing on it but old tracks and the tuned scree.

Not a probe. Probes didn't move at night, and the light was already going.

Someone was coming to the valley who already knew the way.
