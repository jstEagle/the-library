## 28. Sera's Choice

The maps were spread across Amara's table when the idea finally found its shape.

Sera had been staring at them for three days—since the council voted to send Lena out with the copies, since Doss bolted with a horse and half their secrets, since the word *posse* stopped being an abstraction and became a thing with boots and rifles and Verrin's cold arithmetic behind it. Three days of watching Lena redraw the northern routes in charcoal, over and over, hunting for a line through the high passes that the storm hadn't already erased.

There wasn't one. She could see it in his shoulders, the way they'd gone stiff as fence posts. He was going to try anyway, because he'd voted for it, because he'd stood up in front of forty people and said *a valley that hides forever has already lost*, and now the weather was calling his bluff.

So she said it.

"There's another way."

Lena looked up from the table. The lamplight caught the gray coming into his beard—he was older than she'd first guessed, older than the stories made him, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Another way through the north?"

"Through the south." She pulled the western map toward her and turned it so he could read it upside down, the way clerks read everything. "Nobody's looking south. Verrin's whole force came up the eastern approach and split toward the main draw. He thinks the valley's a bowl with one lid. Everyone does."

"Everyone includes you?"

"I was a clerk," Sera said. "I spent four years filing survey quadrants for a company that thought paper was more real than ground. I know what they believe about this country." She put her finger on the map, on the thin unmarked space below the escarpment's southern shoulder. "Before I ran, I scouted a route. It was my job—checking access trails for the survey equipment, confirming which drainages could take pack trains. There's a way down through the Redwall country, along the old sheep drives, that comes out near the dry farms two days' ride from Harrow Bend."

Lena studied the map the way he studied tracks—not reading it so much as interrogating it. "You've walked it?"

"Twice. Once going out, once coming back with the satchel strapped under my coat while I pretended to be collecting lichen samples." She almost smiled at the memory. Almost. "It's hard country. Narrow in places. But it stays below the snowline until the last fork, and even there the drifts won't be chest-deep like the pass. A single person moving light could make Harrow Bend in six days. Maybe five."

"And the northern crossing is nine," Lena said quietly. "If it's possible at all."

"It isn't possible," Sera said. "Not anymore. You know it. The storm closed it yesterday afternoon—I watched the cloud come over the shoulder from the lookout rock. You'd be walking into a white room with no walls."

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Lena Delacroix that the stories never quite captured: he didn't waste breath defending a position once the ground shifted under it. He just went still, the way water goes still before it finds a new channel.

"So we change the plan," he said.

"We split it." She'd rehearsed this part in her head all morning, standing at the garden wall with her hands in the frozen dirt, and now that it was coming out of her mouth it sounded both smaller and larger than she'd imagined. "You stay. You're the only one who knows how to fight Verrin off without turning this valley into a graveyard—you told the council yourself, drive them out, don't slaughter them, and nobody else here can do that. And I take the copies south. Alone."

"No."

The word came out flat and fast, before he'd clearly decided to say it, and something in Sera's chest loosened at the sight of it—the great careful ranger, caught reacting instead of thinking.

"You just argued me out of the north pass," she said.

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because I'm not—" He stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like footing on scree. "Because if the defense fails, someone has to carry the truth out anyway. If I'm holding the line and you're gone, and we lose, then everything we buried in the cache dies with us. Two couriers means two chances. One person carrying copies south while the originals stay hidden—that's not splitting the risk, Sera. That's doubling it."

"That's exactly what splitting the risk means."

"It means if they catch you, they get copies *and* learn the route."

"They catch me, they get papers sewn into a decoy lining and a woman who doesn't know where the cache is." She held his eyes. "I memorized what matters. The rest is salted meat and stitching. You taught me that yourself, when we prepared your kit. Trust made tangible, you called it."

"I was wrong to teach you that."

"No," she said. "You weren't. It's the truest thing anyone's taught me since I left the company."

* * *

They took it to Amara, because the valley had learned in one hard season that no plan belonged to any single person anymore.

Amara listened from her chair by the fire, her wounded arm bound tight against her ribs, her face doing what it always did—giving nothing away until she'd turned the whole thing over twice. The skirmish at the false trails had cost her more than blood; Sera saw it in how carefully she moved now, how she'd started measuring her sentences before spending them.

"A girl alone in company country," Amara said at last. "With the only living copy of the evidence."

"With a dead copy," Sera corrected. "A convincing fake. The real pages stay here, in the cache, where only the three of us know the marker."

"And if Verrin takes the valley?"

"Then it won't matter where the papers are."

Amara's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind she allowed herself when someone said a hard true thing plainly. "You sound like him." A nod toward Lena. "Twenty years ago he would have said the same."

"I know," Sera said. "That's partly why I trust the plan. He taught me how to think about losing."

Lena stood at the window with his back to the fire, and Sera watched him watch the snowfields above the valley, and understood that he was doing the arithmetic he hated: weighing her life against the valley's odds, and finding no column where everyone came home. That was the price of the kind of honesty he dealt in. You couldn't tell people the truth about risk and then flinch when they volunteered for it.

"The southern route," he said finally, without turning around. "Show me every camp, every water source, every place the walls close in. All of it. Tonight. If I'm going to sit here and let you walk into company country alone, I want to have walked it with you first, in my head, a hundred times."

"You could come with me," Sera said, and heard her own voice betray her—too quick, too hopeful. "Leave the defense to Doss's replacement and the traps. Come south together."

"Doss's replacement is a nineteen-year-old who's fired a rifle at tin cans." Now he did turn, and the firelight showed her something raw in his face. "And if I leave, and the line breaks, I'll spend the rest of my life knowing I ran from the first thing I ever chose to stand for. No. This is the shape of it. You go south. I hold the draw. We both live long enough to meet in Harrow Bend when the thaw comes."

"When the thaw comes," Sera repeated. It sounded like a vow. It sounded like a distance too far to measure in miles.

* * *

They worked through the night in the map room, and it was the closest she had ever been to another human being without touching them.

He made her walk the route aloud, segment by segment, and he interrupted constantly—not to correct her, but to test her. What do you do if the slot narrows and there's fresh sign of riders ahead. Where do you sleep if the seep is frozen. How do you cross the sheep-drive cut if there's a man on the rim above you. At first her answers came out halting, clerk's answers, and he'd shake his head and make her start the segment over. By midnight her answers came out sounding like his. By two in the morning she caught herself answering a question he hadn't asked yet, and he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a door opening after years—and said, "All right. You're ready."

"I'm not."

"Nobody is. Ready is a story we tell ourselves after the fact." He rolled up the charcoal sketch of her route and handed it to her to burn. "Memorize it and burn it. Same rule as the papers."

She held the paper a moment longer than she needed to. "Lena. If I don't make it—"

"You'll make it."

"If I don't. I need to say something now, while it's easy to lie about later." Her heart was going hard against her ribs, harder than it had in the narrows, harder than it had the night she ran from the survey camp with the satchel beating against her back like a second heart. "These weeks here. Learning the routes from you. Watching you name those nine people like they were your own family. Watching you refuse Verrin." She stopped. Made herself keep going. "I spent four years being invisible on purpose. Filing things. Erasing myself into other people's paperwork. You looked at me in the narrows like I was a person worth saving, and then you looked at me here like I was a person worth listening to, and nobody—"

"Sera."

"—nobody had ever done both, and I need you to know that whatever happens on the southern route, that's not—"

"Sera." He crossed the room and took her hands, and his were rough and cold and steady. "I'm sixty feet away from you right now, and I'm already afraid of the day you walk out of this valley. Don't make it worse by making me say it first."

"Say what first?"

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the wind worked at the shutters, patient as erosion. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, ranger's voice, the voice he used for truths that didn't bend.

"After the thaw. Harrow Bend. There's a café on the river side that serves coffee strong enough to strip paint, and I haven't sat down in a place like that in twenty years because sitting down in public was the same as being found." His grip tightened slightly. "When this is done—when the indictments fall and the aquifer holds and your name is a witness's name instead of a thief's—I'm going to sit down in that café. And I'd take it kindly if you were sitting across from me."

Sera discovered she was crying, which annoyed her enormously, and laughing, which surprised her completely. "That's the least romantic proposal of a meeting I've ever heard."

"I'm a desert ranger. We court by waypoints."

"Then consider it confirmed." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Waypoint set. Café on the river side. Don't be late."

"I've never been late to a waypoint in my life."

* * *

The next two days passed in stitches and salt.

The valley women who did the sewing worked by lamplight in Amara's house, building the false lining into Sera's coat panel by panel—a decoy dossier, plausible enough to fool a hurried search, worthless enough to protect the cache if it was found. Sera sat for the fittings like a mannequin, arms out, while needles flashed, and listened to the women talk about their children and their gardens and the particular stubbornness of high-country onions, and understood that this was what belonging felt like from the inside: not a declaration, but a chair kept warm for you at a worktable.

Lena provisioned her pack himself, item by item, refusing help. Dried meat wrapped in waxed cloth. Pinon pitch for boot repair. A strip of jerky tucked into an inner pocket "for the day you can't make yourself stop and eat." He weighed everything twice and cut the weight a third time, muttering, and when she teased him about it he said, "Every ounce you carry is a mile you'll feel on day four," and she wrote it down in her head next to all the others.

On the third morning, the sky came up hard and clear, the kind of cold blue that meant the storm had spent itself and the southern window was open—for now.

They walked out together as far as the lower terraces, past the stone houses with their smoke rising straight in the still air, past the garden rows sleeping under straw. The whole valley seemed to turn out along the fences without being asked: Amara leaning on her good arm, the scouts, the children silent and wide-eyed, the old deserters touching their hats. Nobody cheered. This wasn't a departure for glory. Everyone understood it was a woman walking out alone into the country that wanted her caught, carrying the only future any of them had that fit inside a coat.

At the fence line, Lena stopped.

"Route check," he said.

"Redwall drainage, two days. Sheep-drive cut, day three, water at the potholes unless they're iced, then melt snow, don't eat it dry. Day four the country opens and I travel dark and camp cold. Day five, the dry farms. Day six, Harrow Bend." She recited it without breathing hard. "Federal investigators, care of the journalist. Ask for Marta Ellery. Say the escarpment sent me."

"Say the last escarpment sent you," he corrected. "It's the phrase he'll know. It's mine."

"The last escarpment," she repeated, sealing it.

He reached out and touched the coat over the false lining, once, lightly, the way you'd check a knot you'd tied for someone you loved. Then he stepped back, and his face did the thing it did when he laid something down—when he'd named the nine dead, when he'd refused Verrin at the gate—steady and open and unbearably honest.

"Walk it like you walked it in the map room," he said. "One segment at a time. Never the whole route. Just the next mile."

"Just the next mile," Sera said.

She shouldered the pack. She turned south, toward the red walls and the old sheep drives and the six days of country between her and the law, and she did not look back, because looking back was a luxury for people whose hearts weren't set on a waypoint.

Behind her, faint on the cold air, she heard him say it—one ranger to another, a send-off and a promise in three words:

"Next mile, Sera."

She took it.
