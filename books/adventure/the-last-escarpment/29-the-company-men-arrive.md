## 29. The Company Men Arrive

Verrin counted men the way other men counted money.

Fourteen riders strung out along the frozen creek below the escarpment's northern shoulder, breath steaming in the gray light of a February dawn that had come too cold even for the crows. He sat his horse apart from them, the way he always did—not from vanity, though there was some of that—but because a man who rode with the others became one of the others, and Verrin had spent thirty years making sure he was never one of the others.

"Rathbone," he said.

The man who rode up at the sound of his name was broad, weathered, and stupid in the useful way—stupid enough to follow orders without questions, smart enough to survive following them. "Sir."

"Doss said the draw opens on the north side. A creek fork, two bluffs like a gate." Verrin unfolded the map he'd taken off the traitor before the man had been paid and dismissed—or rather, before Doss had been paid and believed himself dismissed. Verrin kept his own counsel about what happened to tools after they stopped being useful. "He also said the valley folk would have moved their caches by now."

"He said they'd know he talked."

"They'll know nothing." Verrin folded the map away. "They'll suspect. There's a difference, and the difference is worth ten thousand dollars to the company, so we're going to be precise."

Below them, the escarpment rose out of the winter flats like the spine of some animal that had lain down a thousand years ago and never gotten up. Verrin had read the old reports—the ones the survey team had filed before it stopped filing anything—and the reports described worthless rock, dry canyons, no water worth piping. The company had paid for those reports. The company had then paid to make sure no second set was ever written.

And now somewhere behind all that rock was a girl with a satchel full of paper that could hang three directors and a senator, and a settlement of squatters who had no legal existence and therefore no legal protection, and a ranger named Delacroix whom Verrin's predecessors had ruined twenty years ago and who had, inconveniently, refused to die quietly in some border town.

"Mr. Vellacott wants this finished before thaw," Rathbone said. "He said—"

"I know what he said." Verrin nudged his horse forward. "He said make it look like bandit trouble. He said leave no paperwork. He said the aquifer stays buried until the land options close in April." He glanced back at the fourteen riders. "What he doesn't understand is that none of that matters if we do the work correctly. Bandits don't need to be invented when you're willing to become one."

The column moved out along the creek, hooves crunching on snow crusted hard enough to hold a horse but not quite hard enough to hide its prints. Verrin rode last, watching the tracks of his own men fill the morning, and thought, as he always did at the start of an operation, that every job began with a clean page and ended with a dirty one, and the only question was whose hands got dirty first.

His answer, historically, was everyone else's.

* * *

Lena heard them before he saw them—the particular wrongness of hoofbeats in a country where nobody traveled in February.

He was halfway up the false trail he'd built through December and January, a route stitched together out of game paths and old sheep drives and one stretch of canyon floor where he'd laid down boot prints and horse tracks in soft mud and let the freeze set them like plaster. From the ridge above the third switchback he could see four miles down the north draw, and what he saw made him go still against the rock the way he'd learned to go still twenty years ago, when stillness was the only thing between him and a rope.

Riders. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, moving in a column with the loose discipline of hired men rather than the ragged spread of prospectors or the tight wedge of a posse with a sheriff at its head. They wore no badges. They carried rifles in saddle scabbards and one of them led a pack string with canvas-wrapped loads that were either supplies or something less innocent.

Company men. He'd known they would come—Doss's confession had bought them days, maybe a week, and Lena had spent those days the way he'd spent twenty years: reading ground. But knowing a storm was coming and standing in its path were different animals, and his chest did something complicated and unwelcome when he counted the riders again and found the number unchanged.

Fifteen men. The valley could field eleven fighters, and two of them were boys under sixteen, and Amara was still binding her ribs where a fall on the ice had cracked them in January.

He slid back down the switchbacks without leaving a print of his own, took the hidden traverse below the rimrock, and ran the last mile to the valley gate with his blood loud in his ears.

Sera met him at the watch post, wrapped in a wool coat too big for her, a spyglass already in her hand. She'd been up since before light. She'd been up every night since Doss left, sleeping in snatches by the fire with her boots on.

"How many?" she asked.

"Fifteen. Maybe a scout or two ahead I couldn't see." He caught his breath. "They're taking the north draw. Straight toward the false trail."

"That's your trail. That's the one that ends at the—" She stopped herself. Even here, even now, she didn't say the word *cache* aloud. The originals were cached in a place only three people knew, and Sera treated that knowledge like a live coal she carried in her mouth.

"The one that ends nowhere," Lena said. "If they're smart, they'll figure that out in a day. If they're Verrin, they'll figure it out faster."

"You've seen him?"

"No. But I've seen men like him." He looked north, toward the wall of the range, white and indifferent. "Men who count things. Come on. We need the council, and we need it now."

* * *

Verrin found the first sign at midday, and it pleased him.

Boot prints. Horse tracks, two horses, maybe three, pressed into a drift that hadn't melted since it fell—which meant the tracks were fresh, made within the last week, maybe the last few days. A trail running up the north draw toward a notch in the bluffs that matched Doss's description exactly.

"Someone's been busy," Rathbone said, dismounting to study the prints.

"Someone's been careless," Verrin corrected. He couched beside the trail and did not touch it. The prints were crisp-edged, unweathered, laid down in mud that had frozen hard within hours of being made. Fresh work. Very fresh.

Too fresh.

He stood and looked up the draw. The canyon narrowed ahead into the notch, walls rising sheer on both sides, and the trail ran straight and inviting up the middle like a road someone wanted him to take.

Doss had told them about the valley's approaches. Doss had told them about caches and watch posts and the general shape of the place. What Doss had not told them—because Doss didn't know—was whether the people in that valley had spent the nine days since his defection doing nothing at all.

Verrin had spent thirty years around frightened people, and he had learned one thing about them that held true across counties and decades: frightened people with time on their hands built traps.

"Nobody rides the trail," he said.

Rathbone frowned. "Sir?"

"We walk it. On foot. And we don't walk it in the middle." He pointed to the base of the east wall, where a shelf of broken rock ran parallel to the trail. "We go up the shelf. Slow. And the first man who sees wire, a trip line, a stone balanced wrong, or any damn thing that looks arranged—he stops, and he says so, and nobody takes another step until I've looked at it."

"And if it's clean?"

"It isn't clean." Verrin remounted. "But we're going to walk it anyway, because the girl is up there, and the papers are up there, and Mr. Vellacott does not accept *the trail looked suspicious* as a report."

He detailed six men to hold the horses at the draw mouth and took eight up the shelf, moving in single file, rifles unslung. The afternoon light went long and coppery on the snow. Twice they stopped while a man examined something—a bent sapling, a scuff of displaced talus—and twice Verrin studied the thing himself and waved them on, because both times it was exactly what it appeared to be: a mountain being a mountain.

It was the third thing that wasn't.

They found it where the shelf pinched against a fallen slab—a length of rawhide stretched ankle-high across the only path forward, anchored to a boulder on one side and disappearing into a jumble of rock on the other. Attached to the far anchor, half-buried in snow, was a deadfall: a flat stone the size of a door propped on a pivot log, loaded with fifty pounds of rock.

A snare. Simple, old-fashioned, the kind of thing a trapper sets for men instead of marten.

"Hold," Verrin said, and the column froze.

He walked up to it alone, crouched, and studied the arrangement with the detached interest of a man reading a letter addressed to someone else. The rawhide was new. The deadfall was set with care but not with genius—it would break a leg, crack a skull if a man went down wrong, but it was a killer of opportunity, not design. It said: *we want you slowed, hurt, turned around.* It did not say: *we want you dead.*

Which meant the people who built it were not yet desperate enough to kill. Which meant they still believed this might be resolved without graves.

Verrin intended to correct that belief.

"Cut the line," he told Rathbone. "Spring the trap from the side. Then mark it—we'll come back this way." He straightened and looked up the draw, where the notch opened onto higher country and the light was failing. Somewhere beyond that notch, he was certain, was a second trail, and a third, because a settlement that had survived invisibility for a generation did not depend on one road.

And somewhere ahead of them, watching, was a man who knew this country the way Verrin knew boardrooms.

"Delacroix," he said quietly, to no one.

"Sir?" Rathbone asked.

"Nothing. Move."

* * *

From a ledge four hundred feet above, Lena watched them spring the deadfall and felt the day tilt.

They were good. That was the ugly truth of it. No one had ridden the trail; no one had blundered into the snare; they'd come up the rock shelf like men who'd been shot at before and had learned. When the trap went off—stone slamming down on empty air with a boom that rolled down the canyon—they didn't scatter or fire wildly at shadows. They marked the spot and kept moving, unhurried, in file.

Beside him, Old Tam lowered his field glasses. Tam was sixty-eight, a deserter from a war most of the valley had forgotten, and he had eyes like a hawk and opinions like a river.

"That's soldiering," Tam said. "Not posse work. Somebody trained them."

"Somebody paid them," Lena said. "Same thing, half the time." He watched the column reach the notch and stop, fanning out to study the ground beyond. Beyond the notch lay the second false trail—the long one, the one that wandered eighteen miles through dead-end canyons and a salt flat that swallowed wheels to the axle. He'd built it to waste time. He was beginning to understand it might only waste a day.

"They'll camp in the notch tonight," he said. "Water there, shelter from the wind. Tomorrow they take the long trail, and tomorrow night they realize it goes nowhere, and the day after that they start cutting cross-country." He turned to Tam. "Get down to Amara. Tell her the invisibility's over. Whatever we were going to be after this, we're it now."

Tam gathered himself to move, then paused. "You all right, boy?"

Lena realized his hands were shaking—not with fear, or not only fear. With something older. For twenty years he had used these skills to stay unseen, to stay safe, to stay nothing. Now every trick he knew was aimed outward, at fifteen armed men, in defense of forty-one people who ate supper together and argued about gardens and had never once asked him for papers.

"Yeah," he said, and found it was mostly true. "Go on."

* * *

That night, in the notch, Verrin sat apart from his men's fire and wrote in a small ledger by lantern light.

*Day one. Approach discovered. One trap sprung, no casualties. Enemy competent, cautious, non-lethal so far. Estimate main settlement two days' hard travel via false routes; direct approach possible through south rim once located. Recommend pressure maintained without pause.*

He considered adding a line about Delacroix and did not. Some assessments were better kept where they couldn't be subpoenaed.

Across the fire, one of the hired men—a kid named Pell, maybe nineteen, who'd signed on in Harrow Bend for wages that should have taught him something about the job—said, "Whose valley you figure it is? The people up there. Squatters, Doss said."

"Doesn't matter whose it is," Rathbone said.

"Sure it does. If they're just squatters, why's the company paying us army wages to—"

"Because the company pays for results, boy, and results cost what they cost." Rathbone threw a stick on the fire. "Sleep."

Pell shut up. But Verrin, listening with his eyes closed, noted the question and filed it away. Questions like that started small and ended in depositions. He would keep an eye on the boy.

High above the notch, on a rimrock shelf where no fire burned, Lena lay on his bedroll in the freezing dark and listened to the company men's laughter drift up out of the canyon, thin and human and horribly ordinary. Fifteen men who laughed around fires. Fifteen men with mothers, probably. He'd promised Amara he would drive them out without making widows, and he meant to keep it, and he understood now—with the clarity that comes only when the thing you feared is finally in front of you—that keeping it might be the hardest promise of his life.

Tomorrow they would take the long trail. Tomorrow night they would find the salt flat. And the day after, the real war would begin.

He closed his eyes and began, methodically, to plan the next three days of lies.
