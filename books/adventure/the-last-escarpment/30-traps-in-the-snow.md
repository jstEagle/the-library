## 30. Traps in the Snow

The storm gave them four days, and Lena spent every hour of them like a man spending his last money.

He stood at the mouth of the main draw with Doss beside him—the man he'd pulled from the floodwater, the man who'd sold them, now working with the desperate energy of someone paying down a debt he knew could never be cleared—and he looked at the ground the way he'd looked at it for twenty years, reading it, arguing with it, asking it what it would allow.

"Verrin comes up here," Lena said. "Not because it's the best way. Because it's the obvious way. A man who's been humiliated once doesn't get clever. He gets certain."

Doss nodded. His face had gone thin since the narrows, since the confession, since Amara had looked at him across the council fire and said only, *Then you will carry stone.* He'd been carrying stone for three days now, hauling it up the draw on his back like penance made physical.

"He'll have thirty men," Doss said. "Maybe more. Company men don't travel light when they're angry."

"Thirty men." Lena crouched and pressed his palm flat against the slope above them, feeling the mountain under the skin of snow. "Thirty men who've never fought at altitude. Who've been riding for a week through country that hates them. Whose horses are going lame and whose tempers are going short." He stood. "We don't have to beat them, Doss. We just have to make this draw cost more than Verrin's employers will pay for."

* * *

The first lesson he taught them was one they already knew in their bones but had never named: the escarpment kills by geography before anyone fires a shot.

He walked the valley council up the draw himself, moving slow, pointing with a stick.

"Here." The trail narrowed between two shoulders of rock, maybe six horses wide. "Above this is a shelf we cut loose three summers ago to keep the flash floods from diverting. It's held back by a lip of frozen scree. Put enough weight on the slope below—horses, men, vibration—and the lip lets go. We don't drop it on their heads. We drop it *behind* them."

Amara's brother Tomas frowned. "Behind them?"

"So they can't retreat the way they came. So the only way out is forward. Into everything else we've built." Lena tapped the stick against the rock. "A man fighting forward is a man committed. A man committed is a man who stops thinking about whether he should be here at all. We want them thinking about that."

Sera stood at the edge of the group, arms crossed against the cold, and he caught her watching him—not the terrain, him—with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to. There would be time for reading later, if there was a later.

"And if the rockfall kills some of them?" she asked quietly.

Lena was quiet a moment. Below them the valley lay white and smoking, woodsmoke rising straight from forty chimneys, forty homes that appeared on no map and belonged to no ledger.

"It won't," he said. "I've walked this shelf. When it goes, it goes as a slide, not a fall—it fills the trail from below, pushes uphill. Men get buried to the waist, not the neck. Horses panic, some break legs. We lose nothing but their nerve." He met her eyes. "But you're right to ask. So hear me, all of you: nobody drops anything on anybody. Nobody rolls a log aimed at a skull. We are not killers hiding in rocks. We are neighbors telling trespassers to go home. Every trap in this draw is built to frighten, to block, to bleed morale. If a man dies because he panicked and fell off his own horse, that's the mountain's doing, not ours. But we will not take a life today that we could send home tomorrow."

Old Ferro, who'd fled a hanging in the territories, spat into the snow. "They came to burn us out."

"They came because they were told this valley was full of outlaws and easy pickings," Lena said. "When they find out it's full of people who won't die easy and won't kill either, half of them will start wondering what their pay is really for. That wondering is our weapon. Everything we build today is built to feed it."

* * *

They worked four days, and the valley worked like it had never worked before.

The false trails went in first—that was Lena's own craft, twenty years of erasing himself distilled into instruction. Branching paths beaten into the snow leading nowhere, toward dead-end box canyons and cliff faces, each one dotted with old campfire rings stacked fresh with cold ash so they'd read as occupied. Decoy smoke: green boughs on low coals at three points along the rim, sending up columns that said *we are many, we are everywhere.*

"Their scout sees five camps," Lena told the fire crews. "By tomorrow there'll be nine. Keep them moving. A posse chasing smoke doesn't sleep, and a posse that doesn't sleep makes mistakes."

The rockfall trigger came next—a rope-and-wedge arrangement at the shelf, rigged so two men hidden in a crevice could drop the whole slope with one pull. Then the snares—not Sera's desperate survival snares from the high country, but their cousins: trip-lines across the trail at ankle height where the path funneled between boulders, tied to branches bent under tension, meant not to maim but to *startle*, to whip snow and gravel into a horse's face at the worst moment.

And then the thing Lena was proudest of, the thing he'd carried in his head since the day he'd seen the valley: the echo.

"The draw bends twice," he explained, standing with Sera and Tomas at the second bend. "Sound comes off these walls strange. If we put drummers up on that ledge—just boys with sticks and hollow logs—they can make ten men sound like fifty coming down the slopes in the dark. Verrin's men will hear an army flanking them that doesn't exist. In a storm, in a narrow place, with no line of sight? They'll believe it. Fear fills in the blanks, and fear always draws more enemies than there are."

Tomas grinned despite himself. "You've done this before."

"I've *been* the man in the dark hearing things," Lena said. "I know how it works from both sides."

* * *

On the fifth morning, the lookouts' signal came down: dust on the southern flats, a long column, moving slow.

Verrin had come early, ahead of the worst weather, exactly as Doss predicted—thirty-one riders, gray shapes crawling up the switchbacks, company rifles gleaming dully under oilcloth.

Lena watched from the rim through field glasses he'd owned longer than most of the defenders had been alive. Beside him, Sera breathed shallow and fast.

"There," she said. "The gray coat. That's him."

Lena found him: a compact man riding slightly apart from his column, checking the trail signs with a tracker's patience. Lena felt something twist in his chest—not hate, recognition. Verrin was what Lena might have become if the world had convicted the guilty man instead of him. A professional doing a professional's work for people who paid him not to ask questions.

"He's good," Lena said. "Watch—he's already spotted the false trail." The column paused; riders fanned out; Verrin studied the cold-ash fire rings. "But good isn't the same as right. Look at his men. They're strung out, horses blowing. They've been in the saddle since before dawn in country that gives nothing back." He lowered the glasses. "Now we see what four days of work buys."

The column took the bait—or rather, took the only trail that didn't obviously lead to a cliff, which was the same thing. They entered the draw at midmorning under a sky the color of dirty wool, and the first snare went off within the hour.

Lena heard it more than saw it: a crack of released branch, a horse screaming and pitching sideways, snow and gravel whipping into the rider behind. Shouting. Rifle shots fired at nothing—at rock, at shadow, at the sound of their own panic echoing off the walls.

"Drums," Lena said quietly.

Up on the ledges, the boys began. Hollow logs, hide heads, a rhythm that rolled and doubled and split in the echoes until it sounded like fifty men descending from three directions at once. The column bunched. Verrin's voice carried up the draw, sharp, ordering skirmish lines—and ordering them into the funnel beneath the shelf.

"Now," Lena said.

The two men in the crevice pulled.

The mountain spoke. Not a crash—a long grinding roar, the whole slope exhaling twenty years of held breath, snow and scree pouring down across the trail *behind* the column, sealing the way they'd come. The sound alone broke something in the posse's discipline. Horses reared. Men dismounted and remounted and dismounted again. One rider bolted forward blindly, hit a trip-line, and went down hard in a spray of powder as a bent branch whipped past his ear.

And through all of it, the drums kept talking, and the decoy fires sent up their smoke, and the valley did not show itself at all.

* * *

By late afternoon the posse had advanced perhaps half a mile into a draw that should have taken an hour to walk.

Lena moved along the rim from position to position, and everywhere he went he found the same thing: his people holding, disciplined, frightened and doing the work anyway. At the second bend, Ferro's crew had turned the column with nothing but rolling boulders into the *side* of the trail—blocking, never crushing—forcing Verrin's men to climb over their own pack animals. Further up, a boy of fifteen named Callum had loosed a single arrow that buried itself in a saddlebag a foot from a trooper's hand, and the man had simply sat down in the snow and refused to go further, and his sergeant had not pushed him.

No blood. Not one drop of blood all day. Lena counted it like coin.

At dusk the column made camp in the draw itself, hemmed in by the rockfall behind them, too exhausted to push on, and Lena stood in the falling dark listening to their camp sounds drift up—voices pitched wrong, too loud, the laughter of tired scared men, a horse groaning.

Sera came up beside him, breath steaming.

"You did it," she said. "You bled them without opening a vein."

"Not yet done." Lena didn't take his eyes off the fires below. "Verrin's still got twenty-some men and a job he can't report as failed. Tomorrow he tries something desperate. Desperate men stop being careful, and that cuts both ways." He finally looked at her. "Get some sleep. Both of us will need it."

She hesitated. "Lena. Today—you wouldn't let us kill them. Even now, with everything they came to do."

"Because the day we kill for this valley," he said, "is the day we become the story the company tells about us. Outlaws slaughtered in defense of stolen land. That story brings armies. But a posse that limps home saying *there's nothing down there but people who wouldn't fight us and wouldn't quit*—" he shook his head, "—that story brings doubt. And doubt is the one thing money can't buy back."

Below, in the enemy camp, a single figure detached itself from the fires and climbed slowly toward the rockfall barrier, alone, unarmed, hands visible. Verrin stopped at the base of the slide and called up into the dark, his voice flat and carrying:

"Delacroix! I know you're up there. I have an offer—and I'll only make it once!"

Lena exhaled, long and slow, breath streaming silver in the cold.

"Well," he said to Sera. "So much for sleep."
