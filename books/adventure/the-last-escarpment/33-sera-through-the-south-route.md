## 33. Sera Through the South Route

The valley let her go at dawn, in the gray hour when the terraces were still blue with frost and the smoke from the stone houses rose straight up into a windless sky. Amara walked her as far as the southern gate and pressed a cloth bundle into her hands—flat bread, dried apricots, a twist of salt.

"You come back," Amara said. It was not a request. It was spoken the way she spoke everything, like a law older than any government's.

"I'll come back," Sera said, and was surprised to find she meant it, and more surprised by what that meant: that for the first time in her life there was a place to come back *to*.

Lena was not at the gate. He had wanted to be; she'd seen it in him last night, that restless weight-shift of a man who measured every problem in miles and hated one he couldn't walk himself. But he was needed on the north draw, where Verrin's broken posse was regrouping somewhere below the snowline, and the southern route was hers alone—a fact she had insisted on with a stubbornness that had surprised them both.

"He'll understand," Amara said, following her gaze up toward the ridge where Lena's figure moved among the rockfall crews, small and dark against the white.

"No," Sera said. "But he'll accept it. That's different."

She shouldered the satchel—the real one, the originals, sewn now into oilcloth and strapped tight—and took the copy of the papers out of her coat and looked at it one last time before handing it back. No. The copies went with her too. Duplicated, hidden in three places on her body, because if she died on the ice the originals would die with her, and the truth could not be allowed to die twice.

She went down through the southern slot canyons she had scouted two years ago, when she was still a clerk with a government badge and a habit of reading maps the way other people read novels. The route existed on no survey quadrant. It existed because she had once been bored, and curious, and looking for an excuse not to return to camp, and had followed a bighorn trail until it became a route and the route became a way through the range that no one in Harrow Bend believed possible.

Two days of climbing first. Then the high shelf. Then the river.

* * *

The avalanche country began above the second night's camp, and it announced itself not with sound but with silence—the particular, pressurized hush of slopes loaded past their tolerance, snowpack sitting on old ice like a lie sitting on a ledger. Sera knew the signs from the survey reports she'd once filed without reading closely enough. She knew them better now. She'd spent the whole climb reading the mountain the way Lena read tracks: slope angle, aspect, wind loading, the whumpf of the pack settling under her boots that made her stand frozen for ten full seconds, counting her heartbeats, waiting for the world to decide.

It decided to hold. For a while.

She crossed the loaded slopes in the cold of early morning, before the sun could warm the surface and pull the trigger. She went one at a time across the steepest pitch even though there was no one behind her—because it felt right, because some instinct older than training said *don't put all your weight on the mountain at once*, and because she was carrying something heavier than paper now. She was carrying the valley's argument. If she buried herself under forty feet of slide, the argument died with her, and the people who had fed her and argued over her vote and hung a coat on a hook for her would have sent their hope into the mountains to freeze.

So she moved like a woman defusing something. Kick, probe, listen. Kick, probe, listen. The sun cleared the eastern ridge and she still had half the traverse to go, and the light came down the slope toward her like a fuse burning.

She ran the last three hundred yards. It was not running so much as a controlled fall with ambition, boots punching through crust, breath tearing, the satchel slamming against her hip. Behind her she heard it—not loud, not dramatic, just a soft exhalation, a *whump* that deepened into a long sliding hiss, and when she threw herself behind a granite rib and looked back, the slope she'd crossed was moving. A whole slab of it, smooth as a tablecloth being pulled, gliding down and then breaking apart into the thunder she'd been dreading since dawn.

The cloud swallowed the trail. It swallowed her tracks. When it settled, minutes later, the mountain had erased her passage completely—which meant, she realized as her heartbeat slowed, that anyone following her would find exactly nothing.

That thought kept her warm for an hour. Then she understood its other edge: if the mountain erased her tracks, it could erase her, and no one would ever know which way she'd gone or why. Her death would be a blank page. The company wouldn't even need to write the ending.

She ate the flat bread in small pieces and made herself chew slowly, and did not let herself think about how far she still had to go.

* * *

The river was the third day, and it was worse than the avalanches, because the avalanches at least were honest.

In summer it was a braided, shallow thing—you could hear it from a mile off, chuckling over gravel bars, the kind of water you crossed without taking your boots off. In January it was a bridge of gray ice spanning black voids, and the voids talked to her all night from her camp on the bank: groans, cracks, a sound like whale-song played backward, the whole river shifting and settling under its lid.

She waited for the coldest hour, near dawn, when the ice would be at its hardest. She tied her rope to a boulder and paid it out behind her, a hundred and fifty feet of it, because Lena had made her practice self-arrest with an ice axe until her shoulders screamed, and because he'd also said—gruffly, not looking at her, in the voice he used for things that mattered—"If the ice goes, don't fight the current. Fight for the edge. Current doesn't kill you. Cold does. Get *out*."

The middle of the river was where the ice thinned. She could see it—could see the black water sliding beneath her feet through six inches of graying glass, fast and patient. Halfway across, a crack opened ahead of her with a sound like a rifle shot and ran sideways thirty feet, and she stood absolutely still while the river considered her.

"Come on," she whispered. To the ice. To herself. To whatever was listening. "I'm not done."

The ice held. Her boots found the far gravel bar and she unclipped the rope with fingers that wouldn't work properly, and she sat down hard on a boulder and laughed—a cracked, hysterical little laugh that echoed off the canyon walls and embarrassed her immediately, because laughing alone in the wilderness was the first symptom of something, though she couldn't remember what.

Then she heard the hoofbeat.

One. Faint. From the south side—the side she'd just left. Impossible. Unless—

Unless someone had crossed lower down, or higher up, or had been on this side of the river all along, paralleling her, letting her break trail through the dangerous ground.

Sera went flat behind the boulder and did not breathe.

* * *

She'd known, in the part of herself she kept locked, that Verrin would split his force. She'd told the council as much. A man humiliated at the north draw doesn't quit; he adapts. And a man like Verrin—who hadn't raised his voice once in the whole parley at the gate, who had offered Lena a pardon the way a banker offers a foreclosure—would keep a reserve. A quiet instrument. One rider, maybe two, moving light, watching the southern approaches in case the valley tried exactly what the valley was trying.

She'd just believed, needed to believe, that the southern route was hers alone—that the map in her head, drawn from a bored clerk's wanderings, was a secret no one else possessed.

Now there was a rider behind her, and the secret was leaking.

She watched the trail along the far bank for an hour, cold working through her clothes, and saw nothing. That proved less than it seemed. Whoever it was had watched her cross the river without making a move—which meant they weren't sure of their shot, or weren't authorized to make one, or were herding her. All three possibilities were bad in different ways.

She broke camp in the shadow of the boulders and climbed away from the river on goat trails, doubling back, cutting her own sign, doing the things Lena had drilled into her until they lived in her hands. At the top of the first bench she allowed herself one look back.

There. A flicker of movement, half a mile down and east, where no movement should be. A dark rider among dark rocks, gone as soon as seen—but seen. Not chasing. Shadowing. Keeping pace at the edge of visibility, patient as weather.

Her fear, which had been a general fog since the river, condensed into something specific and cold: *they know where I'm going.* Which meant they knew about the journalist. Which meant they might know about the federal investigators. Which meant the second front of this war wasn't going to be fought in the valley at all—it was going to be fought right here, on this route, between her and Harrow Bend, and nobody in the valley would even know it was happening.

She thought about turning back. The thought lasted four seconds and she examined it honestly, the way Amara had taught the council to examine things: What does turning back buy? Safety, maybe, if the rider didn't follow her into the maze of the high canyons. And it bought the valley another week of hiding, and then spring, and then Verrin's legal ruin arriving on schedule with the thaw, indictments or no indictments, evidence or no evidence. A valley that hides forever has already lost. Lena had said that at the vote, and the council had agreed barely, and barely was the margin she was walking on now.

She turned south and kept moving.

* * *

The near-death, when it came, was not dramatic. That was the worst part. It arrived as arithmetic.

On the fifth day the storm found her—a front rolling up from the desert, warmer than the air it displaced, and it fell as wet snow that soaked through every layer she owned and then froze solid overnight when the temperature dove behind it. By morning she was moving through a landscape glazed in ice, and by afternoon she had stopped sweating, which she knew from the survey medics' briefings was the wrong direction, and by evening she realized she had not thought an original thought in two hours. Her mind was replaying loops. The taste of the flat bread. Amara's voice. The crack in the river ice. Loop, loop, loop.

Hypothermia didn't feel like dying. It felt like being edited. Whole paragraphs of herself were being cut, quietly, by a calm editor who assured her it was all fine, that lying down here in the snow for just a few minutes was a reasonable sentence, that the rest of the manuscript could proceed without her.

She caught herself unfastening the satchel strap. She did not know why. Her hands had simply decided, and some last garrison of her mind sounded the alarm: *you are taking off the thing you are supposed to protect.*

She stood in the falling dark and forced herself to do the stupidest, most human thing available: she sang. Loudly, badly, a harvest song from her grandmother's county that she hadn't thought of in fifteen years, and the singing made her breathe deep, and the breathing made her angry, and the anger made her walk. She sang it eleven times through the next mile, each verse a rung, each rung a decision not to lie down, and when she finally saw the black mouth of the cedar hollow where she'd planned to shelter, she cried a little, and that helped too, because crying is hot.

In the hollow she stripped, wrung out what she could, got into her spare layer and her sleeping sack, and lay shivering violently—which the medics' briefing said was good, shivering was the furnace still lit—and ate sugar, and did not sleep so much as fall briefly unconscious and climb back out of it all night, checking, each time, that the satchel was still against her body.

It was. She was. Both facts felt earned.

* * *

The rider closed the distance on the sixth day.

She spotted him at midmorning, closer than before—no longer a flicker at the edge of things but a definite figure, a quarter mile back on her own trail, riding her tracks like a man following a string home. He made no attempt to hide anymore. That was its own message: *I want you to see me.*

Sera stopped. Her hand went to the pistol at her belt—one of the valley's, pressed on her at the gate by a woman named Odile who had said only, "You know how to use it?" and Sera had said "Well enough," and Odile had said "Then well enough," and that had been the whole ceremony.

The rider stopped too. He sat his horse easily, gloved hands loose, and then—slowly, deliberately—he raised both hands clear of his sides. Empty. And then he pulled back his hood.

Not a company man. Not Verrin's cold-eyed fixers in their matching slickers. The face under the hood was gaunt and sunburned and bearded, and it was familiar in the way a name in an old report is familiar, and it took her three full seconds to place it because the last time she'd seen it, it had been younger and clean-shaven and wearing a government surveyor's badge.

Halloran. Deputy surveyor Halloran. Second-in-command of the team that had vanished.

He was supposed to be dead, or fled, or complicit. He was none of those things, apparently, or he was all of them, and he was sitting on a horse a quarter mile away with his hands in the air like a man surrendering to her specifically.

Sera drew the pistol anyway and held it low against her thigh, and shouted across the wind-broken distance: "Why are you following me?"

His voice carried thin and cracked across the snow. "Because I've been running longer than you have, miss. And I'm tired. And I know what's in that satchel, because I helped falsify half of it before I grew a conscience." He lowered his hands slowly, watching the pistol. "And because Verrin's men are hunting the rest of us, one by one, and I'd rather testify than disappear. You're the only person heading the same direction as the truth."

The wind moved between them. Somewhere below, miles south, Harrow Bend waited with its telegraph office and its company man and its courts that had never once, in Sera's experience, been on time.

"How do I know you're not his?" she called back.

Halloran laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Because if I were his, you'd already be dead." He dismounted, keeping his palms visible, and stood in the snow like a man stepping off a ledge. "My name is Elias Halloran. I was there the night the orders came. Ask me anything. I'll answer all of it. But not standing in this wind—we've got weather coming, and I think we both need to live long enough to talk."

Sera looked at the man the government had listed as missing, presumed lost with his whole team—the man whose disappearance had started everything, whose ghost she'd been carrying in a satchel for a season. The second front had just opened, and it had opened with a handshake she hadn't offered yet.

She kept the pistol in her hand. She started walking toward him.
