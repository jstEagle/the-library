## 36. The Cost

The storm spent itself by dawn, and what it left behind was not weather.

Lena walked the main draw at first light with his hands in his coat pockets and his breath hanging in front of him like something he kept forgetting to say. The snow was trampled and cratered where the posse had come up the narrows in the dark, believing the decoy fires marked the valley's heart. Rockfall debris lay across the lower switchbacks in slabs the size of wagons. Here and there a dropped rifle, a torn pack strap, a boot print filling slowly with meltwater. No blood that he could see from where he stood. That had been the design—drive them out, don't slaughter them—and for once the design had held.

But the valley had paid anyway.

He found them at the upper terrace, where the fighting had been closest and least controlled. Amara sat on a stone bench outside her house with her left arm bound in a sling of quilted wool, her face gray under its brown, her eyes open and level as ever. Sera knelt beside her, retying the sling with hands that shook only a little. Around them stood perhaps thirty of the valley folk, and they were very quiet, the way people are quiet when the counting isn't finished yet.

"Two," Amara said when she saw him. She didn't soften it. She never softened anything; it was one of the reasons he trusted her. "Hollis Ferro. And the boy—Teodor's grandson, Jem."

Lena closed his eyes for one breath. Opened them.

"Hollis held the second barricade alone for near an hour," Amara went on. "When they finally rushed it, he gave ground slow enough that everyone behind him got clear. He took two bullets doing it." Her voice didn't waver, but her good hand pressed flat against her thigh, and Lena understood that the pressure was holding something in place that wanted to come apart. "Jem was fifteen. He was running messages between the fires. A stray round off the rocks. There wasn't even anyone to blame."

"There's someone to blame," Sera said quietly.

"Yes," Amara said. "And we'll get to him. But first we bury our own, and we bury them right, because if we bury them in anger we'll have lost more than two lives—we'll have lost the reason we fought at all."

* * *

They buried Hollis and Jem the next morning in the small cemetery above the terraces, where forty years of unmarked graves faced the eastern wall of the escarpment so the dead could watch the sun come up over country no government had ever bothered to name.

The whole valley came. Lena counted heads as they gathered—two hundred and eleven, minus two, plus himself, plus the handful of wounded sitting or lying on sledges dragged up through the snow. He had never been to a funeral here before. In twenty years of living at the edges of other people's lives he had attended exactly three funerals, all of them alone, standing at the back of whatever gathering would have him, leaving before the last prayer. This one he stood in the middle of, and nobody looked at him strangely, and that simple fact nearly undid him more than the graves did.

Amara spoke, one-armed, her voice carrying down the slope without effort.

"We built this valley because the world said there was nothing here worth wanting," she said. "We farmed land the maps called worthless. We raised children the law said belonged to creditors and captains and companies. For sixty years our safety was our smallness. Yesterday, two of ours died proving that we are no longer small enough to be ignored." She paused. Snow ticked softly against the pine boughs laid over the graves. "I will not tell you their deaths were cheap. They were not. Nothing about standing in the light is cheap. But I will tell you this: Hollis did not die for a hidden thing. He died for a found one. And a found thing can be defended, and claimed, and kept. That is the difference between us and every fugitive who ever ran until they dropped. We stopped running."

She stepped back. Teodor, Jem's grandfather, a stooped man of seventy who had crossed the range nine times in his life smuggling out families fleeing seizure, came forward and put a single stone on each grave—the old desert custom, older than any church, a marker that says: you were here, and I was here, and the ground remembers both.

Then the valley sang. Not a hymn Lena knew—a work song, low and rolling, the kind sung over harvest rows and roof-raising, the kind meant for many voices. He didn't know the words. He hummed the parts he could find and let the rest carry him, and when it ended, Sera's shoulder was against his arm, and neither of them moved away.

Afterward, while the others drifted down toward the terraces and the work of the day, Lena stayed. He crouched at Jem's grave and set a stone of his own, river-smoothed, picked from the creek below the terraces.

"I've buried a lot of miles," he said quietly. "Never got good at burying people. I'll learn. Seems like the least I owe this place."

Behind him, boots crunched. Doss stood ten feet off, hat in his hands, looking like a man who had asked permission to be somewhere and hadn't received it.

"They'd have buried me in one of these if I'd had my way," Doss said. "Selling the pass knowledge. Selling the cache." His jaw worked. "I keep thinking about the boy. Fifteen. I'm thirty-one and I nearly traded a whole valley for a pardon and a warm room in Harrow Bend."

"You saved my life in the Narrows' aftermath," Lena said. "You told us about Verrin splitting his force. You're standing here instead of gone. That counts for something. Not everything. Something."

"It doesn't feel like something."

"It won't for a while," Lena said. He rose, knees cracking in the cold. "That's how you know it's real."

* * *

Verrin's force broke camp on the third day after the storm.

Lena watched them go from the high lookout with field glasses older than some of the men below. The company posse had come up the range twenty-two strong, following Doss's partial knowledge of the passes, and the mountain and the valley together had returned nineteen of them to the lowlands—hungry, frostbitten, demoralized, three of them limping from rockfall injuries, none of them dead. Nineteen men strung out along the white flats below the escarpment like beads off a broken cord, moving south with the particular hunched gait of soldiers who have learned that the ground itself is against them.

At the head of the column, one figure stopped, turned, and looked up at the escarpment wall for a long moment. Even at half a mile, through glass, Lena could feel the look. Verrin raised one hand—not a salute, not quite a wave. Something between acknowledgment and a promise.

Then the column moved off, and the flats were empty, and the valley was, for the moment, alone with its dead.

"He'll be back," Sera said. She had climbed up beside him without his hearing, which still unsettled him; she moved like the desert taught her, which was to say like it had taught him. "Not with guns. You saw his face at the gate."

"I saw it."

"He's a fixer. Guns are what fixers use when the paperwork fails. His paperwork hasn't failed yet." She pulled her collar up against the wind. "He'll file claims. He'll bring surveyors with legal warrants instead of rifles. He'll get injunctions naming every person in this valley, and marshals who follow the law instead of breaking it, and courts in Harrow Bend where the company owns half the docket. He told you as much."

"He told me," Lena said. "Standing at our gate with a white rag on a stick, offering me a pardon and my old badge if I handed over the documents and the council. Like he'd read my file. Like he knew exactly which wound to press."

"And?"

"And I named the nine," Lena said. "All of them. Ada Marsh. The Okoye brothers. Tomás Reyes. The rest. One by one, loud enough that his whole line heard it. Then I told him the pardon he was selling was printed by the same hand that signed the warrant for my arrest twenty years ago, and that I'd rather stand in front of a rigged court as a free man than accept mercy from the men who rigged it."

Sera was quiet a moment. "What did he say?"

"He smiled," Lena said. "That was the worst part. He smiled like a man who'd gotten what he came for. Then he said—" Lena's jaw tightened around the memory. "He said, 'Then we'll do it your way, Ranger. In the open. I hope you like daylight.' And he turned around and walked back down the draw."

"In the open," Sera repeated. She looked out over the valley—the smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the terraces white and still, the greenhouses with their frost-glazed panes catching the late light. Everything invisible suddenly, irreversibly visible. "He means the courts. The land offices. He means he'll make us fight with documents instead of bullets, and documents take months, and spring is coming, and when the thaw comes the range opens and anyone can walk in here with a warrant and a survey chain."

"That's why the papers have to reach someone the company doesn't own," Lena said. "Before Verrin's paperwork does. Federal investigators, a judge outside the district, a newspaper with teeth. Someone who can freeze the land claims mid-file. It's the only clock that matters now."

"The southern route," Sera said.

Lena turned to look at her. She was watching the horizon south of the escarpment, where the range dropped into a chaos of ridgelines the maps rendered as blank space, and her expression was the one he'd learned to read in the weeks since the vote—the clerk's expression, precise and already three steps ahead.

"No," he said.

"You don't get to say no."

"The southern route runs under the Cornice Faces. You told me yourself—you scouted it as a courier in summer, in fair weather, with mules. It's February. The avalanche chutes haven't finished loading. The river crossing—"

"The river freezes hard enough to walk by mid-month. I've been asking the old-timers. Teodor crossed it in '61 in worse conditions than this." She finally turned to face him, and her eyes were steady in a way that made his chest hurt. "Lena. Verrin knows your face. He knows the northern passes now—he has Doss's partial maps and three weeks of learning them the hard way. If you go out again, you're carrying a target through country he's already patrolled. But nobody knows mine. Nobody's watched the south. I was the junior clerk on a falsified survey—I know how to move through paper country, and I know how to move through real country, and I am the witness. My testimony plus the documents. That's what breaks the company's case open. Not the papers alone. Me, standing in a federal office saying *I copied these originals myself, and here is the ledger page they tried to burn.*"

"The shadow rider," Lena said. "The surviving surveyor. If he reaches Harrow Bend ahead of you—"

"Then he testifies too, or he testifies against us, and either way I need to be there." Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "You voted to carry the evidence out yourself before the passes sealed. The passes sealed. You turned around. That choice cost you something—I watched it cost you—but you made it for us, and I am not going to let it be for nothing. Somebody goes south. It should be me. It has to be me."

Below them, the valley went on with its evening: wood smoke, a dog barking at the terraces, someone calling a child in from the cold. Two hundred and nine people who had taken him in, argued over him, voted with him, bandaged him, fed him, and hung his coat on a real hook. Everything he had spent twenty years convinced he didn't deserve.

"If you go," he said slowly, "you go with everything we can give you. The copies sewn into your coat the way mine were. The code key memorized and then burned. The journalist's name in Harrow Bend—Marta Quill, she worked the reservoir story with my old acquaintance Rennick before Rennick died. She's the door. Knock on it and don't stop knocking."

"Marta Quill," Sera repeated. "Memorized."

"And you wait for the river. Three days minimum. The ice needs another cold snap, and the sky owes us one."

Sera almost smiled. "The sky doesn't owe anybody anything. That's the first thing you ever taught me."

"It's the only thing worth teaching." Lena looked south one last time—at the blank space on the map, at the ridgelines already turning blue with dusk, at the frozen river he couldn't see but could feel waiting out there under the snow like a held breath. "Three days," he said. "Then God help whoever stands between you and that courthouse."

Down in the valley, the evening bell rang for supper, and the light went out of the high country all at once, the way it does in winter, like a door closing.
