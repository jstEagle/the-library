## 37. Out of the Mountains

The southern route ended where all routes end: in mud.

Sera had imagined Harrow Bend a hundred times on the crossing—its crooked main street, the saloon with the warped porch, the assay office where she'd once waited three hours to file a claim report nobody read. In her imagination it had always been summer. Instead she came down out of the foothills in late February with the thaw just beginning, and the town was a brown smear of slush and woodsmoke, half-drowned, its streets running with meltwater that carried last autumn's leaves like small fleeing animals.

She stood at the edge of it for a long time, letting her horse blow.

Eleven days. That was what the crossing had cost her—the frozen river, the slide slope above Cold Basin where she'd crossed on her hands and knees with her satchel strapped to her chest like a second heartbeat, the two nights in a snow cave eating nothing because there was nothing to eat. Eleven days of being hunted by weather and by the rider she'd thought was Verrin's man and turned out to be something stranger entirely.

That rider was behind her now by half a day, moving slower because he was older and his horse was worse. Elias Marsh. Junior surveyor, the one who'd signed the falsified quadrant reports under protest and then run when the others ran. He had spent four months hiding in a line shack above the Redwater, drinking himself toward oblivion, until Verrin's men came looking for him too and he'd bolted into the same mountains Sera had crossed. They'd found each other at a spring neither should have known about, both reaching for weapons, both stopping when they saw the other's face.

*I'm not hunting you,* he'd said. *I'm running from the same dogs.*

Now she watched him come down the trail behind her, hat pulled low, beard full of ice, looking less like a witness and more like a ghost that hadn't finished dying.

"You sure about this?" he called up.

No. She was not sure about anything except the weight against her ribs—the copies sewn into her coat lining, forty-one pages of quadrant reports, aquifer soundings, and correspondence bearing the letterhead of the Meridian Water & Power Company, the same company whose name appeared in the old court records Lena had recited to her by firelight, the same hand that had reached out of the past twenty years ago and closed around a young ranger's throat.

"I'm sure we're out of alternatives," she said.

* * *

Harrow Bend had one hotel, one church, one newspaper—and the newspaper was the reason they'd come here at all instead of pushing straight through to the territorial capital.

Lena had told her about him on their last night before the vote, in the low voice he used for things that mattered: *Aldous Crane. Ran the Harrow Bend Courier back when I was a ranger. The only man in that town who wrote down my trial testimony word for word and printed it whole, including the parts that made the company look bad. They bought his paper out from under him inside a year. He kept printing anyway, off a press in his barn, until they ran him out too. Last I heard he was back. Older. Angrier. Still alive, which for a man like that is a kind of victory.*

They found him exactly as advertised: a narrow, stooped man in an unheated print shop behind the livery, setting type by lamplight with ink-stained fingers and eyes that went from surprise to calculation to something like hunger in the space of three seconds.

"Delacroix," he said, when Sera gave the name. "I haven't heard that name spoken aloud in this county in fifteen years. People say it quiet, like a prayer or a curse." He wiped his hands on his apron. "You're not from the law."

"No," Sera said. "We're from outside it."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he blew out the lamp, checked the street through the shutter crack, and said, "Come in before somebody sees you standing there. And tell me everything, and don't leave anything out, because I have been waiting twenty years for someone to bring me the rest of this story."

* * *

It took two nights.

Sera talked; Crane wrote. Not in shorthand—in longhand, careful, asking her to repeat dates and figures until he had them exact. Marsh sat in the corner with a cup of coffee he never drank, and when his turn came he talked too, haltingly at first and then in a flood, about the orders that had come down before the survey ever started, about the quadrant maps pre-marked in red before anyone had set foot in the escarpment country, about the morning Verrin's predecessor told the team that certain findings would be *adjusted* and that anyone who objected could walk home from the high range.

"The aquifer," Crane said, tapping the page. "How big?"

"Big enough to water half the territory," Sera said. "Big enough that if Meridian controls it, they control every farm and every town downstream for a hundred miles. The survey was never a survey. It was an appraisal. They were pricing the valley to take it."

"And the missing team."

Marsh's cup rattled against the saucer. Crane looked at him.

"Three dead in the storm," Marsh said finally. "Not murdered. But the storm happened because Verrin's man pushed them up the pass a week early—they wanted the eviction paperwork served before the valley could prepare, and the weather didn't care about their schedule. The rest of us scattered. Verrin reported us all missing. Dead, probably. It was cleaner that way." His voice cracked. "Cleaner for everyone but us."

Crane wrote it all down. Then he put down his pen and looked at them over the lamp.

"You understand what happens next," he said. "This isn't a story anymore. This is evidence. The moment I send word, federal investigators will come, and they'll want every page, and they'll want both of you under oath. And Meridian has lawyers the way the desert has sand. They will try to bury you. Both of you. Again, in your case—" a nod at Marsh— "and for the first time, in yours." A nod at Sera. "Are you ready for that?"

Sera thought of the valley. Of Amara's steady voice in council. Of Lena saying, *a valley that hides forever has already lost.* Of the stitches in her coat lining, worked by lamplight while the whole settlement pretended not to watch.

"They already tried burying me once," she said. "In a place with no map. It didn't take."

* * *

The investigators arrived nine days later, ahead of the thaw, which was itself a kind of miracle—two men and a stenographer off the early stage, sent down from the capital on the strength of Crane's telegram, which had been composed with a craftsman's restraint:

HAVE DOCUMENTS AND TWO WITNESSES RE WATER RIGHTS FRAUD MERIDIAN W&P STOP COME BEFORE MUD SEASON OR COME NEVER.

Their names were Inspector Halloran and Inspector Reyes, and they were younger than Sera had expected, and more tired, and they listened the way Lena listened—with their whole bodies, without interrupting, without writing anything down until the talking was done.

Halloran spread the forty-one pages across the print shop floor, weighting the corners with type blocks, and knelt over them for two hours without speaking. Marsh testified first, then Sera, then Marsh again, then Sera again, the stenographer's pencil scratching like a small animal in the walls. When Halloran finally stood, his knees popped and his face was gray.

"Do you understand," he said, "that if even half of this holds, it doesn't stop at Meridian? There are land office men in this. There are probably men in the territorial government. This goes up."

"It was always going to go up," Sera said. "We're just the part that finally pushed back."

Reyes asked the question she'd been waiting for, the one she'd rehearsed answers to on eleven days of empty trail: "And the valley itself? The people living there? What do you want us to do about them?"

And here was the trap, laid so neatly she almost admired it. Because the truth was that the valley existed outside the law—squatters, fugitives, people without papers—and the same machinery she was feeding could grind them up just as easily as Meridian. She thought of Amara. Of Doss, who had sold them out and been forgiven and would never forgive himself. Of Lena, whose conviction she now knew was vacated only weeks ago, quietly, in a filing room, after the first documents reached the capital—a fact Crane had confirmed by wire and delivered to her with visible satisfaction.

"The valley wants one thing," she said. "To be left alone. Every person there will testify if you need them. But when this is done, I want it written down—not promised, written—that no one is prosecuted for living where the map forgot them. They didn't break the law. The law broke them first. Some of them literally." She met Halloran's eyes. "Ask your own archives about a reservoir failure nineteen years ago. Nine dead. One man convicted. Ask who owned the reservoir."

Halloran went very still. "Delacroix," he said. "The ranger."

"He's not a ranger anymore," Sera said. "He's the man holding the pass right now, in the worst snow of the year, so that Verrin's hired guns can't come down here and burn these documents before you read them. Whatever else you decide about the valley, decide it fast. Because he's spending himself to buy you time, and men like him don't have infinite amounts to spend."

* * *

They stayed six days. Long enough for the thaw to open the road east, long enough for subpoenas to be drafted, long enough for word to start moving through Harrow Bend the way word does—sideways, in doorways, in lowered voices at the assay office.

Sera heard her own name spoken in the street for the first time since she'd fled the survey camp. Not *thief*. Not *the girl who ran*. Just *Sera Vance*, junior clerk, witness for the territory, and the strangeness of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

On the sixth night, Crane walked her and Marsh to the edge of town, where the eastbound stage would take them to the capital and the grand jury. He was carrying a copy of the Courier—fresh off the resurrected press, the first edition in twenty years to carry his name as publisher instead of *proprietor emeritus*—and the headline took up half the front page:

**THE LAST ESCARPMENT: WHAT THE SURVEY WAS REALLY FOR**

Below it, in smaller type: *Documents obtained by this paper indicate the vanished government survey team was erased to conceal one of the largest untapped aquifers in the territory...*

"They'll deny everything by noon," Crane said. "Meridian's lawyers are probably drafting the statement now. Doesn't matter. Denial is just confirmation wearing a suit." He pressed the paper into her hands. "For the valley. For Delacroix. Tell him—" He stopped, and for a moment the old anger in his face softened into something older still. "Tell him the record's straight now. All of it. Word for word, like I promised him twenty years ago."

The stage came grinding up the muddy road, four horses steaming, the driver eyeing the strangers with the flat suspicion of a man who'd driven enough trouble in his life to recognize it boarding his coach.

Sera climbed up. Marsh followed, clutching his subpoena like a talisman. As the driver gathered the reins, hoofbeats sounded on the far side of town—fast, urgent, coming from the north road.

A rider burst through the slush at a dead gallop, wool coat crusted white, leading a second horse that swayed with exhaustion. Even at that distance, even through the melting haze, Sera knew the set of the shoulders before she knew anything else.

The rider hauled up beside the stage, breath smoking, and pulled back his hood.

"Lena," she said.

He looked up at her from below, snow-blind and hollow-eyed and grinning like a man who'd won something he couldn't name yet. "Verrin's force broke apart three days ago," he said. "Half of them deserted when the storm hit. The other half followed him back to Harrow Bend to wait for lawyers." He handed the lead rope to the driver and put one boot on the stage step. "So I figured I'd come see the lawyers myself."

Behind him, at the far end of the street, a tall man in a gray coat stepped out of the hotel doorway and stood watching them with the patience of someone who had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Verrin had come to see the lawyers too.
