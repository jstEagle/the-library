## 1. The Debt Collector's Map

The town was called Karabulak, which meant something like *spring water* in a language nobody there spoke anymore, and it smelled like diesel and wet wool and the particular staleness of places that exist only because two borders couldn't agree on whose problem they were. Noor Zhou had been living under the name Mara Ilves for eleven weeks, pouring tea in a truck-stop canteen for a woman who never asked questions because asking questions cost extra.

She had gotten good at being no one. It was a skill, like reading avalanche terrain or tying off a rappel with numb fingers—something you learned by doing it wrong until you stopped.

The trouble with being no one, she had discovered, was that other people's eyes slid right past you, but debts did not. Debts had teeth. Debts could smell a false name through three border crossings and a forged work permit, and on the fourth Tuesday of her new life, one walked through the door of the canteen wearing a good coat that hadn't seen weather in years.

She knew him before she knew his name. That was how it always worked with men like him: they arrived already familiar, as if your life had been drafted somewhere without your signature and he'd simply come to deliver the copy. He sat at the corner table—the one with the wobble she'd fixed twice—and ordered nothing, which told her more than an order would have. He wasn't there to eat. He was there to be waited on.

"Mara," he said when she came over with the cloth and the indifference she wore like armor. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and quiet, the way she said everything now. "Not in here."

He smiled. It was a careful smile, the kind that had been measured against mirrors until it achieved exactly the warmth of a handshake from someone counting your fingers. "Outside, then. Ten minutes. There's a bench by the fuel pumps with an excellent view of absolutely nothing."

* * *

She gave him twelve minutes, out of spite, and spent them wiping down tables that were already clean. Then she went out, because spite only carried you so far, and men like this didn't leave. They compounded.

The cold hit her lungs like a debt of its own. The mountains stood up behind the town in the last light—white, indifferent, enormous—and she made herself not look at them, the way a recovering drinker avoids the liquor shelf. Eleven weeks of not looking. She'd been proud of that.

Voss sat on the bench with his coat open and his hands folded, a man performing relaxation for an audience of one. Up close he was older than his voice, silver at the temples, with the soft hands of someone who paid other people to touch the world.

"Noor Zhou," he said. "Certified high-altitude guide. Two first ascents in the Aksay group. The youngest lead ever contracted by the Meridian Alpine Institute." He recited it like scripture, like a eulogy. "And then the Dzhily-Su expedition."

"Say what you came to say."

"The Dzhily-Su expedition," he continued, gently, as if she hadn't spoken, "where four clients died in a whiteout that your own incident report admits you led them into. Where the inquiry found—" he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping something expensive, "—*gross negligence and falsified route assessment*. Where your license was revoked, your name entered into every registry from here to Kathmandu, and your reputation reduced to a cautionary tale taught to first-year guides with slideshows."

"I know what happened." Her breath came out in a white flag. "I was there."

"Were you?" He tilted his head. "That's the interesting question, isn't it. Even you aren't sure anymore. I've read the transcripts. You contradict yourself beautifully."

The wind came down off the ridge line and pushed between them, carrying grit. She thought about walking away. She thought about it seriously, weighing the door of the canteen, the back room where her bag was packed—always packed, that was rule one—with the bus schedule taped inside the lining. But Voss had found her here, which meant he'd have found her in Tashkent, in Osh, in whatever next nowhere she chose. Men who held paper didn't chase. They waited for interest to do the walking.

"What do you want, Voss?"

"A guide."

"Try again. Guides are easy to find. You want something else, and you're using my name like a hook."

His smile widened a degree. "A government survey team went into the Karakol Range five weeks ago. Six men. Glaciology, geodesy, mineral assessment. Officially they're conducting seasonal measurements. Unofficially—" he let the word hang, "—they were looking for something specific, and their sponsor would very much like to know whether they found it. They stopped transmitting nineteen days ago. The satellite passes show nothing useful. Weather window closes in three weeks, and after that the range eats anyone still up there and keeps them until spring."

"So hire the army."

"The army is why I'm hiring you. Everything official leaves footprints. You don't." He unfolded himself from the bench and produced, from inside that dry warm coat, a folded sheet of paper—not printed, hand-drawn, ink gone brown at the folds. A map. Old survey style, contour lines dense as woodgrain around a high cirque she recognized the shape of before she let herself recognize it. "This is where they were headed. This is where I need you to go, find them or find what's left of them, and bring back what they were carrying."

"And if they're alive?"

"Then bring back them too, if it's convenient." He said it the way you'd say *and pick up milk*.

Noor looked at the map and hated herself for how fast her eyes went to the contours, how her mind started solving the route before her pride could object. Nineteen days missing. Five weeks in. The Karakol in late season was a knife with a pretty handle. Anyone who went up there now went up there broken on purpose.

"What's in it for me besides money? Because I'm guessing money is the small part."

"The small part," Voss agreed. "The large part is this." He drew out a second document, thinner, official-looking, stamped in three colors. "Your blacklist. Every registry, every institute, every licensing body that has your name filed under *disgraced*. One signature from me and the right people, and it's gone. Clean record. Clean name. You walk into any guide office on this side of the world and they shake your hand. Noor Zhou, restored."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped ice axe—clattering, ringing, impossible to ignore. Eleven weeks of *Mara Ilves*, of tea and diesel and not looking at the mountains, and here was a stranger holding her whole life in a folder, offering to hand it back.

That was the thing about bait. It only worked if it was shaped like something you actually wanted.

"Nobody erases a blacklist for a retrieval job," she said. "People erase blacklists for silence. For leverage. For things that stay buried." She nodded at the map. "What did those six men find up there?"

"If they found anything," Voss said pleasantly, "you'll bring it to me and I'll decide what it's worth. That's not your concern."

"It became my concern the moment you offered me my name back for it. Nothing costs that much unless somebody's afraid of it."

For the first time, something moved behind his face—not quite respect, more like a merchant noting a customer who reads contracts. "You always were the clever one on the mountain. That's what the transcripts say, anyway. Clever right up until the whiteout." He stood, folded the map, and held it out. Snow had started coming down, fine and dry, the kind that looked gentle and meant business. "Three weeks. Radio check-ins every second day on this frequency, coded. Miss two, and I assume you've gone native, and then Mara Ilves develops a tax problem, an immigration problem, and a former employer with a long memory. The debt doesn't vanish, Noor. It travels."

She didn't take the map. Not yet. She made him hold it there in the falling snow, arm extended, and she looked past him at the range—finally, deliberately, letting herself look—the peaks going blue-black under the first clouds, the pass she'd sworn off standing open like a wound that hadn't decided to close.

Four dead clients. An inquiry she still couldn't reconstruct in her own memory without the ground tilting. And now a fixer with soft hands wanted her to walk back into the same sky, chasing six men who'd stopped existing on paper nineteen days ago, in exchange for a forgiveness she'd stopped believing anyone could sell.

It stank. It stank like a crevasse breathing out cold air over snow that looked solid and wasn't. Every instinct she'd built her surviving life on said: *walk away, stay Mara, stay small, stay alive.*

But the folder with her name in it stayed in his coat, and the debt stayed in his pocket, and the mountains stayed where they were, patient as creditors.

Noor took the map.

"One condition," she said. "I go alone. No handlers, no shadow teams, no second party. If I find out someone's following me up there, the deal's off and I get loud about what you're really after."

Something flickered across Voss's face—there and gone, quick as a bird's shadow—and he smiled his measured smile and said, "Of course. Alone. It's your name on the line."

Later, much later, she would remember that flicker, and the way he'd agreed too easily, and hate herself for the third time in her life for believing a man who needed her more than he feared her.

That night she packed her bag by lamplight in the back room of the canteen, checking each piece of gear the way you check a rosary: rope, rack, stove, the old aluminum compass with the cracked bezel that had survived worse than her. At the bottom, wrapped in a sock, she found the photograph she'd promised herself she'd burned—Dario grinning on a moraine, sunburned, pointing at a peak neither of them would ever stand on—and she put it in her breast pocket instead of the stove, because some weights you carry on purpose.

Before dawn she left money under the teapot for the woman who never asked questions, and walked north out of Karabulak with the snow squeaking under her boots and the Karakol Range rising ahead of her, white and silent, keeping whatever it kept.

Behind her, at the edge of town, a second set of headlights waited until she passed, then followed.
