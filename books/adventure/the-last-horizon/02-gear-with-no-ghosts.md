## 2. Gear With No Ghosts

The Karakol Range did not welcome her back. It simply endured her, the way stone endures weather.

Noor climbed the last moraine ridge in the grey hour before dawn, when the mountains are neither lit nor dark but merely present, and stopped where the ground flattened into the broad gravel shelf that every map called Base Camp and no one who worked here ever bothered to name. She had expected ruin. That was what Voss's file implied—abandoned camp, scattered equipment, the usual archaeology of panic. Teams that vanished left messes behind them. Fear was untidy. Fear knocked over stoves and tore through tent walls and dropped packs mid-stride.

There was no mess.

The four expedition tents stood in a neat row, guy-lines taut, snow skirts buried exactly as a careful camp boss would have ordered. The cook shelter's fly was staked square. A row of boots sat outside the mess tent, toes pointed toward the mountain, laces tucked inside as if their owners had stepped out of them for a moment and meant to come back. The propane cylinders stood ranked against a rock like soldiers at inspection.

Noor stood at the edge of it for a long time with her breath smoking and her hand resting on the ice axe she had not yet decided she needed.

Camps like this were not abandoned. They were paused.

She moved through the site the way she'd been taught twenty years ago by a lead guide who is now a smear of rumor and blame attached to her own name: perimeter first, then center, then the details nobody thinks matter. The perimeter told her nothing except that whoever had left had done so in order. The center—the mess tent—told her more than she wanted.

Inside, the table was set. Not used—set. Tin plates stacked beside the stove, mugs upside down on a drying rack, a sack of flour rolled closed at the top with two folds and a clip. The food stores were nearly full. She counted calories out of habit: enough for six people for eleven days at hard rations, nine at comfortable ones. The team had been provisioned for three weeks. Their last log entry, according to Voss's file, was nineteen days old.

Eleven days of food, sitting under a fly that any passing herder could have found, in a valley where even the ravens were starving by late autumn. Nobody had come back for it. Nobody local had taken it. That fact alone rearranged something in her chest. Hungry people do not leave eleven days of food. So either the team had walked out alive—and no one walks out of the Karakol in October without surfacing somewhere—or they had not been able to walk anywhere at all.

Or they had gone somewhere they believed they'd return from quickly.

She crouched at the radio table. Two sets: the expedition's long-range unit and a backup handheld, both government issue, both newer than anything the regional survey office had any business owning. Dead. Not low-battery dead—dead dead. She cracked the battery compartment of the handheld and felt her stomach drop a half-inch. The cells hadn't drained. They'd been pulled, all of them, from both radios, and the compartments left open to the cold. Someone had made sure these radios would never speak again, and had done it calmly enough to leave the screws in a tidy little pile.

You don't disable your own radios because you're lost. You disable them because you don't want to be heard.

Noor sat back on her heels in the cold canvas dimness and let that thought finish forming. Then she went looking for ghosts, and found none. No journal. No field notes. No personal effects worth stealing and none left behind anyway—no letters, no photographs, no spare glasses, no lucky charms. Six men had lived in this camp for weeks and had packed away every trace of being people. The tents held sleeping bags rolled tight, pads strapped, duffels zipped. It looked less like an evacuation than like a departure scheduled in advance, down to the minute.

Except for one thing.

* * *

She found it on the second morning, after a night in her own one-person tent pitched deliberately fifty meters downslope of the dead camp, because some instincts you don't argue with. The junior guide was lying in a hollow above the lateral moraine, half-buried in wind-sifted snow, and if the light had been any flatter she would have walked past him.

He was alive. Barely. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three, frost-nipped across the cheekbones, lips split and black at the edges, hands drawn up inside a jacket that belonged to someone bigger than him. His eyes tracked her when she knelt, which surprised her; everything else about him suggested he should have been three days past tracking anything.

"Sefa," he managed, when she asked. Just the name. She gave hers—her false one, the clean one, the woman who had never guided anything worse than a church group—and he didn't react, which meant either he didn't know it or didn't care. She got water into him, then sugar, then the foil bivvy from her pack, working with the economy of someone who has done this in worse places. He was hypothermic but not catastrophically, dehydrated but not beyond saving, and his feet, when she got his boots off, were bad but whole. Somebody had taught this boy how to survive. He just hadn't been taught how to survive alone.

"Where are the others?" she asked.

His eyes went past her, up-slope, toward the glacier headwall, and stayed there.

"Gone ahead." His voice was a dry rasp, like wind through scree. "They said—" A cough folded him in half. She waited. "They said stay with the camp. Radio silence until they came back."

"When?"

He didn't answer. His gaze was still fixed on the headwall, and now she saw why: not at the wall itself, but at a shallow gully cutting its flank, a line of broken ground she'd noted the evening before and filed under *nothing*. She followed his stare and felt the hair rise along her forearms beneath two layers of fleece.

"How long have you been here, Sefa?"

"Nine days." He said it steadily, the way people say things they've repeated to themselves so often the horror has worn smooth. "They said two. Maybe three."

Nine days. Alone, at altitude, on short rations—he'd stretched the camp food with discipline or luck—and he had not touched the radios, because the radios were already dead and he was a junior guide obeying the last instruction a senior had given him. Obedience had kept him alive and pinned him in place simultaneously. Noor knew exactly what that felt like.

"What were they going to find?" she asked.

And there—the question landed, and something moved behind his eyes that was not confusion and not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of a man deciding whether the person crouched over him belongs to the story or to whatever sent the story.

"You're not with them?" he whispered. "The others. The ones with the new ropes."

"No."

"The ones before you?"

"I'm the only one who came."

Something in her voice must have convinced him, or something in his face must have decided it no longer mattered, because he exhaled—a long, rattling surrender of breath—and his hand came out of the bivvy wrap and caught her sleeve with fingers that had almost no strength left in them at all.

"There's a room," he said. "Under the glacier."

* * *

She got him talking in pieces, the way you get any survivor talking—one thread at a time, never pulling. Water first. Sugar. Then words.

The team had found a fissure. Not on any survey map—not on *any* map, Sefa said, and here his voice took on the particular flatness of a man reporting something he'd checked twice because he couldn't believe it once. A crevasse line near the glacier's confluence, unmarked, which happened sometimes, but this one breathed. Warm air coming up out of it. In October. At four thousand meters.

Warm air means a space. A big one. And a big warm space under a glacier means either geothermal activity the entire geological record of this range failed to mention, or something man-made with a heat source still running.

"They went down on ropes," Sefa whispered. "All five. The lead said if the radio crackles, I say nothing. If anyone comes, I say we're surveying the moraine." His throat worked. "They were excited. Even the quiet one. Dr. Aslanov was laughing. I never heard him laugh."

"And then?"

"Then nothing. Three days. Four." His grip tightened on her sleeve, and pain crossed his face that had nothing to do with frostbite. "On the fifth day I heard something. Through the ground. Like—" He struggled. "Like a door. Far away. Under me. One boom, and then a hum, very low, and then nothing." His eyes finally came up off the headwall and fixed on hers, and they were clear, terribly clear, the eyes of someone who has spent nine days arriving at a conclusion. "I think they closed it. I think they closed it behind them, or something closed it, and the others with the new ropes came after, and I ran."

He had run. Down the gully, off-route, through terrain that had beaten him bloody and left him in this hollow to die slowly within sight of eleven days of untouched food. A boy running from a door sound.

"The room under the glacier," Noor said quietly. "That's what they found."

"That's what they found." His eyes were closing now, the sugar and warmth and safety pulling him under the way they pull everyone who has been holding on too long. "Madam. Madam." His hand found her sleeve again. "Don't go down there."

"Why not?"

But he was already going, consciousness folding up like a chart, and the last thing he said was barely a word at all, slurred and sinking:

"...it counts..."

* * *

She left him at dusk, stabilized, wrapped in both their insulation layers, with a note in three languages pinned to the bivvy and a signal mirror hung where the morning light would strike it. There was a Kyrgyz herding family two valleys east who owed the mountains better than they got from them; she'd flagged their winter camp on her way in. Twelve hours' travel for a fit runner. Less, if the weather held, and the weather was going to hold exactly long enough to punish whoever trusted it.

Then she climbed.

Not far. Not into the gully—not yet. Just high enough on the lateral moraine to see the crevasse line in the last flat light, and to read the snow the way she had once read it better than anyone in the range, before a report she never signed took that reputation and fed it into a shredder.

The surface told its story plainly, because the surface always does; it's people who lie. Five rope teams had descended at the fissure over some span of days—she counted the anchors, counted the rope wear on the lip, counted the tracks of the second party, the ones with the new ropes, the ones Sefa had run from. But it was the sixth set of marks that stopped her breath.

A single line of boot prints, crisp-edged, unweathered, no older than two days, walking up the slope from the fissure. Walking *up*. Steady stride, unhurried, a man carrying weight on his right shoulder—the left-side prints dug fractionally deeper. The prints climbed for two hundred meters, past the gully, past the point of sightlines, past any sane turnback—

—and ended.

Mid-slope. Not faded, not drifted over, not cut off by rock or ice. Ended. As though the walker had stepped out of the world between one stride and the next.

Noor stood over the last print for a long time while the temperature fell around her like a closing fist, and the dread she had carried since the cook tent—that orderly, foodless, ghostless camp—sharpened and turned, the way dread does when it stops being fear of dying and becomes hunger to know. She hated herself for it even as she felt it take hold. Obsession was just fear that had found a direction.

Below her, Sefa slept. Above her, the fissure breathed its warm, impossible breath into the freezing dusk.

Somewhere under the ice, a door had boomed shut, and something up here was still counting.

She turned toward the crevasse line and began to climb.
