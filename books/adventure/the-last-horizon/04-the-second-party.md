## 4. The Second Party

Noor found the tracks at dawn, three days above the dead camp, and her first thought was not *who* but *how many*.

Six sets of boots, machine-stamped soles, military gait—short strides, even spacing, no wandering. These were not lost men. These were men following a line someone else had drawn for them. She crouched over the prints where they crossed the wind-scoured saddle below the serac field and pressed two fingers into one. The edges were still crisp. Hours old, maybe less.

She looked back the way she'd come, down the long white throat of the glacier she'd been climbing since Sefa's whisper. Then up, toward the fissure that glowed faint green under the ice when the light hit it right—the room under the glacier, the thing Sefa had died trying to name.

Someone was racing her to it.

* * *

Ilya Renko counted his team by habit: Petrov on point, the Kessler twins sweeping left flank, Marchetti dragging rear with the radio sled, and Danner—Danner, who was supposed to be watching their backtrail and had instead wandered forty meters off the line to photograph an ice formation like a man on holiday.

"Close it up," Ilya said into the wind. "Tighten the rope."

Danner waved without turning around. Twenty-six years old, Voss's nephew's favorite, hired because a senator's office wanted eyes on the ground and Danner's uncle owed somebody a favor. He'd been complaining about the cold since base camp, which in Ilya's experience meant he'd be the first to make a fatal mistake.

The mistake came eleven minutes later.

It came as a sound first—a crack like a rifle shot under their feet, rolling up through the glacier's bones—and then the snowfield simply opened, a blue-lipped seam yawning wide enough to swallow a truck, and Danner dropped out of the world.

"Rope!" Ilya was already moving, driving his axe into the crust, feeling the team's weight catch behind him. The line went taut. Somewhere below, muffled by thirty meters of ice, Danner was screaming in a register Ilya felt in his teeth rather than heard.

Petrov reached the lip first and leaned over. "He's on a bridge. Snow bridge—it's holding, barely. There's a shelf."

"How far down?"

"Twenty-five meters. Maybe thirty."

Ilya did the arithmetic every mountain man does and hates: descent time, storm window, the weight of a panicked man on a rotten bridge. They could get him. It would cost them four hours, maybe five, and the weather coming off the western wall looked like a fist closing.

"We rig a haul system," he said. "Petrov, anchors. Twins, on the line. Marchetti—"

"That won't be necessary," said a voice from the ridge above them.

* * *

Noor had been shadowing the second party since midmorning, staying high on the lateral moraine where her dark coat read as just another rock against the scree. Six men. Satellite phone. A radio sled loaded with gear too heavy for survey work and too specialized for salvage. And a leader who moved like he'd been briefed on the route before he ever saw it—which meant someone had given him the same map Voss had burned into her memory.

That was the moment the shape of it finally resolved for her. Not one team. Two. Maybe more, seeded across the range like spores. Voss wasn't sending people to find the station. He was sending people to find each other—to confirm the route, to test the ice, to die usefully if the mountain objected. Witnesses were liabilities. Redundancy was cheap insurance. She'd spent six years telling herself the disaster that broke her had been bad luck and worse judgment. Now she watched a second expedition walk the same pattern and understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with altitude, that she might have been redundancy once too.

Then the glacier cracked open and swallowed one of them, and every strategic thought in her head got shoved aside by something older and simpler.

She was down the moraine before she'd decided to move, crampons biting, uncoiling her rope as she ran.

* * *

Ilya turned toward the voice and found a woman standing on the ridge silhouetted against the white sky, rope coiled over one shoulder like a bandolier. Dark hair whipped loose from her hood. Eyes the color of the ice they stood on.

"You have six men and one climber," she called down. "You need two climbers and about twenty minutes you don't have. I'm offering both."

"Who are you?"

"Doesn't matter. The storm does." She was already descending toward them, moving with a fluency that made Ilya's stomach tighten—not guide-fluent, not sport-climber fluent. Something harder. Something learned where mistakes weren't survivable. "Your man's on a snow bridge. If he keeps screaming he'll bring it down with him. Somebody needs to get on the rope and shut him up, or you're hauling up pieces."

Marchetti looked at Ilya. Petrov looked at Ilya. Nobody looked away from the woman.

"Give me a reason to trust you," Ilya said.

"I'm saving your man. That's the reason. Trust is what you decide afterward." She reached them, dumped her pack, and started building an anchor with fast, brutal economy—a buried axe, a deadman, equalized, tested with one sharp pull. "Name's Noor. That's all you get until we're done."

* * *

She went over the lip on Ilya's rope while his team hauled from above, and the crevasse closed around her like a cathedral. Blue light fell in shafts through the crack overhead. The walls rose fifty meters, ancient compressed ice banded with ash layers from volcanoes dead a thousand years—strata of the world's memory, and her dangling in the middle of it on a thread of nylon.

Danner hung eight meters below her on a shelf of snow that groaned every time he shifted.

"Hey." She kept her voice flat, bored almost. "Look at me. Not down. At me."

"I can't—I can't feel my—"

"Yes you can, and you're going to keep wiggling your fingers while you talk to me. What's your name?"

"D-Danner."

"Danner. Good name. Boring name. You're going to be fine, because I've pulled idiots out of worse than this, and because you're going to do exactly what I say." She descended, matched positions beside him, clipped into his line. The snow bridge beneath them flexed and made a sound like a held breath. "Now we go up, slow, and you don't look down, and if you throw up you aim away from me."

It took nineteen minutes. When they came over the lip, the twins grabbed Danner and Ilya caught Noor's arm as she rolled onto the surface, and for a second they were face to face, close enough that she could see the frost in his stubble and he could see the scar running through her left eyebrow.

"Renko," he said. "Ilya Renko."

"Noor Zhou." She let him hold the name a beat, watching what it did to him. Nothing. Either he didn't know it, or he was very good at hiding that he did. Both possibilities interested her.

"You knew where we were," he said. "You were tracking us."

"I was tracking everyone. Turns out there's a lot of everyone." She sat back on her heels and looked at him steadily. "Voss sent you."

It wasn't a question, and she watched him decide whether to lie. He decided, visibly, not to bother.

"He sent us to locate a site and secure any materials. Standard contract."

"Standard." She laughed, short and without humor. "How many contracts did he sign this season, Renko? How many teams are walking this glacier right now thinking they're the only ones?"

Ilya didn't answer, and the silence answered for him. She saw it land—the recalculation behind his eyes, the same one she'd done three days ago. Six men who thought they were a mission discovering they were a margin.

"The storm's coming," she said, standing. "There's a shelter—old survey hut, Soviet-era, half-buried, two hours east of here. Room for twelve. Your radio sled won't reach anyone tonight anyway; the wall kills signal past the seracs. We can freeze separately or argue somewhere warm."

"Why would you want to travel with us?"

"Because whoever sent you also sent me," she said, shouldering her pack, "and I want to know why he needs so many of us. Don't you?"

* * *

They reached the hut as the first gusts arrived, hard horizontal grains that sandblasted the paint off anything exposed. Inside: iron bunks, a rusted stove, walls layered with decades of graffiti in languages none of them spoke anymore. Petrov got the stove lit. Danner sat wrapped in every spare layer they owned, shaking, alive, staring at nothing.

Noor took the corner with sightlines to the door and both windows, and Ilya noticed, and chose not to mention it.

"You've done this before," he said, settling across from her with two tin cups of melted snow tea. "Crevasse work. Long-line rescue. That wasn't training, that was scar tissue."

"I guided in the Karakol for nine years."

"And then?"

She turned the cup in her hands. Outside, the wind climbed a register, finding the gap under the door and keening through it.

"And then there was an accident," she said. "Four dead. My name came off every permit list on the range. That's the short version."

"What's the long version?"

"The long version has a man named Voss in it, and a route he needed mapped, and a teammate whose maps were better than anyone's." She looked up. "You knew Dario Vance."

Ilya went very still. It lasted half a second, but she'd spent years learning to read stillness, and half a second was plenty.

"You flinched," she said quietly.

"I met him once. Kathmandu. Before your—" He stopped himself. Before your accident. He drank his tea instead of finishing the sentence.

"Before my accident," she finished for him. "Funny word for it. Everyone uses that word. Accident." She set the cup down. "Here's what I know now, Renko. Voss sent a team to find a station under this glacier. He sent yours. He probably sent others. He burns through people like kindling, and six years ago he burned through mine—and I'm starting to think the fire was set on purpose. So either you tell me what you know about Dario Vance tonight, over tea, or you tell me tomorrow, over something less pleasant. But you'll tell me."

The stove ticked. Danner shivered. The wind tried the door like a hand testing a lock.

Ilya set down his cup, and when he spoke, his voice was low enough that only she could hear it.

"There's something you should know about the maps Dario was carrying," he said. "They weren't his."
