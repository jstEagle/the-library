## 5. Blacklist

The storm shelter had been built by miners, back when the Karakol still had mines, and it showed in its bones: riveted steel walls gone the color of rust and old blood, a stove that coughed more smoke than heat, bunks bolted to the floor as if someone had once feared the mountain itself would shake them loose. Noor had passed it a dozen times over her guiding years and never once needed it. Now it was the only room left in the world.

The wind outside was not wind anymore. It was a solid thing, a wall of white that leaned against the shelter and shoved, testing the rivets, finding the seams. Snow came through the vent pipe in fine needles that glittered in the lamplight like metal filings. Somewhere above them, the second party's camp was being erased—tents, tracks, the whole evidence of Ilya's team, folded into the white.

Noor sat with her back to the steel wall and her boots off, working feeling back into her toes one at a time. Frostnip on the left foot, second toe. She'd seen men lose toes to less. She massaged until the pain came, and welcomed it, because pain meant blood, and blood meant she was still a body and not yet a ghost.

Ilya crouched at the stove, feeding it slabs of peat brick with the economy of a man who had learned to count everything. He hadn't spoken since they'd made the shelter. Neither had she. There was a particular silence between two people who have each just watched the other do something unforgivable-adjacent—she had saved his scout from the crevasse, which meant she now knew things about his operation he'd rather she didn't, and he had let her see his face when he realized Voss had sent him up here to die, which meant she owned something of his that could never be bought back.

He set a tin cup of melted snow on the floor between them. She took it. The water tasted of rust and peat smoke. It was the best thing she'd ever drunk.

"Dario Kessler," Ilya said.

Noor's hands went still around the cup.

"He climbed with you. Four years. The Annapurna traverse, the winter attempt on Dzhengiz, the season you guided the German film crew." Ilya didn't look at her. He watched the stove door, where the fire breathed in and out like something sleeping. "I carried his rope once. In the Pamirs. Before either of us mattered to anyone."

"You're lying," Noor said. It came out flat, automatic, the way a hand jerks back from a hot plate before the mind has decided anything.

"I'm telling you what I know." He turned then, and his face in the lamplight was older than it had been on the glacier, older than it had been an hour ago. "Dario drew maps. You knew that. Not route maps—survey maps. He had a contract nobody talked about. A private client. He'd go out ahead of your expeditions, take bearings, log coordinates, come back with pages nobody was allowed to see."

The cup was warm in her hands. That was the wrong detail for her mind to fix on, but grief does what it wants. *He never told me.* Four years roped together, four years of storms and summit pushes and cheap wine in base camp tents, and he had never told her.

"The disaster," Ilya said quietly. "The Serac Traverse."

"Don't."

"Three dead. Your name on the report. Your license burned. Blacklisted across every range that matters." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You've spent six years believing you did something wrong. Or that you failed to prevent something. Or that the mountain simply took its price and chose you to pay it."

"Stop." Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it.

"Voss bought the serac," Ilya said.

* * *

Later—minutes or hours; time had gone soft as the snow against the walls—she would try to remember exactly how he said it, whether there was any way he could have said it that wouldn't have broken something in her. She would decide there wasn't. That was no comfort at all.

"A fixed rope rots," Ilya said. "Everyone knows it. Everyone checks it. You checked yours—I've read the incident file, Noor, I read everything before I take a contract. Your anchors were sound. The serac above the traverse had been stable for eleven seasons. And then, on one morning, in one hour, it calved onto a route that three separate teams had crossed safely that same week."

"Weather," she said. "Ice fails. It's a mountain. That's the whole—" But even as she said it, she heard how thin it sounded, how much like a prayer repeated past the point of belief.

"There was a man on your support team. Porters' logistics. Hired late, paid well, references from a company that didn't exist." Ilya fed another brick to the stove, unhurried, as if the words cost him nothing, though she could see now that they cost him plenty—that this speech had been sitting in him like a swallowed stone for years. "He placed two small charges under the serac's load line. Low yield. Enough to start a slide that nature would finish and sign. He was gone down-valley before your team reached the traverse."

Noor stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand. Her legs did it without her, and the shelter tilted, and she caught herself against the riveted wall, and the steel was cold through her shirt, cold enough to hurt, and she held on because holding on was all there was.

"That's not possible," she said. "There was an investigation. There were—"

"Bought," Ilya said. "The investigator's report was drafted in an office in Almaty before the bodies came down. Voss needed the blacklist to be real. He needed you ruined, publicly, permanently, because a disgraced guide asks no questions about who her old partner is climbing for. Dario kept drawing maps. Dario kept getting closer to the station. And every year, the client tightened the leash."

"And then?"

"And then Dario found it." Ilya's voice dropped, and the wind filled the space he left, howling against the vent pipe like something demanding entry. "Two years ago. The room under the glacier. He logged the coordinates, sealed them in his maps, and he tried to renegotiate. Wanted out. Wanted money, wanted distance, wanted his name disentangled from whatever Voss was building. Three weeks later he fell on a route he'd climbed solo a dozen times."

The tin cup slipped from Noor's hand. It rang against the steel floor, a small bright bell of a sound, absurdly loud, and neither of them moved to pick it up.

She thought of Dario laughing in base camp, teaching her a card game with rules he invented mid-hand so he always won. She thought of the last time she saw him, at a truck stop below the range, him saying *stay clean, Zhou, stay small, they'll forget you eventually,* and her thinking it was advice about the blacklist. She thought of six years. Six years of false names in border towns, of jobs taken under other women's licenses, of mothers pulling children away from her in teahouses when the whisper went round—*that's her, that's the one who killed them.* Six years of carrying three deaths that were never hers to carry, worn smooth inside her like river stones, so familiar she had mistaken them for organs.

Her exile had been purchased. Her shame had been manufactured. Someone had written her ruin the way she wrote a route plan—in advance, in ink, with contingencies—and she had lived inside it faithfully ever since, the way a prisoner lives inside a sentence, believing the sentence was justice.

"Noor." Ilya's voice, careful. "Sit down. Drink something."

"His maps." Her voice came out low and level, and it frightened her, how level. "Voss has Dario's maps. That's why we're here. That's why any of us are here. He sent you to find what Dario died hiding, and he sent me because—" She stopped. Because a blacklisted guide is expendable. Because a woman with nothing left will walk into anything. Because her ruin had always been a tool, and tools get used until they break.

"Because you're deniable," Ilya said. "Same as me. Same as my scout in the crevasse. We're not a search party, Noor. We're witnesses. And Voss doesn't keep witnesses."

* * *

She slept, or something near sleep, wedged against the wall with her rope coiled under her head. In the dark behind her eyes the mountain rearranged itself: the serac falling again and again, slow now, in her memory's new light, and beneath the white thunder of it a small man with a company that didn't exist, kneeling in the blue shadow with his little charges, doing arithmetic with other people's lives. Three names. Vasquez. Otarov. Lindqvist. She had said those names aloud every night for six years like penance, like a rosary, and the whole time the debt had belonged to someone else, and the someone else had grown rich on it.

When she woke, the lamp had burned low and Ilya was awake too, watching her from his bunk with an expression she couldn't read in the dark.

"You could still sell it," he said. "The vault. Walk away rich, buy your name back, buy a coast somewhere. Voss erases debts. It's the only thing he does cleanly."

"My name isn't for sale," she said. "It never was. That was the whole point of taking it away."

Outside, the storm had begun to spend itself, the shriek dropping by degrees toward a deep, exhausted hush. Through the frost-furred vent window, the first grey light showed the upper glacier, and on the glacier, impossibly, a line of fresh tracks—descending toward the station from the north, from the col where no party should be, cut crisp into the overnight snow.

Someone else had reached the mountain in the night.

And below them, far under the ice, the floodlights of the buried station were burning again.
