## 6. The Room Under the Glacier

The vault door was not locked. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and the thing that frightened her most.

They had spent four hours on the outer seal—a pressure hatch crusted in fifty years of frost, its wheel seized until Ilya beat it free with a piton hammer, each strike ringing down the corridor like a bell tolling for someone. She'd expected the inner door to be worse. Bank-grade steel, maybe, or another seal they'd have to burn through with the thermite kit Ilya carried against regulations.

Instead it swung open at a touch.

"Someone's been through," she said.

Ilya didn't answer. His headlamp swept the room beyond, and the light came back wrong—flat, swallowed. Paper. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, paper.

Noor stepped through and her boots crunched on drifts of documents that had slid from their shelves over the decades, a snowfall of paper deeper than the snow outside. The station's records vault was larger than the rest of the complex combined: a chamber cut into the glacier's heart, insulated by the ice itself, dry as a tomb. Steel shelving ran in ranks into the dark, and every rank was full. Binders. Folders. Canisters of film. Maps rolled in oilcloth and stacked like cordwood.

"It's all here," Ilya said quietly. He had stopped pretending to be anything but hungry. "All of it."

She pulled a binder at random from the nearest shelf and opened it under her lamp. Survey sheets, dense with coordinates and elevation readings, dated forty-one years ago. The handwriting was careful, bureaucratic, patient. Someone had spent an entire summer walking this range with instruments, recording where the water went.

She turned pages. Aquifer maps. Meltwater channels traced through the massif in blue ink, each one annotated with flow volumes by season. A second section tabulated the villages downstream—the same valley communities she'd grown up among, listed by name, by population, by distance from the headwaters. Next to each entry, in the same patient hand, a figure she took a moment to understand: cubic meters per day, potential diversion yield.

They hadn't been surveying the mountain. They'd been pricing it.

"Ilya." Her voice came out strange. "Look at this."

He was already three shelves deep, pulling canisters, cracking them open, fanning film negatives across a crate lid. But he came when she called, and he read fast—he'd been a military cartographer once, before whatever came after—and she watched his face change as he understood what he was holding.

"Water rights," he said. "Complete hydrological surveys of the entire range. Every source, every channel, every—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know what this is worth? A government would pay—no. Companies. The companies pay more, and they don't ask how you got it."

"The villages," Noor said.

"What about them?"

"They're in here. By name. Population counts. Where the wells are. Where the grazing land sits relative to the diversion points." She turned the binder so he could see. "This isn't a survey. It's a shopping list."

Ilya took the binder from her hands gently, the way you take something fragile, and that gentleness frightened her more than greed would have. He laid it open on the crate lid beside the film and stood looking down at both.

"A company buys this," he said, thinking aloud, working it through the way he worked through everything, methodically, without cruelty and without mercy, "they don't need to displace anyone. Not violently. They divert two headwater streams, buy the lowland water rights through shell companies, and inside five years the valleys are uneconomic. People leave on their own. Nobody shoots anybody. It's clean."

"You've done this math before."

"I've seen it done." He met her eyes. "In three countries. This is how it works now, Noor. Nobody comes with soldiers anymore. They come with paper."

* * *

They worked through the station's afternoon, because the station kept no other kind. Noor told herself she was cataloguing—mapping the vault's contents so she could report back to Voss, fulfill the contract, get her name cleared. That was the story she was selling herself, and she almost believed it while her hands were busy.

But her hands kept stopping.

A mineral seam survey, cross-referenced to depth soundings through the glacier: lithium, in quantities that made the numbers look like typos. A geological folio on the eastern approaches, marked with proposed road grades—roads that would run straight through the high pastures where the transhumant families moved their herds every spring, the routes older than any border drawn on paper. A demographic annex, typed on a machine whose ribbon had faded unevenly, listing every settlement in the watershed with columns for ethnicity, land tenure status, and a final column headed only *Disposition*, filled with abbreviations she couldn't decode and didn't need to.

Her own erased history lived in this logic. She recognized it the way you recognize a face in a crowd—a shape you've seen before, wearing different clothes. The expedition that ruined her had been full of this same grammar: reports written about people instead of for them, decisions made in rooms where the people affected were data points, her own name entered into a blacklist ledger by someone who had never stood on the mountain where Dario fell. She had spent six years believing her disgrace was a verdict. Standing here, she understood it had been a transaction. And this room was the ledger everything else was written in.

"Noor." Ilya's voice, careful. "You've been staring at that page for ten minutes."

She looked down. The demographic annex. *Disposition.* "My village is in here," she said. "Karagöl. Population at time of survey, two hundred and eleven. There's a note about seasonal access routes." She closed the binder. "Someone sat in this room fifty years ago and wrote down my grandmother's well."

Ilya was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its broker's polish. "And if we sell it, in fifty years someone else writes down what happened after. Also in a room. Also in numbers."

"That doesn't sound like a man planning to sell."

"It sounds like a man who knows exactly what it's worth." He rubbed his face with both hands, gloves tucked under his arms against the cold. "You want me to say it? Fine. My team gets paid either way. Voss pays for recovery; the buyer pays for delivery. I don't need to feel anything about it. Most days I don't." He looked at the shelves, ranks of them vanishing into the dark, fifty years of patient documentation of everything the valleys were. "But I did the arithmetic on the walk in. Do you know how many people live in these watersheds? I do. I counted. Forty thousand, give or take. And I keep trying to find the version of the calculation where the number on the other side justifies it, and there isn't one. There's just money, moving to other people who already have it."

"So don't sell it."

"And then what?" He laughed, short and bitter. "We burn it? You think Voss doesn't have copies coming? You think whoever built this place didn't duplicate everything before they sealed it? Destroying information is a fairy tale, Noor. Information doesn't die. It waits."

"Then why does your voice shake?"

He didn't answer that. He picked up the water-rights binder again, held it, and set it back on the shelf with exaggerated care, as if returning something borrowed.

* * *

They made camp in the station's mess hall, behind a bulkhead door Ilya wedged shut with a climbing rack. Noor cooked snow over the stove and neither of them ate much. Through the porthole, the glacier's surface glowed faintly blue in the late light, beautiful and indifferent, and beneath it this room waited with its forty thousand lives filed in triplicate.

She lay in her bag listening to the ice tick and settle, and thought about Sefa's whisper—*the room under the glacier*—and about the fresh boot prints she'd found in the corridor, days old, heading in and never out. Somewhere between those prints and this shelf of binders was the answer to what had happened to the survey team, and she found she no longer wanted to know it badly enough to sleep.

What she wanted, lying in the dark under forty meters of ice, was simpler and worse. She wanted the last six years undone. She wanted Dario alive and her name clean and the mountains returned to being just mountains, places where water went where it had always gone. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like vertigo, that none of that was for sale—not with Voss's pardon, not with Ilya's buyer's money, not with anything in this vault. The only thing this room offered anyone was the power to do to other people what had been done to her. That was the entire inventory.

Near midnight, a sound woke her: Ilya, at the vault door, headlamp off, standing in the dark among the shelves. Not taking anything. Just standing there, one hand flat on the steel shelving, the way you touch a wall to steady yourself.

"Ilya," she said.

He didn't turn around. "Radio check," he said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything else.

Because that was when she heard it too—faint, through fifty meters of ice and rock, from the direction of the upper glacier: the rhythmic thrum of helicopter rotors, closing fast through the night air.

Voss had come for his property.
