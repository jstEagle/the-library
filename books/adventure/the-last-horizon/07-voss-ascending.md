## 7. Voss Ascending

I had told myself the lie so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone: *I'm not a traitor, I'm a survivor.* Standing at the mouth of the buried station with the vault key burning a hole in my chest pocket, I finally admitted the stone had been in my shoe the whole time.

The radio crackled at 0900. Not Noor's channel—hers was still dead, or she'd killed it, which amounted to the same thing. This was the frequency I'd been given in Almaty, in a hotel room that smelled of cigarettes and money, when Voss slid a satellite phone across the table and said, *You'll know when to use it.*

"Report," said the voice.

"We're inside. The vault's breached. She has the key."

A pause long enough for me to count my heartbeats. Then, almost gently: "Good work, Ilya. Stay where you are. We're coming to you."

The rotors came out of the south an hour later, gray against a sky the color of dirty wool. A helicopter in the Karakol Range meant one thing: someone with more money than sense, or more sense than mercy. It set down on the glacier shelf below the station with the arrogance of a thing that had never been told no, and six men spilled out into the snow, rifles slung casual across their chests, followed by a seventh figure in a black parka who walked like the mountain owed him rent.

Voss.

I watched from the station entrance as Noor stepped out beside me. She'd slept maybe two hours in three days. Frostbite had whitened the tips of two fingers on her left hand. She looked like a woman carved out of the expedition that had ruined her—and still, when she saw the helicopter, her spine straightened instead of bending.

"That's him," she said. Not a question.

"That's him."

"You called him."

It wasn't a question either. I made myself meet her eyes. "Yes."

Something moved behind her face—not surprise. Confirmation. She'd known. Maybe from the beginning. Maybe from the moment I appeared on her route with too-clean gear and too-precise questions about crevasse lines. She'd let me walk beside her anyway, because a traitor you can see is worth more than a storm you can't.

"Ilya." My name in her mouth sounded like a verdict being read.

"He'll offer you everything," I said quickly, because the men were closing and I had perhaps thirty seconds of honesty left in me. "Pardon, money, your name back. And he'll mean every word, and every word will be a knife. Take nothing he hands you with his right hand."

Voss stopped ten meters out and pulled back his hood. He was smaller than I remembered, grayer, but the eyes were the same—patient, ledger-keeping eyes, the eyes of a man who had never once lost track of what he was owed.

"Noor Zhou," he said, warm as a bank manager. "You have no idea how long I've looked for you."

* * *

He talked the way avalanches start: quietly, then all at once.

We stood in the station's main chamber while his men fanned through the corridors, checking corners that had held nothing but ice and dead floodlights for sixty years. The vault door stood open behind us. Through it, the records waited in their thousands—water rights, mineral seams, village by village, valley by valley, every community below the snowline mapped and priced like inventory. Voss hadn't looked at them yet. He was looking at Noor.

"Do you know what happened to Dario Kessler's family?" he asked.

Noor went very still. I watched her hand curl at her side, the frostbitten fingers not quite closing.

"They took a settlement," Voss continued. "Modest. They signed a paper saying the expedition's failure was operational, not criminal. That paper is why you're a ghost, Noor. One signature, three years ago, purchased by me, paid for with money I earned selling maps Dario drew. You've spent three years paying for a decision other people made about you." He spread his hands. "I can unmake it. Full pardon. The blacklist erased from every registry in four countries. Your guide's license reinstated. Plus enough money that you never have to stand in a border town under a false name again, waiting for men like me to find you."

"And all I have to do," Noor said, "is give you the key."

"The key. The data. Your silence." He smiled. "Consider it severance for services rendered. You found what my survey team couldn't survive finding. That has value. I pay my debts."

There it was—the word. *Debt.* I saw it land on her like a weight dropped from height, because I knew—I'd known since the shelter, since she told me about the collector's map, the false name, the trap she'd walked into with her eyes open—that debt was the architecture of her whole ruin. Voss had built her cage out of obligations she never agreed to, and now he stood there offering to sell her the door.

She looked at the key in her hand. Small thing. Brass and steel, cold enough to burn.

"How much?" she said.

My stomach dropped. Voss's smile widened a fraction, the smile of a fisherman feeling the line go taut.

"Name a figure."

"Everything," she said. "Every page. Every map. You want the valleys? Show me the contract. Show me exactly what happens to Kirgiz-deh, to the shepherd routes, to the villages on the southern watershed. In writing. Before I hand over anything."

Voss tilted his head, genuinely amused. "You want to read the fine print on the destruction of your own people?"

"I want to know what I'm buying," she said. "That's the difference between me and you."

And God help me, standing there in the frozen dark with my betrayal hanging off me like wet rope, I felt something I hadn't felt in years: shame with a direction. Because I knew what the contract said. I'd helped draft the annexes. Resettlement corridors. Access easements. Compensation schedules calculated per household, per head of livestock, per well. The valleys wouldn't be destroyed—they'd be *acquired*, which was worse, because acquisition came with paperwork that made the destruction legal.

"She's stalling," said one of Voss's men, a scarred bulk of a man named Ferro who'd been watching Noor the way a butcher watches a scale.

"Of course she's stalling," Voss said mildly. "She's exhausted. She's frostbitten. She's been hunted across a glacier for a week. Exhausted people stall." He turned back to Noor, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "Take the hour. Read whatever you like. But understand this, Noor: the weather is turning. When the front hits, that helicopter doesn't fly, and neither does your pardon. Everything I've offered walks away with me—or stays here, buried with you."

Outside, the light had begun to change. The clean gray of morning was curdling toward something darker, greener, the color the sky turns when the mountain decides it's done being polite.

Noor walked past Voss into the vault without answering him. I followed, because following her was the only honest thing left available to me.

* * *

Inside, she pulled pages at random and read them under the emergency lamps, her lips moving slightly, her frostbitten hand cradled against her ribs. Voss's men had dragged crates in behind us—Ferro stacking them by the door with the efficiency of a man preparing to move cargo, not archives. They were going to carry it all out by hand if they had to. Sixty years of data. Every life in the valleys reduced to tonnage.

"Ilya." She didn't look up. "The annex on water rights. Page forty. Read it to me."

I read it. The Syr headwaters, allocated. The spring melt, scheduled. Eleven villages flagged for "managed relocation" in phase two. I heard my own voice say the words and understood that I had written some of them, in a warm office, in a city where snow was something that fell on other people.

She closed the folder. For a long moment she just breathed, and I watched her do arithmetic behind her eyes—not money arithmetic. Something else. The cost of a thing versus the price of a thing.

"My whole life," she said quietly, "I thought I was paying off a mistake. Three years thinking maybe I'd misread the weather, missed the signs, killed those people because I wasn't good enough." She looked up at me, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "But it was purchased. My disgrace was a line item. Someone bought my ruin the way you'd buy a permit." She touched the key at her belt. "And now he wants to buy the last thing I have left."

"Noor—"

"Don't." She held up the damaged hand. "Don't apologize yet. Save it. You might need it soon, and I might need to hear it, and neither of us gets to spend it early."

From the main chamber, Voss's voice carried through the vault door, smooth and unhurried, giving orders. Fuel the helicopter. Watch the east ridge. And then, softer, to someone close: "If the woman becomes a problem, we solve it after the transfer. Cleanly. She's earned that much."

I looked at Noor. She'd heard it too. Of course she had. She was already moving—not toward the door, not toward Voss, but deeper into the vault, toward the maintenance shafts marked on the original 1968 schematics, the ones that ran up through the glacier to the incinerator chute on the surface.

The first gust hit the station then—a flat, physical blow that rattled the old floodlight housings and sent a fine powder of ice hissing down through the ventilation seams. Through the doorway I could see the men exchanging looks. Ferro said something low to Voss. Voss checked his watch, and for the first time since the helicopter landed, something flickered across his face that was not patience.

The barometer outside the vault door, a brass relic bolted to the wall by men who'd trusted instruments more than weather, was falling like a stone off a cliff.

Noor caught my eye across the lamplight, and she didn't say *help me*. She didn't have to. She just held up the key between two fingers—offering me the choice I'd come all this way to avoid—and above us, through sixty meters of ancient ice, the storm arrived like a door slamming shut on the world.
