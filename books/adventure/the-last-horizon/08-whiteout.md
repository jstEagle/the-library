## 8. Whiteout

The storm arrived the way debts do—slowly, then all at once.

Noor felt it first as a pressure behind her ears, a change in the air that made the station's dead fluorescent lights seem to hum. Then the wind came down off the col like something released from a cage, and within twenty minutes the world outside the vault door was gone. Not darkened. Not obscured. *Erased.* The windows became white walls. The sound of it was enormous, a freight train with no tracks, no destination, only weight.

They were trapped. All of them.

Voss's men had secured the main corridor by evening, six of them with automatic weapons and the brittle confidence of men who had never spent a winter above four thousand meters. Voss himself had taken the survey office, spreading Ilya's maps across Dario's old table as if he already owned what lay inside them. Noor sat where they'd put her—a chair in the radio room, a guard at the door who kept glancing at the frost climbing the window glass—and she counted.

She was good at counting. It was what you did on a mountain when there was nothing else to do. Count your breaths, count your steps, count the seconds between lightning and thunder, between a rope going taut and a scream. She counted the guards: six now, but one had not come back from checking the generator shed, and another's hands hadn't stopped shaking since the wind began. She counted the hours since the helicopter: nine. Fuel for the return flight: not enough, not in this. She counted Voss's patience, which she judged to be roughly equivalent to his fuel supply.

And she counted, over and over, the weight of the vault key against her sternum, where it hung on a cord under her jacket like a second heart.

Ilya had given it to her an hour before the storm hit, pressing it into her palm in the dark of the equipment bay without a word, his eyes saying everything his mouth wouldn't. *I built this. Let me be the one thing I build that doesn't break.*

She hadn't thanked him. Thanking him would have made it real, and if it was real, then he meant to die for it, and she wasn't ready to carry that yet.

The lights died at 0300.

Not flickered—died, all at once, and in the sudden black the wind sounded closer, as if it had been waiting for exactly this. Noor heard boots in the corridor, voices dropping low, the particular tension in men's breathing when they understand that the plan has stopped working. The generator shed had been buried or flooded or simply frozen; it didn't matter which. What mattered was that in the dark, Voss's arithmetic changed. Six armed men, no power, no extraction, a prize sealed behind a door that needed a key—and one key, hanging under a disgraced guide's jacket.

Noor had been in storms before. She knew what they did to men who thought of themselves as instruments of someone else's will. Instruments rust. Men freeze. And when the cold gets into the reasoning part of the mind, every man starts asking the same question: *why am I still here, and who decided it?*

By 0400, two of them were asking it out loud.

* * *

She heard it through the wall—the argument starting in the corridor, low and then not low. A man named Kessler, the one with the shaking hands, saying the word *helicopter* like a prayer. Another voice, Aris, telling him to shut up, that Voss would pay triple for loyalty in these conditions, and Kessler laughing, a cracked, terrible sound, saying *pay with what, pay us in what, we're going to be ice sculptures by morning and he knows it.*

Then a gunshot.

It went through the wall a hand's width above Noor's head, and plaster snowed down over her shoulders, and in the ringing aftermath she was already moving—not because she had a plan but because staying still in a room where men were shooting each other was the one thing worse than moving blind.

The door wasn't locked. She'd known it wouldn't be; the guard had left it ajar when the shooting started, drawn into the corridor like everyone else. She slipped out into chaos.

The emergency lighting had come on—dim amber strips along the floor, Cold War infrastructure doing its last faithful work. In that amber glow the corridor looked like the bottom of a well. Kessler was down. Aris stood over him with the pistol, breathing hard, and two more of Voss's men were at the far end, weapons up, shouting questions nobody could answer over the wind screaming through some breach in the station's skin.

And at the junction ahead, silhouetted against the amber, Ilya.

He caught her eye and didn't smile. He pointed—down the side passage, toward the old airlock on the station's eastern face, the one Sefa's whispered map had shown her, the one Ilya had checked and re-checked until she understood he'd been planning her exit since before the vault was even open. Then he turned back toward the corridor, toward the muzzle flashes beginning to stutter at its far end, and raised his own weapon.

*I'm sorry,* he mouthed. Or maybe he said *go.* In the wind and the gunfire it was impossible to know, and it didn't matter, because both words meant the same thing.

Noor ran.

Behind her the corridor dissolved into noise—shouting, more shots, Voss's voice rising above all of it, furious and cracking, ordering men to hold, to stop, to think of the money. She heard Ilya answer him, calm and almost gentle, the way you speak to a man you've already forgiven. She heard Voss understand, all at once, what Ilya had done.

She reached the airlock. The wheel was frozen; she broke the ice with her elbow and threw her whole body against the handle, once, twice, three times, and on the third turn it gave with a shriek that the wind swallowed whole.

The outside world took her like water takes a stone.

* * *

There is a kind of blindness that has nothing to do with eyes. Whiteout is its name, and Noor had survived it twice before, and both times she had sworn never again, because whiteout does not kill you quickly. It kills you by convincing you that direction is a myth, that up and down are opinions, that the last thirty meters you walked might have been a circle. Men sit down in whiteouts to rest and are found in spring, kneeling, as if in prayer to nothing.

She clipped herself to the fixed line she'd rigged along the eastern slope two days earlier—her own paranoia, her own rope, sunk into the ice with anchors she'd tested three times—and she stepped off into the white.

The wind hit her sideways. It was minus thirty at least, and falling, and the snow wasn't falling anymore so much as flying, horizontal, a solid thing, a wall that moved. Her headlamp showed her nothing except the inside of the storm itself, billions of particles flashing past like static, like the universe dissolving into its smallest parts. So she turned it off. Light in a whiteout is worse than darkness; it gives you a world of nothing and calls it something.

Instead she climbed by memory.

This was the thing no one had ever believed about her, the thing the inquiry had dismissed as arrogance: Noor Zhou did not need her eyes on this mountain. She had walked the route to the pass four times in the past week. She had counted the steps between the airlock and the first anchor—two hundred and ten, rising left. She knew the crevasse field began where the wind's note changed pitch, a half-tone higher, the sound funneling through the gap below the serac. She knew the slope steepened at the place where the rope would drag rightward across her hip, and that after the steepest section there was a shelf where the wind dropped for eleven paces—she had counted those paces too, standing there in sunlight, hoarding them against exactly this night.

So she climbed in the dark, inside the storm, counting.

Two hundred and ten steps. The rope dragging right. The wind's pitch rising. Eleven paces of mercy. Again. Again. Her world shrank to the length of her arms and the rhythm of her breath and the numbers ticking upward like a rosary. Frost built up inside her balaclava until she was breathing through ice. Her left hand stopped reporting in around the fourth hour; she tucked it into her armpit and let it sleep and climbed one-handed whenever the rope allowed.

Somewhere below and behind her, she knew, the station was burning through its last hours of men. She did not let herself think about Ilya in it. There would be time later, or there wouldn't, and either way the mountain did not care, and neither, tonight, could she.

What she let herself think about instead was the key.

It rode against her chest, warm from her body, and every hundred steps she pressed her mittened fist over it just to feel it there. Behind her it was worth a fortune. Ahead of it lay the pass, and the summit station's incinerator chute, and the only decision that had ever truly been hers. Voss would come. Of course he would. A man who had purchased her ruin years ago would crawl through this storm on his belly for what waited at the top. She had perhaps two hours of lead, maybe less, and the pass was four hours away in good conditions, and these were not good conditions.

But Voss's men had a mountain between them and her now, and a civil war in their ranks, and a leader who had just learned that his informant had chosen a disgraced guide over him. Voss would come himself, eventually, because men like Voss could not bear to lose things personally—but he would come late, and angry, and angry men make mistakes on mountains.

Noor intended to be nothing but a mistake for him to make.

At the fifth hour the wind's note changed again—the long, descending moan that meant the col—and she allowed herself, for the first time, to believe. Her legs were beyond pain, operating on some deeper contract. The rope ran out. She coiled it, stowed it, and struck out on memory alone across the final shoulder, counting steps that no longer mattered, because here the storm itself was the landmark: it broke apart at the pass, torn by the collision of winds, and through the tearing she saw it—

A light. Below and to the east. Moving.

Not the station. Not the pass beacon. A single headlamp, bobbing, climbing the southern approach faster than any man had a right to climb in this weather, closing on the pass from the direction Voss's helicopter had come from.

Noor stood at the threshold of the summit station, one hand on the incinerator chute's frozen hatch, and watched the light climb, and understood that whatever came next, it would happen here, in the thin cold silence above the storm, with everything she had ever lost and everything she refused to lose again gathered on one narrow ridge of ice.

She pulled the key from around her neck.

The lock on the incinerator hatch was older than she was. It resisted, then yielded, and the chute gaped open like a throat, breathing out a faint mineral warmth from the furnaces below—still live, still fed by the station's deep fuel lines, still hungry.

The light on the southern approach was close enough now that she could hear, beneath the wind, the crunch of boots.

Noor opened the satchel, and took out the first sheaf of pages, and held them over the fire.
