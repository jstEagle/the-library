## 10. Snowmelt

The melt came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, as if the mountain had been holding its breath since autumn and finally let it go.

Noor heard it before she saw it—the Karakol thawing in the dark, a sound like a thousand small rivers arguing beneath the snow. She lay in the loft of Tamara Aytbek's house above the valley and listened to the mountain come apart, and for the first time in longer than she could measure, the sound did not frighten her.

Avalanche season was over. The passes were opening.

She was up before dawn anyway. Old habit. She built the fire, set the kettle on, and laid out the day's gear by feel: rope, harness, the battered aluminum poles she'd traded a week of labor for, the map she kept folded in her chest pocket—not a government survey sheet, nothing so grand, just Tamara's husband's old hand-drawn thing, ink fading, trails annotated in a dead man's careful script. *Ice bridge unreliable after noon. Do not trust the moraine in fog.* It was worth more than everything that had burned at the summit pass, and it had cost nothing but a family's trust.

That was the economy she lived in now. Bread for rope. A lamb slaughtered in her honor for three days of guiding shepherds to the high summer pasture. A roof, a table, a place at the fire, in exchange for the one thing she still knew how to do. No one paid her money. No one would. Her name was still on the blacklists in every licensing office from the border towns to the capital—*Zhou, Noor. Negligence resulting in loss of life. Permanently barred.* The paper trail Voss had built around her ruin had outlived the man's ambitions, the way a burned forest outlives the fire.

She had stopped trying to correct it. There was no correcting it. There was only the mountain, and the people who needed to cross it, and the fact that her hands still remembered every route the way a lark remembers sky.

* * *

Sefa came up the trail as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, walking without a stick now, his gait almost even. The frostbite had taken two of his toes and very nearly his left foot; the doctors in the valley clinic, working with instruments older than he was, had managed to keep it. He'd spent the winter learning to walk again in Tamara's front room, cursing creatively in three languages, and now here he was at the trailhead at first light, breathing hard from the climb, grinning like a man who had been handed his life back in a burlap sack and found it mostly intact.

"You're early," Noor said.

"You're always early. Someone has to be late enough to make it interesting." He dropped onto the wall beside her and worked off his boot to check the stump, a ritual he performed without shame. The scar tissue had healed pink and tough. "The Karabulak family wants to move their herd to the high pasture Thursday. Old man Karabulak says he'll pay you in cheese and a rifle."

"Does the rifle work?"

"He says it works."

"He *says*." She snorted and looked down the valley. Below them, the village was waking—smoke from the bread ovens, a dog complaining at nothing, the river running brown and loud with snowmelt, higher than anyone had seen it in a decade. "Tell them Thursday. And tell them the cheese comes first."

Sefa laughed, then went quiet in the way he did sometimes, the way he had since the glacier, since the room under the ice, since the night she'd carried him down from base camp with his feet gone white and his mind full of floodlights. He looked at the mountain the way he always looked at it now—not with a guide's appraisal but with something closer to grief.

"I dreamed about it again," he said. "The station. The lights coming on when you touched the door."

"Dreams are cheap."

"You never dream about it?"

Noor considered the honest answer and, as usual, gave a slanted one. "I dream about the pass. The incinerator. The sound the paper makes when it burns. Like a flock of birds taking off all at once." She had fed the vault's records into the chute page by page while Voss's helicopter circled the col, while he screamed offers through the storm that the wind took and shredded. Water rights. Mineral seams. Every village in every valley below, mapped and priced and filed for disposal. It had burned for forty minutes. She had stood there with frostbite eating her fingers and watched it, and she had felt—this was the part she never said aloud, not even to Sefa—*nothing*. No triumph. Just a vast, clean emptiness, like the air above the snow line.

Then, later, walking down through the whiteout with nothing but memory to steer by, the emptiness had begun to fill with something quieter. Something that had not left since.

"They say Voss is finished," Sefa said. "My cousin works freight at the border. He says Voss's company missed its payments, that the survey contracts were pulled, that men who used to take his calls now hang up on him. He says—" Sefa hesitated. "He says Voss came through the border town in the spring, alone, looking older. Asking after a woman guide who worked under a false name. Nobody would tell him anything."

"Good," Noor said. "Nobody should."

"He offered my medical bills. After. Did you know that? A letter came through the clinic. Full payment, all expenses, if I'd sign a statement about what we found under the ice."

Noor turned to look at him. "What did you do?"

"Burned it in the stove." He smiled, and for a moment he looked very young. "It felt good. I understand now why you did what you did. Not because it was noble. Because some things, once you've seen what they're *for*, you can't let them exist. Even if they'd buy you back your whole life."

Noor said nothing. The river roared below. Somewhere up-valley, a shepherd's flute was playing, thin and wavering, a sound that had been played in these valleys for a thousand years and would be played for a thousand more, because no one had ever gotten around to putting it in a vault and pricing it.

* * *

They took the Karabulak herd up on Thursday, and the pass held, and the cheese was excellent, and the rifle did not work.

That was spring. By high summer, Noor had crossed the range eleven times—herders, a pregnant woman bound for the clinic on the far side, two boys running from a debt in the lowlands whom she ferried across for nothing but the promise that they'd send word when they were safe. The blacklist meant she could not guide for money. It did not mean she could not guide. The distinction had taken her the whole winter to understand, and once she understood it, the weight she had carried for years—the disgrace, the erased name, the expedition she may or may not have destroyed—settled into her like a stone settling into a riverbed. Still there. But the water ran over it now.

She thought about Dario more in those months than she had in years. Not with the old rage—Voss had stolen that from her too, the clean anger of believing her own guilt, and what remained was stranger and sadder. Dario's maps had been the key to everything. Voss had engineered a disaster to take them, spent her reputation as the cover charge, and sent a survey team to die confirming what the maps promised. And in the end the maps, the station, the data—all of it had come down to forty minutes of burning paper and a man's voice cracking through a radio as the storm closed over the pass. Ilya's voice. She did not let herself think of Ilya often. When she did, she thought of him the way she thought of the mountain: something enormous that had stood between her and the killing cold for exactly as long as it could, and no longer.

She kept his carabiner on her pack strap. It was dented. She had never once used it.

* * *

In the autumn of that year, a stranger came up the valley trail.

She saw him from the ridge at midday—a single figure on the switchbacks below, moving well, too well, with that economy of motion that meant either a guide or a soldier. Her hand went to the flare gun on her hip before her mind caught up. The valley had been quiet. The factions that had hunted the station had torn themselves apart over a prize that no longer existed; the last of Voss's rivals had gone quiet by midsummer, their scouts recalled, their money spent. But quiet was not peace, and she had learned the difference the hard way.

The figure reached the pasture by late afternoon. It was a woman, fifty or so, weathered, with a surveyor's theodolite slung across her back in a canvas case that had been mended more than once. She walked into the Karabulak camp, drank the tea that was offered her, and asked—Noor heard it from thirty meters away, the wind carrying it up the slope—whether anyone knew a guide named Noor Zhou.

The shepherds, to a man, said they did not.

But the old man Karabulak glanced up at the ridge. Just once. Just briefly.

Noor could have gone. She had a fast route off the back of the mountain, a route no surveyor would find, and by nightfall she could have been two valleys away. She stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at her jacket and weighed it, the way she weighed every crossing now—snow load, cornice risk, the chance of the bridge holding.

Then she thought of Sefa's burned letter. Of the flute playing up-valley. Of forty minutes of burning paper and the clean, empty sky above the pass.

She walked down.

The woman looked up as Noor entered the firelight, and her eyes went wide with recognition—Noor's face was still on the blacklists, after all, still printed in the licensing offices, still worth money to the right buyer. The woman's hand moved toward her coat, then stopped.

"I'm not here for the bounty," she said. "My name is Aral. I was on the survey team. The one that went into the Karakol." She set down her tea. Her hands were not quite steady. "I wasn't with them when they found the station. I'd turned back at the col—altitude sickness, they said, though I think now it was sense. I'm the only one who came home." She looked into the fire. "For a year I've been trying to understand what happened to them. The official report says crevasse fall. But I found a page of their log in a border archive last month. One page. It mentions a station. It mentions a vault. And it mentions that the records were—" she looked up, and her eyes were sharp now, a surveyor's eyes, measuring, "—*destroyed at the pass by person or persons unknown*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, the first snow of the season was already falling on the high passes, silent and total.

"I spent eight months looking for whoever burned it," Aral said quietly. "I had a whole speech prepared. About what they threw away. About what it was worth." She reached into her coat, and Noor did not move, and the woman drew out not a weapon but a folded, grease-stained page—the log page, the archive page, the last written trace of the men and women who had died in the ice. "And then I spent the winter down here instead. In the valleys. Watching what they'd be worth *to*." She shook her head slowly. "I burned the speech."

She held out the page. "I came to tell you they didn't die for nothing. And to ask you—" her voice caught, steadied, "—to ask the person who burned it to teach me the routes. Not for money. For the same reason, I think, that the records don't exist anymore."

Noor looked at the page a long time. The names of the dead were written there in a junior surveyor's careful hand, the last record of them anywhere on earth, and the mountain wind moved through the firelight, and the snow came down on the high passes, and the valleys below slept unclaimed, unmapped, unpriced, free.

She took the page. She folded it into her chest pocket, beside the dead man's hand-drawn map, where it belonged.

"First lesson's at dawn," Noor said. "And the mountain doesn't care what you burned or why. Remember that, and you might live long enough to learn the rest."
