## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The handcar had a name once, stamped into the iron of its frame in letters gone soft with rust: *Vulcan Iron Works, 1911*. Wendell Underhill had decided, somewhere in the third year of his exile, that this made them kin—two old machines kept running past the date anyone had planned for them, both of them useful only so long as nobody important remembered they existed.

He pumped the handles in a rhythm he could have kept in his sleep, because often enough he did. Down, down, lift, glide. The abandoned spur ran along the shoulder of a valley that had been logged out and given up on forty years ago, and the rails beneath him were weedy and singing, that thin high note steel makes when it is still true despite everything. He listened for it the way other men listened for birdsong. A rail that sang would hold his weight. A rail that had gone silent was rotting from the ties up, and it would take him down with it.

*Never stop longer than two nights anywhere.* He had written the rule on the inside cover of his map book in pencil, early, back when the rule was still a discipline rather than a religion. Three years later the pencil had been traced over so many times it looked like ink, and the rule had grown children: never sleep two nights in the same bedroll spot; never buy from the same shop twice; never let a face learn yours well enough to be described to a stranger. Never, above all, become a story people told. *There's an engineer living up the old spur line,* someone might say, and then someone else would say it to someone who got paid to listen, and then gray trucks would come up a valley at dawn, and that would be the end of Wendell Underhill's long, careful disappearance.

So he moved. He moved the way water moves, finding the low places, the forgotten ones, the routes that had been struck from the timetables and therefore from the minds of every man whose business was minding other men.

The valley opened ahead of him into late-afternoon light, gold lying flat across the flood meadows, and for half a mile the pumping of the car and the song of the rails and the smell of river mud almost amounted to peace. Almost. Peace was a country he had a visa for but no right of residence. It lasted until the bend below the old trestle, where the line dropped toward the river crossing—and there, small and wrong as a splinter under skin, stood the checkpoint.

He knew it was new before he knew anything else about it. New things in dead country announce themselves the way a lie announces itself: too clean, too deliberate. Two vehicles parked nose-out on the gravel bar beside the crossing. A canvas awning. Four men, maybe five, one of them standing with the particular stillness of a man paid to watch rather than work. And strung between two creosoted posts that had not been there last season—a barrier arm, striped red and white, lowered across the rail bed itself.

Wendell stopped pumping and let the car coast, his mind already moving faster than his body ever did.

A checkpoint on an abandoned spur. Not a police post—police posted themselves where people were, and there were no people here, not since the mill closed and took the town's reason for existing with it. This was private. The trucks were gray, unmarked, the kind of gray that costs extra because it is chosen. Men in gray trucks had been a rumor in Kessel Junction three weeks ago, when he'd passed through buying lamp oil, a rumor passed to him by the stationmaster with a look over his spectacles that said *I am telling you this because I like you, which is foolish of me, so go.*

*They're asking after an engineer,* the old man had said. *One who knows the closed lines.*

Wendell had smiled his forgettable smile, paid for his oil, and been on the morning freight's blind side before sunrise. That was the arrangement he had with the world: it could keep its memory if he kept his distance.

But now the world had come to him. Someone had built a fence across a line that appeared on no current map—which meant someone else had a map, or worse, had read the same absences in the official charts that Wendell had learned to read. Closed lines left ghosts. A bridge maintained past its service life. A culvert cleared when nothing upstream needed clearing. A spur kept on the tax rolls though no train had run it in a decade. He had spent three years reading those ghosts like scripture, and apparently he was not the only communicant.

The car drifted to a stop in the weeds, and Wendell did what he always did first, before fear, before planning: he listened. Wind in the alders. Water over the weir downstream. Somewhere far off, a dog—the floating villages maybe, or a farm that survived on the far bank. No engine idling beyond the bend. No radio crackle. Five men at most, and men, whatever their trucks cost, still slept and ate and grew bored.

He got off the car and walked it backward off the main line, into the alder scrub, laying branches over the bright parts of the frame. Then he climbed the embankment on his belly, the way he'd learned to move in another life, when he wore a surveyor's vest and a company badge and the only thing he'd ever hidden was how tired he was.

From the crest he studied the crossing through a gap in the brush. The barrier arm. The awning. A folding table with papers weighted under stones—papers meant to be seen, which was itself information. One man in a gray shirt walked the near abutment of the trestle and shone a flashlight into the trusses even though the light was failing slow and golden and useless for inspection. They weren't guarding the crossing. They were guarding the *idea* of the crossing. Somebody wanted any traveler on this line to believe the way forward was shut.

Which raised the question Wendell sat with, chin on his forearms, while the light went amber and then copper: shut against whom? A checkpoint costs money. Money follows interest. Interest, in his experience, followed land—and there was nothing along this whole drowned valley worth a week's wages except the land itself, and the water rights over it, and the old rail corridors that defined both.

He had surveyed corridors like these once, in the years before. He had drawn the lines that men in offices later closed, and he had never asked hard enough why they closed them, because asking questions was not what disgraced engineers with severance checks were encouraged to do. He had taken the check. He had taken the silence. And when the debts came due anyway—debts he never understood the arithmetic of, debts that arrived like weather—he had run, and discovered that the only thing he owned outright was his knowledge of every track the province had tried to forget.

*You are safe only because you never stay.* He said it to himself sometimes like a prayer, though prayers imply someone listening, and Wendell had stopped believing in listeners around the time the courthouse gavel fell.

Below him, the man in the gray shirt finished his pantomime inspection and called something to the table. Laughter, thin across the water. They'd be there till dark at least; checkpoints were for daylight men.

Wendell slid back down the embankment and uncovered the handcar, and here was the trouble, laid out plain as a junction diagram: the spur ahead crossed the river at the trestle, ran two miles along the far bank, and ended at a junction with the old north-south trunk—the one artery he needed to stay ahead of the season and the debt collectors both. Every alternative added days. Days meant towns. Towns meant faces learning his.

Unless.

He opened the map book. It lived in a oilcloth sleeve inside his coat, and it was the closest thing he had to a Bible: a surveyor's base map of the whole watershed, forty years out of date, annotated in three colors of his own pencil. Green for lines confirmed passable. Red for lines gone. Black for lines unverified—the ghosts, the maybes, the places where the paper said rail and the ground might say otherwise.

His thumb found the black notation just south of the trestle. An unmapped branch, penciled onto the original survey by some long-dead draftsman with a shaky hand: a logging spur, maybe, or a maintenance siding, running off the main line a quarter mile before the crossing and climbing into the hills toward nothing in particular. It appeared nowhere else. Not on the provincial charts, not on the timber company's old holdings maps he'd memorized in a better life. Just this one ghost stroke, this one faint confession that the world had once contained more than it admitted.

*Branch line, condition unknown. Grade appears steep. Do not rely.*

He had ridden past that notation a dozen times in three years and always chosen the known evil of the trestle route. Known evil was a currency he understood. But known evil now had five men and a striped barrier sitting on it, and unknown evil was at least honest about being unknown.

Wendell looked at the sky, doing the arithmetic of light. Two hours, maybe less, before the valley went blue and then black. Riding an unmapped branch in the dark was a good way to die stupidly—through a burned tie, into a washed-out cut, over a bridge that had been waiting thirty years for exactly such a fool. Stopping was against the rule. The rule had kept him alive. The rule was the only structure left in a life he had otherwise dismantled bolt by bolt.

But rules, he reminded himself, were like rails. True today, rotten tomorrow. You tested them or they killed you.

He pumped the handcar back up to the main line and rolled south, quiet, wheels singing low, until the ghost stroke's starting point should be—there. A seam in the weeds where ballast of a different age began, and a rusted switch stand leaning like a drunk against a fencepost. The points were seized with corrosion. Of course they were. Everything worth having was seized with corrosion; that was practically the theme of his life.

He set the brake, took the bar off the car, and went to work with oil can and wrench and the patience of a man who had spent twenty years coaxing machinery that everyone else had written off. Rust flaked. The throw bar groaned, resisted, gave with a shriek that scattered birds out of the alders—a sound like a scream in the empty valley, and he froze, counting seconds, listening for engines. Nothing. Only the river and the evening settling in.

The points threw. He rocked the stand to seat them, walked the first fifty yards of the branch on foot with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, testing ties with his heel the way a doctor tests pulses. Half of them were sound. That was better odds than the branch had any right to offer. Some hand had maintained this line once—not recently, but thoroughly, decades ago, and dry hillside air was kinder to ties than riverbottom damp.

Back at the car, he paused with his hands on the handles and allowed himself ten seconds of the exhaustion he usually kept locked in a box under the competence. His shoulders ached in the old injury's damp-weather dialect. He was thirty-eight years old and he had not slept a full night in the same place since the spring the banks took his license, and tonight he was going to ride a line that existed on no map, into hills that held nothing anyone wanted, because a striped barrier had frightened him off the road he knew. This was what safety looked like for Wendell Underhill. It looked like running. It had always looked like running. He had simply gotten very good at dressing it up as craft.

Then the box closed again, and the engineer came back, and the engineer pumped.

The branch climbed away from the river in long lazy curves, grade steeper than the ghost stroke had promised but manageable, the handcar's chain taking the strain with a complaint he catalogued automatically—link four, worn, replace before winter, if winters still applied to him. Alders gave way to pine. The rails sang true for a mile, then went quiet over a stretch where he dismounted twice to probe, found soft ties, and poled the car across like a ferryman over thin ice. Once the line crossed a ravine on a wooden trestle so old the timber had silvered, and he walked it first, alone in the blue dusk with his lantern and his heartbeat, and it held, and he permitted himself one breath of laughter at the absurdity of it all—a fugitive trusting his life to a bridge no ledger remembered building.

Full dark caught him at the top of the grade, where the branch leveled along a ridge and the trees opened enough to show stars. Below and behind, tiny with distance, the checkpoint's lantern glowed at the crossing like a bad thought he'd successfully outrun. Ahead, the line ran on into a darkness that the map book rendered as blank paper.

And it was there, coasting easy along the ridge with the cold air washing his face, that Wendell saw the light.

Not a star. Not the moon, which wouldn't rise for hours. A steady point of yellow, low in the hills ahead, positioned precisely where the ghost stroke ended on his map—at the terminus, the place where the branch should have died in a log landing or a weed-choked bumper. A light meant habitation. Habitation meant people. People meant risk, exposure, the whole architecture of his rule.

But it also meant, his mind supplied with unwelcome helpfulness, that somebody had built something at the end of a line that officially did not exist. Somebody who perhaps also preferred to live where the maps went blank.

The handcar rolled on, downhill now, gathering speed he didn't quite trust, and Wendell Underhill crouched over the handles with the wind in his teeth and told himself he would pass wide in the dark, unseen, unheard, the way he passed everything. He told himself the rule would hold.

The light ahead did not flicker. It did not move. It simply waited, patient as a lit window in a world that had forgotten this valley, and some treacherous part of him—the part that remembered what windows meant, before—began, against all sense and all policy, to wonder who was home.

* * *

He brought the car to a stop a half mile short, in a cutting deep enough to hide in, and ate cold rice and smoked fish from his pack while he reasoned with himself like a lawyer arguing both sides of a losing case.

Option one: bypass on foot, circle wide through the pines, rejoin the branch beyond whatever waited at its end. Cost: a night of walking broken ground in the dark, arriving exhausted wherever he arrived, with no water resupply and the monsoon season pressing at the edges of the calendar like a creditor. Option two: approach openly, knock, present himself as a wanderer—which he was, and which no one on an unmapped line in stolen country would have any standing to question. Cost: everything. Faces. Voices. A story told about him, existing now outside his own keeping.

Option three, whispered by the tired part of him: turn around, ride back down the ridge, take the trestle in the dark past a sleeping checkpoint, and pretend the light had been a campfire, a reflection, a trick of want.

He hated that option most, because it was the one the rule demanded, and because he noticed—with the clarity that comes at the end of a long day, when competence clocks out and leaves the man alone with himself—that he wasn't afraid of the light. He was afraid of how much he wanted it to be a door.

Three years of doors closing will do that to a man. Make him mistake any crack of yellow for welcome.

Wendell packed his trash, wiped the cutting clean of his presence with a pine bough, and stood a long moment in the dark with his hand resting on the handcar's cold rail, feeling the day's decision balance on a knife edge finer than any switch point he'd ever thrown.

Then, from the direction of the light, faint but unmistakable across the still night air, came a sound that settled the matter the way a gavel settles a courtroom: the rhythmic, metallic, unmistakable clank of a pump—someone working a handcar of their own, somewhere down the line, coming toward him through the dark.

Wendell went very still. There was no version of the rule that covered this. In three years of empty rails, he had met no one. The closed lines were supposed to be *his*—his country, his church, his hiding place. And now, out of the blank space at the edge of his own map, someone was coming.

He doused his lantern, pulled the handcar into the deepest shadow of the cutting, and waited, heart hammering the old familiar rhythm: *down, down, lift, glide.*
