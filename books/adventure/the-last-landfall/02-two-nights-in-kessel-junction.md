## 2. Two Nights in Kessel Junction

The handcar rattled into Kessel Junction an hour past dawn, and Wendell Underhill knew before he saw the town that it was dying. He could hear it in the rails.

A living line sang under a rolling wheel—a long, even note that ran ahead of you like a promise. A dead line spoke differently: a dull clatter at every joint, rust flaking off inside the fishplates, ties gone soft as bread. The spur he'd ridden down from the ridge had been dead for six years by his count, and it had talked to him all night, complaining in the dark like an old man alone in a house too big for him.

He coasted the last quarter mile with the lever down and let momentum carry him onto the stub of track beside the old freight platform, where he braked against a rotted bumper post and stood still for a moment, listening to the town wake up—or fail to.

Kessel Junction had been built to be a crossroads. Two lines had met here once, the north-south freight and the eastbound passenger loop, and the town had grown up around their meeting like a campfire crowd. Now one line was torn up for scrap as far as the river bridge, and the other ran twice a month if the schedule held, which lately it didn't. The water tower still stood over the yard, but its legs were streaked brown with rust weep and its tank had a patch on it that some optimist had hammered on years ago. The roundhouse roof had fallen in at one corner, so the building looked like a man sitting with his head tipped, listening to something far away.

Wendell swung the handcar off the rails and carried it—two hundred pounds of iron and stubbornness—into the shadow of the platform's loading dock, then threw a tarp of woven plastic over it. Rule two, after never stopping longer than two nights: never let your transportation be seen until you know who's looking.

* * *

The general store had been a rail depot office once; you could still see where the ticket window had been bricked over. Inside, a woman named Odile Prater sold him coffee that had been ground sometime last year and canned peaches and a coil of baling wire, and she watched him count out coins with the flat patience of someone who had learned not to ask questions of men who arrived on foot along dead tracks.

"You'll be wanting work," she said, when he'd finished paying and had almost nothing left.

"I didn't say so."

"You didn't have to." She nodded toward the window, toward the yard. "Half this town would work if there was any. Other half is too proud or too gone. You've got hands like somebody who fixes things."

Wendell looked at his hands as if they belonged to a stranger. They were scarred across the knuckles and black in the creases, and they were trembling slightly—not from fear but from three days of short rations. He put them in his pockets.

"What kind of work?"

"Whatever you can do." She ticked items off on her fingers. "The church roof leaks. Mrs. Halloran's pump is seized. The schoolhouse stove smokes. And the stationmaster—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, "—the stationmaster needs what everybody needs, which is company and somebody to listen."

"Stationmaster," Wendell said. "There's no trains."

"There's him." She said it without mockery. "Dell Harrow. He kept this junction running thirty-one years. When the company pulled out, they gave him a paper and a handshake and left him the keys. He still opens the depot every morning at seven. Sweeps the platform. Winds the clock. Some mornings he flags down the freight when it comes through, just so somebody stops." She shrugged. "It keeps him alive, I think. So don't laugh at it."

"I wasn't going to laugh at it," Wendell said quietly. He understood better than she knew. A man needed a ritual or he came apart. His own rituals were rules, numbered and memorized, and they were the only structure left standing in the ruin of his life.

"I'll fix the pump first," he said. "Pump and stove today. Roof tomorrow if the weather holds. For supplies and a place to sleep out of the rain."

"The church has a room behind the vestry," Odile said. "Nobody will bother you there. Nobody bothers anybody here anymore. That's the trouble with us."

* * *

Mrs. Halloran's pump took him forty minutes. It was a shallow-well pitcher pump, cast iron older than he was, and the leather cup in the cylinder had dried to a wafer. He cut a new cup from a piece of harness leather Odile found in the back of the store, soaked it in warm water, and dressed it with tallow from a candle stub. While the leather swelled he cleaned the check valve and re-seated the foot valve with a gasket made from an old rubber boot sole. When he worked the handle, the pump drew water on the fourth stroke, cold and iron-tasting and clean.

Mrs. Halloran stood in her doorway watching him the whole time, a small woman shaped like a question mark, and when he finished she pressed a jar of pickled eggs into his hands and said, "You're the first young man who's fixed anything in this town since the mill closed," and Wendell, who was forty-two and felt ninety, thanked her and did not correct her.

The schoolhouse stove was a longer job—the firebox grate had warped and the flue damper had frozen open, which was why it smoked. He straightened the grate bar over a charcoal fire in the yard, freed the damper with penetrating oil and patience, and re-cemented the flue collar with a mix of wood ash and clay. By mid-afternoon the stove drew properly, and the teacher, a tired young man named Farrow, shook his hand and asked him three times if there was anything else he could do, anything at all, because good hands were so rare now, so rare.

Word traveled fast in a dying town. By four o'clock there were three more jobs waiting outside the store: a wagon axle, a window sash, a radio that mostly worked. Wendell turned them down. Not because he didn't want them—he wanted nothing more than to spend a week being useful, being *needed*, being a man with a name people called across a street—but because rule one existed for a reason, and the reason had a face he saw sometimes in dreams: a hearing room, a lawyer's voice like oiled rope, a gavel coming down on eleven years of his life.

Two nights. Never more. Stopping was how they found you.

* * *

He went to see the stationmaster near evening, partly out of courtesy and partly because Dell Harrow would know more about the state of the lines within fifty miles than any map printed in the last five years—and Wendell's stolen map was eight years old and getting less true every season.

The depot smelled of floor wax and coal oil. The clock over the dispatch desk was wound and running, its second hand ticking with the confidence of a thing that believed in schedules. Dell Harrow sat behind the desk in a uniform shirt whose brass buttons had been polished so many times the faces of the eagles had worn smooth. He was seventy if he was a day, straight-backed, white-mustached, with forearms like dock rope.

"Underhill," Wendell said. "Odile sent me. Said you might know about track conditions north of the river."

Harrow looked at him for a long moment, the way old railroaders looked at men—took in the boots, the hands, the way he stood with his weight balanced like someone always ready to move.

"You've ridden the dead spurs," Harrow said. "I can tell. You walk like the ground owes you money."

"I walk like a man who doesn't want to be followed."

"Mm." Harrow rose and went to the great board on the wall behind him—the old train sheet board, glass-fronted, where the day's movements had once been chalked in a fine railroad hand. The glass was dusty now, but beneath it the chalk grid remained, ghost-faint, the last day's traffic preserved like a fossil: *No. 4 East, delayed 20 min, water stop.* Harrow touched the glass lightly, the way you'd touch a photograph of the dead.

"North of the river," he said, turning back to business. "The Kessel branch is passable to mile twelve, then a washout at Miller's Cut, twenty feet of roadbed gone. After that it runs to Corben's Landing, but the trestle at Corben's is condemned—I condemned it myself, last inspection before they shut me out. Don't trust it under a handcar. The river line is your better road, but—" he paused, and his mustache moved, "—you'll want to see the riverbank first. Come back tomorrow morning. I'll walk you down."

"Why not tonight?"

"Because tonight," Harrow said, "you're going to eat a hot meal at my table and sleep eight hours, and tomorrow you'll look at that riverbank with rested eyes. A tired man sees what he expects. I need you to see what's there."

Wendell should have refused. Every instinct honed over three years told him to refuse—an offer of hospitality was a hook, kindness was bait, and the men looking for him, whoever they were, would use exactly this. But he was so tired. Three years tired. And there was something in the old man's face—not pity, which he could have resisted, but recognition, which he couldn't.

"All right," he heard himself say. "One meal."

"One meal," Harrow agreed gravely, "and eight hours, and breakfast, and then we'll discuss whether that constitutes one night or two, because I keep count of everything in this depot, Mr. Underhill, including nights."

* * *

They ate stew at a table set for one person and used by two, and Harrow talked about the junction the way widowers talk about wives. Which siding had held the cattle cars in '09. How the flood of '14 had come within a hand's width of the platform edge. The engineer, name of Coleridge, who used to whistle three notes through the cut so his wife would have supper hot. Wendell listened and answered when required and understood, with a clarity that hurt, that he was looking at his own future in a mirror: a man keeping faith with a system that had abandoned him, winding a clock nobody read.

Afterward Harrow brought out a bottle of plum brandy, homemade, fierce, and poured two fingers each, and said, without preamble:

"Now. The gray trucks."

Wendell's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Three weeks ago," Harrow said, "maybe four. Two trucks, gray, no markings, government-plain the way private men dress government when they want to look official without being it. Men in canvas jackets. Polite. Bought fuel at Odile's, bought nothing else, asked questions instead. Asking about survey crews upriver. Asking about strangers." He looked at Wendell steadily. "Asking about an engineer. Their words were: *the engineer who knows the closed lines.*"

The brandy sat untouched in Wendell's hand. Outside, wind moved through the yard, and somewhere a loose sheet of tin flapped on the roundhouse roof, counting seconds.

"What did you tell them?"

"That I hadn't seen an engineer in six years," Harrow said. "Which was true, until tonight." He raised a hand before Wendell could speak. "Sit. I'm not selling you. I'm telling you because you'd have found out anyway, tomorrow, on the riverbank, and I'd rather you heard it from a man who polishes his own buttons. Those trucks came back twice. The second time they had a man with them in a suit too nice for this county, and he offered Odile money for a list of everyone who'd passed through. She took the money and gave him a list of names of people dead ten years. Good woman." A dry smile. "But they'll come back again, and next time they may not buy. And whatever you're running from, son, it has a budget."

Wendell set the glass down. His hands were steady now, which was worse than trembling. Steady meant the fear had gone deep enough to stop being a feeling and become a fact.

"I surveyed those closed lines," he said, and heard his own voice as if from a distance. "Eight years ago. Before—" He stopped. The whole story was a locked room and he had spent three years keeping the key in his fist, and here was this old man offering him a chair and a light, and the lock trembled in its frame.

"Before whatever happened happened," Harrow supplied gently. "You don't owe me the story. I've lived long enough to know everybody's carrying one. But I'll tell you what I know about gray trucks, and I've been at this junction through three administrations and two wars: men in unmarked vehicles asking careful questions are never working for the government. Government men carry paperwork and love showing it. These ones had none. Odile asked. They smiled."

* * *

He slept in the room behind the vestry after all—Harrow had walked him there personally, through streets empty except for dogs, and had shaken his hand at the door like a man seeing off a passenger on the last train. Wendell lay on a cot that smelled of cedar and disuse and slept harder than he had in a year, dreamlessly, and woke at dawn feeling like a stranger in his own body, a body unaccustomed to rest.

Rule one said: leave. Pack the handcar, take the river line north before the town woke, be miles away by noon, be nobody again.

Instead he walked down to the riverbank with Dell Harrow, because the old man had asked him to look at something, and because some part of Wendell—some part he distrusted profoundly, some part that had been starving alongside the rest of him—wanted to be the kind of man who did what he said he'd do.

The Kessel River ran high and brown with early monsoon melt, sliding past the town with a sound like continuous conversation. Along the bank, where the flood grass grew thick, Wendell saw it within a minute of looking.

Survey markers. White-painted posts, waist-high, driven into the earth at intervals along the waterline, each banded with fresh paint—red above white, the standard marking for a hydrographic datum—and the paint was *new*. Not weathered, not faded, not the work of seasons past. The brush strokes were crisp. Sawdust still clung to the base of the nearest post, pale and dry despite the humidity, which meant the post had been set within days.

He knelt. The soil around the footing was compacted by boot heels, several sets, recent. He touched the paint with one finger and it dragged tacky under his skin.

"Somebody's surveying the river," Harrow said behind him. "Government crew, by the markers. Came through on a boat about a week back. Set these from the waterline up to the old quarry bend, maybe four miles. Camped somewhere up there—we saw their tarps from the bluff one evening, orange and blue. Then two mornings ago the boats were gone and the camp was quiet, and nobody's come back down the river since." He folded his arms. "Now, a survey crew that finishes packs up loud and files papers. This one just... stopped. Like a clock with the battery out."

Wendell stood slowly. Fresh markers meant active work. Active work meant a crew. A vanished crew meant either they'd moved on—which crews did, constantly, invisibly, that was the nature of the work—or something had happened to them. And the gray trucks had come through asking questions right around the time the crew went silent.

Coincidence was a word he'd stopped believing in eight years ago, in a hearing room, when a lawyer had explained to him that coincidences were simply patterns other people hadn't bothered to trace.

"Harrow," he said. "Those gray trucks. Did they say which way they went when they left?"

"Upriver road," Harrow said. "Toward the quarry bend."

The wind came off the water and flattened the flood grass in long silver waves, and Wendell looked up the river to where it bent out of sight below the bluffs, and felt the particular cold that had nothing to do with temperature—the cold of a pattern assembling itself, of doors closing in rooms he hadn't entered yet.

His rule said two nights. Last night had been the first. He had until tomorrow's dusk, and then the dead rails again, and anonymity, and safety, and the slow erosion of being no one at all.

He looked at the wet red band on the marker post, and at the brown river hurrying north toward whatever waited at the bend, and understood—with the weary certainty of a man reading his own timetable—that he was going to miss his train.

That afternoon, while he sharpened a borrowed machete against a whetstone taken from Harrow's tool drawer, a boy came running down the depot steps, breathless, ten years old, sent by Odile.

"Mister," the boy panted. "Mister, there's trucks on the north road. Gray ones. And a man got out and started walking toward the church."
