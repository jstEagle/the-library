## 4. The Woman in the Reeds

The water was the only place Mara Voss had ever felt safe, and now it had betrayed her too.

She lay among the reeds with her chin barely above the surface, her body angled into the current so the river seemed to pass through her rather than around her. The reeds were thick here—a drowned stand of them at the bend below the survey camp, growing out of silt that had accumulated where the old channel shifted decades ago. She knew this stretch of water better than anyone on the team. Better than Okafor with his depth soundings, better than Pell with his flow gauges. She had waded every inch of it with her plane table and her sounding pole, drawing the river's bones onto paper while the others laughed about how she liked the mud more than people.

They weren't laughing now. They weren't anything now.

She had been underwater when they came. That was the whole of it—that absurd, accidental fact. Pell had dared her to retrieve a dropped theodolite leg from the channel bottom, and she'd gone down without a breath of complaint because the water was cool and the light came down in green columns and for four minutes nobody asked her anything. When she surfaced, breathing hard behind the reed screen, the gray trucks were already pulling into the clearing above the camp, and Dr. Amara was saying something pleasant and professional to men who did not answer pleasantly or professionally.

Mara had gone back under. She told herself later it was instinct, but that was a lie she permitted herself the way she permitted herself small comforts: rationed, and never quite believed. It wasn't instinct. It was calculation, cold and immediate—the same part of her mind that read contour lines saw six men with rifles and one cartographer with no weapon but a river, and chose the river.

She stayed under until her vision sparked. She surfaced in the reeds, filled her lungs, went down again. Three times. Four. Between dives she heard things she would spend the rest of her life unable to unhear. Not screaming—there was almost no screaming, which was worse. Voices raised briefly, then lowered. The flat sounds of work being done efficiently. A truck bed dropping under weight. And then, near the end, a voice—calm, educated, bored—the voice of a man giving instructions to other men, and one sentence carried across the water with terrible clarity:

*Burn nothing yet. We want it to look like they moved on.*

*Look like they moved on.* As if six people simply packed their instruments, boarded their boats, and paddled away from a half-eaten lunch. As if Dr. Amara would leave her field boots. As if young Ilya would abandon his mother's letter, the one he kept folded in his breast pocket and read every night by lamplight.

Mara had surfaced after they left and stood waist-deep in the shallows for a long time, letting the current push against her like a hand trying to turn her around. The camp sat above her on the bank, intact and wrong. The kettle still hung over the fire ring. Okafor's instruments lay wrapped in oilcloth exactly as he'd left them, because Okafor was fastidious even at breakfast. Everything was arranged. Someone had staged the absence of her friends the way she herself might stage a map—carefully, deliberately, with an eye toward what a viewer should believe.

She understood then what she was looking at. Not a disappearance. An erasure. And erasures, in her experience, always served someone.

She had not gone to the camp. Not that first day. She'd taken stock instead, floating in the flooded timber downstream with only her head above water, doing the arithmetic of survival. One knife. One waterproof pouch with her personal logbook sealed inside. The clothes she wore. Her knowledge of the river system, which was encyclopedic and which she now understood was worth money to somebody—worth enough that men had killed for whatever her team had recorded.

And one more asset, though she hadn't known its value yet: the fact that Corvane's men believed everyone at the camp was dead or gone. Nobody was looking for a survivor. A survivor was a loose thread, and loose threads got pulled, but only if someone noticed them. Mara intended never to be noticed again.

That was the plan. Stay in the water. Move at dusk. Eat fish and cattail roots. Work her way downriver toward Harrow Bend, toward the provincial archive, toward the one place her maps could be filed where they became something Corvane couldn't burn. She'd made it two days before hunger drove her back upriver to the camp—not for sentiment, she told herself, but because the camp had food stores, and a starving woman draws attention, and attention was death.

Which was why she was lying in the reeds at midmorning three days after the trucks, watching a stranger walk into the graveyard of her old life.

* * *

He came along the bank from downstream, which interested her immediately. Everyone else came from upstream—from the rail line, from Kessel Junction, from the world of roads and checkpoints. This man came from the water side, moving with the low-shouldered economy of someone who had learned to read ground the way she read water. He stopped at the edge of the clearing and stood there a long moment, utterly still, and Mara felt a flicker of reluctant respect. Most people walked into places. This man let places announce themselves first.

He was lean in the way of someone who missed meals on purpose rather than by accident, with gray coming into his dark hair and hands that looked engineered rather than grown—big-knuckled, precise, scarred across the backs. He wore a canvas coat gone soft with age and a pack that rode high and tight on his shoulders. His eyes moved across the camp in a pattern she recognized: fire ring first, then boatslip, then instrument table, then the tents. Priority of evidence. He was reading the scene.

*Surveyor,* she thought. *Or something adjacent.*

Then he crossed the clearing and began going through their belongings, and the respect curdled.

He was not careless about it. That was the strange thing. He didn't ransack; he lifted. He opened Okafor's instrument case, ran his thumb along the transit's brass fittings, and closed it again without taking anything—and Mara watched his face as he did it, watched something move through it that was not greed. He crouched at the fire ring and touched the kettle's flank with two fingers, the way you'd check a bearing housing for heat, and his mouth tightened. He looked at the half-washed plates in the wash basin. He looked at Ilya's tent, and stood outside it longer than he'd stood anywhere, and did not go in.

*He knows,* Mara thought. *He knows what this is.*

A looter would have taken the transit. Brass and glass, worth months of wages to the right fence. A scavenger would have taken the oilcloth, the rope, the tinned food, the lamp oil—all of it, everything not nailed down, because that was what scavengers did; she'd seen it on every river she'd ever mapped, the way human need stripped a landscape bare. This man took a tin of tea, a coil of good rope, and a block of salt, and he stacked the rest back the way he'd found it, aligning the edges of things, and there was something almost apologetic in the tidiness.

Then he found the notebook, and everything changed.

It was Pell's field notebook—the spare, the one Pell kept in the mess tent because he was forever losing his primary and Dr. Amara teased him about it mercilessly. Waterlogged, swollen fat with river water, its cover warped. The stranger picked it up and turned it over, and Mara saw him understand what it was, and saw him weigh it in his hand the way a man weighs a decision.

He put it in his coat.

Rage went through her like a spear of cold water. *Mine.* Not the notebook itself—Pell's handwriting, Pell's depth figures, useless to her emotionally and priceless to her legally—but the principle of it. Everything in that camp was hers now. Hers by inheritance, hers by horror, hers because everyone who could claim it otherwise was dead. This stranger had no right to carry any piece of her dead forward into the world. He'd take it to a pawnshop. He'd sell it to the first gray truck he met, if he even understood what he was holding, and he clearly didn't, because if he understood he'd have run from it the way you run from a lit fuse.

She was halfway out of the reeds before she caught herself—hand on a reed stalk, water sheeting off her shoulders, close enough to see the stitching on his coat. She froze. He hadn't turned. But he'd stopped moving, and his head had come up slightly, the way an animal's does, and she sank back down into the murk with her heart slamming against her ribs.

*Stupid. Stupid.* Three days of discipline undone by a notebook. She breathed slow through her nose, water lapping at her jawline, and forced the engineer's part of her mind back online. Assess. He hadn't seen her—or he'd seen something and couldn't confirm it, which was different and more dangerous. He was packing up to leave. Downstream, by the look of the angle he took toward the bank. Downstream was her direction. Downstream was Harrow Bend.

She let him get fifty meters along the bank before she slipped from the reeds and followed, submerged to her chin, moving in the slack water near the cutbank where the current wouldn't carry her past him. She could keep pace easily; the river was her treadmill. The question was not whether she could follow him. The question was what she intended to do when following stopped being enough.

* * *

By late afternoon she had learned three things about him.

First, he was a railroad man. He'd cut inland at the collapsed trestle above Miller's Eddy and she'd watched him climb the rotted abutment without hesitation, testing each timber with a heel-strike before committing his weight, reading the structure's failures from thirty feet away. Only someone who had spent years on bridges and roadbeds moved like that. And when he reached the top he stood looking down the abandoned right-of-way with an expression she recognized from mirrors—the expression of a person checking whether the past had followed them.

Second, he was afraid. Not of her—he didn't know about her—but of something behind him. Twice on the portage around Miller's Eddy he stopped, all motion ceasing at once, and listened to a landscape that offered him nothing but birdsong, and twice he moved on faster than before. Men who were merely lost didn't listen like that. Men who were hunted did. She knew the difference because she'd been both.

Third—and this was the thing that decided her, though she didn't know it yet—he had stopped at the mouth of Coe Creek and built a small fire, and he'd laid Pell's notebook open on a flat stone beside it, fanning the pages in the sun and smoke to dry them.

Not selling it. Not burning it. *Drying it.*

She watched from the alder shade across the creek, close enough now to see the pages flutter, close enough to make out the ghost of Pell's blocky capitals bleeding through the swollen paper. The man turned each page with a care that bordered on reverence, separating sheets that had fused, laying them flat. He didn't read them—or he tried, and failed, because the ink had run and the figures meant nothing to a man who thought in gradients and load ratings. But he handled the notebook like something that mattered to someone else.

People who were running didn't do that. People who were hiding didn't do that. People did that when some part of them, buried deep under the fear and the flight, still believed that objects carried obligations.

Mara crouched in the alder shadow and did what she did best: she drew the situation. In her head, on the plane table of her mind, she plotted the variables. Variable one: Corvane's men would come back up this valley eventually—the camp was supposed to look abandoned, but abandoned camps attracted exactly the kind of wanderer now drying Pell's notebook over a fire, and Corvane's people were thorough. Variable two: she could not reach Harrow Bend alone. The lower river was watched—she'd seen the gray truck parked at the ferry landing two nights ago, its occupants cooking dinner with the unhurried ease of men on salary. She needed to move overland, through country she didn't know, and she needed a partner who could read rails and culverts and the hidden geometry of the land the way she read water. Variable three: this man was afraid of something, and afraid men could be managed, but they could also break, and a broken ally was worse than an enemy because enemies you could predict.

Variable four, which she wrote down last and underlined: he had put the notebook in his coat again when the pages dried, carefully, and looked at the fire for a long time afterward, and said something under his breath that she couldn't hear but whose shape she recognized. It was the shape of an apology. Or a promise.

*Useful,* she thought. *Or a threat. Possibly both. Possibly the same thing.*

She had trusted exactly one group of people in her adult life, and the river had given them back to her as a staged absence and a warm kettle. She was not going to trust this stranger. Trust was a currency she no longer carried. But alliance was different from trust—an alliance was just two sets of calculations pointing the same direction for a while, and she could live inside an alliance. She'd lived inside rivers.

The plan assembled itself with the clean inevitability of a well-drafted contour. She would follow him tonight. Tomorrow, when he reached the narrows below the old quarry—she knew he'd go that way; the rail spur on the far bank was passable and every other route funneled into watched ground—she would let him see her. On her terms. In daylight, at a distance, with the water at her back and the quarry cave at hers. She would show him the notebook's twin facts: that she was alive, and that whoever helped her would be helping bury Corvane Land Syndicate. If he was decent, the second fact would hold him. If he was desperate, the first would frighten him, and frightened men either fled or fought, and she'd know within a minute which kind she had.

And if he was the third kind—if he was the kind who sold people—then she'd learn that too, and the quarry cave had a second entrance underwater that no one had ever found except her.

Across the creek, the stranger banked his fire with dirt, shouldered his pack, and set off along the rail spur in the failing light, a dark figure walking the dead line south. Mara waited until the dusk swallowed him completely, then slid into the creek without a ripple and followed the cold pull of the current down toward the narrows, toward the quarry, toward the first conversation she'd have with another living soul since the river saved her life by making her watch it end.

Behind her, far upstream, faint on the evening wind, she heard it: engines. More than one. Moving along the valley road with the patient, thorough rhythm of men finishing a job.

They were coming back to check their work.
