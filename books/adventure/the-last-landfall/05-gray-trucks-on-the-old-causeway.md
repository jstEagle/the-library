## 5. Gray Trucks on the Old Causeway

Wendell smelled the smoke before he saw it.

He was two miles downstream of the survey camp when the wind shifted and carried it to him—green wood burning wet, pitch and canvas, the particular sour reek of a fire set by men who wanted it seen. He stopped on the riverbank with the waterlogged notebook inside his coat and understood, with the cold certainty of a man who has spent three years reading danger in small signs, that he had made a mistake.

He had taken the notebook. Someone had watched him take it.

Or worse: no one had needed to watch at all. The camp had been left as a test, or a trap, or simply unfinished business that Corvane's people had come back to finish. Either way, the smoke rising behind him meant the valley was no longer empty, and the notebook against his ribs had just become the most dangerous object in three counties.

He did what his body knew how to do, what three years of never stopping longer than two nights had burned into his reflexes. He moved.

The river bent east below him, and where it bent, the old causeway ran—a rail viaduct from the days when the Kessel line had carried ore instead of rust, built on stone piers across the floodplain and abandoned when the floods took out its middle third. Wendell had crossed its broken back twice before, years ago, walking the closed lines the way other men walked fences along their own property. He knew its gaps like a man knows the missing teeth in his own mouth. It was the only route out of the valley that didn't pass through open ground, and open ground, right now, meant death.

Behind him, faint but unmistakable, came the sound he'd been dreading since Kessel Junction: engines. Not one. Three, maybe four, low-geared and laboring, trucks working their way down the rough track along the river.

* * *

He reached the viaduct's southern abutment as the first gray truck cleared the bend behind him. He didn't look back—that was a rule too, looking back costs you half a second and half a second is a grave—but he heard it clearly enough: brakes, doors, voices with the flat authority of men who expected obedience. A dog barked once and was silenced.

The viaduct rose ahead of him, forty feet above the floodplain grass, its deck a ribbon of rotted ties and buckled rails running north into the haze. The middle third was gone entirely—three spans dropped into the marsh where the flood of '09 had chewed through the piers—but the fallen spans hadn't vanished. They lay where they'd fallen, tilted slabs of concrete and twisted rail forming a broken staircase down into the reeds and up the far side, crossable if you were careful, sober, and not being shot at.

Wendell climbed.

His hands found the old holds without consulting him—the chinked mortar, the iron rung some section crew had driven into the pier face fifty years ago for exactly this kind of maintenance access. His boots knew the deck's rhythm: step on the tie, not between; trust the rail, not the wood; keep your weight over the pier line where the structure still remembered how to hold. The wind came up the valley ahead of the rain, carrying the smell of the burning camp stronger now, and beneath it something else—gasoline, deliberately poured. They weren't just destroying the camp. They were erasing it, the way someone had erased the tracks leading out of it, the way someone had erased four surveyors so completely that even their kettle had been left warm and their boats gone and no sign anywhere of where they'd went.

Except one. One cartographer who had gone into the water and stayed there until the wolves passed.

He was halfway across the first intact span when the shout came.

"Hey! On the viaduct!"

Wendell didn't flinch, didn't turn, didn't break stride. Flinching tells a marksman where you'll be next. He lengthened his pace instead, keeping low, using the parapet's broken stumps for cover where they existed, which was rarely. The viaduct's railing had been salvaged for scrap decades ago; all that remained was knee-high stubs of iron every eight feet, and between them, nothing but air and the long drop to the floodplain.

A rifle cracked. The bullet went somewhere behind him with a sound like a whip dying.

He ran.

Not well—he was forty-six years old and three years of handcar travel and stolen meals had kept him lean but not fast—but with the economy of a man who had learned that survival is mostly geometry. Angles, cover, distance. The second span ended at the collapse, and he threw himself down onto the fallen slab without slowing, boots skidding on wet concrete, one hand catching a length of exposed rebar that tore his palm open to the bone-white fat beneath. He barely felt it. The slab tilted under him, groaned, settled. Below, the marsh water showed black between the reeds, deep enough to drown in, shallow enough to see a body in.

Another shot rang out, and this one hit close—spanged off the rail beside his head with a bright vicious note, throwing a splinter of steel that stung his cheek. They weren't warning shots anymore.

"Leave him!" a voice bellowed from below, carrying flat across the water. "Get the notebook! He can't go anywhere!"

*They want the book.* The thought arrived cold and clear, cutting through the animal panic. *Not me. The book.* Which meant they believed he'd read it, or that it mattered, which meant Mara Voss—or whoever she was—had been right about what her team had found. Water rights. Land. Something worth killing four government surveyors over and chasing a vagrant across a flooded valley for.

Wendell slid down the second fallen span, dropped the last six feet into the reeds, and came up running through thigh-deep water toward the northern bank. The mud sucked at his boots. The rain arrived then, finally, the monsoon's first real squall coming down the valley in a gray wall, and for a moment he thought God might be on his side after all—visibility dropping to fifty yards, the shooters' sightlines dissolving—

Then he saw the truck.

It had come around the northern end of the causeway road, the gravel track that followed the river's far bank, and it sat angled across the path he needed, engine idling, wipers going. Two men stood beside it under the truck bed's canvas awning, and one of them was talking into a radio handset with the unhurried air of a man closing a gate rather than chasing a rabbit.

They'd known the viaduct was the only way across. Of course they had. They'd probably been sitting there since dawn.

Wendell stopped, chest heaving, water streaming off him, and did the arithmetic every fugitive eventually does. Behind him: armed men and a burning camp. Ahead: armed men and a closed road. To the left: the river, high and brown and full of debris, moving faster than any man could swim. To the right: the floodplain, two miles of open grass and barbed wire and nowhere to hide.

Three years of running, and it ended here, in the rain, over a stranger's notebook.

He put his hand inside his coat and touched the notebook's sodden cover, and something in him refused—not courage, exactly, more the stubbornness of a man who has lost everything else and will not lose the last thing he chose to carry. He turned to face the south bank, where the first pursuers were already picking their way down the fallen spans, rifles slung now that the radio had told them the trap was closed. One of them waved at him, almost friendly. Come on, then. Nothing personal.

That was when the tires exploded.

* * *

Later, Wendell would reconstruct it from fragments, because it happened too fast and too quietly to witness properly. But this much he saw: the truck's front tire simply died, hissing, sagging, the vehicle lurching on its axle—and then the rear tire went the same way, and both men beside the truck spun toward the reeds with their hands going to their holsters, and from somewhere in the tall grass came a sound like a branch snapping, twice, deliberate, and then silence except for the rain.

The men in the reeds behind him shouted. The men at the truck shouted back. Nobody shot at anything, because nobody could see anything, because whoever had cut those tires had chosen her moment the way a surgeon chooses an incision.

"Down here," said a voice at his elbow, low and furious, and Wendell nearly died of fright on the spot.

She had come out of the water itself—no splash, no warning, just Mara Voss materializing beside him in the reeds like something the river had decided to give back. Her hair was plastered to her skull, her clothes were black with mud, and her eyes had the flat green calm of water over a drop-off. In her right hand she held a folding knife, blade wet, and in her left, absurdly, a bicycle pump with a sharpened hose fitting jammed over the nozzle.

"Tires," she said, seeing him look. "Valve stems. You can reach them from underneath if you're patient." She jerked her chin toward the river. "Can you handle a boat?"

"What boat?"

"The one I've been keeping." She was already moving, crouched low through the reeds, and he had no choice but to follow, because staying meant the men with rifles, and following meant a woman who had survived the erasure of everyone she'd worked with by holding herself underwater among the reeds while they searched the camp, and either fact argued for moving.

The boat was a survey skiff, one of the ones that had supposedly vanished with the team—she'd scuttled it in a cutbank slough thirty hours ago and breathed on it again this morning, bailer in hand, waiting to see what the smoke brought. It sat low in the brown water, patched with tar and a strip of canvas, and it looked about as seaworthy as a boot. It looked beautiful.

"In," she said. "South bank first. We're going past them."

"Past them," Wendell repeated. "In a leaking boat. Past the men with guns."

"They're watching the road and the viaduct." She shoved the skiff off the mud and stepped in, taking the stern oars. "Nobody watches the water. That's the whole reason I'm alive." She looked at him then, really looked, the first time their eyes had met, and what he saw in her face wasn't gratitude or fear but assessment—cold, thorough, the look of a woman measuring a beam for load. "You took my notebook."

"I took—" He put his hand on his coat. "Yours?"

"My team's. My field book. I copied the master maps into it the night before—" She stopped. The rain filled the pause. "Yes. Mine. And you're the reason four men are standing on that causeway instead of eating lunch in Harrow Bend, so I'd say we're roughly even in the damage column."

"I didn't lead them to you. They were already—"

"They were already sweeping the valley because Corvane doesn't leave loose ends, and you, Mr. Underhill, are a loose end with legs." She rowed, easy and strong, and the skiff slid out of the slough into the main current, swinging south, toward the burning camp, toward the men. "I watched you at the camp. You picked up the book, you read two pages, and you put back the pistol that was lying right next to it."

"A pistol's evidence," Wendell said. "It points at whoever carries it."

Something flickered across her face—not quite approval, but the shadow approval casts. "Most people would have taken the pistol and left the book."

"Most people aren't engineers. We're trained to know which part of a wreck matters." The current took them, and the south bank began to slide past, gray in the rain, and Wendell kept his head down and his voice low. "Who's Corvane? Truly. Not the land company. The men in the gray trucks don't work for a land company."

Mara rowed through the question, three strokes, four, eyes on the water. When she answered, her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of someone reciting a lesson written in blood.

"Corvane is the reason my team is dead. Corvane bought up every water right on this river through eleven shell companies, and our survey mapped the proof, and the proof went into my field book, and now my field book is in your coat." She shipped the oars suddenly, and the skiff swung broadside in the current, and she leaned forward across the gunwale until her face was a foot from his. Rain ran off her chin. "So here is what happens next, Mr. Underhill. You're going to tell me your real name, and why the stationmaster at Kessel Junction whispered it like a curse, and why a railroad engineer is living like a ghost on the closed lines. And then you're going to decide whether that notebook belongs to a dead team or a live fight. Because I can swim, and I'm very good at disappearing, and I will take that book into the river with me before I let it fall into Corvane's hands. Are we understood?"

Behind them, upstream, the smoke from the burning camp bent sideways in the wind, and through the rain came the whine of a power saw biting into timber—they were tearing down what the fire missed. The valley that had hidden Wendell for three years was being dismantled around him, plank by plank, secret by secret.

"Wendell," he said. "Wendell Underhill. And the reason the stationmaster whispers my name is that I surveyed the closed lines for Corvane before they closed them, and everything they've done to this valley, they learned from maps I drew."

Mara Voss stared at him through the rain for a long moment. Then, slowly, she took up the oars again.

"Well," she said, turning the skiff downstream, into the darkening water and the gathering storm. "Isn't that convenient. Hold tight, Mr. Underhill. You and I are going to be very good for each other, or very bad. Probably both."

The rain thickened. The river rose. And the last thing Wendell saw of the valley that had sheltered him was the old causeway fading into the gray, its broken spans black against the smoke, the place where his invisibility had finally, permanently, ended.
