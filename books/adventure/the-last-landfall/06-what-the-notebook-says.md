## 6. What the Notebook Says

The cave breathed.

Mara had found it three years ago, or rather the river had shown it to her—a seam in the quarry's northern face where the water ran backward at high tide, curling into the rock instead of away from it. The old stonecutters must have known it too; there were iron rings rusted into the ceiling where they'd hung their lamps, and a ledge of dry floor above the flood line wide enough for two men to sleep. She had slept there herself, on and off, since the night her team stopped existing. It was the only place in the valley she trusted, because it was the only place that changed its shape twice a day with the tide, and men who erased people were not patient enough to learn a thing that moved.

Now there was a second bedroll on the ledge, and a man lying on it who had not spoken for an hour.

Wendell Underhill slept like something that expected to be woken badly—on his side, knees drawn up, one hand curled around the strap of his pack even in sleep, as if the bag might be taken from him in the dark. She had watched him for a long time before deciding what that grip meant. It was not greed. She knew greed; she had grown up around land agents and their soft, wet handshakes. This was different. This was a man holding the only object in the world that belonged to him.

She sat with her back against the cave wall, the notebook open on her knees, and let him sleep a while longer. The tide was coming in. Below the ledge, black water slid through the entrance arch and began its slow climb up the quarry walls, erasing the cut-marks of the stonecutters one band at a time, until the whole chamber became a held breath. By full flood the entrance would be half-drowned, a low dark mouth you could swim through if you knew the angle. Anyone who didn't know would break their skull on the submerged lip of rock she'd wedged a lantern post beside. The cave kept her the way nothing else did: by being difficult.

The notebook lay open to the page she had been unable to stop reading since they'd come down off the ridge—the page with her own handwriting on it, dated eleven days ago, describing a bend in the river called Harrow Reach and the parcel boundaries that met there. Her handwriting. Her careful, cramped, drafting-room lettering, each figure ruled true. And beneath it, in the same ink, the thing that had killed four people: *Corvane Holdings — option registered 14 March — see annex.*

She had written that line without understanding it. That was the part that sat in her chest like swallowed stone. She had been doing her job. Measuring, recording, ruling lines true. The survey commission had sent six of them upriver to map the water rights along the closed rail corridor—ordinary work, seasonal work, the kind of work she'd done in three provinces without anyone so much as raising a voice at her. They had mapped the parcels because mapping parcels was what cartographers did. Nobody had told them what the parcels meant. Nobody had told them that a syndicate had spent eighteen months quietly buying options on every riparian holding between Kessel Junction and the provincial line, buying them cheap from farmers who didn't know a reservoir was coming, buying them through shell companies and dead men's names, assembling in secret the legal skeleton of a dam that would drown forty miles of valley—and that the government survey, the innocent, routine, publicly funded survey, was about to write all of it down in ink that any court clerk could read.

They hadn't even understood it themselves, not fully, until Tam Osei stayed behind one evening in the survey tent with the option registry he'd bought off a drunk recorder's clerk, and cross-referenced it against their own field sheets, and came out into the moonlight looking like a man who had seen his own grave being dug.

*It's not a mistake,* he'd said. *None of it is a mistake. Every parcel we've mapped, they already hold paper on. We're not surveying the valley, Mara. We're appraising it.*

Eleven days later Tam was gone, and Dree, and old Halvorsen, and the boy Piet who had joined the team at Kessel Junction because he wanted to learn instruments. Gone, and the camp left mid-meal, kettle warm, boats missing, no tracks out. As though the valley had opened its mouth and swallowed them politely.

Except it hadn't. Because she had been in the reeds.

* * *

She had gone into the water on instinct, which was the only kind of warning she'd ever trusted. A cartographer learns to read ground the way other people read faces, and the ground below the camp had been wrong all afternoon—too quiet, no herons working the shallows, the cattle on the far bank standing bunched and stupid with their tails clamped. When the gray truck came down the causeway road without headlights, at dusk, moving at walking pace, she was already chest-deep among the reed roots with her nose above the surface and her body angled into the current so her wake would read as wind.

She had watched them do it. Not the killing itself—they took the others upriver, to wherever the taking happened—but everything around it, which was worse in a way she was still learning the shape of. Four men in gray coveralls moving through the camp with the unhurried competence of housekeepers. Folding tents. Wrapping instruments. Lashing the boats onto a flatbed. One of them crouched at the fire pit and fed the coals with papers, feeding them slowly, sheet by sheet, so nothing flared, so the smoke stayed thin. He burned the maps first. Then the logbooks. Then, last, with a kind of ceremony that made her stomach turn over even through thirty yards of dark water, the personal things—Halvorsen's pipe case, Dree's tin of letters, Piet's cheap harmonica, still in its box, still unplayed.

Then they had swept the ground. Actually swept it, with brooms and pine branches, dragging the sand smooth from the fire pit to the waterline, erasing even the fact of feet. And when they were done they stood in a row at the edge of the camp and looked at what they'd made—not satisfaction, nothing so human as satisfaction, just inspection, the way a mason looks at a wall—and got back in the truck and drove away, and the river went on being the river, and the camp stood empty and tidy as a stage set, and the kettle on the stones went slowly cold.

She had stayed in the water until her lips went blue. Not hiding anymore. Just unable to make her legs obey.

The notebook survived by accident, which she had decided was not accident at all but the small stubborn mercy of objects. She'd carried it into the reeds because she always carried it—three years of training, the field book goes where the cartographer goes—and when she finally hauled herself out downstream and vomited river water into the mud, it was still buttoned inside her jacket, swollen fat with seepage, every page warped but legible. The only copy. The commission's duplicates were in the burned trunk. The office copies were in a records room in the provincial capital that Corvane's lawyers could reach faster than any courier. What remained of her team's work, of Tam's last furious arithmetic, of the truth about who owned the water—what remained fit inside a waxed canvas cover smaller than a hymnal, and it was sitting on her knees in a flooded quarry cave next to a sleeping stranger who had picked it out of the mud because he thought it might be worth something.

He hadn't taken anything else. That was the fact she kept returning to, the fact she was ashamed of having needed. He had stood in the ruins of her camp with a thief's hands and a thief's caution, and he had lifted the notebook, turned it over, read the watermark, and put everything else back. The instruments wrapped in oilcloth—he'd looked at them, she'd watched him look, the way a starving man looks through glass—and he'd walked away carrying nothing but a dead man's arithmetic and a skin of water. She had followed him for two days telling herself it was surveillance. It was not entirely surveillance. Some part of her, the part that had been alone in the water watching men erase her life with brooms, had needed to see whether the world contained one person who would stand inside a theft and decline it.

* * *

The tide reached the lip of the ledge and stopped, lapping, and Wendell woke the way she'd predicted—all at once, hand closing on the pack strap, eyes open before his head moved. He oriented himself in half a second: cave, water, woman, fire ring, exit. She'd begun to find it almost restful to watch, this inventory he performed on waking, this accounting of everything that could kill him. It was the same inventory she ran. Two people who counted exits the way other people counted blessings.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"Someone had to watch the door."

"I can watch a door."

"Not underwater you can't." She nodded toward the entrance, where the flood now pressed against the arch like a thumb against a drumhead. "This cave changes. Twice a day. If you don't know its moods, it drowns you politely."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of where a smile used to be kept. "You chose a hideout with a personality."

"I chose a hideout nobody patient would bother learning." She closed the notebook and set it on the stone between them, deliberately, the way you set down a card you intend to play. "And now I'm going to break my own rule, engineer, because you saved my tires and I owe you a debt, and debts are the only law I still believe in. So listen."

He sat up. In the lantern light his face had the geography of a man ten years older than his body—she'd guessed thirty-four, thirty-five, and guessed too that the extra decade had been added recently, in installments, by nights exactly like this one.

"Do you know what Corvane Land Syndicate is?" she asked.

"Everyone on these lines knows the name. They buy up land along closed routes. Cheap."

"They buy land along closed routes because closed routes are where the government stops looking. Do you know what they've bought in this valley? Not the land. Look past the land." She opened the notebook again, to the page, and turned it toward him, and put her finger under her own handwriting. "Water rights. Riparian rights. Options on every parcel with river frontage from here to the provincial line, bought through nine shell companies over eighteen months, registered under names of men who are dead or never existed. They have assembled, on paper, the legal right to take every drop in this valley. And paper rights are worthless—" she tapped the page "—unless the water is worth taking. Unless someone official confirms the water is worth taking. Which is precisely what a government hydrographic survey does. We weren't mapping the valley, Wendell. We were pricing it for them. Our measurements were going to be the appraisal that made their paper gold. Except we didn't know that, so we wrote down everything. All of it. Including the option registrations, including the shell companies, including the names. We wrote down the crime in the margins of our own murder, because cartographers write down everything, and then we filed to publish."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the flooded dark below, water dripped from the ceiling with a sound like slow counting.

"And Corvane couldn't allow publication," Wendell said quietly. It wasn't a question. He was an engineer; he could see the load path of the thing as clearly as she could.

"No. Because once those figures entered the public record, any court, any journalist, any farmer with a lawyer could trace the options and see the dam before the first stone was laid. Forty miles of valley drowned, and every family in it paid a tenth of what their land was worth, and the syndicate holds the water, and the water is worth more than the land ever was. That's the whole scheme. It isn't even clever. It's just patient, and illegal in nine places, and it required exactly one thing to go away." She heard her own voice flatten, go drafting-room level, the tone she used for figures that hurt. "Us. Six people whose entire crime was measuring a river accurately."

"The gray trucks."

"The gray trucks. I watched them take my team upriver and burn our work and sweep the ground with brooms." His face didn't change, but something behind it did; she was learning to read the small tectonic shifts of him. "I was in the water. Thirty yards. I watched, and I lived, and I have spent eleven days being the only witness to my friends' deaths, which means I am also the only person alive who knows they need avenging. Do you understand what that is? To be the sole holder of a truth everyone powerful wants buried?" She pushed the notebook an inch closer to him. "You do. Don't you. That's why you didn't steal the rest. You looked at those instruments and you saw your own life, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for the truck."

For a long moment he said nothing. The tide pressed at the arch. Then he said, "Three years ago I surveyed the closed lines. All of them. I drew the map you're living out of." His voice was very even, the evenness of a man holding something heavy at arm's length. "And when the corridor was closed, my surveys were used to value the land for the closure sales. My figures. My name on the certification sheets. I signed documents I never should have signed, and when the sales went through rotten, it was my signature they hung it on. I didn't know what I was signing. That doesn't matter to a court and it didn't matter to Corvane, who needed an engineer to be guilty so no one would ask who'd bought the commissioners. So I ran. Three years, moving every two nights, staying alive by never being anywhere long enough to be found." He looked at the notebook instead of at her. "You're saying they did the same thing to you that they did to me. Same method. Find the honest work, attach your name to it, and when the work becomes evidence, remove the worker."

"Remove the worker," she repeated. "Yes. Except they missed one."

"In the reeds."

"In the reeds." She laughed, briefly, and it came out wrong, half sob, and she let it, because there was no one here to perform strength for except a man who slept holding his pack. "Eleven days I've been asking myself what I'm supposed to do. Run? Run where? They erased a government survey team, Wendell. They'll erase one cartographer between breakfast and lunch. But if I run, the notebook runs with me and dies with me, and Tam's arithmetic dies with it, and the valley drowns on schedule, and Corvane wins so quietly that in twenty years no one will even know there was a fight." She turned the pages, carefully, the swollen leaves soft as cloth under her fingers. Tam's cross-reference tables. Dree's discharge measurements, beautiful, exact. Old Halvorsen's marginal notes in pencil, grumbling about the coffee. Piet's first solo leveling run, initialed and praised in the margin in Halvorsen's hand. Four dead people, alive on every page. "But if I stay—if I get this to the provincial archive, filed, timestamped, entered into the record before Corvane's lawyers can quash it—then it's not a dead woman's souvenir. It's evidence. Public evidence. The one thing in the world that syndicate cannot burn, because the moment it's in the record, burning it proves it mattered."

"That archive is four days downriver," Wendell said. "And every road out of this valley has gray trucks on it. I came in ahead of one."

"So did I. Which is why I'm not asking you for directions." She met his eyes and held them, and made herself say the thing she'd been building toward since the causeway, since the slashed tires, since the moment a disgraced thief had driven a collapsing viaduct like a man who loved the geometry of falling bridges. "I'm asking you for the river. You know these closed lines better than any living soul—you drew them. There are ways down this valley that aren't roads. Old service spurs, towpaths, quarry tramways, ferry stands the charts forgot. I have the evidence and no route. You have the routes and no reason. I'm offering you the reason."

The lantern guttered and steadied. Below the ledge the flood stood at its height, the cave full to the brim of its held breath, and in the silence Mara heard the other sound, faint and rhythmic, coming down the flooded entrance from the world outside: rain. The first fat, deliberate drops of the monsoon's opening argument, striking the river in the dark.

Wendell looked at the notebook—at Tam's tables, at the dead men's initials—and then at her, and she watched him arrive at the edge of the decision the way she'd once watched him arrive at the edge of the viaduct: gauging the drop, testing the geometry, knowing the bridge was already falling.

"There's a boat," he said, "rotting under the old ferry stand at Millbrook Bend. I passed it on the way in. If the hull's sound below the waterline—which it won't be—we could be at Harrow Bend in four days, if the river doesn't eat us first."

"And if the river eats us?"

"Then we'll have died on a surveyed route," he said, "with the figures in hand. Halvorsen would approve."

It was the first joke she'd heard him make, and it was terrible, and it was aimed at the dead with respect, and she found—to her surprise, in the dripping dark, with the monsoon beginning outside and the whole gray machinery of Corvane turning somewhere upstream in the night—that it was the first time in eleven days her chest had felt anything other than stone.

She snuffed the lantern. Outside, the rain thickened, and the river began to rise.
