## 7. The Leaking Boat

The skiff had been rotting under a tarp behind Auntie Sef's cousin's drying racks for longer than either of them wanted to think about, and when Mara pulled the tarp away, the smell that rose up was old rain and dead wood and something faintly sweet—the smell of a thing giving up.

"This is your plan," Wendell said.

"This is the boat," Mara said. "The plan is downriver."

He crouched at the gunwale and pressed his thumb into the hull. The wood gave like overripe fruit. Along the port strake, three planks had opened at the seams, dark lines where the caulking had crumbled to powder, and near the sternpost there was a patch of punky grain he could have scooped out with a spoon. He'd spent eleven years of his life keeping locomotives alive past their designed deaths; he knew the difference between tired and finished, and this boat was somewhere in between, which meant it was exactly as honest as everything else in his life lately.

"She'll float," he said, "if I keep her bailed."

"That's all I'm asking."

"It's not all you're asking." He stood, wiped his hands on his trousers. "You're asking me to go down forty miles of watched river in a coffin with a paint job."

Mara looked at him across the skiff, and the morning light came through the gaps in the drying racks and laid stripes on her face. She hadn't slept much—neither of them had—but she wore it differently than he did. He went gray and slow. She went sharp, like a blade someone had re-honed overnight out of pure spite.

"The roads are watched," she said. "You saw the trucks on the causeway road yesterday. Corvane has men at every bridge crossing between here and Harrow Bend, and they're stopping anyone heading toward the archive with a face that isn't local. But nobody watches the water in monsoon season, because nobody but idiots goes on the water in monsoon season."

"So we're idiots."

"We're invisible," she said. "There's a difference. Idiots get caught. Invisible people just have to stay that way."

Wendell almost laughed. Three years of staying invisible, and it had never once felt like a strategy. It had felt like disappearing by inches—a man subtracting himself from the world one town, one job, one name at a time, until what was left didn't weigh enough to leave a footprint. He'd told himself it was discipline. Standing in the mud behind the fish racks, looking at the rotten skiff and the woman who'd already died once to keep her evidence dry, he understood for the first time that invisibility was also a kind of cowardice, and that Mara Voss had more courage in her wet boots than he'd had in three years of running.

"Fine," he said. "But we do it my way on the water. You bail when I tell you to bail, you move when I say move, and if I say beach her, we beach her, no arguments."

"Agreed," Mara said, "as long as you understand that if I say paddle, you paddle, because I've lived on this river my whole life and you've lived on rails."

"Rails are just rivers made of iron," Wendell said.

"And you wonder why they took your license."

* * *

They launched an hour before dusk, when the light went copper and the river turned to hammered metal, because Mara said the syndicate's spotters kept banker's hours and wouldn't be glassing the water at the end of the day. The skiff rode low from the first minute. Wendell sat in the stern with the tiller against his hip and watched the water come through the seams in slow, patient beads, each one swelling along a crack like sweat on a fevered brow, gathering, letting go, running down into the bilge where it pooled around their heels in a cold insistent way that said: *I'm not in a hurry. Are you?*

Mara bailed with a cut-down kerosene tin, working in a rhythm she'd found within ten minutes and never lost—scoop, swing, dump, scoop—and Wendell found himself timing his steering corrections to her stroke without meaning to. When she leaned left to dump over the port side, the skiff rolled a half-degree and he eased the bow right to compensate, and after a while they were doing it without looking at each other, two people who had met four days ago under the worst circumstances imaginable moving like they'd crewed together for a season.

"You've done this before," he said, when the first stars came out and the banks dissolved into black shapes breathing.

"Bailed?"

"Boats. Rivers. All of it."

"My father fished the upper bends," she said. She didn't stop bailing. "I grew up with river water in my ears. When the survey team hired me, half the interview was whether I could read water. The other half was whether I could draw it." A pause. Her scoop paused mid-swing. "I could do both. That's why I'm the one who's still alive, probably. I knew where the deep channel ran, so I knew where the reeds would hold me, and I knew how long I could stay under in cold water, which is longer than most people think if you don't fight it."

Wendell steered and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to that that wasn't either pity or horror, and she deserved neither from him.

The night settled in. Rain began around midnight—not the monsoon proper, which everyone on the river said was coming early and coming angry, but a fine persistent drizzle that softened sound and made the world feel wrapped in wool. Wendell navigated by memory and instinct: the map in his head of the closed lines ran parallel to the river for most of its length, and every culvert, every bridge abutment, every mile marker he'd once surveyed for the railroad gave him a fix on where they were. It occurred to him, somewhere in the small hours, that this was the first time in three years the map in his head had been useful for going *toward* something instead of away.

"How far to the quarry cave?" Mara asked, near dawn.

"Twelve miles. We'll make it by nine if the wind holds off."

It did not hold off.

* * *

The squall came out of the western hills at mid-morning like something released rather than arrived. One moment the river was gray silk; the next, the surface went white-spotted, then furrowed, then torn, and the wind hit them broadside with a wall of warm wet air that smelled of drowned earth. Wendell had been through storms on foot, in freight cars, once in the cab of a runaway locomotive with the brakes burned blue, and none of it had prepared him for how naked a rotten skiff made a man feel. There was no mass under him. No steel. Just a quarter-inch of dying wood and the water underneath, patient as arithmetic.

"Bail!" he shouted, and Mara was already bailing, both hands now, the tin flashing.

The leak changed character the moment the river got rough. In calm water it seeped. In chop, it *pumped*—every wave that slapped the hull drove a fresh pulse through the open seams, and Wendell could hear the difference, could feel it through the soles of his feet, the bilge going from ankle-deep to shin-deep in twenty minutes. He did the arithmetic he hated doing: at this rate, they had perhaps an hour before the freeboard vanished and the river simply took back what it had always owned.

"Beach her?" Mara called.

"Not yet!" The bank here was a cut of red clay, vertical, six feet high—if they grounded in this chop they'd be stove against it inside a minute. Ahead, maybe half a mile, a willow stand marked a soft point where the current eddied backward. "There! The willows!"

"I see them!"

What followed was the longest half-mile of Wendell Underhill's life, and he later understood it was the moment everything turned—not dramatically, not with any single decision, but in the accumulation of small trusts. He called the waves. She read them. When a swell lifted the stern he feathered the tiller to keep the bow into the trough; when the wind gusted he shouted *lean left* and she threw her weight without asking why, and the skiff, which should by every law of naval architecture have rolled and filled, held its line instead. Twice the hull struck submerged snags and he heard wood crunch and waited for the cold rush that meant the end, and twice it was only the outer planking sacrificing itself, and the inner skin held.

They reached the willows with the bilge at their knees.

The eddy behind the point was brown and boiling but slow, and Wendell put the bow into the root tangle and let the branches take them. For a long moment neither of them moved. Rain hammered the canopy overhead in a sound like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience.

"Well," Mara said finally. She was soaked through, hair plastered to her skull, hands bleeding slightly from the tin's raw edge, and she was grinning. Actually grinning. "You can steer."

"You can bail," Wendell said.

"High praise from the engineer."

"Highest I give."

She laughed, and the sound surprised them both—he saw it land on her own face like something foreign—and then she got back to work, because the bilge didn't care about bonding moments. Wendell stripped off his shirt, wrung it, and went into the flooded stern compartment to find out what else the river had broken.

What he found was bad and getting worse. The transom knee—a wooden bracket older than either of them—had worked loose, and with it gone, the whole stern flexed with every wave, working the seams wider in a slow vicious cycle. On a train he'd have had a shop, tools, steel. Here he had a shirt, a knife, a coil of rotten rope, and the wreckage of a fish rack he'd helped dismantle two days ago, some of which Mara had insisted on bringing aboard over his objections.

He kissed his own objections goodbye and started tearing the bamboo lashing strips into cordage.

"What are you doing?" Mara asked, watching him wedge himself half into the stern compartment, working blind by feel.

"Engineering," he said. "Pray I remember how."

He braced the knee with a forked piece of rack-pole, jammed a folded shirt into the worst seam as packing, and then—this was the part that mattered—lashed the whole assembly tight with the bamboo strips, wet-corded, pulled down with his boot against the hull until the knots bit. Bamboo swelled when it soaked. Within an hour the lashings would tighten themselves, squeezing the packing into the seams, turning a flexing wound into a rigid scar. It wasn't shipwright's work. It was locomotive work—bracing a cracked frame so it could limp home—and it was ugly, and it held.

By late afternoon the squall had blown itself east and the river lay exhausted and swollen, carrying whole trees past them like it was showing off what it could do. They ate cold rice and dried fish in the lee of the willows, and Wendell bailed the last inch of water out himself, and the skiff sat in the eddy riding maybe an inch higher than she had that morning, which was as close to a compliment as the river ever paid anybody.

"You fixed her," Mara said.

"I delayed her death. There's a difference."

"You're always saying things like that. Things that make less mean more."

"Do I?"

"All the time. 'I delayed her death.' 'Invisible people just have to stay that way.' You talk like a man translating himself out of a language nobody else speaks anymore." She looked at him across the little boat, and the evening light came low and gold through the willow leaves and touched the water between them. "Three years alone does that. I know. I only had four days, and I could feel myself starting."

Wendell turned the empty rice tin in his hands. He thought about telling her something true—that he couldn't remember the last time someone had noticed how he talked, or cared; that the silence of those three years had gotten into him like water into a hull, slow and patient, filling up the spaces where other people used to be; that sitting in a leaking boat with a woman who'd hidden underwater to survive, bailing side by side through a storm, was the least alone he had felt since the day they took his license and his name went on a list.

What he said was: "We should run the night stretch while the water's high. The current will carry us to the bend by dawn."

Mara studied him for a moment, and he had the distinct feeling she'd heard the thing he didn't say anyway, filed it away in whatever careful place she kept things, and decided to let him have it.

"Current's with us," she agreed. "And I'll take first bail."

* * *

They ran through the dark with the flood current, the repaired stern holding, the bamboo lashings creaking as they swelled tight exactly as he'd promised himself they would. Around two in the morning the rain started again, heavier, steadier, the sky lowering itself onto the river until there was no horizon anywhere, just water above and below and the small stubborn lamp of their progress between. Wendell steered by the feel of the current against the rudder and the sound of the banks, and once, distantly, he heard something that might have been a truck engine on a levee road, and Mara heard it too, and they looked at each other in the dark and neither said anything, and the sound faded, and they were still invisible.

Near dawn the river bent south around a bluff, and Mara pointed to a fold in the hills where a waterfall fell in a white thread, and said, "There. The quarry cave is behind that fall. We can hide through the day and I'll show you what's in the notebook—all of it, everything I couldn't say on open water."

She reached into the oilskin packet at her breast and drew out the field notebook, checking it the way other people checked a sleeping child. And Wendell saw her face change.

The tunnel under the causeway, the swim through the reeds, the days of damp hiding—it had all taken its toll. The oilskin had kept out the worst, but not all of it. Along the notebook's lower edge, where the cover had warped, the pages had begun to drink the damp, and when Mara thumbed them open with suddenly careful fingers, the ink of the last third of the book shivered and blurred, lines of survey data softening at their edges like breath on cold glass.

"No," she whispered. "No, no—"

"Mara." Wendell put the tiller hard over and brought the skiff around toward the white thread of the falls. "How much is still legible?"

She didn't answer. She was holding the notebook the way you hold something that is trying to die in your hands, and the waterfall ahead grew louder, and the first real light of the day came gray and merciless across the water, and Wendell understood that the storm they'd survived had been the easy part.
