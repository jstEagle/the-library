## 8. River People

Mara saw the lights before she heard the voices—a scatter of lanterns strung low over black water, like a constellation that had lost its nerve and come down to rest on the river.

"Kill the lamp," she whispered, though Wendell had already done it. The skiff drifted with the current, its patched hull whispering against the flood-borne debris. Three days they had been on the water since the quarry cave, three days of leaking seams and bailing and the particular silence two strangers keep when they are learning each other's habits without meaning to. She knew now that he slept in snatches, one hand always near the notebook wrapped in oilcloth beneath his coat. He knew that she counted things—reeds, stars, the strokes of her paddle—because counting was how she kept the fear from pooling.

Ahead, the lanterns resolved into shapes: flat-bottomed boats lashed side by side into a floating street, houses of woven bamboo and salvaged tin perched on rafts of sealed drums, a walkway of planks running bow to bow. Nets hung drying from poles. Somewhere a dog barked, absurd and domestic, and the sound went through Mara like a hook through cloth.

"That's a village," she said. "A whole village that floats."

"River people," Wendell said quietly. "They follow the fish and the floods. There used to be settlements like this all along the lower reaches—before the levees." He said *levees* the way other men said a curse word, flat and bitter, and did not explain.

"We need dry ground. Or something like it." Mara looked at him. In the dark his face was all angles and shadow, three years of running carved into it. "And we can't pay for anything."

"No," he agreed. "But we can work."

He shipped the oars and let the current carry them in, which she understood was deliberate—you didn't row up to strangers at night looking like an attack. A boy of perhaps ten spotted them first, standing on the plank walkway with a lantern held high, and shouted something in the river dialect, and then there were more lanterns, more faces, and Mara felt the old animal urge to turn the boat and vanish. She had survived the camp by being nothing, by sinking into reeds and mud until even her own name seemed like someone else's property. Floating toward thirty watching eyes was the opposite of everything that had kept her alive.

Wendell raised both hands, palms out. "Travelers," he called. "Boat's taking water. We're asking for shelter, not trouble."

An old woman came to the rail of the largest raft, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been knocked off balance by water. She was small and brown and wrapped in a man's coat far too big for her, and her eyes—Mara thought—were the color of the river itself, which is to say they gave back exactly what you brought to them.

"A boat taking water," the old woman repeated. "In monsoon season. On this stretch." She looked at the skiff's patched seams, at the bail bucket, at the two of them, and Mara watched her read the whole journey the way Mara herself read contour lines—from small signs to large truths. "You've come down from the hills."

"From upriver," Wendell said, which was true and told her nothing.

The old woman smiled without warmth. "Everyone comes from upriver lately. Gray trucks upriver. Smoke upriver." Her gaze sharpened. "You'll tie off at the downstream post. You'll eat what we eat. And you'll tell me your names, because on my water, nobody stays anonymous."

*On my water.* Mara noted the possessive. This was not a guest; this was a sovereign of a small wet nation.

"Wendell," he said, after a pause a half-second too long. "This is Mara."

"And I'm Sef," the old woman said. "Auntie Sef, if you're young, and Auntie Sef regardless, if you're smart."

* * *

They ate fish stew under an awning of patched canvas while rain ticked softly on the tin above, and Mara learned the shape of the community by learning the shape of the meal: the boy with the lantern was called Pilo and had three younger siblings and no parents the adults would discuss; the net-mender with the burned forearm was called Doss and asked Wendell four questions about his boat's hull patching before offering two criticisms; a girl of fifteen named Ressa sat nearest Mara and studied her hands—the ink stains, the calluses—and finally said, "You draw maps."

It wasn't a question. Mara looked up. "Why do you say that?"

"You hold your spoon like a pen. And you looked at our lantern line twice, and both times your fingers moved like you were measuring it." Ressa shrugged. "My mother was a surveyor's daughter. Downriver people notice hands."

Mara put the spoon down slowly. *Was.* The past tense sat in the sentence like a stone in a shoe.

Around them the raft-village settled into night sounds—water against drums, rope creaking, a baby crying and being hushed, the dog relocating itself with a jingle of tags. It was, Mara realized, the first time since the camp that she had been inside a human sound instead of hiding from one. It hurt in a way she hadn't prepared for, like weight returning to a limb that had gone numb.

Auntie Sef came and sat across from Wendell, uninvited, and set down two cups of some smoked tea that smelled like a forest fire's ghost.

"Now," she said. "You'll tell me what's chasing you. Not who—I can guess who, and I'd rather not hear their name on my deck. But what."

Wendell turned his cup but didn't drink. "There was a government survey team," he said. "Mapping the upper valley. Water rights, mostly. Their camp was emptied out. Not abandoned—*emptied*. Instruments wrapped, kettle warm, boats gone, no tracks. Someone erased them carefully."

Sef's face didn't change, but the whole raft seemed to lean in slightly, as if the community were one organism listening through its oldest part.

"And you found this camp," Sef said, "and then you came downriver, and gray trucks came downriver behind you, and now you're here." She sipped her tea. "You're either very brave or very stupid, engineer."

Mara stiffened. "We didn't say he was—"

"I know an engineer's hands too," Sef said mildly. "And I know the way he looked at our pump barge when he walked past it this evening. Like a doctor passing a sick child in the street. He couldn't help himself." She set down her cup. "But it's more than the hands. Sit still, Underhill. Let me look at you properly."

She leaned forward into the lantern light and studied his face for a long moment, and Mara watched something move through the old woman's expression—recognition arriving not all at once but in pieces, like a photograph developing.

"Six years ago," Sef said. "Maybe seven. There was a dam dispute. Corvane wanted to build upstream, and their engineers swore on stacks of paper that the flooding would be minimal, contained, a gift to the valley. Our men stood in the hearing hall in their good shirts and got made to look like illiterate fishermen crying about puddles." Her voice stayed level, but her knuckles whitened around the cup. "And then one witness took the stand who spoke their language. A railroad engineer. Knew the land better than Corvane's own surveyors—he'd mapped those valleys for their rail lines, before. He showed the commissioners where the water would actually go. Drew it on a chalkboard with his own hand until the room understood. We won. First time river families ever beat them in a hearing. First time anyone beat them."

The rain filled the silence. Mara looked at Wendell. He had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a door open onto a room he'd bricked shut years ago.

"What happened to him?" Sef asked. "After. He vanished. Some said Corvane ruined him—there were charges, falsified surveys, they stripped his license. Some said he took their money and ran. We never knew which story to believe." She tilted her head. "So which is it, Wendell Underhill? Because you have his face, older and hungrier. And you flinched when I said the chalkboard."

For a long moment Wendell said nothing. Mara could see him calculating—not escape routes this time, but something else. Whether the truth was a thing he could afford. Whether he had any left to give.

"The charges weren't false," he said finally, and his voice was rough, like a mechanism long unused. "That's the elegant part of what they did. They buried me with just enough real dirt. I did cut corners on a survey once—one survey, early, tired, pressured. Corvane bought the record of it and built a cathedral of lies on top. By the end I couldn't prove which parts were mine and which were theirs. So I ran." He looked at his hands. "I've been running along closed lines for three years. Never stopping anywhere longer than two nights. That was the rule."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting on a raft being recognized by a woman I've never met," he said, "so apparently the rule isn't holding."

Sef laughed—a short, hard bark with no cruelty in it. Then she reached across and gripped his wrist, an old woman's grip, surprisingly strong, and held it.

"Do you know what that hearing bought us?" she said. "Seven years. Seven years of water we could fish, banks we could moor on, children who grew up thinking the river belonged to us because one man stood up and made the law agree. Every boat you see tied off here, every drum under every floor—that's downstream of your chalkboard, engineer. You drew us. Then you disappeared, and we grieved you the way you grieve a drowned man whose body never comes ashore." She released his wrist. "And now the river has washed you up on my deck, chased by the same wolves, carrying the same kind of paper against them."

"It's not testimony," Mara said quickly. "It's maps. Survey data. My team paid for it—" Her voice caught on *paid for*, and she steadied it. "Four people died for it. Maybe five. We don't know yet."

Sef turned to her, and the hardness in the old woman's face shifted into something older and sadder. "I heard about the survey camp," she said quietly. "Smoke on the water two days ago, upstream. Trucks on the causeway road. River tells you things, if you listen." She looked from Mara to Wendell and back. "You think you came here for shelter. Maybe. But maybe the river had opinions about where you should drift."

* * *

Later, Mara stood at the downstream rail of the great raft, watching the black water slide past, the notebook's oilcloth bundle heavy inside her jacket. Behind her the village murmured—someone singing low, the dog dreaming, the endless conversation of water on wood.

Wendell came to stand beside her. Neither spoke for a while. That was another thing she'd learned about him: he could share a silence without trying to fill it, which was rarer than honesty and nearly as valuable.

"They knew your name," she said at last. "Not the wanted-poster name. The chalkboard name."

"Yes."

"How does that feel?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. "Like wearing a coat I buried," he said. "It still fits. That's the terrible part. I spent three years telling myself I was never anybody—that invisibility was a skill, a discipline, the only safe way to live. And one old woman looks at me and I'm somebody again. Somebody who *belonged* somewhere, once, and left." His jaw worked. "They remembered what I did, Mara. Not what was done to me. What I *did*. Do you understand what that does to a man who's built his whole survival on being forgettable?"

Mara looked out at the dark. "I hid underwater for two days while they took my team," she said. "I lay in the reeds and breathed through a hollow stem and listened to them wrap up our instruments like they were packing dishes. And afterward I told myself the same thing. If I'm nothing, they can't erase me, because there's nothing left to erase." She pulled the notebook out and held it between them, the oilcloth slick with river damp. "But this isn't nothing. And neither are you. I think that's the whole problem, Wendell. We've been surviving by pretending we don't exist, and the evidence keeps saying otherwise."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "When did you get wise?"

"When I started counting instead of panicking. Counting forces you to admit things exist." She tucked the notebook away. "Sef will hide us. But she'll want something. People like her always trade fair—it's the only currency that holds value in a flood."

"She already wants something," Wendell said. "Have you seen the pump barge?"

Mara had—moored at the village's edge, a squat steel-hulled workboat with a diesel pump on its deck, the heart of the community's flood defenses. Even in the dark she'd noticed the rust bleeding from its seams, the tarp over the engine flapping loose.

"It's dying," Wendell said. "Impeller's shot, intake screen's clogged with silt, and the mounting bolts are corroded to lace. When the big storm comes—and it will come early this year, the barometer's been falling since noon—if that pump fails, the lowest rafts take water. The ones with the children." He stared at the machine's dark silhouette. "She hasn't asked me yet. But she will. And saying yes means staying. Staying means being findable." He exhaled slowly. "Two nights, Mara. That was the rule. We've already broken it."

Behind them, a floorboard creaked. Auntie Sef stood in the lantern glow with her enormous coat and her river-colored eyes, and she did not pretend she hadn't been listening.

"Your pump," Wendell said, before she could speak. "I'll look at it in the morning."

Sef smiled, slow as sunrise. "I know," she said. "I heard you talking to it when you walked past. Engineers talk to machines the way mothers talk to sleeping children." She turned to Mara, and her voice gentled. "Sleep in the dry loft, cartographer. Both of you. Tomorrow there's work, and after work—" her gaze went upriver, into the dark where the gray trucks lived, "—after work, we should talk about what's coming down that water. Because I've seen the clouds building in the north, and I've seen smoke on the causeway, and I'm an old woman who has learned that the storm and the wolves often travel together."

Far upriver, thunder muttered—or perhaps it was engines. On this river, Mara thought, it was getting harder to tell the difference.
