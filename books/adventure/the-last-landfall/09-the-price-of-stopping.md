## 9. The Price of Stopping

The pump barge sat low in the water like a tired animal, listing to port, its engine housing furred with rust and river weed. Wendell stood on its deck in the gray half-light before dawn and listened to it the way he'd once listened to locomotives—with his palms flat against the metal, reading the vibration like a language.

It was a bad language. The main impeller was scored nearly smooth. The intake grate had half-collapsed inward, sucking silt instead of water. The drive coupling wobbled with every revolution, a looseness that would eat itself into something worse within the week. He had known barges like this die. He had watched them die from the windows of passing trains, their crews standing helpless on the banks as floodwater took what the machinery could no longer hold back.

"You can fix it." Auntie Sef stood at the rail behind him, not asking. She was a small woman built like a mooring post, sixty years of river weather cured into her face, and she had the particular patience of someone who had outlasted three governments and two floods.

"I can keep it alive," Wendell said. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "But I've watched you work since yesterday, engineer. You don't keep things alive. You bring them back from wherever they've gone." She turned her eyes toward the eastern sky, where the monsoon stacked itself in towers of bruised purple. "The question isn't whether you can fix my barge. The question is whether you'll still be here when it matters."

Wendell said nothing. That was the trouble with old women who ran floating villages—they asked exactly the questions a man had spent three years learning never to answer.

* * *

He had been given the stern cabin of Sef's own houseboat, a space barely larger than a handcar's tool locker, with a hammock strung between iron hooks and a porthole that looked out on black water. Mara had the bunk across the narrow aisle in the next cabin, close enough that he heard her turn over in the night, heard the small sounds of a person who slept badly because sleeping well required feeling safe.

Neither of them had slept well in three years. But here, rocked by the current against the hull, wrapped in the smell of woodsmoke and fish and tar, Wendell had come closer than he had since the night the warrants went out.

That was what frightened him.

He lay awake on the second night doing the arithmetic he always did—the arithmetic of staying. Two nights was the rule. Two nights anywhere, then move, because men who moved were ghosts and men who stayed became names, and names could be found by the people who paid to find them. Corvane's men knew an engineer was loose in these hills. The lawyer's offer still sat in his memory like a fishhook: money, clean papers, a new life—for the notebook, for silence, for Mara's maps drowned in some quarry lake. And beneath the offer, as he'd discovered too late, the wire: kill them both, sink the boat, let the river do the paperwork.

If he stayed with the flotilla, he was a fixed point. Fixed points could be triangulated. A bribed fisherman had already found them once; another would find them again. Every day the pump barge kept him aboard was a day he handed Corvane a bearing.

And if he left—if he and Mara slipped downstream tonight, ahead of the worst of the storm—he calculated the odds the way he'd once calculated grades and load limits. The skiff was sound enough; he'd recaulked her seams himself. But the monsoon coming up the valley was not weather, it was a wall of moving water, and a fourteen-foot skiff against a wall of moving water was not courage, it was arithmetic with only one answer. Auntie Sef had shown him the flood marks on her pilings. Nineteen years ago. Fourteen years ago. Both times, she said, the boats that left early came back as splinters, if they came back at all. The ones that lashed together and held position through the surge—those lived.

Stay and be found. Leave and be drowned. Those were the choices, and Wendell Underhill, who had survived three years by never choosing anything but motion, found himself cornered by the one thing he had no rule for.

* * *

Mara found him at dawn on the pump barge deck, already elbow-deep in the coupling housing, grease to his shoulders.

"You've been here all night," she said.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Liar." She crouched beside him, handing him tools before he asked for them—she'd learned his kit in a week, learned which wrench went where, learned that he worked in silence until the problem cracked open and then talked for ten minutes straight. "You're doing the math again. I can tell. You get this look, like the whole world is a ledger."

"It is a ledger."

"And what does it say?"

He wiped his hands slowly, buying time. "It says we should have left Tuesday."

"It also says," Mara said quietly, "that there are eleven children on this flotilla under the age of ten, and that the pump barge is the only thing keeping the lower houseboats dry when the river comes up. You did that math too. I watched you do it. You just didn't say it out loud, because saying it out loud means it owns you."

Wendell stared at the ruined coupling in his hands. "I spent three years making sure nothing owned me. No debts, no names, no addresses. It works, Mara. It kept me alive."

"Did it?" She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "Because from where I'm sitting, it kept you *running*. Alive is different. Alive is what Auntie Sef has. She's seventy-one years old and she sleeps like a stone because forty boats answer to her and every one of them knows she'd sink with them before she'd leave. Nobody's hunting her. Do you know why? Because you can't erase a woman who belongs to everyone. There'd be nothing left of the river worth stealing."

He wanted to argue. He was good at arguing with himself; it was most of what he'd done for three years. But the argument wouldn't form. Somewhere between the flooded quarry cave and the leaking skiff and the night currents where he'd almost taken Corvane's money, the ledger had started writing entries he couldn't balance. Auntie Sef feeding them without being asked. The boy Teo who had followed him around the barge deck for two days asking questions about engines until Wendell caught himself explaining gear ratios to an eight-year-old with total seriousness. The women who had mended Mara's coat. The old man who had pressed a jar of pickled eel into Wendell's hands and called him *the engineer*—not the fugitive, not the thief, the engineer—as though the word were a title and not a charge sheet.

They thought he was someone. That was the trap. Not Corvane's trap—Sef's, or the river's, or whatever force arranged such things. They had looked at a man who had spent three years being nobody and decided he was somebody, and now leaving would prove them wrong, and he found he could not bear to prove them wrong.

"When did it happen?" Mara asked, watching his face.

"When did what happen?"

"When you stopped wanting to run."

He turned back to the coupling. "Hand me the shim stock."

* * *

The work took four days, and the storm gave him five.

He rebuilt the coupling first, machining a new bushing on a lathe Sef produced from a storage barge—a relic of some dredging operation, decades old, its bed crusted with paint but its ways still true. He worked by lamplight while the first outer bands of rain came down in long silver curtains and the children were battened below decks, and Teo sat on an upturned crate forbidden to touch anything, watching with the concentration of a man twice his age.

"Why'd you learn all this?" the boy asked on the third night, as Wendell seated the new bushing with a wooden mallet and a block of scrap oak.

"Somebody has to."

"But why *you*?"

Wendell paused, mallet raised. There were answers that were true and answers that were safe, and for three years he had only ever used the safe ones. "Because when I was about your age," he heard himself say, "my father ran a water mill, and when it broke, we ate nothing until it was fixed. I fixed it the first time when I was nine. My father watched me do it and then went inside and drank, because he knew I'd be better at it than him, and I think that was the proudest and saddest day of his life." He struck the bushing home. "So. Somebody has to, and I was the somebody who got chosen. Same as you, probably, someday."

Teo considered this with enormous gravity. "My mother says I'm going to be a fisherman."

"You might be both. Rivers need engineers too."

The intake grate took a full day—wading chest-deep in the rising river to clear the collapsed bars and weld new steel across the gap, working in the brown current while the rain thickened around him and someone on the bank held a lantern and refused, absolutely refused, to take shelter despite Sef shouting at her. The impeller was the worst of it. Scored metal cannot be un-scored; it must be replaced or dressed, and there was no replacement within a hundred miles. So Wendell dressed it—hours at the lathe with grinding compound and a hand-made mandrel, truing the blades by feel, checking balance by spinning the shaft on knife edges and watching for the heavy spot, correcting it with wax until the wax wore even and the wheel spun true. It was slow, patient, invisible work, the kind of work no one ever saw and everything depended on.

He understood, somewhere in the third hour of balancing, that this was the first thing he had built in three years that was not an escape.

Every repair on every handcar, every patch on every stolen mile of track—it had all been in service of leaving. This pump would stay here after he was gone. Its water would rise through a pipe into a tank above forty families' heads. If he died tomorrow, it would keep pumping. Something of him would remain, fixed, located, findable—and instead of horror he felt something so close to relief that it scared him more than Corvane ever had.

* * *

On the fifth evening the storm arrived for true.

It did not build; it simply arrived, the way a train arrives, with a pressure change you felt in your teeth and a sound like the whole sky becoming a tunnel. The flotilla gathered itself into the le of the bluff—forty boats lashed in clusters, riding out the first hours in a chaos of shouted instructions and snapping lines. Wendell stood on the pump barge with Sef and three of her nephews and ran the engine he had rebuilt, and it ran, it *ran*, clean and steady through the first surge while around them other pumps coughed and seized and houseboats shipped water and the bailers worked in relays through the dark.

At some hour past midnight, with the lightning turning the river white every few seconds, Sef appeared beside him at the throttle, soaked through, shouting over the wind.

"You want to know why I offered you the bargain?" she yelled.

"Because you needed a mechanic!"

"Because I needed to know!" Her hand closed hard on his shoulder. "A man running from something will fix an engine. A man running *toward* something will fix it in a flood, at midnight, with children below decks. I needed to know which one you were!"

She squeezed once and went forward to check lines, and Wendell stood alone at the throttle with the rain hammering him flat and the engine humming under his hands like something alive, and he realized he was laughing—or crying, it was impossible to tell the difference in the water streaming down his face.

He was which one he was. That was the whole answer. Three years of rules and two-night limits and ghosthood, and the entire question had resolved itself in a single night of holding a throttle steady while a village trusted him with its dry beds and its children.

* * *

The storm's eye passed near dawn, and in the strange green calm they counted their losses: two awnings gone, one skiff stove, a season's nets shredded. No boats lost. No people lost. On any other stretch of this river, in any other year, that would have been a miracle. Here it was arithmetic—lashed boats, a working pump, a community that moved as one body.

Mara found him sitting on the barge rail afterward, wrung out and hollowed and strangely light.

"Sef says the back side comes tonight," she said. "Worse, maybe. Then two days of rain behind it."

"So we're staying."

"We're staying." She sat beside him. Below them, Teo was showing smaller children how the pump worked, gesturing importantly at the pipe, clearly taking credit for all of it. "You know what happens next, right? Word travels. Forty boats, and every one of them saw the engineer stand the pump through the surge. By next week every landing from here to the delta will know Wendell Underhill is on this river, fixing things, staying put."

"I know."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

He watched the river, high and brown and furious, carrying the wreckage of the world upstream of them. Once, that river had been his road out. Now it was the thing he was holding back, alongside forty families, and he could not remember why the road out had ever mattered more.

"It should," he said. "Corvane will hear. The lawyer will hear. Every day we stay is a day they get a better fix on us." He turned to look at her—really look, the way he'd been afraid to for weeks. "But I did the math again last night, the real math, and here's what it says. Running has kept me alive, and it has kept me *alone*, and those are the same thing only if you think being alive is the point. I'm starting to think it isn't."

Mara said nothing for a long moment. The river breathed beneath them.

"Auntie Sef wants to see us," she said finally. "Both of us. In her cabin. She says—" Mara hesitated, and Wendell heard something in her voice he hadn't heard before, something tight and careful. "She says there's a man coming upriver tomorrow. From the provincial capital. And she thinks we should hear what he's carrying before anyone else does."

Wendell felt the old cold move through his chest, the three-year cold, the checkpoint cold. But it passed through him and kept going, because for the first time it was passing through a man who was standing still.

"What kind of man?"

"The kind," Mara said, "who carries papers with a land syndicate's seal on them. Sef's nephew saw the manifest at the toll dock." She met his eyes. "Wendell. It's addressed to you."
