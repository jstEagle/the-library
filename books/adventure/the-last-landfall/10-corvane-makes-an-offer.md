## 10. Corvane Makes an Offer

Mara saw the fisherman first.

His name was Dossel, a broad-shouldered man with salt-cracked hands who had been mending nets on the neighboring pontoon for two days, and he had never once looked at his nets while mending them. He looked at the water. At the reed line. At the pump barge where Wendell worked with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, grease to the wrists, swearing softly at a coupling that had rusted into its own shape. And twice—only twice—he had looked at her, and both times his gaze had slid away too quickly, like a man stepping off ice that had begun to sing.

She said nothing for those two days. She had learned in the survey camps that suspicion was cheap and certainty expensive; you spent it only when you could afford what it bought. But on the third morning, when Auntie Sef's granddaughter paddled past their skiff and called out that a stranger's launch had anchored below the bend—a gray launch, clean-hulled, with an outboard worth more than every boat in the flotilla combined—Mara stopped pretending to repair the fish trap and went to find Wendell.

He was lying half inside the pump housing, boots sticking out into the gray light, and when she crouched beside him she could hear him talking to the machine. He did that. Low, patient, coaxing, as though the pump were a frightened animal rather than a collection of dead metal. She had watched him do it to engines, to locks, once to a swollen door that would not open, and there was something in it that unsettled her more than any of his silences: Wendell Underhill believed things could be persuaded back to life. It was the most dangerous kind of hope, and he carried it like contraband.

"Wendell."

"Almost got her." A clank. "Third impeller's shot. I can sleeve it if Sef has any brass sheeting—"

"Dossel's been watching you for two days. And there's a launch below the bend that doesn't belong to anyone who fishes."

The clanking stopped. For a long moment there was only the drip of water off the housing and the distant complaint of gulls. Then Wendell backed out of the pump, sat up, and looked at her, and she watched the engineer go out of his face and something older and warier come in behind it—the face of the man who had lived three years on closed lines, reading the country for signs of pursuit the way other men read weather.

"How long below the bend?"

"Since before dawn, by the girl's telling."

He wiped his hands slowly on a rag, thinking. "Gray?"

"The launch is white. But the man aboard—" She hesitated, because she had gone herself, alone, before waking him, paddling a borrowed dugout through the mist to within thirty meters, close enough to see. "He's wearing a suit. A real one. Pressed. There's a document case on his knees and he's drinking coffee from a thermos like a man waiting for a train."

Wendell stood. His joints cracked, forty years of them, and he looked downriver toward the bend where the mist was lifting off the water in slow rags.

"A lawyer," he said quietly.

"You don't know that."

"I know that no one else dresses like that to sit on a river at dawn." He handed her the rag. "Get the notebook. Not on your body—wrap it in oilcloth and put it in the bilge under the floorboards. If someone boards us, I want it somewhere a pat-down won't find."

"And if they search properly?"

"Then we're having a very bad morning." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Wake me if it gets worse than a lawyer."

* * *

The lawyer came at midmorning, poling a skiff borrowed from Dossel—Dossel himself rowing, not meeting anyone's eyes, which told Mara everything about how the arrangement had been made and what it had cost. The flotilla had gathered to watch, the way crowds gather at fires: Auntie Sef on her barge with her arms folded, children hushed onto laps, the whole river community gone quiet in that particular way quiet fishing villages go quiet, which is to say loudly, with every eye doing the talking.

The man stepped from Dossel's skiff onto the pump barge's deck with the care of someone who had been told exactly how much these boats were worth and exactly how little. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face built for sympathy the way some faces are built for cruelty—soft around the mouth, creased at the eyes, arranged to suggest that whatever he was about to say pained him deeply. His shoes were wrong for a boat. That detail pleased Mara obscurely.

"Mr. Underhill." He extended a hand. Wendell did not take it. The lawyer converted the gesture smoothly into adjusting his cuff. "My name is Aldous Renner. I represent certain interests in the provincial capital. May I say first that this is not—whatever you may have been led to expect—not an enforcement matter. No one is here to detain or threaten anyone. I'm here because my client believes mistakes were made. Old ones. Expensive ones. And that you, Mr. Underhill, are owed an apology measured in figures."

Behind him, across the water, Auntie Sef shifted her weight, and thirty people leaned forward without moving at all.

"Mistakes," Wendell said.

Renner opened the document case. "In 1189, the Provincial Rail Authority condemned four hundred hectares along the Vell corridor—your corridor, Mr. Underhill. Land you surveyed. Land your surveys declared unstable, unsuitable for development, worthless except as right-of-way." He drew out a folder, heavy paper, official seals. "Two months after your—disappearance, shall we call it—an amended survey surfaced. New findings. Stable ground. Prime value. The condemnation was reversed, the land released, and acquired by parties who had, by remarkable fortune, positioned themselves to buy."

The river seemed very loud all at once. Mara kept her eyes on Wendell and saw nothing move in his face, which was itself a kind of movement—she had learned to read him now, three weeks of leaking boats and shared watches, and his stillness was the loudest thing about him.

"Corvane," Wendell said.

"My client prefers not to be named in informal settings." Renner smiled gently. "But yes."

"You're saying they forged my survey."

"I'm saying nothing so crude. I'm saying that documents are complicated things, Mr. Underhill, and that engineers' signatures travel in ways engineers rarely anticipate." He let that sit. Then, softly, with the tenderness of a man delivering a diagnosis: "You were convicted of falsifying inspection records on the Harrow Bridge contract. You fled before sentencing. Your name has been mud in three provinces for six years. And the entire time, the land your ruin cleared was being subdivided and sold at eleven times its assessed value." He closed the folder partway. "My client is prepared to acknowledge that the sequence of events was... regrettable."

Mara heard the word *regrettable* land on the barge planks like something dropped from a height. Around them the flotilla had gone absolutely still. Even the children understood, in the way children understand tone before content, that something was being done to this man in front of them, and that it was being done politely.

"What do you want?" Wendell asked.

"Cooperation. Nothing dramatic." Renner produced a second folder, thinner, and laid it on the pump housing between them. "A property deed, registered in your true name—clean papers, Mr. Underhill, no warrants, no record, a man who can walk into any town in the province and be nobody's fugitive. A letter of credit against Corvane accounts. Fifty thousand now, fifty on completion. And in exchange, you surrender a certain field notebook recovered from a government survey camp upriver, along with any copies, tracings, or memoranda derived from it, and you cease to concern yourself with matters that were never yours."

There it was. Mara felt her heart begin to beat in her throat. They knew about the notebook. Of course they knew—they had burned the camp, they had swept the valley, they had chased them down a collapsed viaduct with pistols—but hearing it priced, hearing it set against deeds and letters of credit like a used engine, made it suddenly, horribly real. Her team's last work. Ilse's hydrographic readings. Tomaz's careful, beautiful contour lines, drawn in rain with numb fingers because the schedule wouldn't wait. All of it itemized in a lawyer's thin folder.

"And the survey team," she said.

Renner turned to her for the first time, and his sympathetic face adjusted, recalibrated, became something cooler. "I'm sorry. And you are?"

"The cartographer whose team you're asking him to bury."

"Ms. Voss." A small bow of the head. "I was given to understand you might be present. My client's position on the survey team is unchanged: it is a tragedy, and my client had no involvement in any tragedy. People vanish on rivers. Rivers are dangerous."

"Your client bought the water rights those people died mapping."

"My client acquired assets through lawful channels." He said it the way a man sets down a heavy object—carefully, once, and then steps back from it. "I'd caution everyone present against speculation. Speculation has consequences. So does rumor." His gaze moved, briefly, over the watching boats—over Auntie Sef, over the children—and Mara saw the threat arrive without ever being spoken, saw it settle over the flotilla like a change in the wind. "Now. Mr. Underhill. You've heard the terms. I'll leave the folder. You have until tomorrow's dawn tide. Dossel knows where to find me."

* * *

He left the way he had come, and the flotilla exhaled all at once, a hundred voices resuming as though a held breath had been communal. But Mara barely heard them. She was watching Wendell, who had not touched the folder, who stood looking down at it the way a man looks at a snake he suspects is dead but isn't sure.

"You knew," she said, when the gray launch's sound had faded below the bend. "Some of it. You suspected."

"I knew the timing was wrong." He sat down heavily on the pump housing's edge, and for the first time since she'd known him he looked old—not tired, old, worn down to some earlier version of himself. "Six years ago I couldn't make it fit. My surveys were clean. I checked them until my eyes bled, and they were clean, and the amended version that came out after I ran had my signature on it and numbers I never calculated. I thought—I thought I was going mad. Or that someone had copied my files, and I'd never know how, and never prove it." He laughed, short and terrible. "I ran because running was the only thing I could prove. Every mile of track I stole down those closed lines was evidence I couldn't be in two places. That's all my innocence ever amounted to. An alibi made of distance."

"Wendell—"

"They took my name, Mara." He wasn't looking at her; he was looking at his own hands, the engineer's hands, scarred and grease-darkened. "Not my freedom. My freedom was cheap, I never valued it much. My *name*. Six years I've been the engineer who falsified bridge inspections—do you know what that means, on the rails? Men ride bridges. Families ride bridges. Every conductor who ever spat at my name was spitting at a man who let children drown, in their minds. And the whole time—" His voice caught, and he mastered it, and that mastery was worse than breaking. "The whole time there was an office in the provincial capital where men did arithmetic on what my disgrace was worth per hectare."

Around them the flotilla had drifted closer without appearing to move—boats easing in, Auntie Sef's barge anchoring itself twenty meters off with the deliberate slowness of a woman positioning herself within earshot. Mara pretended not to notice. Wendell probably noticed and pretended harder.

"So take the deal," she said.

His head came up sharply.

"Fifty thousand. Clean papers. A name you can walk around in." She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice of a cartographer reading coordinates. "I mean it. Look at what we're carrying. A soaked notebook with bleeding ink, my memory, and a plan to break into a sealed archive through a flooded tunnel. Against that: money, papers, and a lawyer who tells you the truth to your face because he thinks truth is a weapon he owns." She crossed her arms. "I've watched you for three weeks, Underhill. You sleep like a man listening for dogs. You eat like food might be taken away. You fix everything on this river like fixing it is the only prayer you know. And I am telling you that a deed with your true name on it might be the first thing in six years that lets you stop."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a child laughed, far off, and was hushed.

"That's why you're really saying it," Wendell said slowly. "You want to see if I'll take it."

"I want to know what I'm trusting," Mara said. "Yes."

He looked at the folder for a long time. Then he picked it up—and her stomach dropped, some traitorous part of her already grieving—and carried it to the barge rail, and held it out over the water, and let it go. The pages fanned open as they fell, seals and figures flashing white, and then the river took them, and the current began sorting them downstream toward the sea.

"Clean papers," he said quietly. "You know what's funny? I had clean papers once. And a name. And none of it was mine—it was theirs, loaned to me on condition I never look too hard at anything." He turned back to her, and his eyes were wet and furious and more alive than she had ever seen them. "They wrote my ruin, Mara. Every line of it. Which means it's a document. And documents—" the ghost of the engineer's smile, the one that talked machines back to life— "documents can be corrected. Amended. Signed by the proper authority."

"Which is?"

"Me. Testifying. In the light, on the record, where they can't burn it." He looked out at the flotilla, at Auntie Sef's barge, at the children who had witnessed the whole thing from their mothers' laps. "But that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight we have another one."

"What's that?"

He nodded downriver, toward the bend, where the gray launch still rode at anchor in the failing light—and where, Mara saw now, a second boat had joined it, low and black and riding deep, the kind of boat that carries more men than luggage.

"He came alone," Wendell said. "Lawyers always scout alone first. Whatever comes next comes for the notebook—and he already knows we didn't hand it over." He was already moving, pulling the tarp, checking the skiff's lines with quick sure hands. "Wake Auntie Sef. Tell her to get the children onto the far boats. And Mara—" he glanced back at her, and the fury in him had cooled into something harder and colder and far more useful— "get the notebook out of the bilge. Tonight we stop hiding it. Tonight it sleeps in my coat, next to my heart, where they'll have to go through me."

Out past the bend, the black boat's anchor lamp came alight, a single red eye in the dusk, and the river went dark around it.
