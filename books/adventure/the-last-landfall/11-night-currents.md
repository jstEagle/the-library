## 11. Night Currents

The lawyer came at dusk, when the river turned the color of pewter and the mosquitoes rose off the water in columns you could almost mistake for smoke.

Wendell saw the boat first—a long black skiff with an outboard muffled by a wooden shroud, sliding up the channel toward Auntie Sef's flotilla with the unnatural smoothness of something that had paid for its passage. The fisherman who'd guided it in, a gap-toothed man named Orlin whose nets Wendell had helped mend two nights before, wouldn't meet his eyes. That was the tell. Orlin had looked him in the face every other day of his life.

"You're Underhill." The man in the skiff was fiftyish, soft in the middle, dressed in a linen suit that had no business within a hundred miles of this water. He had a briefcase and a smile that arrived a half-second after everything else he said, like thunder after lightning. "My name is Pellam. I represent certain interests who've been trying to reach you."

"Certain interests," Wendell repeated. He stood on the deck of the pump barge with his arms crossed, and behind him, in the shadow of the wheelhouse, he could feel Mara go very still. "That's a polite way to say Corvane."

Pellam's smile widened by a degree. "Mr. Underhill, I'm going to make you an offer, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide what kind of man you want to be tonight."

* * *

They talked on the barge's aft deck while the flotilla's lanterns came alight around them one by one, little halos of oil flame reflected in black water. Auntie Sef had withdrawn to her own boat with the children, but Wendell knew she was watching. He knew everyone was watching. On the river, privacy was a thing you borrowed, never owned.

"Three years ago," Pellam began, settling onto a crate as though he owned it, "you were the chief survey engineer on the Kessel–Harrow Bend corridor. You signed the land assessments. You certified the grades. And then, quite suddenly, you were the man who falsified them—grades too steep, water tables misread, bridge footings placed on ground your own figures showed would liquefy. The commission stripped your license. The creditors took everything else. There were warrants." He paused delicately. "There still are."

"I know what happened three years ago," Wendell said. His voice came out flat, which cost him something. "I've spent every day since inside it."

"Yes. Well." Pellam opened the briefcase. Inside, Wendell saw paper—clean, cream-colored, official-looking—and a leather folder thick as a Bible. "Here is what my clients propose. You give us the notebook—the Voss woman's field book, the original survey data her team collected on the upper watershed. In exchange: the warrants quashed. Your license reinstated. A settlement figure that will retire every debt you carry, with enough left over to start again somewhere the name Underhill means nothing. Clean papers under a new name, if you prefer. Some men do."

"And if I don't?"

Pellam closed the briefcase gently, like a man closing a coffin lid. "Then you stay what you are. A fugitive on a leaking boat, waiting for the flood or the law, whichever arrives first. My clients are patient men, Mr. Underhill, but they are not infinitely patient, and the monsoon is not patient at all."

Wendell looked out at the water. The lanterns doubled themselves in the current, trembling. Somewhere across the flotilla a child laughed, high and brief, and was hushed.

He thought about the offer. God help him, he thought about it seriously—not for a moment, but for whole minutes, standing there in the failing light while Pellam watched him with the pleasant patience of a fisherman watching a float.

Because here was the truth he'd told no one, not even Mara, not entirely: three years of running had worn him down to something thin. Every checkpoint, every two-night limit, every town where he kept his eyes down and his hands busy so no one would look at his face—it had sanded him. He was tired in a way sleep didn't touch. And the offer wasn't just money. It was permission. It was a door back into the world, into rooms with lamps and chairs, into being a man whose name opened things instead of ending conversations.

One notebook. One woman he'd known eleven days. Against three years of exile.

"What happens to her?" he heard himself ask. "The cartographer. If I hand it over."

"She goes home," Pellam said smoothly. "She's a witness to a tragedy, nothing more. My clients have no interest in her."

Every word was reasonable. Every word fit together. And yet something in Wendell's gut—the same instinct that had once let him read a rail bed's danger from the smell of the clay—registered the sentence the way it registered a bridge humming at the wrong frequency. *My clients have no interest in her.* Men like Pellam didn't waste offers on people they had no interest in. They didn't send a linen suit sixty miles upriver in a muffled skiff for a woman who didn't matter.

"I'll need the night," Wendell said. "To think."

"Of course." Pellam rose, brushed off his trousers, and smiled his half-second-late smile. "Dawn tomorrow. Orlin knows where to find me. Mr. Underhill—one more thing, offered freely." He leaned in slightly, voice dropping to a confessional register. "Whatever the Voss woman has told you about my clients, consider its source. She's a grieving woman who lost her colleagues and needs someone to blame. Grief makes poor cartography."

He stepped down into the black skiff and was gone into the dusk, the muffled engine a low pulse fading downstream.

Mara came out of the wheelhouse shadow. She'd heard all of it; he'd known she would.

"Well?" she said.

"He's lying," Wendell said. "I just haven't figured out about what yet."

* * *

They sat in the pump barge's engine room that night, because it was the only place on the flotilla with a door that locked, and talked with their heads close over a single lamp.

"He knew too much," Mara said. She had the notebook wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, holding it the way she held everything she cared about—too tightly, knuckles pale. "Orlin's a gossip, but he doesn't know about the notebook. Nobody on this flotilla knows exactly what we're carrying. So how does Pellam?"

"Bribed him anyway. Followed us from the quarry cave. Doesn't matter how—he has it right, which means someone's been tracking us since Harrow Bend." Wendell rubbed his eyes. "What matters is the rest. He wants the original. Not copies. Not testimony. The physical book."

"Because the book is the evidence. Water rights purchased through shell companies, dates, parcel numbers, the levee easements upstream—" Mara's voice caught and steadied. "It's the only copy. If it goes in the river, it never existed."

"That's what I keep coming back to." Wendell got up and paced the narrow space between the boiler and the bulkhead, three steps each way, the floor plates cold under his boots. "Why does a syndicate that owns judges and sheriffs send a lawyer instead of the men in gray trucks? Why offer money to a man they already ruined once? They framed me before. They could frame me again, cheaper."

"Unless they're afraid of you now. Of what you know."

"No." He stopped pacing. Something had been assembling itself in the back of his mind since dusk, piece by piece, the way a route assembles itself on a map—and now it clicked home, ugly and complete. "Unless the offer isn't the point. Think about it, Mara. What did he actually buy tonight?"

She frowned. "Time. A meeting."

"He bought our position. He bought Orlin pointing at this barge. He bought me standing on an open deck at dusk, in front of witnesses, agreeing to a deal—so that anyone watching sees the fugitive Underhill negotiating with Corvane's counsel. And he bought the location of the notebook, sitting in a rotting pump barge, on a boat with no keel worth the name, in the worst flood season in twenty years." He heard his own voice drop. "They're not buying the notebook, Mara. They're sinking it. With us aboard."

The lamp flame shivered in the draft from the engine room vents. Outside, rain had begun again, soft on the deck above, a sound like fingers drumming.

"A boating accident," Mara said slowly. "In monsoon season. On a river full of wrecked boats."

"Fugitive engineer takes Corvane's money, gets drunk on his good fortune, drowns in the night current along with the woman who's been feeding him lies. The notebook goes to the bottom sealed in oilcloth—waterproof, they'll assume, until it rots. No warrants to quash. No witnesses to pay. No trial where a lawyer might unravel." Wendell sat down heavily on the toolbox. "And the beautiful part, from their side—they don't even have to do it themselves. They just have to loosen something. Cut a seam. Wait for the current. This river kills people every week this time of year. Who investigates a drowned fugitive?"

Mara was quiet a long moment. Then: "You're sure."

"I'm sure of the shape of it. I've been reading structures for twenty years—rails, bridges, hulls. Tonight I read a man, and the structure's wrong. The offer was too generous and the exit was too easy and he told me exactly where he'd be at dawn, which no honest negotiator does, because honest negotiators expect to come back." He looked at her. "We leave tonight. Now. Before the river does their work for them."

But even as he said it, part of him stayed fixed on the other thing—the small, shameful thing. For those minutes on the aft deck, he had wanted to say yes. He had weighed her life against his tiredness and found the scale moving. He would carry that weighing for the rest of his life, whatever length that turned out to be.

* * *

They slipped away from the flotilla at two in the morning, in the skiff Auntie Sef lent them with a look that asked no questions and promised no forgiveness if they lied to her later. Rain fell in a fine steady veil. The current ran hard, brown and muscular, carrying debris—branches, a drowned crate of oranges, half a fence post—and Wendell worked the sweep oar while Mara ran the lamp hooded, calling the channel markers she remembered from her own surveys.

They made perhaps three miles before Wendell's neck prickled.

He couldn't have said what it was—a rhythm in the rain, a wrongness in the dark downstream. Then he saw it: a low shape holding station against the current where nothing should hold, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the pulse of a muffled outboard.

"They're not waiting for dawn," he breathed. "They're herding us. Keep us off the flotilla, keep us in the open water, and let the river—or them—finish it."

The second shape appeared off the port bow, darker than the dark, and this one had no engine sound at all. It came on the current silently, riding it the way the river people rode it, which meant someone aboard knew these waters—which meant Orlin, or someone like him, at a tiller.

"Mara. Down."

The first shot cracked across the water like a whip, and the lamp exploded in Mara's hands, glass raining into the bilge. Then everything was noise and motion—Wendell hauling the sweep, throwing the skiff's bow toward the near shore where the flooded timberline stood hip-deep in black water, a second shot sparking off the gunwale a hand's width from his knuckles.

He should have been terrified. He was, partly. But underneath the fear, the old competence woke—the thing that had made him an engineer, that read chaos and found load paths in it. The current set eastward here, hard, toward a place he'd noted that afternoon without knowing why: the drowned remains of the old ferry landing, its pilings and collapsed deck just under the surface, a lattice of rotten timber the color of the night itself.

"Hold on," he said, and put the skiff straight at it.

The gray boat followed. Of course it followed—it was faster, its pilot confident, chasing a fleeing target into what looked like open flooded timber. Wendell counted in his head, feeling the current's push against the sweep, hearing the water change its voice over the submerged deck ahead—a different sound, hollow, structured.

"Flat!" he shouted, and threw himself and Mara down between the thwarts as the skiff's hull kissed the top of the sunken deck and slid—slid, on two inches of clearance, wood shrieking along wood—and cleared the far edge into deeper water beyond.

The gray boat, running three knots faster, hit the ferry landing at speed.

The sound was tremendous—a crack of timber, a scream of tearing planking, the outboard rising to a whine and dying. Figures spilled into the black water. One of them surfaced coughing, clawing at a floating plank; another clung to the broken piling. The third—the big silent one from the second boat—was simply gone under, and the current took the wreckage east in long slow pieces.

Wendell shipped the oar and let the river carry them, heart slamming, hands shaking now that it was over and allowed to shake.

"We could go back," Mara said quietly, from the bilge where she crouched among the lamp glass. "For the ones in the water."

"If we go back, we die, and the notebook dies, and every family on that river keeps getting flooded on purpose so a syndicate can drain its fields." He found her hand in the dark and gripped it, hard, needing to hold something true. "I'm not sorry they're in the river, Mara. They put us in it first. But I'm done running blind. From tonight we run toward something—toward Harrow Bend, toward the archive, toward making every one of them answer for it in a room with lights."

Ahead, far downstream, the rain was thinning, and beneath its edge the sky held the first gray suggestion of morning. Somewhere behind them, a man clung to a piling and screamed curses at the current until the current carried the sound away.

Wendell bent to the sweep oar and pulled for the light.
