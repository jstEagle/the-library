## 12. Monsoon Rising

Mara felt the storm before she heard it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron in the rain-wet air, the river going strangely still beneath the flotilla's hulls. Three years of surveying water had taught her the language of rivers, and the Kessel was speaking in a voice she had never wanted to hear: the low, gathering silence of something enormous deciding to move.

"It's early," she said, standing at the rail of Auntie Sef's houseboat. The horizon to the southwest had gone the color of a bruise, a wall of cloud stacked so high it seemed to lean over the valley. "The monsoon isn't due for another week. The elders said—"

"The elders said a lot of things this year." Auntie Sef stood beside her, hands folded into her sleeves, watching the sky with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she did not like. "The levees upstream changed everything. Corvane's concrete pushed the water where it wanted and starved the channels where it didn't. The river doesn't know its own schedule anymore." She turned and raised her voice, and the whole flotilla seemed to pivot toward the sound. "Batten everything! Double the moorings! Children below decks—move!"

The floating village came alive. It was a strange, beautiful thing to watch, Mara thought—thirty boats lashed in loose clusters along the slack water below the pump barge, and in the space of ten minutes they transformed from a sleepy scatter of homes into a single organism bracing itself. Nets hauled in. Livestock pens lashed down. Grandmothers moving with the unhurried certainty of women who had survived forty storms, and young mothers moving with the frantic precision of women who had survived only a few.

Wendell was already on the pump barge.

He had spent nine days on that rusting hulk, and Mara had watched the work change him the way water changes stone—not dramatically, not all at once, but in a deepening. When he'd first climbed aboard, he'd moved like a man crossing ice, testing every plank, half-expecting the whole enterprise to be a trap. Now he moved like the barge was his. He knew which valve stuck in damp weather. He knew that the number three cylinder had a hairline crack he'd sleeved with a section of boiler pipe salvaged from the old rail bridge. He knew the sound the engine made when it was happy and the sound it made when it was lying.

Mara climbed the swaying gangplank after him and found him elbow-deep in the pump housing, a wrench in one hand and a lantern hooked above him against the gathering gloom.

"Tell me it's ready," she said.

"Tell me the storm will wait nine more hours and I'll say yes." He didn't look up. His hands kept moving, sure and quick, seating a gasket with his thumb. "The impeller shaft is true. The intake screens are clear. The engine will turn over." He paused. "Whether it keeps turning over in a flood is a different question. This machine was junk when I started. It's a miracle now, but miracles and monsoons don't have a good marriage rate."

"Then we tie it down and hope."

"Hope's not an engineering discipline." But he said it without bitterness, almost gently, and when he finally looked up at her there was something in his face she was still learning to read—something that might have been fear, or might have been the strange peace of a man who had finally stopped running long enough to have something to lose.

The first rain hit at dusk.

* * *

It did not fall so much as arrive—a solid gray curtain sweeping up the valley, and behind it a wind that bent the reeds flat and set the whole flotilla groaning. Mara worked the mooring lines with Auntie Sef's nephew Tam, a boy of fourteen with river mud permanently in the creases of his knuckles, and between gusts they hauled and cleated and hauled again while the rain tried to pry their fingers off the ropes.

By full dark, the river had risen two feet. By midnight, four.

Mara had seen floods before—had mapped floodplains for a living, had drawn the blue wash of the hundred-year mark across a dozen valleys—but mapping a flood and being inside one were different countries. The Kessel had stopped being a river and become a moving landmass, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees in its grip like a man carrying kindling. The flotilla's boats rose and fell and strained against their lines, and the sound of the wind was not a sound at all but a presence, a vast animal breathing over the water.

And through it, over it, she could hear the pump barge's engine, running steady as a heartbeat, holding the water out of the low fields where half the flotilla's boats had once been moored before the rise drove them together.

"He's good," Tam shouted to her, meaning Wendell, and there was no irony in it. The boy had spent a week calling him *the engineer* in a flat, suspicious tone, and somewhere in the last three days the suspicion had drained away. "Auntie says the old pump would've died in the first hour. He fixed things she didn't know could be fixed."

Mara looked at the dark shape of the barge, lantern-light moving behind its wheelhouse windows, and felt something complicated and inconvenient settle in her chest. She had recruited Wendell Underhill as a useful stranger, a man with a map and a grudge and nothing to anchor him. Somewhere between the flooded quarry and the leaking skiff and nine days of engine grease, *useful stranger* had become something else, and she had not decided whether that was a comfort or a danger. People you cared about could be taken. She had watched it happen. She had hidden in cold reed water for six hours while it happened, watching men in gray trucks carry her colleagues' instruments away and leave nothing else behind.

The storm peaked near three in the morning, and it was then that the mooring line snapped.

Mara heard it rather than saw it—a crack like a rifle shot under the wind's howl, and then Tam screaming, pointing, and the pump barge already swinging, already moving, its stern line parted and its bow line sawing against the cleat in great smoking arcs as the flood took hold of nine tons of steel and engine.

"The children," Auntie Sef was shouting from the deck of her houseboat, and it took Mara a horrible second to understand. "The children are aboard—Wendell asked for them to help feed the boiler—the Vey twins and little Osa—"

Three children. On the barge. With Wendell.

The bow line parted.

* * *

The barge went downstream like a runaway train, and the flotilla went mad.

Mara was moving before she had decided to move—over the rail, into Sef's longboat, Tam leaping in behind her with a boathook and no permission asked. Auntie Sef's voice followed them across the water, cutting through the storm with the authority of forty years: "The oxbow! The old channel by the willow stand—the water slows there—get ahead of them!"

The longboat's engine caught on the second pull. Mara took the tiller herself because Tam's hands were shaking, and drove them out into the black chaos of the flooded river with rain like gravel against her face.

The barge was a dim shape ahead, lanterns still burning, swinging wide across the current. She could see figures at the rail—one tall, one small, one small, one small. Wendell had them by the collars, or the hands, holding all three against the rail like a man holding a door shut against a mob.

"He sees us," Tam yelled. "He's signaling—"

The barge's whistle shrieked, three short blasts, and Mara's blood went cold, because she knew river signals now, Auntie Sef had drilled them into her in the first week: three short meant *distress, coming alongside, prepare to catch lines.*

"He's going to try to throw them a line," she shouted back. "Get the bow lantern. Get everything that lights!"

They ran the oxbow channel with the willows lashing the boat's sides, and Mara did the mathematics she hated—current speed, drift angle, the barge's momentum, the narrow window where the old channel's slack water would let them pull even. It came down to perhaps forty seconds and a hundred meters of brown flood, and if they missed, the barge would go into the lower rapids with three children and the only man who had ever made her believe the world could be put back together.

"Tam. When we come alongside, you catch the children. I'll catch him."

"Why you and not—"

"Because I'm heavier than you. Row."

The oxbow bent. The current slackened. And the barge came around the bend above them like a dark island in motion, riding lower now, shipping water over its gunwales, its engine screaming at a pitch Mara had never heard from it—Wendell running it past every sane limit, she realized, using the pump's own thrust to fight the current, buying them seconds.

They drew alongside in a chaos of water and shouting. The hulls slammed together, parted, slammed again. Mara got her arms over the barge's rail and held on while the two vessels tried to tear each other apart, and through the rain she saw Wendell's face, lit wild by the lantern, and she saw that he had already tied the children together—waist to waist with a length of rope, the Vey boys and little Osa linked like a string of fish, so that no single wave could take just one of them.

"Take them!" he roared. "In order! Osa first—she's the lightest—"

"Come with us!"

"The barge—someone has to steer it out of the rapids or it takes the willow stand down and blocks the channel—every boat downstream—" His hands were already lifting the girl over the rail, into Mara's arms, and there was no time to argue because he was right, and they both knew it, and Mara hated him for it with a purity she had not felt since the reeds.

Osa came first, screaming, soaked, alive. Then the twins, hauled across the grinding gap by Tam's hooked pole and Mara's grabbing hands. And then the longboat's stern swung out and the gap widened and Mara looked up into Wendell's face across three feet of roaring black water.

"Steer for the willow stand and drop anchor in the slack!" she screamed. "The channel bends left—you have to—"

"I know!" And he was smiling, actually smiling, rain and river water streaming off his jaw. "Mara! The notebook—keep it dry!"

Then the flood took the barge and he was gone into the dark, and Mara stood in the longboat's stern with a child in each arm and one clinging to her back, and she did the only thing there was to do.

She turned downstream and followed.

* * *

What happened at the willow stand, the river people would tell for years, and each telling would grow it, but the bones of it were true and Mara carried them in her body like swallowed stones.

The barge came into the slack water above the willow stand moving too fast, and Wendell could not slow it, so he did the thing that only a railroad engineer would think to do: he ran the pump full against the bank, driving a wall of discharge water at the mud until the barge's own thrust slewed it sideways, and he dropped both anchors at once, and the anchors held, and the hulk swung its stern into the current like a animal turning to face a fight and ground to a stop with its bow in the willows and forty meters of churning slack water between it and the rapids below.

Then he stood on the deck of a stranded barge in the worst storm in a decade, alone, and waved his lantern at them—three long flashes, the signal for *all secure*—as if he were a stationmaster reporting an on-time arrival.

They got a line to him within the hour. It took four boats, two anchors, and Auntie Sef standing in the bow of her own houseboat in the teeth of the gale directing the operation with a boat hook like a field marshal's baton. By the time they hauled him aboard the houseboat, gray-lipped and shaking, the storm had begun its long retreat, the wind dropping note by note like a rung-down curtain.

Mara found him wrapped in blankets by the stove, the three children asleep against his sides like puppies, having refused absolutely to be anywhere else.

"You steered a nine-ton barge with its own pump," she said.

"It was that or the rapids." His voice was a ruin. "I'd like it noted that I prefer engines that steer with wheels."

"I'd like it noted that you sent the children away and stayed with the boat."

He was quiet a moment. The stove ticked. Rain still fell, but gently now, apologetically.

"The boat had to be saved," he said finally. "It's the only thing in three years I've built instead of escaped."

Mara sat down beside him, carefully, so as not to wake the children, and found that she had no answer, and that no answer was needed.

* * *

Morning came rinsed and pale, the storm gone east, the river high and brown but slowing, already beginning to think about its banks. The flotilla reassembled in the slack water, counting itself like a shepherd counting sheep, and found that it had lost only one boat—a scow, empty, no one aboard—and gained something no one had expected.

They came to the houseboat all through the morning. Old men who had not spoken to Wendell in nine days of shared river came and shook his hand with grave formality. The Vey twins' mother arrived and said nothing at all, only took his face in both her hands and looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her nod once and release him. Tam followed Wendell around the deck like a duckling, carrying his tools, asking questions about impellers with the shameless hunger of the fourteen-year-old convert.

And Auntie Sef cooked the storm-feast—rice and river fish and the good tea she saved for funerals and miracles, which this morning were the same thing—and when the bowls were passed she stood, and the whole flotilla stood with her.

"Three years ago," she said, "a man testified at the dam hearing. A railroad engineer. He drew the water maps that proved the company had lied, and because of him the river families kept their fishing grounds. And then he vanished, and we thought the river had taken him back." She looked down at Wendell, who had gone very still, bowl in his hands. "The river gave him back to us in a storm, with three of our children in his arms. So." She raised her bowl. "This is not a stranger we are feeding. This is Underhill. Ours."

The flotilla roared. Bowls lifted. And Mara watched Wendell Underhill—a man who had built his whole life on the rule that you never stayed, never belonged, never gave anyone the chance to claim you—sit there in the steam and the noise and the morning light, and she watched the rule break.

He did not say anything. His hands were not quite steady around the bowl. But when Tam leaned against his shoulder, he did not move away.

Later, when the feast was done and the flotilla hummed with repair work, Auntie Sef found Mara at the rail and stood beside her, watching the water.

"He'll want to leave now," the old woman said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Mara said. "He'll think he's endangering us. He'll be halfway to believing it."

"And you?"

Mara watched Wendell across the deck, showing Tam how to true a shaft with nothing but a lantern flame and patience. The notebook was dry in its oilcloth, against her ribs. The archive at Harrow Bend was still a week downstream, and Corvane was still upstream, and somewhere in the drowned brown water between them lay everything that remained of her team, her evidence, her dead.

"I'm going to give him a reason to stay one more day," she said. "And then another. And when we reach Harrow Bend, I'm going to ask him to do the hardest thing he's ever done."

Auntie Sef looked at her sideways, shrewd as a heron. "And what's that?"

"Stop running. On purpose. In front of everyone."

The old woman was quiet for a moment. Downriver, the water gleamed under the washed sky, running clean and high toward the sea.

"Then you'd best start asking gently," Auntie Sef said. "Men like that—men who've been pushed their whole lives—they don't turn well at speed. You have to show them the turn long before you ask for it." She gathered her bowls. "And Mara. When you ask him. Ask him where he was the night the gray trucks came to your camp."

Mara went cold. "He was downstream. He found the camp after."

"I know what he told you." Auntie Sef's voice was mild, terrible. "And I believe him. But there are men in gray trucks asking questions at every landing between here and Harrow Bend, and a bribed fisherman on this river, and a lawyer with clean papers and dirty hands. If he's going to stand in front of everyone, girl, he needs to have already told you everything—so that when Corvane tells his version, no one in that courtroom is hearing it for the first time." She patted Mara's arm once, dry as driftwood. "Storms show you what a boat is made of. So do lawyers. Start asking tonight."

That evening, Mara found Wendell alone at the stern of the houseboat, watching the current, and sat down beside him, and said, "Tell me about the night the survey team disappeared. All of it. From the beginning."

He turned to look at her, and in the last light she saw the fear come into his face—the old fear, the runner's fear—and she took his hand and did not let go, and waited.
