## 13. Afterwater

The rain stopped sometime in the night, and the silence it left behind was worse than the storm.

Wendell stood on the deck of Auntie Sef's lead boat at first light and looked out at a world that no longer had edges. The river had eaten its banks whole. Where the terraced fields of Kessel's outlying farms had sloped down to the water two days ago, there was now only a brown plain studded with the crowns of trees, fence posts standing like drowned men, and here and there the tin roof of a shed, half-submerged, glinting dully in a sky the color of wet slate. A dead cow drifted past with the current, belly up, turning slowly like something on a spit. Nobody said anything about it. There wasn't time.

The flotilla had scattered in the night and come back ragged. Boats he recognized—the blue-hulled skiff of the brothers Odo and Pell, the wide flat barge where the widow Anselm dried her nets—reappeared one by one through the morning mist, some riding low, some listing, all of them carrying people who looked at the water the way survivors of a fire look at a burned house. Children who had been laughing yesterday sat silent in the bilge, wrapped in blankets that steamed faintly in the humid air. The pump barge Wendell had rebuilt was tied amidships now, guarded like a relic, its repaired engine ticking as it cooled.

Mara came to stand beside him. She didn't speak either. She had spent the night hauling children out of the flood on a rope line, and her hands were still trembling, though she held them still against the rail so he wouldn't see—or so she believed he wouldn't see. He saw.

"Count them," Auntie Sef said quietly behind them, and her voice carried across the deck without effort, the way old voices do when they've spent decades shouting over water. "Count every soul. I want names."

They counted. It took most of the morning, because boats kept arriving late, and because counting meant asking, and asking meant learning what people already feared to say aloud.

Old Tam, who mended nets and told fortunes from the patterns of knots, was not among the returned. Neither was his grandson, little Rell, who had been aboard Tam's skiff when the storm hit. The boy's mother came ashore on Odo's boat with her arms crossed tight over her chest, holding herself together by force of will alone, and when Auntie Sef asked her gently if Tam had been wearing his life vest, the woman made a sound Wendell would hear for the rest of his life—a sound with no words in it, just the shape of a door closing forever.

The search began at midday and went until dark. Wendell worked it himself, poling through drowned fields in a shallow punt, following the current lines where flotsam gathered in slow spirals against submerged fences. He knew water. He'd spent three years reading rivers from rail trestles, mapping their floods in his head the way other men memorized timetables. So when he found the snag—a fallen acacia, half-buried in silt, its branches combing the current—he knew before he got close what the pale thing tangled in its lower limbs would be.

He was right, and being right was the cruelest skill he owned.

Tam hung in the branches face-down, his gray hair fanned out in the silted water like weed, his net-mending knife still lashed to his belt exactly as it always was. The boy wasn't with him. Wendell freed the old man with a boat hook and his own two hands, working carefully, respectfully, though respect was a luxury the river had never once extended to anyone. Mara poled the punt alongside him without being asked. She didn't look away, and he understood why: you owed the dead your eyes. Someone had to witness it, or it hadn't happened, or worse—it hadn't mattered.

They laid him on the deck of Sef's lead boat, covered with a sailcloth, and the boy's mother knelt beside him and did not make the sound again. That was somehow worse.

* * *

They found Rell at dusk, four miles downstream, caught in a culvert grate beneath the remains of the county road. Alive. Half-drowned, hypothermic, screaming for a grandfather who would never answer—but alive. When they brought him back upriver by lantern light and put him in his mother's arms, the flotilla made a sound Wendell had never heard a community make: not cheering, nothing so simple. A low collective exhale, grief and relief braided into a single note, like a chord struck on an instrument too large to see.

Auntie Sef stood at the bow and watched the lanterns pass from boat to boat, relaying the news, and Wendell watched her face while she did it. He had learned to read her in the weeks since the pump barge—the way her silence could be warmer than another person's laughter, the way her anger never raised its voice but simply arrived, complete, like weather. What he saw in her face now was not relief. Relief was there, yes, buried deep, for the boy. But above it, older and harder, sat something else. Something that had been gathering all day like pressure behind a dam.

She turned and looked at him, and then at Mara, and said: "Come below. Both of you. Now."

Her cabin was low-beamed and smelled of lamp oil and dried fish, and it was the warmest place Wendell had been in three years. She lit two lamps instead of one, which among river people was a signal of its own—you lit the second lamp when the matter was serious enough to deserve full light. Then she sat down on her sea chest, which creaked under her, and folded her hands, and was quiet for so long that Wendell nearly spoke first.

He was glad he didn't.

"You're engineers," she finally said. "Both of you. Maps and water and machines." Her eyes went to Mara. "Your survey team measured this river. All of it. Upstream past the gorge, past the Corvane holdings, all the way to the high country. Am I wrong?"

"You're not wrong," Mara said carefully.

"And the notebook you carry. The maps in it." Auntie Sef's voice did not change at all, and that was how Wendell knew how much the next question cost her. "Do they show the levees?"

Wendell felt the cabin seem to tilt, though the boat was perfectly still.

"What levees?" he said.

Auntie Sef looked at him for a long moment—not unkindly, but with the weary patience of someone who has just remembered that land people have to be taught everything twice. Then she rose, stiff-kneed, and took a rolled oilcloth chart from a shelf above her bunk, and spread it on the table between the lamps, weighting the corners with a tin cup, a spool of wire, a river stone worn smooth as an egg.

It was a hand-drawn map of the valley, decades old, ink gone brown, annotated in three different hands. And Wendell—who had spent his life reading grades and alignments and drainage the way other men read scripture—saw it immediately. Saw what she needed him to see before she even pointed.

"The old floodplain," he said quietly.

"The old floodplain," Auntie Sef agreed. "This whole valley used to take the monsoon together. The water came down the gorge, spread out, slowed down, soaked in. Our boats rode high for a week, maybe two, and then it drained away and left the fields fat with new silt. That was the bargain. The river takes in the spring, gives back in the summer. My grandmother's grandmother signed that bargain, and every family on this water has kept it." Her finger traced the wide braided channels marked along the banks. "Fifteen years ago, these started filling in."

"Fifteen years," Wendell repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "That's when Corvane started buying upstream."

"That's when their engineers started building." She said *engineers* the way you might say *poachers*. "Levees. Dikes. Channel works on their side of the gorge. Every year, a little more. They wall off their land so the flood can't touch it—and the water has to go somewhere, doesn't it? Water always goes somewhere." Her finger moved downstream, to the flats, to the marks where the river families' moorings clustered like minnows. "It comes here. It comes to us. Faster and higher every season, because they've taken our valley's share and given us theirs."

Mara had gone very still. Wendell recognized the stillness. It was the same look she'd had in the quarry cave, the day she'd opened the notebook and shown him what her team had found—the water rights, the quiet purchases, the syndicate's fingerprints on documents that should have been public record. But this was different. This was not paper. This was a boy pulled from a culvert, and an old man hanging in a tree.

"They knew," Mara said softly. Not a question. She was looking at the map, at the dates scrawled in the margins. "Our team surveyed the gorge reaches last spring. They must have seen the levee works. It would have been in the field notes. It would have been—" She stopped, and Wendell heard her arrive at the thought he was already having.

It would have been evidence.

"That's part of what they erased," Wendell said. "Not just the water rights. The levees. If your team filed those surveys, someone could have forced an inquiry. Flood easements. Compensation. Maybe an injunction." He stared down at the brown ink of the old floodplain, at the patient annotations of three generations of river cartographers, and felt something in his chest turn over with a slow, grinding weight, like machinery engaging after years idle. "They didn't kill your team over a ledger, Mara. They killed them over hydrology."

The words fell into the lamplight and nobody picked them up for a moment. Auntie Sef let them sit there, the way she let everything sit until it was ready.

"I lost a man today," she said at last. Her voice stayed level, but Wendell saw her hands tighten on each other. "Old Tam. Forty years on this water. He pulled three children out of the spring flood of '41 and nobody ever wrote that down anywhere except in knots on his nets. And I'll tell you what I know in my bones, engineer, and you can believe me or measure it yourself." She leaned forward, and the lamplight carved deep shadows under her cheekbones. "Without their walls, that water would have spread thin and slow, and my boats would have ridden it out the way we always have, and Tam would be mending nets tonight and complaining about my cooking. Their levees didn't save them from the flood. Their levees *aimed* it. Like a gun barrel."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the deck; a child laughed, briefly, and was hushed.

"I'm not a violent woman," Auntie Sef continued. "I've buried too many to spend the living cheaply. But I am a patient one, and patience is a kind of weapon, and I am telling you both—" her eyes moved from Wendell to Mara and back again, pinning them like specimens—"that whatever you two are carrying in that notebook, whatever justice you think you're chasing, it stopped being your fight the moment that boy went under the water. It's ours now too. And we are thirty boats, and forty years of charts, and more witnesses than Corvane can buy in a lifetime."

Wendell looked at the map. He thought about the checkpoint at Kessel Junction, the gray trucks, the collapsed viaduct, the lawyer with his clean papers and his soft voice. He had spent three years perfecting the art of being nowhere, of mattering to no one, of keeping his name out of every ledger and his face off every road. He had told himself it was survival. Standing in this lamplit cabin between a grieving woman and a murdered man's chart, he understood for the first time that it had also been surrender—that invisibility was just another way of letting them win, year after year, flood after flood, one Tam at a time.

"We'll need everything," he heard himself say. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "Every chart. Every date a levee went up. Every flood height anyone remembers, matched to the years. Names of workers Corvane hired for the channel works—they talk, eventually, everyone talks. And the notebook." He glanced at Mara. "Her notebook is the spine of it. The surveys prove the levees were deliberate, engineered to divert flow, built on illegally consolidated holdings. Put that beside forty years of river charts and a drowned man, and you don't have a complaint anymore. You have a case."

"A case needs a court," Mara said quietly.

"Then we find a court," Wendell said. "There's the provincial archive at Harrow Bend. Records office. Land registry. If Corvane bought the upstream parcels, the deeds are filed there—if they haven't been burned or bought. And if we can get the notebook's contents on record, officially, dated and stamped, then Corvane can't erase it twice. Paper filed with the crown clerk is paper that exists whether they like it or not."

Auntie Sef studied him across the lamplight, and something in her expression shifted—a settling, like ballast finding its place.

"You've thought about this," she said. "About fighting them properly. In the open."

"No," Wendell admitted. "I've spent three years thinking about how never to be seen by anyone. This is new." He rubbed his jaw, rough with three days of stubble, and almost smiled. "But I was a railroad engineer for eleven years, ma'am. Railroads are just promises written across country in steel—this route, this grade, this bridge will hold. I know how to build a plan that holds weight. I just never had anything worth building one for."

"Now you do," Auntie Sef said simply.

Above them, the river moved on in the dark, carrying its burden of drowned fields and drifting wreckage toward the sea, indifferent as ever to the affairs of people. But down in the cabin, around two lamps, something else was moving—something slower and far more dangerous than any flood. Grief was hardening. Three strangers and forty families were beginning, without ceremony or agreement, to become a single purpose.

Auntie Sef rolled up her grandmother's chart and pressed it into Wendell's hands.

"Take it," she said. "It goes with you to Harrow Bend. And when you stand wherever you're going to stand, in front of whoever you're going to face—you tell them an old woman on the water sent it, and that she keeps count."

* * *

They left at dawn, two days later, when the water dropped enough to run the narrows.

Half the flotilla came out to see them off, engines idling, lanterns still lit against the early fog. Rell's mother stood at the rail of her brother's boat with the boy asleep against her shoulder, and as Wendell's skiff slid past she reached down—not to touch him, she didn't know him well enough for that—but to press her palm flat to the air above his head, an old river blessing he'd seen the fishermen give their boats before a hard passage.

He would carry that gesture longer than he carried the scar from the viaduct.

Mara handled the tiller through the first bend while Wendell checked the bilge and stowed the oilcloth chart in the waterproof satchel beside the notebook, wrapped in waxed linen, triple-tied. Ahead, the river ran broad and swollen between stripped trees, and beyond the haze the hills rose toward Harrow Bend, gray-blue and waiting.

"Do you feel it?" Mara asked, without looking at him. "Like we're being watched."

"I've felt that way for three years," Wendell said. "Difference is, now I hope it's true." He settled back against the gunwale and watched the current carry them forward. "Let them see us coming. For once, I want somebody to send word ahead."

The fog thinned. The river bent north. And somewhere behind them, in the drowned fields, a community was counting its dead and keeping count.
