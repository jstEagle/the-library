## 14. The Archive at Harrow Bend

Mara saw the town before Wendell did—she always saw things first now, her eyes trained by years of reading land the way other people read letters—and what she saw made her put out a hand and press it flat against his chest.

"Down," she said. "Paddle. Don't argue."

He didn't argue. That was one of the quiet revolutions of the last hundred miles: Wendell Underhill had learned to take instruction from a cartographer half his age, and he did it without the wounded pride that would have killed them both weeks ago. He slid low in the skiff, and Mara pulled for the near bank, easing them into the drowned willows where the current ran slow and the branches hung down to the water like curtains drawn against the world.

Harrow Bend sat on the far side of the river, a gray scatter of roofs climbing a bluff above the water. From this distance it looked peaceful enough—smoke rising from chimneys in straight monsoon-sodden columns, gulls wheeling over the market square, the archive itself visible as a long stone building with a copper roof gone green, perched at the top of the town like a hen over its eggs. The provincial archive. The reason they had come two hundred miles through floodwater in a boat that should have sunk three times and only sank twice.

Mara counted the smoke.

That was the thing about being a surveyor: you learned to count. Chimneys, boats, trucks, men. Her team had been seven people, and she had known every one of their habits—the way Ondrej lit his pipe at exactly six each morning, the way little Pem whistled when he staked a line. She had counted seven chimneys' worth of smoke from their camp the morning Corvane's men came up the valley, and she had known something was wrong because there should have been eight. She'd been counting ever since. It kept her alive.

Harrow Bend had too much smoke for its size, and none of it came from kitchens.

"Look at the landing," she murmured. "Tell me what you see."

Wendell rose just high enough to bring his eyes above the gunwale. He was silent a long moment. Rain ticked on the water around them, soft as fingers tapping.

"Two barges moored abreast," he said finally. "Not fishing barges. Flat-decked, no nets, no drying racks. Canvas covers aft—those are crew shelters." His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man measuring a bridge he suspected was rotten. "And there's a chain across the channel. Lighter line below it, probably to snag props or paddles. They're not guarding the town, Mara. They're filtering it."

"Anyone coming off the river gets stopped, searched, logged," Mara said. "Anyone going into the archive gets followed."

"You're sure it's us they want?"

"I'm sure it's the notebook they want, and we're the only two people in this province who know why." She let the skiff drift a hand's width further into the willows. Below them, under a skin of brown water, she could see the ghost of a fence row—a farmer's field, drowned since the levees went in upstream, another small theft written in mud. Everything Corvane touched turned into evidence. That was almost funny, if you were the kind of person who could laugh while hiding in a bush.

Wendell eased back down beside her. In the green half-light under the willow canopy his face looked older than it had on the first day, when she'd watched him pick through her team's abandoned camp with a thief's hands and then, inexplicably, put everything back except a dead man's notebook. Three years of running had carved him hollow, but the flood had been filling him back in, week by week, and she had watched it happen without letting herself believe it. People who belonged nowhere could still choose somewhere. She was beginning to suspect that was the whole secret of him.

"We can't go in by water," he said. "We can't go in by road—they'll have the causeway. We can't wait them out; they've got dry bunks and hot food and we've got a leaking skiff and half a bag of rice."

"No," Mara agreed. "We can't do any of those things."

"So we're done."

She turned and looked at him, really looked, rain dripping off her hood between them. "Do you remember what you said to me in the quarry cave? When I told you the notebook was the only copy?"

A muscle worked in his jaw. "I said copies are how tyrants win. Originals are how they lose."

"And do you remember your map? The old one, the stolen one, the one you've been sleeping on for three years like a pillow full of sins?" She reached into the oilskin bundle wedged under the thwart and drew it out—not the notebook, which rode separately now, wrapped in waxed cloth inside Auntie Sef's lucky fish-gutting tin, but Wendell's map of the closed lines, soft as cloth from handling, covered in his own younger handwriting. She spread it carefully across her knees, keeping it out of the drips, and put her finger on the river where it swung north around Harrow Bend.

"Tell me what's under my finger."

Wendell leaned over. For a moment he was silent, and then she saw it happen—the change she'd seen in him a dozen times now, the fugitive falling away and the engineer surfacing, some deep structural part of his mind waking up and looking at the world with cold bright interest.

"That's not possible," he breathed.

"It's entirely possible. You drew it yourself. Look at the date."

His finger traced the line she'd indicated: a dashed thread, one of dozens on the map, a spur marked in faded red ink with a notation in the margin. *Tunnel No. 3 — Harrow Bend — closed 1911 — flooded — see file.* His lips moved as he read his own marginal note, and Mara watched him travel backward through his own life to the year he'd surveyed these lines, before the frame-up, before the disgrace, back when Wendell Underhill had been a man whose measurements were trusted by courts.

"The river bend," he said slowly. "The old mainline used to cut across the neck of the bend instead of following it around. Two miles saved on every run, so they drove a tunnel under the bluff. Then the river shifted after the big flood—shifted right over the tunnel mouth—and the company sealed it rather than regrade." He looked up at her, and his eyes had gone wide and slightly appalled, the look of a man realizing the trapdoor has been under his feet all along. "You want to go in through the tunnel."

"I want to come up inside the archive district," Mara said. "Look at the geometry. The tunnel runs under the bend and comes out—" she walked her finger along the dashed line, past the river, into the hatched square that marked the built-up town— "here. Under the bluff. The old freight yard was at the base of the cliff; the archive was built on the yard's foundations forty years later. Every building on that street sits on top of the rail cutting. If the tunnel mouth is still open at the waterline—and it will be, because Corvane's men sealed the *channel*, not the shoreline, and nobody guards a door they don't know exists—we walk in under their chain, under their barges, under their checkpoints, and come up inside the district they think they've locked."

"Mara." Wendell said her name the way he said few things: plainly, without armor. "It's flooded. It's been flooded for sixty years. The brickwork will be rotten, the air pockets will be stale or absent, and the water level inside is anyone's guess. People die in flooded tunnels. Experienced people. Prepared people."

"Yes," she said. "They do."

"And you're proposing it anyway."

"I'm proposing it because the alternative is losing." She heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard, because the hardness was honest. "Wendell, I have watched Corvane erase six people I loved. I hid in the river and held my breath behind a screen of reeds while they burned our camp, and I lay in the mud all night listening to them laugh, and I swore on Ondrej's notebook that I would get those maps into an archive if it cost me everything including my life. I have spent four months being a ghost. I am very good at it now. But ghosts don't testify, and ghosts don't file maps, and ghosts don't get justice. At some point one of us has to be a person again, in a room, in the light, with witnesses." She folded her hands over the map. "I'd rather drown trying to surface than float forever."

The rain filled the silence between them. Somewhere out on the channel, a boat engine started—one of the flat-decked barges making a patrol circuit, its wake slapping against the willow trunks. They both froze until it passed.

When Wendell spoke again, it was the engineer talking, and Mara could have kissed him for it, because the engineer was the man she trusted most in the world even though she'd known him less than a season.

"All right," he said. "If we do this, we do it properly. Not brave. Properly." He took the map from her knees and smoothed it flat on the thwart, weighting the corners with the bailer and the tin of matches. "First question: air. A sealed tunnel sixty years underwater may have trapped gas—marsh gas, blackdamp. We test as we go, flame low, and if the flame gutters or burns strange, we turn around and we do not argue about it. Second question: depth. The river's up five meters over dry season—that's the whole point of the monsoon—but the tunnel grade rises toward the portal. Old tunnels were driven on a slight climb so water drained out the working face. If the grade holds, the water shallows as we go in, and there'll be an air space under the crown of the arch before we reach the far end. Maybe. If the crown hasn't fallen."

"And if it has fallen?"

"Then we find out at the fall line, in the dark, with the water at our chins, and we turn around and we're no worse off than sitting here." He was already sketching in the margin of the map, quick confident strokes: the tunnel profile as he remembered driving its survey, the estimated grade, the crown line, the probable air pocket. Watching him work, Mara felt the familiar ache she'd been carrying since the camp—Ondrej would have liked this man, would have argued with him happily for hours about datum points—and pushed the ache down where it lived.

"Third question," Wendell said. "Light. We can't carry torches through water; they'll drown. We need candles in jars, waterproofed, and we need at least six hours of them."

"Auntie Sef gave us candles."

"Auntie Sef," Wendell said, and something crossed his face that Mara had learned to read as the weather front of his feeling for the river people—gratitude and shame and belonging all fighting for the same ground, "gave us more than candles. She gave us a reason to get this done." He looked up. "Fourth question. The notebook stays out of the tunnel. If we drown, it doesn't drown with us. It goes back to Sef, or it goes downstream in the skiff with a note, or—" he hesitated, and Mara watched him wrestle with it— "or it goes to the families. But it doesn't die in the dark with us. Agreed?"

"Agreed." And she meant it, and it cost her, because the notebook was the closest thing she had left to her team, and the idea of parting from it even conditionally felt like leaving a child on a doorstep. But he was right. He was right about the flame test and the grade and the candles, and he was right about this, and the sum total of all that rightness was that she trusted him with her life in a way she had trusted no one since the morning the gray trucks came.

* * *

They spent the rest of the daylight preparing, hidden in a derelict boathouse half a mile downstream, a leaning shed of silvered planks whose owner had evidently given up on the river years ago. Wendell worked the way he did everything—without wasted motion, without commentary—stripping an old storm lantern for its glass chimney, waxing candle stubs into waterproofed pucks, testing the flame behavior in the boathouse's dampest corner until he was satisfied he knew what a dying flame looked like in wet air. Mara mended the skiff's worst seam with pine pitch and canvas, not because the skiff would carry them into the tunnel—it wouldn't, they'd wade and swim the first stretch—but because they might need it again on the far side, and hope, she had decided, was mostly a matter of preparation performed in advance of belief.

Twice during the afternoon she climbed the boathouse loft and watched Harrow Bend through a gap in the boards. The barges patrolled in shifts. Men in gray slickers stood at the landing, checking papers. Once, a family of traders was waved through with a cursory search, which told her the cordon wasn't general—it was specific. They were looking for two particular people, or one particular document, and they were patient about it. Corvane money bought patience the way it bought everything else.

At dusk she came down and found Wendell sitting with the map across his knees, not working, just looking at it.

"My father worked this line," he said, without preamble. "Before me. He drove the last inspection run through Tunnel No. 3 the month before they sealed it. I was nine. He let me hold the lantern." He laughed, short and without humor. "I cried because it was dark and the water dripped on my neck. He told me the dark in a tunnel isn't like other dark. It's a dark men built, so it has rules. Respect the rules and it lets you through."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe my father believed it, and he brought himself and his lantern home every night for thirty years." Wendell began folding the map along its old creases, precise as a surgeon. "So we'll respect the rules. Flame low. Hand on the wall. Never more than arm's reach apart—if one of us goes under, the other feels for the wall and walks backward until contact. And Mara—" he looked up at her, and in the gloom of the boathouse his eyes caught the last gray light— "if it goes wrong in there, you take the notebook and you leave me. Don't be noble. Noble is how Corvane wins. Get the maps to the archive. Promise me."

She thought about lying to him. She was good at lying; four months of survival had honed it. Instead she said, "I promise I'll get the maps to the archive," and watched him decide, visibly, to accept the half of the promise he could live with.

They ate cold rice and waited for full dark.

* * *

The river at night was a different country. They paddled the skiff unlit, close along the northern shore, using the drowned trees for cover, Mara steering by the shapes of the hills she had memorized from her own surveys—she knew this valley's bones better than the syndicate ever would, and there was a fierce satisfaction in that, in being guided through enemy country by knowledge the enemy had tried to kill. The chain across the channel showed as a faint sagging line against the far lights, hung with small bells that stirred in the current. They passed it fifty yards upstream, invisible in the shadow of the bluff, and rounded the bend into dead water.

And there it was.

The tunnel mouth opened in the base of the cliff like a held breath—a great semicircle of blackness twenty feet high, its brick ring furred with moss, half-drowned, the river sliding quietly into it and not coming out the other side. Someone had bricked a gate across it once, decades ago; the gate had partially failed, its lower courses crumbled away, leaving a gap above the waterline wide enough for a boat, and above that the old keystone bore a date worn nearly smooth. Wendell's hand rose and touched it as they drifted in under the arch, and Mara heard him exhale, long and shaking.

"1911," he whispered. "Hello, old girl."

Inside, the sound changed. The river noise fell away behind them and the tunnel took over—drips echoing from unseen heights, the hollow lap of water against brick, and beneath it all a low tidal breathing, the whole flooded passage inhaling and exhaling through some hidden connection to the world outside. Wendell lit the first candle puck in its jar and held it low, and the flame stood up straight and yellow and calm, and Mara felt her chest unlock.

"Air's moving," he murmured. "Feel it on your wet hand? There's a draft. Draft means a second opening. Draft means we're not sealing ourselves into a tomb." He glanced at her, and even in the candlelight she could see the grin fighting its way onto his face despite everything. "My father's rules are holding."

They shipped the paddles, lashed the skiff inside the portal where the water shallowed over a shelf of collapsed ballast, and buried the notebook in the ballast beneath a flat stone, marking the spot with a chalk cross only they would understand. Then they waded in.

The water came to their waists, then their chests. The tunnel ran straight as a die, and Wendell kept one hand on the left-hand wall and called the grade in a low continuous murmur—"rising, still rising, quarter meter"—and Mara swam beside him with the candle jar held overhead, watching the flame the way she'd once watched instrument needles, and the dark closed around them like a hand closing gently over a candle.

Fifty meters in, the ceiling dropped to meet the water, and for eleven terrible meters they swam blind, Wendell's fist knotted in the back of her collar, his other hand tracing the wall, both of them holding their breath in water the temperature of melted snow while the candle guttered in its jar above the surface that no longer existed. Mara counted strokes because counting was what kept her sane—four, five, six—and at nine her shoulder hit open air and she came up gasping into a black vault of space, and Wendell surfaced beside her coughing, and the candle flame leapt upright and burned tall and clean.

"Air pocket," he rasped, laughing breathlessly in the dark. "Big one. Feel the draft? We're through the low section. It rises from here—I can feel the bottom coming up under my feet."

And it did. Meter by meter the water surrendered, waist to knee to ankle, until they stood on silt-covered track bed with the rails gleaming dully under the candlelight, walking upright beneath a crown of century-old brick, two ghosts traveling through the solid rock beneath the town that was hunting them. Mara's legs shook and she let them shake. Above them, muffled through sixty feet of earth and brick, she could hear Harrow Bend: a dog barking, a truck changing gear, the ordinary sounds of a town that had no idea what was walking under its streets.

Then Wendell stopped so suddenly she walked into his back.

Ahead, maybe thirty meters, the tunnel ended—not in a portal but in a wall of fallen brick and shattered timber, a collapse, old and settled, blocking the passage floor to crown. Beyond it, through a gap the size of a bread box near the top of the rubble, came a thread of light. Not daylight. Lamplight. Warm, flickering, human.

And with it, faint but unmistakable, voices.

"—search the lower stacks again, he said floor by floor, and I don't care if the basement floods, Voss and Underhill are in this town or they're dead in the river, and either way the boss wants certainty—"

Mara stood in the dark with river water running off her and her heart slamming against her ribs, and understood, all at once, exactly where the tunnel had delivered them.

Not under the archive district.

Under the archive itself.
