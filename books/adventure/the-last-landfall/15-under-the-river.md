## 15. Under the River

The tunnel mouth was exactly where the map said it would be, which was the first thing that frightened him.

Wendell had spent three years learning to distrust anything that behaved as advertised. Maps lied. Timetables lied. Men in gray trucks lied with the fluency of professionals. But there it was: a black arch of Victorian brickwork half-drowned at the base of the river bend, its keystone still legible under a beard of moss — *HARROW BEND & WESTERN, 1887* — and beyond it, water running flat and patient into the dark.

"You're sure about the grade," Mara said. She stood hip-deep beside him, holding the notebook wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant. Her hair was plastered to her skull. She had not stopped shivering since they'd slipped away from the last of Auntie Sef's boats, but her voice was steady, and he'd learned by now that steadiness from Mara Voss was not calm. It was decision wearing calm's clothing.

"Two percent down going in," he said. "Which means two percent up coming out. The old builders couldn't drain a level tunnel under a river, so they pitched it toward the portals. Water runs out both ends." He looked at the arch again. "In theory."

"And in practice?"

"In practice, that tunnel's been sealed for forty years, the river's been eating it for thirty, and we're about to find out how much brick is left between us and a very long drowning." He cinched the last strap on his pack. Inside it: rope, three candles, a tin of matches in waxed paper, a pry bar, and the inflated bladder from the skiff's emergency kit, which would serve as their float if the water deepened past standing height. Everything else they owned was gone or abandoned — the skiff scuttled upstream, their tracks wiped behind them with palm fronds, because Corvane's men were watching every road into Harrow Bend and patience was one thing Wendell Underhill no longer possessed.

He took the notebook from her hands, weighed it, then handed it back. "You carry it. If I go under, you swim. Not the other way around."

"I know how to swim in reeds," she said. "I don't know how to swim in tunnels."

"Nobody does. That's what makes it fair."

She almost smiled. Then she pulled the oilcloth tighter and stepped through the arch, and the river closed over the place where she'd been, and Wendell followed her into the dark.

* * *

The first hundred yards were the worst kind of easy.

The water came to his chest, cold enough to make his teeth ache, moving with a slow interior current he could feel pressing against his back like a hand. The candle he'd wedged into a jar lid threw a circle of amber light maybe eight feet across, and inside that circle the world was brick and water and Mara's shoulders ahead of him, rising and falling with her breath. Outside the circle was nothing at all.

He counted his steps. It was an engineer's habit and a coward's comfort, and he made no apology for either. Forty years of rail work had left him with lengths and grades living in his body the way other men carried songs — he knew without measuring that the tunnel ran six hundred and forty feet beneath the bend, because he'd helped survey its closure, back when he was a man whose name opened doors instead of closing them. Six hundred forty feet. At this pace, four hundred steps. He was at sixty when the ceiling began to come apart.

It started as sound. A soft mineral ticking, like rain on a tin roof played backward — drips finding their way down through cracks overhead, each one striking the water with a small blind precision. Then the candlelight found the damage itself: a seam in the brickwork where mortar had been eaten out entirely, leaving a gap wide enough to put a fist through, and beyond the gap, darkness that wasn't tunnel-darkness but earth-darkness, the wet black of saturated soil holding its breath above them.

"Mara." His voice came back off the brick flattened and strange. "Stop. Don't touch the walls from here on."

"Why? What—"

"Because the walls are load-bearing fiction." He raised the candle higher. Above the gap, a crack ran along the crown of the arch, following the line of an old construction joint, and from the crack hung a single thread of silted water, gleaming like wire. "The river's on top of us right now. Maybe twelve feet of it. This brick is all that's deciding we don't find out what that feels like."

She looked up. He watched her do the same arithmetic he'd done — watched it move through her face the way weather moves across open country — and then she nodded once and kept walking, and he understood that she had decided the arithmetic didn't matter, which was either courage or exhaustion, and after three days in a leaking boat he suspected it was both.

At step one-forty, the floor dropped out from under them.

There was no warning — just a sudden absence where the bottom should have been, and Wendell went down into water so cold it felt like a held note, plunging to his chin before his boot found something solid again, a shelf of collapsed brick sloping down into deeper black. Ahead, Mara gasped — she'd gone in to her neck — and the oilcloth bundle rose above the surface, held high in both hands, her head tipped back, breathing in short hard pulls.

"Hold the wall side," he said. "Feel with your feet. There'll be rubble along the haunches — the collapse always piles at the springline."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've read a hundred reports on tunnel failures and slept through most of them." He worked his way along the left wall, boots searching, and found the shelf he'd promised: a ridge of broken brick running along the curve where wall met roof, submerged but firm. "Here. Walk the haunch. Left foot high, right foot probing. And whatever happens, don't let that notebook go under."

"It won't."

"Mara."

"It *won't*, Wendell." And there was something in her voice then that shut him up — not anger, exactly. Grief with its sleeves rolled up. He remembered what she'd told him in the quarry cave, in the green dark with the water dripping: *that notebook is eleven people.* He carried his own dead differently, folded up in guilt and old court transcripts, but he recognized the weight. You didn't drop that. You didn't let the river have it.

They crossed the collapse in silence. Twice the floor gave way beneath his probing boot and twice he caught himself on the wall, fingers finding purchase in joints worn smooth by a century of floodwater, and once something moved in the water beside him — long, sinuous, indifferent — and was gone before his heart finished its argument with his ribs.

At step two-sixty, they reached the air pocket.

He smelled it before he saw it: a change in the way the dark tasted, wet stone giving way to something older and dryer, and then the candle flame leaned sideways, bending toward a low hollow in the ceiling where the brick bellied upward into a chamber no bigger than a chapel. They climbed into it out of the water, shaking, and sat with their backs against warm-ish stone while the river breathed below them.

Mara unwrapped the notebook. Just to look at it, she said. Just to be sure.

It was dry. The oilcloth had held. She touched the cover the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and Wendell, watching her, felt something in his chest he refused to name, because naming things was how you lost them.

"The air pocket ends in eighty feet," he said, when his voice worked again. "After that the crown rises and we should surface-walk the rest. Two percent up. The water gets shallower."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we've drowned in a tunnel built by men who never imagined anyone would be stupid enough to thank them for it."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and in the candlelight he saw that her lips were blue and her hands wouldn't stop trembling and she was, without question, the most stubborn person he had ever shared a catastrophe with.

"Eighty feet," she said. "Tell me when."

* * *

The trouble began at ninety feet, which was the sort of thing the universe did to engineers on principle.

The pocket ended not in rising crown but in a chokepoint — a fall of brick and river silt packed tight from floor to ceiling except for a gap at the top, perhaps eighteen inches high, where water slid through in a smooth black tongue. Beyond it, faintly, impossibly, the candlelight caught movement on the far wall: ripples. Moving air. There was space on the other side. There had to be.

"That's not a gap," Mara said quietly. "That's a throat."

"It's passable."

"For an otter."

"For a thin man with good lungs and a bad history of judgment." He was already uncoiling the rope, tying one end around his own chest under the arms, testing the knot twice, three times. "I go through, I anchor the line, you follow the rope. Count your breaths on my tugs. Three tugs means come. One long pull means don't."

"What does no signal mean?"

He looked at her. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, exhausted and ferocious, and he thought: *this is the woman who lay underwater among the reeds for a day and a night while her team was erased around her.* She had already survived the unsurvivable once. He was not going to insult her by pretending this was worse.

"No signal means you take the notebook back to Sef," he said. "You tell her story. You let the river people raise hell until someone with a badge listens. That's the no-signal plan, and it's a good one, so don't argue with it."

"I wasn't going to argue." She took the candle and held it close while he worked himself up into the gap, and the last thing he saw before the water took him was her eyes, steady as survey markers, fixed on his face.

The throat was worse than he'd budgeted for.

Brick pressed his spine; brick pressed his chest; the water pushed against him with the dumb patient strength of everything that has all the time in the world. He exhaled and went flat and pulled, arm over arm through a space measured in inches, the rope dragging behind him, his lungs beginning their slow bright complaint. Somewhere in the middle — he would remember this forever, the way you remember the exact center of any nightmare — a jagged edge of brick caught his shirt and held him, and for three full seconds he hung there in the black with the river in his ears, feeling the panic rise in him like floodwater, feeling the old familiar certainty that this, finally, was the shape his life had been aiming at all along.

Then he thought of Mara counting his breaths in the dark behind him, trusting a knot and a stranger, and the panic turned into something usable.

He tore free. The fabric went; he barely noticed. Ten more pulls and his head broke surface into a space of echoing nothing, and he hung onto the rubble rim and hauled in air that tasted of wet stone and iron and, God help him, freedom.

Three tugs on the rope. Then he tied off, braced his boots, and waited, counting her breaths by feel through the line — quick, quick, slower, slower — while somewhere behind him in the black water a woman who trusted no one decided, breath by breath, to trust him.

When she surfaced beside him, coughing and swearing with a fluency that would have embarrassed a boatyard, he discovered he was grinning like an idiot, and that she was too, and that neither of them said a word about it, because some things don't survive being said.

* * *

The rest was almost merciful.

The crown rose as promised, the grade lifted as promised, and within two hundred yards they were wading shin-deep through water clear enough to show the old rail bed beneath — rotted ties furred with white mold, a rusted spike here and there glinting in the candlelight like coins on a grave. The brickwork improved too; the river's reach hadn't gotten this far, or hadn't gotten here often. Once, the light fell across a maintenance niche cut into the wall, and inside it, stenciled in paint gone brown as dried blood: *HB&W LINE 4 — INSPECT QTRLY.* Quarterly inspections. Wendell laughed aloud, a raw bark that echoed down the tunnel, and Mara looked at him like he'd gone mad, and maybe he had, a little, because it seemed to him suddenly and unbearably funny that men had once been paid to care whether this place stood, and had stopped, and it had stood anyway, out of sheer mule brickwork stubbornness, waiting forty years for exactly two such idiots as themselves.

Then the dark ahead turned gray, then silver, then the color of rain-washed morning, and there it was: the far portal, a ragged arch opening onto green light and the smell of cut grass and coal smoke, and beyond it, glimpsed through a screen of willow, the slate roofs and brick chimneys of the Harrow Bend archive district, close enough to count windows.

They came out of the tunnel like survivors of a shipwreck, which is what they were, and stood blinking in the willow shade with water streaming off them, and Wendell felt the triumph hit him whole and entire — *we made it, we actually made it, Corvane's men are watching the roads and the bridges and every door in town, and we just walked under the river and came up inside their net* — and he turned to Mara to say something about it, something worthy of the moment.

And saw her face.

She had unwrapped the notebook. She was holding it open in both hands, and the oilcloth had done its work for six hundred feet of tunnel and failed in the last ten, in the throat, in the tearing and the panic and the river getting in — and the pages were soaked through, and the ink was moving.

Not ruined. Not yet. But moving: lines feathering at their edges, coordinates softening into gray blooms, the precise beautiful handwriting of eleven dead and disappeared surveyors dissolving even as she watched, even as she tilted the pages against the light as though angle could stop chemistry. He saw her thumb press down on one page — instinct, a printer's reflex, save the text — and saw the pressure smear a column of figures into a ghost of itself.

"Mara—"

"They can't read it now either," she said. Her voice was very quiet and very level, the way it got when the ground was giving way. "If the ink goes, the maps go. It's the only copy. It was the only copy *before*, and now—" She stopped. Closed the book. Opened it again, as if checking whether the disaster would hold still if she kept looking at it.

Wendell crouched beside her in the willow shade and took the notebook gently from her hands and did the only engineering he could do, which was to think in terms of what could still be saved. Separate the pages. Blot them. Dry them flat, fast, away from heat. Some ink would set again if it dried before it migrated. Some was already gone. And some — the parts that mattered, the water rights, the parcel numbers, the levee surveys — might survive in fragments sharp enough to corroborate, if there was anything left to corroborate against.

"There's a witness requirement," he heard himself say. "For the archive filing. Standing evidence. You told me that in the quarry."

"Yes."

"Then the pages aren't the only copy anymore." He looked up at her, water still dripping from his hair, the archive district lying open and unsuspecting beyond the willows. "You're the copy. Everything you memorized. Every coordinate you can rebuild from memory. The ink was always going to be the fragile part. You were always the durable part."

She stared at him. Behind her, through the willow screen, a church bell in Harrow Bend began to ring the hour — eight strokes, unhurried, ordinary, a town waking up on a day that had no idea what had just crawled out of its river. And Wendell understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the exact moment everything is at once won and lost, that he had just said the truest sentence of his life, and that it was also a sentence of surrender: because if Mara's memory was the evidence, then the evidence needed a witness with standing, and there was exactly one man alive whom Corvane had given standing to, years ago, by ruining him.

But that was a bill for another hour. For now there were wet pages to blot, and a town full of enemies to slip through, and the bell still ringing.

"Come on," he said, folding the notebook into the last dry cloth they had. "We've got a city to sneak into, and for once, nobody in the world knows where we are."

From the willows behind them, unhurried, a gray truck rolled slowly across the far end of the archive square — and stopped.
