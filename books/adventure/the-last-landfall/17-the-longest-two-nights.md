## 17. The Longest Two Nights

The basement of the Harrow Bend Provincial Archive smelled of cold paper and river stone. Somewhere above them, floor by floor, boots were moving.

Wendell Underhill had spent three years learning to read the sounds of pursuit — the pitch of an engine idling too long, the rhythm of footsteps that swept rather than walked, the particular silence a man made when he was pretending not to be there. He knew the sound of the search above them now: methodical, unhurried, thorough. Corvane did not hire men who rushed. Rushing left gaps.

They were in the sub-basement, past the boiler room, among the dead records — the shelves nobody visited because the documents they held had been superseded, repealed, or simply forgotten. Tax rolls from sixty years ago. Land grants for parishes that no longer existed. A whole aisle of survey plats, water-stained and buckling, filed under a department name that had been dissolved before Wendell was born. The irony was not lost on him. He was hiding inside the paper corpse of the very profession that had ruined him.

There was one lamp, a brass thing with a green glass shade, wired to a line some clerk must have run down decades ago for the winter inventories. Mara had turned it low, so low the light barely reached past their shelf. She sat cross-legged on the concrete with the soaked notebook open before her, its pages separated by strips of blotting paper she'd torn from a ledger on the way down. Between every page, the paper drank what it could of the river.

And between every page, Wendell wrote.

He'd found a legal pad in a drawer marked STATIONERY — DISCONTINUED and a pencil stub worn to a nub. He wrote small, pressing hard, because pencil was all there was and because he wanted to be sure of every word before he committed it. His testimony. The thing he had promised himself he would never write, never speak, never become — because the moment he became it, he stopped being invisible, and invisibility had been the only thing keeping him alive.

*My name is Wendell James Underhill,* he wrote. *I am thirty-eight years old. I am a licensed civil engineer, license number—* He stopped. Did he remember the number? Of course he remembered the number. You didn't forget the number that got revoked. *License 4-1187, revoked in the spring of the year I stopped being anyone.*

Mara looked up at him across the lamplight. Her face was smudged with tunnel grime, a cut healing along her jaw from the brickwork under the river, and her eyes had that quality he'd been learning to read these past weeks — the look of a woman doing arithmetic about trust, running the sums over and over to see if the answer ever changed.

"You don't have to write it tonight," she said quietly. "We can hear them coming. There'll be time."

"That's exactly why I have to write it tonight," Wendell said. "Because if they find us tomorrow, this goes in my pocket and it has to say everything."

She nodded slowly and went back to her pages. And Wendell kept writing, and the boots kept coming down through the building above, one floor at a time, like water filling a hull.

* * *

By the second hour, they could hear the searchers clearly. Not words — the archive's construction swallowed voices — but the texture of them. Doors opening. Drawers. The scrape of something heavy dragged across tile. Once, directly overhead, a long pause, then the crack of a filing cabinet kicked open, then nothing, then movement again. They were working downward. Reading Room first, then Records, then Microfilm. Two floors between the boots and the boiler room.

Wendell finished the first page of his statement and started the second, and the second page was where the story turned ugly, because the second page was where the story turned true.

"Read me the part you just wrote," Mara said.

It wasn't a request, exactly. It was a test, though maybe neither of them would have called it that. If he was going to testify, she needed to know what kind of witness he'd be — whether he'd sand the edges off the truth until it slid easily into a courtroom, or whether he'd give it whole.

So Wendell read it aloud, his voice pitched under the lamp's glow, under the distant boots:

*I had the contract to survey the eastern rail corridor for the provincial expansion — eleven hundred kilometers of line, river crossings, land acquisitions. It was the work of my life. I was good at it. I want that on the record, because everything after is going to make it sound like I was stupid, and I wasn't stupid. I was honest. Those are different failures.*

"I was honest," he repeated, and heard how strange the words sounded in his own mouth after three years of saying almost nothing true to anyone. "Those are different failures."

"What happened?" Mara asked. Not gently. She didn't do gentle; gentleness was a currency she couldn't afford. But she leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and gave him the thing that was rarer than gentleness: complete attention.

Wendell set down the pencil. Picked it up again. Set it down.

"The corridor crossed the Verrone family's holdings," he said. "Before it was Corvane, it was the Verrones — same money, new letterhead. Old man Verrone held forty thousand acres along the proposed route, most of it worthless scrub except where the line needed to go. The acquisition price was set by independent appraisal. That was the law, and I believed in the law the way you believe in a bridge — you put your whole weight on it without thinking, because why would you think?"

Above them, a door opened and closed. Closer now.

"The appraiser came back with a number four times what the land was worth. I flagged it. I flagged it in writing, which was my first mistake, because it created a document. My supervisor told me to let it go — said the province would eat the cost rather than delay the line, that it wasn't my problem. But four times market value isn't waste. Four times market value is a payment. So I wrote a memo saying so, and I sent it up, and six weeks later I was arrested for falsifying the survey data." He laughed, short and airless. "Falsifying. They produced engineering reports under my name — reports I'd never seen — showing the corridor was geologically unsound except across Verrone land. Forged well enough to fool a magistrate who'd never read a soils analysis in his life. My signature was good. Whoever did it had samples. Plenty of samples. I signed things all day, every day, back then."

Mara was very still. "Your own firm helped them."

"My firm fired me the day before the arrest. Clean conscience by paperwork." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "The trial took nine days. My lawyer was paid for by — I learned later — a foundation that traced back to Corvane. He advised me to plead to a lesser count. Said fighting it would bring the full fraud charges and ten years instead of two. I was twenty-nine years old and terrified and I believed I had no choice. So I pled, I served fourteen months of an eighteen-month sentence, and when I came out my license was gone, my name was in the trade journals as a cautionary tale, and every railroad in three provinces had blacklisted me before my train cleared the prison yard."

"And the corridor?"

"Bought at the inflated price. Built on the route old man Verrone wanted. The geological problems turned out to be real, incidentally — there's a stretch near Mile 340 that floods every spring and costs the province more each year than the entire original overpayment. Nobody's ever connected it, because nobody's ever looked, because looking requires admitting what happened." He picked up the pencil again, and this time he didn't put it down. "That's the confession. That's the whole architecture of my fall. I trusted the wrong people, I pled to protect myself, and then I ran for three years as if running could undo a signature. It can't. All it does is keep you alive long enough to be ashamed in new places."

* * *

Somewhere above them, on what Wendell calculated was the ground floor now, the searching paused. Long enough that both of them stopped breathing, listening. Then resumed.

One floor left. Maybe ninety minutes, if the men were as careful as they sounded. Maybe less.

Mara moved closer to the lamp and began sorting her salvaged pages into order — the ones whose ink had survived the tunnel, the ones gone to gray ghosts, the ones she could reconstruct from memory because she had drawn those maps twice already, once in ink and once in her head during the long nights underwater and afterward, hiding, watching Wendell move through her camp like a ghost checking another ghost's house.

"They offered you money once, didn't they," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "In the boat camp. The lawyer. You told me he offered clean papers."

"He did." Wendell turned the legal pad to a fresh page. "And I nearly took it. You know that. You watched me nearly take it."

"I watched you turn the trap around instead. Different thing."

"Is it?" He looked at her across the small circle of light. "I've been asking myself that since the night currents. Whether I turned the trap around because I'd finally chosen the right side, or because taking the deal would have meant staying alone forever, and even I couldn't stomach that anymore." He shook his head. "It matters for the record. Motive matters in a hearing. If I stand up in that courtroom and they ask why I'm testifying, and the honest answer is partly loneliness —"

"Then the honest answer is partly loneliness." Mara's voice was flat and certain. "You write it down anyway. I spent six days underwater in the reeds watching those men burn my team's camp, and I'll tell you what I decided down there in the mud with my lungs burning: a truth with an impure motive attached is still the truth. Corvane doesn't get to win because your reasons weren't pretty. Write the ugly parts, Wendell. The ugly parts are what make the rest believable."

He stared at her. In the low lamplight, with the river still faintly in her hair and the notebook's ruined maps spread around her like shed skins, she looked like something out of one of the old plats on the shelves behind them — a woman surveyed against impossible terrain, contour lines everywhere, elevation gained the hard way.

"You should write testimony," he said. "You're better at it than I am."

"I draw. You talk." She pushed the pad back toward him. "Talk."

So he talked. And wrote. Through the next hour he gave her the parts of the story he had never given anyone — not because they were secret but because there had never been a person safe enough to hold them. How his father had worked the Kessel Junction freight yard for thirty-one years and died with a lantern in his hand, and how Wendell had sworn he'd build things instead of maintaining other men's decay. How his mother had stopped writing to him in prison, not from anger but from shame, and had died before his release, and how he'd learned of it from a chaplain's form letter. How, in his first year of drifting, he'd stood on a trestle outside Marrow Creek and seriously considered stepping off it, and how what stopped him was the map — the stolen map, the closed lines, the idea that somewhere ahead there was always one more branch, one more spur, one more place the world had forgotten where a forgotten man could breathe. How the rule — never longer than two nights — hadn't really been about the law at all. It had been about making sure no place ever had the chance to reject him twice.

"Kessel Junction rejected me once already," he said. "When the news reached town. The stationmaster — old Hobb, who taught me telegraph code when I was nine — he took my picture down from the depot wall himself. I heard about it years later. He didn't burn it. He put it in a drawer. I've thought about that drawer for three years. Why keep it? Why not burn it?"

"Why do you think?"

"Because Hobb knew the truth and kept the evidence. Same as you." Wendell smiled, and it hurt his face, because smiling had gone out of practice. "People who know the truth keep things. That's what this whole valley is — drawers full of things Corvane thinks were thrown away. Your notebook. Auntie Sef's boats. My signature on a confession I never wrote."

Above them, the boots reached the ground-floor stacks.

* * *

They heard the basement door try. The handle rattled; the lock — an old iron thing Wendell had wedged with a folded plat an hour ago — held. Voices now, muffled but real, two or three men, arguing briefly about whether the sub-level mattered. One of them said, distinctly enough through the old brick: *"Nobody's down here, it floods every spring, check the windows and let's go."*

Another said: *"Orders say every floor."*

Footsteps descended.

Mara pinched out the lamp. Darkness total, wet and mineral-smelling. Wendell found her hand in it — not reaching, just knowing where it would be, the way you know where a lever is on a machine you've operated a thousand times — and drew her back into the deepest aisle, between the buckled survey plats and the wall, where the shelving's shadow made them into shadow.

The footsteps came down the stairs. Three men. Lantern light swung through the doorway in blades. Wendell pressed his palm flat against Mara's shoulder blade, feeling her heartbeat through the fabric — fast, controlled, a runner's heart deciding — and she did the same to him, and the strangeness of it struck him even now, even here: that he could be found in ninety seconds and executed in ninety more, and what occupied the front of his mind was that her heart and his were beating at nearly the same rate, two engines synchronized on the same failing track.

The lanterns swept the aisles. Light crawled over the spines of dead ledgers. A man coughed, said something about rats. Their light passed within six feet of the aisle mouth — passed over it, actually, because the collapsed shelf Wendell had pulled down earlier still lay across the entrance like a fallen tree, and men searching for fugitives do not stop to inventory rubble.

They checked the boiler room. They checked the coal chute. They stood for four minutes — Wendell counted — discussing whether to check the flooded crawlspace, and decided the smell argued against it, and climbed back upstairs, complaining.

The lock clicked shut behind them. Their boots faded upward. A vehicle started in the alley. Another answered. Then the street sounds of Harrow Bend at night, indifferent and ordinary, rain beginning again against the high windows.

Neither of them moved for a long time. Then Mara relit the lamp, and the green shade threw its small pool of safety back onto the concrete, and they sat down again as if nothing had happened, because something had happened, and sitting was how you proved it hadn't beaten you.

"They'll be back at first light," Mara said. "With the flood crews, officially. Unofficially, they'll tear this level apart."

"Then we're done hiding by first light." Wendell turned to the last clean page of the legal pad. "Which means we're done drafting by first light. Read me your pages. We'll match dates. If my testimony and your maps agree on every crossing, every parcel, every payment date, then Corvane can't call one of us a liar without calling the other one a liar, and two liars who've never met don't produce matching numbers."

"Engineers," Mara said, with something that was almost affection. "Everything's a load calculation."

"Everything is a load calculation. Even the truth. Especially the truth." He smoothed the page. "Start with the water rights. Start with what they bought and when, and I'll tell you what I saw on the rail surveys that matches."

They worked. Hours. The rain thickened overhead, drumming the high windows, running down through the old building in threads they could sometimes hear ticking off the stairwell rails. Mara recited parcels from memory — coordinates, acreages, purchase dates — and Wendell matched them against the corridor surveys still living in his head, the ones no one had ever confiscated because no one had ever known they existed: the numbers he carried the way other men carry scars. Every figure locked. Every date aligned. By the fourth hour the legal pad was half full and the notebook's surviving pages sat in ordered ranks like a company formed up for review.

Near dawn, Mara fell asleep sitting up, her chin dropping toward the ruined maps, her hand still resting on a page as if holding its place in a conversation. Wendell eased her down onto the rolled plats they'd gathered as bedding, draped his coat over her, and returned to the pad.

He wrote the last lines standing, because some sentences won't come out any other way.

*I, Wendell James Underhill, swear that the foregoing is true. I understand that in giving this testimony I surrender myself to the court, and I make this surrender freely, having spent three years learning that a man cannot hide inside his own life and still be said to be living it.*

He read it over twice. Then he folded the pages into oilcloth from his pack, wrapped them tight, and tucked them inside his shirt, against his chest, where a man keeps the things he has finally decided to carry openly.

Through the high windows, far off across the drowned rooftops of Harrow Bend, the sky had gone the color of wet slate — but along the eastern edge, thin as a wire, a paler line was forming. Dry season's first light, or near enough. And with it, faintly, across the water, came a sound Wendell knew from a lifetime of rail yards and had never expected to hear with relief: bells. Many bells, low and rhythmic, carried over the floodwater from the direction of the river landing.

Mara woke at the sound instantly, the way soldiers wake, and was beside him at the window in two strides.

"What is that?" she breathed.

Wendell pressed his forehead to the cold glass, squinting through the rain-haze at the gray water beyond the archive district — and felt something turn over in his chest that he hadn't felt in three years.

"Lanterns," he said. "Dozens of them. On the water." He paused, counting, disbelieving. "Mara — those are fishing boats."
