## 18. Dry Season's First Light

The rain stopped the way a held breath is finally let go.

Mara woke in the archive basement to a silence so complete it felt like an event. For eleven days the monsoon had hammered Harrow Bend without pause—water drumming on the roof, sheeting down the windows, turning the streets outside into brown canals—and she had learned to sleep through it the way you learn to sleep through your own heartbeat. Now the absence of it pulled her up out of a dreamless dark, and for a long moment she lay on the cot among the map cases and didn't move, listening.

Dripping. That was all. Water finding its way off eaves and ledges, ticking down onto stone. Somewhere above, a gutter overflowed with a soft irregular gurgle. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the river itself—the Kessel running high and fast past the town, no longer roaring but speaking, the way it did when it began to think about falling.

*Dry season's first light,* Auntie Sef had said, back on the flotilla, when Mara had asked how they would know when to move. *You'll hear it before you see it. The river changes its voice.*

Mara sat up. Her shoulder ached where she'd wrenched it hauling Wendell out of the tunnel water, and her hands still carried the ghost-stain of soaked ink that no amount of scrubbing had fully lifted. On the worktable beside her cot lay the notebook, open to a page of bleeding gray ghosts where the survey coordinates used to be, and next to it, three weeks of her own labor: the restored maps, drawn from memory, cross-checked against the archive's older charts, every line annotated in fresh black ink that would not run.

Beside them, weighted down with a brass paperweight shaped like a compass rose, lay Wendell's testimony. Forty-one pages in his narrow engineer's hand, each sentence built like a bridge truss—nothing decorative, everything load-bearing. She had read it twice. The second time she'd had to stop halfway through the account of his framing and walk up the basement stairs just to stand in the rain and breathe.

He was asleep in the map room at the far end of the stacks, or he had been when she'd last checked. The provincial archivist, a stooped woman named Ilsa Brandt who had taken one look at their soaked clothes and Corvane's name and decided, with the quiet ferocity of someone who had spent thirty years guarding paper against powerful men, that they were now part of the collection—had given them cots, kerosene, and a bolt lock on the basement door.

Mara dressed in the dark, rolled the maps into their oilcloth case, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

The world after the monsoon looked like something newly made and not yet finished.

She stood in the archive's upper gallery, at the tall window overlooking the river bend, and watched the light come. It came the way Sef had promised—not gradually but all at once, a single clean blade of gold splitting the western clouds, laying itself across the flooded fields and the drowned orchards and the great brown muscle of the Kessel, so that the whole valley seemed to catch fire and steam. Mist lifted off the water in slow columns. A heron stood in the flooded plaza below, ankle-deep where a fountain should have been, regarding its own reflection with the indifference of a creature that had seen worse seasons.

And on the river: boats. Dozens of them, moving down from the north with the current, sails patched, hulls riding low. The flotilla, coming down from the high-water camps, following the receding flood home.

Her chest tightened. Somewhere in that scattered fleet was a pump barge with children aboard who knew her name, and an old woman with forearms like dock rope who had fed them, sheltered them, and never once asked what they were running from until they were ready to say it.

Behind her, on the stairs, floorboards creaked.

She turned, hand going to the oilcloth case, before she recognized Wendell's silhouette—tall, slightly stooped, one hand trailing the wall the way he still did in unfamiliar buildings, as if checking the structure by touch. He came to stand beside her at the window. He smelled of kerosene and cold stone and, faintly, of the tunnel water that never quite seemed to leave his hair.

"You felt it too," he said.

"The silence." She nodded toward the river. "Look."

He looked. For a while neither of them spoke. The light spread; the mist burned away; the boats grew larger and more distinct. Wendell's face, in the growing gold, was the face of a man watching something he did not believe he would be allowed to keep.

"Brandt opened the west door," he said finally. "Streets are draining. By noon the steps will be passable." He paused. "By noon, everyone will know we're here."

That was the arithmetic they had been living inside for six days, ever since they'd surfaced from the tunnel with the notebook dying in its oilcloth: the archive was sanctuary, but sanctuary was also a trap. Corvane's men had sealed the town before they entered it. They were waiting for the waters to drop, because men in gray trucks could not swim. Sooner or later, the roads would open, and so would the doors.

"I've been thinking about that," Mara said. "About what happens when they come."

"And?"

"And I think we stop hiding." She turned to face him. In the strengthening light she could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, three years of it, and beneath that something newer and more frightening: hope, unarmored. "We walk out the front steps, Wendell. In daylight. With the maps and your testimony and anyone in this town willing to stand with us. We make them take us in front of witnesses."

"Corvane doesn't care about witnesses."

"No," she agreed. "But courts do. And so does the provincial assessor, who I found in Brandt's registry has been asking questions about Corvane's levee permits since before the flood. And so does every fisherman on that river who watched gray trucks burn our camp." She touched the oilcloth case. "Evidence hidden is evidence that can be stolen. Evidence witnessed is evidence that exists."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a handcar starting to roll.

"You know what my rule was? Three years. Never stop longer than two nights anywhere." He shook his head. "I spent last night drafting a legal testimony in a basement. I don't even recognize myself anymore."

"Is that bad?"

"I haven't decided." But he was smiling when he said it, and the smile did something to his whole face, took ten years off it and gave back something younger she suspected he'd buried along with his name.

They went down to tell Brandt.

* * *

Ilsa Brandt received the plan standing behind her reading desk with her spectacles pushed up into her white hair, looking at them both over the rims the way she probably looked at forgeries.

"You want to surrender yourselves on my front steps," she said.

"We want to file," Mara said. "Publicly. Under the archive's own charter—right of public deposit, witnessed deposition. Your charter, Brandt. Article Four. You told me about it yourself."

"I told you about it because you wouldn't stop asking questions." Brandt took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which Mara had learned was what she did instead of sighing. "Article Four requires a public reading. On the steps. Before any citizen who wishes to attend." She set the spectacles down and looked at Mara with an expression that was almost, almost warm. "Do you know how many times Article Four has been invoked in this archive's history?"

"How many?"

"Once. 1889. A mill owner tried to suppress a ledger showing he'd short-weighted grain during a famine year. The archivist then was a man named Oskar Vell. He read the entire ledger aloud on these steps over two days while the mill owner's men stood in the street deciding whether to shoot him." She closed the register. "They didn't. There are some things even bought men won't do in front of a crowd. Usually."

"And if they do?" Wendell asked quietly.

"Then there will be a very great deal of paper about it afterward," Brandt said, "and paper is the one thing I have never once run short of." She rang a small bell on her desk. "Noon, then. I'll have the charter flag raised. In this town, that flag means the archive speaks. People will come out of curiosity if nothing else. Curiosity is the one crop the flood didn't drown."

* * *

At half past eleven, Mara stood at the top of the archive steps and watched the water leaving Harrow Bend.

It went reluctantly, in braided silver channels down the middle of the main street, carrying leaves and fence pickets and one entire chicken coop. People were already out in the shallows—shopkeepers sweeping mud off thresholds, children barefoot in the runoff, an old man on a balcony beating a mattress with the rhythm of a man performing a sacrament. The town smelled of wet brick and woodsmoke and, underneath, the green rot smell of a valley that had been underwater for two weeks.

The gray trucks were visible from here. Two of them, parked at the far end of the causeway road where it climbed out of the flood line, mud to their axles, engines idling. Men moved around them with the unhurried patience of people who had been paid to wait and were good at it. Through Brandt's field glasses Mara counted nine. She thought of the camp on the riverbank, the warm kettle, the wrapped instruments, the absence of tracks, and her hands went cold around the brass barrels of the glasses.

"They're waiting for the road," Wendell said beside her. "Not for us specifically. They don't know we surfaced here yet—or they suspect and can't confirm. Either way, the moment those trucks roll into town, this stops being a choice."

"Then we choose first."

Below them, Brandt's assistant ran the charter flag up the pole—a plain white banner with the archive's seal, faded nearly blank by forty years of weather. It caught the new wind and snapped taut, and Mara felt the sound of it go through the street like a struck bell. Heads turned. Doors opened. People drifted toward the steps in ones and twos, wiping their hands, murmuring.

By quarter to noon there were perhaps sixty people gathered in the flooded plaza, standing in ankle-deep water and on whatever dry ground remained, looking up at the archive with the particular wariness of people who had learned, over generations, that official buildings were where bad news lived.

Mara's mouth was dry. Wendell stood beside her with the testimony under his arm, and she saw that his hands were steady—engineer's hands, hands that had kept a leaking skiff alive through a squall and a pump barge beating through a flood—but his jaw was tight as cable.

"It's not too late to go out the back," he said.

"It was too late the day I hid in the reeds and listened to them burn my team's camp." She adjusted the oilcloth case on her shoulder. "This is just the part where I stop being quiet about it."

Brandt came out onto the portico with the great ledger under her arm and a reading stand that two assistants wrestled into place. She surveyed the crowd the way she surveyed damaged documents—with clinical pity—and began, in a voice trained by decades of cataloguing to carry without effort:

"Citizens of Harrow Bend. By right of Article Four of the Provincial Archive Charter, a public deposit will now be read and witnessed. The deposit consists of hydrographic surveys, land records, and sworn testimony concerning the Corvane Land Syndicate, its acquisition of riparian water rights, its construction of unauthorized diversion levees, and its actions against a government survey team in the Kessel valley." She let that settle. Rain-washed faces tilted upward. Somewhere in the crowd a woman said, *the survey team,* and someone else said, *they found one alive?* "The depositors are Mara Voss, cartographer, and Wendell Underhill, engineer of rails."

The name went through the crowd like current through wire. Mara heard it repeated—*Underhill, Underhill*—and saw old men exchange looks, and understood that in a river town, the engineer who had once testified for the fishing families against the dam company was a story people still told their children.

Wendell heard it too. She felt him go still beside her.

"Read," he said to Brandt, hoarsely. "Before I lose my nerve."

Brandt opened the ledger, and began.

* * *

She got through the charter preamble and the first deposition page before the trucks came.

Mara heard them before she saw them—engines climbing the causeway road, laboring, four of them now, gray paint streaked with dried flood-mud. The crowd heard them too, and turned as one body, and the plaza went quiet in that specific way towns go quiet when trouble arrives wearing an official face.

The trucks halted at the edge of the plaza in a rough arc. Nine men became fourteen. They wore gray coats and carried themselves with the loose readiness of hired muscle pretending to be clerks, and the one who stepped down from the lead truck was broad and neat and smiling, and Mara recognized him from the quarry cave—she had described him to Wendell a dozen times—the syndicate's senior man in the valley, a lawyer named Dreele whose smile never reached anything above his cheekbones.

"Citizens," Dreele called out, spreading his hands, walking forward through the shallow water with his trousers held clear of the flood like a man stepping through a garden. "There's been a misunderstanding. These two individuals are wanted in connection with theft of corporate property and obstruction. We're simply here to take them into custody. Nobody else needs to be involved. Go home, dry out, and let the law work."

"This *is* the law working," Brandt said from the portico, without lowering her voice or her gaze from the ledger. "Article Four. Public deposit. Any citizen may attend. You're attending. Welcome."

A ripple of laughter went through the crowd—nervous, but real—and Dreele's smile tightened at its hinges.

"Madam Archivist, I have a warrant—"

"For two fugitives. Not for interrupting a lawful public proceeding in front of sixty witnesses." Brandt turned a page with terrible calm. "If you wish to contest the deposit, you may do so at the hearing in the provincial courthouse, which—" she glanced at Mara, and Mara understood that Brandt had spent the morning doing exactly what archivists do best, "—has been petitioned this morning and docketed for the week's end. Everything read here today will be there. Continue, Miss Voss."

Mara stepped forward to the reading stand. Her heart was slamming. Fourteen men, sixty townspeople, water everywhere, and the trucks' engines still idling like held breath. She opened the oilcloth case and lifted out the restored maps, and held the first one high so the light caught it—the river, the valley, the blue lines of water rights laid over the red lines of Corvane's purchases, the whole architecture of the theft made visible in ink.

"My team mapped this," she said, and her voice held. "Eleven of us. This is what they erased us for."

Dreele stopped smiling entirely. He said something low to the men by the trucks, and two of them started forward through the water—

—and that was when the river answered.

It came around the bend in the flooded channel behind the plaza: masts first, then patched sails, then hulls, thirty of them, forty, riding the last of the high water straight into the heart of Harrow Bend with the easy confidence of vessels coming home to a harbor they had used for a hundred years. Fishing boats, punt boats, the wide flat barges with their drying nets, and at their head, standing at the tiller of a barge with a repaired pump housing gleaming at its stern like a medal, a small old woman with forearms like dock rope and a voice that carried across the whole plaza without effort:

"Well, look at that. The archive flag's up. Somebody's telling the truth in Harrow Bend for the first time in years."

The boats came in and did not stop. They came alongside the flooded storefronts, tied to lamp posts and railings and each other, and the river people stepped out into the shin-deep water of the plaza—forty of them, sixty, whole families, nets over their shoulders, children perched on their hips—and they arranged themselves, without anyone ordering it, in a wide crescent between the archive steps and the gray trucks. Between the syndicate's men and the two people on the portico.

Auntie Sef planted herself at the point of the crescent, directly in Dreele's path, and looked up at him the way she might look at a fish that had made the mistake of claiming to be a bird.

"These are the ones," she said, not loudly, but every word landing like a mooring stake driven home. "The engineer who kept our pump barge and our children above the flood. The cartographer whose friends your people drowned in dry boots and burned in a river camp. You'll not take them quietly, Mr. Gray-Coat. Not from this town. Not from this river. Not while any boat of mine still floats."

Dreele looked at the crescent of river people. At the crowd of townsfolk, who had begun, slowly, to move closer to the crescent rather than away from it. At the white charter flag snapping overhead. Something worked behind his face—the arithmetic of a man discovering that the price of a thing had changed while he wasn't watching.

"You're making a mistake," he said at last, to no one in particular.

"No," said Auntie Sef. "You made it years ago, upstream, with your levees. Today everybody gets to read about it."

Mara found Wendell's hand beside hers on the portico rail. His fingers were cold and closed around hers hard, and when she looked at him he was watching the boats—the boats, the people, the impossible fact of being defended—and his eyes were bright and wrecked and disbelieving, a man seeing dry land after a long drowning.

"Miss Voss," Brandt said mildly, tapping the ledger. "Page twelve. The levee surveys. Read them aloud. All of them."

Mara turned the page, lifted her voice, and began—and the words went out over the water and the crowd and the gray trucks and the whole washed-clean valley, ink and memory and witness, while behind her the sun climbed clear for the first time in eleven days, and the dry season's first light lay gold on the river, on the boats, on the faces of everyone who had come to stand between two fugitives and the men sent to erase them.

Dreele's men did not advance again. But Dreele himself drew a folded paper from his coat, glanced at it, and smiled a new smile—thin, patient, aimed—and Mara, reading aloud about water rights and stolen land, saw it and understood:

The hearing was in five days. And Corvane had just begun to spend.
