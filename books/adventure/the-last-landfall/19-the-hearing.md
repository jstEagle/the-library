## 19. The Hearing

The courthouse at Harrow Bend smelled of wet plaster and old varnish, the way all official buildings smell after a flood—like a body that has been underwater and come back up changed. Wendell Underhill stood in the corridor outside Courtroom Two, his hands cuffed in front of him, watching water stain spread across the ceiling in the shape of a country no one had ever bothered to map.

He had imagined this moment for three years. Not like this, exactly. In his imaginings he was always running from it, always one junction ahead of the writ, always stepping off a handcar into fog. He had never once imagined walking toward it. And yet here he stood, in a borrowed shirt that Mara had pressed with a flatiron borrowed from the archive clerk, feeling strangely lighter than he had since the day the marshals first came for him.

"You don't have to say it the way we rehearsed," Mara said beside him. She'd argued her way past the bailiff on the strength of being listed as a material witness, and now she stood close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. Her hair was still damp at the temples; she hadn't been able to stop herself from checking the restored maps one last time, and the archive's leaking roof had found them. "Say it the way it happened. That's stronger."

"The way it happened took eleven years," Wendell said. "I have until lunch."

Mara almost smiled. It was the almost that he'd learned to read—the real smile lived underneath, and you had to earn your way down to it. "Corvane's lawyer will try to make you sound like a man inventing a conspiracy to save his own skin. Don't fight the frame. Just tell them what you did with your own hands. Nobody can cross-examine your hands."

A door opened somewhere down the corridor and the smell of the river came in with it—mud, fish, diesel, rain ending. Auntie Sef's people had been arriving since before dawn, thirty boats and then more than thirty, tying up along the flooded courthouse steps until the landing looked like a fair. They weren't allowed inside in any great number; the courtroom held sixty and half of those seats belonged to Corvane's hired gallery, men in gray suits who sat with the stillness of furniture. But the river people stood outside in the drizzle, and their presence came through the windows anyway, a low hum of voices, an anchor dropped against the current.

"They're still out there," Wendell said.

"Of course they are." Mara adjusted his collar with two brisk tugs, the way she'd once straightened a surveyor's transit. "You fixed their pump barge. You pulled their children out of the flood. You think they'd miss the day the law finally looks upstream?"

The bailiff touched his elbow. "Underhill. It's time."

* * *

Courtroom Two had a ceiling fan that turned without conviction and a judge named Oyelaran who had been brought in from three provinces away precisely because she owed nothing to anyone local. She was a compact woman with reading glasses on a chain and the patient expression of someone who had spent thirty years listening to powerful men explain why the rules didn't apply to them. When Wendell was led to the witness stand, she looked at him over the glasses for a long moment—not unkindly, but thoroughly, the way you'd inspect a bridge before trusting it with weight.

"Wendell Marcus Underhill," the court clerk read. "You are appearing today as a cooperating witness under provisions of the Provincial Evidence Act, having surrendered yourself voluntarily into custody on the morning of—" the clerk paused, checked a paper, and something in his voice shifted, "—the first day of the dry season."

"That's correct," Wendell said.

"For the record," Judge Oyelaran said, "you understand that surrendering yourself does not immunize you. Charges pending against you remain pending. Do you understand?"

"I do, Your Honor." He folded his cuffed hands in his lap, an engineer's habit of keeping the tools where he could see them. "I'd like to state for the record that I'm not asking for immunity. I'm asking to be heard. There's a difference, and I think this province has spent a long time confusing the two."

A rustle went through the gray suits. At the Corvane table, the lead counsel—a silver-haired man named Aldous Frey whose cufflinks probably cost more than the courthouse roof—made a small note with the air of a man writing down something he intended to use as a knife later.

The provincial prosecutor, a tired-eyed woman named Inspector Dagny Rook who had driven through the night from the capital, rose first. "Mr. Underhill. Let's begin at the beginning. Eleven years ago, you were the senior engineer on the Verrin River Line survey. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And what was the purpose of that survey?"

"To establish the right-of-way and the hydrological profile for the eastern extension of the line. Where the rails would run, and—just as important—where the water would go when we built embankments and culverts. A railroad through river country is mostly a negotiation with water. You can't just lay steel. You have to promise the river somewhere to be."

"And did you complete that survey?"

"I completed it. I signed it. And eighteen months later I watched it get rewritten." He kept his voice level, the voice he'd used a thousand times explaining load tolerances to men who wanted to hear a different number. "The final filed version moved the proposed levee alignments. On my original drawings, the flood easements protected the downstream settlements—the fishing communities along the lower bend. The version Corvane Land Syndicate filed redirected the easements upstream. Their land upstream. Which meant that in a hundred-year flood, the water would be pushed onto the downstream parishes instead. Onto land Corvane didn't own yet—but intended to buy cheap, because who pays full price for ground that's scheduled to drown?"

"You're saying the syndicate altered engineering documents to engineer a disaster."

"I'm saying someone altered them, and every alteration made money for Corvane and cost lives downstream, and I was the convenient man to blame when it started coming apart. My signature was on the originals. Easy to argue I'd sold them, or botched them, or both. The inquiry found 'irregularities attributable to the supervising engineer.' That was me. I lost my license, my pension, and roughly my entire existence in a single afternoon. So I did what guilty men and innocent ones both do." He looked down at his hands. "I ran. Three years on the closed lines. I told myself staying invisible was survival. Mostly it was shame with a timetable."

Inspector Rook let the room sit with that. Then: "How did you come to be involved with the present matter? The survey team. The Voss expedition."

Wendell glanced toward Mara. She sat at the prosecution table with the restored maps stacked in front of her, each sheet sleeved in archival plastic, and beside them the waterlogged notebook, its pages separated by blotting tissue like a patient in intensive care.

"Three years ago I found their camp," he said. "Abandoned mid-meal. Kettle warm. Instruments wrapped and oiled—surveyors don't oil instruments if they're planning to leave in a hurry. Boats gone. No tracks leading out except mine going in. I'd spent three years learning how to disappear, and I knew exactly how much work disappearing takes. Somebody had done that work to six trained professionals, carefully, and left no bodies, because bodies raise questions and questions raise inquiries." His jaw tightened. "I took a field notebook out of the mud. First thing I'd stolen in my life that wasn't already stolen from somebody else."

"Objection." Aldous Frey rose, smoothing his lapels. "The witness is testifying to matters outside his knowledge. Who removed the survey team is speculation—"

"With respect, counselor," Judge Oyelaran said dryly, "your client's conduct is rather the subject of this hearing. Sit down, and let the witness speculate. Courts exist partly for that reason."

Frey sat. The gray suits around him did not move, but Wendell felt the temperature of the room change, the way air changes before a squall.

"Continue, Mr. Underhill."

* * *

He told it in order, because order was the only discipline he had left. The checkpoint that forced him onto the unmapped branch. Kessel Junction and the stationmaster's warning about gray trucks. The camp in the reeds. The woman surfacing out of the water like the river itself deciding to intervene. The viaduct, the slashed tires, the alliance neither of them had wanted. The quarry cave, where Mara had laid out the truth: her team had mapped the water rights Corvane had been quietly buying, parcel by parcel, shell company by shell company—and the map of those purchases matched, line for line, the altered flood easements from eleven years ago.

"They weren't erased because they found something new," Wendell said. "They were erased because they found the same crime, still running. Eleven years apart, same signature. Same greed wearing different paperwork."

Then Inspector Rook turned to the levees, and the room got very quiet.

"Afterwater," she said. "The flood of this past monsoon. Tell the court what you observed."

"I was there." His voice caught on it, and he let it catch; Mara had told him not to sand the edges off. "I helped repair the pump barge at the flotilla. I was on the water when the storm hit early. And afterward I walked the drowned fields with Auntie Sef's people, and I'll tell you what an engineer sees that a lawyer might miss. The flood crests were wrong. Water doesn't lie about its arithmetic. The lower bend took a crest volume far beyond what the rainfall explained—and upstream, behind the new Corvane levees, the fields stayed nearly dry. Those levees weren't built to keep water off Corvane's land. They were built to send it somewhere else. Eleven years ago on paper. This year in brick and concrete. One dead fisherman. Fields that won't grow for two seasons. Children on a breaking barge in the dark." He lifted his cuffed hands slightly, an odd, helpless gesture. "I've spent my life calculating where water goes. I know intention when I see it flowing downhill."

Frey was on his feet again before the echo settled. "Your Honor, the witness is offering engineering opinion as though it were evidence of criminal intent—"

"Actually, counselor," Judge Oyelaran said, making a note, "that is precisely what makes it evidence. An expert's reading of physical facts is admissible. What is not admissible is your client's habit of interrupting whenever the arithmetic turns inconvenient. Overruled. Continue."

* * *

The cross-examination began after the midday recess, and Aldous Frey came at Wendell the way he had promised himself he would: not with fury but with courtesy, the velvet over the crowbar.

"Mr. Underhill. You've admitted to three years as a fugitive. Evading process. Living under false names. Traveling on stolen rolling stock, I believe—a handcar?"

"A borrowed handcar," Wendell said. "Borrowed indefinitely."

A ripple of laughter from the public benches, quickly suppressed.

"You admit you stole a notebook from the survey site. You admit you fled rather than report what you found. Why should this court accept the testimony of a confessed fugitive, a disgraced engineer stripped of his credentials, over the documented record of a licensed land development firm?"

"Because I'm not asking you to take my word," Wendell said. "I'm asking you to take the water's word. Take the maps'. Ms. Voss has reconstructed the survey data independently—from memory, from the notebook, from instrument calibrations logged before the team disappeared. Two sources that never met, drawn eleven years apart, describing the same alterations. Counselor, in my trade we call that redundancy. It's how you know a measurement is true. One reading might be error. Two independent readings agreeing isn't error. It's the shape of the thing itself."

"Or," Frey said softly, turning to the bench, "two conspirators aligning their stories. Ms. Voss has every motive to fabricate—her colleagues vanished, her career destroyed, her grief in need of a villain. And Mr. Underhill needs a hero's exit from his own crimes. Isn't it convenient, Mr. Underhill, that the man blaming everyone else surrendered only when surrender became his best defense?"

For a moment Wendell said nothing. He looked at his hands, at the cuffs, at the scarred knuckles from three winters of handcar cranks and pump repairs. Then he looked at Frey, steadily.

"Counselor, you're right that I'm a compromised witness. Everything you said about me is true, and I won't waste the court's time disputing it. But ask yourself one question." He leaned forward slightly. "If Corvane is innocent—if the surveys were honest and the levees are lawful—why did your client spend eleven years buying up the exact parcels the altered easements would drown? Why did a land syndicate dispatch armed crews to erase a government survey team? And why, when a broken-down engineer and a cartographer surfaced with a notebook, did your client's lawyer offer me clean papers and cash—through a bribed fisherman—rather than simply letting the truth come out?" He straightened. "Innocence doesn't buy silence, counselor. Only guilt can afford it."

The courtroom was silent enough that Wendell heard the ceiling fan's bearing grind. Frey consulted his notes, and Wendell watched him do it—watched the silver-haired man search his prepared pages for a footing that wasn't there anymore, because the ground had been mapped too well. Frey tried twice more: once on the provenance of the restored maps, which Mara dismantled from the witness chair afterward with the calm of a woman reciting coordinates; once on the legality of the tunnel passage beneath Harrow Bend, which Judge Oyelaran dismissed with "We are determining whether laws were broken, counselor, not cataloguing every law that was."

By late afternoon, Frey's questions had grown shorter, and short questions from a defense lawyer are the sound of a structure settling—cracks propagating, load paths failing. His final attempt was almost gentle.

"No further questions," he said, and sat down, and did not look at his clients.

* * *

Judge Oyelaran adjourned for findings to be drafted overnight, but before the gavel fell she did something Wendell would remember longer than the verdict. She removed her glasses and addressed the room.

"This court notes," she said, "that the evidence in this matter entered the legal system by irregular means—a fugitive's theft, a survivor's memory, a tunnel beneath a river. Let no one mistake irregularity for illegitimacy. The law is a channel, not a source. The water comes from upstream—from witnesses, from victims, from people who stood in the rain when it was dangerous to stand anywhere at all." Her gaze moved, briefly, to the windows, where the river people still waited. "This court's task is only to give that water somewhere lawful to go. We will endeavor to do so."

The gavel fell. The bailiff took Wendell's elbow to lead him back to holding, and he went, but at the courtroom door he stopped—one second, no more—and looked back. Mara stood at the prosecution table with her hand flat on the sleeved maps, and Auntie Sef filled the aisle in her oilskin coat, and beyond her the doors stood open onto the steps and the boats and the low gray light of the dry season's first clear evening.

Mara didn't wave. She gave him the almost-smile, the earned one, and mouthed two words he couldn't hear over the crowd.

He was fairly sure they were *we won*. But there was a chance they were *they're coming*, and as the bailiff steered him down the corridor toward holding, Wendell noticed two things in quick succession: the gray suits leaving the courtroom not in ones and twos but as a single coordinated block, moving fast—and Aldous Frey speaking quietly into a telephone at the end of the hall, his eyes fixed on Wendell with an expression that had stopped being a lawyer's expression entirely.

Verdicts, Wendell knew, were just load calculations on paper. What mattered was whether the structure held overnight.

And somewhere in the building, a door he couldn't see had already begun to open.
