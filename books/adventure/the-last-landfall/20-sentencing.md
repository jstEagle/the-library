## 20. Sentencing

The courthouse at Harrow Bend had been built by men who believed in permanence—grey granite blocks quarried from the same hills that had tried to drown us all summer—and on the morning of the sentencing it stood dry and pale in the first honest light the valley had seen in weeks. The rains had broken two days before. The air smelled of wet stone and river mud and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the cook-fires along the levee where Auntie Sef's flotilla still rode at anchor, thirty boats riding a brown, swollen current with the patience of people who had outlasted worse.

Mara Voss stood on the courthouse steps with her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a ferry, because that was what she was doing, in a way. Waiting. The doors were closed to the public for the hearing itself—the magistrate had cited security concerns, which everyone present understood to mean Corvane's lawyers and their talent for finding witnesses who later found themselves unable to remember anything—but she had been permitted inside as counsel's assistant, a fiction arranged by the tired young advocate from the provincial capital who had taken the case after three others refused. She had sat through every hour of it. She had watched Wendell Underhill stand in the witness box for six hours and give himself away piece by piece, name by name, date by date, until there was nothing left of him that belonged only to himself.

Now the doors opened, and the crowd came down the steps into the light.

* * *

She found him last, because he let them take him slowly.

The constables flanked him on either side, one hand each on his elbows, not gripping so much as guiding—a courtesy, she suspected, extended to men who cooperated. He wore the same clothes he'd worn through the tunnel under the river: canvas trousers gone stiff with dried silt, a shirt that had been white once, boots that had walked more abandoned rail line than any man alive. Someone had offered him a coat. He had declined it. She understood why without asking. A coat would have been a kindness, and he seemed determined this morning to accept nothing he hadn't earned.

His hair had been cut by a barber in the archive district, an old man who worked out of a chair on his porch and charged nothing to men going where Wendell was going. It made his face look older and younger at once—the grey at his temples suddenly visible, the exhaustion around his eyes suddenly less hidden. He looked, for the first time since she'd known him, exactly his age. Forty-one. Three years spent being no one had ended, and the man who emerged from the erasure was thinner than the photographs suggested, quieter, with hands that hung at his sides like tools set down after a long shift.

He saw her halfway down the steps and stopped walking.

The constables stopped too, and waited, because they had been told about her. Everyone in Harrow Bend had been told about her by now. The cartographer who hid underwater while her colleagues were made to disappear; the woman whose maps came back from the dead in a flooded tunnel; the witness whose memory was the second copy of the truth. Auntie Sef had told the story so many times, in so many versions, embellishing freely, that half the fishing community believed Mara had breathed water for three days. She hadn't corrected them. There were worse things to be than a small legend.

"Voss," Wendell said.

"Underhill." She matched his flatness deliberately. This was not a morning for softness. Softness would have cracked him open in front of the constables, and he would have hated her for it, and she would have deserved it.

"Eighteen months," he said. "They gave me eighteen months."

"I heard."

"Obstruction. That's all they could make stick from the old business—the statute on the fraud itself ran out two years ago, can you believe that. Corvane's lawyers found the loophole before we did. But the obstruction stuck, and the cooperation weighed heavy, and the magistrate—" He stopped. His jaw worked once, the way it did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Eighteen months is nothing. I've served longer sentences in rail yards waiting for freight."

"It isn't nothing."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

The current moved around them—the crowd dispersing toward the levee, toward the cook-fires, toward the taverns where the hearing was already being retold with better drama than it had possessed in life. Somewhere in the noise a child was shouting about the pump barge, about how the engineer had fixed it, about how it had held. The story had grown overnight. By winter it would be a song.

"They're taking me upriver today," Wendell said. "There's a work facility past Kessel Junction. Old depot they converted." He almost smiled. "They're sending me back down my own map. Closed lines and all. There's a joke in there somewhere and I'm too tired to find it."

"I'll find it for you," Mara said. "I'll write it down and bring it when I visit."

"You don't have to visit."

"I know I don't."

He looked at her then—not the wary sideways assessment she'd learned to read during the monsoon, when every glance between them had been a negotiation conducted in silence over the sound of water in a leaking hull—but directly, openly, the way a man looks at something he has decided to stop pretending he doesn't see.

"Mara." Her name in his mouth sounded strange and careful, like a word in a language he was learning late. "I want to say something and I'd like you to let me finish it before you argue."

"I don't argue."

"You argue constantly. You argued with me about rivet spacing in a sinking boat."

"That was different. You were wrong about rivets."

"I was *right* about rivets—"

"Wendell." She said it gently, and he caught himself, and something passed across his face that was very close to laughter and very close to grief, and she understood that for him those two things had always lived in the same room.

He finished what he'd come down the steps to say. "Three years I stayed invisible because I thought visibility was what ruined me. And it turns out staying invisible nearly got you killed, and the river people drowned, and Corvane got everything it wanted. The only thing I ever did that mattered, I did standing in the open with my real name in my mouth." He looked out past her, at the river, at the boats. "So thank you. For making me visible. However you managed it."

"I didn't manage anything. You followed survey markers up a river because you couldn't stand an unsolved puzzle."

"That too."

* * *

They gave him twenty minutes before the transport left. The constables, who had names—Brasic, the older one, with the bad knee from the flood rescue; Oyelaran, the younger one, who had been on the barge the night the pump held—moved off a respectful distance and pretended to discuss paperwork.

Auntie Sef came up the steps leaning on a boat hook she used as a cane, and behind her came a delegation: six of the river elders, two of the women who had cooked for the whole flotilla through the worst weeks, and the children—four of them, scrubbed and solemn, dressed in their driest clothes, carrying things.

The smallest girl carried a tin plate wrapped in cloth. Inside it was fish, fried that morning, the way the river people fried it, crisp and salted and smelling of woodsmoke. "For the road," Auntie Sef said, pressing it into Wendell's hands. "Prison food will kill your spirit before it kills your body. You eat this first. You think of this fire. Eighteen months is a long time to go without a decent meal, but it is not a long time to go without remembering who fed you."

Wendell held the plate like it might break. Mara watched him try to speak and fail, watched the engineer who could calculate air pockets in a flooded tunnel by feel be undone entirely by fried fish on a tin plate.

The eldest boy handed him something else: a length of cord, braided, river-grass and scrap wire, the kind the children made for luck. "Everyone on the boats has one," the boy said. "We made yours first."

"We voted," said the smallest girl, with the gravity of someone reporting a matter of state. "Unanimous."

Wendell tied it around his left wrist immediately, right there on the steps, fumbling the knot because his hands weren't steady, and Mara looked away because some things were not hers to watch.

Auntie Sef took his arm then, and turned him slightly away from the children, and spoke low enough that Mara caught only pieces—but she caught the important ones. *"You listen to me, engineer. My husband did four years in the quarry camp for organizing the net licenses, and he came home thin and he came home angry and he came home. What kept him was letters, and visits, and knowing the river kept his place. Your place is kept. Do you understand? The river keeps what belongs to it."*

And Wendell, who had spent three years believing he belonged nowhere, who had built his entire life on the principle of never stopping longer than two nights, nodded like a man agreeing to terms he'd been negotiating his whole existence without knowing it.

* * *

Afterward, when the transport had gone—when the last sight of him was his face at the barred window of the prison van, turning backward to look at the river until the road bent and took it away—Mara stood on the courthouse steps a long time.

The advocate from the capital came out and stood beside her. He was young, maybe thirty, with ink stains on his fingers and the shell-shocked expression of a man who had expected to lose and hadn't. "Corvane's counsel filed a motion to appeal within the hour," he said. "It won't survive review. The magistrate's findings are clean, the maps corroborate, and frankly—" he glanced at her, embarrassed by his own admiration— "frankly, Miss Voss, what you restored from memory is better drafting than most licensed surveyors produce fresh. The appellate judges will see that."

"The families' suit is next," Mara said. "Against Corvane directly. For the erased team. For the levees."

"That's a bigger fight. Years, probably. They'll spend money like water to bury it."

"Then it's fortunate," Mara said, "that we know exactly how water behaves."

The advocate laughed, surprised, and went down the steps into the bright cold morning, and Mara stayed.

Below her, the river ran high with the last of the monsoon, brown and muscular, carrying branches and silt and one overturned skiff that some fisherman had already gone after. On the far bank the drowned fields were draining, silver sheets of water shrinking back into channels, revealing mud that would be the richest planting soil in the province by spring—if the levees were rebuilt honestly this time, if the water rights were honored, if everything they had bought with eighteen months and a soaked notebook and five lives that could not be bought back actually held.

She thought about the notebook. Its ink had bled in the tunnel, ghosting blue across the pages, some entries lost forever. But she had spent eleven days in the archive basement reconstructing them from memory, working by lamplight while Wendell drafted testimony ten feet away, and the strangest thing had happened: the act of redrawing the maps had returned things she hadn't known she remembered. The smell of the survey camp's coffee. Toma's laugh. The exact angle of morning light on the theodolite the day before the gray trucks came. Grief, she had learned, was just another kind of data, and data could be plotted, and plotted things could be used.

Eighteen months. Four hundred and thirty-eight days, if you counted the way engineers counted, which she now did, having absorbed the habit the way you absorb a companion's speech. She had counted worse spans. She had counted three years underwater, in a manner of speaking.

* * *

That evening she went down to the flotilla. Auntie Sef had claimed the bench at the stern of the lead boat, the one with the repaired pump barge lashed alongside, and Mara sat with her and drank the bitter tea the old woman brewed from river herbs and said nothing for a long time, which was its own kind of conversation, and one they had gotten good at.

"He asked about you," Auntie Sef said eventually. "Before the van came. Asked if you had family waiting anywhere."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him the truth. Said his family was floating on thirty boats behind me and didn't know it yet." She sipped her tea. "He didn't argue. Man's learning."

Mara smiled and looked out at the water. The first stars were coming up over the hills, sharp in the washed-clean air, and below them the lanterns of the flotilla were coming alight one by one, thirty small fires reflected in the current, a constellation anchored to the earth.

"Eighteen months," she said.

"And then?"

"And then I'll be there."

Auntie Sef studied her with the frank appraisal of a woman who had married twice, buried one husband, raised nine children not all her own, and been lied to by experts. "You'll be where?"

"At the gate. At Kessel Junction, or wherever they put him. When the sentence ends." She heard herself say it and realized she had decided it weeks ago, probably in the tunnel, possibly on the leaking boat, maybe the first night in the reeds when she'd watched a stranger refuse to steal from a dead man's camp and thought: *there. That one. That is a man who puts things back.* "I'm filing the families' suit from Harrow Bend. The archive work will take most of a year. Then the hearings. Then whatever comes after. I'll be busy. But I'll be here, and the river runs past the work facility, and I own a boat now—Sef, don't look at me like that, you gave it to me."

"I gave you a leaky skiff."

"I fixed the leaks. I learned from the best."

Auntie Sef laughed, a dry rustling sound like wind through reeds, and refilled both cups. "Eighteen months is a long time, girl. People change in eighteen months. Men especially. Prison makes some of them smaller."

"Not this one." Mara said it with total certainty, and knew it for knowledge rather than hope, because she had watched Wendell Underhill choose the larger thing three times when the smaller thing was offered—on the causeway, in the lawyer's trap, on the witness stand. "He'll come out of there the same man who tied that boy's braid-cord on his wrist in front of the constables. Maybe tired. Maybe older. But the same."

"And you'll wait."

"I don't wait," Mara said. "I plot courses. There's a difference. Waiting is passive. Plotting means you know where you're going and you've measured the current." She turned her cup slowly in her hands. "I know where I'm going."

The old woman was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and patted Mara's knee once, hard, the way she did when she meant something. "Then the river has two keepers now," she said. "Him for the lines, you for the maps. Good. Between you, you'll put this valley back together."

Far downstream, past the bend, past the drowned fields draining silver in the starlight, the rail line ran north toward Kessel Junction and the converted depot where a man was spending his first night behind walls in three years—walls he had chosen, a sentence he had accepted without bitterness, a debt he was paying not to the law but to the living. Mara looked at the dark line of the embankment where the rails caught the last light, steel threads stitching the hills together, and thought about the map in her satchel: the closed routes, the abandoned spurs, the branch lines waiting like held breath.

Somewhere in those pages was a line marked *reopened*, in her own handwriting, dated eighteen months from now.

She intended to hold herself to it.

* * *

The letter arrived eleven days later, carried up from the work facility by a supply barge and delivered to the flotilla by a grinning deckhand who had been paid in fried fish.

It was written in pencil, on the back of a requisition form, in the cramped precise hand of a man who had once lettered entire signal diagrams freehand:

*Mara —*

*They put me on maintenance detail for the old spur line. My line. The one from the map. I walked it yesterday end to end and it's all still there — the culverts, the mile markers, the cut through the ridge where the snowberry grows. I found a survey marker of yours at Mile 14. Still legible. I repainted it.*

*Tell Sef the fish lasted four days because I rationed it like a coward. Tell the boy his cord hasn't come off. Tell the little one I haven't forgotten the vote.*

*Four hundred and twenty-seven days. I counted the way you'd count.*

*Repaint the marker at the junction landing. I'll know what it means.*

*— W.*

Mara read it three times by lantern light, sitting on the gunwale of her skiff with the river talking against the hull. Then she got out her kit—the good brushes, the government-issue paint she'd been hoarding—and waited for morning.

At first light she rowed to the junction landing, found the old marker post weathered grey and half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, and knelt in the mud and scraped it clean and painted it fresh, white on brown, the surveyor's code, the coordinates, the date.

And beneath it, small, where only someone looking closely would ever find it, she painted a single new mark: an arrow, pointing upriver.

Toward the facility. Toward the man. Toward the day, four hundred and twenty-seven days out, when the current would carry her boat to the gate and the counting would finally end.

The paint dried fast in the dry season air. The river ran on. And far to the north, though she couldn't have known it yet, a requisition clerk at the work facility was reading a proposal from the provincial recovery office—one that bore the title *Reopening of Closed Rail Lines: Engineering Consultation Requested*—and would, within the week, walk it down the corridor to a cell block and ask a prisoner named Underhill if he happened to know anything about the old routes.

The map was waking up. All of it. Every line at once.
