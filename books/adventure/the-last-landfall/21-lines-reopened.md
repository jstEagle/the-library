## 21. Lines Reopened

The work detail came for him on a Tuesday in the seventh month of his sentence, when the floodwater had finally begun to let go of the low country and the air outside the workshop windows smelled of drying mud and cut grass instead of rot.

"Underhill," said the guard, a broad woman named Okafor who had long since stopped treating him like the rest of them. "Warden wants you. Bring your hands."

He wiped the grease from his fingers on a rag that had once been white and followed her through the corridors of Halloway Correctional—past the laundry steam, past the chapel where someone was practicing a hymn badly, out into the yard where the light hit him like a physical thing after four hours under fluorescent tubes. Eighteen months. He'd served seven. The arithmetic lived in his chest the way a train schedule used to live there: constant, ticking, something to be counted on.

The warden's office had been rearranged since Wendell's last visit—the one where he'd been told, in flat administrative tones, that his cooperation in the Corvane matter had been "weighed heavily" by the court, and that he would be expected to conduct himself accordingly. Now the warden's desk held not files but drawings. Large ones. Rolled and weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, spread across the blotter like a hand of cards someone had dealt carelessly.

"You're Underhill." The man behind the desk was not the warden. He was sixty-odd, sunburned to the color of old brick, wearing a canvas jacket with the provincial Recovery Authority's patch sewn crookedly at the shoulder. His handshake was a grip test and his eyes went straight to Wendell's forearms, reading the muscle there the way another man might read a résumé.

"I am."

"Dorn Vasquez. Recovery Authority, Northern Basin." He tapped the drawings without looking at them. "You drew these lines."

It wasn't a question. Wendell looked down at the topmost sheet and felt something move in him that he hadn't permitted himself to feel in years—not hope exactly, nothing so warm as hope, but recognition. The Kessel Junction spur. The branch line through the collapsed viaduct country. The flooded tunnel beneath Harrow Bend, drawn in his own cramped drafting hand three years and one lifetime ago, when the map had been a private thing, an escape route, a secret kept against the world.

"Somebody's been in my old files," Wendell said.

"Your old files were evidence," Vasquez said. "Evidence became public record after the hearing. Public record became—" he flipped the sheet over, and beneath it lay a survey overlay, fresh ink, government stamps still sharp-edged, "—became this. Forty-one miles of closed rail through the worst of the flood corridor. Every bridge washed or undermined, every culvert silted, every mile of ballast drowned for five weeks. And every road into that corridor either gone or owned by people we can't trust, which is its own kind of gone." He leaned back. "The roads are Corvane's roads, Underhill. Half the levee contracts, half the haulage contracts. We rebuild on their terms, we pay their freight rates until the province drowns in debt, and they get to decide which towns dry out first. But if the rail lines reopen—*your* lines—we route around them entirely."

"And you want me to tell you if it can be done."

"I want you to tell me what's wrong with it." Vasquez slid the overlay aside and put down a condition report, thick as a hymnal, compiled by engineers who had never walked a foot of the ground. "These men surveyed from helicopters and county records. You know these lines the way a man knows his own scars. So." He uncapped a pen and set it down between them like a challenge coin. "Tell me what's wrong with it."

Wendell pulled the report toward him and opened it, and for the next two hours the cell block, the sentence, the whole gray machinery of Halloway fell away. There was the Kessel Junction water tower marked *serviceable*, which was a lie—it had been leaning when he last saw it, and five weeks of flood scour around its footing would have finished the argument. There was the viaduct listed as *collapsed span 3, spans 1–2 intact*, which was true but useless, because span 2's piers stood on the same gravel bar the river had been eating all spring. There was the tunnel—and here Wendell stopped, pen hovering, because the report called it *flooded, structural condition unknown, recommend indefinite closure*, and he knew things about that tunnel no helicopter could have learned. He knew the sound the brickwork made under water. He knew which arches had breathed against his shoulders in the dark. He knew the air pocket near the north portal where a man could stand chest-deep and breathe, and he knew that if the brick above it was still standing—which some stubborn part of him believed it was—then the crown of the tunnel was standing too, and the tunnel was not dead but sleeping.

"The tunnel's viable," he heard himself say. "Conditionally. I'll write you the conditions."

Vasquez wrote nothing down. He just watched Wendell with the particular attention of a man confirming a purchase. "They told me you'd be worth the trip," he said. "They undersold you."

* * *

After that, Tuesdays belonged to the Recovery Authority. A guard brought Wendell to a converted storage room off the workshops—a real room, with a window and a drafting table and a lamp that didn't flicker—and each week Dorn Vasquez or one of his junior engineers arrived with new questions and left with Wendell's answers inked onto the margins of their reports. The work crept outward from the page into the world. In the eighth month, word came back that the Kessel Junction tower had been braced and guyed according to his sketch, and that the crew chief had radioed in to say whoever drew it had saved them two days of guessing. In the ninth month, a photograph arrived, passed along the chain of custody from warden to guard to prisoner with the solemnity of contraband: a work train—actual locomotive, actual flatcars loaded with ballast—standing on reopened track at the mouth of the viaduct country, with men in hard hats swarming the gap where span 3 had been.

Wendell kept the photograph folded inside his copy of the condition report. At night, lying in his bunk listening to the institutional sounds of the block—the pipes, the coughing, the far-off door—he unfolded it and looked at the rails running east into the hills, and he thought about what a strange instrument a map was. Three years ago those lines had been his alone, drawn in secret, useful only for flight. Now crews of strangers walked them in daylight, hauling stone toward the drowned towns, and his name appeared in the file headers in plain type: *UNDERHILL, W. — CONSULTING ENGINEER (INMATE WORK DETAIL)*. Visible. On the record. It should have terrified him. Some mornings it still did, in a small cold way, like a draft under a door. But mostly it felt like standing on solid ballast after years of walking ties in the dark.

The other prisoners noticed. Not the details—they didn't get details—but the change. A lifer named Braddock, who ran the metal shop and missed nothing, summed it up one evening over the workbench.

"You sleep different now," Braddock said, filing a bracket with slow strokes. "First months, you slept like a man listening for dogs. Now you sleep like a man with somewhere to be tomorrow."

Wendell didn't answer. But he turned the observation over for a long time afterward, the way he'd turn a bearing housing, looking for the flaw in it, and never found one.

* * *

Mara came in the eleventh month, on a gray Thursday with rain threatening and not delivering.

He knew before the guard announced it. He was at the drafting table when the visiting-room door opened, and something in the quality of the footsteps—quick, even, a walker's stride and not a shuffler's—reached him half a second before her voice did.

"They let you have a window," Mara Voss said. "I'm almost offended. My office has a window too, now, and I fought six months for it."

He turned and she was standing in the doorway with a document case under one arm and rain-damp in her hair, and she looked—this was the thing that caught him, that he carried back to his cell that night—she looked *settled*. Not soft; he doubted Mara Voss would ever be soft. But rooted. The wariness that had lived in her face when he first saw her across the ruined survey camp, watching him from the reeds with a survivor's arithmetic in her eyes, had been replaced by something more expensive: the look of a person with work worth doing and the standing to do it.

"You got the certification," he said.

"Provincial cartographer, second class." She sat down across the table and set the document case between them like a piece of ordnance. "Third class, technically, until I finish the field hours. Auntie Sef says I can make up the difference surveying the fishing grounds, so don't congratulate me yet." She glanced at the drafting table, at the overlay pinned there, at his own handwriting marching across the margins. "Vasquez shows my maps to his crews, you know. Your annotations sitting right on top of my contour lines. It's the closest we've come to working together since the boat, and neither of us signed off on it."

"How is she? Sef?"

"Meaner than ever. The levee suit made her famous—there are children up and down the river who think she's a general." Mara's mouth did something complicated, grief and pride sharing the same ground. "She sends you fish. She has been sending you fish since the fourth month. I don't know how she gets it past the mailroom and I've decided not to ask."

"She sent dried eel in October," Wendell said. "It was excellent. I shared it with a man serving twenty years for arson and he wept."

For a moment they just looked at each other, and the visiting room's noise—the other tables, the vending machine, a child somewhere negotiating loudly for coins—seemed to arrange itself around their silence rather than through it.

Then Mara opened the document case.

"We filed yesterday," she said. She laid the papers out with a cartographer's precision, squared edges, aligned corners. "The families. All nine of them—every member of the survey team. Wrongful death, conspiracy, destruction of property, and the water-rights fraud itself, which the province joined as co-plaintiff after your testimony gave the attorney general enough spine to stand up straight." She tapped the top sheet. "Corvane's response came back this morning. Their lawyers are calling our maps 'unverified reproductions.' Calling the notebook 'a document of uncertain provenance.' They're going to fight the admissibility of everything, Wendell. Everything except one thing."

He looked at her. Outside the window, the threatened rain had begun at last, fine and silver, streaking the glass.

"They can't call your testimony unverified," Mara said. "You were there. You're the engineer whose survey Corvane used to value the lands they stole, the engineer they framed to bury the question of who else had surveyed those lands, and the engineer who rode their closed lines for three years and found what they'd buried. Your sworn statement is already in the record. Every map they attack, they attack through you. Which means—" and here she paused, and he saw her choose the words carefully, the way she chose contour intervals, "—which means for the next year, maybe two, you're going to be deposed. From in here, through counsel, but deposed. Corvane's lawyers will come at you with everything. Your debts. Your flight. The theft of the map itself. They'll try to make the jury see a fugitive instead of a witness."

"They'd be right to try," Wendell said quietly. "That's what I was."

"That's what you *were*." Mara leaned forward. "Do you know what Auntie Sef says about that? She says a river doesn't apologize for the bends it took getting to the sea. She says it a lot. It's insufferable." But her eyes were steady on his, and underneath the Sef-ism was the real message, delivered the way she always delivered the important things: plainly, without decoration. "The families asked me to tell you themselves, but half of them can't afford the trip and the other half can't walk past a guard station without shaking. So I'm telling you. They know what it costs you, being visible again. They filed anyway. Because you made it possible."

Wendell looked down at the papers. Nine names. He had carried the survey team's absence for a long time—the warm kettle, the wrapped instruments, the boats gone and no tracks leading out—and he had assumed that weight was his alone to carry forever, the way he'd assumed the map was his alone to use. Both assumptions had been wrong. The families had taken up their own dead. The province had taken up the maps. And the lines he'd ridden in flight were carrying ballast and lumber toward the drowned towns under other men's hands.

"I'll sit for whatever depositions they want," he said. "Every one. Let them bring the debts, the warrants, all of it. I'll answer it on the record." He met her eyes. "You should know—I'm good on the record now. I've had practice."

"I heard." Something warmed in her face, quickly controlled. "Braddock writes to me, you know. Don't ask how. He says you redraw the pump specifications for the shop's hydraulic press from memory and made it run quieter, and that you've started teaching the younger ones drafting on Thursdays, and that you've stopped flinching when doors open."

"He talks too much."

"He also says," Mara went on, relentless, mild, "that you keep a photograph of a work train folded inside a government report, and that you look at it at night."

Wendell exhaled slowly through his nose. "I'm going to have words with Braddock."

"Do that." She began gathering the papers, squaring them back into the case, and then paused with her hand flat on the topmost sheet. "There's one more thing. The recovery authority approved the tunnel assessment. Harrow Bend, north portal. Vasquez wanted to lead it himself, but the province insisted on an independent engineer—someone with documented knowledge of the structure." She looked up. "They asked for you. Obviously they can't have you. But they asked, and Vasquez told them the man who mapped that tunnel under flood conditions, in the dark, with a leaking skiff and no instruments, was the only engineer alive qualified to certify it. They're putting his recommendation in the file. With your name on it. When the parole board reviews your case next spring, that file is going to be thick."

"Spring," Wendell said. The word came out careful, tested, like weight on ice.

"Eleven more months." Mara stood, slinging the document case over her shoulder, and for a moment she was again the woman in the reeds—watchful, exact—but the watchfulness was aimed outward now, at the world, at Corvane, at the work ahead, and not at him. Never at him. "The river will be high when you come out. Sef says the fishing will be extraordinary. She says it like a threat."

At the door she stopped, hand on the frame, and turned halfway back.

"Wendell. The suit goes to trial in the autumn. Corvane's lawyers are going to come here before that—they'll want to rattle you early, find the crack in you before the deposition. When they do—" she smiled then, brief and real and a little fierce, "—remember that you're the only man in this building who knows exactly how much longer they have. That's a kind of freedom too."

Then she was gone, her quick even stride fading down the corridor, and Wendell sat alone at the drafting table with the rain writing long silver sentences down the window glass.

He stayed a while after the guard told him time was up. Then he rolled the Harrow Bend overlay, squared its edges the way Mara squared her maps, and set it upright in the rack beside the others—forty-one miles of closed line, waiting, patient as track bed, ready to carry whatever came next.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and eleven months away, at a junction where the rails met the water, something was already being built to receive him.
