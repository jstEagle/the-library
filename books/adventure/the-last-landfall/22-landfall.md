## 22. Landfall

The train came down out of the hills at dawn, and Wendell Underhill rode it like a man riding a horse he no longer trusted—hands loose, weight easy, ready for anything.

Eighteen months. He had counted them the way he had once counted miles: in the body, in the bones, in the slow accumulation of days that could not be outrun and did not need to be. The work camp at Ferrow Creek had been a low gray place of gravel and ledger books, but it had been a place, fixed on the earth, with a cot he returned to every night and a name stenciled over his bunk that was his own. He had slept better there than he had in three years of running. That fact still embarrassed him, so he kept it mostly to himself.

The train was not one of his. It ran on rails he had never surveyed, through country he knew only from the wrong side of a window, and the locomotive hummed with the smooth indifference of new money spent by people who had never heard of Corvane or cared what it buried. But when the conductor came through collecting tickets, Wendell handed his over without flinching, and when the man glanced at his face and then at the paper, nothing happened. No checkpoint. No gray truck idling beyond the platform lights. Just a ticket, torn twice, handed back with a nod.

He watched the country change as they descended. The flood scars were still there—he would carry those images longer than any sentence—but the water had made its peace with the land for now. Rice stood green in the drowned fields, planted late and growing fast, and the river ran high and brown and busy between levees that were not Corvane's. Men worked a culvert below the line with shovels and a winch, and Wendell found himself reading the grade of their ditch, the fall of the ground, the way the water would move in the next big rain—and then caught himself and stopped, because it was not his problem anymore, and because it was, a little, always, and both of those things could be true.

Kessel Junction announced itself before it appeared: first the smell of the river, mud and fish and woodsmoke; then the water tower, repainted since he'd last seen it, the town's name in letters tall enough to read from the far bend; then the yards themselves, four tracks where there had been two, a new signal bridge, fresh ballast. The closed lines were opening. He had known this—had drawn half the plans himself, from a table in the prison workshop, his old map unrolled under a guard's indifferent eye while engineers from the provincial office asked him questions and wrote down his answers as though the answers mattered. His map, once his escape route, once his curse, now a public document with his name in the corner of the title block. *Surveyed by W. Underhill.* They had offered to take the credit. He had said no. It was the only vanity he had left, and he found he did not mind spending it.

The train slowed. Brakes sang. And Kessel Junction came alongside the window the way towns do in early light—unhurried, half-awake, smoke standing straight up from the bakery chimney because there was no wind yet.

He stepped down onto the platform with everything he owned in one canvas bag, and the bag weighed almost nothing, and that was its own strange freedom. Eighteen months ago he had stepped off a handcar into this same town with a stolen map and a rule: never stop longer than two nights anywhere. Two nights. That had been the whole architecture of his life, the load-bearing wall of it. Never be anywhere long enough to be found, long enough to be owed, long enough to be missed.

A woman was walking up the platform toward him, and she was not hurrying either.

Mara Voss looked older and younger at once—the way people do when they have stopped being afraid. Her hair was shorter. She wore surveyor's boots and a canvas coat with a pencil behind each ear, and she carried a leather tube slung across her back the way other people carried rifles, which is to say: like something essential. She stopped three feet away, looked him up and down with the same measuring attention she gave a contour line, and said:

"You look terrible."

"I've been told I clean up well."

"By whom?"

"Nobody credible." He set the bag down. His hands did not quite know what to do, so he put them in his pockets like a man on a platform waiting for a train, which was, technically, what he was. "You filed the suit."

"We filed the suit. Your deposition opened it." She said it plainly, without ceremony, but her eyes were doing something else, some arithmetic of relief she would never say aloud. "Corvane's lawyers have been in court eleven weeks. Their levee permits are suspended pending review. Two of the directors are indicted. The third one fled to the coast and is discovering that money does not buy boats as fast as it used to." She paused. "Your testimony is taught now. Did you know that? At the survey school, in the second-year ethics unit. *The Underhill Precedent.* What an engineer owes to the land and the people on it."

"That's the most embarrassing thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It gets worse. There's a portrait."

"There is not a portrait."

"There's a charcoal sketch in the hallway. You look very serious and slightly damp."

Wendell laughed—a real laugh, from the bottom of him, the kind he had not had room for in years—and Mara smiled the small dry smile that was all she ever gave away for free, and just like that the eighteen months folded up small, the way distance does when you finally arrive.

* * *

They walked down through the town together, and the town was different, or he was, and he suspected it was mostly him.

Kessel Junction had grown along the river the way towns do when the water becomes a road again instead of a threat. New pilings. A covered market. The stationmaster's old depot restored, glass in the windows, a schedule board with actual trains on it—two a day now, three on market days. The old man himself was dead; Wendell learned this at the bakery, from his granddaughter, who ran the place now and refused payment for two rolls and a pot of coffee because, she said, her grandfather had talked about the engineer who knew the closed lines until the end of his life, and had always insisted he'd get to shake his hand. She shook it. Wendell let her. He was learning to let people do things like that.

But the true center of the town had moved to the water, and the water was where Auntie Sef's fleet waited.

He saw them from the top of the levee road: thirty boats, maybe more, moored along the flooded willows in the loose, living arrangement of a community that has never trusted a dock. Fishing skiffs, houseboats, the great flat pump barge riding heavy and proud at the center of it all with its new engine housing gleaming—his engine housing, rebuilt to his drawings, running sweet, he could hear it from here, a low even throb like a heart that had decided to keep going. Children ran the decks. Laundry hung between masts. Smoke rose from cooking fires, and the smell of it—fish and rice and river greens—reached him on the levee and did something to his chest that he chose not to examine closely.

"They're waiting," Mara said.

"For me?"

"Don't be dense."

They went down the landing in the gold of mid-morning, and the flotilla saw them coming the way water finds a crack. Word moved boat to boat faster than they walked—Wendell could see it happen, heads turning, a shout, a child dispatched ahead at a run—and by the time his boots hit the lowest plank of the main walkway, the pump barge's deck was lined with people, and Auntie Sef stood at the rail in the front of them like a figurehead that had decided to become an admiral.

She had not shrunk. If anything the months had consolidated her, the way a river consolidates after flood, carrying less and meaning more. She looked down at him for a long moment, and the whole flotilla held its breath with her, and Wendell Underhill—who had faced gray trucks and black water and collapsing brickwork and the inside of a courtroom with his freedom on the scale—stood on a plank and felt his eyes sting.

"You're late," she said.

"Eighteen months late."

"The coffee's been cold twice a week since spring." She came down the gangway herself, which she did for no one, and took his face in both hands, and studied him the way she studied weather coming upriver. Then she kissed his forehead, once, hard, the way you seal a letter. "Welcome home, engineer."

And the flotilla broke into noise—cheering, gulls lifting off the rigging, someone banging a pot lid, children pouring down onto the walkway to grab his sleeves and haul him aboard, and Wendell let himself be hauled, laughing, stumbling, the canvas bag abandoned on the planks because everything he owned weighed almost nothing and everything that mattered weighed several tons and floated.

On the barge deck, the crew of the pump showed him what they'd done with his design—improvements, real ones, a strainer modification he would never have thought of—and asked his opinion with the particular gravity of people who already know they are right. The cook pressed a bowl into his hands before he'd finished greeting anyone. A girl of about nine, one of the ones who had been aboard the night the barge broke loose in the storm, stood in front of him with the fierce formality of a child delivering memorized lines and informed him that she was going to be an engineer, and that he was going to teach her, and that this was not a question.

"Yes," Wendell said. "All right. Yes."

* * *

Evening came down on the river the color of hammered copper, and the flotilla lit itself lamp by lamp until the whole reach of water was strewn with floating lights, a constellation that had come down to live among the reeds.

They ate on Sef's largest deck, forty people and more, and the talk was of ordinary things—prices, weather, a wedding in the fall, whose engine was running rough—which Wendell understood, gradually, was the point. Nobody asked him about the trial. Nobody asked about prison. The story was finished; they had all lived inside it; what remained was Tuesday. He had spent three years being a rumor and eighteen months being a case file, and here, in the space of one meal, he had become a neighbor, which was the only rank he had ever wanted.

Later, when the fires burned low, he and Mara sat on the bow of the pump barge with their legs hanging over the black water, watching the current carry the reflected lamps downstream in long ribbons.

"The archive sent word," Mara said. "The restored maps are certified. Full evidentiary standing. They want them for the permanent collection." She turned the leather tube in her hands, the one she had carried across half the province, through floods and tunnels and a war fought with lawyers. "I told them they could have copies. The originals stay with the river people. Seemed right."

"It is right."

"And there's this." She passed him a folded sheet of paper, official stock, a provincial crest at the top. He read it twice. The rail authority was offering him a position. Resident engineer, Kessel Junction district. The closed lines, reopening—one by one, on his surveys, under his signature. A desk. A home address. A name on a door, if he wanted a door.

"They know my record," he said. "They know what I served time for."

"They know exactly what you served time for. That's why they're offering." Mara looked out at the water. "Turns out the province would rather have an honest man with a past than a liar with a clean one. Took them long enough."

Wendell folded the paper carefully and put it in his coat, over his heart, which was where he had carried the stolen map for three years, and noticed the symmetry only after, and decided not to mention it.

Below them, the river moved in the dark, high with the season, steady, going somewhere. He thought about the man who had ridden a rusted handcar down an abandoned spur with a rule about two nights, and how that man had believed safety was a kind of speed, and how wrong he had been, and how expensive the lesson, and how worth it. Safety was this. A deck that expected your weight. A bowl kept warm. A girl who had decided you would teach her. A woman beside you who had seen you at your worst in a leaking boat and chosen, against all evidence, to stay.

"Mara," he said.

"Hm."

"The hearing. When I surrendered. On the courthouse steps—you asked me why I wasn't afraid." He had never answered her. Eighteen months was long enough to draft an answer. "It was because of the boats. I heard them coming upriver before the police arrived. Thirty of them. And I thought: whatever happens in that building, somebody came for me. Nobody had ever come for me before. So whatever they did to me after, I'd already won."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then she leaned her shoulder against his, and the barge rocked gently under them, and the river said what rivers say, which is everything and nothing.

"Well," she said. "Now you know how we felt. Watching you walk up those steps."

* * *

He woke before dawn, as he always did now, some habit of the camps that had outlived its purpose and stayed on like an old dog. He climbed to the barge roof with coffee and sat in the dark while the east went from iron to ash to rose.

The mist lay on the water in long banks, and out of it, upstream, came a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: a train whistle, far off, crossing the high trestle above the junction. One long note, then two short. The morning local, taking the curve where the old viaduct had fallen, running on track that had been closed for six years and open for six weeks, carrying mail and freight and a handful of passengers toward places that were, as of this season, reachable again.

Wendell raised his cup to it, an engineer's salute, engine to engine.

Then he heard footsteps on the deck below—Mara, in her boots, carrying the leather tube and a rolled chart, climbing up to join him with two pencils behind her ear and the sunrise catching her face—and she spread the chart out on the roof between them, weighting the corners, and it was a survey plat of the upper valley with blank space at the northern edge where nobody had ever bothered to look.

"River's dropping two inches a day," she said. "In three weeks the back channels will be passable. There's country up there no one's mapped since before the war. The province wants it done properly." She handed him a pencil. "I told them I knew someone."

Wendell took it. It was warm from her hand.

"Two nights?" she asked, one eyebrow up, testing the old joke, the old wound, the old rule.

He looked north, where the mist was lifting off the water and the land was coming into the light—his land now, in the only way land ever belongs to anyone, which is slowly, and with company.

"No," Wendell Underhill said. "I'm done counting."
