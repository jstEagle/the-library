## 1. Dead Water

The freighter had been dead for nine years, and it smelled like it.

Petra Galloway hung in the green murk off the port bow, her torch cutting a cone through water the color of old tea, and listened to the Cormorant's engine idling somewhere far above. Thirty meters down, the *Meridian Star* lay on its side in the silt like a beached whale nobody had bothered to bury. Her hull was a garden of soft coral and rust weep-stains, orange streaks running down the steel like old blood. Somewhere inside her, wedged between the number two hold and the engine room bulkhead, were four tons of bronze fittings and a propeller shaft worth more than Petra's boat.

More than her boat was worth on paper, anyway. The bank had a different valuation.

"Petra." Teo's voice crackled through the comm, tinny and close. "Survey vessel just cleared the headland. Navy gray, twin screws, moving slow. You've got maybe twenty minutes before they're overhead."

"I need twelve," she said.

"You've got eight if you want a margin."

She didn't answer that. Margins were for people with fuel to burn and hulls that didn't leak. She kicked for the breach in the freighter's side where the divers before her—her divers, Marisol and Kofi—had already rigged the cutting slings around the shaft coupling. The bronze caught her light and threw it back warm as candle flame. Nine years underwater and still beautiful. That was the thing about salvage: everything down here was dead, but nothing down here lied about what it was worth.

Unlike boats. Unlike men.

"Slings are set," she said. "Marisol, Kofi, surface slow. Teo, bring us over and take up the load."

"Aye." A pause. "Petra. They're not surveying. They're holding station off the point. Like they're waiting."

Waiting. Everything in these waters was waiting—the navy, the creditors, the monsoon season rolling down from the north like a debt collector with a calendar. She looked up through the water column at the wavering silver disc of the sky and felt the cold press in against her ears, patient as arithmetic.

"Lift it," she said.

* * *

The Cormorant came up out of the swell like something being born reluctantly. Forty years old, sixty feet of riveted stubbornness, her paint gone the gray of dishwater and her crane arm patched with plate steel from three different wrecks. When the winch took the strain, the whole deck shuddered, and Petra heard the sound she'd been hearing for two months now—a low groan from below the waterline, from the seam aft of the keel that the yard in Port Callas had quoted her eleven thousand to fix.

Eleven thousand. They'd made four hundred and sixty today, gross, before diesel and dive gas and Marisol's medical fund, which wasn't really a fund so much as an envelope of cash that kept getting thinner.

The shaft coupling broke the surface, streaming, and swung inboard on the crane. Kofi guided it down onto the deck cradle, grinning under his mask, teeth white in his salt-cracked face. "Bronze don't rot, boss. Bronze forgives."

"Bronze doesn't pay mooring fees either," Petra said, but she let herself grin back. It was hard not to, with Kofi. He was twenty-six and had been diving since he was fourteen and treated every wreck like a cathedral he'd been given permission to loot.

Marisol pulled her hood back and shook out her braids, squinting east. "How long do we have?"

Teo was already at the rail with his binoculars. He was fifty-one, built like a dock bollard, with a beard going iron-gray and hands that could splice cable in the dark. He'd been with Petra's father before he'd been with Petra, which meant he'd watched her make every mistake of her life and catalogued them all without comment. "Twenty minutes," he said. "Maybe less if they've got a helicopter."

"They won't fly in this cloud ceiling." Petra stripped her gloves. "Take us out along the reef line, low wake. We'll be behind Saddle Island before they finish their coffee."

The Cormorant turned for home, and Petra went aft to look at the water coming in.

It always came in. Every boat takes some water; hers took more than most. But lately the bilge pump had been cycling every eleven minutes instead of every forty, and this morning there'd been a new stain on the stringer, dark and fresh, down near the garboard seam. She crouched at the inspection hatch and watched the bilge water slosh, oily and gray, and did the math again even though she'd done it twice at breakfast. Diesel. Food. Dive gas. Marisol's next round of treatment, which the clinic wanted paid in advance now, because they'd learned what the Cormorant's account looked like.

Four hundred and sixty dollars. Eleven thousand for the hull. And somewhere above them, a navy vessel holding station over a wreck they had no legal claim to, because the waters were disputed and the paperwork was a fiction everyone agreed to enforce whenever it suited them.

"Boss." Kofi at her shoulder, quieter now. "We good?"

"We're good."

He nodded and went forward, and Petra stayed crouched at the hatch a moment longer, watching the water rise and fall, rise and fall, like breathing.

* * *

They tied up at Fisherman's Reach just before dusk, in the shadow of the cannery ruins, where the harbor master asked no questions and the mooring was cheap because the pilings were rotten. The navy vessel never came after them. It didn't need to. Its presence was the message: *we see you, we know what you are, stay small.*

Petra sold the bronze to Yusuf at the scrap yard for less than it was worth, because Yusuf paid cash and cash was oxygen. Four hundred and sixty became three hundred and ten after fuel. She stood at the counter while he counted it out and thought about her father, who had built the Cormorant's business on the principle that a salvor's word was worth more than a contract, and who had died owing money to people who did not believe in words.

Back at the dock, the others had already turned in. The boat smelled of diesel and instant noodles and the antiseptic tang of Marisol's medication. Petra sat in the wheelhouse with a chart spread out and a red pen in her hand, circling wrecks she couldn't reach and crossing off jobs she couldn't take, when she heard footsteps on the gangway that didn't belong to any of her crew.

She knew the walk before she saw him. She'd have known it across a crowded stadium, across a decade, across the grave of everything they'd once been to each other.

Dario Voss came up the wheelhouse steps and stopped in the doorway like a man posing for a portrait. Expensive rain jacket, city boots ruined by salt, jaw shaved clean. Older, of course. They both were. But the eyes hadn't changed—pale, quick, always calculating the angle of approach.

"Petra."

"Dario." She didn't get up. "This dock's private."

"It's a rotten pier behind a cannery nobody's used since the nineties." He glanced around the wheelhouse—at the mildewed chart, the taped-over gauge, the envelope of Marisol's cash sitting on the dash where Petra had forgotten it—and something moved behind his eyes that she chose to read as pity, because reading it as anything else would have required violence. "I hear the Cormorant's taking water."

"The Cormorant's fine."

"The Cormorant is one bad sea state from becoming a salvage job itself. Which, frankly, would be poetic." He stepped inside uninvited and put a folded paper on the chart table. "Triple rate. Hazard pay on top. Cash advance tonight, half up front."

Petra looked at the paper without touching it. "What's the job?"

"Recovery. Single item. Deep water, difficult conditions, tight window."

"What's the item?"

"Does it matter? At triple rate?"

"It matters."

Voss smiled, and it was the same smile from eleven years ago, from the funeral, from the graveside where he'd shaken her hand with gloves on and said *she was the best of us* like a man reciting lines. "It matters to you," he said. "That's always been your problem, Petra. You price things in feelings. I'm pricing this in money. Enough money to fix your hull, pay your crew, cover your friend's treatment, and still eat next winter. All you have to do is say yes and not ask questions."

Outside, the rain started, fat drops ticking on the wheelhouse roof. Petra thought about the bilge pump cycling every eleven minutes. She thought about Marisol's envelope. She thought about Ruth Okonkwo, though she couldn't have said why—Ruth had been dead eleven years and had nothing to do with this man standing in her wheelhouse smelling of cologne and secrets.

"No," she said.

Voss nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd expected. "Pride," he said. "It's expensive pride, Petra. I hope you can afford it." He left the paper on the chart table anyway, squared its corners neatly, and went down the gangway into the rain.

Petra sat looking at the chart for a long time. Then she picked up the paper, read the coordinates printed at the top, and felt the floor of her stomach drop away.

She knew those coordinates. Every diver on this coast knew them. Eleven years ago she'd stood on a headland in the rain and watched the search boats grid a patch of empty water, and she'd sworn on Ruth Okonkwo's grave that no crew of hers would ever touch it.

The Kestrel.

She folded the paper into quarters, then eighths, then crushed it in her fist, and carried it topside to throw it in the sea. But when she got to the rail, the gangway lights were on and Teo was standing at the foot of them, talking quietly into his phone, and he had the particular stillness of a man closing a deal.

He ended the call as she came down. He didn't hide the phone. That was the worst part—he didn't even try.

"Teo," she said. "Tell me that wasn't him."

Teo looked at her the way he'd looked at her father's coffin, steady and sorrowful and immovable. "Advance clears at midnight," he said. "Fuel in the morning. We sail with the tide."

"Teo—"

"Eleven thousand for the hull, Petra. Six weeks of Marisol's treatment. Two months of wages I owe these kids." His voice didn't rise. It never rose. "You swore your oath. I heard you. I stood beside you." He tucked the phone away and looked past her at the black water slapping the pilings. "But oaths don't pump bilges. Somebody had to keep us alive long enough to feel guilty about it."

Petra stood in the rain with the crushed paper dissolving in her fist, ink bleeding blue down her wrist like a wound, and understood that the job she had refused had already begun—that it had begun the moment Voss walked up the gangway, or perhaps eleven years ago, or perhaps the day the Kestrel went down with Ruth aboard and the truth sank with her.

"We'll talk at dawn," Teo said, and climbed the gangway into the light.
