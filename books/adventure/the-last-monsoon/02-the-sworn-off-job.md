## 2. The Sworn-Off Job

Teo didn't come to the wheelhouse right away. Petra gave him an hour, watching him through the glass as he coiled lines on the aft deck with the exaggerated care of a man putting off a conversation. He'd always been like that. Twenty-two years of salvage work together, and she could read his silences the way divers read current—by what they bent around.

The Cormorant sat low in the water at her berth, riding heavier than she should. Petra had spent the morning with a flashlight in the bilge, tracing the crack along the port seam with her thumb like a doctor palpating a wound. Three days of welding, maybe four if the yard could fit them in. Four days of yard fees she didn't have. The account stood at eleven thousand pesos and change, and the crew of six had to eat off it until the next job.

Which was the problem. There was no next job. The navy survey vessel, *Almirante Iriarte*, had been sniffing up and down the coast for two weeks, and every charter operator within two hundred miles had gone cautious. Nobody wanted to dive while a gunboat was taking photographs of their transom.

So when Voss had stood on the dock yesterday in his dry, expensive coat, offering triple rate, Petra had said no. She'd said it clearly, and she'd meant it, and she'd watched him walk away with the particular satisfaction of a woman who still owned her own spine.

She should have known Teo would pick up the pen Voss left behind.

She came down to the aft deck. Teo didn't stop coiling.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been rehearsing for an hour. Say it so we can both stop pretending."

He set the coil down. Squared it. Squared it again, which was worse—Teo only fussed with things when his hands needed somewhere to hide.

"He came back," Teo said. "After you went below. Offered the contract to me direct. Said he'd deal with the owner of record." He tapped his own chest. "That's me, Petra. The Cormorant's registered in my name. Has been since the bankruptcy. You signed it over and maybe you forgot, but the paperwork didn't."

"I didn't forget." Her voice came out flat and cold, which was better than what was underneath it. "I trusted you not to use it."

"You trusted me to stay broke with you." He said it without heat, which somehow made it land harder. "Petra, Marisol's tuition cleared last month because I sold my truck. The diesel bill is forty days past due. And he's paying triple. Triple, for one recovery dive."

"Where."

Teo looked at the water. Then at her. And she saw it coming the way you see a swell coming before it breaks—too far out to do anything but stand there.

"The Kestrel."

The word went through her like cold water through a cracked seam.

* * *

Eleven years ago, in a church in Puerto Cabral that smelled of lilies and diesel from the fishing fleet's flowers-and-fuel budget, Petra Galloway had stood in a black dress two sizes too big and watched them lower Ruth Okonkwo into the ground.

Ruth had taught them both. Not in a classroom—in the water, in the wheelhouse, in the particular grammar of the sea that you can't write down. *Read the water before you read the chart,* Ruth used to say. *The chart is what somebody thought yesterday. The water is what's true.* She'd pulled Petra out of a rip current at fifteen with one hand and never mentioned it again, because Ruth believed a rescue you bragged about was a rescue that hadn't happened. She'd taught Teo navigation when no one else would take on a dock kid with a police record. She'd taught them all that a crew was the only family you got to choose, and that you chose it every single day, in every single decision, or you didn't have it at all.

The Kestrel had gone down in the October storm of that year, forty miles north, in the deep water off the glacier fjord. Ruth had been aboard as salvage master, contracted by a shipping concern whose name nobody at the funeral would say out loud. Seven souls. One survivor, a deckhand who'd been drunk below and remembered nothing except the sound of the hull going. The maritime board had ruled it weather. *Act of God,* the finding said, and Petra had stood in that church thinking that if God had been on that boat, He'd have been in the engine room with Ruth, keeping her hands busy.

After the burial, the crew had walked down to the water. All six of them, still in their funeral clothes, boots sinking in wet sand. Teo had brought a bottle of rum because Ruth would have wanted something practical. And Petra—Petra had made them swear. She hadn't planned it. The words had just come up out of her like blood from a cut.

*No one works the Kestrel.* That was the oath. *Not for money, not for the navy, not for God Himself if He comes down asking with a contract. She's Ruth's grave, and you don't dig a grave to get at what's in the pockets.*

They'd each poured a measure into the surf. Teo had cried, openly, without shame, and Petra had loved him for it. Then they'd gone back to work the next morning, because that was the other thing Ruth taught: the sea doesn't pause for your grief, and neither can you.

Eleven years. And now Teo stood in front of her with Voss's contract in his jacket pocket and Ruth's name in his mouth, and the oath sat between them like a body on a deck.

* * *

"You read the contract?" Petra asked.

"Of course I read it."

"Then read it to me. Out loud. The part that says what we're recovering."

Teo hesitated. That was the answer, right there, but she made him do it anyway. He pulled the folded pages from his jacket and read in the flat voice of a man reciting his own sentence: "Recovery of specified materials from the wreck of the vessel *Kestrel*, position per annex. Materials to be delivered intact and unexamined to the client or his designee. Client reserves sole rights of assessment and disposal."

"Unexamined," Petra said. "We dive a grave we swore off, and we're not even allowed to look at what we bring up."

"He's paying triple—"

"I heard you the first time." She walked to the rail. The harbor was doing its evening business around them, gulls and forklifts and the far-off clang of the navy tender. "Why did you sign it, Teo? Not the money. The money's a reason, not a reason. Why *this*?"

He was quiet long enough that she turned around. When he spoke, his voice had dropped all its defenses, and what was underneath was just tired.

"Because if we don't take it, somebody else will. Voss will find another boat inside a week. Some crew that never knew her, that'll strip the wreck for whatever he wants and leave the rest to rot. At least this way it's us. At least this way, whatever else happens, Ruth's boat gets handled by people who loved her." He swallowed. "That's not the reason I signed. I signed for the money. But it's the reason I'm telling you, because it's the only one that isn't shameful."

Petra looked at him for a long time. The anger was still there—hot, personal, the specific anger of a betrayed trust—but underneath it, rising like silt in disturbed water, was something else. Because he was right. That was the poison of it. He was right that Voss would find another boat, and he was right that it should be them, and he was even right that she'd been hiding behind the oath the way a broke woman hides behind her pride.

"Here's what happens," she said. "We take the contract. We go to the Kestrel. We recover one thing and one thing only: Ruth's effects. Her sea chest, her tools, her personal log if it's there. Whatever Voss actually wants can sit on the bottom and fertilize the coral. And when we bring her effects up, we put them where they belong. Then we hand Voss his box or his papers or whatever the hell it is, we take his triple rate, and we never speak his name again."

"Petra—"

"That's the deal. That's the only deal. You signed us onto a grave, Teo. I'm telling you how we dig in it." She held out her hand. "Contract."

He gave it to her. She read it through twice, slowly, while he stood there sweating. Then she took the pen from his pocket—the same pen, she noticed, that Voss had handed him on the dock—and wrote in the margin of the annex, beneath the position coordinates, in her own hand: *Recovery limited to personal effects of R. Okonkwo. All other materials untouched.* She signed it and handed it back.

"He'll never accept that," Teo said.

"Then he'll never get his dive. And you can explain to Marisol's school why the tuition bounced."

Teo almost smiled. Almost. "You know he's not hiring us for her sea chest."

"I know exactly what he's hiring us for. And I know that whatever it is, it's been down there eleven years, and it can wait another week while we do right by the dead." She looked north, toward the deep water and the glacier country beyond it, where the Kestrel lay in the dark. "One more thing. We'll need a pilot for the fjord pass. The charts are eleven years stale and the glacier's been chewing the coastline the whole time. Nobody local will take it this season."

"I'll ask around."

"Ask around all you want. I already know we won't find anyone." She said it lightly, but the thought had been forming since the dock, since Voss, since the word *Kestrel* had cracked her open: that the sea was going to make her take on strangers for this, that grief was going to cost her more than money. "Get the diesel paid. We sail when the yard's done with the hull."

She was halfway up the wheelhouse stairs when Teo spoke again, quiet enough that she almost didn't hear it over the gulls.

"She'd have taken the job, you know. Ruth. If it was the only way to keep the crew fed."

Petra stopped, one hand on the rail, the cold iron under her palm.

"Yeah," she said. "That's what scares me."

She went up and spread the chart of the northern fjords on the table, and that was when the knock came at the hull—a stranger's knock, three sharp raps, from someone standing on a dock the *Cormorant* wasn't tied up to.
