## 3. Stranger at the Lock

The lock at Puerto Cabral opens twice a day, and Nadia Reyes has learned to read its moods the way she reads the glacier: by sound. The groan of the gates, the churn of the exchange, the gulls that scatter when the water level drops too fast. This morning the gulls are wrong. They're inland, riding a wind that smells of rain that hasn't arrived yet. The monsoon is early.

She's on the seawall with her instruments spread across a folding table—ice cores, salinity logs, a laptop in a waterproof case that has seen better years—when the Cormorant comes through the lock.

She knows the boat before she knows the name. Salvage trawler, converted, running on stubbornness and duct tape. She's been watching the harbor traffic for three weeks, cataloguing which vessels are fool enough to head north this season, and the Cormorant is the first one rigged for diving. Hydrophone cradled on the aft deck like a coffin. Dive compressor bolted where the fish holds used to be. A woman at the rail with gray coming into her dark hair, watching the lock walls slide past with the expression of someone doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

Nadia checks her notes. Cormorant. Out of Puerto Cabral, salvage and recovery. Last registered owner: P. Galloway.

The other name in her notes is Voss, and the fact that both names are heading for the same fjord in the same season is either coincidence or the opening chapter of a disaster, and Nadia has spent enough years on the ice to stop believing in coincidence.

She packs her table in ninety seconds and walks the quay to meet the boat.

* * *

The woman at the rail—Galloway, it has to be—looks her over with the flat assessment of someone who has been offered a lot of things on docks and trusted none of them.

"You're the glaciologist," Petra says. It isn't a question. "The one who's been leaving notes at the harbor office."

"I'm the one who's been leaving *warnings* at the harbor office. There's a difference." Nadia holds up her tablet, the bathymetric chart glowing on it. "The pass through the Serrano Fjord closes in three weeks. Maybe four. The calving season started early—the glacier's shedding faster than the models predicted, and the ice field is drifting into the narrows. After that, nothing goes in or out until the water clears in October. Nothing."

"We're aware of the season."

"You're aware of a season the way a man is aware of a train." Nadia lets that sit. "I've been measuring the terminus for six years. I can tell you which leads will be open on which day, and I can tell you that the window is closing, and I can tell you that every salvage crew that's ignored me has ended up on the evening news with their hull on a reef."

Petra's eyes narrow. "We haven't hired you."

"No. But someone paid a dockhand to ask about my research last week, and someone else chartered a seaplane out of the capital with dive equipment aboard, and both of those someones are pointed at the same fjord I am. I don't know what's down there. I know it's worth enough that people are being stupid about it." She taps the chart. "You need a pilot through the ice. I need a platform closer to the terminus than any research vessel will take me. I'm not asking for a salary. I'm asking for a berth and a say in when we run the narrows."

Behind Petra, at the wheelhouse door, a broad man with a beard going to salt-and-pepper—Teo, the first mate, if the harbor gossip was worth anything—shakes his head almost imperceptibly. *Don't.* A younger woman in the doorway just looks tired.

Petra studies Nadia for a long moment, and Nadia recognizes the look, because she's seen it on expedition leaders deciding whether to trust a stranger with their lives in whiteout conditions. It isn't about her credentials. It's about whether she'll hold when it matters.

"You've read the water your whole life," Petra says finally. "What's it telling you?"

"That we have eighteen days of safe passage, and that the storm coming up from the southeast in two days is the first real one of the season." Nadia closes the tablet. "And that whoever's racing you to that wreck is willing to run the narrows blind. Which means they'll either beat you by luck or die by it. I'd rather you weren't the second thing."

Something shifts in Petra's face—not agreement, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman who has already lost people to water and is tired of pretending the sea takes sides.

"Berth's aft, next to the compressor," Petra says. "It's loud and it smells like diesel. We dive at first light."

* * *

Teo doesn't speak to her for the first day, which Nadia expected. The younger woman—Marisol, the deckhand and cook—does, in the way of people who feed you and watch how you eat. The engineer, a silent man called Bram, lets her stand next to him in the engine room while he checks the cracked seam he pretends isn't there, and says exactly four words the entire time: "Don't tell the boss."

By the second evening, Nadia has the fjord chart pinned above the mess table and is walking the crew through it.

"Here." She marks the narrows. "The glacier calves into the upper basin. The ice drifts down and jams against this sill—shallow water, fast current. Right now there's a lead through the eastern channel, maybe sixty meters wide. In three weeks it'll be a wall of brash ice. The Kestrel sits in the basin beyond, at the glacier's front door."

"You've been to the wreck?" Marisol asks.

"No one's been to the wreck. That's the point. The navy survey vessel turned back two weeks ago." She lets that land. "Your employer knows the window. That's why the money's good. He's not paying you to dive a ship. He's paying you to dive it *before the sea closes over it for another season.*"

Petra is quiet through all of it, arms crossed, watching the chart like it owes her money. When Nadia finishes, she says only: "First dive tomorrow. You'll run the deck while we're down."

"I don't run decks."

"Everyone runs decks on my boat." Petra's voice doesn't rise. "You wanted a platform. The platform has rules."

* * *

The Kestrel comes up on the sonar at 0700, a hard return in forty meters of green-black water, lying on her starboard side in the silt like something that lay down and refused to get up.

Nadia stands the deck while Petra and Teo kit up. The water is two degrees, the visibility maybe eight meters, and the surface chop from yesterday's wind hasn't fully settled. Everything about it is wrong for a dive, and everyone on the deck knows it, and nobody says it, because the window is closing and they all feel it now, the way you feel a storm front in your teeth.

Petra goes down first. Teo follows. Nadia runs the lines, watches the bubbles, calls the depth every five minutes into the mask mic because Petra asked her to and because the rhythm of it—*forty meters, holding, forty meters*—is the only thing keeping her own hands steady.

Twenty-two minutes in, Petra's voice comes up the line, flat and strange.

"Nadia. Tell Bram to stand by the crane. We're cutting free a hatch cover. And then get down here when you can. Bring a camera."

"What did you find?"

A pause. Bubbles. Then, quietly, in a voice Nadia will think about for weeks afterward:

"The way she died."

* * *

She dives the wreck that afternoon, with Petra beside her and the floodlights carving a cathedral out of the dark.

The Kestrel is eleven years dead and the sea has dressed her in anemones and rust, but the hull tells its story to anyone who knows how to listen, and Nadia—raised on a hydrologist father who made her read riverbeds the way other children read books—knows how to listen.

They swim the engine room. Petra's light finds the seacocks.

Nadia has seen scuttled vessels before, in surveys, in salvage reports. There's a grammar to it. A ship that sinks in a storm drowns from above—hatch covers sprung, windows gone, the damage written on her back. A ship that sinks from below drowns quietly, deliberately, the sea let in through her own veins.

The Kestrel's seacocks are open. All of them. Opened by hand—the wheels are turned past hand-tight, past the position a flooding crew would leave them in a panic, and one of them is jammed open with a wedge of wood driven in to keep it from being closed. Someone stood in this engine room, in the dark, in the last minutes, and made sure.

Above them, through the collapsed overhead, the flooded accommodation spaces stretch away. Forty meters down, in two degrees of black water, somewhere in that tangle, Ruth Okonkwo died with her ship.

Petra's breathing has gone ragged in her regulator. Nadia puts a hand on her arm—not comfort, exactly. Anchor. The older woman doesn't shake it off.

They surface in silence. It's Teo who breaks it, hauling off his mask on the deck, water streaming down his beard, his voice cracking on the words.

"Seacocks don't open themselves. Petra. *Seacocks don't open themselves.*"

"I know." Petra is staring at the water, at the place where the wreck lies below, and Nadia watches eleven years of grief reorganize itself into something harder and colder. "I know, Teo."

Nadia wraps her camera in a towel and says the thing she has been turning over since the engine room, because someone has to say it and she has known these people for three days, which makes it easier.

"Whoever scuttled her wanted the sinking to look like the storm. Which means the storm was the alibi, not the cause. Which means—" She stops, because Petra has turned to look at her, and the look on the woman's face is not grief anymore.

It's arithmetic.

"Voss told you she went down in the storm," Nadia says slowly. "Everyone says she went down in the storm. The navy report says she went down in the storm. And the man who's paying you triple rate to dive her—" 

"Is the same man who chartered a seaplane with dive equipment," Petra finishes quietly. "And who asked the harbor office about you."

The wind has come around to the southeast while they were under. Nadia feels it on her neck, smells the rain in it, two days early. The first real storm of the season, and they are anchored in open water above a murdered ship, and somewhere behind them, past the closing ice, a man who knows exactly how the Kestrel died is on his way to find out what they know.

Petra looks at the chart, at the narrows, at the eighteen days.

"Nadia," she says. "How fast can we run those narrows in weather?"
