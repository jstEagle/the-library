## 4. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The patrol boat came out of the rain like a rumor made real—grey hull, no running lights, no hail until it was fifty meters off the Cormorant's port beam and closing.

Petra had been in the wheelhouse nursing her third coffee of a morning that refused to warm up when Teo's voice crackled through from the bow.

"Contact. Two points off port. Moving fast."

She set the mug down. By the time she reached the rail, she could read the name stenciled on the hull: *NSV Tern*, coastal patrol, forty meters, a deck gun that was mostly for show but a boarding team on its fantail that very much wasn't.

"Everyone breathe," she said, loud enough for the deck crew. "We're a licensed salvage vessel in international-adjacent waters with current paperwork. We've done nothing but pull scrap off a wreck we're permitted to touch." She paused. "Teo, get the permit folder. Nadia—" The hydrologist was at the rail beside her, hood up, watching the grey shape grow. "Stay inside. You're not crew on paper yet, and I'd rather not explain you."

Nadia gave her a look that said she'd survived worse than paperwork, but she went.

* * *

They came aboard at 0940. Four of them, rifles slung, led by an officer who climbed the pilot ladder like he'd been born on one—a compact man, forties, weathered to the color of old rope, with a name tape that read **R. ASHWORTH** and eyes that did not blink enough.

Petra met him on the aft deck with the permit folder already open.

"Lieutenant Commander Ashworth," he said, not offering a hand. "Coastal Patrol Division. Captain Galloway, I presume."

"You presume right. Here's our salvage license, our transit clearance, our crew manifest, and our waste-disposal certification, which nobody ever asks for but you're welcome to read."

Ashworth took the folder without hurry. He flipped through it while his men fanned out—not searching, exactly, just being present in the way armed men are present. One of them lingered by the dive locker, reading the stencil on a tank: *KESTREL SURVEY — DO NOT PURGE*.

Petra watched Ashworth watch his man watch the tank.

"Salvage work," Ashworth said mildly. "Out here."

"That's where wrecks tend to be."

"Mm." He closed the folder. "Your permits cover the *Meridian Star* debris field. Scrap recovery, contracted through the Port Authority. All correct." He handed the folder back, then didn't let go of it. "So why does your dive manifest list a survey site eleven miles north, inside the exclusion zone?"

The rain ticked against the wheelhouse glass. Somewhere below, Teo had gone very quiet.

"Charting," Petra said. "We run surveys between contracts. Keeps the sonar operator honest."

"Eleven miles north," Ashworth repeated, "is the *Kestrel*."

There it was. Not a question. A name said flatly, the way you'd set a card down face-up.

Petra kept her face still, which took more effort than any dive she'd ever done. "Old wreck. Eleven years. Nobody's diving it."

"No," Ashworth agreed. "Nobody is. That's rather the point of the exclusion zone." He finally released the folder. Then he stepped closer, close enough that she could smell wet wool and gun oil, and lowered his voice to something meant only for her. "I knew the *Kestrel*. My first posting, we ran the search grid when she went down. Seven bodies recovered. One never found." His eyes didn't move. "Captain Okonkwo."

The rain filled the silence.

"I don't know what you're doing out here," Ashworth went on, "and I have decided, provisionally, not to care. But I will tell you what I told the last vessel that anchored over that site, three years ago, before it turned around and left in the night." He straightened. "Whatever you think is down there—the navy has files about the *Kestrel* that are classified above my clearance, and those files have never been declassified, which means someone very senior decided they should stay shut. People who open things like that do not stay shut themselves. Do you understand me, Captain?"

"I understand you're threatening me without threatening me."

"I understand nothing," Ashworth said. "That's the beauty of my position." He turned, gestured his men back to the rail, then paused at the ladder. Without looking at her: "Okonkwo taught half the salvage officers on this coast their trade, back when she ran the training program. She certified me on mixed-gas. She was the best of us." A beat. "If someone were going to disturb her grave, I'd want it to be someone who loved her. And I'd want them to know exactly what they were walking into."

Then he was down the ladder, and the *Tern* was turning away into the rain, and Petra stood on her own deck holding a permit folder with hands that wanted to shake and didn't.

* * *

Teo found her in the wheelhouse twenty minutes later. He didn't sit. He stood by the chart table with his arms crossed, jaw working, and finally said, "He knew."

"He knew the ship. Maybe he knew Ruth. Doesn't matter."

"It matters if he reports us."

"He won't. Not yet." Petra spread the chart, tapped the exclusion zone. "Men like Ashworth follow orders until following them costs more than breaking them. We're not there yet. Which means we have a window, and windows close."

"So we abort."

"We don't abort." She looked up. "You signed us onto this job, Teo. Voss is paying triple rate, and triple rate keeps the *Cormorant* floating and your daughter's tuition paid, and we both know it. So we finish the survey, we find whatever Voss thinks is down there, and then we decide what it's worth. On our terms. Not his."

Teo held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever he saw there made him nod once, slowly, like a man agreeing to a debt he hadn't finished counting.

"And if what's down there is what got Ruth killed?" he asked quietly.

Petra had no answer for that. She turned back to the chart.

* * *

Voss came two days later, out of a sky the color of dishwater, in a seaplane white as a tooth.

Petra heard it first—a drone above the rain, wrong pitch for a gull, too steady for weather—and came out onto the deck in time to see it drop toward the water a quarter mile east, touch down in a long silver smear, and taxi toward them with the unhurried arrogance of money that has never once been late.

It moored alongside. Three men came down the float line before Voss himself appeared—big men, matching jackets, earpieces, the kind of security detail that exists to make a point rather than prevent a problem. Then Dario Voss stepped onto her deck in a dry coat worth more than her winch, and smiled like they were old friends at a wedding.

"Petra." He spread his hands. "You look well."

"I look wet. There's a difference." She didn't move from the wheelhouse door. "This is my ship, Dario. You called ahead for permission to board, or are we doing this the honest way?"

"The honest way." He gestured, and one of the big men produced a hard case, set it on the deck, opened it. Inside, nested in foam: a laptop, a decryption unit, and a document folder thick as a brick. "Below decks, perhaps. Your first mate mentioned you prefer business conducted standing up, but the rain disagrees."

"My first mate talks too much."

They gathered in the mess—Petra, Teo, Voss, and one of the security men, who stood by the door with the posture of furniture. Nadia stayed conspicuously absent; Petra had told her to, and the hydrologist had gone without argument, which Petra was beginning to suspect was itself a form of judgment.

Voss opened the folder across the table.

"Eleven years ago," he said, "the *Kestrel* was operating under charter to Meridian Consolidated. Environmental survey work. Routine." He slid a photograph across. A ship's interior, a rack of electronics, a yellow-and-orange canister bolted to the frame. "Every commercial vessel carries a voyage data recorder. Position, audio, sensor feeds, everything the ship knew, preserved after death. Like a black box on an aircraft."

Petra looked at the photo and felt the temperature of the room change.

"The *Kestrel* went down in a storm," she said carefully. "Official finding. Structural failure in heavy seas."

"The official finding," Voss agreed, "was written by lawyers retained by Meridian Consolidated. Which is why it says structural failure instead of what the survey data actually showed." He tapped the photograph. "Ruth found something during that last voyage. Contamination plumes off the glacier coast—chemical signatures that shouldn't have been there. She traced them. She was thorough; you remember how she was. What she found was a dumping operation. Meridian's processing subsidiary had been pumping tailings and solvent waste into the fjord system for six years. Ruth documented all of it. Sensor logs, water samples, chain-of-custody records—all of it routed through the *Kestrel*'s data systems. All of it still sitting in that recorder, on the seabed, because the recorder was built to survive exactly what happened to her ship."

The rain hammered the deck overhead. Nobody moved.

"They scuttled her," Petra said. It wasn't a question anymore. It hadn't been since the first dive, since the seacocks, since the clean, deliberate way that ship had died.

"The investigation concluded storm damage," Voss said. "The seacocks were noted as 'anomalies consistent with impact.' Nothing further. The exclusion zone was established—for navigation safety, officially. Unofficially, it keeps divers away from the one object on that seabed that can end Meridian Consolidated, several political careers, and—" he paused, and for just a moment the polish thinned, "—my family's name, which is entangled with Meridian in ways I will not insult you by pretending are innocent."

"Why you?" Teo asked from the corner. "Why not send your own people? Why hire a rival?"

"Because my own people would be watched. Every diver Meridian knows about gets followed within a day. You—" Voss looked at Petra, and there was something almost respectful in it, which was worse than contempt. "You're broke, you're invisible, and you're the only crew on this coast with a personal reason to be over that wreck that isn't about me. Grief makes excellent cover."

Petra stood very still. Outside, the light was failing early, monsoon clouds stacking up over the headland like a debt coming due.

"What happens to the recorder once you have it?" she asked.

"It comes to me. Encrypted extraction, verified destruction, witnessed. The evidence dies. The dumping operation stays buried, the families who lost people on the *Kestrel* keep their tidy official story, and my children keep a father whose name isn't a byword for corporate murder." He closed the folder. "Triple rate stands. Bonus on delivery. And Captain—" he rose, buttoning his coat, "—the navy boarded you yesterday. I know because Meridian pays people to know things. Ashworth gave you a warning because he's a decent man in a machine that doesn't employ many. Next time it won't be a warning. So: window's closing, weather's coming, and I'd like my property recovered before the fjord closes for the season."

At the door he stopped. Turned halfway.

"She talked about you, you know. Ruth. In her letters home. Said you were the best diver she'd trained and too stubborn to lie to." A thin smile. "Let's hope she was only half right."

Then he was gone, down the float line, and the seaplane lifted off into the murk trailing spray, and Petra stood in her own mess with the photograph of the black box lying on her table like a loaded thing.

Teo spoke first, hoarse. "Triple rate. Plus bonus. That's the boat fixed, the debts cleared, two seasons of—"

"It's also the price of Ruth staying dead," Petra said.

She picked up the photograph. Yellow canister, steel frame, eleven years of cold dark water. Somewhere under that grey sea, her mentor's last voice was waiting in a metal shell, holding the truth about who killed her and why.

*We come to recover her effects,* Petra had sworn at the funeral, over an open grave, with salt wind flattening the grass. *Nothing more.*

But Ruth's effects, it turned out, included the truth.

And outside, the barometer was falling like a stone.
