## 5. Monsoon Season

Nadia felt the storm coming in her teeth before the barometer confirmed it. Twelve years on the ice had taught her to read pressure the way sailors read water, and all day the air had tasted wrong—thick, metallic, holding its breath.

By dusk the horizon had gone the color of a bruise.

"Everyone says monsoon like it's one thing," she told Teo as they lashed down the deck gear together. "It's not. It's a season of decisions. The sky decides, and you find out what you're made of."

Teo grunted, cinching a strap over the spare dive tanks. "You always talk this much?"

"Only when I'm scared."

That earned her a half-smile, the first she'd gotten from him. "Then you'll be quiet by midnight."

He was wrong about that. By midnight nobody on the Cormorant was saying anything at all, because the wind arrived like a verdict.

* * *

It came out of the northwest with no grace period, no gentle build—just a wall of rain and sound that erased the world beyond the wheelhouse glass. Nadia stood beside Petra at the helm, watching the anemometer climb: forty knots, fifty, sixty. The Cormorant pitched into the swells and shuddered like something alive being shaken.

"Anchor's holding," Petra said, and then, twenty minutes later, she stopped saying it.

Nadia heard it first—a long, grinding shudder through the hull, the particular vibration of steel dragging across rock. The GPS plot on the chart screen told the rest: their position was moving. Backward. Toward the headland, where the water shallowed into surf and black stone.

"We're dragging." Petra's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Teo! Second anchor, now!"

The crew moved like they'd done this a hundred times, because they had. Teo and a wiry deckhand named Sefa wrestled the spare anchor to the bow while the boat yawed beneath them, and Nadia—useless with anchors, useless with boats, useful only for weather—took the wheel when Petra went forward. The rain hit the windshield sideways. The running lights showed nothing but gray chaos and the white flash of breaking water where the headland waited.

The second anchor caught. The Cormorant swung, groaned, and held—or seemed to. Nobody relaxed. In a storm like this, *seemed to* was the only honest word available.

* * *

The seam cracked at 0300.

Nadia was in the galley trying to make coffee that wouldn't launch itself off the stove when she heard it: not a bang but a sigh, a low exhalation from below the waterline, and then Sefa's voice from the engine room, flat with adrenaline. "We're taking water. Aft bilge. It's coming through the old seam by the prop shaft."

Petra was down the ladder before Nadia finished setting the kettle down. She followed anyway, because standing still felt more dangerous than moving.

The engine room was a green-lit cave of diesel smell and dripping iron. Water seeped through a hairline crack in the plating—not gushing, not yet, but steady, patient, the way glaciers calve. One inch at a time until the map is redrawn.

"Bilge pump's rated for half this," Sefa shouted over the engines. "If it opens up—"

"It won't." Petra already had the backup pump out of its case, hauling hose. "Nadia—there's a second manual pump in the locker aft. Go."

She went. The deck between superstructure and stern locker was a river in motion, rain hammering down, the boat rolling through arcs that turned walking into geometry. She clipped her tether to the jackline and hauled herself hand over hand, thinking absurdly of her field camp on the glacier, how she'd complained about the cold tents, the freeze-dried food. This was what the ice had been preparing her for: work in conditions that did not care whether you survived them.

She got the manual pump rigged and started the rhythm—one stroke, two, three—and discovered that pumping bilge water in a monsoon is a full-body argument with physics. Her shoulders burned. Cold water sloshed around her boots. Through the sole grate beneath her she could see the bilge level rising anyway, black water climbing the stringers.

They pumped all night.

That was the truth of it, stripped of drama: four people and a rotating shift, passing buckets when the pumps clogged, shouting readings back and forth through the hatch. Petra ran the engine room like a surgeon who'd lost her instruments—calm hands, short words, zero panic. Sefa bailed. Teo kept the anchors and took the watches nobody wanted. And Nadia pumped until her palms blistered, and found, to her own surprise, that she didn't quit, didn't complain, didn't ask whose fault any of it was.

Somewhere around dawn the rain slackened from murderous to merely hostile, and the bilge level stopped climbing. They'd won nothing permanent—the seam would need welding, drydock, money none of them had—but they'd won the night, and the night was what there was.

* * *

It happened after dawn, in the treacherous false calm.

The wind had dropped enough that they dared move gear on deck, securing what the storm had thrown around. Nadia was coiling line near the rail when a rogue swell came out of nowhere—the sea's version of a lie—and the Cormorant rolled hard to starboard.

Her boots went out from under her.

The deck tilted past the angle where balance is a choice. She slid, grabbed the jackline stanchion, missed, and suddenly the rail was rising toward her feet and the gray water beyond it was very large and very close, and she understood with total clarity that she was going over.

A hand closed on her ankle.

Not gently—there was nothing gentle left in the world—but with absolute authority. Teo had thrown himself flat across the tilting deck, wedged his boot against the base of the rail, and caught her as she went. For three seconds—which lasted approximately a geological age—he held on while the ocean tried to take her, and then Sefa was there too, and Petra, and they hauled her back over the rail onto the deck like fishermen landing something heavy and stunned.

She lay on her back in the runoff, gasping, staring up at a sky still the color of wet slate.

"You okay?" Teo asked. He was breathing hard, blood on one knuckle where the rail had skinned him.

"I can't swim well," she said. It came out small. "Cold water. I never learned properly. On the ice we—" She stopped. There was no good ending to that sentence.

Teo looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached down, pulled her to her feet, and said, "Rule one on this boat: nobody goes in the water. Not the crew, not the cargo, not the secrets." A ghost of his earlier smile. "Glad you're heavy."

"Glad you were faster."

From the wheelhouse, Petra called them in for coffee that was mostly condensed milk and gratitude. They drank it scalding, shoulder to shoulder, four people wrung out and salt-burned and laughing at things that weren't funny—the way Sefa had screamed at a flying fish, the sound Petra's wrench had made when it went overboard, the fact that Nadia had nearly died and her first coherent thought had been about her unfinished survey data.

Something had shifted in the night. Nadia could feel it sitting among them like a fourth crew member. She'd come aboard as a stranger, hired against everyone's instinct, tolerated because the fjord pass required her. But fear shared at three in the morning is a solvent. It dissolves the coatings people put over themselves. She'd seen Petra's hands shake only after the danger passed, seen Teo pray quietly under his breath in Tagalog, seen Sefa cry without shame and go back to bailing. You couldn't unsee those things. You couldn't be strangers afterward.

"Your glacier," Petra said, apropos of nothing, staring into her cup. "The calving season. How long do we have on that pass?"

Nadia did the math she'd been doing since the dock. "Two weeks. Maybe less if the melt accelerates. After that, the ice bridge closes and nothing bigger than a kayak gets through until winter."

Petra nodded slowly, and Nadia watched her file that away next to everything else—the dragging anchor, the cracked seam, the navy patrol that had circled twice yesterday, the employer who hadn't answered his radio since the storm began.

Two weeks. The number sat in the cabin with them, patient as rising water.

Later, as the sea flattened toward evening, Nadia found Teo at the bow checking the anchor rode. She stood beside him in silence for a while, two people watching a bruised sunset leak through the clouds.

"Thank you," she said finally. "For my ankle."

He didn't look over. "Debt's paid when you save me back. That's how crews work."

"And if I'm not crew? Just a pilot you hired?"

Teo tested the shackle, satisfied himself, and glanced at her sidelong. "Nobody on this boat stays a stranger after a night like that. That's the problem with this crew." His voice dropped, almost to himself. "And it's going to be the problem with this job."

She wanted to ask what he meant. Before she could, the radio crackled to life in the wheelhouse behind them—Voss's voice, thin and static-shredded, asking for a status report, asking about the box, asking why no one had answered him for eighteen hours.

Petra's answer carried out through the open door, calm and cold as deep water:

"We were busy staying alive. We'll talk about the job when the weather breaks."

Through the wheelhouse glass, Nadia saw Petra's hand close around something in her pocket—something small, dark, and rectangular—and hold it there, as if making sure it was still real.

She hadn't noticed it before. Now she couldn't stop seeing it.
