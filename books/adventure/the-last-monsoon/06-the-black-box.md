## 6. The Black Box

The barometer climbed for six hours, and Nadia called it a window.

"Eight hours, maybe ten," she said, spreading the forecast across the chart table with both hands, pinning the corners like she was pinning down something alive. "The low stalls over the peninsula and the gradient flattens. After that it comes back meaner. If you're going down, go now."

Petra stood at the rail and looked at the Kestrel's mooring buoy, a rusted eye floating in gray water two hundred meters off the bow. Eleven years of not thinking about this wreck, and now she'd been down twice in three days, and both times she'd come up with her heart hammering against her teeth.

The first dive had told her what she hadn't wanted to know: seacocks opened. All four of them, handles turned clean, no corrosion fracture, no storm damage consistent with foundering. The Kestrel hadn't sunk. She'd been let to sink.

"You don't have to be the one," Teo said behind her. He was coiling line with the mechanical care of a man keeping his hands busy so his mouth wouldn't start. "I can run the recovery."

"It's my boat."

"It's your grief," he said quietly. "Those aren't the same thing."

She didn't answer that. She checked her tanks instead—eighty percent, good—and her dive knife, and the lift bag folded flat against her harness. The recorder housing Voss had described was bolted into the aft hold, below the engine room bulkhead, in a compartment that would have flooded first and flooded fast. If it was intact at all after eleven years of cold brine, it would be because the housing was built to survive exactly this.

Ruth Okonkwo had taught her that housings were built to survive exactly this. *Anything worth recording is worth protecting from the sea,* she used to say, tapping the hull of whatever boat they were aboard at the time. *The sea takes everything else.*

* * *

The water at forty meters was the temperature of forgetting.

Petra dropped through it in a silence so complete she could hear her own regulator's every breath, could hear the tick of her own pulse. Her light cut a cone through the green-black, and then the Kestrel rose up out of the dark to meet her, and Petra Galloway, who did not believe in ghosts, felt one put its hand on the back of her neck.

The wreck lay on its starboard side at a slight list toward the fjord wall, settled into silt like a body into a bed it had finally stopped fighting. Marine growth had softened her lines, draped her in white and orange plumage, but the shape of her was still the shape Ruth had loved—a coastal survey vessel, sixty feet of stubborn steel, built for bad weather and worse charts. Petra had helped paint the boot stripe the summer she was twenty-two. She'd been seasick over the rail and Ruth had laughed at her and made her finish the job anyway.

*Focus,* she told herself. *Aft hold. Recorder. Nothing else.*

But the dead don't let you take shortcuts through their houses.

She swam the length of the hull, past the propeller furred with anemones, past the rudder cocked at the angle of a last command never given. Through the cargo hatch, which gaped open the way hatches do when a ship goes down stern-first and the sea presses in from every direction at once. Inside, her light found the corridor they'd all walked a hundred times, now a cathedral of silt and shadow, deck plates furred gray, doorways standing open like mouths.

The galley first. She hadn't meant to go there. Her fins took her anyway.

A mug sat wedged in the corner of the sink where someone had left it, ceramic gone soft-edged with eleven years of growth, and Petra's chest did something complicated and painful around it, because she knew whose mug that was. Ruth drank her coffee black and too hot and always left the mug in the sink because *a crew member who has time to wash dishes has time to check the bilge.* Petra could hear her saying it. Could hear the whole crew groaning. Could hear Teo laughing in the wheelhouse.

She hung there in the drowned galley of a murdered ship and breathed, and breathed, and made herself turn away.

The aft hold was aft. Of course it was. She followed the corridor with her light, past the engine room bulkhead, through the watertight door someone had dogged open from the inside—the seaman's instinct to keep an escape route clear, even as the water came—and into the space where the recorder should be.

Her light swept the mounting bracket.

Empty.

For three full seconds Petra felt nothing at all, which was how terror worked in her experience—not a spike but a hole, a white absence where the future used to be. Then her training caught up with her fear and she made herself look again, properly, methodically, the way Ruth had drilled into her. Not empty. *Moved.* The bracket bolts were sheared, not unscrewed, and the silt on the deck below the mount was disturbed in a fan pattern, a drag mark leading deeper into the hold, toward the void space behind the fuel tanks.

Someone had gotten here before her. Recently. The silt didn't lie about that; it settled in weeks, not years, and the fan was fresh.

Voss's security divers. They'd been down already, hunting for it, and either failed or—

Or hidden it. Staged it. Marked it for retrieval when the navy patrols shifted their pattern.

Petra followed the drag mark into the void space, a crawl of maybe four meters between tank and frame, her tank valve scraping overhead, her light held out ahead of her like a lantern in a story her grandmother should never have told her. And there, wedged into the frame where the drag mark ended, wrapped in a diver's mesh bag with a strobe clipped to it—a retrieval beacon, still blinking its slow red pulse through the murk—

The recorder.

And beside it, tucked under the bag's strap with a deliberateness that stopped Petra's heart, a logbook.

Waterproof casing, brass corners, the kind Ruth had carried for thirty years. Petra knew the model the way she knew her own hands. She pulled it free and the cover came up and even through the fogged plastic of the case she could read the embossed initials, worn by a decade of thumbs:

*R.O.*

* * *

She surfaced into gray light and the smell of rain coming, and Teo hauled her over the gunwale by her harness like she weighed nothing, and it wasn't until she was sitting on the deck plates with the oxygen mask on that anyone noticed she was holding the logbook against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a thing you're afraid will be taken.

Nobody took it.

Nadia crouched in front of her, said nothing, just watched her breathe. Teo stood at the rail with his back to all of them, shoulders rigid. The rest of the crew drifted close without drifting too close, the way crews do when they understand that some cargoes are personal.

Finally Petra peeled the mask off.

"She hid it," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Not the recorder—I think the recorder was mounted where it was supposed to be, and Voss's divers sheared it loose and stashed it for pickup. But the logbook. She wedged it under the strap. Deliberately. That's not a shipwreck artifact, that's a message."

She opened the case. The pages inside were warped but legible, ballpoint ink gone brown but holding, and Ruth's handwriting marched across them in the tight, level script of a woman who'd logged ten thousand hours of sea time and never once trusted the sea to remember anything for her.

Petra read. The crew read over her shoulders. The wind came up out of the southwest and nobody moved to shorten sail because there was nothing to shorten, they were on engine, and nobody went below, and the Cormorant rocked in the building chop while her captain read a dead woman's handwriting aloud in fragments, voice breaking and steadying and breaking again.

*"...fuel transfer logs don't match manifest. Discrepancy of 40 tons over six months. Someone is taking on cargo we're not being told about."*

*"...asked D. directly. He smiled and changed the subject. Dario does not change subjects. Something is wrong."*

*"...found drum residue in the bilge. Chemical. I've sealed samples in the forward locker. If anything happens to me, whoever reads this should know: I am not worried about the storm."*

The last entry was dated three days before the sinking.

*"Seacocks were inspected Tuesday. All sound. I counted them myself. If we go down, we were opened from the inside. Watch the crew roster. One name doesn't belong."*

The name that followed had been written and then scratched out—scratched out hard, the pen pressed through the paper—but the ghost of the letters remained, and Petra stared at them for a long time, and beside her she heard Teo stop breathing.

Because the scratched-out name was legible anyway. Ink leaves ghosts. Pressure leaves memory.

It wasn't Voss's name.

* * *

Voss's call came at 1900, as the first squall line came up the fjord like a wall falling toward them, rain hissing across the water in advancing curtains. The radio crackled and his voice filled the wheelhouse, smooth and urgent, a man who could make even satellite static sound rehearsed.

"Galloway. My divers report the site was disturbed. Tell me you have it."

Petra keyed the mic and looked at the recorder sitting on the chart table, and at the logbook beside it, and at the crew crowded into the doorway behind her watching her decide.

"I have it," she said.

"Good. First light tomorrow, my seaplane meets you at the north anchorage. Handover, payment, and your crew walks away rich. Every one of them. You have my word."

*Your word,* Petra thought. *The word of a man who opened the seacocks. Or ordered it. Or stood in the wheelhouse and let it happen while Ruth counted handles she'd already counted once.*

"The storm's turning," she said instead. "North anchorage won't hold a seaplane tomorrow. Might not hold anything."

"Then you'll find a way. You always do—that's why I hired you." A pause, silk over wire. "Petra. Whatever you found down there besides the recorder—it stays in the case. We have a contract. Contracts are simple. Don't complicate yours."

He signed off. The radio hissed. Outside, the squall hit the Cormorant broadside and the whole boat leaned, and the crew grabbed for handholds, and nobody spoke.

Petra looked down at the recorder. Cold steel, dead weight, eleven years of a dead woman's last argument with the world sealed inside it. Voss wanted it buried. The navy wanted it buried. Half her crew needed the money more than they needed the truth, and she could see that arithmetic running behind their eyes, and she didn't blame them for it. Money bought new hull plating. Money paid Teo's debts. Truth bought nothing but trouble, and trouble was already anchored off their stern in the form of a patrol boat's running lights, far off but not far enough.

Ruth's voice, thirty years of it, in the back of her skull: *Anything worth recording is worth protecting from the sea.*

And underneath it, newer, colder, from three days before a murder: *Watch the crew roster. One name doesn't belong.*

Petra picked up the recorder. Weighed it. Then she unzipped the front of her foul-weather jacket, slid the data module out of its housing—the small, dense core, the actual memory, small enough to vanish—and zipped it into the inner pocket against her ribs, where her heartbeat would keep it company.

The empty housing stayed on the chart table, in plain sight, waiting for first light and a seaplane and a liar.

Teo saw. She watched him see. Watched his face do the terrible math of a man with debts on one side and a dead mentor's handwriting on the other, and she watched him lose.

Outside, the monsoon came down like a verdict.
