## 7. Voss's Bargain

Dario Voss had spent eleven years learning how to sleep in rooms that cost more than the Cormorant was worth, and he had never once managed it.

He sat in the cabin of the *Albatross*, his seaplane bobbing at anchor two hundred meters off the Cormorant's stern, and watched the salvage boat through rain-streaked glass. The monsoon had pulled back for the night, leaving a wet, bruised calm, the kind of stillness that in his experience was never a gift. It was a held breath.

His security chief, Ibarra, stood by the door with the particular patience of a man paid to wait. On the table between them lay a leather folio, a bottle of thirty-year rum, and a single sheet of paper Voss had rewritten four times since dawn.

"You're going to tell her the truth," Ibarra said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to tell her a truth."

"There's a difference."

"There's always a difference," Voss said. "That's the whole business. That's been the whole business since the beginning."

He thought about the beginning sometimes. Not often, and never voluntarily, but it came for him anyway—usually at three in the morning, usually with the sound of water in it.

* * *

Eleven years ago, the Kestrel had been his father's ship, and his father's ship had been his father's coffin waiting to happen. Old Emil Voss ran cargo through the archipelago the way other men ran errands—casually, constantly, and with a ledger nobody was ever allowed to see. The Kestrel's aft hold had a false floor. Under the false floor, on the night of the storm, there had been forty drums of industrial solvent bound for a buyer who did not exist on any manifest, because the buyer was a shell inside a shell inside a company with the Voss name on the letterhead.

Ruth Okonkwo had been aboard as marine surveyor. Hired by Emil. Trusted by everyone, which was the problem, because Ruth trusted no one, and she had found the false floor on the second day out.

Voss had been twenty-six. He'd been aboard because his father believed—correctly, as it turned out—that a son should learn the family business from the bilge up. He remembered the exact moment Ruth confronted them in the chart room: her voice calm, almost gentle, the way a doctor tells you the test results are bad. *I have to report this. You know I have to.*

He remembered his father's face going gray. He remembered his own panic arriving like a passenger, uninvited, taking up all the air.

And he remembered the storm. God, the storm. It had come early, off-schedule, a wall of black water that the forecasts had missed by twelve hours, and by the time the Kestrel's pumps were screaming, the choice had already been made—not by him, he would swear to that until his dying day, not by him—but the seacocks had been opened, and the Kestrel had gone down in two hundred meters of disputed water with forty drums of solvent in her belly and Ruth Okonkwo in the chart room, gathering evidence no one would ever read.

That was the version he told himself. *The storm did it. The panic did it. My father did it.*

But there was a data recorder in the aft compartment, and Ruth had been recording everything—cargo weights, hull sounds, a running log in her steady, unimpressable voice. And in the last minutes, according to the manifest fragments Voss's people had recovered over the years, she had been recording names.

His father had died three years later, comfortable, decorated, eulogized by ministers. Voss had inherited the name, the company, and the recording's existence, and for eleven years he had kept all three afloat the way you keep a leaking boat afloat: constant vigilance, constant pumping, and never, ever admitting how much water was coming in.

* * *

Petra Galloway came aboard at 0600, alone, which he had not expected. He'd expected Teo—the debt-ridden first mate, the pressure point, the man Voss's people had been cultivating since the contract was signed. He'd expected the crew. He had not expected the rival herself, standing in his cabin with salt in her hair and the recorder's absence written all over her face.

"You don't have it," he said.

"You knew that before you invited me."

"I wanted to hear you say it."

She looked around the cabin—the rum, the folio, the single sheet of paper—and something like contempt crossed her face, quick and honest. "You wrote a speech."

"I write a lot of speeches. It's what the name is for." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. Please. Ten minutes. Then you can walk out and we never have to have this conversation again."

She sat. She did not touch the rum.

"Triple rate," Voss began, and heard how it sounded, and stopped. He set the speech aside—literally, sliding the paper away—and for the first time in eleven years said the thing underneath it.

"I was on the Kestrel the night she went down."

Petra went very still. It was the stillness he remembered from her twenties, from the funeral, from the moment at the dock when he'd offered her the job and she'd looked at him like he was something she'd scrape off her boot. He had spent a decade telling himself that stillness was hatred. Sitting across from it now, he understood it was something worse. It was attention.

"Ruth Okonkwo was my father's surveyor," he said. "She found what we were carrying. And when the storm hit, the ship was scuttled to hide it, and she was aboard when it went down, and I have spent eleven years telling myself I didn't open those seacocks." His voice did something he hadn't authorized. "I didn't. But I was in the chart room, and I saw her recording, and I could have made her stop, or made her leave, or put her in a raft, and I did none of those things, because I was twenty-six and terrified and my father was standing right there, and by the time I understood what was going to happen, the water was already coming in."

The rain had started again, soft against the glass. Petra hadn't moved.

"The recorder has her voice on it," Voss said. "Her last hours. And it has my father's voice, and mine, and it has the cargo manifest, and if it surfaces, it doesn't just end me. It ends two hundred people who work for my company. It ends my mother, who signed nothing and knew nothing. It ends the Voss name, which is the only thing my father ever loved, and which I have spent my entire life holding up like a roof that wants to fall."

"And Ruth?" Petra said. Quiet. Terrible. "What did holding up the roof do for Ruth?"

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing. That's the answer. I know it's the answer. I've had eleven years to find a better one and there isn't one."

He slid the folio across the table. Inside: a transfer authorization for a sum that would retire the Cormorant's debts twice over, a legal shield for every member of her crew, and a signed confession to the scuttling—his name, his father's name, dated and notarized, everything except the recording itself.

"You keep the recorder's existence out of it, and this confession goes to the investigators anyway. The truth comes out. The name falls. My family pays." He leaned forward. "But it pays in a courtroom, on a schedule, with the two hundred innocent people kept whole. Not in a broadcast, in a storm, with a transmitter dying, with no one controlling what gets heard and what gets twisted." He heard himself begging and could not stop. "Petra. I am not asking you to protect me. I am asking you to let me be the one who confesses. Let it be a man walking into the light instead of a box dragged out of a grave."

Petra looked at the folio for a long moment. Then at him.

"Ruth's logbook was beside the recorder," she said. "Did you know that?"

His chest went cold. "No."

"Last entry. She wrote that she knew someone aboard was going to betray her. She didn't say who. She just wrote it down, calm as anything, and kept recording." Petra stood. "You want to be the one who confesses. Fine. But you don't get to choose the order, Dario. You don't get to curate your own drowning. She didn't get to."

She walked to the door. Stopped.

"The crew's vote is tonight," she said. "Teo's already radioed you. I know. I'm not blind." Her hand rested on the latch. "But you should know something about the woman you're trying to buy. Ruth taught all of us the same first lesson: the sea doesn't care what you can afford to lose. It only cares what you'll actually give up."

The door closed behind her.

Voss sat alone with the rum he hadn't poured and the confession no one would read, and outside, far to the north, the sky over the glacier had gone the color of a bruise, and the barometer on his father's old chronometer—wound every morning for eleven years, a ritual he'd never examined—was falling faster than it had fallen on any night since the Kestrel went down.
