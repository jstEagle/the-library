## 8. Mutiny

Petra woke to the sound of the generator coughing and knew before her feet hit the deck that something was wrong. The Cormorant never coughed. Teo kept that engine tuned like a violin.

She found the recorder gone from the dry bag in her cabin—the one she'd wedged under the bunk frame, the one she'd told no one about except, apparently, everyone who mattered.

The chart table light was on in the wheelhouse. Teo stood over it with a handheld radio pressed to his ear, his back to the door, shoulders hunched like a man braced against weather that hadn't arrived yet.

"—yes, she's here, she's aboard, I'm telling you she has it—" His voice cracked on *it*. He turned, saw her, and the radio dropped half an inch. "Petra."

"Say it again," she said. "Into the radio. I want to hear how it sounds the second time."

He set the radio down on the chart table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you're afraid might go off. "Voss called me at anchor. Two days ago. Before the dive."

"Before the dive." Petra leaned against the doorframe. Her hands were steady. She made them steady. "You've been his since before we ever touched the Kestrel."

"He offered to clear my debt. All of it. The Cormorant's hull work, the fuel we owe the yard in Puerto Calvo, my mother's—"

"Your mother's what?"

"Her care home, Petra. Fourteen months of arrears." His jaw worked. "You think I don't know what this boat costs? You think I don't lie awake doing the arithmetic? We were three weeks from losing her before Voss ever showed up at that dock."

"So you sold us."

"I bought us time!" It came out louder than he meant, and he flinched at his own voice. Outside, the sky was the color of wet slate, the fjord walls dissolving into rain. "He's offering a full crew share plus salvage rights. Legal salvage, Petra. Papers, lawyers, all of it. We hand him the recorder, we walk away rich, and nobody drowns for a dead woman's grudge."

"She wasn't a grudge. She was Ruth."

"I know what she was." Teo's eyes were red-rimmed, and Petra realized he'd been crying already, maybe for hours, maybe since the radio call started. "I loved her too. That's not the point. The point is she's eleven years dead and we're alive and broke, and one of those things can still be fixed."

Petra crossed to the chart table and picked up the radio. It was still live—she could hear the faint hiss of an open channel, someone listening. She keyed it once, deliberately, and said nothing, then set it back down.

"Is he coming?" she asked.

"His boat. The security team. They're forty minutes out."

* * *

By the time Petra got the whole crew into the mess, the rain had arrived in earnest, hammering the deck plates hard enough to make conversation a matter of leaning close. Six of them around the table: herself, Nadia, Teo, Marisol from engineering, the Okada brothers diving pair, and young Ferro who'd signed on in Puerto Calvo because nowhere else would take him.

Petra laid it out flat. The recorder. What was on it. What Voss had done to the Kestrel—not just the scuttling they'd found on the wreck, but the dumping operation Ruth had died trying to document. Eleven years of poisoned water and paid-off officials, sitting in a metal box under her bunk until four hours ago.

"Teo took it," she said. "He's called Voss. Voss is inbound."

The silence lasted three seconds. Then the room split down the middle so cleanly it was almost funny.

Marisol spoke first. "We take the money." She said it without apology, arms folded. "I've got a daughter in school and a hull that needs plating. Voss pays triple rate and clears our debts. I didn't sign on to be martyrs."

Ferro nodded fast, too fast. "Triple rate. Petra, that's—I could send money home for a year."

The Okada brothers looked at each other, some silent twin-language negotiation, and Kenji said, "We're with you, Captain. Whatever that means."

Nadia hadn't moved. She sat with her hands flat on the table, watching Petra with an expression that wasn't surprise—it was confirmation. "I came aboard because of the glacier," she said quietly. "But I stayed because of you. I'm with you."

"Six against four," Marisol said. "Or five against five, if you count yourself twice, Captain."

"I don't count myself twice."

"No," Teo said from the doorway. "You count yourself last. You always have. That's the whole problem." He stepped into the room, and Petra saw the recorder in his hand, held against his chest like a child. "It's done, Petra. The deal's struck. Don't make this harder than it has to be."

"The recorder stays on this boat until I decide otherwise."

"It stopped being yours when you pocketed it instead of handing it over! You made us all accessories to your conscience!"

"And you made us accessories to his cover-up. Same crime, better pay."

Teo's face went gray. For a moment—just a moment—Petra saw him waver, saw the eleven years of shared watches and shared funerals fighting the fourteen months of arrears behind his eyes. Then the moment passed, and he turned and went up the ladder into the rain.

* * *

They heard the engine flood twenty minutes later.

Not the main engine—one of the raw-water intake pumps, the port side, the one Marisol had been warning about since the Kestrel dives. Seawater where coolant should be. By the time Marisol got the compartment open, the bilge alarm was screaming and there was six inches of standing water sloshing through the engine room with every roll.

"Intake hose let go," Marisol shouted over the noise. "Or somebody let it let go."

Petra looked at her. "Say that again?"

"I'm saying I don't believe in coincidence on a night like this." Marisol's face was grimy, furious, frightened. "Either way, Captain—we have no propulsion. Not tonight, not in this sea."

The Cormorant began to drift.

Petra stood in the wheelhouse with Nadia beside her, watching the rain erase the world beyond the glass. Behind them, the fjord walls. Ahead, open water and the dark shape of Voss's boat somewhere out there, closing. And further out, on the radar's green sweep, a contact that had been holding station for two hours at the fjord mouth: the navy patrol, patient as a shark.

"They'll board us," Nadia said. "If we drift toward the mouth, they'll board us. And if Voss gets here first—"

"Then we'd better not drift toward the mouth." Petra spun the wheel, useless, feeling the rudder answer nothing. "Options."

"One." Nadia pointed aft, up-fjord, into the black. "The glacier. There's a shallow shelf two kilometers short of the ice face—old moraine. If we ground there, we ground somewhere the patrol boats won't follow and Voss's people can't easily reach. And the calving zone—"

"You want to take a crippled boat toward a calving glacier in a rising monsoon."

"I want to take a crippled boat *away* from two crews of men who want what's in your cabin," Nadia said. "The glacier is just geography. It doesn't want anything."

Petra almost laughed. Almost. Then the wheelhouse door banged open and Ferro came through it soaked to the skin, breathless.

"Captain—it's Teo. He's got the dinghy launched. Him and Marisol and Ferro's brother-in-law—they're pulling away now, heading for Voss's lights." He swallowed. "Kenji's trying to talk them back. They won't."

Petra watched the small running light shrink across the water, swallowed by rain. Half her family, rowing away from her in the dark, carrying eleven years of Ruth in a waterproof case.

"Let them go," she said finally. "Cut the painter loose. We've got a boat to save."

Nadia was already at the helm, hands finding the wheel, reading the wind by feel the way she read ice. "Give me everything the battery's got," she said. "We're going up-fjord."

Behind them, the navy contact on the radar began to move. Ahead, invisible in the rain, the glacier waited—and somewhere between, Voss's security boat was cutting its engines to idle, listening to the same rain, hunting the same darkness.

The monsoon chose that moment to arrive all at once, the way it always did, and the Cormorant turned her broken stern into it and ran.
