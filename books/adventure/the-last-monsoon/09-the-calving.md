## 9. The Calving

The fjord had narrowed to a throat.

Nadia stood at the helm of the dying Cormorant with both hands on the wheel and rain hammering the glass like a fist that would not stop. Ahead, the water ran black between walls of rock streaked white with icefall debris, and at the end of it—two kilometers, maybe less—the glacier face rose out of the sea like a blue-white cliff, two hundred meters tall, cracked and groaning under its own weight. The calving season she'd spent three years studying was happening right now, in real time, in front of her bow.

Behind her, the engine coughed again. A wet, grinding sound, metal chewing on itself.

"Port shaft's gone," Teo shouted from below, before the mutiny, before everything. Now he wasn't shouting anything. He was tied to the mast rail with his own dock line, wrists raw, watching her steer his boat toward a wall of falling ice.

She didn't look at him. She couldn't afford to.

"Petra!" she called aft. "We've got maybe ten minutes of steerage left. After that we're drifting."

"Then we use them well." Petra came up the companionway soaked through, one hand pressed against the bulkhead as the Cormorant rolled. She carried the recorder in a waterproof case strapped across her chest like a heart. "How far to the open channel?"

"Past the glacier? Six kilometers. We don't have six kilometers." Nadia fed the wheel a hard correction as the current grabbed the stern. "The fjord bends east past the calving face. If we can make the bend before the ice lets go, there's an eddy behind the point—slow water, deep enough. We could anchor."

"And if it lets go while we're abeam?"

Nadia did the math she'd done a hundred times in her head since they'd entered the fjord, and hated it every time. "Then we're inside the surge zone. Displacement wave, thirty meters, maybe more. Nobody survives that in this hull."

Petra nodded once, as if Nadia had told her the weather forecast. "So we hurry."

* * *

The storm found its rhythm then, the way monsoons do—a long inhale, then a shove. Rain came sideways off the glacier, cold enough to sting. Visibility dropped to a few hundred meters. Nadia flew by memory: the chart she'd drawn from three summers of survey work, the shape of the water in her hands.

The Cormorant limped forward at four knots, listing two degrees to starboard, bilge pumps running on a battery that was bleeding out faster than the alternator could feed it. Every surface below decks was slick. Somewhere aft, water was still finding its way in through the seam the storm two nights ago had opened, the wound they'd never properly closed because there'd been no time and no money and no port that would take them.

That was the whole story of the last eleven years, Nadia thought. A wound nobody closed because closing it cost too much.

Ruth Okonkwo's voice on the recorder had changed all that. Nadia had heard it only once, played low in the chart room while Petra's face went gray in the lamplight—but once was enough. Eleven years of silence, and Ruth had known. Had named names. Had said *if anything happens to me, it wasn't the weather.*

Ahead, the glacier spoke.

It wasn't a sound so much as a pressure—a deep concussion rolling through the water and into the hull, into the deck plates, into Nadia's boots. A crack spread down the face of the ice, visible even through the rain, a black lightning bolt two hundred meters long.

"That's going," she said quietly. "That's going now."

* * *

Petra clipped the recorder case to her harness and went forward along the rail, one hand on the lifeline, the other holding the boathook she'd taken from the rack without deciding to. Teo watched her come. Rain ran off his chin. He didn't struggle against the line. That was somehow worse.

"You could cut me loose," he said over the wind. "Save yourself the conversation."

"I want to understand it." Petra crouched to his level, bracing against the roll. "Eleven years, Teo. You carried her memorial coin in your pocket every dive. You taught the new kids her knots. And you sold us to him for what—a debt?"

Teo laughed, and there was nothing in it. "For eighty thousand dollars of debt, Petra. For the loan sharks who know where my sister lives. Voss didn't buy me. He bought the thing I was already drowning in." His eyes were red-rimmed, wrecked. "I told myself I'd take the money and disappear before anyone got hurt. I told myself a lot of things."

"The recording goes public or it doesn't exist. Those are the options. Not you, not me, not your sister's address."

"He'll ruin us."

"He killed Ruth." Petra said it flat, and watched it land. "Maybe not with his hands. Maybe just with panic and a locked door and a seacock wrench. But he was there, Teo. He admitted it. And every day that recorder sits in a private safe is another day the Kestrel stays a weather accident instead of a murder."

Teo looked away, toward the glacier, toward the black lightning spreading across its face. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "I radioed him our position. They're coming. Twenty minutes, maybe less. Rigid hulls, six men, armed."

"I know. I heard the traffic."

"So cut me loose and let me take my chances, or keep me tied and let me watch you die doing the right thing. Either way—" His voice broke. "Either way, I'm sorry. I need you to hear that once. I'm sorry."

Petra stood. She took out her knife, and for one long second held it where he could see it, and saw him brace for it—and then she leaned down and cut the line off the rail.

"No," she said. "You don't get to be cargo. You get to bail water. Stern locker, bucket's already floating. Move."

* * *

They came out of the rain at 14:40 by Nadia's watch—two rigid inflatables, running dark, riding the chop with the arrogant ease of boats that had never leaked a day in their lives. Six men in gray. No flag, no markings. Voss's security detail, the same crew that had boarded the Cormorant in the bay with polite smiles and document folders.

Now the smiles were gone. One of them stood in the bow of the lead boat with a loudhailer.

"*Cormorant, heave to. Hand over the device and no one gets hurt.*"

"They're flanking to port," Nadia called. "I can't outrun them. I can barely outrun the tide."

"We're not going to outrun them." Petra was back at the helm beside her, dripping, calm in a way Nadia recognized now as the eye of something. "We're going to let the fjord do it."

Nadia understood a half-second later, and her stomach dropped. "You want to run the calving face."

"I want to run *inside* their decision window. They're professionals. Professionals don't follow a target into a displacement wave." Petra pointed at the glacier, where the crack had widened into a seam wide enough to swallow a house. "How long?"

Nadia read the face the way she'd taught graduate students to read it—the lean of the serac, the meltwater pouring from the base, the sound frequency of the groaning, which had climbed all morning from a rumble toward a shriek. "Minutes. Could be five. Could be sixty seconds. There's no clock on it. That's the whole point of a calving."

"Then we go now, and we pray they're smarter than we are."

"*Cormorant, final warning—*"

Petra took the wheel from Nadia. "Hold the throttle. I'll hold the line."

The Cormorant turned toward the ice.

* * *

It was the worst water Nadia had ever been on, and she'd once spent nine hours adrift in the Denmark Strait. The swell reflected off the glacier face in confused, crossing sets; the wind funneled down the ice and hit the water like a slap. Behind them, the inflatables followed for exactly ninety seconds—she counted—before the lead boat's driver wised up and swung away toward the eastern shore, shouting into a radio. The second boat hesitated.

The second boat hesitated too long.

The glacier let go.

There was no dramatic single block. It was the whole lower face, a hundred meters of it, sliding at once—a silent, obscene shrug, ice the size of city blocks entering the sea without a splash so much as a *swallow*. Then the sound arrived, a crack of thunder that Nadia felt in her teeth, and then the wave.

"Hard to starboard!" she screamed. "Bow into it! BOW INTO IT!"

Petra hauled the wheel over. The Cormorant clawed around, engines screaming their death rattle, and the wave came on—a gray-brown wall, taller than the mast, carrying boulders and shredded ice. It took them like a hand takes a toy.

Nadia's world became white noise and rotation. She remembered the harness line snapping taut, remembered swallowing seawater, remembered Teo's arm locking around her chest plate and hauling her back from the rail as the deck went vertical. Remembered thinking, absurdly, of Ruth's logbook, of the sentence she'd written in a flooded hold eleven years ago: *the sea keeps what it's given.*

Then the wave passed over and past, and the Cormorant surfaced, rolled upright, and floated.

Floating. Still floating.

"Bilge!" Petra roared, and Teo was already moving, and Nadia dragged herself back to the wheel with salt burning her throat and put them on the heading for the eddy behind the point, because the boat was alive and therefore the job was alive, and the job—

The job was the truth.

* * *

The radio room smelled like burnt insulation. The main transmitter had died with the first flooding, but the emergency EPIRB unit had a short-range broadcast function, and Petra had stripped it onto the chart table with a multitool and shaking hands while Nadia kept them steady in the slow green water behind the point.

"It'll carry maybe forty nautical miles," Petra said, splicing wires with her teeth. "Line of sight. But the navy survey vessel is anchored nineteen miles southeast, and they monitor everything. Everything." She looked up, and for the first time since the funeral, since the oath, since any of it, her eyes were clear. "Ruth recorded the dumping operation. Dates, tonnage, the company manifest numbers, Voss's own voice giving the order to open the seacocks if anyone came asking. It's all here. It dies with us, or it doesn't."

"Play it," Nadia said.

Teo stood in the doorway, soaked, holding a half-full bucket he'd forgotten he was carrying. He didn't say anything. He just nodded, once, the smallest motion a man can make and still have it count.

Petra keyed the broadcast. Open maritime channel. No encryption, no call sign, nothing to hide behind.

"This is the motor vessel Cormorant, position the Kestrel fjord. We are transmitting the recovered data recorder of the vessel Kestrel, lost with all hands eleven years ago. What follows is the voice of Ruth Okonkwo, and the voice of Dario Voss. Listen to both."

She hit play.

Ruth's filled the little room first—calm, precise, furious in the way that only very honest people are furious. Then Voss, younger, frightened, saying the words that would end him: *open them. Open them all. If the inspectors board, there's nothing aboard but ballast.*

The recording ran. Outside, the rain began to soften, the way storms do when they've said their piece. The transmitter grew hot under Petra's hands, then hotter, then there was a small pop and a curl of smoke and the light died, and the last audible words trailing out over the water were Ruth's:

*"If anything happens to me, it wasn't the weather."*

Silence. Just the bilge pump, chugging faithfully.

"Did it send?" Teo asked hoarsely.

Petra checked the log buffer. Ninety-one seconds transmitted before the failure. Enough. More than enough.

"It sent," she said.

And then, through the rain-streaked window, all three of them saw it at once: running lights rounding the southern point. Not gray. Not unmarked. White hull, naval pennant, searchlight sweeping the water—the survey vessel, or whatever she truly was, coming up the fjord at speed. Toward them. Or toward the wreckage of the story Voss had built.

Toward the truth, one way or another.

Petra exhaled, and put her hand flat on the dead transmitter, as if taking a pulse.

"Hold station," she said. "Let's go meet them."
