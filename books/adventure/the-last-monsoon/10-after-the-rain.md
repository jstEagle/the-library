## 10. After the Rain

The Cormorant died the way she'd lived—stubbornly, and on her own terms.

Petra felt it through her boots first, a shudder in the deck plates as the flooded engine room gave up its last argument against the sea. Then the list, slow and dignified, like an old woman lowering herself into a chair. Nadia had run them aground in four meters of water off Sermitsiaq Point, close enough to shore that the masts would stay visible at low tide, and now the old boat settled onto the seabed with a groan that Petra felt in her teeth.

"Everyone off," she said. "Now. She's done fighting."

Teo came up the ladder last, soaked to the bone, his hands shaking so badly he couldn't work the lifeline clips. Petra clipped him in herself. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the storm hadn't already said.

They waded the last fifty meters through waist-deep surf, eight of them strung out on a line like beads on a frayed cord, and collapsed onto black sand beneath a sky wrung pale and clean. The monsoon had spent itself overnight. The air smelled of salt and wet stone and, impossibly, of green—the first green any of them had smelled in weeks.

Petra counted heads twice. Eight. All of them.

She looked back at the Cormorant. Her hull sat canted in the shallows, waves breaking white over her deck, and Petra thought: eleven years I've kept her afloat on stubbornness and borrowed money, and she carried us through hell anyway.

"She sank fighting," said Yusuf quietly, beside her. He was holding his broken wrist against his chest, splinted with a piece of chart tube. "That's more than most of us get."

* * *

The investigators arrived by noon.

Two gray-hulled cutters came around the headland with the navy's insignia fresh-painted over older, scraped-off paint, and a helicopter that circled the beach twice before setting down on the shingle above the tideline. Petra stood to meet them, mud to her thighs, hair crusted with salt, feeling like the least credible witness in maritime history.

The lead investigator was a compact woman with a clipboard and the weary eyes of someone who had read too many reports about things everyone already knew. Commander Adeyemi. She looked at Petra for a long moment.

"You're the broadcast," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the broadcast."

"We've been listening to that recording for six hours." Adeyemi glanced toward the cutters, where men in civilian clothes were being walked down a gangway with their hands zip-tied—Voss's security detail, scooped up from their tender when it tried to make open water at dawn. "Half my chain of command is on it. The other half is pretending they haven't heard it yet. You understand this is going to be complicated."

"I understand you're going to need the original file," Petra said. "It's not on the transmitter anymore."

Adeyemi almost smiled. "Where is it?"

"Everywhere," said Nadia, coming to stand at Petra's shoulder. "I pushed it to four mirrors before we lost power. Two of them are academic servers. Good luck making those disappear quietly."

The commander studied them both, then wrote something on her clipboard that Petra suspected was not on any official form.

"The man who owns the seaplane," Adeyemi said, "is currently sitting in my brig, asking repeatedly to speak to whoever has the recorder. He's been told no one has it. He doesn't believe us."

"He's right not to," Petra said. "But he can talk to me when he's ready to say true things. Not before."

"And the crew member who took it from you?"

Petra looked down the beach. Teo was sitting alone on a drift log, elbows on knees, staring at the water as if it might give him back something he'd thrown away. His shoulders were the shape of a man carrying a load he'd finally set down and didn't know how to stand without.

"He'll tell you himself," Petra said. "All of it. That's the deal I made with him, and it's the only one I'd have made."

* * *

They gave Teo a blanket and a thermos of terrible coffee, and he talked for three hours.

Petra didn't listen to most of it. She sat outside the improvised interview tent—a tarp strung between two beached tenders—and watched the tide come back in over the Cormorant's rail. When Teo finally emerged, hollow-eyed and lighter somehow, he crossed the sand to her and stopped a meter away, as if the distance were a border he no longer had papers for.

"They're giving me immunity for testimony," he said. "Against Voss. Against the shipping company. Against—" His voice caught. "Against myself, mostly. They want me to plead to the theft, the radio call. Maybe obstruction."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I signed a contract with a man I knew was rotten because I was scared of being poor." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Eleven years ago I swore an oath on Ruth's grave, and last week I sold it for enough to cover the Cormorant's hull work. That's the arithmetic of me, Petra. That's what I am."

"You're what you do next," Petra said. It wasn't forgiveness. She didn't have forgiveness in her yet, maybe wouldn't for years. But it was a door, left unlocked. "Ruth used to say the sea keeps honest books. Everything gets entered eventually. You entered yours yourself. That counts for something."

Teo nodded, wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and went to sit with the others by the fire. And after a moment—after a long moment—Yusuf shifted over and made room.

* * *

Voss asked to see her at dusk.

They met under guard on the cutter's aft deck, two people who had once shared a mentor and now shared nothing but the wreckage of a name. He looked smaller than he had on the seaplane. Older. The tailored coat was gone; he wore a navy surplus jacket that hung off him like an accusation.

"You could have taken the deal," he said. "The share. The quiet."

"I know."

"Ruth would have—" He stopped. Closed his eyes. "No. Ruth would have done exactly what you did. That's the worst part. I spent eleven years telling myself she died because the storm was cruel, and then eleven days knowing she died because I was a coward, and I can't find the version of the story where I'm not the villain. So I stopped looking."

"Good," Petra said. "Stop looking. Start testifying."

He nodded slowly. "Her family. Does anyone—"

"Her sister's in Lagos. She'll hear it from investigators first, and then from me. In that order. It's what Ruth would have wanted—paperwork before grief."

Voss laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "You really did love her."

"Everyone on that boat loved her. That's why you're in a brig instead of at the bottom of the fjord." Petra turned to go, then paused. "Dario. The Kestrel's going under the ice. Permanently. Whatever's still aboard stays aboard. I hope you can live with that, because you don't get a choice."

* * *

Three days later, the glacier answered.

Nadia found Petra on the headland above the anchorage, where the trail climbed through dwarf birch to a granite shelf overlooking the fjord. Below, the Cormorant lay awash at low tide, salvage crews already picking at her like patient gulls. Beyond her, six kilometers up the sound, the glacier face stood blue and ancient in the clearing air.

"Calving season's peaking," Nadia said. "Should be any day now. I'd have hated for you to miss it."

They stood together without speaking, the way people do who have been through something that words only shrink. Nadia's shoulder touched hers. Petra didn't move away.

"I don't have a boat," Petra said finally. "Eleven years, and I own a hull full of seawater and a duffel bag."

"Mm." Nadia consulted an imaginary ledger. "You also own a recording that brought down a shipping company, a crew that stayed when they could have scattered, a hydrologist who knows every pass on this coast, and—" she reached into her pocket and produced a sodden, salt-stained notebook, its cover warped but its pages legible— "Ruth Okonkwo's final logbook, which the investigators say is now historical record, and which they returned to the person who recovered it."

Petra took it in both hands. The paper smelled of brine and time. She opened it to the last written page, to the handwriting she'd know anywhere, steady even at the end:

*If anything happens to me, the truth is in the hold. Tell my kids I went down asking questions. That's the only way worth going.*

"My kids," Petra repeated. Her voice came out rough. "That's us. She meant us."

"Yeah," Nadia said softly. "I figured that out somewhere around the third bilge pump."

Below them, the fjord went silent—even the gulls seemed to hold their breath—and then the glacier spoke.

The crack came first, a gunshot rolling up the valley, and then the whole face of it moved, a wall of ice the size of a city block leaning out over the water and letting go. The splash reached them a full ten seconds later, a white roar climbing the headland, and the wave rolled down the sound and broke over the Cormorant's exposed rail, and when the water settled, the wreck sat lower, greyer, already being claimed.

Sediment plumed from the calving zone, great brown clouds billowing across the fjord, and Nadia watched them spread with a scientist's satisfaction.

"Glacial flour," she said. "It'll settle over everything in the basin. Within a season, the Kestrel will be buried under meters of silt. Within a decade, nobody finds her again without sonar and a permit." She glanced sideways. "Sealed forever. Just like you promised."

Petra watched the ice settle, watched the plume drift out and begin its long fall toward the wreck below, toward the hold where Ruth Okonkwo had gone down asking questions, and felt something in her chest come loose—not grief, exactly, or not only grief. More like a knot that had been tied for eleven years finally slipping free.

"Rest now," she murmured. "We've got the answers."

They stood until the light went gold and then grey, until the fire smoke from the beach camp rose straight in the windless evening, until Yusuf's voice floated up the trail calling them down for food. Petra put Ruth's logbook inside her jacket, against her heart, and turned to go.

"So," Nadia said, falling into step beside her. "No boat. No contract. No money. What does a famous salvage captain do next?"

Petra considered the question all the way down the trail, past the birch trees, to the edge of the firelight where eight people—or seven, plus one who was learning to be forgiven—looked up as they arrived, and made room without being asked.

"I hear there's a hydrologist who needs a crew," she said. "Somebody told me the whole coast is changing. Glaciers moving, passes closing, wrecks getting uncovered every year." She held out her hands to the fire, and around her, her family leaned in. "Sounds like somebody ought to be there when the water gives up its dead."

Nadia smiled. "Triple rate?"

"Double," Petra said. "And we split the salvage."

Above the fjord, the glacier calved again—smaller this time, almost gentle, like a door closing softly behind them—and the sound rolled out over the water, over the buried Kestrel, over the drowned Cormorant standing her final watch in the shallows, and out to sea.
