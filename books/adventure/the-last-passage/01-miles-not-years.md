## 1. Miles, Not Years

The wind came off the Cordillera del Viento at four in the afternoon the way it always did, like something with a grudge, and Kestrel Villalobos stood in the doorway of her office — a converted shipping container painted the color of rust and regret — and counted the gusts before the first one hit.

Three. It would be a three-gust day. Below five, you could still take clients up to the mirador. Above eight, you didn't even fuel the truck.

She'd stopped counting birthdays years ago. Birthdays were for people whose lives moved in a direction. She counted seasons instead: eleven since the blacklisting, six since she'd opened Villalobos Guias y Travesías out of a container behind the gas station in Puerto Espejo, four since the last season she'd actually turned a profit. The seasons stacked up inside her like sediment, each one pressing down on the last, until what remained was not a woman so much as a compressed record of every year she'd failed to leave.

Puerto Espejo was the kind of town that existed because the map demanded it — a border crossing needed a place to put its customs shed, its diesel pump, its two hotels that were really the same hotel with different signs. The mountains rose behind it in walls of gray and white, close enough that on clear mornings you could see the icefall on Cerro Tres Dedos glittering like a broken chandelier, far enough that nobody who lived here went up there unless someone paid them to.

Kestrel was the only one left who still did.

* * *

The morning had started with inventory, which was to say it had started with arithmetic she already knew the answer to. Sixty meters of rope, half of it glazed and stiff. One rack of hardware missing two cams she'd pawned in a bad February. A satellite phone with a battery that held charge the way a sieve held water. The ledger in her head ran the numbers without being asked: if the season produced nothing, she had maybe fourteen months before the bank took the container, and then the truck, and then whatever version of herself was left after that.

She was re-coiling the bad rope when the Land Cruiser came through town.

It came slow, which told her everything before the paint job did. Tourists drove fast through Puerto Espejo, eager to be elsewhere. Miners drove fast too, drunk on wages. But government vehicles came slow, the way predators walk when they've already decided. Dark green, unmarked except for a plate number that began with the prefix she hadn't seen in eleven years — the prefix that belonged to the ministry office that had ended her career with a single certified letter she could still recite from memory.

*By resolution of the disciplinary commission, your certification as mountain guide is revoked throughout the national territory.*

Throughout the national territory. As if the mountains themselves had signed it.

The Cruiser stopped in front of the container. The engine ticked. Nobody got out for a full minute, and Kestrel understood that too — they wanted her to come to them, wanted the small humiliation of it, wanted to watch her cross her own threshold toward their window the way a client crosses toward a guide, except inverted, except wrong.

She stayed where she was and kept coiling rope. Let them wait. It was the only currency she had left and she spent it carefully.

Eventually the passenger door opened, and a woman stepped out into the wind.

Kestrel had expected a man. They usually sent men — men with soft hands and hard shoes, men who'd read her file the way you'd read a menu, looking for the cheapest thing they could order. Instead: a woman perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, wearing a field jacket that had actually been in the field, boots with real mud on them. She stood with her weight balanced the way climbers stand, feet apart, center low, and Kestrel felt the first cold thread of unease go through her, because this was not an administrator. This was someone who knew what mountains cost.

"You're Villalobos." Not a question. Her voice carried flat against the wind without effort, pitched the way you learn to pitch it above treeline.

"Depends who's asking."

"Sorel." The woman didn't offer a hand. Good sign or bad sign; Kestrel couldn't tell. "Dirección de Recursos Estratégicos."

"There's no such office."

"There is now." Sorel's eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they moved over the container, the rope, the truck, the faded sign — *GUIAS Y TRAVESÍAS, EXPEDICIONES A MEDIDA* — with the unhurried thoroughness of someone cataloguing assets for seizure. "You're a difficult woman to find, Señora Villalobos. No listed number. No forwarding address. Your bank is in another province."

"I find banks don't need to know where I live to keep taking my money."

Something flickered at the corner of Sorel's mouth. Not quite a smile. An appraisal. "May I see your operation?"

"You're looking at it."

"Inside."

"It's a mess."

"So is my schedule. Inside, please."

* * *

The container smelled of paraffin, old coffee, and the particular staleness of nylon stored too long. Kestrel cleared a folding chair of maps and kicked a second one toward Sorel, who sat down without inspecting it for cleanliness, which surprised her more than anything else so far. Government people always inspected things. This one sat like she'd sat in a thousand worse places.

Sorel set a gray folder on the desk between them. Standard ministry stock. Kestrel looked at it and felt her own pulse change, a small betrayal of the body, and hated it.

"Do you know what I do, Señora Villalobos?"

"You said. Strategic resources."

"I find things." Sorel folded her hands. "Mineral surveys. Water tables. Border geology. My office commissions scientific teams to go into terrain that is difficult, remote, and — this is the important part — quiet. We require discretion from everyone involved. Guides especially."

"I haven't guided anyone official in eleven years."

"I know." Sorel opened the folder. "That's why I'm here."

Kestrel made herself look at the page Sorel turned toward her, and the floor of the container seemed to tilt a degree, the way a snow bridge tilts just before it stops being a floor.

Her own name. Her own photograph, twenty-six years old, hair still dark, face still carrying the arrogance of a woman who believed the mountains owed her nothing and therefore could never take anything. Below it, the file number she'd memorized against her will. Below that, the incident report — the one from the north face of Cerro Cumbre, the one about the client named Tomás Herrera, thirty-four years old, fell approximately two hundred meters, body recovered on the glacier nine days later.

Cause of death: fall during guided ascent. Contributing factor: guide error, per commission finding.

Guide error.

Eleven years, and the words still landed in her chest like a cam dropping into a flaring crack — that sick half-second of false security before the hold fails. She had been forty meters above Tomás when the serac let go. Not near him. Not below him. Forty meters above, on a ridge the route description called objectively safe, on a day the forecast had called stable, leading a climb she'd run eleven times without incident. The ice had come down anyway. Ice does that. Ice has never once cared about a route description.

But the commission needed a cause, and the agency running the survey concession on Cumbre that season — the same ministry family, she'd learned later, the same initials on the letterhead — needed the accident buried under a tidy explanation that didn't touch their permits. So they'd found one. They'd found her.

"You've read this," Sorel said. It wasn't sympathy. It was confirmation, the way you confirm a bearing.

"I wrote half of it in my head every night for the first year."

"And the other half?"

"The other half I drank."

Sorel nodded slowly, as if this were useful data. Maybe it was. "Here's what matters now." She turned another page, and the photograph that emerged was of a camp — dome tents, solar panels, a scatter of instrument cases — stamped across the top with coordinates Kestrel recognized before she'd finished reading them. The high basin above the Ventisquero Grande. Above the icefall. Terrain she knew the way you know the layout of a house you grew up in, including the parts that want to kill you.

"A geological survey team," Sorel said. "Six personnel. They went in nineteen days ago to sample a formation in the upper basin. Their last scheduled transmission was twelve days ago. Since then: nothing. A storm system sat on the massif for six days — you'll remember it, it closed the pass — and when the air cleared, our reconnaissance flight found this."

She slid the photo fully onto the desk.

Kestrel leaned over it and her breath went shallow, because the photo was wrong in ways that a person who hadn't lived in ice wouldn't see. The tents were zipped — but zipped from the outside, the pulls hanging loose, the fabric slack in a way that meant empty. The gear was stacked, not scattered — ropes coiled, packs upright, stoves nested — the order of departure, not disaster. And around the camp, the snow lay unbroken. No tracks out. No tracks in. Six days of storm should have drifted the site, softened it, erased detail. Instead the scene looked swept. Preserved. Like a room someone had left on purpose and locked behind them.

"No tracks," Kestrel said.

"No tracks."

"At that altitude, after that storm, there'd be drifts against the tent walls. There aren't. Either this photo was taken within hours of whatever happened—" 

"It wasn't."

"—or someone maintained the site afterward. Or the storm report is wrong." She looked up. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because we need it read by someone who can read it." Sorel's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had shifted, a load transferring, a rope coming taut. "And because you're the only certified—or formerly certified—guide operating out of Puerto Espejo who has led the upper basin. You know the icefall on Tres Dedos. You've crossed the Ventisquero Grande in calving season. There is no one else in this province with your specific experience who isn't either dead, retired, or employed by us already."

"That last category is small because of people like you."

"Yes," Sorel said, without apology, without flinching, and Kestrel felt the strange vertigo of meeting an honest predator after a decade of dishonest ones. "I'm aware of the history between your profession and my ministry. I'm aware some of it is deserved. None of it changes what I'm authorized to offer."

"Which is?"

Sorel named a figure.

Kestrel kept her face still, which took work, because the figure was not a fee. It was a ransom. It was the container paid off, the truck repaired, the cams unpawned, two full seasons of margin — enough to stop counting seasons and start, absurdly, dangerously, counting years again. Enough to buy back the life the file had taken, without ever touching the file itself.

"And the threat?" she asked, because there was always a threat, and better to see it in daylight than step on it at night.

Sorel closed the folder and rested her hand flat on top of it, and Kestrel understood that the gesture was the answer.

"The file stays closed as long as this goes smoothly," Sorel said. "A recovery contract, properly executed, properly documented. If it doesn't go smoothly — if you refuse, if you talk, if you improvise — then the file becomes active again. There's a provision in the original commission findings, never publicly applied, that permits criminal referral. Negligent homicide carries eight to twelve years. At your age, Señora Villalobos, you would come out of it into a country that had forgotten your name entirely. Assuming the mountains left anything to come out to."

The wind hit the container then, a full broadside, and the whole box groaned on its blocks like a ship taking water. Neither woman moved. Outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"One team," Kestrel said finally. "Mine. I choose who comes. I choose the route. When I say a day is dead, it's dead, and nobody above me second-guesses it — including you. Especially you."

"Accepted."

"And I want it in writing that any survivors are brought down alive. All of them. Whatever they saw, whatever they did. That's not negotiable, that's not a term, that's the job. If your office has other intentions, hire someone else and let them live with it."

For the first time, Sorel paused. Just a beat — a hairline crack in the composure, there and gone, like sun glinting off something deep in a crevasse. Then she nodded. "All survivors recovered alive. In writing."

Kestrel looked at the photograph again. The zipped tents. The stacked gear. The snow with no story written on it. Somewhere up there, above the icefall, in the white silence the storm had supposedly filled, six people had vanished so completely that even the weather seemed complicit.

And somewhere underneath her ribs, under eleven years of sediment, something that had been lying very still rolled over and opened its eyes.

"When do we leave?" she said.

"Tomorrow, if you can be ready."

"I've been ready for eleven years," Kestrel said. "I just didn't know what for."

Sorel stood, took the folder, and left the photograph on the desk — deliberately, Kestrel was almost certain, the way you leave bait in a trap you've already sprung. At the door of the container she paused, half in the wind.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the commission findings on Cumbre were wrong. I've read the raw reports. The serac release wasn't forecastable. You were forty meters above him on safe ground. Someone needed a culprit, and you were convenient." She said it the way she'd said everything — flat, factual, a bearing confirmed. "I'm not apologizing. I wasn't there. But you deserve to know that someone in that building knows."

Then she was gone, boots crunching toward the idling Cruiser, and Kestrel stood alone in the paraffin-smelling dark with a photograph of an impossible camp and the sound of her own heart doing something it hadn't done in a decade.

Not fear. Not grief.

Momentum.

She picked up the photo, studied it one more time — the zips, the stacks, the clean snow — and felt the question form that would follow her up the valley for three days and into the ice beyond:

*What kind of people leave a camp like that — and which direction were they facing when they went?*

Outside, the wind dropped to two gusts. A two-gust evening. Good travel weather, tomorrow.

Kestrel reached for the phone to call Dario, and her hand was steady, and that frightened her more than anything Sorel had brought through the door.
