## 2. The Empty Camp

Sorel didn't touch his coffee. That was the first thing Kestrel noticed about the man — he'd ordered it, paid for it, wrapped both hands around the cup like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust, and in twenty minutes not one drop had passed his lips. In her experience there were two kinds of people who did that: the sick and the professional. Sorel had the gray, well-rested look of neither illness nor altitude. He was something else.

He'd asked for a table at the back of La Ruta Perdida, away from the window where the morning regulars watched trucks grind up the valley road. Away from ears. Kestrel had given him the table because refusing would have been a kind of answer, and she wasn't ready to answer yet. She sat across from him with her own coffee going cold and her hands flat on the scarred wood, and waited for him to stop being polite.

"Villalobos," he said again, tasting the name like it came off an old manifest. "You don't use that name much anymore, do you."

"I use the one on my permits."

"Which is the same name." He smiled without warmth. "I've read your file, Ms. Villalobos. All of it. I want you to understand that before we go any further. I'm not here to waste your time with pleasantries I don't mean."

The stove ticked. Outside, a dog argued with a mule train loading for the upper valley, and someone laughed at something that had nothing to do with them. Kestrel turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer.

"Then say what you came to say."

Sorel reached into the inner pocket of his jacket — no briefcase, she noted; men who carried briefcases wanted to be seen carrying them — and laid a manila folder on the table between them. It was thin. Thin folders frightened her more than thick ones; thick ones meant paperwork, and paperwork meant process, and process meant time. Thin folders meant decisions already made.

"This isn't yours," he said. "Yours is in a drawer in the capital, and we'll come back to it. This belongs to a survey team. Six people. Geologists, a glaciologist, two technicians, a camp cook. Contracted through a ministry subsidiary you've never heard of, working a grid above the Ventisquero icefall. They went in on the fourteenth of last month. Their scheduled radio check was the twenty-second." He paused. "There has been no check since."

Kestrel looked at the folder and did not reach for it. "And the ministry called you instead of sending their own people?"

"The ministry called me because their own people can't work above three thousand meters without dying of it, and the ones who can are all either retired or—" his eyes flicked up, unhurried, "—unavailable. You know how it is in this country. Every good guide is either dead, disgraced, or driving tourists to waterfalls."

"Not all."

"No," he agreed. "Not all. But most of the rest won't take a contract from my office, and the ones who will aren't good enough for what's up there." He slid the folder across the wood until it touched her fingertips. "Open it."

She opened it.

The photographs were aerial — drone, by the angle and the clarity, shot low over the glacier in flat midday light. The first showed the survey camp from perhaps two hundred meters: six dome tents in a horseshoe against the rock wall below the icefall, a cook shelter, solar panels angled south, supply crates stacked and tarped. A tidy camp. A camp built by people who expected to come back to it.

The second photograph was closer, and this was where the cold started in her chest and worked downward.

The tents were zipped. All of them. Zipped from the inside — she could see the pulls hanging slack against the fabric, the little nylon tabs lying still, and zippers did not pull themselves closed from outside unless someone was very determined to fake it, and even then they'd have left the pulls down, reachable, not floating mid-door. Gear was stacked beside each tent: boots, some of them, lined up in pairs. A parka folded over a crate. A mess kit arranged with its cup nested inside the bowl. One pair of sunglasses set on top of a fuel can, lenses up, waiting for a face.

Nobody walked into a whiteout wearing socks. Nobody left their sunglasses behind on a glacier at five thousand meters, where the light could blind you in an afternoon. And nobody — no guide alive, no rescuer, no body-recovery team she'd ever heard of — packed out nothing and left everything neat.

"You see it," Sorel said. It wasn't a question.

"I see a camp that was abandoned on purpose," Kestrel said. "By people who intended to come back. Or people who were made to leave in a hurry without their gear." She turned the photograph toward the light. "When was this taken?"

"Three days after the missed check-in. Weather window. We flew the whole grid." He tapped the folder. "Next page."

The third image was wider — the glacier above the camp, the icefall itself, a chaos of blue seracs and leaning towers caught mid-collapse across centuries, and beyond it the high plateau where the survey grid was marked by little orange flags, visible even from altitude as a scatter of bright points like spilled paint. And between the camp and the flags: nothing. No tracks. No trail. The snow lay unbroken from the tents to the rock wall and from the tents down-glacier, a smooth white field with the texture of poured sugar, and the only marks on all of it were the wind's own calligraphy.

"There was a storm," Sorel said. "Day nineteen through twenty-one. Severe. Fifty centimeters of new snow, maybe more, with transport. It would erase footprints."

"It would erase footprints," Kestrel said. "It wouldn't erase a camp. Snow loads tents. Collapses them, if they're not dug out. Those tents are standing clean. Look at the guyline shadows — no drift against the windward walls. Someone dug them out, or someone was there after the storm ended, or—" she stopped, because the third possibility was the one she didn't want to say aloud in front of this man, and saying it would tell him how much she already understood.

"Or?" Sorel prompted softly.

"Or the photographs were staged." She said it anyway, because hiding from it would be worse. "Flown after the fact, dressed to look abandoned. I'm not accusing your office of anything. I'm telling you what the picture shows me."

Something moved behind Sorel's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. Like a man doing arithmetic and getting a number higher than he'd budgeted for.

"The storm ended on the twenty-first," he said. "We flew on the twenty-fourth. Three days. At that altitude, in those temperatures, three days is enough for surface reworking but not enough to bury a camp under fresh accumulation. Your reading is consistent with our own analysts'." He leaned back. "Go on."

"There's nothing to go on. That's the point." She pushed the folder back an inch. "Six people don't vanish from a fixed camp. Either they walked out — in which case there were tracks once, and a direction, and a destination, and someone knows which — or they were taken, in which case there was a helicopter, and your ministry knows about the helicopter, or they never left at all and those tents are full." Her voice stayed level. Inside, some older machinery had begun to turn, slow and heavy, gears she'd spent seven years letting rust. "Which is it, Agent Sorel? Because if the tents are full, this isn't a search. It's a recovery. And recoveries get handled by police, not by men who buy coffee they don't drink."

For a long moment Sorel looked at her, and the café noise seemed to recede around them, the way sound does before weather changes.

"The tents are empty," he said finally. "We know that. Thermal imaging on the second flight. Cold. All six."

"Then where are they?"

"That," said Sorel, "is what I'm paying you to find out."

* * *

He named the figure while the light through the window shifted from white to gold, and it was a figure designed to end arguments. Three years of guiding fees. Five years. Enough to clear the loan on the truck, the loan on the equipment, the quiet debt she owed the outfitter in Pucón whose patience was the only reason she still had a roof that didn't leak. Enough to stop counting seasons and start counting something else — though what, she couldn't imagine, because counting seasons was the only calendar she'd trusted since the mountain took Tomás Arrieta off the north face and took her name off every permit list in the country the same week.

She didn't answer the number. She let it sit on the table between them like a weight, and she watched Sorel watch her not answering, and she understood that the money was the smaller half of the offer.

"Say the other part," she said.

Sorel took a photograph from his own jacket this time, separate from the folder, and held it up without giving it to her. It was a scan of a document — dense type, stamps, signatures — and at the top, in the small print she couldn't read from across the table, a header she recognized by its shape alone. Incident Review 114-C. North Face, Cordillera del Viento. Seven years ago.

"Your file," he said. "The official version says client error. Unroped movement above the fixed line. Tragic, regrettable, and entirely the fault of a thirty-four-year-old accountant from Santiago who ignored his guide's instructions." He lowered the page. "The unofficial version is longer. There were two review boards, not one. The second board's findings were sealed. Your blacklisting was administrative, not judicial — which means it can be un-administered. By the same office that administered it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere behind the counter the radio murmured a news bulletin about rain in the lowlands, and Kestrel sat very still and felt the trap assemble itself around her with the quiet precision of a crevasse bridge holding, holding, holding.

"You're offering to clear my name."

"I'm offering to restore it. There's a difference. Clearing is forgiveness. Restoration is erasure — as if it never happened. As if Tomás Arrieta fell because of his own mistake and Sebastian Crane's best guide simply went on guiding, season after season, with an unblemished record." He set the photograph face-down on the table, next to the folder, so that the two documents lay side by side: the money and the name, the carrot and the knife, though he'd been careful not to show her which was which. "Or you refuse, and the file stays where it is, and every season you scrape together another outfit, another permit, another marginal living at the edge of the map, some clerk in the capital red-stamps your application and you spend another year wondering why."

"I stopped wondering why years ago."

"Did you?" Sorel picked up his coffee at last and drank, and grimaced slightly, as if he'd only now remembered he didn't like it. "Because I read the incident report twice, Ms. Villalobos. All forty pages. And I noticed something the boards didn't discuss. The radio log from base camp that day contains a transmission at fourteen-twelve, ninety seconds before the fall. Incoming, priority flag, origin code belonging to a government frequency allocation. Ninety seconds later, your client was above the fixed line, and ninety seconds after that, he was dead." He turned the cup slowly on its saucer, mirroring her earlier gesture, whether deliberately or not she couldn't tell. "You told the first board you never heard that transmission. The second board sealed the question rather than answer it. I've always found that interesting."

Kestrel's hands had gone cold on the warm mug. Fourteen-twelve. She remembered fourteen-twelve. She remembered the squelch click on channel three, the half-second of static, the way she'd looked up from the rope bag expecting base camp and gotten silence, and then the shout from above, and then the world rearranging itself into the shape it would keep for seven years. She had told herself for a decade of nights that the static was nothing. That radios crackled. That mountains killed people for simpler reasons than messages.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked quietly.

"Because I need you to understand that I know things about your past that you don't know yourself. And because the same office that buried that transmission is the office asking you to walk onto a glacier where six people have vanished without tracks." He met her eyes. "I am not the man who buried it. But I work in the building where it's buried, and I am offering you a shovel. Find my survey team, bring back whoever's alive to bring back, and Incident Review 114-C gets reopened with my signature on the request. Refuse, and it stays buried, and so does whatever really happened at fourteen-twelve."

Outside, the light had gone long and amber, and the mule train was gone, and the dog had won or lost its argument, and the town went on with its small evening business around a woman who could feel her life tilting on its axis like a serac in October sun.

"And if I find them dead?" she said. "All six. Frozen. What then?"

"Then you bring back proof of death, and the contract pays the same, and the file opens just the same." Sorel stood, buttoning his jacket, a man whose business was concluded. "But I'll tell you what my analysts think, since you read photographs better than they do. Six people don't walk off a glacier barefoot into a storm. Something up there made them leave everything and run — or hide. And whatever it was, it's still up there, because nothing has come down. Not one of them. Not in three weeks." He put money on the table, too much, covering both coffees and a tip for a waitress who hadn't served them. "Think tonight. I'm at the municipal hostal under the name Reyes. I'll expect your answer by eight."

He paused at the edge of the table, one hand on the chair back, and for the first time since he'd walked in, something almost human crossed his face — fatigue, or its impersonation.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the fee clears your debts. But that's not why you'll say yes." His eyes went to the face-down photograph of the sealed report. "You'll say yes because you've spent seven years wanting to open that file yourself, and now someone's handing you a reason the office can't refuse."

He left. The door swung shut behind him and the café exhaled, and Kestrel sat alone with two cold coffees, a folder of impossible photographs, and the memory of a radio click at fourteen-twelve on a day the mountain ate a man whole.

She turned the folder toward her and looked at the second photograph again — the zipped tents, the paired boots, the sunglasses waiting on the fuel can for faces that hadn't come back for them — and beneath the dread, unwelcome as meltwater under snow, she felt the other thing rising. The old hunger. The pull of an unsolved route, an unread mountain, a question with teeth.

Someone was alive up there. Everything about the camp said it: the gear stacked for return, the zippers pulled shut by hands intending to come back through them. People who died on glaciers died messy. These six had left like travelers leaving a room they meant to sleep in tomorrow.

She thought of the storm-erased snowfield, the unbroken white running up toward the icefall, and of what might be walking on it — or hiding under it — right now, tonight, while she sat drinking cold coffee in a border town at the edge of the map.

Eight o'clock, he'd said. Under the name Reyes.

Kestrel finished her coffee anyway, cold as it was, and stared out the window at the Cordillera del Viento going violet and then black above the valley, and began, without deciding to, to count the days until calving season.
