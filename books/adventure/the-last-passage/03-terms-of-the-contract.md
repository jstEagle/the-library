## 3. Terms of the Contract

Sorel left the file on my table overnight. That was the first move in his game and he knew it — leave the thing open like a wound, let it bleed while I slept badly beside it.

I didn't sleep. I sat up with a tin cup of aguardiente going warm in my hand and read every page twice, because reading it was like pressing on a bruise to confirm it still hurt. Ten years old and the paper still had teeth. *Villalobos, K. — Incident Report 44-C.* The dead man's name was Tomás Reyes, forty-one, engineer, father of two. Cause of death: fall into crevasse, unroped, during whiteout conditions. Guide error: failure to enforce rope protocol. My signature at the bottom of the acknowledgment page looked like it belonged to someone else — smaller, more careful, a woman who still believed signatures meant something.

What the report didn't say — what no report ever said — was that Tomás had unclipped himself against my direct order, that he'd been carrying samples for a client whose name never appeared anywhere in the file, and that when I'd tried to tell the inquiry board this, the client's name had been redacted from my own testimony before it reached them. Someone above me had decided how the story went, and I'd been handed the ending they'd already written.

I'd spent ten years telling myself I'd made peace with it. Sitting there with Sorel's folder open under the lamp, I understood that peace was just a word for a debt you've stopped trying to collect.

By dawn I'd made my list. Not of reasons to say yes — of terms. If I was going to sell something, I was going to set the price myself. It was the only dignity left in the transaction.

* * *

Sorel came back at eight, punctual as a tax, and found me waiting with the file squared on the table between us like a border marker.

"Terms," I said, before he could sit down. "You want my expertise, you pay for it like it's expertise."

He sat anyway, unhurried, folding one long hand over the other. "Go on."

"One. The fee doubles from whatever number you were planning to say. Half now, half on return. Cash."

"Half now is possible. Doubling—" He tilted his head, doing arithmetic he'd already done. "The budget has limits."

"Then the budget can find out what happens when the only guide willing to go above the icefall in calving season decides her conscience is more expensive than yours." I let that sit. "Two. When this is done — however it's done — my file closes. Not annotated. Not 'under review.' Closed. Reyes incident expunged, blacklist lifted, my certification reinstated with backdated clearance."

"That's not mine to give."

"Everything's someone's to give, Agent. You knew that when you walked into my kitchen with ten years of my life in a manila folder. Find whoever owns it and buy it. Three." I leaned forward. "My team comes off the mountain whole or I don't come back at all. Whatever's up there, whatever your agency actually wants — my people are not collateral. You write that into the contract. In writing."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. He'd expected a broken woman grateful for scraps; he was getting a negotiator, and negotiators are harder to bury.

"And if I refuse these terms?" he asked mildly.

"Then I take the file to the newspaper in Río Blanco. All of it. Redactions and all. A disgraced guide's word against the state's isn't worth much," I said, "but it's worth more than nothing, and it would embarrass you, and men like you hate embarrassment more than failure."

For a long moment he studied me the way a buyer studies a horse — checking the legs, the teeth, the temperament. Then he smiled, thin and professional, and I understood I hadn't frightened him. I'd amused him. That was worse.

"You misunderstand your position, Señora Villalobos," he said quietly. "You believe you're negotiating. You're not. You're choosing which version of the next month you'll live through. There's the version where you go up that glacier with my money in your pocket and come down with your name clean. And there's the version where you don't go, and the file stays open, and other people — people less careful than you — get sent instead, and we both know how those expeditions end." He stood, buttoning his jacket. "But I admire the attempt. Half now. The rest on delivery. The file — I'll make a call. Whether anyone listens depends on results."

"And my team?"

"Your team is your responsibility. That's what a guide is for, isn't it?" At the door he paused. "One week. The icefall won't wait, and neither will I."

The door closed. I sat looking at the money he'd left — an envelope thick enough to stop a door — and hated him, and hated the mountain, and most of all hated the small, traitorous part of myself that felt relief. Because the truth was simple and ugly: I wanted to go. Not for the money. Not even for the file. I wanted to go because above the icefall was the only place I'd ever been certain of who I was, and ten years of certainty withheld leaves a hunger that doesn't care who feeds it.

That was the sin I carried up the stairs to pack: not greed, not fear. Wanting.

* * *

Dario Ferreyra kept bees now. Of all the things ten years had done to the people I used to know, that was the detail that undid me — Dario, who'd once led a traverse across the Gran Ventisquero in a storm that killed two others teams' clients, Dario with hands like root vegetables and a voice that carried over avalanche terrain, standing in a backyard full of smoking hives in a village three hours south, wearing an apron.

He saw me coming up the lane and didn't smile. Rope partners don't need to. He took off a glove and shook my hand anyway, and his grip was still the same: brief, hard, a question asked and answered.

"Kestrel Villalobos," he said. "Somebody finally dug you up."

"Somebody with a government badge."

"Ah." He looked at me for a moment, then turned and shouted something at the hives — a habit, I realized, talking to bees so he wouldn't have to talk to people — and waved me toward the house. Inside smelled of wax and woodsmoke. His wife put coffee in front of us without being asked and left, and Dario sat across the table and waited, because he'd always known that I'd talk eventually and rushing me had never once worked.

So I told him. The camp, the photos, the tents zipped from inside. The coordinates above the icefall. Sorel and the file and the envelope of money. I watched his face do what faces do when they hear about the high ice in calving season — the flinch he hid, the calculation he didn't.

"No," he said, when I finished.

"Dario—"

"I said no." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a nervous tic I remembered from twenty years ago, from bivouacs and storm camps. "You know what's up there this time of year. You know what the seracs do when the melt starts. We're not young. I have bees. I have—" he gestured vaguely at the house, the yard, the life he'd built out of the wreckage we'd both crawled away from, "—this."

"The fee clears my debts. All of them."

"I don't care about your debts."

"It's Elias Vidal's team, Dario."

That stopped him. Everyone in the guiding world knew the name; the Cordillera survey had been famous before it vanished, front-page material, six scientists and their porters gone above the icefall like the mountain had swallowed them whole. But that wasn't why Dario went still. Elias Vidal had been at our wedding — not mine, there had never been a wedding of mine, but the wedding of Marisa, Dario's sister, where Elias had played guitar badly and drunk too much and cried about a woman none of us ever met.

"Elias," Dario said slowly, "is dead. Everyone says he's dead."

"Everyone says. Nobody knows. The camp was struck deliberately. Somebody walked out of there alive, and somebody is paying me to find out who, and I don't trust the somebody." I leaned forward. "Which is exactly why I need you. Because you're the only person I've ever roped with who I'd trust to tell me I'm wrong. Come with me and if I start lying to myself up there, you say so. Like always."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, a bee blundered against the window glass, persistent, stupid, doomed, and I thought: there's my portrait.

"My wife will kill me," Dario said at last. "Slowly. Over years. With commentary."

"That sounds like marriage."

"It is." He exhaled through his nose, the sound he made before committing to anything irreversible. "Half share. And Kestrel — if this goes bad, if it goes the way the Reyes thing went bad, I'm not carrying that again. Neither are you. We get everyone down or we don't play hero. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, and we shook on it, gloveless, and his hand was shaking slightly, and so was mine, and neither of us mentioned it, because that too was rope-partner language.

* * *

Nena Quispe found me, which should have warned me. Twenty-three years old, built like a whip, she showed up at my door in Punta Fría two days later with a borrowed rucksack and a look of such naked, uncomplicated admiration that I felt like a fraud before she said a word.

"They said in town you were hiring for a high route," she said. "Porter work. I'm strong. I carry double loads. I've done the Vallecitos circuit twice and the north ridge of Cerro Hacha solo."

"Solo?" I said. "In what season?"

"October." She lifted her chin. "It was fine."

"It was luck. October on the north ridge is a lottery ticket." I looked her over the way Sorel had looked at me — legs, teeth, temperament — and hated myself for it. "Who told you to come?"

"Nobody. I've been watching your outfit since I was fifteen. You're the best guide this range ever produced. Everybody says so."

*Everybody says so.* The words landed like a slap, because everybody also said the other thing, the thing that traveled faster than any reputation: the guide who lost Tomás Reyes. Nena stood there glowing at me, twenty-three and certain, and she had no idea she was asking to apprentice herself to the worst year of my life.

"Nena." I stepped outside and closed the door behind me, because some conversations shouldn't happen under a roof. "Look at me. Do you know why I run a marginal outfit in a town nobody guides from? Why the best guide in the range takes tourists to waterfalls for fees a taxi driver would laugh at?"

She hesitated. "People say... there was an accident. Years ago."

"There was a man named Tomás Reyes. He fell four hundred meters into a crevasse because he unclipped his rope in a whiteout, against my order, and I couldn't reach him. Two daughters grew up without a father. The inquiry blamed me. The state blacklisted me. And every year since, I have counted seasons instead of birthdays, because seasons are honest and birthdays lie." I held her gaze. "I'm telling you this so you understand what you're signing up for. This job — the one I'm hiring for — is above the icefall, in early calving season, for an employer I don't trust, hunting a man who may not want to be found. People die on routes like that. Good people. Prepared people. If you come, you come knowing all of it, and if you die, there will be no one to blame but the mountain and me, and the mountain gets the larger share."

Most people my age would have softened it. I didn't, because softening it would have been the real cruelty — letting her walk into the white room of the high ice believing stories instead of numbers.

Nena was quiet for a moment. Wind moved through the street, rattling a tin sign across the way. Then she said, "My brother died in a mine collapse when I was sixteen. The company paid for the funeral and nothing else. I've been climbing out of holes ever since." She adjusted the borrowed rucksack on her shoulders. "I'm not afraid of dying, señora. I'm afraid of staying. Quarter share, and I cook better than anyone you've ever hired."

Against every rule I had — against the rule I'd made on the glacier ten years ago, *never bring anyone who reminds you of yourself* — I heard myself say, "Be at the trailhead Thursday. Six a.m. Bring your own rope."

Her face lit up like a headlamp, and I turned away before she could see what the light cost me.

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless, the kind of sky that means business higher up. I stood at the trailhead with the packs laid out and checked each one twice, the old liturgy: stove, fuel, rope, protection, medical kit, radio. Dario arrived first, quiet, his bees left in his sister's care, his eyes already on the white line of the range. Nena arrived second, early, breathless, having walked through the night from her village to prove some point she didn't need to prove.

We weighed loads. We tied in for a practice drill in the parking lot like children, and I watched Nena's hands move — fast, correct, hungry — and felt the old machinery of leadership engage whether I wanted it to or not. Some things live in the body past all disgrace.

"Route plan," I said, crouching over the map with them. "Three days up the valley to base of the icefall. We cross on the fourth morning, cold hours only. Above that, we follow the coordinates and we find out what happened to six people who left their tents zipped shut." I looked up at them both. "Rules. One: nobody moves unroped on glacier, no exceptions, no matter how experienced, no matter how short the distance. Two: we turn around when I say turn around, and I say it earlier than any of you will want. Three—" I paused, and the third rule almost didn't come out, because saying it aloud made it true. "Three: whatever we find up there, we find it together. Nobody breaks off alone. Not for anything."

Dario nodded slowly. Nena wrote it in a little notebook, actually wrote it, and I thought: God help me, she's taking notes on how to become me.

We shouldered the packs. Behind us the town lay flat and brown and safe, and ahead the valley climbed into cloud, and somewhere above the icefall the mountain was keeping its own ledger, its own files, its own terms.

We'd made maybe two kilometers when Nena, ranging ahead on the trail, stopped and crouched. "Señora. Look at this."

It was a survey marker — aluminum stake, orange flagging, the kind Elias Vidal's team had planted along their transect lines. Standard. Except the flagging was fresh, bright as a wound, and the stake had been driven into new ground a full kilometer uphill of where any sane survey line would run — straight up-slope, against the grain of the terrain, pointing at the high glacier like a finger.

Dario crouched beside me. "That wasn't here last season," he said quietly. "And nobody plants markers going up. Survey lines follow contour."

I looked at the flagging snapping in the wind, and then up the valley to where the icefall glinted white and blind behind its curtain of cloud, and I felt the first cold thread of it slide down my spine — the sense, familiar as an old injury, that the mountain had already started rearranging itself around us.

"Mark it," I said. "And keep your eyes open. From here on, everything means something."
