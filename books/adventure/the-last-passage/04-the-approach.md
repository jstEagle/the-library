## 4. The Approach

The valley took them the way a throat takes water — slowly, and with no intention of giving anything back.

Kestrel set the pace at the front because she always did, boots finding the old drover's path beneath two seasons of flood silt. Behind her came Nena with sixty pounds of rope and hardware she'd insisted on carrying herself, and behind Nena came Dario, quiet in the way of men who were saving up their words.

Three days to the icefall. Four if the river crossing was running high. She'd told Sorel five, to buy margin, and Sorel had smiled like a woman who knew exactly what margin was for.

The first day was all green. Poplar and aliso crowding the trail, the river loud on their left, the peaks still hidden behind their own foothills like actors refusing the stage until they were sure of the audience. Kestrel counted her steps without meaning to. It was an old habit from the years when counting steps was how you kept a client alive — a thousand paces to the moraine, four hundred more to the spring, and if your count drifted you stopped and looked up and found yourself again by the shape of the mountain. Nobody had needed her counts in six years. Her body hadn't gotten the message.

"Señora Villalobos." Nena's voice, bright with the effort of keeping it level. "How do you know where the trail is? I can't see anything."

"Silt," Kestrel said, not turning. "Floods bury a path flat, but they don't change what's under it. The ground remembers even when it looks like nothing."

A pause, then the scrabble of boots as Nena caught up to walk beside her, wanting to see. Wanting to learn. That was the trouble with porters who wanted to learn — they became guides, and then they became liabilities, or legends, depending on the season. Kestrel had been both.

Dario watched this exchange from ten meters back and said nothing, which was itself a kind of speech. Twenty-two years roped together said things that mouths didn't have to.

* * *

They camped the first night below the confluence, where the white water met the grey and the two rivers argued in a long braided seam. Kestrel got the stove going while Nena sorted gear with a competence that surprised her — every buckle cinched, every pole sectioned, the tent fly hung to dry without being asked. Somebody had raised this girl right, or raised her hard; from a distance the two looked identical.

"You've done this before," Kestrel said.

"My father drove mules to the mines above Pampa Blanca." Nena fed the flyline through its tensioner, precise. "He died in a whiteout coming down. They found him in spring, twenty meters off the trail."

Kestrel's hands went still on the stove valve. "I'm sorry."

"He wasn't lost." Nena said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "He was blown off course. There's a difference. My mother made me learn the difference before she let me take work in the mountains."

*There's a difference.* Kestrel turned back to the stove so the girl wouldn't see her face do whatever it was doing. Six years ago there had been a man blown off course too, a client named Tomás Reyes, and the difference between lost and blown off course had been the difference between Kestrel's whole life and the ruin of it. The inquiry had used the word *negligent*. The blacklist had used the word *unfit*. Neither word had ever once asked what the wind was doing at 4,800 meters at fourteen hundred hours, because the wind didn't sign contracts and couldn't be held liable.

She lit the burner. Blue flame, steady. She made herself watch it instead of the darkening valley.

Across the fire ring, Dario was watching her watch the flame, and he saw everything, because he always had.

* * *

The second day the valley narrowed and the mountains finally showed themselves — granite walls going up and up into cloud, hanging glaciers leaking meltwater in silver threads. The trail climbed away from the river onto a shelf of old moraine, and the air began to taste of stone and distance.

It was mid-morning, on a stretch of switchbacks, that Dario drew level with her.

"You look well," he said. "For a woman who doesn't sleep."

"I sleep fine."

"You sleep like someone listening for a door." He adjusted his pack straps, casual as a man discussing the weather. "Sorel. What did she show you?"

"A file."

"Your file."

"The agency keeps files on everyone. You probably have one too. Thicker than mine, knowing your politics."

Dario laughed, short and dry. "My politics are cheap. Two kids in school in Huaraz and a wife who thinks I guide rich tourists because rich tourists tip. That's my file." He let three switchbacks pass. "Yours has a body in it."

The word landed between them like dropped gear.

"Don't," Kestrel said.

"I'm not saying anything. I'm just remembering out loud." His voice stayed gentle, which was worse. "Tomás Reyes. 2019. You brought his party down through the serac zone because the forecast window closed early, and the mountain did what mountains do. And some clerk in Lima decided that a dead senator's nephew needed a reason that wasn't weather."

"Stop."

"So you stopped guiding high routes. You run tourists up to hot springs and call it an outfit. And now a government agent walks into your office with your own sins in a folder and asks you to go back above the icefall, and you say yes inside of a day." He shook his head slowly. "Kestrel. In twenty-two years I have never known you to say yes to anything that fast."

She kept climbing. One step. Another. The rhythm held her together the way it always had.

"There's money in it," she said finally.

"There's money everywhere. There's never been money in you before." Dario let himself fall back a few steps, giving her the mercy of his absence. "Whatever she's holding over you, you could tell me. That's all. You could tell me, and we'd carry it together, the way we carried everything else."

But that was exactly what she couldn't do, because telling Dario meant making it real, and making it real meant admitting that after six years of building a small hard life that no one could take from her, she'd handed it back to the people who'd taken everything the first time.

Ahead on the trail, Nena had stopped. She was crouched over something at the edge of the path.

* * *

It was a survey marker — an aluminum stake with a reflective tag, the kind the geology teams used, stamped with a code: **CV-114**. Standard enough. Except for where it stood.

Kestrel knelt beside it and felt the cold come up through her knees before her mind caught up with the wrongness.

Survey markers don't move uphill.

That was the whole grammar of them. You planted a stake, you logged its coordinates, and gravity and time did the rest — markers slid downslope with the freeze-thaw, toppled into gullies, sank into melt. Every marker in the Cordillera drifted down. This one had been pulled up out of its socket and re-set forty vertical meters higher, wedged into a crack in the bedrock, angled deliberately toward the glacier.

And the soil around it was undisturbed. No boot prints. No drag marks. Nothing.

"When was the survey team here?" Nena asked.

"Six weeks ago," Kestrel said. "Before the storms."

"Then the storm moved it."

"Storms push things downhill." Kestrel worked the stake loose and weighed it in her hand. The base was clean — recently pulled, recently reset. Within days, maybe. After the storms. After the team vanished. "Someone put this here."

Dario had come up quietly and was looking at the angle of the tag, the way it faced the ice. "A signal," he said. "Or a joke."

"Geologists don't joke with stakes."

"No," he agreed. "But sometimes they leave messages."

Kestrel looked uphill, along the line the tag pointed, toward the white shoulder of the icefall where the clouds sat low and unmoving, like something waiting. Six weeks ago seven people had walked up there with instruments and permits and the full faith of the state behind them. Now there was a marker standing where no marker should stand, pointing where no survey pointed, and the ground around it was swept clean as a table after dinner.

She drove the stake back into its new socket, exactly as found, and marked the position in her notebook.

"We make the old camp tomorrow afternoon," she said. "We move fast and we don't talk about this again until we're there."

Nena looked like she wanted to ask why. She was learning already, Kestrel thought. Learning that on a mountain, some questions you carry instead of asking.

* * *

The second night was colder, the first real bite of altitude, and they camped in the lee of a lateral moraine with the glacier's breath moving over them — that particular wind that comes off ice after sundown, colder than the air it replaces, smelling of nothing at all. Kestrel took first watch because she never slept early anymore, and sat wrapped in her bag with the thermos, watching stars come out in the gap between the peaks.

Nena couldn't sleep either. Kestrel heard her unzip and come sit nearby, close enough to share warmth without asking, which was good mountain manners.

"Can I ask you something?" the girl said.

"You just did."

"Why did you say yes to this job? Dario asked you today and you wouldn't answer."

Kestrel drank her tea. It had gone lukewarm and tasted faintly of the metal flask, which was the taste of every expedition she'd ever been on. "You heard that."

"I hear most things."

Of course she did. Kestrel almost smiled. "You want the true answer or the professional one?"

"Is there a difference?"

"On this mountain there always is." Kestrel turned the flask in her hands. "The professional answer is that the fee clears my debts and the route is within my ability and the client is the state, which pays whether it likes you or not."

"And the true one?"

"The true answer is that a woman came into my office with a folder, and in that folder was a photograph of me at age thirty-one standing on a summit I swore I'd never stand on again, and she said *we need you*, and some stupid animal part of me — the part that's been starving for six years — stood up and wagged its tail." She said it flatly, without self-pity, because self-pity above 3,000 meters was a luxury that killed. "That's the true answer. Don't repeat it."

Nena was quiet for a while. Then: "My mother says the mountains keep what you give them. She says my father gave them his attention, and they kept it."

"That's a cruel way to think about it."

"It's an honest one." The girl pulled her bag tighter around her shoulders. "What did you give them?"

Kestrel looked up the valley, to where the icefall would be, invisible now, somewhere beyond the black teeth of the ridgeline. Somewhere up there was a camp with tents zipped shut from the inside and no tracks leaving it, and tomorrow she would have to read that scene like a page, and she already knew, in the place where guides know things before they know them, that the page was going to be wrong in ways she didn't have words for yet.

"Everything," she said. "Ask me again in a week."

* * *

The third day the green gave out entirely.

By midmorning they were walking on mineral earth — grey scree, rust-stained rock, the bones of the range showing through. The river shrank to a milky thread of glacial melt. The air thinned and sharpened until every breath cost something, and Kestrel shortened the pace without announcing it, the way you do, letting the clients believe they were strong rather than letting them discover they were slow.

Dario didn't probe her again. He walked all day with his eyes on the terrain, reading it the way he read everything, and twice he glanced back down-valley at nothing, and the second time Kestrel noticed, and filed it, and said nothing. Paranoia was a tool like any other. You didn't swing it until you knew what you were driving.

Nena, meanwhile, was proving tougher than her frame promised. At the last steep pull before the bench where they'd camp, with the altitude pressing on all of them, the girl climbed like she'd been poured from a different mold — steady, uncomplaining, breathing through her nose in a rhythm Kestrel recognized because it was her own rhythm, learned from the same dead mule-driver tradition. When they topped out, Nena didn't collapse dramatically the way clients did. She set down her load, unpacked the stove, and had water heating before Kestrel had finished picking the campsite.

"You'll want to lead your own ropes someday," Kestrel said, taking the pot from her.

"I want to work," Nena said. "Leading is extra."

* * *

They saw the camp at four in the afternoon, and Kestrel made them stop a full kilometer short.

It sat on the flat bench above the icefall where the survey team had established base: three dome tents, a cook shelter, instrument cases stacked and lashed under a tarp. From this distance it looked orderly. Abandoned, but orderly — the way a camp looks when people mean to come back.

Except.

Kestrel lay on her stomach at the edge of the moraine with her binoculars and read the scene top to bottom, left to right, the way she'd been taught by a man in Chamonix who claimed you could read a camp like a face.

No tracks. Not degraded tracks, not storm-erased tracks — the ground around the tents was *clean*, scoured in a way that wind alone didn't manage. Wind left drifts, left tails of snow behind obstacles, left the story of its own passing. This ground had been erased.

The tents were zipped from the inside. All three. She could see the pulls sitting in their sliders, tiny bright points in the glass.

And beside the cook shelter, weighted under a flat rock too deliberate to be accident, a single sheet of paper fluttered in the wind — white against the grey, turning over and over, like something waving.

Or like something left to be found.

"Nobody goes down there yet," Kestrel said. She lowered the binoculars. Her mouth had gone dry in a way the altitude didn't explain. "We camp here tonight. First light, we go down together, roped, and nobody touches anything until I've read it."

Dario crouched beside her and took the glasses. He looked for a long time.

"Kestrel," he said quietly. "If those tents are zipped from the inside—"

"I know what it means."

"Then either seven people walked out of sealed tents and vanished into a storm—"

"Or," Kestrel said, "someone wanted us to think they did."

Below them, on the bench, the paper turned in the wind. White. Patient. Waiting for a reader.

She reached for her notebook, and her hand was not quite steady, and she hated that it wasn't, and she wrote down the coordinates anyway — the same ones, she realized with a cold settling in her chest, that had been inked on the journal page in Sorel's photograph. The camp was exactly where the file said it would be.

Exactly. As if someone had arranged it that way.

As if someone wanted her to find it — and wanted her to find it looking exactly like this.
