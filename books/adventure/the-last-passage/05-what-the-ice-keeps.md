## 5. What the Ice Keeps

The camp appeared out of the weather like a shipwreck surfacing from deep water.

One moment Kestrel was breaking trail through knee-deep windslab above the icefall, Nena's breath sawing behind her and Dario a gray shape further back, and the next the slope flattened into a bench of blue ice and there they were: three dome tents, still pitched, their flysheets furred with rime, standing in a row like headstones.

She stopped so abruptly that Nena walked into her pack.

"Is that—" the girl began.

"That's it." Kestrel's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Camp Two. Survey team."

Nobody moved for a moment. The wind had died in the last hour, and the silence up here was not empty but pressurized, the kind of quiet that made you aware of your own pulse in your ears. The glacier ticked somewhere beneath them, settling, patient.

"Zippers," Dario said quietly. He had come up beside her, and his eyes — older than the rest of him, always — were already working. "Look at the doors, Kes."

She was already looking. All three tent doors were zipped shut from the inside. Not torn open by wind, not slashed in panic, not collapsed under snow load. Zipped. Neat. The kind of thing a person does when they intend to come back, or when they want whoever finds the camp to believe they intended to come back.

"Storm got them?" Nena offered, but without conviction. Even she could see it didn't fit.

Kestrel unclipped her trekking pole from its wrist leash and walked forward alone, the way she always did first, because that was the job and because some habits were older than her disgrace. The snow around the tents was drifted and sculpted, wind-carved into long scalloped tongues that pointed away to the northeast, and she read it the way other people read a page. No tracks leading out. No tracks leading in, anymore — the storm had taken everything older than four days, maybe five. But the drifts against the tent walls told their own story: they had built up evenly, undisturbed, which meant no one had brushed past these walls, no one had stumbled out at night to vomit or piss or flee. Whatever had happened here had happened inside those nylon walls, deliberately, and then the people had gone.

Gone how? That was the question Sorel wanted answered, the question with a number attached to it. But Kestrel had stopped believing in questions with numbers attached a long time ago. Numbers were what people used when they didn't want to know the shape of the truth.

She crouched at the nearest tent door and worked the zipper. It ran smooth — too smooth, oiled recently, or at least not frozen, which it should have been after days at this altitude unless someone had kept it moving. Inside, the tent was dry. Sleeping bags rolled and strapped. Foam pads stacked. A stove, disassembled, nested in its pot. A book — a paperback novel, Spanish, spine cracked halfway through — laid face-down on the groundsheet as if the reader had just stepped out for a moment.

Nobody left a book behind if they were running for their lives. Nobody packed a stove neatly if they were dead.

"Dario," she said. "Get in here."

He crawled in beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. They had shared rope for eleven years, and in that time they had developed a language made mostly of silences, and this one said: *you're seeing it too.*

"They took the essentials," Dario said finally. He lifted a stuff sack, weighed it, set it down. "Rope's gone. The rack is gone — look, there are carabiner marks on the groundsheet where they racked up. Food's stripped down to the heavy tins nobody wants to carry. They took the medical kit." He tapped an empty spot where a kit's outline was pressed into the foam pad. "And the satellite phone case is here, but the phone isn't."

"So they left," Nena said from the tent door, her young face framed against the white. "On purpose. With gear. Then why did Sorel say—"

"Sorel said what gets funding," Kestrel said. "Vanished team, storm, tragedy. That's a rescue. This—" she gestured at the neat, zipped, emptied camp, "—this is something else. This is a withdrawal."

* * *

They spent two hours going over the site the way she'd once taught clients' guides to go over a crime scene, back when she'd been someone whose teaching mattered. Nena turned out to have an eye for it — found a boot print half-melted into a refrozen hollow, a man's boot, large, pointing uphill toward the icefall rather than down toward the valley. Found a scatter of instant coffee sachets tucked under a rock with a stone on top, the way you cache things you mean to return for.

Uphill. Everyone in the valley below assumed the survey team had been swept away by the storm, carried down-glacier, buried in a crevasse field, any of the hundred deaths mountains keep in stock. But the evidence pointed up. Toward the high ice. Toward the calving line, where no sane person would go in late season.

"Why would anyone go up?" Nena asked, hugging herself against the cold. "There's nothing up there. There's nothing to find."

"There's always something to find," Kestrel said. "That's usually the problem."

It was Dario who found the page.

He was circling the third tent, the smallest one, the leader's — you could tell because it was the only one with a name written in marker on the guyline loop, *E. VARGAS*, in blocky engineer's capitals — when he went still in that particular way he had, one hand raised slightly, like a man listening to a frequency no one else could hear.

"Kes."

She came over. Under the flat rock he'd lifted, wedged into a crack in the ice so it wouldn't blow free, was a single sheet of paper, folded twice, protected inside a plastic map sleeve. The sleeve was fogged with cold. She worked it out with numb fingers and held it up to the flat white light.

Handwriting. Fast, cramped, the writing of a man working against time and cold. Coordinates — a full grid reference, precise to six figures, higher on the glacier than anything marked on the survey maps Sorel had shown her. Below the coordinates, a string of characters that looked like a file designation or a drive key. And beneath all of it, underlined twice, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the reverse:

**ALIVE**

Just that. One word. Not *help*, not *lost*, not *dying*. Alive. Written by someone who knew that the difference between those words was the whole story, and who had no way to tell more of it than that.

Kestrel stood very still, the page trembling slightly in her grip, and felt the search turn over beneath her like a boat.

Because she understood now what she was reading. This wasn't a note left for rescuers — rescuers get directions, conditions, casualty counts. This was a message left for one specific kind of finder: someone competent enough to reach this camp, careful enough to look under rocks, and skeptical enough to distrust the official version of events. Elias Vargas hadn't been leaving a trail for Sorel's people. He'd been leaving a trap for them — or a test. And whoever passed the test would find coordinates and a word, and would have to decide, alone, on the ice, what to do about both.

"He planned this," she said aloud. "The whole thing. The zipped tents, the tidy camp, the missing bodies. He staged a disappearance."

Dario was quiet for a long moment. "Why?"

"That's the wrong question."

"What's the right one?"

She looked up-slope, toward the icefall's upper gates, where the seracs leaned against the sky like teeth. Somewhere up there, a man was alive, and had wanted — needed — someone to know it. Someone specific. Someone who could read a camp like a crime scene and wasn't afraid of what the reading turned up.

Or someone expendable enough that it wouldn't matter if they never came back down.

"The right question," she said slowly, "is what did he see that made disappearing better than being found."

* * *

They made cold camp that night a hundred meters below the site, off the ice shelf, in a hollow of scree that broke the wind. No stove — Kestrel didn't like flame this close to the serac zone, and besides, firelight carried. They ate cold noodles and hard cheese and drank water melted over a chemical heater, and the glacier talked to itself in the dark, groaning and cracking, a language she'd learned as a girl and never quite stopped understanding.

Nena slept first, curled like a comma, exhausted and still somehow radiant with the adventure of it. Kestrel watched her and felt the old ache, the one shaped like every young climber she'd ever failed to protect.

"She's good," Dario said softly, across the dark.

"She's green."

"She's good anyway. You were worse at her age. I remember."

"You remember wrong."

A pause. In the dark, his voice lost its banter. "You're going to follow the coordinates."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it.

"Kes." He shifted, and his silhouette resolved against the faint glow of the icefield, shoulders squared. "Sorel gave us a task. Find the camp, confirm deaths, plant the beacons, come home. That was the contract. What you're holding right now — that's outside the contract."

"Everything worth doing is outside somebody's contract."

"Don't do that. Don't make it a slogan." His voice stayed low, careful, the way it always did when he was most serious. "I've got kids, Kes. I signed on for a recovery, not a crusade. If we go up past the calving line chasing a ghost's treasure map, I need to know what I'm roped to."

She let the silence sit. Above them, far up the glacier, something calved — a single deep concussion rolling down the valley like a door slamming in an empty house. Nena stirred but didn't wake.

"I don't know yet," Kestrel said finally, and it was the truest thing she'd said in years. "I know he's alive. I know the agency's story doesn't survive contact with this camp. And I know that when a powerful man tells me a thing ended badly, and the mountain tells me it didn't, I've started believing the mountain."

"And if the mountain's wrong?"

"Then I'll be wrong on the mountain, where I've always been wrong. At least the air's honest up here."

Dario laughed once, without much humor, and lay back. But he didn't say no. That was the thing about Dario; he argued like a lawyer and obeyed like a rope partner, and she had never decided whether that was loyalty or cowardice, and probably it was both, the way most things were.

* * *

She took the last watch, as she always did, sitting with her back to a boulder and the page in its plastic sleeve in her lap, studying it by the gray pre-dawn light. The coordinates sat in her mind like a splinter. Six-figure precision. That wasn't a guess, wasn't a rough bearing — that was a surveyed point, taken with instruments, which meant Elias Vargas had been to wherever it led, had stood there long enough to fix his position exactly, and had then climbed down to this camp in a storm window narrow enough to kill him, to leave a message only a certain kind of person would ever find.

Not a distress call. An invitation.

And the word — *alive* — underlined twice, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn through. She kept coming back to the pressure of it. That was not the handwriting of a man staging a disappearance for strategic reasons. That was the handwriting of a man who had been told, by someone, that staying dead was safer than being found — and who couldn't bear it, who had to leave one thread of himself trailing out of the grave, betting everything that someday someone would pull it.

She knew that handwriting. Not the letters — the pressure. She'd written things like that once, in another life, in the margins of incident reports that no one above her ever read honestly. *Alive* was what you wrote when the machinery had decided you were dead and you refused, in the only ink you had.

The sun crested the eastern ridge and lit the upper glacier, and the whole face of it caught fire, blue and gold and blinding. And there — she almost missed it, would have missed it if she hadn't been staring upslope with nothing else to do — a flicker of light, high on the route beyond the icefall's gates. Brief. Deliberate. A mirror catching the sun and letting it go. Once. Twice.

Then nothing.

Nena sat up, hair full of frost, blinking at her. "What is it? Did you see something?"

Kestrel folded the page closed along its old creases and slid it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

"Break camp," she said. "We're going up."

Behind them, far below, small as a beetle on white cloth, something moved along their own back-trail. Something that hadn't been there yesterday. She watched it for a long moment before shouldering her pack, and said nothing, and began to climb.
