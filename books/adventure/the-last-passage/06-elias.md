## 6. Elias

The melt water talks all night.

Elias Voss has learned its language in the nine days since he crawled into this blue throat of the glacier — the trickle that means the sun is working on the surface above, the groan and tick of the ice settling into its own weight, the long exhalations from deeper down where the glacier dreams in compressed centuries. He knows now which sounds are harmless and which ones mean the mountain is rearranging its furniture. He lies in his hollow of sleeping bags and foam pads, listening to a drip fall from an ice tooth three meters overhead into the puddle he's dug for himself, timing it against his heartbeat, and he thinks about how a man can survive almost anything if he can turn it into data.

Drip. Beat. Drip. Beat.

He is forty-one years old, a glaciologist by training and a surveyor by trade, and until eleven days ago he had spent his whole professional life believing in measurement. You measure a thing honestly, you report what you measured, and the world is slightly better for it. That was the creed they gave him in graduate school, wrapped in the language of service — *your work will inform policy; your maps will keep people safe* — and Elias had believed it with the clean, untested faith of a man who has never been asked to hand over anything he loved.

Eleven days ago, the world asked him to hand over four people.

He closes his eyes and lets the flashback come, because he has learned that memory, like meltwater, will find its way through you whether you invite it or not, and it's better to give it a channel.

* * *

They found the seam on the fourth day of the traverse, at the bottom of a crevasse so deep their headlamps couldn't find the floor.

It was Tomás who spotted it first — a discoloration in the ice wall at sixty meters, a band of color wrong for ice, too warm for ice, a bruise running through the glacier's flank like a vein through marble. Tomás Okonkwo, geophysicist, twenty-nine, the youngest of them and the most alive, a man who sang off-key on the rope to keep morale up and could name every bird in the valley below them. He'd gone down first, of course he had. He'd rigged the pitch himself, tested everything twice, and when he called up on the radio his voice had that quality it got when the science took hold of him — breathless, reverent, a boy's voice wearing a scientist's vocabulary.

"Elias," he said. "You need to come down here. I'm not going to be able to explain this."

The descent took twenty minutes. At fifty meters the light changed — went green-blue, submarine, as though they were sinking through the eye of some enormous animal. At sixty meters Elias swung off the rope and stood on a shelf of debris the crevasse had swallowed decades ago, old rockfall furred with refrozen melt, and turned in a slow circle with his lamp held high.

The seam ran the length of the wall. Four hundred meters of exposed strike, maybe more, disappearing into the dark on both sides. It wasn't ore the way a layman pictures ore — no glittering pocket, no nuggets in quartz — it was subtler than that, a banding in the country rock, mineralization so concentrated it read as color: rust and violet and a strange oily black that ate his lamplight and gave nothing back. He walked to it and put his glove against it and felt something he had never felt from stone before, or maybe only imagined he felt, a faint warmth, as though the deposit were a living thing with its own slow metabolism.

"Monazite," Tomás said behind him. "Bastnäsite. Look at the xenotime signatures." He was running the handheld XRF along the band, and the numbers coming back were making him laugh, actually laugh, a bright shocked sound in that cathedral silence. "Elias. These grades are — there are mines operating right now at a tenth of this concentration. A *tenth*. This is one seam. One visible seam in one wall of one crevasse. The body could run for kilometers under the ice."

Elias made him repeat it. Made him re-run the assay. Made him calibrate the instrument against the standard reference material they carried, because Elias Voss did not permit himself wonder until he had permitted himself doubt, and doubt took time.

The numbers held. The numbers held and then they got worse — better — worse, depending on which side of the ledger you stood. Rare earths: neodymium, dysprosium, terbium, the metals inside every wind turbine and electric motor and guided munition on earth, the metals nations went to war over quietly, through proxies and purchase agreements and men like the one Elias would meet later in a tent that smelled of coffee and cold steel. He stood in the green light doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. A deposit of this grade, this size, in a range claimed by two governments and coveted by three more. He thought: *this changes the map.* He thought: *this changes everything.*

He thought, God help him, *what a privilege to be the one who finds it.*

That was the last pure hour of his life. He didn't know it yet. That's the thing about the last hour of anything — it never announces itself. It just ends, and you spend the rest of your life locating exactly where.

They spent two days sampling. Two days of careful, methodical, beautiful work — Tomás running assays, Priya logging stratigraphy, Marcus shooting GPS fixes on the seam's extent, Camille sketching the crevasse geometry in her fast sure hand. Four people doing the job they were trained for, well, in the best office either heaven or the Cordillera del Viento ever built. Elias sent the preliminary figures down-mountain on the satellite uplink, encrypted, flagged for the ministry contact listed in their contract. Then they packed the samples in padded cases, and they climbed out of the green light, and they went to Camp Two to wait for instructions.

The instructions came in thirty-six hours later. Not from the ministry contact. From a number that resolved, when Marcus traced it, to a shell within a shell within a shell — but the voice on the other end introduced herself as Sorel, and she used words like *asset security* and *compartmentalization*, and her tone was the tone of a person reading from a script she had read many times.

She told them the survey was concluded. She told them their contract was fulfilled ahead of schedule. She told them a recovery team would meet them at Base Camp to collect the samples and the data, and that they should say nothing to anyone, and that their discretion would be "remembered."

Priya asked the obvious question: remembered how?

Sorel said, "Generously," and hung up.

They sat around the stove that evening and argued the way educated people argue, which is to say politely and with footnotes. Priya said it was standard practice for strategic-mineral finds — the state sweeps in, nationalizes the data, thanks everyone, moves on. Marcus said the flag protocol was wrong, that legitimate government channels didn't route through shells, that his brother worked in procurement and he knew what these numbers looked like from the inside. Camille said little. She was watching Elias, because Camille always watched Elias, and later he would understand that she had already understood something the rest of them were still assembling.

Tomás said: "We should celebrate. We should absolutely not celebrate. We should send everything we have to a journalist and let the state choke on its own secret."

They laughed. It was a good joke, in the way that true things are jokes.

Then the second call came.

It came at 0300, waking them in their tents, and Sorel's voice had changed. The script-reader was gone. What remained underneath was flat and patient, the voice of a functionary describing a procedure, and procedures do not care about you the way people do.

"The assessment phase is terminated," she said. "All personnel will remain at Camp Two. A ground team will arrive within seventy-two hours to conduct debriefings individually. Do not attempt to descend. Do not transmit further. Confirm receipt."

Marcus confirmed receipt, because you confirm receipt, because that's what you do when a voice like that asks. Then he came out of the comms tent and stood in the blue midnight among the sastrugi and said, very quietly, to the four of them huddled in the cold:

"She didn't say we're going home."

And the wind came down off the col and moved through camp, and somewhere below them the glacier shifted with a sound like a door closing in a house with no doors, and Elias Voss, who had spent his life believing that measurement was protection, understood in his stomach before he understood it in his head that they had stopped being surveyors. They had become a variable. And variables, in certain kinds of equations, get eliminated.

* * *

Here is what Elias knows about power, now, that he didn't know eleven days ago. Power does not roar. Power does not send armies. Power sends a polite woman with a flat voice and a seventy-two-hour window, and it lets geography do the killing, and afterward there is an avalanche report, or a crevasse-fall report, or simply a line in a ledger noting that four contracts have been closed out, and the mountains keep their mouths shut because the mountains always do.

He knows this because he stayed one day too long trying to prove otherwise. He spent that day on the radio, climbing the chain — calling the ministry, calling the university, calling the emergency coordination office in the valley town, using every name and credential he had earned in twenty years of honest work. Every channel was dead air. Not busy — dead. Their transponder code had been struck from the civil registry. Their expedition insurance showed lapsed. The weather service that had issued their daily forecasts stopped issuing forecasts. It was as if a large soft hand had reached down over the Cordillera and switched them off, four lives' worth of infrastructure, with the unhurried ease of someone turning off lights in a room they own.

Camille figured out the plan before anyone said it aloud. She came to him at dusk on that second day, while the others slept, and sat beside him on a gear crate, and said: "Individual debriefings, Elias. Separately. Seventy-two hours apart, if they stagger us. You've read the same reports I have. What happens to witnesses who get debriefed separately by people who needed them eliminated?"

He had no answer. There was no answer. There was only the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *run.*

But you cannot run from Camp Two with four people and live. The icefall below them was a maze of shifting seracs; the valley routes would be watched; a helicopter had already passed twice that day, high and slow, the way a hawk passes over a meadow. So they did what mountaineers do, which is convert terror into logistics. They split the samples and the data drives — half to Camille and Marcus, who would go down the east flank toward the pass, moving fast and light, carrying the truth like contraband. Half to Elias and Tomás, who would go up. Up onto the glacier proper, into the white chaos above the calving line, where no recovery team would want to follow and no helicopter could see beneath the cloud deck. Up was suicide. But down was a scheduled appointment, and Elias had decided he would rather die of the mountain than of the paperwork.

They left the tents zipped. They stacked the gear neat as a shop display, because a tidy abandoned camp reads as *accident* or *evacuation*, and both stories buy time. Camille hugged him once, hard, her chin against his shoulder, and whispered: "Get it out. However you have to. Get it out." Then the four of them stepped into the storm that was conveniently arriving, and the snow began eating their tracks even as they made them, and Elias looked back once at the empty camp glowing in the last light and thought: *if we live, we come back for them.*

They did not live. Or — Camille and Marcus did not. On the third morning, alone on the high glacier, Elias raised the radio on a coded frequency Camille had given him, the one they'd agreed to check at dawn and dusk. Static. At dusk, static. On the fourth day, a single burst transmission, ten seconds long, cut off mid-word. He has replayed those ten seconds in his head so many times they have worn smooth as river stones. He cannot make them mean anything except that someone tried, and then couldn't.

Two days after that, he watched, from six kilometers and through binoculars, a figure in a white parka move along the east flank route — the route. Moving in the practiced glide of a trained operator, sweeping the slope with optics, following a line of travel that could only be the line Camille and Marcus had taken. The figure paused at a point on the route. It crouched. It stayed crouched for a long time.

Elias lowered the binoculars and did not look again, because he understood what crouching means on a mountain, and he was saving whatever was left of his mind for staying alive.

Tomás lasted five days longer. The cold got into his hands on the sixth night out — a stove failure, a fuel leak, bad luck compounding bad decisions, the tax every expedition pays eventually. By the eighth day he couldn't work a carabiner. On the ninth day, in a snow cave below the serac field, feverish and lucid by turns, he made Elias promise things. Make it public. Say our names. Don't let them be a line item. And then, quieter, the one that still walks through Elias's nights: *don't let them make you into them.*

Tomás died at 0400 with his head in Elias's lap, singing, off-key, the song he always sang on the rope. Elias buried him in the snow cave and collapsed the entrance, and marked the spot on his map with coordinates he will carry until he dies, and walked on alone into the white.

That was seven days ago.

* * *

Now it is night in the melt cave, and the drip keeps its time above him — drip, beat, drip, beat — and Elias lies in the dark doing the only work left to him: staying alive long enough to matter. He has the samples. He has the data drives, taped against his skin inside his base layer, warmed by a body that is running lower on fuel every day. He has rations for perhaps four more days if he eats like a man who wants to suffer slowly. His left boot is dying; the sole is delaminating, and he has wrapped it with cord and tape and prayer. His fingers ache with frostnip that will become frostbite if he makes one careless mistake, and on this glacier, mistakes are the local currency and everyone pays.

He knows they will send someone. Of course they will. The samples exist; the seam exists; a loose end named Elias Voss exists, and organizations like Sorel's do not tolerate loose ends any more than glaciers tolerate heat. They will send a guide, probably. Someone local, deniable, expendable — a tracker with debts, a climber with a file, some broken professional they can aim like a rifle and disavow like a misfired round. He has imagined this person for days now. He has given them a face, a history, a reason to say yes. He has rehearsed what he'll do when they find him: the pistol he took from the survival cache at Camp Three is loaded and dry and within reach of his right hand, and he has told himself, firmly, that he will use it.

He has also noticed, with the detached curiosity of a scientist observing his own deterioration, that he hopes he won't have to. Because whoever comes up that icefall will be a person, and persons are the one resource the machinery of power cannot manufacture, and Tomás's last instruction stands between him and the trigger like a rope between two anchors: *don't let them make you into them.*

Outside, far down-valley, the weather is building. He can feel it in the pressure behind his eyes, in the changed note of the wind crossing the col, in the way the glacier's groans have deepened from ticks to moans. A storm is coming up from the south, a big one, the kind that erases tracks and buries camps and kills the unprepared — and also the kind that blinds hunters. He has survived this long by turning everything into data. Storm front: forty-eight hours, maybe less. Visibility: zero for the duration. Calving season: early this year; he heard the first great detonations yesterday, thunderclaps from the glacier's terminus, the ice giving birth to itself.

Which means his window is closing. Which means whoever they sent is climbing into the same closing window, and will be forced up onto the high glacier by the same logic that forced him, and at some point — soon — two sets of tracks are going to converge in the white.

The drip keeps time. Drip, beat. Elias checks the pistol out of habit, checks the data drives against his ribs, and stares into the blue dark of the ice, waiting for footsteps.

Somewhere below him, past the icefall, past the empty camp, past the valley where nobody is looking for anything but money and silence, someone is already climbing.
