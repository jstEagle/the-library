## 7. Tracks in the Wrong Direction

The journal page had given them a heading, and headings on ice are promises the mountain is free to break.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the abandoned camp with the paper flattened against her thigh, the wind trying to take it, and read the coordinates a third time. They pointed up-glacier, past the icefall, into the high accumulation zone where the survey team's mandate should have ended and where no rational person would go in a warming week of calving season. The single word — *alive* — sat at the bottom of the page in handwriting that had pressed hard enough to tear through to the next sheet. Whoever wrote it had been cold, or frightened, or both, and had wanted the message to survive being found.

"Alive," Nena said over her shoulder. She'd read it upside down, which was apparently a skill porters acquired young. "Alive how? Alive like the team is alive?"

"Alive like one of them is alive." Kestrel folded the page into her chest pocket, under the windbreaker, against the skin. "One person writes a note like that. Not six."

Dario crouched at the perimeter of the camp where the snow began, running his mitten along a line only he could see. He was fifty-one and built like something the glacier had refused to digest, and he had been quiet since the icefall's first seracs had announced themselves that morning, which for Dario meant he was thinking, and Dario thinking was usually the beginning of bad news delivered gently.

"Kestrel." He didn't look up. "Come look at this."

She came. The line he was tracing was a trench, shallow, wind-negotiated, but unmistakable once you stopped looking at the snow and started looking at what the snow was doing. It ran from the camp's eastern margin toward the moraine, and it was not a drift pattern. Drifts feather. This cut.

"Someone walked out of here," Dario said. "Days ago. Before the storm finished, maybe during it."

"Alone?"

"Alone, loaded, and in a hurry." He pointed with his chin at the stride. "Look at the spacing. Long steps. He wasn't picking his way, he was running on the flat and saving his legs for the climb."

Kestrel felt the shape of it settle over her, the way weather settles — not sudden, but total. The camp had read wrong to her from the first hour. Tents zipped from inside, gear stacked with a stowage discipline that no one abandoning a camp in panic would bother with, food caches sealed and marked. She'd told Sorel it looked like a departure, not a disaster, and Sorel had written that down without expression, the way he wrote everything down, and she had understood even then that her observation was being filed somewhere it would be used against someone.

"He left before the others disappeared," she said. "Or the others never disappeared at all. Or—" She stopped. The third option was the one the coordinates and the word *alive* were already shouting, and she made herself say it. "Or he's the one they wanted gone, and everyone else is part of it."

Nena had gone still the way young people go still — completely, as if motion itself might reveal her. "Part of what?"

"We don't know yet." Kestrel heard her own voice do the thing it did when she was managing a client's fear: even, warm at the edges, a wall with wallpaper on it. She hated that it came out that way with Nena, who deserved better than wallpaper, and she hated more that she didn't have anything else to give her yet.

They followed the trench.

* * *

It climbed. Of course it climbed. Everything in the Cordillera del Viento climbed; the only question was whether it climbed toward something or away from it, and this line went up the lateral moraine on the glacier's true left, hugging the rock where the ice pulled away from the valley wall and left a shelf of rubble and old snowbridge. It was a porter's route, a smuggler's route, a route for people who knew the mountain well enough to hate it efficiently. Not a surveyor's route. Surveyors walked where their instruments told them to walk. This person had walked where the mountain couldn't see him.

By midday they had his story in fragments. A bivouac hollow under an overhanging slab, melted snow in a foil tray, the wrapper of a ration bar tucked into a crack with the tidy guilt of a man who practiced leave-no-trace even while fleeing for his life. A dropped carabiner, a cheap one, the gate sprung — he'd lost gear and hadn't gone back for it, which told her more than the bivouac did. You go back for gear when you believe you have time. He had stopped believing.

"His boots are wrong," Nena said.

Kestrel looked. The footprints, where the wind had spared them in the lee of the moraine, were deep at the toe and shallow at the heel, the print of a man leaning into a climb with weight forward, and the tread was a city tread, a rubber pattern made for pavement and airport floors.

"He's a scientist," Nena said, and there was something in her voice between awe and grief. "He's a scientist and he's been walking on this for three days."

"Four," Kestrel said, reading the drift fill in the prints, the refreeze crust, the way yesterday's wind had softened yesterday's edges. "Maybe five. He's not fast. He's not eating enough. And he's heading—" She turned, following the line of the route with her eyes until it left the moraine and went up, up onto the shoulder of the high glacier, toward the accumulation zone, toward the icefall's upper basin, toward country that in three weeks would be a shooting gallery of calving seracs and in one week would already be groaning with it.

Toward the coordinates on the page.

"He's going back," she said slowly. "The camp was the departure point, not the destination. He came down from something, wrote the note, cached it where a search party would find it, and went back up to whatever he found." She looked at the page in her pocket through the fabric, as if she could feel the ink. "He's not running from the mountain. He's hiding in it. Because the mountain is the one place the people looking for him can't burn down."

Dario had stopped walking. That was the thing about Dario; he stopped walking the way other men raised a hand. She turned back and found him looking down-valley, back along their own approach track, back along the line of their own prints stitched across three days of snowfield.

"Kestrel," he said. "How many people are on our team?"

"Three."

"Then explain to me," he said, with the terrible patience of a man who has already done the arithmetic, "why there are five sets of tracks behind us."

* * *

They went to ground in a crevasse field off the moraine's shoulder, a maze of blue fractures and snow bridges that a stranger would read as chaos and that Kestrel read as a house with rooms. She put Dario on the entrance with the radio and took Nena up a rise of wind-carved ice where they could look back down their own route without being silhouetted against the sky, and she glassed the snowfield below with the battered monocular she'd carried for eleven seasons and trusted more than any instrument with a battery in it.

The tracks were there. Her own three, unmistakable — her long stride, Dario's deliberate tread, Nena's lighter print with the left boot's worn outer edge. And threaded among them, spaced with a discipline her own team didn't have, two more lines. Two. Moving in her tracks where her tracks were firm, breaking their own trail where hers had drifted full. They were a day behind. Maybe less. The wind had been kind to them, or they were good, and the two were probably the same thing.

She watched the snowfield for a long time. At the far edge, where the valley bent and the light did the thing light did at altitude in early afternoon, she caught it — a flicker, not of movement but of wrongness, a patch of white that was white in a way snow isn't. Then it was gone. Camouflage, or cloud shadow, or her eyes lying to her the way eyes lie at altitude when you want them to.

"Recovery unit," she said.

Nena looked at her. "Like, they're recovering the team?"

"They're recovering the *problem*." Kestrel lowered the monocular. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have bet on a tremor. "Sorel told me the survey team vanished. He didn't tell me anyone else was looking. Governments don't send one searcher for six missing citizens, Nena. They send one searcher when they want the search quiet, and they send a second team when they want the first searcher to be the noise."

"You're the noise," Nena said.

"I'm the noise." She said it without self-pity, because self-pity was a luxury the mountain didn't stock and she'd learned that lesson at the cost of a man's life and her entire career. "I'm the leash. They let me off the collar because a blacklisted guide with debts will walk into places a government team can't be seen walking into, and I'll find things, and then the second team follows my tracks to whatever I find and makes it not exist." She was thinking it through as she said it, and the shape of it got worse with each piece. "That's why the contract was so clean. That's why he didn't blink at my price. The money was never the cost. The money was the *bait on the hook*."

Below them, faint on the wind, the radio in Dario's hand made its squelch-click. Then Dario's voice, pitched low, carrying anyway in the thin cold air the way voices do. "Kestrel. Radio. Says it's for you. Says—" A pause, and she heard him swallow. "Says he knows we stopped. Says we should keep moving. Says the weather window closes in four days."

She climbed down to him and took the handset. The voice that came through was Sorel's, unhurried, urban, absurdly out of place, a voice for conference rooms wearing the static like an ill-fitting coat.

"Señora Villalobos. You're making good time."

"You're watching my tracks."

"I'm watching *progress*," Sorel said. "There's a support element in the valley. Standard protocol for a search of this priority. They'll coordinate with you when you locate the survey equipment."

"Equipment." She let the word sit. "You said the team. Now you say equipment."

The pause was half a second too long, and she had spent enough years reading clients' silences to know that a half-second too long was a man choosing which lie to build on. "The priority is the data they were carrying," Sorel said. "The team's fate is, I'm afraid, likely settled. The data is recoverable. Bring me the survey records, Señora Villalobos, and everything we discussed is honored. Everything."

"And if the team's fate isn't settled? If someone's alive up there?"

This pause was longer, and colder, and it had a shape, and the shape was a door closing. "Then you should consider," Sorel said, "how much simpler your own position becomes if he isn't." The click of the transmission ending was very small and very loud.

Kestrel stood in the crevasse field with the radio in her hand and looked up the route, toward the high glacier, toward the coordinates, toward a man in city boots who had written one word under a rock and gone up into the white to hide from his own government inside the only witness that couldn't be bought.

"He knows," Nena said quietly. She'd come down the rise without Kestrel hearing her, which was its own data point about who Nena was becoming. "Sorel knows there's a survivor. That's why us. That's why the second team. He's not looking for a body."

"He's looking for a witness," Kestrel said. "And I'm the dog he sent to point."

Dario had his back to them both, facing down-valley, facing the direction of the second team, and he was very still in the way he'd been still all morning. Kestrel looked at him for three seconds longer than she'd ever needed to look at Dario in fifteen years of rope partnership, and she filed what she saw — or thought she saw, or was maybe only now, under pressure, beginning to invent — in the place where she kept everything she wasn't ready to know.

"Four days," she said. "Weather window. Calving season. And a recovery unit a day behind us that Sorel will feed our position every time we radio in." She shouldered her pack. The straps bit in the old familiar way, and the old familiar way had never before felt like a harness. "Then we go dark. No more radio. And we move at night."

"Move at night," Nena repeated. "On the glacier. Toward the icefall."

"Toward the man in the city boots." Kestrel looked up the route one more time, at the shelf of moraine climbing into the light, at the high white country beyond it where something worth billions and worth killing for was buried in the blue ice, and where one frightened man was the only thing standing between the truth and the machinery sent to erase it. "Because he's the only person on this mountain," she said, "who's telling it straight."

She started up. Behind her, two sets of boots followed, and somewhere below, patient as the ice, two more sets were already climbing in hers.
