## 8. The Icefall Crossing

The icefall announced itself before we saw it, the way a predator announces itself — not with sound but with silence, a hush in the valley where the wind should have been, and then, underneath it, a groan so deep I felt it in my molars rather than heard it.

We stood at its toe at first light, three small figures at the base of a frozen waterfall the size of a cathedral district. The glacier came down off the col in a chaos of seracs and crevasses, blue ice fractured into towers and slabs, some of them the height of houses, leaning against each other like drunks holding each other up. Meltwater ran somewhere beneath all of it. You couldn't see it, but you could hear it — a rushing, patient sound, like the mountain breathing through its teeth.

"Calving season," Dario said. He said it the way you'd say *cancer* or *landslide*. Not information. A verdict.

"It's early yet." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "The big releases don't start for another week. Maybe ten days."

"You said maybe on the approach too." He was checking his harness buckles for the third time, which he only did when he was frightened. Twenty years of rope partnership had taught me his tells the way a wife learns a husband's. "You've been saying *maybe* since the valley."

I didn't answer that, because he was right, and because the answer would have been another *maybe*, and we both knew what the maybes were adding up to.

Nena stood slightly apart from us, looking up at the icefall with an expression I recognized and hated seeing on a twenty-three-year-old face. It wasn't fear. It was hunger. She hadn't yet learned that the mountains didn't care about her hunger, that they ate the hungry first precisely because hunger made people climb things they shouldn't.

"Eat," I told her. "All of it. We'll need everything today."

She tore into her ration without argument. Dario drank water and wouldn't look at me. And above us the icefall waited, ticking.

*

An icefall is not climbed. That's the first thing you have to understand, and the thing no photograph can teach you. A wall is climbed. A face is climbed. An icefall is *negotiated* — you enter into a negotiation with several million tons of broken ice that is actively, continuously collapsing, and your only leverage is speed and judgment, and your only guarantee is nothing. There is no safe line through an icefall. There are only lines that are safe *right now*, and right now is a currency that spends itself whether you use it or not.

We went in roped, thirty meters apart, me leading. The route Sorel's satellite imagery suggested — the route Elias's journal page pointed toward, up and left through the central maze — began benignly enough. A ramp of compacted snow between two walls of ice, steep but solid, steps cut easily with the front points of my crampons. Behind me I could hear Nena's breathing, quick and controlled, and Dario's, slower, deliberate, a man counting his own heartbeats.

For the first hour it was almost kind.

That's how it gets you. Every icefall has a polite foyer, clean and walkable, and you pass through it thinking you've been lied to about the danger, and then the foyer ends and the house begins.

The house began with a crevasse four meters wide, its far lip overhanging, its depths going down into a darkness that swallowed my headlamp beam. I bridged it with the aluminum ladder we'd carried up from the valley, weighted the near side with my pack, and watched each of them cross it the way I'd been taught to watch — not their feet, but their eyes, because feet follow eyes and panic lives in the eyes first.

Nena crossed like she'd done it a hundred times. She hadn't; I knew her logbook. But she crossed flat-footed and calm, weight on the rungs, and when she reached the far side she clipped in and turned immediately to watch Dario instead of celebrating herself. Good instinct. Better than mine had been at her age.

Dario crossed fine too. It was what came after that nearly killed him.

*

The middle section was the maze — a half-kilometer of shattered blocks and leaning seracs where the glacier had, in some convulsion decades ago, simply torn itself apart and never healed. We moved through it in the rhythm I'd drilled into them on the approach: I go, I test, I call the hold, you come. No one moves unless someone is watching. No one stands under anything leaning. When the ice speaks, you stop and listen until it finishes.

The ice spoke often. Little sounds mostly — cracks like knuckles popping, a hiss of settling snow, the deep internal groans that travel through a glacier the way words travel through a closed door. Once, a serac the size of a bus shed a slab somewhere above us and to the east, out of sight, and the report rolled across the maze like artillery, and we all three froze in place for a full minute, heads tilted, listening for the echo that would tell us where it had come from.

"Calving season," Dario murmured again, when we moved on. Not a verdict this time. A prayer.

It was past noon when we reached the pitch that broke the day open — a wall of ice sixty meters high, blue and glassy, split by a chimney of sorts where two seracs leaned together and left a gap. It was the way through. There was no other way through; I'd spent the last hour confirming that with my own eyes, traversing the base of the wall twice while Dario and Nena sheltered under a lip of ice and watched me with the particular helplessness of people who can do nothing but wait.

The chimney was steep — near vertical for ten meters, then a squeeze, then easier ground. Old screw holes pocked the walls at intervals, gray-rimmed, years old. Someone had climbed this before us. Survey men, maybe. Or someone older. The holes were good news and bad news at once: proof of a line, and proof that the line was old enough that the ice had shifted since.

"Ice screws are fresh placements only," I called down. "Nothing older than today. If a placement creaks, shout before you weight it."

Nena went second, climbing well, placing her screws with a patience that surprised me — she'd been rushy all week, hungry for the summit of every small challenge, but here in the throat of the icefall she slowed down and got precise. Dario followed last, cleaning the pitch, and I watched him come up the chimney with the sun full on the upper wall and thought, absurdly, *we're going to make it*.

That's the other thing about icefalls. They let you think that. Right up until they don't.

*

It happened forty minutes later, on the long traverse beneath the great leaning serac that dominated the upper maze — a block of ice the size of an apartment building, canted fifteen degrees off vertical, held in place by friction and luck and nothing else. Every guide who worked this range knew it by reputation. The old-timers called it *el Obispo* — the Bishop — because of the way it seemed to preside over the whole icefall, mitre-shaped, silent, judging.

The route passed directly beneath it. Of course it did. The mountain's sense of humor.

I was fifty meters ahead, having just placed a deadman anchor in a snow bridge, when I heard the sound.

Not a crack. Not a groan. A *whump* — the soft, compressed-air exhale of a mass of snow letting go somewhere above — and then the sharper sound I knew better than any sound on earth, the sound of a man falling.

"DARIO!"

He'd been moving fast — too fast, trying to close the gap I'd opened, trying to prove something to himself or to me — and the slope beneath him had looked like slope and been something else: a snow bridge over a hidden crevasse, its crust weakened by three days of sun. It gave with no warning at all. One moment he was walking. The next there was a hole in the mountain where my oldest friend had been, and the rope between us snapped taut and sang.

I threw myself onto the deadman, arms wrapped around the anchor, body low, all my weight converted into friction. The rope burned across the snow lip as the load came on — Dario's eighty kilos plus the acceleration of a six-meter fall, multiplied by physics, delivered to my shoulders through thirty meters of static line. Something in my left shoulder wrenched. I held anyway. You hold anyway. That's the whole job, distilled: *you hold anyway.*

But I was fifty meters away, and the angle was wrong, and I could feel it in the rope's terrible vibration — the fall wasn't stopping. He was penduluming in the dark below the bridge, swinging into the crevasse walls, and every swing was more meters of rope running through the system I was lying on, and my deadman was good but it wasn't built for this, and I could feel it beginning to creep, millimeter by millimeter, the anchor whispering loose in the snow—

And then the rope stopped.

Stopped dead. Clean. The vibration died out of it like a struck bell falling silent, and the load settled, and held.

I lay on the ice with my cheek against the snow, my shoulder screaming, and turned my head, and looked back down the traverse.

Nena was on the ice in a self-arrest position I had taught her exactly once, picket driven, boot-axe buried, her whole slight body stretched along the slope behind a snow anchor she had built in — I would learn later — perhaps four seconds. She'd seen him fall, dropped into arrest without being told, and when the load came to her she hadn't fought it with muscle, which would have failed. She'd fought it with geometry. She'd built a T-slot anchor in the firm snow, slung it, equalized it, and transferred the entire falling weight of a grown man into the mountain before his pendulum finished its first arc.

Four seconds. I'd taken twice that long to build worse anchors with ten times her experience.

"Nena." My voice came out wrecked. "Report."

"He's hanging," she called back, breathless but level. "About eight meters down. He's moving. He says—" She paused, listened. "He says his leg's bad. He says he's sorry."

"Tell him sorry is for later." I was already moving, hauling myself along the rope toward the hole in the mountain, shoulder shrieking with every meter. "Tell him to keep his hands warm and his mouth shut unless it's information."

*

Getting him out took an hour and cost us most of our margin.

The crevasse was narrow enough at the top that I could lean over the lip and see him — Dario, dangling in blue gloom, one boot braced against the ice wall, blood on his trouser leg where the crampon had bitten him during the pendulum. His face, when he looked up at me, was the face of a man who had seen the bottom of the world and been pulled back from it by a thread.

"The bridge is rotten," he said. "Kestrel, the whole traverse—"

"I know. New line. Haul system. Save your breath for screaming if you need to."

We built a 3:1 haul off the picket and the deadman together, Nena's hands moving beside mine, quick and sure, not waiting to be told. She'd read the situation the way I had: Dario couldn't prusik up on a ruined leg, so the rope had to do the work. I hauled. She managed the backup and kept him talking — steady, bright, relentless little pieces of talk, *what's your fingers say, wiggle them for me, okay, now toes, okay, good, you're doing great, Dario, really* — and I understood, hauling until my vision spotted, that she was doing for his mind what the anchor had done for his body. Holding him. Keeping the panic from loading onto the system.

When his head came over the lip and his hands found real snow, he lay there a long moment, face down, breathing the ordinary air like a man returned from underwater.

Then he looked at Nena. Then at me.

"That anchor," he said hoarsely. "Four seconds. Who taught you that?"

"You did," Nena said. "Second day of the approach. You said, *the mountain doesn't warn you twice.*" She shrugged, embarrassed, already coiling rope. "I just listened."

Dario laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound, half sob — and then pressed his forehead against the snow and stayed like that for a while, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the icefall hadn't already said better.

*

His leg was bad but not broken — a deep gash, a wrenched knee, pain that would slow us and stop him. We taped it, dosed him, redistributed the loads. The arithmetic was ugly: slower team, less daylight, and the Bishop still leaning over the route ahead, and the calving season still ticking down somewhere in the mountain's deep interior like a fuse we couldn't see.

"We go around," I said. "Higher and wider under the Bishop. It costs us ninety minutes. We take them."

"Or we go under it fast, like the survey team did," Dario said. "Their track logs put them there at midday."

"Their track logs also put them in a camp that vanished." I met his eyes. "We go around."

He didn't argue. Neither did Nena. We went around, and the detour took two hours instead of ninety, because the mountain charged interest on every prudent decision, and by the time we cleared the Bishop's shadow the light was going amber and long, and the temperature was dropping fast enough that the fresh snow squeaked underfoot.

From the high shoulder at the top of the icefall, we looked back at what we'd crossed. The maze lay below us in the evening light, beautiful and insane, a city of glass ruins. And as we stood there, catching our breath, the mountain spoke again — not a crack this time but a *boom*, deep and final, and we all turned to watch a serac on the eastern flank shear off entire and fall a thousand feet, hitting the glacier below in a rising bloom of snow that took a full minute to settle.

Where we would have been, if we'd gone under the Bishop fast. Where the survey team had gone.

Nobody said anything. Nena's hand found my sleeve — just briefly, just a touch, then gone, as if she'd surprised herself.

We made the flat ground above the icefall as the light failed, and there I called the halt, though we were short of the day's objective. Exhaustion makes its own arguments. We dug in behind a windlip, melted snow, forced food and water into Dario, checked his leg, checked each other.

It was Nena, on the last rotation of the watch, who woke me.

Not with alarm. With a whisper, and a hand on my shoulder, and when I surfaced from sleep she was pointing down-slope, into the blue dark below our camp, where the glacier flattened out toward the high basin.

"Light," she breathed. "Down there. In the ice. It went out just now, but I saw it — a flicker, like a lamp behind a wall."

I crawled to the edge of the windlip and looked, and told myself it was a star, a reflection, my own tired eyes.

Then it flickered again. Faint, gold, unmistakable. Deep inside the glacier, behind three meters of blue ice, something down in the dark was burning.

Someone was alive down there. And they had just shown us their light.
