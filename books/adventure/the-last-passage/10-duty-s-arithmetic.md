## 10. Duty's Arithmetic

The melt cave breathed.

That was the thing Kestrel couldn't get past, even as she sat with her back against blue ice and watched Elias Vidal wrap his frostbitten hands in strips of sleeping bag. The cave breathed — air moving through some hidden throat of the glacier, in and out, slow as a sleeping animal — and every exhalation carried the smell of stone water and old snow and, faintly, of the man himself: woodsmoke, sweat, the chemical sweetness of emergency rations eaten cold for eleven days.

She had come up here to find a body. She had found instead a man with a pistol, a folder of evidence, and eyes that had gone past desperation into something calmer and far more dangerous. He'd lowered the gun eventually. They had talked until her headlamp batteries began to dim, and now she sat in the failing light doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

On one side of the ledger: Sorel's contract. Full exoneration. Her file burned, her name restored to the register, debts cleared, a fee that would buy her three seasons of not guiding anyone anywhere. On the other side: a stack of laminated survey pages, a crevasse full of ore worth more than the province's annual budget, and a chain of decisions made in offices so far above the ice that the men who made them would never feel cold again.

"You keep looking at the folder," Elias said. His voice was rough from disuse, from screaming into wind no one had heard. "Say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're calculating. I've watched you do it for an hour. You have a face like a man adding columns."

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. "You wouldn't like the numbers."

"I've been living on numbers I don't like since June."

Outside, the glacier ticked and settled. Somewhere below them — two hundred meters down through ice that groaned like ship's timbers — Dario and Nena were at Camp Two, boiling water, trusting her. That was the part of the equation that refused to stay put. Every time she pushed it to one side, it slid back: *Dario. Nena.* Whatever she decided in this breathing cave, they would carry it down the mountain in their bodies.

* * *

She thought about Tomas Rey the way you think about a tooth that aches only when you bite down — deliberately, briefly, hating yourself for testing it.

Tomas Rey, thirty-one years old, software engineer from Mendoza, who had saved for four years to climb the north face of Cerro Ceniza with a guide whose reputation said *she gets people home*. He'd been strong, cheerful, slightly clumsy on rock but iron on snow. On the third day he'd called her "jefa" and she'd pretended to hate it. On the fifth day he'd asked her whether she ever thought about quitting, and she'd laughed and said the mountain doesn't let you quit, it just stops calling.

On the sixth day, on the descent route everyone called the Chimenea — steep, exposed, objectively within her ability and his — a fixed anchor she had personally inspected forty minutes earlier came out of the rock.

Not failed. Came *out*, whole, as if the mountain had picked its teeth with it. Tomas went past her into the void before she could even shout, and the rope paid out, and the world became a sound — his sound — that she still heard sometimes when the wind came off the ice in a certain way.

The investigation took nine months. The finding: anchor failure due to "unforeseeable geological instability." Her record was stained anyway — a dead client sticks to a guide like blood in a stream, diluting forever but never quite gone. Sponsors withdrew. The federation suspended her pending review, then reinstated her to a limbo where no outfitter would touch her name. She'd gone from the youngest lead guide certified on the range to running marginal trips out of a border town for tourists who wanted photographs of llamas.

For eight years she had believed it. Unforeseeable. Geological. Bad luck, which is the mountain's native language, and she had simply been standing too close when it spoke.

Then Elias opened his folder, and page after page after page described a different language entirely.

"Read this one," he said now, and passed her a sheet by headlamp light because his hands couldn't hold it steady.

It was an internal memorandum, agency letterhead half-redacted, dated fourteen months before Tomas fell. *Reconnaissance activity in the Ceniza sector is to be discontinued. Mineral survey assets in adjacent sectors will be relocated. Any independent expedition activity in the corridor should be discouraged through available administrative means.*

Discouraged through available administrative means.

"The Chimenea is in the corridor," Elias said quietly. "Your route. Your client. You inspected that anchor yourself?"

"Forty minutes before."

"And it came out whole."

"It came out whole."

Elias let the silence sit there between them, breathing with the cave. "I'm not telling you what happened. I don't know what happened. I'm telling you that three weeks after your accident, a private geotechnical firm with an agency contract filed a stability report on exactly that face. It exists. I found the reference number. The report itself has been sealed for eight years under a classification that doesn't apply to geology." He looked at her, and there was no pity in it, which she was grateful for; pity would have broken something. "They didn't kill your client, probably. They just needed climbers to stop looking at that wall. And when one died anyway, the finding was convenient, and someone chose not to look too hard, because looking too hard might have raised questions about why the report was sealed."

Kestrel set the page down on the ice very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate.

Eight years. Eight years of counting seasons instead of birthdays. Eight years of watching younger guides take clients up routes she knew better than their own mothers' kitchens, of eating debt and dust and the particular loneliness of a woman the community liked but did not quite believe. She had built a whole self out of that guilt — a lean, hard, penitential self, a woman who lived off-grid because she'd concluded she deserved the grid's absence. And underneath all of it, all along, the truth had been sitting in a filing cabinet: not unforeseeable. Not geological. *Administrative.*

Her rage, when it finally arrived, did not come hot. It came glacial — vast, slow, with a direction.

* * *

"So what do you want from me?" she said. "Honestly. Not the version you gave the man with the gun."

Elias leaned his head back against the ice. In the dying headlamp light his face looked carved, cheekbones sharp from eleven days of ration hunger, a week-old cut over one eyebrow scabbed black. "I want to go home," he said. "But home is a word for people who aren't carrying what I'm carrying. So. Realistically?" He flexed his bandaged fingers, winced. "I want to get this data somewhere it can't be un-happened. There are copies — Nena's pack has one set, if your porter hasn't lost it, and there's a cache at the old Refugio Sur with another. But copies mean nothing without a witness who'll testify. Me. And me means nothing while Sorel's recovery unit can decide I died of exposure and save everyone the trouble."

"There's a unit on the mountain."

"There's a unit behind *you*. They've been following your rope team since the valley. I watched them through my scope two days ago — four men, good boots, bad manners. Sorel didn't send you to find me, Kestrel. He sent you to find me *for* them. You're the leash. They're just letting out line."

She had suspected it since the marker moved uphill against logic. Hearing it stated flatly, in a voice hoarse with cold, landed differently than suspicion. Suspicion you could argue with. This was just weather.

"If I radio Sorel tonight," she said slowly, working it through, "and give him your position, I walk away clean. File closed, name restored, money in the bank. You go down the mountain in a bag or a helicopter, depending on how cooperative you are, and the story dies with you, and the seam in the ice gets quietly mined by a company nobody's ever heard of, and in ten years some valley town has a tailings pond where the school used to be."

"That's the honest version, yes."

"And if I don't—"

"If you don't, you're a fugitive. Armed, technically, once you're seen with me. Your little team becomes accomplices. Sorel will list you, freeze whatever you own — which I gather is a tent and a grudge — and the recovery unit stops being careful about who falls into a crevasse." He said it without drama, the way you'd read a weather forecast. "I'm not going to pretend the arithmetic favors us. I've done it every night for eleven days. It never does. The only variable that changes anything is whether the evidence gets out. Everything else is just choosing how we die if it doesn't."

Kestrel got up and walked to the mouth of the cave.

The night outside was enormous. Stars in the hard clarity of altitude, the glacier below a pale river of shadow, and far off — south, toward the calving faces — a flicker of silent lightning inside the ice where something vast was letting go. The season was turning. She could feel it in the way the cold came up through her boot soles, older and wetter than last week's cold. Calving season. The mountain was already closing its accounts.

She thought about Sorel's face in the border-town office: patient, reasonable, a man who offered you a pen as though the signature were a formality your hesitation was merely delaying. She thought about the file he'd laid on the desk — her file — thick as a novel, every mistake annotated, every year of her exile summarized in bureaucratic prose. He had known exactly what it was. Leverage. *Available administrative means.*

And she thought about Tomas Rey's mother, who had sent her a letter two years after the funeral. Not angry. Worse than angry — grateful. *Thank you for giving him those five days. He wrote that he was happier on the mountain than he'd ever been anywhere.* Kestrel had kept that letter in her gear box through six moves, unable to answer it, because answering it honestly would have meant saying *your son died on a route the state wanted empty, and I spent eight years apologizing to a ghost for a crime I didn't commit, and I still don't know who to give the apology back to.*

Now she knew.

The rage found its direction, and the direction was downhill, toward the lowlands, toward filing cabinets and redactions and men in warm rooms. But the road there ran across the ice, through calving season, past a recovery unit with bad manners, and the only currency she had left to spend was herself.

* * *

When she came back inside, Elias was asleep — or pretending to be, which amounted to the same trust. She sat down across from him and did the arithmetic one final time, out loud, in a whisper, because saying things aloud made them real and she needed this real.

"Exoneration," she said. "Money. My name back." The old words tasted like the ration bars: engineered to satisfy, nourishing nothing. "Versus a dead engineer's mother getting an answer. Versus a valley that keeps its water. Versus—" she stopped, because the next item didn't have words yet, only a weight: the young porter down at Camp Two who idolized her without knowing why she didn't deserve it, and the rope partner who'd followed her up here on eight years of unfinished history.

Versus becoming, permanently, the woman the file said she was — purchasable, leashed, useful.

"No," she said to the cave, to the breathing ice, to the eight-year-old ghost of herself. "No deal."

Elias opened his eyes. He hadn't been asleep at all.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'd have understood if you'd taken it."

"I know. That's why I'm not taking it." She checked her watch against the dark. Four hours until the pre-dawn window, when the ice was coldest and most stable, when they'd move. "Here's what happens now. We go down to Camp Two. We tell Dario and Nena enough of the truth to keep them alive — which is more of it than I'd like. We move fast, we stay off the standard lines, and we get you and that folder to the Refugio Sur cache before Sorel's unit closes the gap. After that—"

"After that?"

After that was the part she hadn't finished calculating. The routes off this side of the range were two: the pass, which the unit would be watching, and the Chimenea descent — the route that had killed Tomas Rey, the route no guide had led a client down in eight years, the route that lived in her nightmares with its whole, pulled-out anchors. If it came to that, if the net closed, she knew which way her feet would point. The mountain had taken a life from her on that route. It owed her a passage.

"After that," she said, "we improvise."

Elias studied her for a long moment, and then he did something she hadn't expected: he smiled, small and crooked and human, the first one she'd seen on him. "You know," he said, "Sorel told me you were the best guide on the range before the accident. I thought it was flattery to make the job easier."

"It wasn't flattery."

"No," Elias agreed. "I don't think it was."

Above them, deep in the dark, the glacier exhaled — a long, grinding sigh that shivered frost from the ceiling of the cave — and Kestrel felt the mountain shift its weight around them like something settling in to watch.

Four hours. Then down, into the arithmetic of survival, with the first thunder of the calving season already rolling somewhere beyond the stars.
