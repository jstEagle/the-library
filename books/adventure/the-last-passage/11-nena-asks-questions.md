## 11. Nena Asks Questions

Nena had learned to count danger the way her grandmother taught her to count sheep: not by looking straight at it, but by noticing what was missing.

Missing, since Camp Two: the second radio. Kestrel had carried both units out of the tent that morning, one clipped to her pack strap and one zipped into the lid of her own bag, and Nena had watched her do it without comment. Missing: the satellite phone's charging cable, which Kestrel kept in an inside pocket now instead of the communal duffel. Missing: any mention of the extraction plan. Every night Kestrel marked their position on the map, but she never marked where they were going to come down, or when, or who would be waiting.

And missing — this was the one that kept Nena awake in the orange bite of the tent light — was Dario. Not physically. He snored three feet away like a landslide with a heartbeat. But he'd taken to walking off alone at dusk, standing on the moraine with his back turned, hands cupped around something small and dark. The first time, she'd thought it was a cigarette. Dario didn't smoke. The second time, she'd seen the green pulse of a screen between his fingers before he killed it and turned around smiling.

She lay in her bag doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

Three clients' worth of gear stacked at an empty camp above the icefall. Tents zipped from the inside. No tracks. A government man in a pressed shirt who'd looked at Kestrel like a man looks at a tool he already owns. A fee big enough to clear debts — debts Nena hadn't known Kestrel had until Sorel's arrival made them visible, the way wind makes a flag visible. And now a rope partner whispering into a device at sunset while the woman paying them all pretended not to see.

Nena was twenty-three years old and had grown up below a mountain that ate people politely, one season at a time, so she knew better than most that the dangerous thing was never the mountain. It was what people brought onto the mountain.

* * *

She waited for the right hour. There was a right hour; her grandmother had taught her that too. You didn't confront a person mid-task or mid-fear. You confronted them when their hands were busy with something small, because busy hands couldn't reach for defenses.

Kestrel's hands were busy with the stove at first light, coaxing flame out of a canister that wanted to die. Steam rose off the pot in the gray cold. Dario was still zipped in his bag. Elias — the frostbitten man they'd pulled out of the ice cave two days ago, the man whose existence had rearranged every fact of this expedition — sat propped against his pack, his bandaged hands resting on his knees like something borrowed.

Nena crouched across the stove from Kestrel and said, quietly, "I want you to tell me what this is."

Kestrel didn't look up. "Oatmeal."

"You know what I mean."

The flame hissed. Somewhere below them, far down the glacier, the ice spoke — a long groan like a door opening in a house with no rooms left.

"I've been counting," Nena said. "Two radios. One charger. A partner who talks to somebody every night facing away from camp. A client contract nobody's read me. And a man we found alive in a hole in the ice who your government friend described as a casualty." She looked at Kestrel directly. "I'm not stupid, and I'm not a child, and I carried sixty pounds up the icefall for you without one complaint. So tell me what this is, or I take my share of the rope and I walk down alone."

Dario stirred in his bag. Kestrel's eyes flicked toward him and back.

"Not here," she said.

"Then here is exactly where."

Something moved behind Kestrel's face — Nena watched it happen, watched the guide weigh the young porter against the mission and find the scales tipping — and then Kestrel reached over and unzipped Dario's bag herself, briskly, the way you wake someone you've decided not to be gentle with.

"Up," she said. "Team meeting. Now."

* * *

They sat in a triangle around the stove because there was no other furniture at five thousand meters. Elias stayed at the edge of it, half in his bag, watching with the stillness of a man who has learned that movement attracts fire.

Kestrel poured tea into four cups even though Elias's hands could barely hold one, and Nena understood that the pouring was a ceremony, a way of making the truth official before speaking it.

"You're right," Kestrel said. "There are things I haven't told you. Some of them I wasn't allowed to tell you. Some of them I was too much of a coward to tell you. I'm going to fix the second kind right now, and after that you can decide about the first."

Nena wrapped both hands around her cup and waited.

"The survey team didn't get lost," Kestrel said. "They were erased. The agency that hired me sent a recovery unit behind us — armed men whose job is to make sure Elias Reyes dies above the calving line and that no one who saw him alive comes down to say so. That includes us. Dario has been reporting our position to them, and before you say anything"—she raised a hand, and Nena realized her mouth was already open—"you should hear why."

Dario looked at his tea. His jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing gravel.

"My daughter needs an operation," he said. "The kind of operation you don't get in this country unless somebody important owes you. Sorel found that out. He didn't offer me money. He offered me a surgeon." He lifted his eyes, and they were wet and furious at himself. "I told myself I was protecting you. Feeding them our position meant they didn't need to come looking hard, didn't need to shoot first. I told myself a lot of things at dusk, Nena. None of them survive daylight."

Nena stared at him. She felt the betrayal land somewhere under her ribs, but underneath it, worse, she felt recognition — because she understood loving someone enough to sell out a mountain, and she was suddenly grateful she'd never been tested.

"And you?" she said to Kestrel. "What did Sorel hold over you?"

Kestrel set down her cup. For a moment Nena thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "You've heard the story. Everyone in the valley has heard the story. Eight years ago, a client died on my rope. Tomás Herrera. They took my license for it. They said I turned back too late, that I pushed past weather I should have respected, that a guide who does that is a murderer with a resume."

"That's not true?"

"What happened is true. The reasons aren't." Kestrel fed a splinter of wood to the stove, and the firelight climbed her face. "We were descending the north side. The route that goes through the serac zone — everyone calls it the Last Passage, because that's usually what it is. A block came off above us. It hit Tomás. It didn't hit me. Forty meters of rope between us, and the mountain chose." Her voice went flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who has said these words to a ceiling many nights. "Except the mountain didn't choose. I found that out later. The agency had a seismic survey running on that face that week — blasting charges, stress tests, the same program that's sitting under this glacier right now waiting to become somebody's battery metal. They triggered instabilities all along the north side and never filed it, because filing it would have shut down the operation. Tomás died because a ministry wanted data. My career died as the cleanup. My blacklisting wasn't punishment. It was packaging."

The stove ticked. Above them the sky was going from gray to the color of old bone.

"They buried the report," Kestrel said. "Same way they'll bury Elias. Same way they'd have buried you, if you'd been on the wrong rope that day." She looked up, and her eyes were steady in a way that frightened Nena more than tears would have. "So no, I didn't bring you up here to find a lost team. I brought you up here because a man put a file in front of me and a gun behind it, and I said yes, and then I found out what yes meant. And now I'm telling you, because you asked, and because you deserve to decide with your eyes open whether you stay roped to me."

Nena set her cup down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"You let him report our position," she said. "Dario. You knew, and you let him."

"I used him," Kestrel said. "Every position he gave them was one I chose to give. It bought us time and it kept their helicopters predictable. It also used a man's family as leverage, which makes me no better than Sorel, and I know it."

Silence. The glacier groaned again, closer this time, or maybe just more honest in the cold morning air.

Elias spoke for the first time, his voice cracked and low from the frostbite and the days of silence. "For what it's worth — I ran from them for six weeks because I thought anyone I touched would end up dead. These two proved me half right." He nodded at Dario. "But only half."

Nena looked at the three of them: the traitor with a sick daughter, the blacklisted guide who'd just handed her the truth like a live charge, the fugitive with bandaged hands. Any one of them could destroy the other two before noon. And yet.

Her grandmother had a word for a rope team after it has seen each other fall. Nena couldn't translate it. It meant something like: the knot holds not because the rope is strong but because everyone agrees to fall together.

"I'm staying," she said. "Not for the fee. The fee's blood money now and we all know it. I'm staying because if I walk down that glacier alone, I'm walking down into the country they own, carrying nothing, knowing nothing, worth nothing to them except silence." She picked her cup back up. "Up here, at least, I'm worth a bullet, and bullets miss."

Dario made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief.

"There's one condition," Nena said. "From now on, everything gets said at the stove. No more dusk walks. No more private radios. If we fracture, we fracture in the open, where the ice can't use it against us."

Kestrel studied her for a long moment — and Nena saw something shift in the older woman's face, some assessment re-running and returning a different number. Respect, maybe. Or relief. Or the particular exhaustion of a leader who has been carrying a secret so long she'd forgotten it had weight.

"Agreed," Kestrel said. "Everything at the stove."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, moving fast because the sky to the west had gone the bruised purple that meant weather, and weather above the calving line was not weather, it was verdict.

Nena took point on the moraine because Kestrel asked her to, and understood it for what it was: not a test, a promotion. Behind her, Dario's footsteps fell in rhythm with hers, and once he said, without preamble, "If we get down — if my daughter gets her surgery — I'm going to tell her about this. All of it. I don't want her growing up thinking her father was brave."

"That's the bravest thing you've said," Nena said, "and it's still a lie you're planning."

He almost smiled.

At midmorning they gained the shoulder where the glacier bent east, and Nena stopped, because the world had changed. Below them, spread out along the lower icefall like beads on a gray string, were six points of orange. Men. Moving. Roped in pairs, ascending the exact line Kestrel had chosen, following the exact track Dario had reported.

The recovery unit. Hours behind them, closing.

Kestrel came up beside her and stood looking down at the orange dots for a long time. When she finally spoke, her breath came out slow and white.

"They're not following us anymore," she said. "They're herding us. Everything above the calving line funnels into one chute, and the chute ends at a wall of ice nobody crosses." She rolled the map open against her knee, and her finger went to a place near its torn eastern edge — a place drawn in the old surveys as a blank, a nothing, a nameless col. "Unless we go here."

Nena leaned in. The contour lines crowded together like startled birds.

"What's there?"

"Nothing," Kestrel said. "That's the point. Nobody goes where there's nothing." She folded the map, and her eyes came up, and Nena saw that the woman standing on the shoulder of the glacier was no longer the same woman who'd poured tea at dawn. "Sorel will call tonight. He always calls when he thinks we're cornered. And when he does, I'm going to tell him exactly where we're going."

Nena frowned. "Why would you—"

"Because by the time he believes it," Kestrel said, "we'll already be somewhere his map doesn't have a name for."

Far below, the first of the orange figures raised an arm, and the wind carried up to them — faint, distorted, unmistakable — the flat electronic voice of a radio transmission beginning.
