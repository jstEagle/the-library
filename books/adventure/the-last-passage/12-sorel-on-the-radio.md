## 12. Sorel on the Radio

The radio crackled to life at 0400, when the sky was still the color of a bruise and the wind had that hollow, teeth-aching quality it only got above four thousand meters.

Kestrel was awake before the first burst of static. She'd been awake most of the night, lying in her bag listening to the glacier groan beneath them like something enormous turning over in its sleep, running the arithmetic she couldn't stop running: three days up, two days back if they turned now, five days if they kept going, and somewhere behind them — or maybe not behind them at all — Sorel's recovery unit moving through the dark with better gear and no conscience.

She crawled out of the tent into air so cold it felt personal. The stars were brutal and close. Below their camp on the moraine shelf, the icefall glimmered faintly, a staircase of broken light descending into blackness. Above, the high glacier sat white and silent under the last of the night, waiting for whatever came next.

The radio was Dario's watch. He had it clipped inside his parka, and he was already out of his tent when Kestrel turned, standing in the blue gloom with the handset pressed to his ear, his face lit from below by the dial's green glow.

"Who is it?" she said.

Dario didn't answer right away. That was the first wrong thing. Dario answered questions the way he placed protection — fast, instinctive, twenty years of habit. This pause lasted two full seconds, which in Dario-time was a geological era.

"Sorel," he said finally. "Says he wants you."

Kestrel held out her hand. "Give it."

Dario looked at the handset. Looked at her. Then he handed it over, and his eyes did something she didn't like — something quick and sideways, a flick toward Nena's tent and back again, like a man checking whether anyone else was listening.

Kestrel pressed the transmit key. "Crane."

"Señora Villalobos." Sorel's voice came through thin and precise, stripped of everything but the words themselves, as if the man had been designed for exactly this bandwidth. "You're up early."

"You called me."

"I did." A pause filled with wind. "I wanted to be the first voice you heard today. I find that matters, in your profession. The first voice sets the tone for the whole day on a mountain."

"What do you want, Sorel?"

"Progress, primarily. You're past the icefall. I'm told the crossing cost you a rope and nearly a climber. Congratulations. Most people take longer."

The words landed cold. They'd made the icefall crossing yesterday afternoon. Dario's fall, Nena's anchor — it had happened in ninety seconds of screaming rope and flying spindrift, and afterward none of them had spoken much about it. Nobody had reported it. Nobody could have reported it.

Unless someone had.

Kestrel kept her voice flat. "You have eyes up here."

"I have a satellite pass every six hours and a very patient analyst. Your camp moved eleven hundred meters since yesterday. You're on the medial moraine below the upper basin. I know about the fall because your porter's harness shows stress damage and your rope partner has been favoring his left knee since Camp Two. Observation, señora. Not surveillance." Another pause, and this one had texture to it, a smile in it somewhere. "Though I'll admit some of my information comes from closer sources."

* * *

She made herself walk while she talked. It was an old trick — motion kept the mind honest, kept the body between the voice and the fear. She paced the length of the moraine shelf with the handset against her ear and her breath pluming silver, and behind her she could feel Dario standing where she'd left him, not packing, not moving, just standing, and that silence had its own weight now, its own specific gravity.

"The survey team," Sorel said. "Tell me what you've found."

"Tents zipped from inside. Gear stacked neat as a shop display. No tracks. You've seen the photos — you sent me up here with them."

"And above the camp?"

"Above the camp is ice."

"Come now." His voice softened, the way a man softens when he thinks he's being generous. "You found the marker moved uphill. You found the journal page. You're a thorough woman, Kestrel Villalobos — it's why I chose you, despite everything. 'Despite' doing so much work in that sentence, don't you think? So don't insult us both. You know Elias Vidal is alive. You may even know where he's going."

The wind gusted off the upper basin and Kestrel turned her shoulder into it. Somewhere far below, down in the valleys where morning hadn't arrived yet, a dog barked once and gave up.

"If I knew where he was," she said, "why would I tell you?"

"Because I'm about to make you an offer that will sound, in retrospect, like the last reasonable moment of your life." Sorel let that sit. When he continued, his voice had shifted registers entirely — the bureaucrat gone, and underneath him something older and more patient, a man who had spent decades learning exactly what people were worth. "Full exoneration. Not expungement — exoneration. The file opened, reviewed, formally corrected. The incident reclassified. Your certification restored, your name cleared in every registry in the republic. The blacklist becomes what it always should have been: a clerical error."

Kestrel stopped walking.

She hadn't expected it to land. She'd told herself on the approach, in the icefall, in every hour since, that whatever Sorel offered would be paper, and paper was what had ruined her — a report, a finding, a signature that turned a dead client into a dead guide's fault. But hearing it said aloud, hearing the word *exoneration* pronounced carefully by a man who had the power to make it true, something in her chest moved anyway, some old animal hope she'd thought she'd buried under ten seasons of counting miles instead of years.

"The client's family gets the truth too," Sorel went on, reading her silence like a route description. "A formal apology. Compensation. Closure. I understand you still write to the daughter. I understand she doesn't write back. This would change that."

"You've read my mail."

"I've read your file. There's a difference, and the difference is that everything in the file is true." He paused. "Except the conclusion. And I can fix conclusions, señora. It's the one thing I'm genuinely good at."

Below her boot, deep in the glacier, something boomed — a single tectonic note, the ice settling, or shifting, or beginning. The calving season was coming on. Everyone on this mountain could feel it the way animals feel weather.

"And the price," Kestrel said.

"Elias Vidal's location. Alive, if possible. His data recovered intact. And your discretion regarding anything he may have shown you or told you." A breath of static. "That's all. One man, one memory card, one signature on a form that gives you your life back."

"One man," Kestrel repeated. "You keep saying it like he's inventory."

"He's a variable, and variables are what I manage." Sorel's patience was starting to show its seams, very fine ones, the kind you'd only notice if you'd spent a career reading weather and men. "He walked into a crevasse field in winter with no resupply and a frostbitten hand. Whatever he thinks he found, whatever he thinks it means — it changes nothing. The deposit will be developed or it won't. The decision was made in offices he's never seen, by people whose names he'll never learn. He can freeze up there holding his evidence, or he can come down, sign a confidentiality agreement, and be a footnote. Those are the only two endings available to him. All you control, señora, is which ending you're standing next to."

* * *

She looked back at the tents.

Nena's was stirring now — he'd hear none of this, thank God, the kid slept like the dead after a carry day. But Dario stood beside his own tent with his arms folded, watching her across forty meters of frozen scree, and in the pre-dawn gray his face was unreadable in a way Dario's face had never been unreadable. Twenty years of rope together. She'd caught his falls; he'd caught hers. She'd held his daughter at her baptism. She knew how he took his coffee, how he prayed, how he lied — badly, always badly, with too much eye contact.

And he was looking at her now the way a man looks at a radio conversation he already knows the shape of.

"Your rope partner," Sorel said, and the name arrived gently, almost kindly, which was how Sorel did his worst work. "Dario Fuentes. Good man. Family man. Three daughters, isn't it? The eldest starting university in the capital this year. Expensive, university. I imagine the contract fee covers it, but I imagine a man like Dario worries about second semesters, third years, books, rent—" He let the list trail off into the static like a rope paying out over an edge. "He checks in twice a day. Position reports. Very disciplined. You trained him well."

The world went very quiet and very large.

Kestrel became aware of her own heartbeat, of the wind, of the boom and mutter of the glacier, of the forty meters of frozen ground between herself and the man she'd trusted with her life on more routes than she could count. Dario was still watching her. Still not moving. And she watched him watch her, and she thought: *he knows I know*, or *he knows I might know*, and either way the arithmetic had already changed, the way it changes in a crevasse field when you realize the bridge you're standing on has been hollow all along and the question is no longer whether the ice holds but how long it's been failing.

"Dario," she said into the radio, not breaking eye contact, "has been reporting our position."

"Dario," Sorel agreed, "has been ensuring my analysts can do their jobs. Which protects everyone. Including you. If we didn't know where you were, señora, we couldn't guarantee your safety up there. The recovery unit moves fast and asks fewer questions than I do. Consider his reports your insurance policy."

"Consider them what they are."

"Consider them loyalty under pressure," Sorel said. "It's the only kind there is. The other kind is just habit, and habit fails the first time the mountain gets serious. You of all people know that." A pause. "So. The offer stands until noon tomorrow. Exoneration, compensation, closure for the Ordoñez family — everything you've wanted for ten years, priced at one location. You don't even have to bring him in. Just the coordinates. Just the word. Say it on this frequency and by spring you're guiding again under your own name."

Kestrel said nothing.

"Think about it," Sorel said. "But not too long. The calving season won't wait, and neither will the recovery unit. They're two days behind you now. By tomorrow night they'll be one." Another pause, and then, almost as an afterthought, almost human: "For what it's worth, I hope you say yes. I've read your file very carefully, señora. You're not the woman the paperwork says you are. It would please me to correct the record."

The line clicked dead.

* * *

For a long time Kestrel stood on the moraine with the dead handset in her fist, listening to the mountain breathe.

Then she walked back to Dario.

He didn't run. She'd half expected him to — half braced for it, the way you brace for a bridge to go. But he stood his ground, arms still folded, jaw working around something, and when she stopped in front of him he spoke first, which was also unlike him.

"How much did he tell you?"

"Enough. Twice a day, he says. Position reports." She kept her voice low, level, pitched under the wind so it wouldn't carry to Nena's tent. "How long?"

Dario closed his eyes. Opened them. "Since Basecamp."

"Since Basecamp." She repeated it without inflection, which took everything she had. "Every camp. Every route decision. Every place I put my rope and my name."

"He said it was safety. Coordination. That the recovery unit wouldn't move unless—"

"Dario." She said his name once, quietly, and he stopped talking. In the growing light his face looked suddenly older, the lines around his mouth cut deeper, and she understood with a strange, sick clarity that he wasn't lying anymore — he was past lying, standing on the far side of it, and the thing in his face wasn't guilt exactly. It was the look of a man who had done the math himself and gotten an answer he could live with and was now discovering, watching her face, that maybe he couldn't.

"They have my girls," he said finally. "Not — not literally. But the scholarship program, the one through the ministry, Marta's tuition, it all routes through—" He made a gesture, helpless, at the whole invisible apparatus. "One phone call. One phone call and it's gone, and he made sure I understood that. He never threatened. He just... described things. The way he does."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "The way he does."

"So I told them where we camped. Where we were headed. Nothing else, Kestrel, I swear on my mother — I never told them what we found, because you haven't told *me* what you found, and maybe that was cowardice too, maybe I didn't want to know, but the positions, yes. The positions." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "Say something. Call me a traitor. Take my share. Do whatever you have to do, but say something."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. The sun was coming up now behind the eastern peaks, and the light came down the glacier in a slow gold flood, touching the icefall, touching the upper basin, touching the high white silence where a frostbitten man was hiding with evidence worth billions and a head start measured in storms.

"Noon tomorrow," she said. "That's how long Sorel's offer runs. Exoneration. My file, fixed. Ten years of my life handed back for one set of coordinates." She watched Dario absorb that — watched him understand what it meant, that she was telling him the size of the thing she was being asked to trade, and that she was telling him *before deciding*. "And the recovery unit is two days behind us. One, by tomorrow night. You've been making sure of it."

"Kestrel—"

"I'm not deciding yet." She turned away, toward the tents, toward Nena's sleeping shape and the smell of melting snow and the small domestic machinery of breakfast at altitude. "Get the stove lit. We break camp in an hour."

"Where are we going?"

She paused at the flap of her tent. Above them the upper basin waited, white and featureless, and somewhere in it a melt cave, a man, a seam of ore glowing under a headlamp, and a truth that Sorel's entire machine existed to bury.

"Up," she said. "We're going up."

Behind her, Dario stood very still among the rocks, a man holding a radio he could no longer pretend was innocent, and Kestrel lay down in her bag without sleeping and began, for the first time in ten years, to count hours instead of miles — because noon tomorrow was coming, and the mountain between her and it was going to make her spend every one.
