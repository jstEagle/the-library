## 13. The Confrontation at Camp Three

Camp Three sat in a shallow col between two ribs of rock, close enough to the glacier's edge that we could hear it breathing — that deep, tectonic groan the ice makes when the temperature drops and the whole mass contracts like something sleeping and dreaming badly. We pitched the tents in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, melted snow on the stove, and for an hour everything was almost ordinary. Nena laughed at something Elias said about freeze-dried rations. The wind dropped. The sky over the western peaks went the color of a bruise healing.

I let myself believe, for one hour, that we had outrun it.

Then Dario came back from checking the cache line, and he wouldn't look at me.

I'd known him twenty years. I'd seen him lie to customs officers, to clients, to his first wife, to himself about his knees. Dario lying was a performance — shoulders loose, eyes bright, hands busy. What I saw now was not a man lying. It was a man who had stopped being able to hold his face together. He set the ice screws down by the tent door with exaggerated care, each one placed like a chess piece, and when he finally raised his head his eyes went to Elias first, then to me, then away toward the glacier as if the answer to something might be written there.

"Dario," I said.

"Give me a minute."

"You've had a minute. You've had a minute since the moraine."

Nena looked up from the stove. Elias went very still, the way he'd gone still in the melt cave when I first found him — a stillness I now understood was not calm but readiness, a body remembering what it was to be hunted.

Dario sat down on the cache bag. He put his face in his gloves. And then, because he had never in his life been able to do anything halfway, he said it plainly.

"The radio's gone."

* * *

I want to say I was surprised. I want to tell you my heart did something dramatic, that the world tilted. But the truth is uglier: some part of me had been carrying the knowledge for two days without letting it become a thought. The way you carry a stone in your boot and refuse to stop and take it out because stopping would mean admitting how much it hurts.

The radio had been in the blue duffel. The blue duffel had been Dario's responsibility since the trailhead, because Dario always carried the radio, the way he always took point on scree and always claimed the tent with the broken zipper — small sovereignties, the territory of a man who needed to feel useful. This morning the duffel had felt wrong to me. Lighter. I'd told myself it was the fuel canister we'd burned through.

"When?" I said.

"Last night. While you slept." He pulled the glove off his left hand, worked the fingers like they were stiff, though they weren't. "There's a spot two hundred meters down the col where the ridge reflects signal off the valley wall. I keyed it twice. Once from Camp One, once from here." His voice was flat, reciting. Confession as inventory. "Grid references. Headcount. Direction of travel."

"And they answered?"

"They don't answer." He almost smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all day, worse than the serac field, worse than Sorel's voice on the satellite phone in chapter after chapter of my old nightmares. "They just acknowledge. Three clicks. That's all. Three clicks and you know someone heard you, and you never know who."

Nena had stood up. She was holding the spoon from the stove pot like she'd forgotten it was in her hand. "Dario. What are you saying? Who did you—"

"He's been reporting us," I said. "To Sorel's recovery unit. To the people whose job is to put a bullet in Elias and make the rest of us a footnote."

The words came out level, and I was proud of that, and ashamed of it too, because level was what Sorel sounded like, and somewhere in the last ten days I had learned the language of the people I hated.

Elias moved. Not toward Dario — around him, placing himself between Dario and the slope, between Dario and the route down. I understood the geometry instantly. If Dario ran, Elias wanted to be the thing he ran into. Frostbitten, half-starved, armed with a battered pistol and four days of desperation, and still thinking tactically. Survival does that to a man. It had done it to me once. I'd sworn I'd never let it again.

"How much?" Elias asked quietly.

Dario flinched. "It wasn't money."

"Everything is money. Money, or what money buys."

"My family." And there it broke open — the flatness cracking, the performance of confession collapsing into the real thing underneath. Dario's voice came apart like wet rope under load. "Lucía's surgery was eight months ago. You knew that, Kestrel. You gave me the advance yourself. But it didn't cover the second operation. It didn't cover the rehab, the travel, the — do you know what a hospital in this country costs if you're not the kind of person hospitals care about? Do you know what I'm worth? My house is worth less than her spine. My *life*—" He stopped. Breathed. "Sorel's people came to me before we even left town. They knew about Lucía. They had her file. They had photographs of her at the clinic. They didn't threaten. That's the thing everyone gets wrong about them. They didn't threaten at all. They just made sure I understood exactly how alone we were, and then they offered to not be alone anymore."

The stove hissed. Somewhere below us, the glacier groaned again, long and low, like a held note.

"So every night," Nena said, and her voice was shaking but her chin was up, and God help me I loved her for it, "every night we slept next to you, you were telling them where we slept."

"Yes."

"And the icefall. When Dario fell—"

"That wasn't planned," Dario said sharply, and for the first time there was anger in it, real anger, the kind that comes from being accused of more than you did. "You think I'd feed Tomás into a crevasse? You think I'd risk any of you? I gave them position and heading. That's all I ever gave them. Position and heading. What they did with it—" He spread his hands. "I told myself it wouldn't matter. I told myself they'd wait until we found what they wanted, until it was clean, until no one got hurt. People tell themselves things. You of all people know that, Kestrel."

That landed where he meant it to. I let it land. There was a version of this conversation where I threw it back at him — the client who died on my rope, the inquiry, the blacklist, the years I'd spent telling myself stories about what happened above the icefall on Cochrane's expedition. He'd earned the right to use it. He'd carried me through those years, cooked for me, roped up with me when no other guide in the province would touch my name.

That was exactly why it cost so much.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about betrayal in the mountains: you cannot walk away from it. In the city, betrayal is an ending. You slam a door, you change your number, you grieve and move on and the betrayer becomes a story you tell at parties. Up here, betrayal is a logistics problem. The man who sold your position is also the man who knows the descent route, who carries the spare stove, whose body you have trusted on rope-end for twenty years. You cannot fire him. There is nowhere to fire him *to*. The nearest human settlement is two days down through terrain that kills the careless, and the calving season has already started — we'd heard the first collapses that morning, distant thunder with no clouds, the mountain clearing its throat.

So you stand in the cold with the wind picking up and you do arithmetic.

Elias was watching me do it. I could see him watching, cataloguing, the way he must have watched his own survey team in the last hours before they scattered or died or however it had ended for them above the icefall. Waiting to see what kind of animal I was.

Nena was watching too, and hers was a different gaze entirely — young, appalled, waiting for me to be the person she'd decided I was when she signed on with a legend who turned out to be a blacklisted drunk with debts. I hadn't corrected her yet. That debt was coming due soon; I could feel it. But not here. Not in front of him.

Dario sat on the cache bag with his ruined face in his hands and waited for whatever I was.

I thought about Sorel. I thought about the file, the fee, the smooth voice on the phone offering exoneration like a coupon. I thought about the client — Tomás Reyes, twenty-six years old, who fell four hundred meters down the north face because a state survey team had been ordered to keep their presence secret and had cut fixed lines that were never logged, and because I had led my client into a corridor I couldn't fully verify, and because the agency that cut the lines had buried the evidence and buried me along with it. I had spent eleven years believing the mountain killed Tomás. Then I'd found Elias, and the melt cave, and the documents, and I'd learned the mountain was only ever the instrument. There were hands on it the whole time.

And now one of those hands had reached into my team and turned my oldest friend into a finger pointing at us.

"Dario." I crouched down so we were eye to eye. Up close I could see what the last week had taken from him — the gray in his beard he used to pluck out, the tremor in his jaw he used to hide behind coffee. "Look at me."

He looked.

"I'm not going to hurt you. I want that said out loud, in front of witnesses, so there's no misunderstanding later, on either side." I kept my voice low and even. "But hear the rest of it too. Your three clicks. Your grid references. Every one of them pointed at Nena. At Elias. At me. You didn't sell me out — I'm already sold, I've been sold for eleven years, Sorel owns my file and my name and probably my grave plot by now. You sold *her*. A nineteen-year-old porter who thinks you're funny. So here's what happens now."

He nodded, small, like a man accepting a sentence.

"You keep your gear. You keep your skills — I'm not stupid enough to cut loose the strongest climber on this team in calving season, and you know it, which is why you confessed instead of running. You'll rope with me, same as always, because on the rope there is no yesterday. On the rope you are beyond reproach; that's the law and you taught it to me. But your share is gone. All of it. The fee, the balance, everything Sorel promised and everything I promised. You climb home with us and you get nothing but your life and your legs, and you will spend the rest of yours knowing that's more than you earned."

"Kestrel—"

"I'm not finished." Something in my tone closed his mouth. "And you don't touch the radio again. Not to report, not to warn them off, not to negotiate, not to save us at the last minute with some grand gesture. Your hands are done. If I see you within fifty meters of any transmitter for the rest of this traverse, mercy expires. Is that understood?"

"It is." His voice was sand. "Kestrel. For what it's worth—"

"It isn't worth anything. Not yet. Maybe on the far side of the range it'll be worth something. Ask me then."

* * *

We ate in silence after that, or near-silence — the small sounds of spoons, of snow hissing on the stove, of Nena re-packing gear with more force than nylon requires. Elias ate steadily, methodically, a man refueling machinery. Only when Nena turned in did he speak, quietly, across the flame.

"You could have made him leave."

"He'd have made it two kilometers. Then the recovery unit picks him up, and he tells them everything anyway, except now he tells them angry." I fed another pinch of snow into the pot. "A Dario who hates us is worth more to Sorel than a Dario who's ashamed. Shame slows a man down. Hatred aims him."

"And stripping his share?"

"That wasn't punishment." I watched the flame. "Punishment would've been leaving him for them. Taking the money — that's so he stays. A man with nothing waiting for him at the bottom has no reason to go down. A man with a debt to repay has a reason to follow us anywhere." I glanced up. "Mercy's cheaper than it looks. It's also a leash. I hate that I know that."

Elias considered me for a long moment, firelight moving in his eyes. "You'd have made a good agent."

"Don't." 

"I meant it as—"

"I know what you meant it as. Don't."

He almost smiled, and let it go, and lay back against his pack. But before sleep took him he said one more thing, so quietly I nearly missed it under the wind.

"For what it's worth — you did it differently than they would have. That matters. It matters to me."

I didn't answer. Outside, the glacier spoke again — longer this time, deeper, a sound with weight and intention behind it, and beneath the groan, faint but unmistakable, a sharp crack like a rifle shot. Then another. Closer.

Calving. Not distant anymore.

I crawled out of the tent into the dark and stood looking north, toward the high glacier, toward the pass Elias swore existed on the far side. The stars were out hard and cold, and against them the serac field above our route showed as a paler darkness, leaning, waiting. And far below us — so far I might have imagined it — a light blinked. Twice. Steady. Deliberate.

Three clicks' worth of somebody saying: *we hear you.*

I stood in the cold a long time, doing the only arithmetic left, and then I went and woke Nena, because there were things she deserved to hear from me before the mountain decided we'd run out of nights for honesty.

"Nena," I said. "Get up. We need to talk about why I really lost my license."

She sat up in the dark, and even before I began, even before the first word, I saw in her face that she had already started asking the questions — and that whatever answers I gave her tonight, the team that woke tomorrow would be smaller than the one that slept.

Below us, the glacier calved again, and the sound rolled up the col like a door closing.
