## 15. The Deposit

I had dreamed about this crevasse for four months. Not the ore — I'd tried not to dream of that — but the way down: the blue throat of it, the ladder of ice steps I'd cut with a borrowed axe while my team slept above and my hands shook so badly I had to stop every ten swings. In the dreams the walls were always closer than they were in life, pressing in like a jaw. In life they were worse, because in life I knew what waited at the bottom.

We came at it from the east, along the medial moraine where the glacier's two great arms ground against each other and threw up a spine of rock that no serac could reach. Kestrel walked point without being asked. She had been walking point for three days now, reading the ice the way other people read weather, and every hour she spent on it I watched something come back into her that the low country had taken out. Her body remembered. That was the thing about guides — you could strip them of their certification, their clients, their name, but the mountain kept its copy of them filed away, and all it took was altitude to retrieve it.

Nena came behind her, small and tireless, carrying more weight than any of us and complaining less than any of us, which worried me more than complaints would have. Dario was gone — sent down two days ago with a stripped pack and Kestrel's mercy, which was heavier than any punishment would have been. He'd taken the moraine route west toward the recovery unit's last known position, and none of us had said what we were all thinking: that his radio might already be warm.

So there were three of us when there should have been six. Three of us standing at the lip of a crevasse that didn't appear on any survey map because I had made sure of it myself, burning the coordinates out of the digital record with a magnet passed over the data cards, one slow pass, like erasing a name from a grave marker.

"Here?" Nena said. She looked down into the blue and her face did what faces do at edges — went smooth and young.

"Here," I said.

Kestrel crouched at the lip and studied it the way she studied everything, with a patience that looked like stillness but wasn't; it was motion held in reserve. "The bridge," she said. "There was a bridge."

"There was." I pointed to the far side, where a shelf of old snow clung to the wall like a torn sleeve. "It collapsed in the last calving cycle. I've been going around, through the tunnel." I nodded upstream, where the crevasse narrowed and a roof of packed ice arched over the gap, left over from some earlier season when the glacier had been under compression instead of tension. "It's tight. It's sound. But you'll want to hear me before you trust me on that."

"I don't trust you on anything yet," Kestrel said. There was no cruelty in it. It was simply the current state of the ledger between us, stated plainly, the way she stated everything. "Show me anyway."

* * *

The tunnel was exactly as tight as I remembered, which is to say it was an argument your body lost slowly. We went in single file, packs pushed ahead of us, headlamps throwing our shadows huge and wavering across walls of ice so old and so compressed that they had turned the color of deep water. Somewhere above us the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of a thing too large to hurry — and the vibration came down through the ice into my chest and stayed there.

Nena's breathing echoed ahead of me, quick and controlled. She'd been frightened since the icefall, I think, maybe since the empty camp, but she hadn't once suggested turning back, and I had begun to understand that this was not innocence. It was a decision. She was nineteen and she had decided, deliberately, with open eyes, to keep walking toward the thing that frightened her. I had been thirty-four when I first managed that, and I'd needed a government contract to talk me into it.

Kestrel stopped where the tunnel kinked and let me come abreast of her. In the close dark her face was half lamp-light and half shadow, and for a moment neither of us spoke, because the next few meters were the last ordinary meters either of us would ever stand in.

"It's below," I said. "Fifty meters of fixed line, then the chamber. What you're going to see —" I stopped. Started again. "I've shown it to no one. My team saw the seam from above, the day we found it. They never saw the rest. By nightfall they were dead."

"You're sure it wasn't the storm."

"The storm killed no one." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I'd used in briefings, the voice I hated. "Ruy died falling. That part was real — he fell into a moulin on the descent, and we couldn't reach him, and I have carried that. But the other three didn't fall. Camila was found at the edge of camp with her harness cut. Tomás and Inés were found in their tents, zipped in, and the coroner's report — the report Sorel's office wrote — says hypoxia. At five thousand two hundred meters, after nine days of acclimatization, both of them, the same night, in sealed tents they'd ventilated every previous night. The agency didn't even bother to make it good. They just made it official."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. The ice ticked around us, settling, patient.

"And you ran," she said.

"I ran." No excuse attached itself to the words. I let them stand bare. "I told myself I was running to carry the evidence out. Some days I even believe that. Other days I know I ran because staying meant dying, and I wanted very much not to die, and everything else — the data, the testimony, the truth — was the story I wrapped around the wanting." I met her eyes. "You should know which man you're roped to before we go down there."

She considered me with those gray eyes that gave nothing away cheaply. Then she said, "Everyone runs. The question is the direction." And she turned and kept moving, and I understood that I had just been weighed, and that the weighing was not finished, and that it might never be finished, and that this was as much grace as I was going to be offered.

* * *

The chamber opened below us like a held breath finally released.

I went down first, down the fifty meters of line I'd fixed in another life, my boots finding the steps I'd cut, each one worn rounder by my own repeated descents until they fit my feet like a signature. The lamp swung beneath me and the light fell into the space and the space took it and gave back nothing at first — just depth, just cold, just the sense of standing inside the planet's own thought.

Then I reached the floor and turned, and raised the lamp, and showed them.

The seam ran the length of the chamber wall, a vein of mineralized rock perhaps forty centimeters wide, exposed where the crevasse had split the mountain's shoulder like a surgeon's incision. Under ordinary light it would have been dull — brownish, unremarkable, the kind of rock you'd step over on any trail in the range. But under the full flood of the lamp it woke. Threads of metal caught the beam and threw it back changed: dull copper, then a strange greasy silver, then flashes of a color I have never found a right name for, somewhere between violet and oil-slick green, the light of rare earths doing what rare earths do, which is hoard light and pay it out wrong.

Nena made a small sound. Not a word. Something older than words.

"That's it," I said. "That's the seam. Neodymium, dysprosium, terbium — the metals that make the modern world possible and the modern world unable to live without. One vein. Conservative estimate, from the samples we pulled before —" I stopped myself. Before. Everything in my vocabulary had a *before* in it now. "From the samples. Enough to reprice an entire economy. Enough to make whoever holds it the arbiter of two continents' manufacturing for thirty years. Enough that men who think in billions looked at our little survey and saw not a discovery but a problem."

Kestrel hadn't spoken. She had walked to the wall — carefully, her crampons biting the ice floor — and put her lamp close to the seam, and stood there with her breath smoking in the frozen air, looking at it the way you look at a wound. Not with wonder. With recognition.

"How much is here," she said. It wasn't a question so much as a request for a number she could hate accurately.

"Fourteen billion dollars," I said. "At last year's prices. More every quarter, because every quarter the world needs more of what's in this wall and finds less of it anywhere else."

"And the agency knew before we did."

I took the breath that I had been taking for four months, the one that preceded every telling, and this time — with her, with the ice holding us like cupped hands — I let it out all the way.

"They commissioned us, Kestrel. That's the part I couldn't make my team believe, and it's the part that got them killed. We weren't explorers. We were confirmation. The agency's geophysics branch ran a satellite pass over this valley eighteen months ago — spectral analysis, the kind they use for missile sites — and the pass flagged an anomaly. A magnetic and radiometric signature consistent with heavy rare-earth mineralization, at depth, under the glacier. They didn't send us to find it. They sent us to measure how much of it there was, and how recoverable, and whether it could be extracted quietly." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Quietly. As if a glacier gives a damn about anyone's quiet."

Nena had come to stand beside Kestrel. Her lamp was shaking slightly. "So why kill you? Why not just take the data?"

"Because the data couldn't be taken cleanly." I moved to the alcove at the chamber's north end, where I had cached the heart of it four months ago, wrapped in a bivvy sack, sealed in a dry bag, buried under blocks I'd cut and fitted so precisely that the wall of ice looked uninterrupted. "Because a discovery becomes real the moment a second party can verify it. Our survey was public knowledge at the institute level — six names on the permit, six sets of field notes, a base-camp log transmitted by satellite every third day. You can't erase a number that six people memorized. So the plan was never to hide the deposit. The plan was to hide the *crime*. Let the deposit exist on paper as a geological curiosity. Let the extraction happen years from now, under a different permit, a different company, a different story. And let the only people who could connect the deposit to the agency's involvement die in a storm that everyone would agree was tragic and no one would examine."

I pulled the first block free. Cold air breathed out of the cache, air that had been sealed since autumn, air that smelled faintly of the sample bags and the chemical fixative and, underneath it, I swear, of fear — my own fear, soaked into the wrapping during those last nights, preserved at temperature like everything else the glacier keeps.

* * *

I laid it out on the ice floor between us, because evidence means nothing in a bag. It has to be touched. It has to be read aloud. It has to be witnessed, and I had waited four months for witnesses, and now here they were, kneeling on the floor of the earth with their lamps lit, and I spread the record of the worst thing I had ever been part of out flat like a map.

The field notebooks first. Six of them, mine and my team's, water-stained at the corners, the pages soft from handling. I opened to the entries from the eleventh day — the day we bottomed the crevasse and pulled core samples, the day Ruy was still alive, the day before the world ended. My own handwriting, steady then, describing the seam width, the grade estimates, the sample numbers cross-referenced to GPS fixes. And beside those entries, in a different pen, added later in camp: the transmission codes. Every evening we'd uplinked our data to the agency's relay, and every evening we logged the handshake codes, and those codes proved the uplinks had gone to a server registered not to the science ministry but to a directorate whose name appeared nowhere on our contract.

"The survey data," I said, and handed Kestrel the drive — a ruggedized card, physically intact, its contents mirrored twice in the cache and once, now, in a copy sewn into the lining of Nena's pack, because I had learned in four months of hiding that evidence carried by one man dies with one man. "Full spectral results. Core logs. Grade maps. And this —" I tapped the screen as it woke, pulling up the file I had opened a thousand times in the dark, the file I could recite like scripture, if scripture were written by accountants. "This is the internal memo. Dated nine days before our summit push. Classification stamp, distribution list of four. It references 'the Villalobos incident' as a successful precedent."

Kestrel went very still.

I watched her read it. I watched the lamplight move on her face as her eyes tracked the lines, and I watched the exact moment — a fractional tightening at the corner of her mouth, a stillness in her hands that was different from her usual stillness, colder — when the words stopped being about a stranger's operation and became about her own mountain, her own client, her own ruined name.

"Read it aloud," Nena said softly. "Please. I want to hear it in the air."

So Kestrel read it. Her voice was level, which is how I knew what it was costing her. "'Re: Cordillera del Viento concession. Precedent review. The 2011 incident involving guide K. Villalobos demonstrates the viability of environmental attribution for operational security purposes. Weather-based fatality, client-side, no surviving witnesses with direct knowledge, guide discredited via licensing board channels. Recommend replication of methodology for current asset protection. Note: guide remains active in region. Monitor.'"

The words hung in the frozen air. The glacier ticked. Somewhere far above, a serac shed a block of itself with a sound like a door closing in an enormous house, and the concussion rolled down through the ice and set the seam shimmering, all those trapped colors shivering in the lamplight, beautiful and obscene, the wealth that had bought four deaths and a woman's life laid out glowing in the dark like treasure in a tomb.

"Monitor," Kestrel said again. Just the word. She said it the way you'd say the name of someone who owed you money.

"They've watched you for thirteen years," I said. "Your blacklisting wasn't the board acting independently. It was the second half of the same operation. They built your ruin the same week they built their cover story, and then they kept the file warm, because a discredited guide is an asset — you can be hired cheap, threatened easily, and blamed retroactively for anything that goes wrong on the ice." I looked at her, and I made myself say the rest of it plainly, because she deserved plainness and I had spent four months rationing it. "Sorel didn't come to you because you're the best guide in the range. She came to you because you're the best guide in the range *and* the perfect scapegoat. If you die up here, the file closes itself. Guide loses client in calving season. Tragic. Familiar. No inquiry required."

Nena had both hands pressed flat against her thighs, the way she did when she was keeping herself from shaking. "And if we bring this out," she said. "If we get it to someone. What happens?"

"What happens," I said, "is that fourteen billion dollars acquires witnesses. And the people who own that number have helicopters, radios, and a recovery unit currently reading Dario's transmissions somewhere below us on the moraine. This—" I gestured at the seam, at the notebooks, at the whole glittering indictment of it— "this is worth more than all of us together, and worth less than nothing to them unless it reaches daylight. Which means every meter between here and a working transmitter is the most expensive real estate on Earth, and they know it, and they are coming up this glacier to collect."

Kestrel rose from her knees. She stood in the colored light with the memo still open in her hand, and for a long moment she looked at the seam — at the vein itself, the dumb shining stone that had never asked to be worth anything, that had lain in the dark for a billion years minding its own business until men with satellites found it and started killing — and something in her face finished settling, the way scree settles after a tremor, into a new and permanent configuration.

"They used my name," she said quietly. "In a planning document. As a *methodology*." She folded the memo along its original crease and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where a person would keep a letter rather than evidence. "Elias. Show me the way out of here. And then show me the fastest line off this glacier that a helicopter can't follow."

I felt something loosen in my chest that had been clenched since autumn — not relief, nothing so gentle as relief, but the specific release of a man who has been carrying a secret alone in the cold and finally sets it down in front of someone strong enough to help lift it.

"There's a route," I said. "Off the north shoulder, through the seracs. It's never been done."

Kestrel was already moving toward the fixed line, crampons ringing on the ice, and she answered without turning around.

"Neither was surviving you," she said. "Get your rope. We're leaving."

Above us, faint through fifty meters of ancient ice, we heard it: the first distant thunder of the calving fields opening for the season, and underneath it, thinner, mechanical, unmistakable — a helicopter, coming up the valley.
