## 16. Sorel Closes the Net

The helicopter came at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the cloud deck — a gray brightening that turned the glacier from black to pewter. Kestrel heard it before she saw it, a sound like a giant insect worrying at the edge of the world, and her body knew it before her mind did. She'd spent too many years in places where the only engines were the ones you carried yourself. A rotor note carried differently than wind. Wind moved through the mountains; engines moved against them.

She was out of the tent with her boots half-laced, standing on the sastrugi-hard snow of Camp Four, when the shape resolved through the murk above the eastern col. It didn't cross over them. It held position two kilometers off, hovering at the lip of the cloud like something deciding whether to descend, and Kestrel understood the message in that restraint. It wasn't looking for them. It was letting them know it had found them.

*They're here.*

The thought arrived flat and cold, without surprise. For three days she had been reading the signs — the too-regular spacing of the aircraft passes, the radio static that came and went in patterns no storm produced, the way Dario's absence still sat in the camp like a tooth socket. She had stripped his share, let him walk down alone with a day's food and her contempt, and told herself the recovery unit would need time to reorganize around a man who'd lost his value as an informant. She had given herself until the calving season. The mountain, she'd thought, was the clock.

She'd been wrong about whose clock it was.

Elias emerged from the second tent, moving carefully on his healing frostbite, and stood beside her. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then: "That's not a survey bird."

"No."

"It's been up twice since midnight."

"You were awake."

"I'm always awake now." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "Kestrel. How long do we have?"

She watched the helicopter hang there, patient as a vulture, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing all night. Fuel for a bird at this altitude in cold air: maybe ninety minutes on station before it had to drop to a lower refuel point. Which meant it would land men somewhere below the icefall — the broad bench where the route flattened, where a light utility heli could touch down if the pilot was good and the wind cooperated. From there, on foot, climbing their own broken trail —

"Six hours," she said. "Maybe eight if the weather turns. It won't turn. Sorel will have picked the window."

"Sorel doesn't pick windows. Sorel buys them." Elias's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "There's a weather station contract. State meteorology. Every forecast for this range routes through an office in the capital. I found the paperwork in the third month, after I stopped trusting my own team's radio traffic."

Nena crawled out last, hair frozen into a dark helmet, and read their faces the way she'd learned to read slope angles — fast, total, no wasted motion. "How many?" she asked.

"Unknown," Kestrel said. "Enough."

* * *

They struck camp in silence, the practiced silence of people who had learned each other's rhythms over hard weeks — Elias rolling the rope in long, even coils despite his hands, Nena breaking down the stove and packing it still warm so the metal wouldn't crack. Kestrel stood apart with the map, and the map gave her nothing she wanted.

South lay the way they'd come. South was the recovery unit, walking up their boot prints like a road. West was the calving face, three kilometers of seracs leaning over the glacier like rotten teeth, and every hour the morning sun warmed them closer to collapse. East was the border ridge — four days of technical ground they hadn't scouted, carrying a man with damaged feet and a girl who'd never led a pitch in her life.

And north, above them, was the deposit. The crevasse where Elias had hidden his data cache, the seam of ore that glowed orange-white under a headlamp like the mountain's own secret heart. They'd gone up to see it two days ago because Elias needed to stand in front of it once more before he left it forever, and Kestrel had understood that need the way she understood the need to visit graves.

North was a dead end. North was where the ice went to die.

She folded the map. There was one more option, and she had been refusing to look at it directly for six days, the way you refuse to look at a cornice you must eventually cross.

"Kestrel." Nena's voice, low. "Radio."

The set sat in its foam cradle, and it was already speaking. Not static — a voice, clipped and calm, repeating on a loop, the cadence of a man reading terms he'd drafted himself and enjoyed drafting.

*"—Villalobos. This frequency is monitored continuously. You are instructed to surrender the person Elias Vant and any materials in his possession. Do this and your file is closed. Not expunged — closed, which in practical terms is better; expungement invites questions. Full exoneration. Reinstatement of certification. Payment of the original contract plus a completion bonus. You have my word, and you know I keep my word, because you've read what happens when I don't. Refuse, and at eighteen hundred hours today you, Vant, and the porter Nena Quispe are designated armed fugitives engaged in violent resistance. Every patrol, every post, every border crossing from here to the coast gets your names and photographs. There is no version of the mountains south of the icefall where you survive that list. Come down, Kestrel. You were always the reasonable one—"*

The loop restarted. *Villalobos. This frequency is monitored—*

Nena had gone very still, her mittened hands wrapped around the stove pot like it was the last warm thing in the world. "He knows my name," she said. "My full name. My mother's name."

"He's read your file," Kestrel said. "He reads everyone's file. It's how he prays."

"That's not — " Nena stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said *the porter Nena Quispe.* Like I'm inventory."

"To him everything is inventory." Kestrel reached over and switched off the radio, though she knew it changed nothing; the voice lived in the air whether the set listened or not. "Which means we have exactly as much worth as we refuse to have."

Elias had lowered himself onto a gear crate, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd learned to dread over the past week — the look of a man watching someone else arrive at a conclusion he'd reached long ago. "You heard the deadline. Eighteen hundred hours."

"I heard it."

"And you heard what comes after. Fugitive lists don't expire, Kestrel. I've lived under one for eight months. You stop being a person with a history and become a shape on a map that people are paid to erase. He's offering you a way back to being a person."

"He's offering me a leash and calling it a spine."

"Maybe." Elias rubbed his damaged fingertips together, a habit he'd developed, feeling for sensation that was slow to return. "I need to say this plainly, because you deserve it plainly. If you give me up, they'll take me alive if they can. Sorel wants testimony more than he wants a corpse — a corpse raises questions, a confession closes them. And they'll treat you as a cooperating witness. Nena walks away clean. That's the deal on the table. It's not nothing."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "It's not nothing. It's everything except the part that matters."

"And what's that?"

She looked north, toward the high basin where the crevasse waited with its glowing seam, and then west, toward the calving face, and felt the two directions pull against each other inside her chest like a rope coming taut.

"The truth," she said. "All of it, in public, attached to names. Not buried in a crevasse. Not sitting in a dead man's cache waiting for someone brave enough to climb down and get it. Sorel's whole machine runs on silence — on files nobody reads and accidents nobody investigates. The moment this story exists outside these mountains, his ultimatum becomes worthless. You can't list as fugitives the witnesses who already testified."

Elias was quiet for a moment. "The copies are safe. Nena carries them."

"I know what Nena carries." Kestrel turned to the girl, and Nena met her eyes with that steadiness that kept surprising her, that kept making her revise downward the number of years between them that mattered. "But copies in a pack are just paper until they reach a hand that can use them. And between us and that hand is the entire descent, a recovery unit, and a man with a helicopter."

"So we run east," Nena said. "Four days to the border ridge. We move fast, we move dark—"

"We move slow," Kestrel said. "We move slower than they think, in a direction they don't expect, and we make them come to us on ground where four of them against three of us means nothing, because the mountain evens the odds. We've been running since the empty camp. Running is how they herd you." She tapped the map, the western margin, where the contour lines stacked up like a wall of text. "Time to stop."

* * *

They climbed north through the morning, and Kestrel hated every meter of it, because north was backward and every mountaineer's body knew it. But the plan had a logic, and the logic was hers: the recovery unit would expect flight. Flight meant tracks leading away, and tracks were a gift to pursuers. So instead they would climb toward the calving face itself, traverse beneath it on the shelf Elias had used during his months of hiding, and reach the deep crevasse system where the glacier split into a labyrinth of blue-walled canyons — terrain where a helicopter was useless, where numbers meant nothing, where a single guide who'd spent twenty years learning how ice failed could hold three lives against a dozen men with rifles and a deadline.

It was a plan built on one assumption: that the ice would hold another day.

By noon the sun had cleared the cloud deck, and the calving face began to speak.

The first crack came as a sound Kestrel felt in her molars before she heard it — a deep, structural groan, like a ship changing its mind. Then a report, sharp as artillery, echoing off the cirque walls, followed by the long rumble of something enormous relocating itself at the base of the face. Snow dust bloomed from the glacier's foot, hanging in the still air like smoke after a battle.

"Calving season," Elias said quietly. "Early this year."

"Everything's early this year." Kestrel counted the interval between cracks, an old habit, a guide's habit — five minutes, then four, then three. The face was warming faster than the air suggested. Seracs that had leaned in place for decades were deciding, all at once, that they were tired.

They reached the shelf by early afternoon: a rib of old ice along the face's flank, sheltered from the direct fall line, wide enough for one careful footstep at a time. Below them the glacier spilled away in a thousand broken shapes, and far down-valley — a kilometer, maybe more — Kestrel saw them.

The recovery unit. Six figures in matching shells, roped in two teams, ascending the route she'd broken. Even at that distance she recognized the geometry of them: the efficient rest-step, the disciplined spacing, professionals moving on a schedule. And behind them, at the bench where the helicopter had landed that morning, the aircraft sat on the snow with its blades still, a black insect mid-meal, two more figures beside it working a tripod-mounted antenna.

"They're close," Nena breathed.

"They're slow," Kestrel corrected. "Look at their spacing on the icefall pitches. They're moving like men who've been told the quarry is trapped. Nobody hurries toward a trap." She raised her binoculars. Through the glass she picked out details that made her jaw tighten: carbines slung across backs, a radio operator checking a mast, and at the head of the lead rope a figure she knew by posture alone, broad-shouldered, unhurried, turning once to scan the upper glacier.

Sorel hadn't sent soldiers. Sorel had come himself.

"He never could resist closing a file personally," Elias said, when she passed him the glasses. He studied the distant figure for a long moment. "Eight months I ran from his name. It's strange. Seeing him as a man in a red shell suit, doing a rest-step on my escape route. He looks small."

"He's not small." Kestrel took the glasses back. "Men like that are never small. They're just far away until they aren't."

As if the words had been an invocation, the radio in her pack crackled. Not the loop this time. A live voice, and she knew it instantly — that measured baritone, warm as a handshake, cold as a ledger.

"Kestrel. I'll assume you're listening, since you haven't surrendered, and you've never in your life done the stupid thing twice." A pause, almost companionable. "You're on the shelf below the west face. Good position. I taught routes like that myself, once. Before I learned that mountains are just real estate with weather."

She unclipped the set and keyed it. Her voice came out level, and she was grateful for that. "You're a long way from a desk, Sorel."

"I go where the work is. You know that better than anyone — you wrote the book on going where the work is, right up until the work went wrong and you needed someone to blame." The warmth thinned. "Here's the final version, and then I stop asking. Eighteen hundred hours. Bring Vant down to the bench, hand over the data, and you three walk out of these mountains with more money than you've seen in your life and names that open doors instead of closing them. Miss the deadline, and I stop treating this as a retrieval and start treating it as a containment problem. Containment has different tools. You've seen some of them. You just didn't know what you were seeing."

*The client,* Kestrel thought. *Tomas Reyes, falling into a crevasse on a route you'd flagged clear. The accident you buried to protect whatever you were moving through that valley that summer.* Rage rose in her, old and familiar, but it came up different now — cooled, forged, pointed. "I know exactly what I was seeing," she said. "I just didn't know it yet. That's the difference between us, Sorel. You've always known, and you chose it anyway."

A silence on the line, long enough to be interesting.

"You've been talking to Vant," he said finally, and there was something new in his voice — not doubt, but the careful recalibration of a man discovering his model of the world needs revision. "It doesn't matter what he told you. Stories don't survive contact with institutions. Data does, and the data is in a hole in the ice, and in eighteen hours there won't be a hole, because I've authorized a seismic charge package for the deposit site. Containment, Kestrel. The mountain keeps its secrets. I just help it."

The line clicked to standby. Above them, the calving face answered him — a crack like the world's largest door unlocking, and then a serac the size of a cathedral leaning out of the face and coming apart in the air, its debris fanning across the glacier a kilometer downslope, the thunder rolling on and on while the dust plume climbed white against the blue.

When the echo died, Nena spoke first, her voice thin but steady. "He'll really do it. Blow the crevasse."

"He'll really do it." Kestrel looked at Elias, and found him already looking at her, and in his face she saw the thing she'd been circling for days finally land: the cache, the data, the proof — everything they'd planned to retrieve, everything the whole desperate chess game had been about — now had a fuse burning under it.

"How long to reach the deposit site from here?" Elias asked.

"Three hours, moving hard. Less if the shelf holds." She glanced at the sky, measuring the light. "Less if the face gives us those three hours."

"And the recovery unit?"

"Four hours behind us, and gaining once they realize where we've gone." She shouldered her pack and felt the weight settle into the old familiar groove across her shoulders, heavier now with everything it carried besides gear. "So we stop being clever and start being fast. We get the data before Sorel's charges do, and then—" She stopped. Looked west, where the glacier bent around the shoulder of the mountain toward a country of cliffs and couloirs that appeared on no permitted map, the descent route with a name the locals wouldn't say aloud and a body count she'd contributed to. The route she'd sworn, on Tomas Reyes's grave, never to touch again.

Elias followed her gaze. He'd heard the stories too; everyone in these mountains had. "No," he said softly. "Not that."

"Not yet," Kestrel said. "First the crevasse. First the truth in our hands instead of his crosshairs." She clipped into the rope and nodded Nena forward onto the shelf, one careful footstep at a time, the calving face breathing its thunder beside them like a sleeping animal dreaming of waking. "Then we'll talk about the last passage."

Behind them, far below, the radio antenna at the bench caught the low sun and flashed once, like an eye opening.
