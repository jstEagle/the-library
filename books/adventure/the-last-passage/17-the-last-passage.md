## 17. The Last Passage

The helicopter's drone faded northward around four in the afternoon, and in its absence the glacier made its own argument: a deep report from the calving face, miles below, like a door slamming in an empty house.

Kestrel lay on her stomach at the lip of the moraine ridge, binoculars pressed to her eyes, and counted the recovery unit again. Six figures on the ice below, roped in two teams, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once wondered whether they were allowed to be where they were. Behind them, at the edge of the whiteout's memory, she could see the orange smudge of their advanced camp. They had radios, satellite uplink, probably thermal imaging by nightfall. They had Sorel's voice in their ears.

She had three people, one working stove, and Elias's satchel of evidence strapped to her chest like a child.

"They'll reach the serac field by tomorrow midday," Elias said beside her. He spoke without drama, the way he'd reported survey data for eleven days, as if numbers could hold the world together. "Maybe sooner if they push through the night. They have lights."

"And we can't outrun them on the glacier. Too open. They'd have us in the scope before dark."

Nena crouched behind them, saying nothing, watching Kestrel's face the way she'd learned to watch the mountain — reading the small movements, the tells. Dario was gone, sent down alone with his stripped share and his family and his shame, and his absence had left a hole shaped exactly like the question nobody wanted to ask: *what now?*

Kestrel lowered the binoculars and looked east, across the glacier's grinding spine, to where the range gathered itself into the wall they called La Pared del Viento. And there it was, exactly where twelve years had failed to erase it: the notch. A black diagonal slash in the east face, running from the col at six thousand meters down two thousand vertical meters of ice and rotten rock to the valley floor. From this distance it looked almost gentle. Almost passable.

The Garganta. The Throat.

Every guide in the Cordillera knew the story. In '09 a pair of Argentine climbers had gone in and only one had come out, half-dead, raving about ice that moved like water. In '94 a French team had turned back at the upper narrows after their lead man fell four hundred feet into a crevasse no rope could arrest. And in the season Kestrel still measured all other seasons against, a client named Daniel Ossa had paid her outfit an obscene sum to attempt it, because Daniel Ossa collected first descents the way other men collected watches, and because Kestrel — young, hungry, certain of her own infallibility — had said yes.

They had gotten to the lower narrows. The icefall there did not calve so much as exhale, whole towers of it leaning against each other in the throat of the couloir, held up by friction and arrogance. Something shifted. Not an avalanche, not a collapse — just the mountain adjusting its weight, the way a sleeper turns over. A serac the size of a church leaned two degrees further than it had leaned that morning.

Daniel Ossa had been standing where the guidebook said to stand. Kestrel had been ten meters ahead, breaking trail, when the world came down between them.

She had dug for nine hours with her hands. The inquiry found no fault in her technique, her judgment, her anchors. The agency investigating on behalf of Ossa's family — the same ministry, she would learn years too late, that had been running a mineral survey in those very mountains — found something more useful than fault. They found a scapegoat who could be blamed quietly, a file that could be thickened, a career that could be ended with a signature instead of a trial. *Negligence unproven but suspected. License suspended pending review.* Pending forever.

She had spent twelve years telling herself she avoided the Garganta out of respect for the dead.

She knew better now. She'd avoided it because it was the place where she had stopped being the person she thought she was, and you do not walk back into your own grave to admire the view.

"You're looking at it," Nena said quietly. Not a question.

"I'm looking at it."

"The Throat is a myth. My uncle says—"

"Your uncle is right. It's a myth. Nobody survives it." Kestrel stood, knees cracking in the cold, and brushed the snow from her jacket. "Which is why they won't be watching it."

Elias followed her gaze, and Kestrel watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she was doing, but arriving from the other direction. He didn't know the route. He knew the mountain as data: gradients, crevasse maps, the seismic behavior of the icefield. He knew what the Garganta cost statistically.

"That's a two-thousand-meter descent through active icefall," he said. "In what, four days of food?"

"Three."

"Three days of food, into a couloir that has killed everyone who entered it."

"Not everyone." Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using her mouth. "One person survived it. She was ten meters ahead when it came down, and she walked out the bottom, and she has spent twelve years being able to close her eyes and see every meter of it. Every serac lean angle. Every rock step. Every place the ice bridges over nothing." She turned to face them both. "I've climbed this route in my head every night since I was twenty-six years old. I know it better than any living person knows any route on earth, because I'm the only living person who's been inside it and come back."

The wind picked at them, worrying the ropes, the tents, the edges of everything.

"Sorel will expect us to go south," Kestrel went on. "Down-valley, toward the border posts, where the terrain favors runners. That's what the file says about me — that I run. That's what she built her whole plan around. So we don't run south. We go east, straight through the wall they think is impassable, and we come out in the Quebrada Honda on the far side, forty kilometers from anywhere, and by the time anyone thinks to look there, we're ghosts."

"It's suicide," Elias said.

"It's the only door."

He looked at her for a long moment. There was frost in the stubble of his beard and exhaustion carved under his eyes, and still she saw it happen — the moment the scientist in him yielded to something older and less reasonable. Trust, maybe. Or the recognition that some knowledge can't be audited, only believed.

"Okay," he said. "Show me the line."

* * *

They spent the last hour of light at the entrance of the couloir, and Kestrel showed them the mountain the way she'd shown no one in twelve years — not the postcard version, not the guidebook version, but the true one, the one that lived behind her eyelids.

"The upper narrows are climbable if we move before dawn, while everything's frozen solid. Two hours of climbing, no stopping, no talking except rope commands. After nine the sun hits the east wall and the rockfall starts — you'll hear it like applause. We need to be through the narrows and onto the middle shelf before the applause begins."

She traced the line downward with her mitten. "Then the shelf, which is safe, which is a lie, because it lulls you. Then the lower icefall — the Throat proper. That's where Daniel died. That's where the mountain breathes. The seracs down there aren't stable and they aren't unstable; they're *deciding*. You cross during the cold hours, fast, in single file, and you do not stop for anything. Not a dropped glove. Not a scream. Nothing."

"And if something shifts?" Nena asked.

Kestrel met the girl's eyes. Nena was twenty-three and had carried sixty pounds over passes that would have broken porters twice her age, and she had earned the truth. "If something shifts, you pray it isn't your shift. That's the whole technique. Speed and luck. I can give you the speed. The luck you bring yourself."

Nena nodded slowly. Then, in the small voice she used when she was about to be braver than everyone: "Daniel Ossa. Was he the one? The client? The reason they took your license?"

The wind filled the silence.

"Yes," Kestrel said.

"And you're taking us back in."

"I'm taking you back in."

Nena looked at the notch in the wall, black against the bruised evening sky, and then back at Kestrel, and whatever she saw there seemed to settle something for her. "Good," she said. "Then let's make it mean something."

* * *

They descended to the couloir's mouth as the light died, moving carefully through the boulder field, and made cold camp beneath an overhang that smelled of stone and ancient ice. No stove — flame discipline, no glow to betray them to the recovery unit's night optics. They ate chocolate and dried meat and drank water melted against their stomachs, and Kestrel checked and rechecked the racks on each rope until her fingers ached.

Elias sat close beside her in the dark, shoulder to shoulder, sharing warmth the way the mountain permitted — practically, without ceremony, though neither of them moved away.

"When we're through," he said quietly, "what happens to you? After."

"After?"

"You burn Sorel. You burn the contract. You cross a border with a man whose name is on a kill list. There's no guiding outfit at the end of that road, Kestrel. There's no file to clear. There's just... after."

She considered it. Twelve years she had organized her life around a debt — the suspended license, the name she couldn't use on permits, the seasons counted instead of birthdays because counting seasons meant she wasn't counting how long she'd been waiting for permission to exist. Sorel had offered her that permission back, priced at Elias's life. Tomorrow she would spend the last of everything she had refusing it.

"Then I'll finally have to find out who I am when nobody's holding my file," she said. "Terrifying."

His laugh was soft, barely a breath. His hand found hers in the dark, gloved fingers clumsy together, and held on.

"Get some sleep," she said. "We move at three."

* * *

At 2:40 she woke without the alarm she hadn't set, the way she always did before hard days, her body keeping its own counsel. The sky was a ceiling of stars cut by the black wall of the couloir. The temperature read minus nineteen on the little gauge clipped inside her jacket. Perfect. Frozen. The mountain at its most honest.

She woke them with a hand on each shoulder, and they rose without words, and by three-ten they stood at the base of the upper narrows, headlamps off, letting their eyes adjust to starlight, breathing steam into the vast silence.

Kestrel looked up the line one last time. Somewhere above, in the dark, the ice waited in its old positions — the leaning towers, the hanging blocks, the whole architecture of her worst memory, preserved by cold like a photograph. Twelve years ago she had left a piece of herself in there, buried under a serac beside a man who paid too much and trusted too completely. Everyone had told her she'd never go back.

"Rope commands only," she whispered. "Nena, you're on my hip. Elias behind her. If I stop, you stop. If I say *run*, you run and you don't look back, not for me, not for anything. Understood?"

Two whispers: *Understood.*

She clipped into the first pitch, tested the ice with one swing, and felt the pick bite deep and clean into rock-hard blue glass. Good ice. Honest ice. For now.

Above her the Garganta opened its throat, and Kestrel Villalobos — disgraced, blacklisted, burned, free — began to climb into the passage that had killed her old life, betting everything she had left on the one map no one else alive had ever read.

Behind them, far below on the glacier, a radio crackled faintly on the night air. The recovery unit was moving early.

She climbed faster.
