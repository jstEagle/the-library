## 18. Whiteout

The mountain had been talking all morning, and by noon it was screaming.

It started as a sound I felt before I heard — a deep concussion rolling up through the soles of my boots, through the rope, through the bones of my hand where I gripped the ice axe. Somewhere below and to the east, a wall of glacier the size of a cathedral had let go. The calving season wasn't coming anymore. It was here.

"Keep moving," I said, and hated how thin my voice sounded against the wind.

We were four hours onto the upper glacier, past the calving line, past the point where Dario would have turned back if he'd still been with us. Three of us now: me on point, Nena in the middle, Elias at the rear with the survey data sealed in a waterproof sleeve against his chest like a second heart. The route I'd chosen — the last passage, the one that killed Tomás Reyes eleven years ago — threaded between two walls of rotten seracs that any sane guide would have given a wide berth in June. It was October. The sane window had closed weeks ago. We were climbing on borrowed time and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because saying it would have made it true.

The wind came off the summit plateau in long horizontal sheets, carrying ice crystals that hit like sand. Visibility had been shrinking all morning — first the far peaks went, then the near ones, then the horizon itself dissolved until the world was a gray bowl twenty meters across, with our three bodies at the center of it like flies in amber.

I'd seen whiteouts before. I'd survived them, led clients through them, taught younger guides how to navigate them by compass bearing and count-of-steps and faith. But this one had teeth. This one came with the sound of the glacier breaking apart beneath us, and every few minutes another report rolled through the ice — *crack, boom, rumble* — each one closer than the last, each one rearranging the map we were standing on.

"Bearing?" Nena shouted. She had to put her mouth an inch from my ear. Her voice had changed since Camp Three, since Dario stood in the snow and confessed what he'd done. There was no idolatry left in it. What remained was harder and better: a woman who knew exactly who she was roped to.

"Two-sixty," I shouted back. "Fifty-meter legs. Count your steps."

She nodded and began counting aloud, her voice a metronome against the wind. Fifty steps. Stop. Check the bearing. Fifty more. It was the oldest trick in mountaineering and it worked because it gave the mind something small to hold while everything large tried to kill it.

Elias counted too. I could hear him behind me, his voice rougher than hers, half-lost in the wind. He hadn't slept properly in nine days — not since the melt cave, maybe not since the crevasse, since he'd looked down into the earth and seen a seam of ore glowing like a wound and understood what it would cost him. But he kept counting. That was the thing about Elias I was still learning: he didn't have a mountaineer's body or a mountaineer's instincts, but he had something rarer. He refused, absolutely and without drama, to stop.

*Crack.*

The sound came from directly above us this time, sharp as a rifle shot, followed by a groan that traveled down through the glacier like a note played on a stringed instrument the size of a country. I stopped. Held up my fist. Nena froze mid-count — forty-one, forty-two — and Elias's voice cut off behind her.

Above and to our right, maybe eighty meters up the slope, a serac the size of a house leaned out over the route. I couldn't see it. Nobody could see anything. But I could feel it, the way you feel a stranger watching you from a dark doorway, and every instinct I owned was screaming the same word.

*Move.*

"Off the line!" I roared. "Downslope, thirty meters, go!"

We plunged left, off the packed track, into knee-deep sugar snow that hid God knew what underneath. I went first, probing with my axe, driving it in and leaning my weight on it before committing each step. Behind me the rope paid out — ten meters, fifteen, twenty —

The mountain let go.

There was no crack this time, no warning groan. Just a sound like the sky tearing, and then the wind changed direction, sucked backward toward the slope for one impossible heartbeat, and I threw myself flat and buried my face in my arms as the world exploded.

The serac didn't fall so much as detonate. Tons of blue ice hit the glacier below it and shattered into a moving ocean of blocks and powder that poured across the route we'd just abandoned. The shockwave hit me as a physical weight, pressing me into the snow, filling my hood, my collar, my mouth. I heard Nena scream something — my name, or a prayer, or both — and then the roar swallowed everything, and the world was nothing but white violence and the thunder of a mountain eating its own face.

It lasted maybe forty seconds. It lasted a year.

When the roar finally guttered into silence, I lifted my head out of the snow and spat ice and listened. Wind. My own heartbeat. And under it, faint and wrong, the sound of rope running taut somewhere behind me.

"Nena!" Nothing. "NENA! ELIAS!"

I rolled over and hauled myself upright, following the rope backward through waist-deep debris. Twenty meters back I found the problem: a slab of serac ice the size of a truck door, half-buried, its edge razor-sharp where it had sheared against another block. The rope ran over that edge, and the edge had bitten through the sheath. Not cut — not yet — but frayed down to a fistful of white filaments, trembling in the wind like something alive.

And beyond it, nothing. No Nena. No Elias. Just the churned white sea of avalanche debris, already filling in with new snow, erasing everything.

My hands were moving before my mind caught up. Axe out, chop a stance, anchor in — a deadman buried in the debris, buried again, stomped down with both boots. I clipped in, took the rope over my shoulder, and pulled. It came. Slack, then resistance, then slack again. They were still attached — the rope proved that much — but they weren't where I'd left them, and they weren't answering, and the wind was pouring fresh snow into every depression faster than I could read it.

Then I saw it. A single line in the chaos, unmistakable to anyone who'd spent her life reading snow the way other people read letters: a slide track. Where the avalanche debris had poured downslope, it had split around a buried rib of bedrock ice, dividing into two branches — one angling west, toward the lower glacier and the route home, and one bending north, uphill, toward the base of the icefall where the terrain funneled into a maze of crevasses no sane person would enter in a whiteout.

The rope tension told me which branch they'd taken. North. Uphill. Wrong.

They'd gone the wrong way. In zero visibility, deafened, disoriented — of course they had. Every study, every accident report, every grim case history I'd ever read confirmed it: in a whiteout, disoriented people walk uphill. They lean into the slope without knowing it. The body seeks the angle of least resistance and the mind calls it level ground.

Every rule I lived by assembled itself in my head with terrible clarity. *Never leave your anchor. Never follow victims into terrain that killed them once already. One guide dead is a tragedy; two is a pattern.* I could see Sorel's voice saying it, could see the incident reports, the inquiries, the careful bureaucratic language about judgment and risk. I could see Tomás Reyes's face, eleven years old in my memory now, frozen mid-laugh on a slope exactly like this one, on a route exactly like this one, because I had gone back when every rule said stay.

Going after them meant unclipping from my anchor. It meant leaving the one fixed point in a featureless white world and walking blind into avalanche terrain that was, even now, still settling, still deciding whether to move again. It meant betting everything — the mission, the evidence in Elias's jacket, my own life, and whatever strange unfinished thing had grown between Elias and me on the glacier above the calving line — on my ability to find two people in a white room with no walls.

Staying meant surviving. Staying meant the rules. Staying meant standing at this anchor until the storm ended or the recovery unit found me, alone, having done the arithmetic correctly, having lost everything that mattered while keeping my professional reputation intact.

I thought about Tomás. Not the death — I'd carried the death so long it had worn smooth, worn weightless, worn into something I could lift with one hand. I thought about the laugh. About why I'd gone back for him, that day, against every rule. Not because the odds were good. They were terrible. I'd gone back because some things are not decisions. Some things are simply what you are, revealed at last under pressure, the way ore shows in a cut face of ice.

I unclipped.

For one moment I stood free on the debris field with no anchor, no rope, no fixed point anywhere in the universe, and the wind pushed at me like a hand testing a door. Then I coiled the damaged rope fast, shouldered it, and turned north, uphill, into the wrong branch, into the white.

* * *

I found their trail by luck and by craft, and I've never known how much of each.

The avalanche debris was settling as it cooled, and where it settled it cracked — hairline fractures in the surface, opening and closing like breathing. Their passage had disturbed the settling. Two lines of broken crust, side by side, wandering drunk-fashion uphill. I followed them the way I'd followed tracks my whole life: not looking at the snow but at the light on the snow, at the micro-shadow of each heel print, at the way wind-sluff chose to fill one mark and not another.

The cracks in the ice were getting louder. Every few minutes the whole glacier spoke — a groan, a snap, a long descending moan like a whale dying somewhere beneath us — and each time I stopped and counted the seconds, listening for the echo that would tell me how close the next collapse would be. The answers were getting shorter. The icefall above us was loading up, shedding weight, and we were inside its throat.

"FIRMIRIN!" I screamed it until my throat tore. "NENA! ANSWER ME!"

Wind. Snow. And then — so faint I might have invented it — a whistle. Three short blasts. The international signal for distress, drilled into every porter and guide on the range, and Nena had blown it, Nena was alive, Nena was *north*, and the sound was coming from my left, from the lip of something.

I reached the edge and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

A crevasse. Not a crack — a chasm, eight meters wide, its lips overhung with snow bridges that the avalanche had cracked like eggshells. The visibility ended two meters below the rim; beyond that was blue-black nothing, depth without bottom, and hanging in that nothing, thirty meters down, a single point of color.

Red. Nena's parka. She was on a snow bridge — a shelf of compacted snow spanning the crevasse's throat — and she was braced against its edge with her axe, and below her, visible only as a darker shape against the dark, was Elias. He'd gone through a thinner section. He was hanging on the rope between them, swinging slowly over a depth I couldn't measure, and the bridge under Nena was sagging, compressing, shedding fine streams of snow into the abyss with every one of his movements.

"Kestrel!" Her voice floated up, stripped of everything but function. "The bridge is failing! I can't hold him and climb out — the anchor won't take us both!"

She'd done everything right. Of course she had. She'd arrested his fall, built what anchor she could with frozen fingers, gotten herself onto the thickest part of the bridge. She'd done everything right and it wasn't enough, because the mountain doesn't reward correctness, it only occasionally forgives it.

"I'm coming down!" I shouted.

"On what?!" And there it was — not panic, not accusation. A real question, asked by a real mountaineer. On what? I had no anchor. I'd burned my anchor to come after them. I had a coil of half-damaged rope and thirty years of knowledge and nothing else.

I looked at the crevasse lip. Overhanging, unstable, crowned with shattered bridge remnants. Any anchor I placed on that lip would fail with the first load. Unless —

Unless I didn't place one at all.

There is a technique so old it predates equipment, a technique from the era when men climbed in hobnail boots and hemp ropes: the body belay. You sit at the edge, wrap the rope around yourself, and become the anchor — your mass, your friction, your willingness to be dragged to the brink and over it. It fails unless the person holding it refuses, absolutely, to be moved. It is not a technique. It is a statement.

I backed up to a buried block of serac ice near the lip, sat, wrapped the rope twice around my hips and once over my shoulder, and tied the end to my harness anyway, because I am reckless, not stupid. Then I leaned back and took the strain.

"Elias!" I bellowed downward. "On my count, climb! Hand over hand, the rope is live, MOVE!"

He moved. I felt him through the rope like a pulse — the judder of his boots on the wall, the surge of his weight as he hauled himself up. Each surge dragged me forward across the ice, my boots plowing furrows, my hip screaming against the wrap, the crevasse lip sliding closer meter by meter. Nena held her position on the bridge, feeding slack, praying aloud in a language I didn't recognize and didn't need to.

Five meters below the rim, Elias's headlamp appeared — a pale coin in the murk. Four meters. His glove reached the lip and found nothing to grip, only crumbling snow. Three meters. My heels were at the edge now. I could feel the lip beginning to fail under my own weight, snow crumbling away beneath my boots into the dark, and I wrapped the rope one more turn around my forearm and let it bite flesh and held.

"TAKE MY HAND!" Nena was stretched flat on the bridge, arm extended down into the void, and Elias surged upward one final time and their gloves met — slapped, slipped, caught — wrist over wrist, the oldest grip there is, older than carabiners, older than rope.

I hauled. Nena hauled. The bridge groaned, sagged, dropped ten centimeters, held. And Elias came over the lip like a landed fish, rolling onto solid ice, gasping, alive, his face a mask of frost and blood where the crevasse wall had chewed him on the way up.

For three seconds none of us moved. The wind filled the silence with snow.

Then the glacier spoke again — the loudest yet, a boom that came from everywhere at once, from the ice under our knees, from the air itself — and the snow bridge Nena lay on shivered like a struck drumhead and began, visibly, to sink.

"OFF THE BRIDGE! NOW!"

Nena scrambled. Elias grabbed her pack and dragged. I hauled the rope. She came off the collapsing span as it slumped into the crevasse behind her, dissolving into powder, and the four of us — three of us, plus the ghost of Tomás Reyes, who had been present the entire time — collapsed in a heap on the glacier's skin and breathed.

That was when I saw it. Through the murk, upslope, perhaps two hundred meters: a shape. Dark, vertical, wrong. Not ice. Not rock.

A tent. Orange fabric snapping in the wind, guy-lines humming.

The recovery unit's advance camp. Sorel's people. They were already above the calving line, already on the glacier, closer than any of us had believed possible — and between us and the pass, planted squarely on the only viable route, their tents glowed through the whiteout like a lit doorway in a burning house.

Elias saw it too. I watched the understanding cross his ruined face, and beside me Nena's breath went ragged, and somewhere beneath our feet the glacier gathered itself for the next blow.

We had minutes, not hours. And the only way out was through them.
