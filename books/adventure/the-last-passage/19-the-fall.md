## 19. The Fall

I have spent eleven days learning to read Kestrel Villalobos the way I once read survey data — patiently, obsessively, looking for the flaw that would tell me what everything else was worth. And what I learned, somewhere between the icefall and the whiteout, is that there is no flaw. There is only a woman who has made a discipline out of surviving her own grief, and who moves through the high ice like someone settling an old debt with the mountain, line by line, meter by meter.

I was behind her on the rope when the bridge failed. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger that will never exist: I was behind her, which means the fall was hers to catch, and she caught it, and it cost her everything she had left.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Elias Reyes has always gotten ahead of himself. It's how a geologist ends up running from his own government across a glacier at the top of the world.

* * *

The storm had broken an hour before dawn, and what it left behind was a glacier remade — a white country with no borders, no horizon, no memory. We moved by compass bearing and by Kestrel's instinct, which I had come to trust more than the compass. Nena led the second rope with Dario, who had been silent since Camp Three in the way of a man carrying something heavy that isn't gear. Kestrel and I took the front rope, tied together at twelve feet, close enough that I could watch her breath plume and vanish, plume and vanish, a metronome counting down something I didn't want to name.

We were crossing a field of snow bridges — the glacier's lies, the places where a crevasse hides under a lid of drifted snow and pretends to be ground. Kestrel read them the way I imagine a blind man reads a room: by weight, by sound, by the faint hollow note her boot made on the crust. She probed each one with her axe before committing her weight. She made me do the same. She had been making me do the same for two days, patiently, without contempt, and I had begun to understand that this patience was the largest act of faith she was capable of.

"The passage is close," she said. Her voice was raw from the storm. "Below the serac band there's a ramp. If it's still there."

"If?"

"Glaciers don't keep promises." She glanced back at me, and even wind-burned and gray-lipped, there was something in her face that I had no word for then and still don't, a look that was equal parts warning and invitation. "Neither do people. But we try."

I wanted to tell her — I had been trying to tell her for two days, in the fragments the storm allowed — what the deposit meant, what I'd seen in the blue throat of that crevasse, the seam of ore running through the ice like a vein in a wrist, glowing under my headlamp with the dull gold of money and death. I'd spent six weeks running from it. Now I was going back toward it, because she had decided the data had to come out, and because — this is the part I keep circling, the part I can't file under strategy — because she had decided, and I had discovered that her deciding was something I would follow into anything.

"Kestrel." I said it against the wind. "If we get down—"

"Save it." No cruelty in it. Just economy. She was a woman who spent words the way she spent fuel: only on what would keep her alive. "Talk at the bottom."

There was no bottom. That's the thing about the Cordillera del Viento. There is always another fall beneath the fall.

* * *

The bridge gave way at 9:40 in the morning, though I only know the time because Nena called it out afterward, her voice shaking, because someone had to know when the world broke.

It was my weight that did it. I want that recorded too.

Kestrel had crossed, had probed, had waved me forward. I stepped where she had stepped. But I was carrying the data case — the survey drives, the field books, the photographs, six weeks of proof that the state had found a treasure and decided to murder its finders — and I was carrying it high on my back like a fool, like a man who thinks the weight he's used to is the weight he'll always carry. My extra kilos came down on the crust a half-second after hers, at the exact center of the span, and the snow made a sound I will hear until I die: not a crack, nothing so honest as a crack. A sigh. The sound of something that had held as long as it could.

Then there was no ground.

I went in arms first, the case swinging, and the world became a blue throat. I remember the color — that impossible, luminous, submarine blue that lives inside glaciers, the color of the crevasse where my team found the deposit and where my team, one by one, stopped existing. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like laughter: *of course. Of course it's this color again. The mountain remembers what it's holding for me.*

The rope snapped tight.

The arrest was not graceful. It was violence — pure, mechanical, the physics of a small woman and a long rope and a falling man meeting in the space of a meter. The harness bit into my ribs and my sternum and I stopped, swinging, spinning, the case slamming against the ice wall, and above me, at the lip, I heard Kestrel make a sound I had never heard her make. Not a scream. A word. One word, torn out of her:

"*No.*"

Not *no* to me. I understood that later. *No* to the mountain, to the fall, to the whole arithmetic of loss that had been running her life for six years. She was refusing it. Out loud. To the glacier itself.

I hung there turning slowly, thirty meters down in a blue cathedral, and I heard her moving — the deliberate, terrible sounds of a body anchor going in. She had thrown herself flat, driven her axes, kicked her boots into the crust, and turned herself into a deadman. I could hear her breathing through it, ragged and controlled at once, the breathing of someone who has done this before and knows that the next ten minutes are the whole of everything.

"Elias." Her voice came down the shaft, flattened by the ice, echoing strangely. "Are you whole?"

I inventoried myself. Ribs screaming. Wrist wrenched. "Whole enough."

"The case?"

"Intact."

A pause. Then, dry as the wind: "Of course it is. It's the only thing on this mountain that's had an easy day." And beneath the dryness, something else — relief so large it had to disguise itself as a joke or it would have undone her.

"I'm going to prusik up," I called. "Give me ten minutes."

"Take twelve. Don't rush. Rushing is how people die on rope." A beat. "I'll be here."

She was. That's the thing I need the record to show, whoever finds this, whoever reads it in whatever world comes after Sorel's world. She was there, holding the whole weight of me on her body, for forty minutes, because my prusiks iced and my wrenched wrist failed twice and I slid back twice, each slide jerking the rope and jerking her, and each time I heard her take it — a grunt, a scrape of axes, the sound of a woman absorbing a man's failure with her skeleton.

When I finally rolled over the lip, gasping into the snow, I looked up at her and I saw it.

Her left leg was wrong.

She was still in the anchor position, axes planted, and she was looking down at her own lower leg with the expression of a surveyor noting an anomaly — clinical, distant, already calculating. The boot was angled a way boots don't go. Below the knee, something had shifted that shouldn't shift. The rope had caught her across the thigh when I hit the end of it, and the ice beneath her had done the rest. I could see, even before she said it, that she knew. She had known since the moment she'd stopped my fall, and she had said nothing, because I was on the rope and her job — her whole self, her whole rebuilt, ruined, remade self — was to get the people on her rope to the bottom.

"Kestrel—"

"Shh." She was already reaching for the splint in her pack, already working, hands steady, face white as the glacier. "Nena. Dario. Get over here. Nobody else crosses this bridge. Nobody."

* * *

We got her off the lip and into the lee of a serac, and I watched Nena's face do what mine was doing — the quick horror, the swallowed scream, the terrible arithmetic of a young person watching competence break. Nena had idolized Kestrel for three weeks without knowing her history, and now she was splinting her hero's leg with hands that shook, and Kestrel was coaching her through it.

"Wrap tighter. Tighter. Pain is information, Nena, not damage. If I yell, that's the bone talking. Keep going."

"You're so calm," Nena whispered, and her voice cracked on it.

"I'm not calm. I'm furious." Kestrel's eyes came up and met mine over the splint, and what was in them wasn't fear. It was a decision, already made, being handed to me like a rope end. "The ramp below the serac band. It's the only way down. I can't walk it."

Dario said, from the edge of the shelter, "Then we wait for the recovery unit. They'll have a stretcher. They'll have—"

"They'll have orders," Kestrel said. "You know what their orders are. All of us are cleaner dead than talked to." She said it without looking at him, and I watched him flinch, and I understood that the mercy she'd shown him at Camp Three was still being paid for, by both of them, in different currencies. "The storm bought us hours. The calving will buy us more — nobody flies a helicopter into falling ice. We go down the ramp now, or we don't go down at all."

"And you?" Nena's voice was very small.

Kestrel looked at her leg. Then at me. And for the first time since I'd met her — since the empty camp, since the standoff in the melt cave where she'd had me dead to rights and chosen questions over bullets — she let me see the whole of it: the fear, the fury, the exhaustion, and underneath all of it, a question she was too proud to ask.

So I answered it before she had to.

"I'll carry you."

* * *

She fought me for exactly four minutes. I counted, because fighting her was a thing I'd learned to do carefully, the way you handle a loaded tool.

"You can't. Sixty kilos plus gear plus that slope—"

"I've been carrying sixty kilos of data across this glacier for six weeks. You're lighter than the truth, Kestrel. And the truth is coming down with us either way."

"That's not—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. The wind filled the pause. "I don't get carried. I'm the one who carries. That's the whole — that's what I *am*—"

I crouched in front of her, splinted leg stretched out, this woman who had caught my fall with her body and then apologized for breaking. I took her face in my hands, mittens and all, and I said the thing I'd been carrying since the melt cave, since before the melt cave, since a crevasse of impossible blue where I'd watched my team die for a number in a ledger:

"I spent six weeks running from everyone. From Sorel, from my team, from the whole machine. I got very good at leaving people behind — it's the one skill the state ever actually taught me." My voice was not steady. I let it not be steady. "I am not leaving you on this mountain. Not for the data. Not for the route. Not for anything. You caught me. Now I catch you. That's the whole contract, and I don't care if you sign it."

The wind screamed. The serac groaned somewhere above us, the glacier clearing its throat.

Kestrel Villalobos, who had negotiated with a government agent without blinking, who had stared down a gun in a room of ice, closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she was furious about that too, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.

"Front carry," she said finally, hoarse. "Piggyback puts weight on your bad wrist and I can't watch the route over your shoulder. You'll need my eyes. I'll call the line for you." A breath. "And Elias — if I slow you past the point of—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"Then don't make me finish it." But her hand found the front of my jacket and held on, just for a second, just long enough. "Okay. Okay. Get me up."

* * *

I carried her down off the glacier through the long gold afternoon, and I will carry her for the rest of my life, in whatever life I have.

She was right about her eyes. Lashed to my chest, her splinted leg braced against my hip, she became the route-finder I couldn't be — calling the line in that stripped, economical voice, *left of the crevasse, two meters, hard crust here, trust it,* her breath hot against my neck, her weight settling into me with a trust that undid me more than the fall had. Nena broke trail ahead. Dario carried both packs and did not complain and did not speak, and I understood that this was his penance and his payment both, and I let it be.

The ramp below the serac band was still there — a shoulder of old ice angling down through the rock band, steep enough to make my thighs burn, gentle enough to be possible. Twice the glacier spoke around us, deep calving thunder rolling up from the icefall below like artillery from a war we couldn't see, and twice Nena flinched and Kestrel's hand tightened on my collar and her voice stayed level: *keep moving, the ice is doing its own work, it isn't ours.*

At the top of the ramp she made me stop. The sun was going down behind the western peaks and the glacier below us had turned the color of the inside of a crevasse, that blue, that impossible blue, and far down, tiny as insects, the lights of the border valley were coming on.

"You should know," she said quietly, against my ear, "that the last person I carried down this mountain, I carried because he was dead. And they took my name for it." Her breath shuddered once, and steadied. "So understand what it means, that I'm up here breathing on your neck. Understand what you're carrying."

"I do," I said. "I'm carrying the woman who caught me. Everything else is just weight."

She was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the calving thunder rolled again, closer now, the glacier beginning its season of letting go.

"Then put your boots where I tell you," Kestrel Villalobos said, "and let's go get our names back."

I took the first step down the ramp. Behind us, high on the white shoulder of the mountain, a serac the size of a cathedral leaned, groaned — and began, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, to fall.
