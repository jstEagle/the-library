## 20. The Evidence Goes Public

The village was called Tres Ríos, which was a joke, because the river that ran through it had only one channel and even that one was dying, braided thin with glacier silt. Nena came down into it at dusk on the ninth day, alone, her boots split at the seams, her hands still carrying the ghost-ache of the rope burns Kestrel had wrapped in Valparaíso tape three days and a lifetime ago.

She had not stopped moving since the serac. Since the whiteout tore the team in two and Kestrel's voice came through the storm like something thrown from a great height — *go, Nena, go, take the drive, get it down* — and Nena had gone, because going was the one thing she knew how to do without asking permission.

The drive was sewn into the lining of her jacket, against her left ribs. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

Tres Ríos was eleven buildings and a church with a leaning bell tower, strung along the river like beads on a fraying cord. Smoke rose from two chimneys. A dog lifted its head from a doorstep, considered barking, and decided against it. Nena stood at the edge of the village square with glacier water frozen in her eyebrows and understood, suddenly, that she had no plan beyond this point. Kestrel had given her a name — *Marisol Vega, she runs the radio co-op in Tres Ríos, tell her the Condor sent you* — and Nena had repeated it to herself for nine days like a prayer, like a rope hand over hand, and now that she'd arrived she found her mind blank past the name.

She knocked on the third door she tried. It was the right one, because the woman who opened it was already holding a shotgun, and Nena had learned on the ice that the people who answered doors armed were the ones who took things seriously.

"I'm looking for Marisol Vega."

"Who sent you?"

"The Condor."

The shotgun did not lower. The woman's eyes went over Nena the way Kestrel's went over a slope — reading it, weighting it, deciding what would hold and what would slide. Nena let her look. She was too tired to be anything but true, and maybe that was why the woman's shoulders finally came down half an inch.

"You're the porter," Marisol said. "From the radio traffic. They've been asking about you on the government bands for two days. A recovery unit, they called it." She spat the word *recovery* like gristle. "Get inside before someone who counts chimneys counts you."

* * *

The radio co-op was the back room of Marisol's house: a desk, a diesel generator that coughed like a sick man, a shortwave rig older than Nena, and a wall of notebooks in which, Nena would learn over the next two days, Marisol had logged every anomalous transmission in the valley for eleven years. Mine closures that weren't accidents. Survey flights with transponders off. The slow, patient cartography of a state that treated the Cordillera as something to be emptied.

Nena sat at the desk with her jacket open and the drive in her palm and could not, for a long moment, make herself hand it over.

It wasn't distrust. It was the opposite. It was that the drive was the last thing connecting her to them — to Kestrel's voice in the storm, to Elias's frostbitten hands copying files in the blue light of a melt cave, to the whole impossible chain of decisions that had carried a porter who'd never been above five thousand meters up onto a glacier where mountains calved like artillery. Handing it over meant the chain was in other hands now. It meant her part might be done, and she wasn't sure who she was on the far side of done.

"Whatever's on it," Marisol said quietly, "it's not safer in your jacket."

"I know."

"And whoever gave it to you — they gave it to you because they trusted you to finish. Not because they wanted to be rid of it."

Nena looked up. "How do you know who gave it to me?"

"Because only one person on that glacier says *go* like it's a kindness." Marisol held out her hand. "The Condor and I go back. Give it to me, *chinita*. Let's see what they were willing to kill for."

* * *

What they were willing to kill for was forty-one gigabytes of ordinariness.

That was the thing that undid Nena, sitting beside Marisol through the night while the generator stuttered and the files opened one by one on a laptop with a cracked screen: how ordinary it all looked. Spreadsheets. Spectrometer readouts. Core sample photographs — gray stone shot through with veins that glinted even in the flat laptop light, like frost on black ice. A rare-earth deposit, Elias had told them in the melt cave, his voice hoarse, his frostbitten fingers steady on the documents: the largest untapped deposit on the continent, worth more than the province it sat under, worth more, possibly, than the government that owned it.

And threaded through the survey data like wire through stone: the other thing.

The memos. Nena made Marisol stop on the third one and read it twice, because the language was so clean it took a moment to understand that it was describing murder. *Asset recovery protocol. Containment of survey findings. Disposition of personnel.* Disposition. Elias's team of six had not vanished in a storm. They had been *dispositioned* — and Elias had lived only because he'd read the memos before his own agency's shooters arrived at the camp, and had walked up into the ice with nothing but a radio, a pistol, and the truth.

And further back, in an archived folder Elias had flagged with a single word — *precedent* — Nena found the thing that made her get up and stand in Marisol's cold patio until her breathing steadied.

A mountain guide's incident report, seven years old. A client's death on a descent route in the Cordillera del Viento. The official finding: guide error. The buried annex, stamped with the same agency letterhead as the memos: the client had been a geologist, contracted quietly, carrying preliminary data on the same seam. The annex recommended using the accident. Recommended *letting the finding stand*, because a disgraced guide made a better story than an inconvenient corpse.

Kestrel Villalobos had not lost her career. Her career had been taken and used as a lid.

Nena stood in the dark with her breath steaming and thought about every story she'd heard in the valley about the Condor — the guide who'd killed a client, the guide you didn't hire, the guide whose name was a caution told to young porters — and felt something in her chest go hot and clean, like a flame in wind.

They had taken nine days of Nena's terror and Kestrel's blood and Elias's frostbite and built it on top of seven years of a lie. Fine. The lie had been built out of paper. Paper could burn.

* * *

Marisol's journalist contact was a man named Tomás Arrieta who wrote for a paper in the capital that the government considered a nuisance and the government's opponents considered a lifeline. He was not reachable by the co-op's shortwave — the bands were being monitored; Marisol had logged three new interception stations brought online in the last week, which meant Sorel's operation was scared, which meant they knew Elias was alive — so the data had to travel the old way.

"You'll carry it," Marisol said.

"I just carried it."

"You carried it down a mountain. Now carry it to a bus." Marisol was already copying the drive's contents onto three separate cards, wrapping each in wax paper, sealing them into the kind of foil sachets that pharmacies used. "The drive stays with me as a dead-man copy. If you don't reach Tomás, I send it slow, through three hands, over a month, and by then they've had a month to kill everyone involved. You reach Tomás tonight, the story breaks before Sorel can finish whatever she's doing on that glacier." She pressed one sachet into Nena's palm and closed Nena's fingers over it. "The second card goes in your boot. The third — you'll figure out a third place. You're a porter. Porters know how to move things."

Nena almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a sob. "I carried flour and fuel for three years. This is the first thing I've ever carried that mattered."

"No," Marisol said. "You carried it the moment you took it from a dying woman's rope team and didn't stop walking for nine days. Everything before was practice."

* * *

The bus to Abancay left from the fuel depot at five in the morning, a rusted Mercedes with a cracked windshield and a driver who took bribes and asked no questions. Nena sat in the back with a sack of seed potatoes on her lap and the sachet taped flat against her stomach under three layers of clothing, and watched Tres Ríos shrink to a smudge and then to nothing, and did not let herself think about what was happening above the calving line, because thinking about it would either break her or make her do something stupid like get off the bus, and neither of those would help anyone.

Instead she thought about the files. She made herself a discipline of it. Every time the bus jolted, every time a checkpoint loomed, she recited the data to herself like a rosary: *forty-one gigabytes, six memos, one annex, coordinates 9-something-something, seam depth 340 meters, assay values, Tomás Arrieta, the paper is called La Verdad del Sur, ask for the man with the hearing aid.* If the cards were taken from her, the numbers would still be in her head, and heads were harder to search.

There was one checkpoint, at the bridge over the Río Blanco. Two soldiers, bored, waving through trucks. The soldier who looked into the bus had eyes the color of glacier melt and a chin strap worn loose, and he looked at Nena for perhaps four seconds, and in those four seconds Nena discovered what her body did instead of fear: it went perfectly, glacially calm, the way it had gone calm on the icefall when Dario fell and her anchor held. She looked back at him with the flat patience of someone carrying potatoes to market, and he waved the bus through, and only when the bridge was a kilometer behind her did her hands begin to shake, and she let them, because shaking hands could still hold a rosary of numbers.

* * *

La Verdad del Sur occupied three rooms above a print shop in Abancay, and Tomás Arrieta turned out to be a heavy, unhurried man of sixty with a hearing aid and the eyes of someone who had been lied to professionally for four decades and had developed an earworm for the specific frequency of truth.

He listened to Nena's account without interrupting once. Then he read the files for six hours while Nena slept in a chair with her boots on, and when she woke, the light through the window had gone orange and Tomás was sitting very still in front of the laptop with his reading glasses pushed up into his hair.

"Do you understand what you brought me?" he said.

"The biggest mineral find on the continent, and the government tried to kill the people who found it."

"That." He waved a hand, dismissive, as if she'd named a minor detail. "That's Tuesday. That's the kind of story I bury three of a month because no one goes to jail and the paper gets audited into dust." He turned the screen toward her. It showed the annex — Kestrel's annex, the buried report, the guide error that wasn't. "This is different. This has a face. This has a woman the whole valley already believes is a killer, and this document proves the state built that lie to bury a geologist's corpse, and now the same machine has sent a recovery unit — their words, *recovery unit*, God, they even put it in a radio log — after the survivors of a second massacre on the same seam." He took off his glasses. "Do you know how long I've waited for a story where the lie has a paper trail?"

"There are people still on that glacier," Nena said. "Kestrel. Elias. Maybe Dario. The recovery unit has a helicopter. Every hour this sits in your laptop—"

"Is an hour the story sits in my laptop instead of in print, where it can't be unprinted." Tomás was already moving, pulling a phone from a drawer, dialing. "We go tonight. Full package: the memos, the annex, the assay data, your testimony, Elias's if we can get it recorded. And the radio logs — Marisol's logs, did she copy those?"

"Everything's on the drive."

"Of course it is. Porters." He said the word with something like reverence. "The whole country runs on people who carry things and aren't thanked." He covered the phone's mouthpiece. "One question, and I need you to think before you answer. Once this prints, there is no version of your life that goes back to what it was. Sorel's people will know your name by morning. Are you ready for that?"

Nena thought about the icefall. About Nena-before, who had signed on with a famous disgraced guide because famous was exciting, and who had learned on the glacier that famous was just a word for someone the mountain hadn't finished with yet. She thought about Kestrel saying *go* like it was a kindness.

"I was never going back to what it was anyway," she said. "Print it."

* * *

The story broke on a Thursday, though Nena would always think of it as the ninth day plus two, because calendars had stopped meaning much.

It broke the way water breaks through a dam — not all at once but in the specific place the pressure had already found. La Verdad del Sur ran it across eight pages with the annex reproduced in full, the memos facsimiled beside typed transcripts, Nena's testimony printed under her full name at her insistence — *if they're going to hunt me, they can at least hunt someone who signed her work* — and by midmorning the wire services had it, and by noon a television station that had spent years as the government's megaphone discovered, with the shameless agility of all megaphones, that it had always suspected something was wrong.

The agency denied everything for eleven hours. That was the number Nena fixed on, the number she would carry the rest of her life: eleven hours. Eleven hours between the denial and the first resignation — a deputy director, "to pursue other opportunities." Fourteen hours to the leaked internal audit. Twenty hours to the announcement of a "special commission" with a name so bland it was clearly designed by someone panicking. Thirty-one hours to the first photograph of Sorel, leaving a ministry building at night, coat collar up, face turned from the cameras — a woman who had spent her career making other people disappear, now doing it to herself one step at a time down a ministry staircase.

And through it all, from Tres Ríos, Marisol's shortwave kept logging what the official bands stopped saying. The recovery unit's frequency went silent on the second day. The helicopter's transponder, which had been circling the high glacier on a holding pattern, turned south and did not come back. On the third day a military patrol radioed a query about "the Villalobos party" and received no answer, and Marisol underlined the silence in her notebook twice, and showed Nena, and said, "Either they're dead or they're across the pass, and knowing the Condor, I know which bet I'm placing."

Nena placed the same bet. But a bet was not a person, and the nights were long, and she spent them in the co-op room listening to static and learning the radio the way she'd learned rope work — hands first, understanding after — because someone had to be on the frequency if Kestrel called, and Nena had decided, with the same flat calm she'd found at the checkpoint, that someone was going to be her.

On the fifth night after the story broke, past two in the morning, the generator humming, frost ferning up the inside of the window glass, a signal rose out of the static. Weak, skipping off the ionosphere, half-swallowed. A callsign Marisol had taught her, the one the Condor had used for years and the government had never bothered to flag because who listened to a dead guide's frequency.

Nena's hands were on the dial before her mind caught up. She keyed the transmit switch, and her voice came out steady, glacially calm, carrying potatoes to market.

"Condor, this is Nena. The story's out. Sorel's finished. The whole country knows your name — the right name this time." She swallowed. "Everyone down here is waiting. Come down when you can. I'll keep the light on."

Static. Long enough that her heart began to fall. Then, through it, a voice she knew — rough, wind-scoured, unmistakably alive — three words, and nothing more, and Nena put her hand over her mouth and laughed and cried into the cold room while the signal faded back into the hiss of the world:

*"Coming down. Together."*
