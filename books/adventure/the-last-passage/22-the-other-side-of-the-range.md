## 22. The Other Side of the Range

The pass had no name on any map Kestrel owned. She had named it herself, years ago, in the private cartography of a woman who counted seasons instead of birthdays: *the door*. It sat between two shoulders of black rock at five thousand four hundred meters, a notch of sky barely wide enough for the wind to squeeze through, and beyond it — if the old survey sketches were honest, if her memory of a route she'd never climbed was honest — the range folded down into valleys that drained toward another country entirely.

They climbed it in the blue hour before dawn, when the ice was still frozen hard enough to hold their weight.

Kestrel went first. She always would; that was not pride anymore but simply the shape she'd been carved into, twenty years of breaking trail through ground that wanted her dead. Behind her, Elias moved with the careful economy of a man who had spent eleven days learning to trust his legs again. The frostbite had taken the tip of one finger and most of his left ear's feeling, but it had taken nothing else, and he had stopped apologizing for the slowness somewhere around day nine.

Nena came last, carrying the heaviest pack out of pure spite, because Dario had turned back two days ago at the col and someone had to carry what he'd left behind.

* * *

Kestrel thought about Dario more than she wanted to.

He had stood at the col with his hands in his pockets and his face doing something complicated — shame and relief fighting each other like weather fronts — and he had said, "My girls eat because I walk. You understand that. You've always understood that."

And she had. That was the worst of it. She had understood him completely, because she had been him once: a person who made arithmetic out of love, who fed the people they loved by feeding themselves to something that didn't care whether they lived. Sorel's agency ran on people like that. It always had. Her own file proved it — the client dead below the Serpent's Stair, the report rewritten, her name blacklisted so the operation above would stay clean. Dario hadn't betrayed her out of malice. He'd betrayed her out of the same hunger that had once made her take clients up routes she should have refused.

She had stripped his share and let him live, and both of those things had cost her more than she could name, and now, climbing toward a pass with no name, she found she wasn't angry anymore. What she felt instead was closer to grief for a version of herself that had died on the icefall and hadn't yet been replaced by anything.

Maybe this climb was the replacement. Maybe that's what the other side of a range was: not a place, but a person you hadn't met yet.

"Stop," Elias said quietly behind her.

She froze, axe planted. "What."

"Look back."

She almost told him she didn't look back — it was a rule, one of the few she'd kept intact through everything — but something in his voice made her turn.

Dawn was coming up behind them, and the glacier they had crossed was burning with it. The whole upper basin had gone rose and gold, every serac lit from within like a cathedral window, and as she watched, a section of the eastern wall calved — a house-sized block of ice peeling away in silence, then the thunder arriving seconds later, rolling across the basin and up the rock walls and into her chest. The route they'd taken three days ago was already gone. Not damaged. *Gone*, erased under fresh collapse, the crevasse field rearranged into something no guide alive would recognize.

"There," Elias said. "Now it's official."

"We could have died on that crossing," she said.

"We did die on that crossing." He smiled with half his mouth. "This is just the paperwork."

* * *

They reached the notch at midmorning.

It was anticlimactic, the way all true thresholds are. No view of paradise, no choir. Just wind funneling through the gap hard enough to lean on, and beyond it — clouds, and beneath the clouds, the green-brown suggestion of valleys falling away toward a border that existed only as a line someone had drawn on paper in a room far from any ice.

Kestrel stood in the notch and did the thing she had done at the top of every route of her life: she measured. Distance to the valley floor. Angle of the descent gully. Hours of light remaining. Snow conditions, wind loading, the stability of the exit couloir. Twenty years of habit, running its arithmetic automatically, and then she caught herself doing it and stopped, and the stopping felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing.

"What are you thinking?" Nena asked, coming up beside her. The girl's face was wind-scoured raw, her lips cracked, her eyes brighter than Kestrel had ever seen them. She looked like someone being born.

"I'm thinking I don't know what's down there," Kestrel said. "No contacts. No safehouses. Elias knows a journalist in Valparaiso and that's the entire plan."

"It's a good plan," Elias said.

"It's not a plan. It's a hope with a mailing address."

"That's the only kind I've ever had that worked."

Nena laughed — a short, startled sound, as if she'd surprised herself — and then the three of them stood together in the wind and looked down at the rest of their lives.

* * *

They descended through the afternoon, and the world changed around them the way it only changes on long descents: gradually, then suddenly. Ice gave way to old snow, snow to scree, scree to the first shocking tuft of grass, brown and brittle and so aggressively alive after weeks of white that Kestrel knelt and touched it like an artifact. By late day there was water running free over stone, unglaciated, murmuring, and the air thickened until breathing felt luxurious instead of rationed.

Elias walked beside her. Neither of them spoke for a long time. There was a language between them now that didn't need words — built in whiteouts and on rope, in the dark of the melt cave where he'd pointed a gun at her and she'd talked him down not with courage but with honesty, telling him exactly how frightened she was and exactly why she'd come anyway. Trust, she had learned, was not a feeling. It was a record. A ledger of small kept promises, and theirs was full.

At the stream, they stopped, and he filled the bottles, and while he crouched there she watched him and thought about the question she'd been circling for days.

"When we get down," she said, "what do you want?"

He didn't answer right away. He screwed the cap onto a bottle, set it down, looked at the water.

"Everyone keeps asking me that like the answer is complicated," he said finally. "Sorel asked it, in his way. *What do you want, Elias? Exoneration? Money?* As if I'd want anything from him. My team is dead because of what we found. Marta. Okonkwo. Reyes. They're dead because a mineral seam under the ice was worth more than four lives, and some man in an office decided that before we ever knew it existed." He picked up a smooth stone, turned it over. "I want them remembered as people, not as a line item in a cover-up. And after that—" he looked up at her, and the low sun caught the scarred side of his face "—after that I want something very small. A town where nobody knows my name. Work with my hands. Mornings where the first thing I think isn't *how long can I survive today.*"

"That's not small," Kestrel said. "That's the biggest thing there is."

"And you?" he asked. "What does Kestrel Villalobos want, when nobody's holding a file over her head?"

She opened her mouth to give the practiced answer — *a functioning outfit, debts cleared, a season without incident* — and heard how hollow it was even before it reached her tongue. Those were the wants of a woman in survival mode. A woman counting seasons. But the woman who had gone back into the collapsing whiteout for Nena and Elias, against every rule she lived by, was not in survival mode anymore. Something else had been operating in her that day, something that didn't do arithmetic.

"I want to stop measuring," she said. "That's what I want. I've spent my whole life measuring — vertical meters, hazard angles, seasons survived, sins accumulated. Miles instead of years. And I told myself it was discipline, professionalism, but it was fear. If you measure everything, nothing can ambush you." She looked at the water running bright over the stones. "I'm done. I want years. Unmeasured ones. With—" she stopped, and made herself finish it, because she had decided on the glacier that she would never again leave the important sentence unsaid, "—with you. If you want that. If the man who hid in the ice for a month can imagine a life where hiding isn't the point anymore."

Elias stood up slowly. He crossed the two steps of wet stone between them, and he took her hand — the frostbitten one, the one with the missing fingertip, the hand that had held the rope that saved his life — and pressed it flat against his chest, over the heartbeat.

"I spent a month in the ice planning how to stay alive," he said. "Every plan ended at this pass. Every single one. I just didn't know your face was going to be on the other side of it."

The wind came down the valley and moved through the grass, and neither of them moved for a long time.

* * *

They camped that night below the tree line, the first trees in weeks — stunted queñua, twisted by wind, more sculpture than timber — and Nena built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. They ate the last of the real food, the emergency rations they'd been saving for exactly this kind of evening, and Nena talked with her mouth full about the village she'd grown up in, two valleys over from this one, and how her mother was going to kill her and then feed her for a week straight, in that order.

"You'll go see her?" Kestrel asked.

"I have to deliver the copies first." Nena's voice changed, firmed. The data was split three ways now — drives sewn into three separate packs, three separate bodies, so that no single accident or arrest could end it. "Valparaiso. The journalist. Then home." She looked into the fire. "Do you think it will work? The story. Do you think it actually changes anything?"

Kestrel considered lying to her, and decided against it. That was the new rule too.

"No," she said. "Not by itself. Stories break and governments absorb them and in six months Sorel has a different job with a different name. But it changes some things. It puts the names of the dead on the record where they can't be edited out. It makes the next survey team harder to disappear. And it means that when people like us look at a mountain, we know what's underneath the ice, and knowing is a kind of ownership." She poked the fire. "Mountains keep secrets. People shouldn't have to be one of them."

Nena nodded slowly, absorbing it, and Kestrel saw the exact moment the girl stopped being a porter and became whatever she was going to become next. It was like watching a serac calve in reverse — something assembling itself instead of falling.

"Will I see you again?" Nena asked. "After Valparaiso?"

Kestrel glanced at Elias. He gave the smallest shrug: *your call.*

"The border town," Kestrel said. "End of the season. There's a bar called the Ferro Carril with terrible wine and a stove that works. If you're ever passing through, ask for the woman who runs the guiding counter and doesn't smile much."

"You run a guiding counter now?"

"Not yet." Kestrel allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "But I'm told I'm good at routes nobody survives."

* * *

On the third morning they reached the valley floor, and the border was a river.

That was all it was — a cold, quick river coming down out of the high country, maybe eight meters wide, with a footbridge of planks and wire cable strung across it, rusted at the joints, older than any of the politics it divided. On the far bank, a track led down through scrub toward smoke rising from chimneys in the distance. A different flag flew over those chimneys, if any flag flew at all. A different language in the market, probably. The same mountains overhead, indifferent, keeping their mineral secrets for another century.

Kestrel stood at the near end of the bridge and took inventory one last time, out of habit.

Behind her: the Cordillera del Viento, its summits hidden in cloud, the glacier sealed under the calving season's wreckage. Somewhere back there was the empty camp, the crevasse with its glowing seam of ore, the recovery unit that had lost them in the storm, the radio frequency where Sorel's voice had offered her everything and she had switched the set off mid-sentence. Somewhere back there was the client whose death had broken her, buried now under truth instead of snow. Somewhere back there was her whole former life, complete, closed, the route finished.

Ahead of her: a plank bridge, a dirt track, a town, a continent, a man standing beside her waiting to see what she'd do.

"No contract," Elias said quietly. "No file. No miles left to hide behind."

"That's the idea."

"It terrifies you."

"Completely." She tested the first plank with her boot. It held. "You know what the funny thing is? I've crossed worse than this a hundred times. Crevasses wider than this river. Bridges of ice thinner than these boards. And I was never scared, because the mountain doesn't judge you. It just kills you or it doesn't. But that—" she nodded at the smoke, the town, the unbuilt life "—that can refuse you. That can look at who you actually are and say no thanks. The mountain never says no thanks. It doesn't care. That's why I loved it. That's why I hid in it."

"And now?"

She looked at him. Wind-torn, frost-scarred, exhausted, alive. A man who had chosen, at every fork since the melt cave, the harder kinder thing — going back for Nena, carrying her down the mountain when every rule said leave her, refusing to let her fall alone even when falling together meant they might both die. He had fled everyone, once. He wasn't fleeing anymore either.

"Now I'd rather be refused by people than protected by distance," she said.

She stepped onto the bridge.

The planks rang under their boots, a thin metallic music, and the river rushed below, and halfway across Kestrel stopped — not from fear, but because the view was suddenly perfect: the whole range behind them catching the morning, white and enormous and final, the pass already swallowed in cloud, the route they'd descended already becoming legend, becoming the story Nena would tell in Valparaiso and the story Kestrel would tell, someday, over terrible wine, to whoever needed to hear that a person could lose everything — name, career, country — and walk out the other side of the range with the only things that mattered still roped to them.

Elias put his hand on her back, between the shoulder blades, warm through the worn fabric.

"Together?" he said.

She realized she was smiling — really smiling, an unfamiliar muscle, one she'd have to train like any other.

"Together," she said. "Unmeasured."

They crossed. Behind them the wind came down off the ice and shook the bridge cables, and the river carried the melt of ten thousand untouched years toward the sea, and the mountain said nothing at all, which was, Kestrel understood at last, the mountain's way of letting go.
