## 1. Miles, Not Years

The river was green glass at dawn, and Marguerite Okafor counted the first mile of the day before her feet even touched the deck.

It was a habit now, the counting. Not years—she had given up on years the way a drowning woman gives up on the surface. Years were too big. Years had weight and shape and the terrible habit of containing anniversaries. Miles were honest. A mile was a mile, whether you swam it or steamed it or lay awake listening to the current drag against the hull. Three years, one hundred and eleven days since Tomas Okafor—no relation, though everyone assumed—had gone into the water at Kilometer 340 and not come out. Three years, one hundred and eleven days of river measured in miles instead of grief, and today the odometer in her head turned over another one as the *Magdalena's Answer* pushed off from the mud dock at Puerto Hondo with her engine coughing and her paint peeling and nobody aboard but Marguerite and the ghost she refused to name.

She stood at the wheel with her coffee going cold in one hand and watched the mist burn off the water. The river here was wide and slow and brown as tea left standing, the kind of water that forgave nothing and forgot nothing. Somewhere under that surface lay the *San Cristóbal*, a grain barge that had gone down nine months ago with a load of maize and a hole in her port side, and somewhere under that lay Marguerite's next paycheck, and that was all. That was all it had to be.

That was the rule. The rule had a name, though she never said it aloud: *be water*. Be water—no edges, no reflection, nothing the world could grip. No crew to remember your face. No port records with your real name. No photographs. The *Magdalena's Answer* was registered to a shell company that was registered to another shell company that was registered, if you followed the paper far enough, to a lawyer in the capital who had died two winters ago and whose signature Marguerite had gotten quite good at. She paid her fees in cash. She anchored away from the other boats. When salvage brokers called, she was M. Okafor, voice like river gravel, gender ambiguous over a bad line, and by the time anyone met her in person the job was half done and she was half gone.

Invisibility wasn't cowardice. She'd had three years to think about that and she was still deciding, but on most mornings, in most lights, she believed it. Invisibility was discipline. Invisibility was what you did instead of drowning. Tomas had been the visible one—the laugh that carried across a whole dock, the man who knew every barkeep's mother's name between the headwaters and the sea—and look where visibility had gotten him. A plaque, maybe, in a bar she couldn't walk into anymore. A name she couldn't say. Visibility got you loved, and being loved got you a funeral where you had to stand in the front row and listen to people describe a person you had killed.

No. Not killed. *Failed.* There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of her life, and some days the architecture held and some days it was a house of cards in a wind tunnel.

She drank the cold coffee. Mile two went by, marked by the leaning kapok tree with the lightning scar, and she logged it in the ledger she kept in her head: *three years, one hundred and eleven days, two thousand four hundred and six miles.* The number was getting long. She had never decided what happened when it got too long to hold.

* * *

The *San Cristóbal* lay in eleven meters of brown nothing, and Marguerite went down to her alone, because that was the other rule: dive alone, surface alone, need no one.

Eleven meters in this river was a special kind of dark. Not the clean black of deep ocean, which at least had the decency to be honest about its hostility, but a thick, particulate brown, a darkness with texture, water that had been a river for ten thousand years and had opinions about intruders. She ran her gloved hand along the guide line she'd set yesterday and felt the world reduce itself to the things that mattered: pressure in her ears, breath in her lungs, the line in her fingers. Down here there was no past. Down here there was only the next handhold, the next meter, the next breath, and Marguerite Okafor was very, very good at next breaths.

The barge rose out of the murk like a drowned cathedral roof. She worked her way along the port rail, feeling for the tear, and found it where the survey notes said it would be—a two-meter gash, plates curled outward, steel peeled back like the rind of a fruit. She ran her palm across the fracture and let her fingers read it the way a blind woman reads a face. Struck by a sunken log, the insurance assessor had written. Marguerite had pulled enough curled steel out of enough hulls to know a log strike when she felt one, and this was a log strike, and that was that. Not every wound was a story. She had learned to stop looking for stories in steel. Stories were how you ended up with a partner at the bottom of Kilometer 340.

She marked the tear with a paint pen, checked the cargo hold access, confirmed the maize had long since dissolved into sludge and silt, and began the slow ascent with her mind already drafting the salvage plan: lift bags on the four corners, a slow bring-up over two days, the barge righted and patched enough to float to the scrap yard at Puerto Hondo. Three weeks of work. Clean money. Invisible money.

She broke the surface into blinding morning and hung on the ladder for a moment, water sheeting off her, and for no reason she could name she looked downstream, toward the narrows, toward the border, toward the stretch of river that appeared on no chart she owned. Nothing there. Brown water, white birds, the heat shimmer standing up off the far bank like the river breathing.

She climbed aboard, and mile nine went by uncounted, which hadn't happened in a long time.

* * *

The navy liaison came at dusk, which was the first wrong thing, because navy liaisons did not come at dusk. Navy liaisons came at noon, in pressed whites, with clipboards and bad coffee and the smug patience of men whose salaries never depended on the weather. This one came at the moment when the light dies and the river turns to hammered pewter, and he came alone, in a civilian skiff with a tarp over something in the bow, and he stood off fifty meters and waited to be acknowledged, which meant he knew the rules, which meant someone had briefed him, which meant Marguerite was already in trouble and just hadn't been informed.

She let him wait. She finished splicing the lift-line she was working on, coiled it, stowed it, and only then looked up and lifted her chin a centimeter, which in her language was an entire conversation.

The skiff came alongside. The man who stepped onto her deck was fifty-ish, gray at the temples, with the build of a man who had been athletic in a way the navy had used up and discarded, and he moved like someone who had spent his career being careful about which floors creaked. He wore civilian clothes that had been chosen by someone whose job was making officers look like civilians and failing. He stopped a polite three meters away and put his hands where she could see them, which was either courtesy or a message, and said, "Marguerite Okafor."

Not a question. That was the second wrong thing. Nobody knew that name. The lawyer in the capital was dead; the shell companies were clean; the last person who'd called her by her full name had been a priest at a funeral, and he was dead too, or might as well be.

"Captain Okafor," she said. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Colonel Vasquez." He did not offer a hand. "Retired, if it matters. It doesn't. I'm told you're the best wreck man on this stretch of the river."

"I'm told a lot of things."

"I'm told you work alone." His eyes moved over her boat with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory—engine, deck gear, the dive compressor under its tarp, the absence of any second bunk made up. "I'm told you don't ask questions. I'm told you don't file reports. I'm told that when the *Santa Elena* went down off Punta Roja, the navy's own divers spent nine days looking for her black box, and a certain anonymous contractor found it in two dives and asked no questions about why a coastal ferry would be carrying a military flight recorder."

Marguerite said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere across the water a fish jumped, once, like a punctuation mark.

"I'm told," Vasquez continued, "that you are invisible."

The word landed on her deck between them like a landed fish, still moving. She kept her face the way she kept her boat—battened, gray, nothing to grip. "People tell you a lot of things, Colonel."

"People tell me everything. It's my profession." He reached into his jacket, and she let her hand drift to the pry bar leaning against the rail—not subtle, but she'd never claimed to be—and he smiled thinly and produced instead a folded paper, which he did not hand over but held up, like a teacher with a note from a parent. "A contract. Salvage and recovery, single site, restricted waters. The fee is four times what the *San Cristóbal* will pay you for three weeks."

"Restricted waters," Marguerite said. "The narrows."

"The narrows." He said it the way other men said a woman's name they weren't supposed to say. "There's a wreck on the bottom at Kilometer 112. Small vessel, courier class, went down—according to our records, which are, I'll admit, incomplete—during the flood season, six years ago. We'd like it recovered. Quietly."

"Recovered," Marguerite said. "Or searched?"

The colonel's face did something very small. It was the kind of movement most people would have missed, the kind of movement that happened in the last half-second of a life, sometimes, when the truth got out ahead of the lie. Marguerite did not miss it. She had spent three years learning to read faces the way she read steel, because faces were the only wrecks left that she could bear to dive.

"Recovered," he said. "There may be materials aboard of interest to certain parties. You bring the vessel up or you bring the materials up. You bring them to a location we designate. You tell no one. You log nothing. You were never on that stretch of river."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no." He set the contract on her deck, weighted it with a coin that was, she noticed, old currency—Veltanian, a face and a date from a country that had stopped existing before she'd ever seen a river. He stepped back to the rail. "But I'll tell you what I tell everyone, Captain. The river keeps everything. Every hull, every body, every secret. The only question is who it gives them back to."

He went down the ladder and the skiff pulled away into the pewter dark, and Marguerite stood at the rail and watched until the running light was gone, and then she stood a while longer.

Four times the fee. Restricted water. A courier boat that official records couldn't quite remember. A colonel who knew a name that no living person should have known, and who had put a dead country's coin on her deck like a signature.

She should burn the contract. She knew that the way she knew the tear in the *San Cristóbal* was a log strike—by feel, by the accumulated grammar of twenty years of reading steel and water. Contracts with secrecy clauses were holes in hulls. Restricted rivers were rivers that had already eaten someone.

But four times the fee meant the compressor rebuilt, the hull re-plated, a year of anchoring away from other boats without having to take a single job she didn't choose. Four times the fee meant the shell companies stayed clean and the dead lawyer's signature stayed convincing and the invisibility held for another season. And underneath all of that, in the hold of her where she kept the things she never hauled up, something else stirred—something that had looked downstream at the narrows that morning for no reason, something that had left mile nine uncounted, something that had spent three years being water and was tired, in a way she would not examine, of having no edges.

She picked up the contract. The paper was heavy stock, official, and in the light of her single deck lamp the secrecy clauses stood out like a gash in a hull—paragraph after paragraph of *shall not disclose, shall not retain, shall not approach, shall not survive*—no, that last one wasn't there. She read it twice to be sure. It wasn't there. It just read that way.

She went below, took out the battered notebook she kept locked in the chart box, and turned to the ledger page. *Three years, one hundred and eleven days. Nine miles, one uncounted.* She sat with the pen above the line for a long time.

Then she wrote, in the column where she recorded what each mile had cost or carried: *Kilometer 112. Four times the fee.*

She signed the contract by the dead lawyer's name, because that was who she was to the world, and she slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of brown water, which was normal, and woke at dawn to the sound of her own engine turning over, which was the closest thing to a heartbeat she had left.

The narrows lay downstream, past the leaning kapok, past the last charted buoy, past the edge of the map she trusted. She pointed the bow at them and did not count the miles, and told herself that was discipline too.

Behind her, far behind, at a naval station whose name did not appear on the contract, a young officer with her name in a file he wasn't cleared to read was about to learn that the dead sometimes surface. But that was a mile she hadn't reached yet.

The river kept everything. She'd find out soon enough what it intended to give back.
