## 2. The Navy's Silence

The *Pelican's Rest* sat low in the water at Berth Nine, looking exactly like what she was: a working salvage boat with a rusted crane arm, a dented hull, and no ambitions. Marguerite had spent three years making sure the boat looked forgettable, and three years making sure she did too. It was the first rule of the river, the one she'd learned the hard way, the one written in a name she no longer said aloud: *be nothing, and nothing finds you.*

She broke that rule every time she signed a contract, of course. A name on paper. A thumbprint. She used a different variation each time—M. Okafor, Marguerite O., Okafor M.—and told herself the small compromises didn't count. The river kept its own ledger anyway. She could feel it sometimes at night, the way the water slapped the hull like a slow hand counting.

Today the ledger would grow by four hundred miles, and she hated herself a little for how quickly she'd said yes.

The navy liaison had appeared at her door in Puerto Cobre eleven days ago with a leather folio and a face like a closed door. Commander's insignia, but no name she recognized. The contract was for salvage and recovery of a "vessel of administrative interest" in a restricted stretch of the Serrano River—restricted, which meant the charts showed it as blank water, which meant whatever lay at the bottom of it had been declared not to exist. The pay was three times her usual rate. The secrecy clauses ran to eleven pages.

She'd read all eleven pages twice. Clause 4: no communication regarding the operation, by any medium, with any party not named in the contract. Clause 7: all recovered materials to be surrendered to the contracting authority, unexamined. Clause 9: in the event of discovery of human remains, operations would continue at the discretion of the contracting authority. She'd read clause 9 three times. *At the discretion.* Not *operations would halt.* Most countries had laws about that. Most countries had laws about a lot of things.

The money was too good. That was the second thing she'd noticed, after the clauses. Money like that wasn't payment; it was a muzzle. She signed anyway, because the *Pelican's Rest* needed a new compressor, because Kofi needed boots that didn't leak, because the alternative to taking the job was sitting in Puerto Cobre with her accounts bleeding out and her silence growing more expensive by the month. She signed, and the liaison took the folio, and neither of them shook hands.

Now she stood at the rail of her own boat and watched the river do what the river always did, which was carry everything away except the things that mattered.

* * *

Bo arrived first, as Bo always did, on foot, carrying his toolbox the way other men carried children. He was sixty-one years old and built like something the river had failed to erode—wide hands, a neck like a piling, a face so creased it looked topographic. He'd been an engineer on the big dredges before the war, then a smuggler's mechanic, then hers. He didn't ask questions. That was the whole of his character, or so people assumed; Marguerite knew better. Bo didn't ask questions because he already knew the answers, and he'd decided long ago that knowing quietly was the only honest way to know anything.

He stepped aboard, set down the toolbox, looked at her face, and said, "Navy."

Not a question. She almost smiled. "Is it that obvious?"

"You signed something. People who sign things they like don't make that face." He opened the toolbox and began, without being asked, checking the crane hydraulics. "How bad?"

"Eleven pages of silence. Restricted water. Triple rate."

Bo grunted. The grunt contained an entire engineering assessment. "Triple rate means somebody's paying for something other than salvage." He pulled a gauge, frowned at it, replaced it. "I'll rebuild the winch brake before we cast off. When do we go?"

"Tomorrow. I'm picking up Ines and Kofi at San Feliz tonight."

"Kofi." Bo said the name the way he said all names, flatly, but Marguerite caught the warmth underneath it, the way you catch heat from a stone that sat in the sun all day. "The boy's grown two inches since the last job. He'll want to dive."

"He's not diving. He's sixteen."

"He was diving at fourteen when you weren't watching."

"I'm always watching."

Bo looked at her then, one long look from under the brim of his ruined canvas hat, and said nothing, which from Bo was a whole sermon.

* * *

San Feliz at dusk was a town the river had half-swallowed and decided to keep. The market stalls were folding up, the fish smell was thick as paint, and the ferry dock sagged into the brown water at an angle that suggested surrender. Ines was waiting on it with two canvas bags and a boy who had grown, in fact, two inches since the last job, and who was pretending with great discipline not to be excited.

Ines Ferreira was fifty-three and moved like water finding its level. She'd cooked on river boats for thirty years and dove on them for twenty, and she had a scar that ran from her left ear down into her collar that she never explained, though everyone on the river knew the shape of the war that had made it. She looked at Marguerite across the gap between dock and gunwale and read her the way she read weather.

"Navy," Ines said.

Marguerite laughed despite herself. "Does everyone know?"

"Everyone who's ever worked for you knows that look. It's the look of a woman who's read a contract twice and signed it anyway." She stepped aboard, handed down her bags, then Kofi's, then took Kofi's arm and swung him across the gap like cargo, which he endured with the long-suffering dignity of the adolescent. "Terms?"

"Restricted water. No communications. Everything recovered goes to the navy, unexamined."

Ines was quiet while she stowed the bags under the wheelhouse bench, and her quiet was different from Bo's—denser, more expensive. "Human remains clause?"

"Continue at the discretion of the contracting authority."

Ines straightened up. For a moment her face did something old and far away. "That's not a salvage contract, Marguerite. That's a grave-robbing license."

"It's a paycheck. It's four months of paychecks. And it might be nothing—might be a sunk patrol boat with a bent propeller and a paperwork problem." Marguerite held her gaze. "I read the clauses. I'm not blind."

"No," Ines said. "You're not blind. You're hungry. There's a difference, and the river doesn't care which one you are." She said it without cruelty, which was worse, and then she went aft to the galley to see what Bo had done to her spice shelf, and the sound of her outrage covered the whole boat like a tarp.

Kofi stood at the rail, watching the town slide away, and Marguerite came to stand beside him because that was their ritual now, had been since the day she'd found him—sixteen months ago, half-starved, poling a leaky skiff through the reeds off the Veltanian border marshes, a boy with no papers and no family and a way with water that made her chest ache. She never asked where he'd come from. He never offered. The river had given him to her, and the river didn't explain its gifts.

"Restricted water," Kofi said. He'd seen the chart. Of course he'd seen the chart; she'd stopped hiding things from him after the first month because hiding things from Kofi was like hiding meat from a heron. "That's the old Veltanian border stretch. Nobody's charted it since the war."

"You know the charts better than I do."

"I know everything about that stretch." He said it simply, not proudly. "I used to fish it. Before." The word *before* sat between them like a stone neither of them would pick up. "What's down there?"

"A boat the navy says doesn't exist."

Kofi nodded slowly, and Marguerite saw him file it away in whatever deep, orderly place he kept the facts of his childhood. Then he grinned, sudden and bright, and the boy came back over the top of whatever else he was. "Triple rate?"

"Triple rate."

"Then I'm glad you signed it." He paused. "You did sign it, right? Because if you're going to make me dive it without signing, that's a different kind of job."

"You're not diving."

"We'll see," said Kofi, with the serenity of someone who had already won.

* * *

They ran upriver through the night and the next day, past towns with fewer lights each hour, past the last fuel dock, past the point where the charts went politely blank and the banks closed in—tall cane, strangler fig, the green throat of the border country. Marguerite ran the wheel herself through the narrows. Bo kept the engine at a low, thrumming patience. Ines fed them river fish and rice with a pepper sauce that made Kofi's eyes water, and the boy ate it anyway because refusing Ines's food was not a thing that happened aboard the *Pelican's Rest*.

The site was a wide bend where the river deepened suddenly, the water going from tea-brown to a cold black-green, the kind of hole where the river keeps its secrets at temperature. The navy's coordinates put the target under forty feet of it, off the old channel marker, near a drowned village whose rooftree tips broke the surface like the knuckles of a submerged hand.

Marguerite suited up on the foredeck in the gray of early morning. Ines checked her gear without being asked, running through the ritual—valves, gauges, knife, light—because that was Ines's way of saying things she didn't say. Bo held the crane steady. Kofi tended the tag line, his face carefully, deliberately professional.

"Forty feet," Ines said. "Visibility?"

"Zero to a foot, if the hole's like the others." Marguerite put the regulator in her mouth, then took it out. "If I'm not up in forty minutes—"

"We come get you," Ines said. "We always come get you."

The words went through her like a fishhook. *We always come get you.* There had been a time when someone had said something like that to her, a different river, a different life, and the coming had not happened in time. She put the regulator back in and let herself fall backward off the platform, and the river closed over her like a door.

* * *

Down was down. The thermocline hit her at fifteen feet like a wall of winter, and below it the water was so cold and so dark that her light made a small, exhausted circle. She followed the shot line into the black, equalizing, watching her gauge, feeling the old calm come down over her like a second suit. This was the part of the work she still loved—the silence, the weight, the absolute democracy of depth, where nobody's name and nobody's history mattered and there was only the next kick, the next breath.

The wreck materialized the way wrecks do: first a shadow wronger than the dark around it, then a shape, then a boat.

She'd expected a patrol craft, maybe forty feet, military lines. What she found was a river courier—sixty feet, flush-decked, a working boat built to carry mail and light cargo fast. Civilian lines, or something close to it, though there was a hard, purposeful set to the bow that made her think of a knife worn in an apron pocket. She swam the length of her light's reach. Silt had taken everything soft. The wheelhouse was a collapsed shell. The funnel was gone. Her light found the stern, and there, under a beard of silt and weed, letters: *V.S.N. ALMENA.*

She didn't know the name. But the prefix snagged in her like a hook—*V.S.N.* She filed it. Forty feet of black water was no place to think.

She worked her way along the starboard side, looking for the reason a courier boat ends up in a hole the navy pretends isn't there. Grounding damage, maybe. Explosion. And then her light crossed the hull amidships and stopped.

There was a hole in the hull. That wasn't strange; boats sink. But Marguerite had raised eleven wrecks in three years, and before that a hundred more in another life, and she knew the grammar of broken steel the way Ines knew pepper. This hole had been pushed *outward*. The plates were peeled from the inside, edges flared into the current like petals, like something blooming. The rivets around the breach had blown away from the frame, not into it.

She put her gloved hand against the torn edge and felt, beneath the silt, the clean bright metal of the fracture—no corrosion bloom, no decades of rust. The break was recent. Years, not decades.

The boat had been holed from the inside. Someone had blown it open—or blown something open—*after* it went down. And then the navy had spent money and silence making sure nobody ever looked.

Her breath sounded loud in her own ears. Above her, forty feet up, the *Pelican's Rest* sat on the surface with her whole small world aboard: an old man who knew too much, a woman with a war in her neck, a boy who fished these waters *before*. The word *before* surfaced in her mind again, and she pushed it down with the rest.

Marguerite Okafor had one rule, and the rule had kept her alive and invisible for three years. The rule said: find the propeller, find the cargo manifest, get paid, say nothing, go nowhere, be no one.

Her light swept back along the peeled plates, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water in her lungs: *whatever was inside this boat, somebody wanted it to stay inside even after the boat died.*

She kicked for the breach, and the dark of the cargo hold opened in front of her light like a mouth.
