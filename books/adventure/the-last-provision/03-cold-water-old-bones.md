## 3. Cold Water, Old Bones

Ines woke before the alarm, the way she always did when her body remembered a date her mind had tried to bury.

She lay in her bunk in the *Marta's* forward cabin and listened to the river. The border water moved against the hull with a sound like slow breathing, patient and enormous, indifferent to everything that had ever been done on its banks or beneath its surface. Somewhere aft, Bo was already up—she could hear the particular clank of his wrench against the engine housing, the rhythm of a man who talked to machines because people had become complicated for him. Kofi's hammock was empty; the boy slept on deck most nights now, claiming he liked the stars, though Ines suspected he liked not being enclosed, after whatever years had made him flinch at closed doors.

And Marguerite—Marguerite was already gone from her bunk, which meant she was on the foredeck with her coffee, counting something. Miles, probably. She did it every morning, lips moving slightly, eyes fixed on the current as if the river were a ledger only she could read. Three years of miles since Tomas. Ines had never asked how many there were. Some arithmetic you don't interrupt.

Ines dressed, braided her hair back tight the way she'd learned to do in another life, another uniform, and climbed out into the grey light of dawn on the border river.

* * *

The site was beautiful, which should have been the first warning.

The navy liaison's charts had put the wreck in a bend where the current slowed and dropped its silt, forty meters down off a shelf of red clay, and when Ines went over the side on the first survey dive she descended through water so clear it felt like a lie. Most rivers were soup. This one, this morning, was glass, and the light came down in long amber columns, and the wreck appeared below her like a cathedral rising out of a dream: a river steamer, sixty meters if it was one, lying on its starboard side with its superstructure fanned open like a ribcage, every surface draped in a pale fur of algae that turned silver when her lamp swept across it.

She hung at fifteen meters and let herself look. It was the kind of wreck divers paid fortunes to see, and here she was getting paid to pretend it was cargo.

"Pretty one," she said into her mask mic, and heard Marguerite's voice come back calm and level: "Save your air. Run the line."

They worked the perimeter first, Marguerite and Ines moving in the wordless choreography of two divers who had done enough jobs together to know each other's habits—the tap-tap-pause that meant *look here*, the flat palm held out that meant *stop, I'm thinking*. Kofi topside on comms, his voice cracking with adolescent excitement every time they reported a measurement. Bo running the winch, grumbling about the navy's mooring coordinates being off by thirty meters, which of course they were, because everything the navy touched was half-true.

The hull told its story slowly. Ines found the name plate first, crusted but legible under her knife: *V.S. Aldermoor*, registered out of a port that no longer existed on any map she owned. Veltanian letters. Her stomach did something small and cold when she read them, and she told herself it was just the thermocline, the cold layer of water they were passing through at twenty meters, the river's own memory pressing up against her skin.

Then Marguerite signaled her over to the stern quarter, and Ines saw the hole.

It was not a reef strike. It was not groundings or corrosion or any of the hundred mundane ways boats die. It was a breach blown outward from inside the hull, metal peeled back like petals, edges bent away from the ship rather than into it. Ines put her gloved hand near the torn steel and understood, in the way you understand things underwater—wordlessly, completely—that something had exploded in the cargo hold, and that someone had wanted it to.

"Holed from inside," she said. "You seeing this?"

A pause on the line. Long enough that she could hear Kofi asking what was wrong, and Bo telling him to let the adults talk.

"I'm seeing it," Marguerite said finally. "Finish the survey."

* * *

They planned the penetration dive that evening over Ines's stew—cassava and smoked fish, the pot passed around the deck, the lantern swinging—and it was during that meal that Ines said what she'd been carrying since the hole.

"We should pull the contract."

Bo looked up from his engine manual. Kofi stopped chewing. Marguerite didn't stop doing anything; she just turned her head, unhurried, the way she did, as if Ines had commented on the weather.

"The hull was blown from the inside, Marguerite. That's not an accident. That's a scuttling, or an execution, or both. Navy contracts on restricted water with secrecy clauses and a boat that somebody murdered—" She spread her hands. "We're not salvage crew anymore. We're evidence handlers, and nobody told us."

"The money clears us through winter." Marguerite's voice was even. "All of us. New compressor for Bo, real wages for Kofi, and I stop owing Ines for the regulator she bought me in March."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is. We're three bad months from losing the boat. You know it. I know it." Marguerite took the ladle, refilled her bowl without asking, which was a liberty Ines normally would have fought her over and tonight let pass. "One hold dive tomorrow. Cargo manifest says machine parts. We catalog, we tag, we lift what's listed, and we're done with the hold in a day."

"And if it's not machine parts?"

Marguerite looked at her across the lantern light, and Ines saw the thing she always saw underneath the calm: a woman holding very still so nothing inside her could spill. "Then we'll know what we're carrying."

That was the answer of a woman who already knew. Ines had interrogated frightened soldiers and lying officers in another life, and she knew the tone of someone managing information. But she was tired, and the stew was good, and the money argument was true, and so she let it go—which was, she would think later, the second mistake of that week. The first had been signing.

* * *

The hold dive began at 0700, in fog so thick the river disappeared ten meters from the boat and the world contracted to the sound of their own breathing.

They went down the shot line together, Marguerite leading, Ines on her left shoulder, lamps cutting cones through water gone from clear to tea-dark as the silt of the shallows gave way to the deep channel's murk. At thirty-five meters the temperature fell off a cliff. At forty, the wreck's open cargo hatch yawned above them like a mouth, and they swam into the throat of the *Aldermoor*.

Inside, the darkness was total and the silence was worse. Their bubbles went up and didn't come back. Ines kept her lamp moving in slow arcs, cataloguing by reflex—rib frames, dunnage timbers rotted to lace, crates collapsed into brown pulp, the sediment lying over everything like dirty snow. Machine parts, some of it actually was. Crates stamped with the same dead nation's crest, engines half-disassembled, the bureaucratic debris of a war economy.

Then her lamp caught the chain.

It ran along the deck plating in a long slack curve, and Ines followed it the way you follow anything in the dark—stupidly, helplessly—to the manacle at its end, and the wrist in the manacle, and the arm attached to the wrist, and the rest of him.

He had been dead for twenty years and the river had kept him the way cold deep water keeps things: leached, softened, more suggestion than body, cloth still clinging to the bones in strips, boots still on his feet. There were others. Her lamp found them one by one as it swung—a figure slumped against the port bulkhead, two more tangled together amidships, chains running between them, chaining them to each other and to the ring bolts in the deck. Not cargo. Not passengers who drowned when a ship went down. People who had been locked in a hold and kept there while the ship was sunk around them.

Ines's breath went loud in her own ears. Somewhere far away, her mask mic crackled with Marguerite's voice saying something procedural—*mark it, log it, keep moving*—and the words arrived in Ines's head without meaning, like radio traffic from another war.

Because she had seen holds like this before. Not underwater. Above it, in the dry heat of a conflict whose name she no longer said aloud, in a year she counted her life from the way Marguerite counted hers from miles. She had been twenty-three and wearing someone else's uniform, and she had helped open a warehouse outside a town whose name was later erased from the maps for different reasons than Veltania's, and what had been inside had been people who had been waiting, and the waiting had ended badly, and she had spent eleven years afterward learning to cook food that made other people feel safe because it was the only safety she knew how to make.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake, absurdly steady in her vision, the gauntlets trembling in the beam of her own lamp.

"Ines." Marguerite's voice, closer now. "Talk to me."

"We're done." Her own voice came out flat and strange, compressed by the regulator into something mechanical. "Marguerite. We are done. These are people. They were chained in the hold and the ship was scuttled on top of them. This isn't salvage. This is a mass grave with a hull around it, and we are swimming through it with cargo tags."

Silence on the line except breathing. Then Marguerite's lamp swung toward her, and Ines saw her face behind the plate of her mask—pale, set, jaw working.

"There are manifests in the wheelhouse," Marguerite said. "Paperwork. If we surface now and report remains, the contract voids, the navy seals the site, and whatever happened here gets filed under *resolved* by the same government that hired us quietly. Nobody will ever come back. They'll be down here for another twenty years with nobody but catfish for witnesses."

"So we report it anyway! To someone else. Balogun, the commission, anyone—"

"With what proof? Our word? A diver's testimony about bones in brown water?" Marguerite shook her head, and the motion sent her lamp beam slewing wildly across the chained figures, illuminating them in strobes, hideous. "We document it properly. Cameras, measurements, positions. We do it right, Ines. Then it can't be buried, because we'll have it."

"You want to work a grave site to get better photographs of it."

"I want the truth to survive contact with the navy." Marguerite's voice cracked then, just once, just a hairline fracture in all that control. "I have spent three years knowing what it does when things go down quiet. I am not doing that again. Not to these people."

It was a good speech. It was probably even sincere. And Ines floated in the black water of a murdered ship, twenty meters of river pressing on her chest like a hand, and understood that none of it changed the essential fact: Marguerite Okafor needed the money, and she had found a way to make the need sound like principle.

"Two hours," Ines said. "Maximum. And Kofi doesn't dive this hold. He's a child, Marguerite. Whatever's down here, he doesn't carry it."

"He won't. I promise."

Another promise made by someone managing information. But Ines was cold, and tired, and the dead were watching with the terrible patience of the dead, and she gave the smallest nod her neck would give, and they turned back to the work.

* * *

Topside, the fog had burned off into a hard blue morning, and the world looked unbearably ordinary.

Kofi met them at the ladder with towels and questions, and Marguerite fielded them smoothly—*structural survey, old cargo, nothing dramatic*—and Ines watched the boy's face accept the lie without suspicion, because he trusted them, because trust was the whole architecture of a four-person boat, and something in Ines's chest went tight and stayed tight.

She cooked lunch because cooking was what her hands did instead of shaking. Rice and beans and too much pepper, the way Kofi liked it, and she stood over the galley stove and listened to Marguerite on deck giving Bo new lifting points for tomorrow, her voice brisk and professional, planning the recovery of a chest Ines hadn't yet been told about but already knew existed, because she had seen the shape of the secret in Marguerite's shoulders all afternoon.

Bo appeared in the galley doorway as she stirred. He was a big man who moved carefully, as if aware of his own tonnage, and he lowered himself onto the bench and sat with his hands around a mug he wasn't drinking.

"You dove the hold," he said.

"Yes."

"And?"

Ines turned off the burner. Through the galley's little porthole she could see Marguerite at the rail, silhouetted against the bright water, and beyond her the far bank of the border river, green and featureless, belonging to everyone and no one.

"There are men chained in that hold, Bo. Have been for twenty years. And Marguerite wants to finish the job."

Bo was quiet for a long moment. Steam rose off the pot between them.

"She saved my license, you know," he said finally. "After the inspection board. Nobody else would hire me. So I'll follow her somewhere stupid, maybe twice." He looked up, and his eyes were older than his face. "But you're the one who's seen it before. Whatever's in you from before—I can hear it in how you cook today. If you say we stop, say it loud. Because I don't think she's going to stop on her own."

From the deck, Marguerite's voice called something cheerful about an early start, and Kofi laughed, and the river breathed against the hull, patient and enormous, keeping everything it was given.

Ines lifted the lid, checked the rice, and did not answer him—not yet. But that night she wrote a letter she didn't send, to a journalist named Ade Balogun whose number she had carried in her wallet for six years like a splinter, and she slept badly, and in the morning the *Marta* weighed anchor and moved another mile downriver, toward the wheelhouse, toward the paper, toward whatever the *Aldermoor* had died carrying.
