## 4. What the River Kept

The river at forty feet was a different country.

Marguerite felt the change as she always did—a pressure behind the ears, a dimming of the world to green and iron-gray, the sun above reduced to a trembling coin of light that might have been a memory rather than a fact. Down here there was no navy liaison pacing her deck with his clipboard and his careful questions. No contract with its clauses like fishhooks. There was only the water, which had never once asked her who she was or what she'd done, and the wreck, which had been waiting for someone long enough that it no longer cared who came.

She hung in the column above the *Coriander*—that was the name stenciled on her stern in flaking paint, half-eaten by rust—and let herself sink the last few meters without finning. The silt took her weight gently, puffing up around her boots like smoke. Visibility was two meters on a good day, and today was not a good day; the river had been riled by rain upstream, and everything beyond arm's reach was a suggestion, a rumor of shape.

Good, she thought. She worked best inside rumors.

Three years of diving had taught her that the river kept its secrets the way a miser kept coins: grudgingly, and only because it could not help itself. Every wreck was a ledger. Hulls told you where they'd been struck. Cargo told you what they'd feared losing. And the way things lay—the angle of a dropped lantern, the drift of a coat against a bulkhead—told you how the last minutes had gone, if you were willing to read them. Most salvagers weren't. Most salvagers wanted steel by the ton and didn't ask the river to gossip.

Marguerite asked. It was the one habit from before that she had never managed to break, though she had broken nearly everything else about herself: her name in the trade papers, her face at the dive bars along the border towns, her habit of letting people close enough to matter. Tomas used to tease her about it—that way she had of hovering over a wreck like a priest over a confession, listening for sins. *You'll drown in other people's stories, Margot,* he'd said once, laughing, his mask pushed up on his forehead, his hands busy coiling line. She could still see his hands. That was the cruelty of memory; it kept the hands and lost the face.

She shook him off the way she did every morning, deliberately, the way you shake off a wet dog, and turned her attention to the hold.

* * *

The *Coriander* sat upright on the bottom, which was unusual and therefore interesting. River wrecks rolled. They settled onto their beam ends as their cargo shifted, or they broke their backs over snags and sandbars and came apart like toys. This one had gone down straight, keel first, almost politely, as if the river had simply opened its hand and let it through. Marguerite had noted it on the first survey and filed the note away where she filed all her uneasiness—in the same mental drawer as the holed plating they'd found on the starboard side, blown outward, not in. Bo had run his fingers along the fracture edges and looked at her over his mask with an expression she'd pretended not to understand.

Inside, the hold was a cathedral of murk. Her lamp carved a cone out of the dark and the dark closed behind her like water closing over a stone. Cargo netting hung in rotting swags from the overhead. Crates lay stacked in the corners, their stenciling long since dissolved into brown bloom. And everywhere the fine gray silt, ankle-deep, hip-deep in places, lying over everything like the ash of some enormous fire.

She moved through it slowly, methodically, running her gloved hand along the port-side stringer, counting frame bays the way she counted everything now. One. Two. Three. Miles, frames, breaths—it was all the same arithmetic, the same trick of keeping time without keeping years. Three years since Tomas, and she had learned that grief was not something you got over but something you measured around, the way a river measured around a stone, wearing itself smooth against it forever.

Frame seven. Her hand stopped.

There was a shape under the silt ahead of her, low and long, too regular to be wreckage and too deliberate to be cargo. She fanned the silt away with slow sweeps of her glove—not fast, never fast, fast stirred the river into blindness—and the shape resolved into a chest. Not a crate. A chest, iron-banded, with handles forged rather than bolted, the kind of thing built to be carried by men who expected to carry it a long way and wanted it to survive the carrying.

It was big. Bigger than a sea chest, longer than her torso, and when she pressed her palms flat against its lid she felt something no ordinary chest would give her: a dense, dead heaviness, a reluctance, as though the object beneath her hands were not empty space and paper but something poured solid. Lead, she thought. Lined with lead. Nobody lined a chest with lead to keep water out. Water got in everywhere eventually; the river proved that daily. You lined a chest with lead to keep something else in—or to keep something else from reading what was inside. Radiation, said one part of her mind, the part that had grown up around salvage yards and their rumors. Or instruments, said another part. Or paper that somebody did not want found by anyone with a magnet or a scanner or a curious eye.

Her heart was doing something stupid against her ribs. She made it stop. She was a professional. She had raised engine blocks bigger than this off bottoms deeper than this, and she had done it quietly, cleanly, without a single question asked, because asking questions was how salvage divers ended up famous, and fame was just another word for being findable.

But her hands stayed flat on the lid a moment longer than professionalism required, and under the crusted silt her fingertips found a ridge of metalwork—a seal, stamped proud of the surface, centered on the lid like a signature.

She brushed it clear.

A double-headed bird, wings spread, crowned twice. Beneath it, in letters worn shallow but still legible, a word she had not seen written anywhere in twenty years, not on maps, not on manifests, not in the mouths of the traders and fixers who moved along this border like water finding cracks:

**VELTANIA.**

Marguerite went very still, the way you go still when you realize the forest has gone quiet around you.

Veltania. The state that wasn't. Everyone knew the story the way everyone knows a scar they've decided not to look at: there had been a country on the north bank, small and stubborn and full of mountains nobody wanted, and then there had been a war that lasted eleven months and a peace that lasted twenty years, and afterward the maps were redrawn and Veltania was gone, absorbed, erased, its name struck from atlases so thoroughly that schoolchildren in the border towns grew up believing the river had always marked the edge of the world. Its people had been dispersed or worse. Kofi, she thought suddenly, unbidden—Kofi with his silence and his careful eyes and the way he watched the north bank when he thought no one was looking. But that thought was a door she was not ready to open, and she shut it.

What she could not shut was the chest.

* * *

She should have flagged it. That was procedure—her own procedure, the rules she'd written for herself after Tomas, the rules that had kept her invisible and alive for three years. Anything anomalous gets logged, photographed, reported to the client. The client here was a navy that officially did not exist on this stretch of river, represented by a liaison named Vasquez who smiled too easily and whose contract contained clauses forbidding her crew from photographing anything, retaining anything, or discussing anything, penalties accruing in language so dense it could only have been written by someone expecting to use it.

Anything anomalous gets reported. And yet.

She thought about the hull, holed from the inside. She thought about the human remains Ines had found chained in the aft compartment three days ago—Ines coming up the ladder white as bone, pulling her regulator out with shaking hands, saying *we stop, we stop now, Marguerite, there are people down there, they were chained*—and Marguerite standing on the deck with the money already half-spent in her head, saying the words that had put the first hairline crack through her crew: *We finish the job.* She thought about Vasquez's face when she'd mentioned the chains, the flicker there and gone, faster than a fish turning, and the smooth voice saying the remains were consistent with a prison transport, nothing more, please continue.

Prison transports did not get sunk by explosions from within their own holds.

And prison transports, she was now quite certain, crouched in the green dark with her gloves on a lead-lined chest bearing the seal of a murdered country, did not get contracted out to a no-name salvage outfit on a river the navy officially pretended not to patrol. Unless whatever was inside was worth more buried than found. Unless the job had never really been salvage at all. Unless she and Bo and Ines and Kofi were not workers but witnesses-in-waiting, hired for the same reason you hire a man to dig a grave—to be present at the burial and then paid to forget it.

The coin of light trembled far above her. Somewhere up there, Vasquez was watching her lift bag markers on his sonar, tracking her progress with the patient attention of a man minding a fire.

Marguerite Okafor had spent three years learning how to be no one. She had changed rivers, changed flags on her boat, changed the name she gave at fuel docks. She had trained herself out of laughter, almost out of anger, entirely out of hope. It had worked. Nobody looked at her. Nobody saw her. It was the safest way to live, and she had believed—with the whole flat conviction of the genuinely frightened—that it was the only way.

Now she knelt in the silt of a drowned ship, thirty-eight meters beneath a river that belonged to a government that had signed away a nation, with her hands on the last physical proof that the nation had existed, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water entering her suit, that invisibility was over. It hadn't ended with a bang or a decision. It had ended here, quietly, the moment her fingertips found those worn letters. You could not unknow a word like VELTANIA. You could not unfeel the weight of what someone had died to seal and sink. From this moment, every choice she made would be a visible one, made by someone who had seen something, and the world—which had ignored her beautifully for three years—would begin, very soon, to look back.

She should flag it. Procedure said flag it.

Instead, Marguerite did the thing she would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to explain, first to herself and then, eventually, to everyone: she began to work.

* * *

Raising the chest alone was impossible; it must have weighed four hundred pounds with the lead and whatever slept inside it. So she did what she'd done a hundred times on a hundred jobs: she prepared it. She cleared the silt from the lifting points, checked the bands for corrosion, rigged a sling of braided line with knots Tomas had taught her in another life—her hands making them without her permission, muscle memory outliving love—and then, instead of attaching a lift bag and sending it up into the light and the sonar and the waiting clipboard, she dragged it.

It took her most of an hour and both tanks' worth of air, humping the chest frame by frame down the flooded hold, through the collapsed section aft where the deck plates gaped, out through the breach in the starboard side—the wound in the hull, blown outward—and into the shadow of the wreck's lee, where the hull's mass would screen her from any sonar pulse aimed at the main site. There was a fold of riverbed there, a scour hollow where the current had dug a basin against the old hull, deep enough and dark enough. She settled the chest into it, laid silt over it with sweeps of her glove until only the faintest geometry showed, and pinned a marker buoy line to a snag six meters away, memorizing the bearing by three features she could find blind: a bent davit, a tangle of chain, a rock shaped like a sleeping animal.

Then she hung in the green dark above her secret, breathing borrowed air, and let herself feel what she felt.

It was not fear, exactly. Fear she knew; fear was a tool, it sharpened you. This was older and stranger—the feeling of a door opening in a wall she'd believed was load-bearing. For three years she had organized her entire existence around a single principle: *carry nothing that can't be dropped.* No attachments, no causes, no truths that owned her. The river miles had accumulated—she kept the count faithfully, eleven hundred and forty since the accident, each one a small stone laid between her and that day—and she had believed the count was a wall.

But walls, she understood now, kneeling in the cold, were just stories told from the inside. On the outside they were cages, and someone had always lived on the other side of them. Someone had chained people in this hold and sunk them with their country's name on a box, and the box had waited twenty years at the bottom of the world for a woman who wanted nothing more than to be forgotten. The river kept its secrets, yes. But it also, sooner or later, handed them to whoever came looking. It had handed this one to her. And a thing given could not be ungiven, no matter how much silt you shoveled over it.

She checked her gauge. Low. Time to go up and lie.

* * *

The surface took her back the way it always did, abruptly, all noise and glare. The *Marta-K* rocked on her moorings, patched and unlovely, the only home Marguerite had left. Bo was at the rail with the winch controls, and Kofi stood beside him pretending not to watch the water, which was how Kofi did everything—pretending not to watch, not to listen, not to care, a seventeen-year-old practicing invisibility with a fluency that sometimes hurt Marguerite to recognize. Ines was below, cooking, or performing the act of cooking; since the day she'd found the chains she had barely spoken to Marguerite except in the clipped syllables of necessity.

Vasquez stood amidships with his tablet, and his smile arrived right on schedule as she climbed the ladder.

"Productive dive?"

"Cargo's mostly rotted through," Marguerite said, peeling her hood. "Timber, textiles, some tin plate. Nothing worth the diesel to raise." She heard her own voice do what it did best—go flat, go boring, become the voice of a woman about whom no one had ever wondered anything. "Hull's deteriorating faster than your survey suggested. I'd recommend writing the site off."

Something moved behind Vasquez's eyes—there and gone, faster than a fish turning. He glanced at his tablet, at the sonar returns, at whatever the navy's instruments believed they had seen of her hour below.

"You were down ninety-four minutes," he said pleasantly. "For timber."

"Currents in the hold," Marguerite said. "Slow going."

He held her gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed, and she held his back, because she had learned that the safest place to hide a lie was inside a steady stare. Then he smiled again, wrote something on his tablet, and turned away to make a call on the encrypted radio, standing at the stern rail with his back to her, his voice pitched too low to catch.

Bo drifted over, wiping his hands on a rag, and spoke without moving his lips, an old dockyard habit. "You found something."

"I found timber," Marguerite said.

"Mm," said Bo, who had known her four years and been lied to by better.

That night, after Vasquez's launch chugged away downriver toward the checkpoint station, after Kofi had fallen asleep in the wheelhouse with a paperback open on his chest, after Ines had banked the galley stove and retreated behind her curtain of silence, Marguerite stood alone at the stern and looked north across the black water toward the bank where a country used to be.

Tomorrow, she would tell the crew the site was a bust. Tomorrow, she would invent a reason to run the *Marta-K* over the scour hollow—"checking moorings," perhaps, or a fictional fouled prop—and somewhere in the gray hour before dawn she and Bo would bring four hundred pounds of lead and history up out of the river, and she would have to decide where to hide a thing that size on a boat with three other souls aboard, one of whom missed nothing, one of whom trusted her less every day, and one of whom was a boy from a country that no longer existed, who might take one look at a double-headed crown and know exactly what he was looking at.

Eleven hundred and forty-one miles, she thought, and added today's to the count out of habit. Then, for the first time in three years, the number felt less like a wall than like a debt coming due.

Below her keel, in the scour hollow, the chest waited in the dark with its dead king's seal, patient as the river, and Marguerite understood that she had not hidden it at all.

She had only moved it—from the river's keeping into her own.
