## 5. Officer Reyes

The river at dawn was the color of gunmetal, and Lieutenant Commander Tomás Reyes had learned to read it the way other men read faces. This morning it told him nothing he wanted to know.

He stood on the aft deck of the *Vigilante*, a patrol cutter too large for the narrow channel and too small for the weight of what it carried, watching the salvage barge's lights wink out one by one as the Okafor crew began their day. Three hundred meters of brown water between them. Close enough to see the deckhand—still a boy, really—hauling a coil of hose across the wet planking. Far enough that no one aboard could see the way Reyes's hands had gone still around his coffee cup.

Three years, and the river had finally given her back.

He had not believed the report at first. Names on manifests were like fish in murky water: you saw what you expected to see. *M. Okafor* could be a cousin, a widow keeping the business name alive, any of a dozen women who worked the border rivers with welding torches and dive gear and no questions asked. He had told himself this for six days, all through the contract negotiations conducted by proxy, all through the secrecy clauses he himself had drafted—the clauses that required a salvage crew to work blind, to surrender anything recovered to naval custody, to ask nothing about a wreck the charts marked only as *Obstruction 44-C*.

Then yesterday, at the fuel dock in Puerto Ceniza, he had seen her.

Not her face—that would have been too easy, too merciful. He had seen her hands. Marguerite Okafor checked her rigging the way she did everything, with a economy that looked like carelessness until you understood it was the opposite: every motion stripped of everything unnecessary, three years of grief compressed into muscle memory. She had run a carabiner along a lifeline and tested it with a single sharp tug, and Reyes had felt that tug somewhere behind his sternum, in the place where he kept the things he did not think about.

She was supposed to be dead.

That was the official record, anyway—the one he had signed off on, three years ago, with a pen that had felt heavier than any weapon he'd ever carried. *Lost diver, presumed drowned, body unrecovered.* He had written the condolence letter himself because there was no next of kin listed and someone had to do it, and he had written it in the passive voice because the active voice would have required him to say *I sent her*, and he could not have survived writing that sentence down where anyone might read it.

He had not gone to the memorial. There hadn't been one. He had gone instead to the stretch of river below the falls where she'd last been seen, and he had stood on the bank in his dress uniform like an idiot, and he had thrown a single white flower into the current, and he had watched the river take it the way the river took everything—without malice, without ceremony, without giving anything back.

And now here she was, forty meters away, alive, hauling anchor chain in the gray light, and the first thing he felt was not joy. The first thing he felt was fear.

Because Marguerite Okafor did not take contracts like this one. He knew her history—he had helped write part of it, back when knowing each other's histories had been the whole point of the long nights in riverports up and down this waterway. She avoided restricted zones the way other divers avoided unexploded ordnance. She valued invisibility above profit; he had teased her about it once, called her a ghost who billed by the hour, and she had said, *Ghosts survive, Tomás. Heroes get rivers named after them.* It had been a joke then. Now it was doctrine, apparently, and a woman with three years of careful silence behind her had signed a contract full of clauses designed to make a careful person walk away.

Which meant either she needed the money badly enough to burn her own rules—

Or she already knew what lay at the bottom of Obstruction 44-C.

Reyes set down the coffee he had not tasted and went below to the signals compartment, where the morning traffic waited in a folder stamped with the classification level that had begun to feel less like protection and more like a diagnosis.

* * *

The folder contained four items, and he hated every one of them.

Item one: the original survey request from the Ministry of Interior, dated eleven months ago, requesting that "a navigational hazard" be removed from the channel at kilometer 212. Standard language. Except navigational hazards were Navy business, and this request had come from Interior, and Interior did not care about shipping lanes. Interior cared about what was *in* things.

Item two: the manifest fragment recovered from a riverside archive in Veltania's old provincial capital, before the province had stopped existing. A courier boat, the *Meridian Star*, registered to a government that had been erased from the maps two decades ago, listed as lost with all hands in a flood season nobody could verify. The manifest's cargo line read, in the dry bureaucratic hand of a dead nation: *Sealed diplomatic pouches, priority one. Do not open. Return to capital.*

A capital that no longer existed. Priority-one pouches ordered returned to a city that had been burned, renamed, and repopulated with people who had never heard of Veltania. Reyes had spent one long night with that sentence, turning it over, and by dawn he had understood what it meant: whatever those pouches contained had been worth more than the boat carrying them. Worth more than the crew. The *Meridian Star* had not been lost. She had been *kept*—sunk deliberately, in deep water, in a border zone where no one dredged and no one asked—and the men chained in her hold had been kept with her.

He had not shared this analysis with his superiors. He had written instead a bland confirmation of the hazard-removal mission, and he had watched their reply come back within hours, unusually fast, unusually grateful, and he had understood that his blandness was exactly what they wanted. They did not want an officer who thought. They wanted an officer who supervised.

Item three: a photograph, grainy, taken from the far bank two days ago. It showed the Okafor barge at anchor above the wreck site, and it showed something else—a shape being lowered over the side on a lift bag, wrapped in canvas, roughly the size of a sea chest. The timestamp put the lift at 0340. No salvage operation raised cargo at 0340 unless the cargo was something the daylight would have questions about.

Item four: a single typed instruction from Naval District Command, unsigned except by office stamp: *Upon recovery of historical materials, secure immediately. Crew is not to be informed of contents. Contractor is not to be debriefed. Report completion via courier only.*

No radio. Courier only. As if the words themselves were contraband.

Reyes sat with the folder open on his desk in the tiny compartment that served as his office, and he listened to the sounds of the ship around him—the diesel thrum, the water against the hull, Ensign Duarte's footsteps overhead on the watch—and he thought about the distance between the man he had been trained to be and the man the folder was asking him to be.

The training said: recover the materials, detain the contractor for questioning regarding unauthorized recovery operations, file the reports, go home. The training had an answer for everything, and the answer was always the same shape: procedure, applied until the problem stopped resembling people.

But the photograph showed a chest coming off the bottom at 0340, and the instruction said the crew was not to be informed, and Reyes had spent enough years around rivers to know what happened to evidence that passed into unofficial custody. It evaporated. It became a hole in a file, a line in a ledger, a thing that had never been there. Whatever the *Meridian Star* had died protecting, the Ministry wanted it to die a second time—quietly, bureaucratically, with his signature on the certificate of destruction.

And Marguerite—Marguerite, who was alive, who had let him believe she was at the bottom of the river for three years—was standing between the Ministry and its second drowning.

He should have felt betrayed. That was the correct emotion, the trained emotion. Instead, sitting alone in the signals compartment with the folder open in front of him, Reyes found himself doing arithmetic he had no business doing: if she had faked her death, when? Before or after the accident? Had the accident even been real? He remembered the search—he had personally extended it past the point of operational sense, three days of dragging the river below the falls while his executive officer suggested, gently, twice, that they consider the possibility of foul play rather than drowning. Foul play. He had rejected the theory because the alternative was unbearable: that she had chosen the river over him, over everything, and had not trusted him enough to say so.

Now the alternative was not unbearable. Now it was simply true, probably, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. She had looked at the same world he lived in—had looked at *him*, who loved her, whose love he had never once doubted—and had concluded that disappearing was safer than confiding. What kind of fear does that to a person? What had she seen, in those years, that taught her the people closest to you are the ones who can bury you deepest?

He closed the folder. His hands were steady. He noted this with a kind of clinical detachment, the way he noted engine temperatures and barometric pressure, because steadiness was the only inheritance his father had left him and he had learned to inventory it like equipment.

* * *

At 0900, per the contract schedule, he crossed to the barge in the inspection launch.

It was a ritual they had performed six times now, and each crossing had its choreography: he came aboard, the crew assembled with the loose formality of people who resented being inspected but valued the overtime, he walked the deck, checked the safety equipment, exchanged the necessary words. Bo, the engineer, answered his questions about the winch hydraulics with the flat competence of a man who resented authority on principle. Ines, the cook-diver, watched him with eyes that had seen something bad somewhere and were still deciding whether he was it. The boy, Kofi, looked through him entirely, which was its own kind of discipline.

And Marguerite stood at the rail and gave him nothing.

That was the part he had not prepared for. Not hostility—hostility would have been information, would have confirmed she recognized him, would have given him something to push against. What she gave him was worse. She gave him the smooth, courteous blankness of a professional stranger. *Good morning, Commander. Water's down another ten centimeters. We should reach the hull by Thursday.* Her voice was level, faintly accented, pleasant. Her eyes met his exactly as long as courtesy required and not one frame longer.

Either she did not recognize him—and after what they had been to each other, that would be its own wound—or she recognized him perfectly and had decided that the safest version of Marguerite Okafor was one who had never known a naval officer named Tomás Reyes at all.

He completed the inspection. He checked the dive locker and found it properly maintained, the tanks logged, the regulator kits sealed. He checked the cargo hold and found it empty, swept, smelling of diesel and tar—which was itself an answer, because a hold that empty meant whatever had come up from the bottom was no longer aboard, or was hidden so well that finding it would require tearing the boat apart, and tearing the boat apart was not in his instructions. His instructions wanted the chest secured, not sought. The distinction had a smell to it, and the smell was cover-up.

"Conditions permitting," he said, at the rail, preparing to leave, "we'll want a progress survey tomorrow. I may come out with the dive team myself."

Something moved behind her eyes. It lasted less than a second—a flicker, a shutter closing—but he had spent three years learning to see her in reflections and half-light and memory, and he caught it. Recognition, or alarm, or both braided together so tightly they were indistinguishable.

"Of course, Commander," she said. "We'll keep the ladder rigged."

He went down into the launch. Duarte cast off. The barge fell away behind them, brown water folding over its wake, and Reyes stood at the stern rail and watched it shrink and thought: *she knows I know.* And beneath that thought, quieter, older, wearing the voice of a man he used to be: *she was forty meters away for twenty minutes and you didn't say her name.*

* * *

That evening he did the thing he had been avoiding for six days. He opened the personnel archive and pulled her file.

It was thin. Salvage contractors filed minimal paperwork—it was practically a professional religion among them—and hers was thinner than most. Certifications, vessel registration, insurance bonds, a tax number issued in a riverport province two borders south of where she actually worked. And one document he had never seen, because it predated his involvement: an incident report from three years ago, filed by the regional maritime authority, concerning the loss of a dive team on the lower falls reach.

Two divers. One recovered alive, hypothermic, concussed. One unrecovered.

The survivor's name on the report was not Marguerite Okafor.

He read it four times. The survivor was listed as *Okafor, M., contracted diver*, and the description matched—same vessel class, same certification numbers, same home port—but the name was wrong, or rather the name was right and the report was wrong, because the woman pulled from the water below the falls three years ago, the woman he had signed the death record for, had been diving under a different name entirely. Marguerite Okafor had not survived the accident. Marguerite Okafor had been *born* of it—constructed from the wreckage the way salvagers build new boats from old hulls, plank by plank, name by name.

Which meant the woman he had loved—whoever her name had originally been—had looked at the river that nearly killed her, at the partner she'd lost in it, at the life she'd been living, and had chosen to drown herself on paper. To become a ghost who billed by the hour. And he had helped. He had signed the death certificate. He had thrown the flower. He had mourned a woman who was, at that exact moment, probably standing on some dock two hundred kilometers downstream, watching him grieve, and saying nothing.

He sat in his cabin for a long time after that, the file open on his desk, the lamp making a small island of light in the dark, and he let himself feel the whole architecture of it: the anger, yes, and under the anger the hurt, and under the hurt something that refused to curdle into either. Relief, maybe. The obscene, unspeakable relief of a man who has spent three years mourning and has just been told the grave is empty.

Then he made himself think about the chest.

Because that was the other thing the evening had given him, the thing he kept trying to set down and kept picking back up. If she had built a whole new self to stay invisible—if invisibility was her religion, her penance, her armor—then she had not taken this contract for money. People don't break a three-year fast for wages. She had taken it because of what was inside the wreck, or because of what she suspected was inside it, or because someone had made her an offer she couldn't afford to refuse. And now a lead-lined chest bearing the seal of a murdered nation had come off the bottom at 0340 and vanished from an empty hold, and his government wanted it secured without debriefs and without records, and the fixers were already circling—he'd had a query from a broker's office in Puerto Ceniza that morning, asking, with elaborate casualness, about "salvage activity at kilometer 212."

Everyone wanted what was in that chest. The Ministry wanted it dead. The brokers wanted it sold. And Marguerite Okafor, ghost, widow of her own name, wanted—what? He turned the question over and over. To disappear again, presumably. To take the money and the secret and sink them both somewhere even the archives couldn't dredge.

He wanted to believe that. Believing it made his duty simple: find the chest, close the file, and let her vanish back into the legend of her own death, and he would spend the rest of his career carrying the knowledge that she was alive somewhere, counting river miles, and that he had given her back to the river like a gentleman returning a borrowed book.

But he had seen her face at the rail. He had seen the flicker. And he had read the incident report, and he knew now what she had paid for her invisibility—not money but a man, a dive partner, dead in the water while she surfaced alone—and he knew that people who pay that much for silence do not spend it lightly. If she had broken the silence, it was because the silence had become the smaller of two evils.

Which meant the chest contained something the world was not supposed to survive knowing.

Reyes closed the file. He squared its corners precisely, the way his father had taught him, and he slid it into the drawer beneath the false bottom he had installed himself, because a man in his position learned early that some documents were safer hidden from his own superiors than from his enemies.

Tomorrow he would go out with the dive team. He would walk the deck again, check the logs again, exchange the necessary courtesies with a woman who was legally dead and standing in front of him. And somewhere in the middle of all that correctness, he would find a moment—on the ladder, at the rail, over the sound of the generator—to look at her and let her see that he knew. Not to trap her. God help him, not to trap her. Just to say, in the only language left to them, the language of loaded silences and held gazes across brown water:

*I know you're alive. I know what you're carrying. And I have not yet decided which side of me is going to win.*

On the bulkhead above his desk, the river chart of kilometer 212 hung in its roller frame, and the obstruction mark sat on it like a period at the end of a sentence. Tomorrow, he thought, he would take down the chart and look at what was underneath it—the older chart, the Veltanian one, the one with the village names that no longer appeared anywhere official. The dead country had kept its records better than the living one. That, he suspected, was the whole story of the chest in miniature.

He killed the lamp. Through the porthole, far upstream, the barge's riding lights burned steady against the dark—small, warm, defiant, like a lit window in a house everyone officially agreed was empty.

He watched them until his eyes ached, and then he turned in, and he did not sleep, and the river went on saying nothing, all night, in its voice like gunmetal.
