## 6. A Chest of Paper Bones

Kofi had learned to read a boat by its silences before he could read words on a page.

The *Marta June* spoke a language of creaks and shudders, and tonight every one of them was wrong. Bo's engine ticked the way it always did when it cooled after a long day — a slow metallic heartbeat in the stern — but beneath it there was another sound, human and hushed: voices in the hold, low as prayer. Marguerite's voice he knew, clipped and careful even when it was quiet. Ines's came in soft waves, Spanish curling around English like river grass around a piling. And Bo, whose voice usually arrived like a dropped wrench, was barely more than a rumble.

Kofi stood at the top of the companionway ladder with one hand on the rail and his heart going like a bilge pump running dry.

They hadn't invited him down.

That was the thing about being fifteen and the newest aboard. You learned the shape of doors that were closed to you. The chart table where Marguerite worked alone after midnight. The way Ines went silent whenever the radio crackled. And now this — lamplight leaking up through the hatch boards like something buried trying to get out.

He should have gone to his bunk. That was what a smart deckhand did. A smart deckhand kept his head down and his hands busy and never, ever made himself into a question nobody wanted to answer.

Kofi had been a smart deckhand for eleven months. He had been an orphan for nine years. He was tired of being smart.

He went down the ladder.

* * *

The hold smelled of diesel and old rope and, underneath it all, the cold mineral breath of the river itself — the smell that got into everything they pulled up from below, the smell Kofi privately thought of as the water's memory. A single work lamp hung from a hook over the cargo well, its light pooled yellow on the deck plates, and around that pool sat the three of them like people gathered at a sickbed.

The chest lay open between them.

It was smaller than Kofi had imagined from the way Marguerite carried herself since they'd raised it, smaller than the weight of her silence these past two days. Lead-lined, she'd said, which was why the magnetometer had sung so sweetly over it, why she'd known there was metal worth chasing down in the mud of the courier wreck. It was maybe the size of a sea chest, dented along one corner where something — a bullet, a boot heel, thirty years of silt pressure — had kissed it. The crest on the lid was half worn away, but Kofi had seen enough of it in glimpses to know what it was: a double-headed bird, wings spread over crossed keys.

Veltania. The country that wasn't anymore.

Nobody had said much about it out loud. That was the rule Marguerite ran the boat by — you didn't say things until saying them couldn't be unsaid. But Kofi had watched her stow the chest in the deepest part of the hold, under false floorboards, under spare chain, and he'd watched her check on it twice a day the way other captains checked their fuel lines. Whatever was inside, it was heavier than lead.

"Kofi." Ines saw him first. Her face did something complicated — surprise folding into something gentler, almost relief. "You should be sleeping."

"So should you," he said, and immediately wished he'd found something cleverer, because Bo snorted and Marguerite looked up at him with those gray diver's eyes that seemed to see straight through skin and bone into whatever a person was hiding.

She didn't tell him to go back to bed. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she moved aside on the crate she was sitting on, making room.

That small gesture undid him more than anything else that night. He sat down.

"We were just deciding," Marguerite said slowly, "whether opening this was a mistake."

"It's already open," Bo rumbled. He was a mountain of a man folded onto a child's stool, his huge hands resting on his knees like they were holding themselves down. "Been open an hour. Question is whether we look or we burn it."

"You don't burn paper like this," Ines said quietly.

"You don't know what's in it."

"No," Ines agreed. "But I know what kind of chest carries paper across a border in wartime, sealed in lead." She glanced at Kofi, then away. "I know what happens to the people who were supposed to receive it."

The lamp swung slightly with the river's breathing, and shadows slid across the open lid.

Marguerite reached in and lifted out the first bundle.

* * *

Paper, Kofi had expected. He hadn't expected the paper to feel alive.

It came up out of the chest in wax-sealed packets, each one wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job too well — the documents inside were dry, crisp, almost brittle with their own preservation, as if the river had held them in cold storage all these years. Marguerite unwrapped the first packet with the same deliberate care she used on dive gear, and laid the contents out on a spread tarpaulin: typed pages, dense with columns, stamped with faded ink the color of dried blood.

"Manifests," she said. Her voice had gone flat, professional, the voice she used for survey reports. "Cargo manifests. Passenger lists."

"Passengers," Bo repeated. He picked up a sheet and turned it toward the light. His lips moved as he read. Kofi watched the mountain go still. "These aren't passengers. Look at the column headers. Name. Age. Category." He stopped. "There's no destination column. There's a disposition column."

"What does disposition mean?" Kofi asked, and hated how young his own voice sounded.

Nobody answered him right away. Ines took the sheet from Bo and read it without expression, the way she did everything, but Kofi had spent months learning to read Ines, and he saw the way her thumb pressed white against the edge of the page.

"It means what they decided to do with them," she said finally. "Disposition means what was decided."

Marguerite had gone very still. Then she reached back into the chest and brought out the photographs.

* * *

They were packed in a tin box, film negatives and prints both, and when Marguerite fanned the first few prints out on the tarpaulin the lamplight fell across them and Kofi's whole body went cold, as if he'd slipped beneath the surface of the river without moving.

Gray squares. Gray figures. Rows of people behind wire. Baracks-roofs marching away to the horizon like the ribs of some enormous dead animal. Men in uniforms standing with the loose, bored posture of men doing routine work, while beside them —

Beside them, other things that Kofi's eyes refused to assemble into meaning at first, and then assembled all at once, the way a face comes up out of dark water.

He didn't hear himself make a sound, but Ines later told him he did — a small noise, high and involuntary, like a gull.

Because he knew the place.

Not the photograph. Not the exact frame. But the place. The gate with its brick pillars, one of them cracked from top to bottom like a lightning scar. The water tower behind the wire, leaning, its legs rusted to the color of old blood. The line of poplars beyond the fence that the prisoners called — what had the old man called them? — the witnesses. Because they stood outside the wire where the sky could see them, and they saw everything, and they said nothing.

Camp Nine. Karst-Hollow, on the maps that still bothered to name it. The place where the trains went in full and the paperwork came out light.

Kofi had been four years old when his mother carried him past that gate. He had no memory of it — four-year-olds don't keep memories of gates — but he had the story, told to him in fragments over seven years by the woman who'd smuggled them both out in a coal truck, before the fever took her in the resettlement camp at Marović, before Kofi became a name on a dock worker's roster and then a deckhand's boy and then nobody's.

His mother had described that gate. She'd described the crack in the brick, because she'd touched it, pressed her palm against it the night they left, and told him — he was four, he remembered nothing, but she told him anyway, over and over through the years, the way survivors tell children things the children can't carry yet — *remember this, malysh, remember that we walked out and others didn't.*

And here it was. The crack. The tower. The witnesses.

A photograph taken from inside the wire, looking out.

* * *

"—Kofi. Kofi."

Ines's hand was on his shoulder. He blinked and the hold came back: the lamp, the tarpaulin, the three faces turned toward him. His cheeks were wet. He wiped them with his sleeve, furious at himself, and found he couldn't speak.

"Take your time," Marguerite said. She said it quietly, without pity, which was somehow the only way he could have borne hearing it.

"I know that place," he managed. His voice came out scraped. "The gate. The tower. That's Camp Nine. Karst-Hollow." He pointed, and his finger shook, and he let it shake. "My mother — we came through that gate. Before. When I was small. She used to—" He stopped. Started again. "There are lists in there. Names. Ages. Dispositions." He looked at the typed pages fanned across the tarpaulin, hundreds of names in neat bureaucratic columns, and felt the floor tilt under him. "Some of those names might be my family."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the kind of silence a hull has when it takes on water — full, pressing, waiting to see what will give.

Bo broke it. He reached across and turned one of the photographs toward himself, studying it with his jaw working. "I fought in that war," he said, to no one in particular. "Northern sector. We heard stories about the camps. Everyone heard stories. Stories are easy to not believe." He set the photo down gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "This isn't a story."

"These are orders," Marguerite said.

She'd gone deeper into the chest while they talked, and now she held up a single sheet, larger than the rest, heavy stock, an official letterhead crowned with the double-headed bird. Even faded, the stamp at the bottom was unmistakable — a government seal, pressed deep, authoritative, real.

Her voice, when she read aloud, was steady in the way ice is steady.

"*Directive 44-C. Regarding the final disposition of persons remaining in administrative custody following the cessation of transport operations. No records of prior transfers are to be maintained. All documentation pertaining to categories B and C is to be destroyed upon completion. The existence of the facilities is to be regarded as a matter of state security.*"

She lowered the page.

"They weren't just killing people," she said. "They were killing the fact that the people had ever existed. This chest is the only reason we know Directive 44-C ever happened. Somebody sealed it in lead and put it on a courier boat, and somebody else put a hole in that boat's hull from the inside, and the river did the rest." She looked around the circle of lamplight, at each of them in turn. "Twenty years, and this is the evidence. All of it. Right here."

Kofi looked at the photographs again. At the rows of faces behind the wire. Somewhere in those gray smudges of humanity, statistically, mathematically, there might be an aunt. A grandfather. Someone who had carried his mother's eyes, her stubborn chin, the laugh she'd passed to him without knowing she was passing anything.

History had always been, for Kofi, a thing that happened to other people in books — dates and borders and the names of generals. Teachers in three countries had tried to teach it to him and he had learned it the way you learn the shapes of clouds: interesting, distant, unconnected to weather.

Now history was lying on a tarpaulin six feet away from his knees, and it had his mother's gate in it, and it was personal as a knife.

"The navy sent us here," Bo said slowly. "Off the books. Restricted stretch of river. Secrecy clauses thick enough to choke on." He looked up at Marguerite. "They knew what was down there."

"The government that signed this," Ines said, tapping the directive, "is the grandfather of the government that runs the country now. Same ministries. Same families. They erased Veltania from the maps, but they didn't erase themselves." Her voice stayed level, but her eyes had gone somewhere far away and terrible. "I've seen what armies do when their old crimes start surfacing. I've been on the receiving end."

"So what do we do?" Kofi asked.

Three adults looked at him. He realized he'd asked the question everyone was circling, the question that had been sitting in the middle of the hold all night like a third person, and that it had fallen to the youngest and least important among them to say it out loud.

Marguerite opened her mouth — and closed it. For a moment she looked, not like a captain, but like a woman balancing on a spar in open water, knowing that whichever way she leaned, something would go under.

Before she could answer, the lamp flickered.

Bo's head came up like a dog's. "Engine room," he said. "Compressor cycle. It does that." But he was already rising, unfolding off the stool, and Kofi saw him glance at the hatch — at the night beyond it, black and wide and carrying every sound of the river straight to them.

And then, faintly, from the direction of the bow, came a sound that did not belong to the *Marta June* at all.

An engine. Distant, low-throated, big — the deep diesel growl of a vessel far heavier than theirs, moving without lights, upstream, toward them.

Ines killed the work lamp.

In the sudden dark, Kofi knelt beside the open chest and, without quite knowing why, drew one photograph out of the tin and folded it into his shirt, against his skin. The gate. The tower. The witnesses.

Whatever happened next, someone was going to remember.
