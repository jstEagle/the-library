## 7. The Fixer

The riverport at Kessoum was the kind of place that existed only because the river slowed there, as if the water itself needed a rest from carrying everything downstream. Marguerite felt a kinship with it. The town had a fuel dock with a pump older than she was, a chandlery that sold rope by the arm's length, three bars of descending respectability, and a market that smelled of diesel, dried fish, and overripe mangoes going soft in the heat like the whole settlement was quietly giving up. It was not on the navy's charts as anything but a navigation hazard, which was exactly why she'd chosen it.

They had come down from the wreck site in three days of careful, unremarkable travel, the *Providence* riding low and anonymous among the fuel barges and fishing boats, her crane folded down like a sleeping insect. The chest lay in the bilge beneath the engine room floor plates, wrapped in tarpaulin and wedged against the fuel tanks, where Bo had built it a false cradle that would take an hour with a cutting torch to find. Marguerite had checked it twice a day since they'd raised it, not because she thought it would move, but because checking it was the only thing that made the weight in her chest—the other one, the one she couldn't bolt down—feel like something she was managing.

She was managing. She told herself that in the morning, counting the day's river miles the way she counted everything now, and she told herself again as she climbed the fuel dock ladder into the heat of Kessoum with a shopping list in her pocket and Ines's voice in her ear.

"Buy limes," Ines had said, pressing coins into her palm. "Real ones. And if you see a woman selling that smoked pepper from upcountry, the dark kind, buy all she has. We are going to eat like people, Marguerite, even if we die like idiots."

"You have a gift for morale."

"It's why Bo keeps me. That and the diving."

Kofi had wanted to come ashore—Kofi always wanted to come ashore, sixteen years old and allergic to the sight of the same four faces—and Marguerite had said no without looking up from the chart. It wasn't a punishment. It was the new arithmetic, the one that had started the moment she'd felt the lead lining of that chest through her gloves: every one of them was worth more than the job now, and jobs could be replaced. People couldn't. She didn't say any of this aloud. She just said *stay aboard* and watched the boy swallow his disappointment and pretend he hadn't, and hated herself a little for how easily she'd learned to spend other people's patience.

The market was loud and mercifully indifferent to her. She bought rope, a case of engine oil that Bo would complain was the wrong viscosity and then use anyway, limes, and all of the smoked pepper, which made the pepper woman laugh and press an extra packet into the bag like a blessing. She was haggling over a coil of polypropylene line when she felt it—the small cold shift in the air behind her shoulder blades, the feeling of being looked at not the way strangers look at strangers but the way a man reads a shipping manifest.

She finished the transaction. She counted her change slowly. Then she turned.

He was leaning against the fruit stall opposite with the ease of a man who had been leaning against things his whole life and had never once been asked to stop. He was perhaps fifty, brown as old rope, with a silver-shot beard trimmed too well for this town and a linen shirt that had been white once and had decided, with dignity, to be cream. He wore no watch, which on the river meant either poverty or power, and his shoes—soft, dark, city-made, absurdly clean—said power. He was eating a mango with a small knife, and he ate it the way surgeons cut: no waste, no hurry.

"You're the salvage captain," he said. His accent was coastal, educated, with a curl of somewhere else underneath it. "The *Providence*. Out of—well. Out of nowhere, as far as the harbor office is concerned, which I find charming. Most people lie about where they're from. You simply decline to be from anywhere at all."

"I'm not having a conversation," Marguerite said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're buying rope. But you're going to finish buying rope, and then you're going to realize that a man who buys a woman a drink is a nuisance, but a man who buys her cargo is an opportunity, and you're going to want to know my name." He folded the knife away. "Sable."

"I know what I'm carrying," she said, "and it's bilge sludge and bad rope."

"Mm," said Sable, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing she'd seen in three years, because it was patient. "The pepper is excellent here. You'll want to keep it dry."

* * *

She walked back to the dock the long way, past the net sheds, taking two turns she didn't need to take, watching the reflections in the shuttered windows for the shadow that followed. Nothing followed. That was somehow worse. A tail you could lose. A man who let you walk away was a man who already knew where you were going.

The *Providence* sat at the fuel dock with her paint flaking and her name faded to a rumor. Bo was under the deck plates in the engine room, half of him visible, a wrench in one fist and a cigarette burning down between his fingers where he'd forgotten it. Ines was on the foredeck teaching Kofi to splice, their four hands knotted together around the same rope, and for one second—looking at them from the ladder—Marguerite let herself feel the thing she never let herself feel, which was that she had, without meaning to, collected people.

Then she said, "We're leaving within the hour," and the feeling closed like a fist.

Bo's head came up through the plates, wrench and all. "The fuel line's half bled. I said tomorrow."

"We're leaving within the hour."

Ines looked at her the way she looked at a diver whose air gauge was lying—kindly, and with total refusal. "What happened in the market?"

"A man talked to me."

"Men talk to you every day. You're a beautiful woman with a boat; it's a whole tragedy." Ines set down the splice. "What kind of man?"

Marguerite hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hairline crack, but Ines heard it the way she heard everything.

"Marguerite."

"He knew the boat's name. He knew we're not on any harbor register. He called himself a buyer." She made herself say the rest flat, a report, no blood in it. "He said he buys cargo."

The engine room went very quiet. Somewhere across the water a barge horn moaned, long and stupid, and nobody moved.

"Nobody knows about the chest," Bo said. "Nobody *can* know. It's under my floor plates. I welded the brackets myself."

"The navy knows we raised *something*," Kofi said. He'd gone pale under his freckles, and he kept his eyes on the rope in his hands like it might hold him up. "They watched us dive for six days. You don't put a liaison on a job and then act surprised the job has a smell."

"The liaison thinks it's munitions," Marguerite said. "That's what the survey report says. Corroded ordnance, low yield, disposal pending."

"Then who told Sable?" Ines asked, and nobody answered, and the question sat down among them like a fifth crew member.

Marguerite made herself think it through, out loud, because thinking out loud was how she kept panic in its cage. "Options. One: the harbor office sold our registration the hour we cleared the last lock, and Sable is fishing. He saw a salvage boat with a navy contract running a quiet river and he's casting a wide net, hoping something lands in it. Two: someone at the wreck site talked—a fisherman, a spotter, one of Reyes's own people on the take. Three—" she stopped.

"Three," Ines said quietly, "someone on this boat talked."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight and temperature. Bo stood up out of the engine room entirely, wrench forgotten, and looked at each of them in turn with the flat offended stare of a man who has spent thirty years being trusted with other people's engines. Kofi looked stricken. Ines just looked at Marguerite, level and unblinking, and Marguerite understood that Ines was not asking to be believed. Ines was waiting to see whether Marguerite would need to be convinced.

"It's not us," Marguerite said.

"You know that how?"

"I know it the way you know a hull isn't holed. You've been inside it." She met Ines's eyes. "I've been inside this crew. It's not us."

Ines held the look for three seconds, four, and then nodded once and turned back to her rope, and the matter was closed the way things were closed on the *Providence*—without ceremony, with the wound left to close on its own or not.

"Then it's Sable fishing," Bo said. "Or the navy playing games with itself. Either way, the answer's the same. We run. Now, tonight, dark, and we don't stop until—"

"We don't run *yet*," Marguerite said. "If we run the moment a stranger says hello, we tell every watcher on this river that we're carrying something worth running from. A boat with nothing to hide doesn't flee a fuel dock." She heard herself say it and recognized, with a distant professional disgust, the voice of the woman who had spent three years teaching herself that stillness was safety. *Be nothing, want nothing, move like the water moves.* The doctrine had kept her alive since Tomas. It had never once been tested against something like this.

"And if he comes back?" Kofi asked.

Marguerite looked down the dock, at the market roofs, at the slow brown water carrying everything it knew toward the sea.

"He'll come back," she said. "Men like that always finish their mango."

* * *

He came back at dusk, when the river went to hammered copper and the bars along the waterfront began to pour their first dishonest light onto the water. Marguerite was on deck with a mug of Ines's terrible coffee, keeping the watch she'd assigned herself, when Sable came down the fuel dock without hurrying, hands in his pockets, and stopped at the gangway as if he'd been invited.

"Captain Okafor," he said.

Her name. Not the boat's name, not *captain* alone—her name, the one that existed on exactly four documents in the world, two of them forged. The coffee went cold in her hands. She set it down with great care, because she had learned long ago that the moment you wanted to throw a thing was the moment you most needed to set it down gently.

"You're a long way from anywhere," she said.

"I'm exactly where the cargo is." He didn't come aboard uninvited; that was part of the craft of him, she realized. He stood on the dock with the water slapping the pilings between them and made the boat come to him. "I'll be brief, because you strike me as a woman who prices her time honestly, even when she lies about everything else. I represent buyers with a serious interest in pre-war river salvage. Historical materials. Paper, mostly. They pay for condition and completeness, and they pay in ways that don't trouble your bank's curiosity." He produced a card—not a business card, something heavier, cream stock, a single telephone number embossed on it and nothing else—and set it on the bollard at the head of the gangway. "One hundred thousand. For a lead-lined chest, seal intact, contents unexamined. That's my opening figure, which means you should hear my closing one before you decide anything."

The river kept slapping the pilings. Somewhere behind her, below decks, she could hear Bo's radio murmuring to itself, and Kofi laughing at something Ines had said, and the whole small warm architecture of the life she had built on the water—and she understood that Sable could hear it too, that he had come at this hour on purpose, with her family's voices floating over his offer like a hostage's.

"I think you've confused me with someone else," Marguerite said. "I raised a chest of corroded ordnance. It's tagged for navy disposal. If you'd like to make an offer on rusted mortar shells, I'm sure the liaison would take your number."

"Of course," Sable said, and his voice didn't change at all, which was how she knew she'd lost. "The mortar shells." He turned to go, then paused, and when he spoke again it was softer, almost kind, and that was the worst yet. "Twenty years ago there was a country on this river called Veltania. You can walk its whole length now and find no sign of it—not a road sign, not a school, not a grave with the right name on it. There are people who prefer it that way. And there are people—" he tapped the bollard, once, beside his card, "—who pay a great deal to make sure that what's left of Veltania ends up in the right hands. Whichever those turn out to be." He smiled his patient smile. "The pepper from upcountry is excellent, by the way. You should keep it dry. Everything spoils, on the river."

He walked back up the dock into the failing light, and Marguerite stood at the rail with her heart going like a pump taking on flood water, and did not move until the linen shirt was a smudge, and then a rumor, and then nothing.

* * *

They convened in the wheelhouse with the door shut and the lamp turned low, which was not a habit of theirs and therefore felt, horribly, like the beginning of one. Marguerite laid the card on the chart table. Four faces looked at the single embossed number as if it might go for their throats.

"One hundred thousand," Bo said. "That's not a fishing number. You don't open at a hundred grand on a rumor."

"You don't open at a hundred grand unless you know the *weight*," Ines said. "He said lead-lined. Marguerite—did you tell anyone it was lead-lined? The liaison? The survey report?"

"No. I never wrote it down. I never said it out loud." Her own voice sounded far away to her. "The lead lining is under the outer planking. You'd have to have opened it, or lifted it, or—" she stopped.

"Or been told by whoever sealed it," Kofi said.

They all looked at him. He stood by the wheel with his arms crossed, and he had stopped trying to look like a deckhand; the fear had burned that off him, and what was underneath was older and harder.

"Think about it," he said. "Somebody sealed that chest and sank the boat with it. Somebody wanted it at the bottom of the river. And somebody else—him—knows exactly what it is, what it weighs, and that we have it. That's not a leak. That's a *map*. There are people on both ends of that river who've been waiting twenty years for that chest to come back up, and one of them just told us their opening price."

"And the other end is the navy," Bo said.

"The other end is *everywhere*," Kofi said, and his voice cracked on it, and he pushed through anyway, sixteen years old and carrying more history in his body than the rest of them combined. "You don't understand, none of you understand, because for you this started three weeks ago. For me it started before I could swim. There is no place on this river where that chest is safe. There is only the place where it *matters*."

Marguerite looked at the card on the chart table, at the single number embossed there like a hook waiting for a mouth. She thought about running tonight, dark, no lights, Bo's engine and Ines's river-sense and Kofi's eyes, the *Providence* swallowed by a thousand miles of brown water. She thought about how Sable had said her name. She thought about the fact that she could not remember, now, whether she had ever actually been invisible at all, or had only never before been worth finding.

"We don't answer the card," she said at last. "We don't run tonight either—that's what he'll be watching for. We run tomorrow, midday, in full view, like a fuel stop and nothing more. And we change the route." She pulled the chart toward her and began, with a grease pencil, to draw. "Nobody decides anything about that chest until we're a hundred miles from this town. And from now on—" she looked up, at each of them, and heard the old doctrine die in her mouth, "—nobody on this boat is alone. Not on deck, not ashore, not for five minutes. Whatever's leaking, it leaks through people. So we stop being findable one at a time."

Outside, the river went on with its long indifferent work, carrying everything it knew toward the sea, and Marguerite stood at the window of her own wheelhouse and watched the dark come down over Kessoum, and understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones like cold water into a boot, that somewhere out in that dark, someone was already counting the miles to them.
