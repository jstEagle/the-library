## 8. Night Run

Bo Okonkwo had a rule about engines: they told you the truth before anyone else did. People lied. Charts lied. The navy lied so fluently it had probably stopped noticing. But an engine, if you kept your ear against the right part of it, would confess everything—every worn bearing, every cracked mount, every lie of fuel mixture and compression. He had spent thirty years listening to machines confess, and in all that time he had learned one other thing worth knowing: you could forgive an engine almost anything except silence.

The *Marta J.* was silent now, drifting on the black water two bends above the Sere Rapids, and Bo hated it.

He crouched in the engine room with a hooded lamp turned down to a coal-glow, his palm flat against the housing of the main diesel like a man taking a pulse. Around him the boat breathed—the tick of cooling metal, the slosh in the bilge, Ines's footsteps overhead soft as rain. He had killed the engine twenty minutes ago because sound carried three miles across flat water at night, and somewhere behind them, at the confluence where the border river widened into the navy's patrol reach, there was a checkpoint with a floodlight array and a radio mast and men whose orders, he was increasingly certain, had nothing to do with customs.

"Talk to me," he murmured to the engine, though what he meant was *forgive me for what I'm about to do.*

The plan—if Marguerite's plan deserved the word—was simple in the way that things are simple right up until they kill you. The navy checkpoint sat at the narrows below Kessel Bend, where the channel pinched between rock walls and every vessel on the river had to pass within a hundred meters of the searchlight platform. Upstream of the narrows, the river split. The main channel went left, wide and slow and lit like a stage. The right branch went through the Sere Rapids—a mile and a half of broken water, boulder gardens, and a drop that the pilot charts marked with a notation Bo had read four times just to be sure he'd read it correctly: **NOT RECOMMENDED FOR VESSELS OVER 12 METERS. NOT RECOMMENDED AT NIGHT. NOT RECOMMENDED.**

The *Marta J.* was fourteen meters.

But she drew shallow, and she was built like a stubborn woman, and Bo had rebuilt her heart with his own hands two winters ago, and Marguerite had stood at the chart table that evening and said, in the flat voice she used when she had already decided something, "We go right."

Nobody had argued. That was the strange part. Ines had looked at Kofi, and Kofi had looked at the deck, and Bo had looked at the engine, and somehow the argument had already happened inside each of them and come out the same answer. Bo understood why. It wasn't courage. It was the chest.

He hadn't seen inside it. None of them had, not really—not whole—but he had helped carry it from the flooded hold, and a lead-lined box has a weight that gets into your arms and stays there. And he had seen Kofi's face when the lid came open far enough for the lamplight to find the photographs. Bo was sixty-one years old and had buried a wife and outlived two wars' worth of rumors, and he thought he was past the age when a face could change anything. Then Kofi had gone white as paper over those photographs, and Bo had understood that whatever was in the chest was not cargo. It was a debt. Somebody, somewhere, had paid for it with their lives, chained in a hold while the river came in through holes punched from the inside, and the least the living owed the dead was to not hand them back to the people who had done it.

So: the rapids. At night. Without lights.

Bo wiped his hands on a rag that had been clean in another decade and climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse.

* * *

Marguerite stood at the helm in the dark, and Bo had learned to read her by silhouette the way he read engines by sound. Tonight her shoulders were set forward, weight into the wheel, the way she stood when she was diving—leaning into the water instead of away from it.

"Moonset's in forty minutes," she said without turning. "After that it's full dark until the checkpoint changes shift around two. We hit the head of the rapids at moonset or we don't hit them at all."

"The engine will start," Bo said. "That's not the problem."

"What's the problem?"

"The problem is everything after the first hundred meters." He came to stand beside her at the window. Below them the river ran silver-black, deceptively calm, the way a liar looks right before he lies. "I've been down the Sere twice, daylight, on a boat half this size. There's a chute at the top you enter blind—you commit before you can see the bottom of it. Then a boulder field where the water braids into three channels, and only one of them is deep enough for us, and which one changes with the season. Then the drop. Two meters, maybe more, over a sill. After that it opens out."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I steer by sound and memory and prayer, in that order." He said it lightly, because someone had to, and it wasn't going to be Marguerite, and it wasn't fair to make it Kofi.

Ines came down the companionway with a thermos of coffee thick enough to patch hull plate, pouring four cups without asking. She handed one to Kofi, who had appeared in the doorway the way teenagers do—suddenly, silently, having heard everything and pretending otherwise.

"You should be below," Marguerite said.

"With respect," Kofi said, "no."

Bo watched Marguerite weigh it. Three years ago—before Tomas, before the accident that Bo knew about only as a shape in Marguerite's silence, a hole below her waterline that she never let anyone survey—she would have ordered the boy below and locked the hatch. Now she looked at him for a long moment and said, "Then you're on the bow line. You do exactly what I say, exactly when I say it, and nothing else."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Kofi." Her voice changed, dropped into something quieter. "If we hit, you get to the high bank. You don't come back for the boat. You don't come back for the chest. You swim for the trees and you keep walking south until you find people. Promise me."

Kofi didn't answer right away, and Bo saw the boy's jaw work, seventeen years old trying to hold down a grown man's refusal. Finally: "I promise," he said, in the tone of someone who intended to break it, and Marguerite must have heard it too, because she turned back to the wheel and said nothing more, and Bo drank his coffee and thought: *this is what a family is. People making promises they mean to break out of love.*

* * *

They started the engine at moonset.

It caught on the second crank, and Bo pressed his ear to the housing and listened to it settle into its idle rhythm, and for a moment, in the dark of the engine room, he allowed himself the small vanity of pride. Thirty years of diesels, and he still loved the moment of catching—that stutter, that roar, that settling into a heartbeat. The *Marta J.*'s heart was strong. Whether it would survive what he was about to ask of it was a different question.

He throttled up to half and felt the boat gather herself beneath him, the hull coming alive the way a horse comes alive when it feels the rider decide. Above him, boots crossed the deck. A rope creaked. Then Marguerite's voice, pitched low but carrying through the deck plates the way her voice always did, as if the boat itself were listening:

"Kill the running lights. Bo—on my mark, full ahead. We go in fast. Speed is depth."

That last was an old river truth: in broken water, a boat moving fast rides higher, answers her helm quicker, spends less time in the places that eat slow boats. It was also, Bo reflected, a completely insane thing to do blind. But the navy's floodlights were visible now off the port quarter, a white smear against the sky at Kessel Bend, and the choice between insane and caught wasn't a choice at all.

"Mark," Marguerite said.

Bo pushed the throttle to the stop.

The *Marta J.* surged, wallowed, found her stride. The banks began to slide past faster than Bo liked, black shapes resolving into trees and then dissolving again. Ahead, faintly, the river made a new sound—a long, low, continuous rumble, like the earth clearing its throat. The Sere. He had heard it both times in daylight and it had sounded like danger. Tonight it sounded like a door closing behind them.

Marguerite cut the engine to idle at the head of the chute.

In the sudden quiet, Bo heard everything: the water ahead, dropping away; the hiss of it over rocks he couldn't see; the groan of the current taking hold of the hull, swinging the bow, patient as a hand on a shoulder. He moved to the starboard side of the wheelhouse where he could hear best, cupped his hand behind his ear like a fool, an old man's superstition, and listened.

The chute ran along the left wall. He could hear it—the deeper note, the hollow boom of water over the sill—and to the right, a thinner, angrier hiss where the shallows broke over stone.

"Left third of the channel," he said. "Follow the boom. Stay out of the hiss."

"I hear it," Marguerite said. She did; she had diver's ears, calibrated by years of listening to rivers through three meters of brown water. She rang the bell—full ahead—and drove them into the dark.

* * *

What Bo remembered afterward, all his life, was not fear. Fear came later, in the engine room, in the form of temperature readings and the smell of hot metal. What he remembered from the wheelhouse was beauty, which frightened him more.

The chute took them and the world became noise. Water stood up on either side of the boat—white shapes in the starless dark, ghosts rising and collapsing. Spray came over the bow and hit the wheelhouse windows and ran down in ropes. Somewhere aft, Kofi was on the bow line, a small figure lashed to the rail, and Bo heard him whoop once—a single wild, involuntary yell of a boy riding something enormous—and heard Ines shout at him without any real anger in it, and heard Marguerite say, "Steady," to no one, to the boat, to herself.

Then the boulder field.

The boom of the deep channel split into echoes, bouncing off stone, and Bo's careful map of sound dissolved. He pressed his ear to the wheelhouse frame, eyes shut, sweating despite the cold spray, sorting the noise the way he sorted engine knock from valve clatter. Left: hiss, shallow. Center: a grinding undertone, gravel bar, death. Right—

Right there was a gap in the sound. A pocket of quiet water, deep enough to swallow their noise, and beyond it the boom resumed.

"Hard right," he said. "Now. Then hard left on my word."

Marguerite didn't question it. The wheel spun; the boat leaned; the hull shuddered as the current grabbed her stern and tried to swing her broadside into the gravel, and for three seconds—Bo counted them, he would count them for the rest of his life—the *Marta J.* was not a boat but a leaf, and then the current let go and she answered the helm and slid into the quiet pocket, and Bo said "left," and she came left, and the boom swelled under the keel like a cathedral organ finding its lowest note.

"Deep water," he said. "Ride it."

The drop came without warning, because at night everything comes without warning. The bow rose, hung—a full second of weightlessness, the engine racing free—and then the world fell fourteen meters of steel and five souls and one lead-lined chest two meters down the face of the sill, and the river closed over the bow, and the *Marta J.* punched through the standing wave at the bottom and came out the other side shipping half the Sere Rapids in her well deck.

Below, in the engine room, Bo would later find the raw-water intake strainer half-clogged with grit, the bilge pump running hot, and one engine mount bolt sheared clean through. But in that moment, in the wheelhouse, there was only the sudden widening of the river, the fading of the boom into a broad, deep murmur, and the four of them standing in the dark, drenched, breathing like divers surfacing.

Behind them, small and bright and useless, the navy's floodlights burned at Kessel Bend, illuminating an empty channel.

* * *

They drifted for a mile before anyone spoke.

It was Ines who finally laughed—a short, disbelieving bark—and then Kofi, and then, worst of all, Bo himself, helplessly, the laugh of a man whose body has just informed him it is still alive and doesn't quite believe the report. Marguerite didn't laugh. She stood at the wheel with her hands loose on the spokes, and in the darkness Bo saw her lift one hand and press it briefly, flat, against the wheelhouse frame, the way he touched his engine. Taking a pulse. Thanking something.

"Bo," she said. "Report."

"She held. Barely." He gave it honestly, because lying to Marguerite Okafor about machinery was like lying to a priest about sin—pointless, and she'd find out anyway. "Sheared a mount bolt. Strainer's choked. Bilge pump's working harder than I like. I can patch all of it by morning if we find slack water, but she shouldn't take water like that again for a week. Maybe ever."

"Then we won't ask her to," Marguerite said, and Bo heard in her voice the thing he had been slowly recognizing for weeks: that she had stopped thinking of the *Marta J.* as equipment. They all had. The boat had become what it became on boats like this, on long rivers, among people with nowhere else to be—the fifth member of the crew, the one who couldn't speak but told the truth.

They tied up at dawn in a backwater Bo knew from his freight-running days, a slough behind a willow island where the current forgot about you. While the light came up gray and gentle, he stripped the sheared mount, re-shimmed the engine on a hardwood block he'd been carrying for six years against exactly this kind of morning, and cleared the strainer while Ines cooked breakfast that smelled, impossibly, like a home none of them had. Kofi bailed the well deck and wrung out the lines and talked the entire time, a bright unbroken stream of words about the wave, the drop, the moment the bow hung in the air—"a full second, Bo, I counted"—and nobody told him to be quiet, because everyone aboard understood that the talking was how the boy put the fear down and picked up something else.

Bo worked with his arms in the engine's guts and listened to all of it—the boy's chatter, Ines humming, Marguerite's footsteps on deck doing the rounds she never skipped even exhausted—and he thought about his wife, dead nine years now, who had told him once that he loved engines because they were the only family that never disappointed him. She'd meant it as a wound. Standing in the warm smell of diesel and coffee and frying plantain, Bo understood that she had simply been wrong, or early. Families were not the thing you were born into or married into. A family was what assembled itself around a shared weight, when enough people carried it together long enough. He had spent his life believing he traveled light. He had never in fact traveled alone since the day he signed onto this boat.

Near noon, Marguerite came down the ladder and sat on the step beside the engine, close enough that he could see the tiredness under her eyes, and watched him torque the last bolt.

"Holds?" she asked.

"Holds." He wiped his hands. "For what it's worth—I'd have said no, three months ago. To all of this. The contract, the secrecy clauses, the chest. I took this job for the money and I'll say so plainly. But somewhere between the hold with the chains and the rapids last night, it stopped being a job." He looked at her. "I'm telling you so you know. Whatever comes next—Reyes, the checkpoints, whoever's buying and whoever's burying—I'm not here for the pay anymore. I'm here because these are my people and that's our chest."

Marguerite was quiet a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and Bo saw her throat move as she swallowed whatever she might have said, and she put her hand on his shoulder—brief, hard, the closest thing to speech she had for feelings—and went back up the ladder.

Bo stayed below a minute longer, his palm resting on the engine block, feeling it tick as it cooled.

That evening, as they cast off into the golden water below the rapids, the radio—which they kept on scan, low, for weather—crackled with a fragment of traffic from the naval band. Bo caught only pieces before Marguerite snapped it off, but the pieces were enough: a patrol designation, the word *checkpoint*, and then, clipped and official, a name that made the whole wheelhouse go still.

*"…be advised, subject vessel may be operating under alias. Officer Reyes has operational authority. All units…"*

Bo looked at Marguerite. She was looking at the radio as if it were a hole that had opened in the hull, and her face had gone to the place it went when she dove deep—the place past expression, where the pressure lived.

"Reyes," Ines said quietly. "Who's Reyes?"

Marguerite didn't answer. She turned to the wheel and rang the bell for full ahead, and the *Marta J.* leaned into the current and ran south, and Bo went below to listen to his engine tell him the truth, which was that it would hold, barely, the way all of them would hold—barely, together, for as long as the river demanded.
