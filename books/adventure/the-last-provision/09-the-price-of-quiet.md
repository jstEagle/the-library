## 9. The Price of Quiet

Ines had learned to read boats the way other people read faces, and the *Marta Luz* was lying.

Not in the way engines lie—a rattle that means a bearing is dying, a vibration that means the shaft has gone true out of alignment. Boats lied like people lied: in small omissions, in the things they carried below decks and pretended not to. For six days now the lead-lined chest had sat under the tarp in the forward hold, wrapped in a cargo net, weighted with chain, and every person aboard had arranged themselves around it the way water arranges itself around a stone. Nobody said its name. Everybody steered around it.

Ines said nothing either. She cooked. She dove when Marguerite asked her to dive. She kept Kofi fed and busy and away from the radio, which was its own kind of confession, because there was no reason on God's green river to keep a sixteen-year-old away from a radio unless you were afraid of what he might hear—or say.

But tonight, with the boat tied up in a backwater of flooded gallery forest where the fireflies came down to the water like falling stars, and Bo asleep in the engine room with his boots still on, and Kofi curled in the bow under a mosquito net like a chrysalis—tonight Ines found Marguerite at the stern with a cigarette she wasn't smoking, just holding, letting it burn down between her fingers, and Ines decided the boat had lied long enough.

She set two cups of coffee on the deck plates, sat down across from Marguerite without asking, and waited. She was good at waiting. She had learned it in a place where waiting was the only thing they let you do.

Marguerite looked at the coffee. Looked at her. "It's late."

"It is." Ines wrapped both hands around her cup. The night was warm but the river breathed cold off the water, and the steam rose between them like something trying to escape. "I want to talk about the boy."

Something moved behind Marguerite's eyes—the quick shuttered look of a woman recalculating. Ines had seen it before, on dive boats and worse places, on people who thought three moves ahead as a survival habit. It never fooled her. She had grown up among people who did arithmetic with their lives.

"What about him?"

"You know what about him."

"Ines—"

"He sleeps ten feet from that chest." Ines kept her voice low, level, the voice she'd used to talk frightened soldiers down from ledges of their own making. "He eats breakfast next to it. He helps you wrap the net around it every time we move, and he hums while he does it, because he thinks if he hums it's just salvage, it's just work, it's just another job for another client who doesn't want his name on anything. And every mile we go downriver with it, we are painting a target on his back. On Bo's back. On mine. On yours. You know it. I've watched you knowing it for six days."

The cigarette had burned to the filter. Marguerite looked at it as though surprised to find it there, and dropped it over the side, where it hissed once and died.

"Say what you came to say," Marguerite said.

"I'm saying it." Ines set her cup down. "We give it up. We find a way—Sable, the liaison, drop it in the river in a hundred meters of silt, I don't care. We make it not ours anymore. Because right now it's ours, and everything that comes for it comes through us, and the first one it comes through will be Kofi, because he's the smallest and the slowest and the one who trusts us enough to sleep."

For a long moment Marguerite didn't answer. A fish broke the surface somewhere out in the black, a soft gulp of sound, and the fireflies rearranged themselves.

"You think I haven't run it?" Marguerite said finally. Her voice was flat, stripped of command, and that was how Ines knew this was going to be different from all their other conversations. "Every night since we raised it. Drop it deep, tell Reyes we found nothing, take the money and disappear. I've run it. I've run it so many times I could navigate it blind."

"Then why—"

"Because I opened it." Marguerite turned and looked at her straight on, and Ines saw that whatever was in her face had been waiting there for days, held down by force, and was done being held. "Before Kofi. Before any of you. The second night, alone, at anchor, I cut the seal myself. I told you all we should wait, decide together—and that was a lie too, because I'd already decided. I needed to know what I was carrying before I asked anyone else to carry it."

Ines said nothing. She had known. She had watched Marguerite come up from the hold that night with wet hair and a face like a woman returning from a country with no embassy, and she had known, and she had said nothing, and the silence between them had been complicity ever since.

"Manifests," Marguerite went on, quietly. "Shipping orders. Photographs. Train schedules for deportations, Ines. Requisitions for lime. Do you know what they requisitioned lime for? There were camps on my maps that were marked as agricultural stations. There were photographs of a yard with a wall around it, and I have dived rivers my whole life, and I have pulled cars and guns and bodies out of the water, and I have never—" Her breath caught, and she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, and Ines suddenly hated how well she did it. "I have never seen anything that was put in the water on purpose to stay there. That chest wasn't lost. It was sunk by men who wanted it forgotten, and everyone on that courier boat was chained in the hold so they'd go down with it and keep the secret. And I fished it up. I dug it out of the dark and brought it into the light, and now it exists again, and I can't—I can't be the one who puts it back."

"So you'd rather it cost us," Ines said. Not cruelly. She needed it said plainly, the way you needed a wound cleaned plainly.

"I'd rather it cost me." Marguerite's jaw tightened. "That was the plan. Me. My risk. I'm the one Reyes wants, I'm the one Sable smells, I'm the one whose name is on the contract. But I know how it works. I've known since the night we raised it. Risk isn't a thing you can carry alone, no matter how you rig the load. It distributes. Like pressure. Like depth." She laughed, short and airless. "You want to talk about targets? I'll tell you about targets. Let me tell you why I count miles."

And Ines, who had been braced for argument, for the cold professional logic Marguerite wore like a drysuit, understood that she was about to be given something Marguerite had given no one—not Bo, not Kofi, not the navy man whose name surfaced sometimes in her sleep. She sat very still, the way you sat still in a pirogue in crocodile water, and listened.

"Three years ago I ran a two-man operation. Me and Tomas Ferreira. Salvage, mostly—same as now, smaller. He was my dive partner for four years. He was—" Marguerite stopped. Started again. "He was the only person who ever made quiet feel like company instead of punishment. You'll understand what that costs me to say."

"I understand," Ines said softly.

"We took a contract on a wreck off a shipping channel. Deep-ish, cold, bad visibility. Routine. Except the wreck was unstable, and I knew it, and I pushed anyway, because the client was paying by the day and I had a rule then too—my invisibility rule—but underneath it I had something worse. Pride. I wanted to be the best small operator on that whole river system, and I wanted Tomas to see it." Her hands had closed around the coffee cup hard enough that Ines heard the ceramic creak. "We went down together. The section I chose to work was load-bearing. I knew the signs—fresh scouring at the base, sediment pluming when we touched down. Every instinct I had said move to the other side of the hull. And I said, five more minutes. I said it twice. The second time, the collapse came."

The fireflies drifted. Somewhere in the flooded forest a night bird called, three notes, like a question nobody answered.

"The deck came down on him. Not on me. I was two meters outside the fall line—two meters, Ines, because I hesitated and he didn't, because he saw it first and shoved me clear, and the same current of water that threw me clear took him under the slab." Marguerite's voice didn't break. It went thin and even, which was worse. "I dug for forty minutes on a rebreather built for twenty-five. I dug until my vision narrowed and my hands were meat, and then my regulator started giving me that sweet taste it gives you before it fails, and I surfaced, and he didn't. They never recovered him. The river kept him. So now I count miles instead of years, because years are what you get when you can live forward, and I couldn't. Miles are just distance. Distance doesn't ask you what you're running from."

Ines reached over and put her hand over Marguerite's, over the straining knuckles, and felt the tremor there that the voice would never permit. She left it. Some tremors you don't stop; you just witness.

"And the accident," Ines said. "You said you caused it."

"I caused it." No flinch. Marguerite had clearly rehearsed this sentence in the dark for three years, and delivering it aloud cost her less than Ines expected and more than she could measure. "My call. My site. My five more minutes. The inquiry cleared me—operator error attributable to conditions, no negligence proven. Everyone was very kind. Kindness was the worst part. I sold the boat, changed my name on the licenses, moved to a stretch of river where nobody knew the name Okafor, and I built a life out of jobs where nobody asks questions, and I told myself it was discipline. Professional discretion. A diver keeps her head down." She shook her head slowly. "It was penance wearing discipline's clothes. I made myself invisible because I believed I deserved to be erased. And then the navy offered me money to stay invisible on a restricted reach, and I took it, because invisible was the only shape I knew how to be."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting here telling you that I fished a massacre out of a river and I can't put it back." Marguerite finally unclenched her hands and turned one palm up under Ines's, an old gesture of surrender that divers used, showing empty hands. "You're right. About all of it. Keeping the chest paints a target on everyone aboard, and Kofi is the smallest target and the dearest one, and I have been telling myself for six days that I can manage the risk, and that is exactly the sentence I said before the deck came down. I can manage it. Five more minutes. I know how that sentence ends."

Ines exhaled, long and slow. She had come armed for a fight, and instead she'd been handed a wound, and she found she didn't know where to put it. She looked out at the black water, at the drowned forest standing hip-deep in its own reflection, and thought about the camps she herself had walked out of—the ones with fences, not water—and how the survivors she'd trusted most were always the ones who could say the worst sentence about themselves out loud without looking away.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me yet." Marguerite almost smiled. It was a battered thing. "Telling you doesn't solve it. I still don't know what to do."

"Yes, you do." Ines squeezed her hand once and let go. "You just can't say it yet, because saying it makes it real, and real things can be taken from you. You learned that." She stood, joints cracking, forty-three years old and feeling every flood season of them. "So I'll say it for you. We take it to Balogun. To the delta. To the journalist. We put it in print where no navy and no fixer and no government can sink it twice. And we do it knowing what it costs, all of us, together—which is the only way I've ever survived anything worth surviving."

Marguerite looked up at her for a long moment. The firefly light caught the wet at the corners of her eyes, or maybe it was river spray; on the water you learned not to ask which.

"When?" Marguerite said.

"Tomorrow. At first light, we vote—all of us, including the boy. He gets a voice. If we're spending his blood, he gets a vote." Ines picked up the empty cups. "And Marguerite. When we vote yes—and we will—you stop steering around that chest like it's your sin. It's not yours alone anymore. That's the whole point of a crew."

She was halfway to the cabin door when the radio on the bulkhead crackled.

Both of them froze. It was past midnight; the channel was supposed to be silent; Bo slept with the set powered down. But the speaker hissed, popped, and resolved into a voice—smooth, amused, unhurried, a voice Ines had heard for exactly ninety seconds at a riverport dock and had spent every night since trying to forget.

"Evening, Captain Marchetti," Sable said, warm as engine oil. "Lovely anchorage. Very secluded. I do hope you're getting some rest—you'll want your strength for the conversation we're going to have tomorrow. Oh, and do give my regards to the cook. She makes wonderful coffee. I could smell it from the channel."

* * *

Ines set the cups down very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them.

Marguerite was already moving—killing the lamp, checking the tree line through the porthole, her body shifting into a register Ines hadn't seen in her before, something older than professionalism. Out in the black water, far downstream where the channel bent, a single navigation light blinked twice and went dark.

"He's not here," Marguerite murmured. "He's showing us he could be."

"No," Ines said quietly, watching the spot where the light had been. "He's showing us he's already been watching."

Above them, faint through the deck plates, Kofi turned over in his sleep, humming.
