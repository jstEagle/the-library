## 10. Dead Drop

The rain had been falling on Porte-Cendre for three days, and Commander Elias Reyes had stopped noticing it somewhere around the second morning, the way a man stops noticing a toothache that has settled in for good. It came down in gray sheets off the harbor, sheeting the windows of the customs house where he'd established his temporary command, turning the river into something featureless and vast, a single color from bank to bank — the color of old tin, of gun metal, of the inside of a coffin.

He stood at the window with a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking and watched a lighter barge wallow downstream under too much canvas, and he thought about drowning.

Not his own. He'd made his peace with water years ago; a naval officer who feared it was in the wrong trade. He thought about a specific drowning, three years gone now, on a stretch of the upper Sere that he could still draw from memory with his eyes closed — every bend, every sandbar, every drowned tree standing in the shallows like the masts of a sunken fleet. The report had come to him secondhand, the way everything about Marguerite Okafor's death had come to him secondhand: a salvage boat lost, two divers down, one recovered alive, one not. *Okafor, M.* The name had sat on the page for a long time before he understood what he was reading. He had read it eleven times. He had counted.

And then, four months ago, a contract crossed his desk with her company's registration number on it, and the world had tilted sideways and never quite righted itself.

*She's alive.* Three words he still couldn't say aloud. He'd built his whole grief on them being false — the funerals he hadn't attended because he couldn't bear them, the letters he'd written and burned, the bottle of wine he'd bought on the anniversary and poured into the harbor like a libation to a woman who apparently hadn't needed one. She was alive, and she was diving a restricted wreck under his authority, and she didn't know he was the officer behind the liaison's signature, and some mornings this fact amused him in a way that frightened him, because amusement was the first symptom of a man losing his grip on what he was supposed to do.

What he was supposed to do was simple. That was the obscenity of it. The orders were one page long and perfectly clear: recover the sealed chest from the courier vessel *Vigilant*, transport it to the naval depot at Kessel Point, log nothing, speak to no one outside the chain. The word *recover* was doing quiet work in that sentence. Not *inspect*. Not *document*. Recover, as in take back, as in make as though it had never surfaced at all.

Reyes had spent twenty-two years in the navy learning to read the white space between the lines of an order, and the white space around this one was screaming.

Behind him, the door opened. Lieutenant Achebe came in shaking rain off his cap, a signal flimsy in his hand, and Reyes turned from the window with the particular stillness he'd learned to wear like armor — the stillness of a man who has already decided how surprised he is going to look.

"Sir. Harbor watch reports movement at the fish market. Our friend."

"Our friend." Reyes let the words sit. "Sable?"

"Yes, sir. Third time this week. He's meeting someone at the smokehouse on the north quay — the one owned by the Ferreira family, if that means anything."

"It means the Ferreiras will deny owning it by Friday." Reyes set down the untouched coffee. "Alone?"

"One bodyguard, maybe two. He travels light when he's buying."

When he's buying. Reyes took the flimsy and read it twice, though it said little more than Achebe had. Sable. The fixer had appeared in their operational picture six weeks ago like mold spreading through bread — first a rumor out of Vellacourt, then a name attached to a wire transfer, then a face, smooth and pleasant and forgettable by design, asking questions in riverports about salvage contracts on the middle Sere. Asking, specifically, about a chest. The intelligence section had flagged him as a commercial broker with regime ties; the regime, after all, was the only client with money enough to care about paper sunk twenty years ago. But flags were cheap. Reyes wanted the man himself. He wanted to hear what voice a man used when he sold other people's dead.

"Get the car," Reyes said. "No escort. If we roll up looking like a tribunal, he'll be a priest giving last rites by the time we reach him."

Achebe hesitated, which Achebe always did, which was why Reyes trusted him. "Sir, the directive says—"

"The directive says recover the chest at any cost. It doesn't say how I spend my afternoons." He pulled his rain cloak from the hook. "Come on, Lieutenant. Let's go buy something ourselves."

* * *

The smokehouse on the north quay had been a church once, or part of one — you could still see the arch of a window high in the gable, filled now with wire mesh instead of glass, and the smell that lived in the walls was salt and creosote and herring smoked so long ago the fish themselves had become geology. Reyes had grown up in a port town not unlike this one, forty miles upcoast, and he knew the way such places absorbed history without comment. Buildings here didn't get torn down. They got repurposed, the way people did. The way governments did.

Sable was exactly where the watch had put him, seated at a plank table among hanging racks of fish with a cup of tea steaming in front of him, and he looked, infuriatingly, comfortable. He was a compact man, silvering at the temples, dressed in the kind of clothes that cost more than they appeared to — a fisherman's oilskin over a shirt that had never seen a deck. His bodyguard stood by the door with the studied boredom of a man paid by the hour. When Reyes and Achebe came in dripping, Sable did not startle. He smiled instead, slow and warm, and gestured to the bench across from him as though they'd arranged the whole thing weeks in advance.

"Commander Reyes," he said. "I wondered when you'd come yourself. Your lieutenants are thorough but they lack conversation."

"You know my name." Reyes remained standing. Rain ran off the hem of his cloak onto the flagstones, darkening them in a spreading stain.

"I know everyone's name. It's the only asset I own that can't be seized." Sable lifted his tea, considered it, set it down untasted. "Sit, please. You're making the fish nervous."

Reyes sat. Achebe stayed by the door, and the bodyguard and the lieutenant regarded each other with the mutual professional courtesy of men whose jobs might require them to hurt each other later.

"I'm going to be direct," Reyes said, "because I suspect indirection is your native language and I don't want to give you home-field advantage."

"A wise instinct."

"There's a chest. Lead-lined, stamped with the Veltanian crest, raised from the *Vigilant* by a civilian salvage crew under contract to this office. You've been asking about it in every port between here and the falls. I want to know who sent you, what you're authorized to offer, and what happens to the information if I decline to sell."

Sable's smile deepened by a degree, the smile of a teacher watching a student arrive at the correct answer by the wrong road. "All three questions at once. You are direct." He folded his hands on the table. They were small hands, neat, the nails clean. "Very well. Since you've been honest with me, I'll be honest with you, and you may be the last person in this country I say this to, so savor it. I wasn't sent to buy the chest, Commander. I was sent to find out whether it still exists."

"And does it?"

"That depends entirely on your salvage crew, doesn't it? On a certain captain." Sable watched him with eyes that gave away nothing and missed nothing, and Reyes felt the trap close around his own face before he could stop it — some fractional tightening, some shift of weight, some tell he'd carried since boyhood and never trained out. Sable saw it. Sable catalogued it. "Ah," the fixer said softly. "You know her."

"I know all contractors operating under my authority."

"You knew her before she had an authority to operate under." Sable leaned back against the smoke-blackened wall, unhurried, a man who had decided the meeting would last as long as he wanted it to last. "Marguerite Okafor. Drowned three years ago, according to the record. Resurrected four months ago, according to a contract filing. Do you know what I love about rivers, Commander? Everything that goes into them comes back out eventually. Bodies. Boats. Chests. Truths. The river keeps nothing forever. It just holds things until it's ready."

"Who sent you?" Reyes kept his voice level, but he heard the effort in it, and hated the effort.

Sable studied him for a long moment, and whatever calculation ran behind those pleasant eyes, it ended in something almost like generosity — or its counterfeit, which was indistinguishable and cheaper. "I represent parties interested in the historical record," he said. "Exiles. Survivors. People who lost a country and would like, at minimum, to lose it accurately. There are documents in that chest, Commander. Orders. Manifests. Photographs. The paperwork of the deportations — the ones your government inherited rather than committed, or so the story goes. My clients want them. So do others. And others want them destroyed, and those others have better telephones than I do." He paused. "Including, I believe, yours."

The rain hammered the roof. Somewhere out on the quay a winch groaned through its gears, hauling something heavy and unseen.

"My government," Reyes said carefully, "inherited a border. Not a massacre."

"No." Sable agreed with him instantly, warmly, which was worse than disagreement. "Of course not. And yet the orders in that chest — the transit manifests, the camp requisitions, the fuel allocations for the crematoria barges — they carry two signatures at the bottom of every page. One belongs to a Veltanian interior minister who died in a Geneva hotel room in '09, very conveniently. The other—" Sable reached into his oilskin and produced a photograph, laid it on the table between them with two fingers, and slid it across, "—you'll recognize. Everyone recognizes him. He's your Minister of State now. He signed the requisition for Camp Tereza's winter fuel. Forty-one tons. Enough to burn eleven thousand people, give or take the ones who died of cold first."

Reyes looked down at the photograph.

It was a copy of a document, grainy, third-generation, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. An allocation form. Bureaucratic columns. And there at the bottom, unmistakable even through the blur, the confident slanted hand he had seen a thousand times on ministerial letterhead, on commissioning ceremonies, on the dedication plaque of the very depot where he was ordered to deliver the chest. He knew that signature the way he knew his own reflection. He had saluted beneath portraits bearing it.

The floor of the smokehouse seemed to tilt a degree and settle. Reyes found that his hands, resting on the table, had gone very still — the stillness of a man keeping himself physically together by main force while something structural gives way inside.

"You understand my problem now," Sable said gently. "My clients want justice, or memory, or leverage — depends which client, they blur. Your government wants silence. And you, Commander, are ordered to recover the chest at any cost and deliver it to the depot at Kessel Point, where it will be inventoried by men who answer to the second signature on those pages. You are the instrument of the erasure. That's a hard thing to learn about oneself over smoked herring. I apologize for the venue."

"How do you have this photograph." It wasn't really a question. Reyes heard his own voice as if from across the table, belonging to someone calmer.

"Because copies exist. Because the people who typed those orders kept carbon paper, the way bureaucrats everywhere keep insurance. Because eleven thousand people went into the winter at Tereza and eleven thousand people had relatives, and relatives are patient." Sable retrieved the photograph, held it a moment, and then — deliberately, watching Reyes's face — tore it in half, and in half again, and dropped the pieces into his teacup. "I show you so you understand I'm not bluffing. I destroy it so you understand I don't need it. There are more. There are always more. Paper is the one thing the dead learned to do well."

Reyes sat motionless. Through the wire-meshed window above, the gray light of the harbor came down in bars, and in one of those bars, dust moved like something swimming.

Twenty-two years. He thought about twenty-two years — the academy, the border patrols, the flood relief in '14 where he'd carried children out of attics two at a time, the commendations, the quiet pride of a man who believed the uniform meant the country, and the country, whatever its sins, meant something better than this. He thought about the oath. He tried to remember its exact wording and found he could only remember the feeling of it, which was worse, because feelings could be reinterpreted and words could not.

"What happens to her," he said, "if I follow my orders."

Sable spread his small neat hands. "Nothing. Probably. The chest goes to Kessel Point, the contract is quietly terminated, the crew is paid and scattered, and Captain Okafor returns to the anonymity she's been hiding in — unless she's seen something she shouldn't have, in which case accidents happen on rivers all the time. You know that better than anyone. You've read the accident reports. You wrote some of them." He picked up his tea, stirred the drowned photograph with one finger, and drank. "But you're not going to follow your orders, are you, Commander. I can see it. You have the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"And what do you want from me?"

"Nothing today. Today I wanted you to know what you're carrying, and for whom. Consider it a gift. My clients believe an informed officer is more useful than a loyal one." Sable rose, buttoned the oilskin, and paused beside Reyes's shoulder long enough to lower his voice to something almost kind. "One piece of free advice, from a man who trades in everything. When the moment comes — and it's coming, the river's narrowing — decide quickly what you can live with. Everything else is negotiable. That's the whole secret of my profession, and I'll give it to you gratis: everything is negotiable except what you can live with."

Then he was gone, out into the rain, the bodyguard folding after him like a shadow closing, and the door swung and let in a gust of harbor smell — diesel, brine, wet rope — and then the smokehouse was quiet except for the rain and the slow drip of water from the fish racks.

* * *

They walked back along the quay without speaking. Achebe waited until they were in the car, wipers thrashing, before he said anything, and Reyes was grateful for the discipline of it.

"Sir. What he told us—"

"Is unverified."

"Yes, sir. Unverified." Achebe's hands were steady on the wheel; his voice was not quite. "But if it's true, sir, and we bring that chest to Kessel Point—"

"Then we deliver the evidence to the arsonist and call it fire prevention." Reyes watched the quay slide past, crates and cranes and tarps snapping in the wind, everything gray within gray. "I know what I said, Lieutenant."

"I didn't ask what you said, sir. I asked what you're going to do."

Reyes was silent for a long time. The road followed the river, and the river ran high and brown with the rain, carrying everything toward the delta — silt, branches, the unclaimed dead of a hundred years, all of it moving in one direction with terrible patience. He thought about the arithmetic Sable had named. He had been doing it for days, actually, weeks, since the first survey report came back noting anomalies in the hull plating, since the liaison's routine updates began arriving with gaps in them shaped exactly like a woman he used to know. He had been telling himself the discomfort was procedural. It was not procedural. It had never been procedural.

There was a version of him that followed orders. That man delivered the chest, filed the report, accepted the promotion that would surely follow, and spent the rest of his career understanding precisely what he was, the way a man understands a scar. There was another version, and that man was already off the map, in unmarked water, and the terrifying thing — the thing that made his chest feel tight and young — was that the second man did not feel like ruin. He felt like the cadet who'd taken the oath. He felt like the flood year, carrying children out of attics.

"Lieutenant," Reyes said at last, "when we locate the salvage vessel, I want approach protocols amended."

"Amended how, sir?"

"No live fire without my explicit authorization. No boarding under weapons present. And if resistance occurs, we withdraw and regroup rather than escalate." He turned and looked at Achebe directly, and let the lieutenant see whatever it was in his face that had changed. "Captain Okafor comes in alive. Whatever else happens — however this ends, whoever it ends for — she comes in alive. That's a concession I'm requesting from my own operation, and I'm putting it in writing, and if it costs me my command, then it costs me my command."

Achebe held his eyes for a moment longer than regulation allowed. Then he nodded, once, and looked back at the road. "Yes, sir. In writing."

The rain thickened. Ahead, the river bent north toward the falls and the middle reaches, toward the marshes and the locks and the long lonely miles where a salvage boat with a hidden hold was running dark through the weather, carrying eleven thousand voices in a lead-lined box. Reyes watched the water and did his arithmetic one final time, and arrived, at last, at a number he could live with.

It was not zero. It was not the number his orders demanded. But it was his.

That night, alone in the customs house, he took out the signal pad and wrote the amendment in his own hand, dated it, signed it. Then he sat for a long while with the pen still uncapped, staring at the blank space below his signature, until finally he added a single line that belonged to no protocol and no file he intended to submit:

*M., if you can hear this — run north. I'm slower than I used to be.*

He did not send it. Not yet. He folded it into his breast pocket, over his heart, where a man keeps the things he hasn't yet become brave enough to mean out loud — and outside, the river kept moving in the dark, patient as paper, carrying everything toward the sea.
