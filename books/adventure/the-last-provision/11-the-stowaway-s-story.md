## 11. The Stowaway's Story

Kofi had never told anyone the whole of it, not even Ines, and she had fished him half-dead out of a drainage canal when he was eleven years old with river mud in his lungs and a number inked on his forearm that he had spent six years trying not to look at.

But tonight the chest sat open on the deck of the *Marta Kay*, lamplight swinging gently with the current, and the photographs were spread across Bo's tarp like a hand of terrible cards, and everyone was looking at him because he was the one who had said the name.

*Serrenholt.*

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had come up his throat like a fish bone when he saw the photograph — the long low sheds, the wire, the water tower with its ladder rungs missing, the same water tower he had climbed at nine years old to see if the horizon held anything besides wire.

"Say it again," Marguerite said quietly. She wasn't looking at the papers anymore. She was looking at him, and her eyes had the stillness she got underwater, the patience of someone who knew how to wait without breathing.

"Serrenholt," Kofi said. "It was a processing camp. On the Veltanian side of the border marshes. They moved people through it toward—" He stopped. His mouth had gone dry as rope. "Toward the trains. And after the trains stopped running, toward the river."

Ines made a small sound. Not quite a word. She pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders even though the night was warm.

Kofi looked down at the photograph again because it was easier than looking at their faces. The paper had gone soft at the corners, swollen slightly from its years inside the lead-lined chest, but the image was sharp enough. A column of people on a levee road. Women with bundles. Children carrying smaller children. Soldiers in the grey-green uniforms he would know anywhere, would know in his sleep, would know if you woke him from drowning.

"That's the north levee," he said. "You can tell by the willows. They cut all the willows down for fuel in the last winter, so this is before that. This is before the last winter."

"How do you know that?" Bo asked. His voice was gentle in the way big men's voices are gentle when they are trying very hard to be.

"Because I carried water past those willows." Kofi touched the edge of the photograph, careful, the way you touch something that might be alive. "Every morning. Two buckets. The guard who counted us used to say I walked too slow and then he'd make me walk it twice."

The lamp swung. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere downstream a night bird called once and gave up.

"I was born in Veltania," Kofi said. "I don't remember much of before. My mother said we lived in a town called Ostrava Bend, and there was a ferry, and my father worked the ferry. Then there was an order — one of these orders, probably, or one like them — and the ferries stopped being ferries." He swallowed. "They put us in trucks. My mother told me it was a holiday. She told me that the whole way. She kept saying it like if she said it enough times it would become true, and I believed her, because I was seven, and because believing her was easier than the other thing."

"What was the other thing?" Ines asked, though Kofi thought she already knew. Ines always asked questions she already knew the answers to. Maybe that was how she survived her own war — by making the world confirm itself.

"The other thing was that she was lying," Kofi said. "And that she knew she was lying. And that she lied anyway because it was the last thing she could give me."

Nobody moved. Even the lamp seemed to swing more slowly, as if the boat itself were listening.

* * *

He told them about Serrenholt in pieces, the way you haul things up from deep water — a little at a time, letting the pressure equalize, because if you come up too fast everything tears.

He told them about the sheds: three hundred people in a building built for eighty, the straw that started clean and stopped being anything, the latrine trench that froze in winter and stank in summer and was the only place you could whisper. He told them about the counting, morning and evening, the guards with their ledger books, and how he understood now — looking at the manifests spread out under the lamp, columns of names in careful bureaucratic script — that the ledger they kept at Serrenholt was probably one of these. That somewhere in this chest there might be a page with his mother's name written in a clerk's tidy hand, reduced to a line in a column, a weight in a shipment.

"Page forty-one," he said suddenly, and reached for the manifest before anyone could stop him, though no one tried.

His hands shook. The paper whispered. He ran his finger down the columns — names, ages, villages, and then a final column headed only *Disposition*, which was such a clean word for what it meant — and he found the block of entries from Ostrava Bend, thirty-one names, and there, fourth from the bottom:

*Amara Okonkwo-Vey, 34.*

"They wrote my name wrong," he heard himself say. "See? *Kofimiri Okonkwo-Vey, 7.* They put me down as a girl. Because my hair was long. Nobody ever fixed it." He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "Somewhere in a government archive there's a record that says a seven-year-old girl named Kofimiri was processed through Serrenholt, and that girl doesn't exist, and I do, and neither fact ever got corrected because nobody expected either of us to matter."

Marguerite reached over and closed her hand around his wrist — not grabbing, just resting, an anchor weight. Her hands were diver's hands, scarred and strong, and they were very warm.

"You matter," she said.

"That's not what the paper says."

"The paper also says the camp had a capacity of four hundred," Marguerite said, nodding at the manifest header. "Papers lie. That's rather the point of this whole chest, isn't it? These documents were written by men who wanted the world to believe nothing happened here. The paper is the lie. You're the truth it was trying to erase."

Kofi looked at her. Three months ago she had been a legend on the docks — the salvage diver who worked alone, who took contracts nobody else wanted, who spoke maybe forty words a day and none of them wasted. He had signed onto her boat because Ines vouched for him and because a berth was a berth. He had not known she was a woman carrying a dead partner like ballast in her chest. He had not known she counted her life in river miles. But he knew it now, the way you come to know a crew, and he knew that she did not say things like *you're the truth* to just anybody.

"So why does it matter?" Bo asked, and his voice cracked on it, and Kofi realized the big engineer was not asking a challenge. He was asking because he needed the answer. Bo had been quiet since the photographs came out, quieter than usual, sitting with his huge hands flat on his knees like a man holding himself down in a wind. "The dead are dead. Twenty years gone. What does digging this up do for them?"

"Nothing," Kofi said. "Nothing at all. That's what everybody gets wrong." He stood up, because he couldn't say this sitting down; his legs wouldn't allow it. "My mother is dead. Everyone in that photograph is dead, or scattered so far they might as well be. Raising this chest doesn't give them back a single hour. I used to think that was the whole argument — that remembering was for the dead, a kind of flowers-on-the-water, and I hated it, because flowers don't feed anybody."

The lamp swung. He steadied it with one hand, and the light went steady across the papers, across the faces turned up toward him: Marguerite with her stillness, Ines with her shawl, Bo with his hands pressed flat, and beyond them the dark river sliding past, carrying everything it carried.

"But it's not for the dead," Kofi said. "It's for the living. Look at the disposition column. Look at the dates. The last shipments out of Serrenholt are dated *after* the armistice. After the war was officially over. After the new government signed the peace accords and promised the world there had been no deportations, no camps, no massacres — that Veltania's troubles were internal matters, regrettable, exaggerated." His voice was rising now and he let it rise. "These aren't records of the war. These are records of the *cover-up*. Somebody on that courier boat was carrying proof that the killing didn't stop when the treaties were signed — it just changed paperwork. And the government that exists today, the one whose navy hired us, the one whose flag is on the patrol boats — that government is the direct heir of the men who signed these orders. That's why the boat was sunk. That's why the stretch of river is restricted. That's why they paid Marguerite off the books and made her sign clauses about silence."

He picked up one of the order sheets — the one Bo had found folded inside the manifest, bearing a ministerial seal and a signature — and held it up.

"This is dated eleven days after the armistice. It authorizes, quote, *final disposition of remaining inventory*. Inventory." His hand was shaking again and he let it shake. "I was inventory. My mother was inventory. There are people alive right now — ministers, generals, respectable old men with medals — who signed papers like this, and they have spent twenty years building a country on top of our graves and calling the graves reclamation projects. And every year the story gets smoother. Every year there are fewer of us left who can stand up and say *no, I was there, I carried the water, I climbed the water tower.*"

He set the paper down. His legs felt strange, too light, as if the deck were tilting.

"Ines pulled me out of a canal when I was eleven," he said. "I'd been walking the border for two years after the camp liberated itself — that's a story for another night, how a camp liberates itself — and I ended up in a country that spoke a different language, where the teachers said Veltania was a troubled region and the war was complicated and it was better to look forward. Better to look forward. Do you know what that means when you're a child? It means *stop existing.* It means your mother goes in a column and the column goes in a drawer and the drawer goes in a basement and the basement gets paved over for a parking structure, and everyone agrees this is progress."

Ines was crying. She wasn't hiding it. Tears ran down into the creases of her face and she let them, and Kofi understood that she was crying for herself as much as for him — that her war, wherever hers had been, had drawers in it too.

"So when I saw that water tower in the photograph," Kofi finished, "and I said the name — I wasn't talking to the dead. I was talking to the living. To you. Because you're the first people who've ever held these pages and looked at me instead of away. And because there's a journalist in the delta named Ade Balogun who has spent ten years printing stories nobody wants printed, and Ines trusts him, and if Ines trusts him that's worth more to me than any government's word."

* * *

Silence took the deck for a while. The good kind of silence — the kind a crew earns, where nobody needs to fill it.

Bo broke it, as Bo always did, by doing something practical. He gathered the photographs into a stack, squared their edges with great care, and weighted them with a wrench so the breeze off the water couldn't take them.

"I'm not a brave man," Bo said. "I want that on the record. I fix engines. Engines don't ask hard questions." He looked around at each of them. "But my grandmother came from the border country. She used to sing songs in a language I thought was just nonsense until I was grown, and then I heard it spoken once in a market and nearly fell over, and I asked her where it was from, and she said *a place that isn't on maps anymore,* and she wouldn't say more, and I was young and I let it go." His jaw worked. "I've been letting it go for thirty years. I'm done."

He looked at Marguerite.

"It's your boat. Your contract. Your neck. But if you call the vote — I vote delta."

All eyes went to Marguerite. Kofi watched her take her time, the way she took her time about everything, weighing the current before committing to it. He knew what she was calculating: the navy liaison waiting upstream with his secrecy clauses; the patrol schedules; the fact that every mile between here and the delta was a mile hunted. He knew she was thinking about Tomas, too — he had pieced that much together from Ines, from the way Marguerite went silent at certain names, certain depths — and he knew that for three years she had chosen invisibility the way a drowning woman chooses air, and that what he was asking her to do was to surface, in open water, in daylight, where anyone could see.

She looked at the chest. At the seal of a nation that no longer existed. At Kofi.

"My rule," she said slowly, "has always been that the river gives up what it gives up, and I take what I'm paid for, and I don't ask what the cargo means." A pause. "That rule kept me alive. It also kept me small." She straightened, and something in her face shifted — a decision arriving, visible the way weather arrives, all at once and from a long way off. "We go to the delta. We find Balogun. We put these pages in front of the world if it's the last thing this boat ever does."

"And the navy?" Ines asked softly.

"We'll deal with the navy."

"There'll be checkpoints. Patrols. Sable, if he's still sniffing around—"

"*Ines.*" Marguerite almost smiled. "You trust Balogun?"

"With my life," Ines said. "He pulled my brother's name out of a file nobody else would touch. He printed it when printing it cost him his press license. He's stubborn as river rock and honest as one, too."

"Then we trust him." Marguerite looked around the circle of lamplight. "All in favor?"

Four hands. No hesitation, not even Bo's, not even Kofi's own — though afterward his heart hammered like he'd swum the rapids.

* * *

Later, when the others had turned in, Kofi stayed on deck. He wrapped the photographs back in oilcloth himself, each one, and lowered them into the chest, and closed the lid, and rested his palms on the cool lead lining through the wood.

Below him, through the deck planks, he could hear the sounds of his crew going to sleep — Bo's snore like a slow engine, Ines murmuring something in her sleep-language, Marguerite's bunk creaking once and going still. Ordinary sounds. Family sounds, though he'd never dared use that word even in his own head.

Downriver, two hundred miles of water lay between them and Ade Balogun's print shop. Somewhere along that water there would be checkpoints, and patrol boats, and men whose job was to make sure the chest stayed sunk. Kofi was fifteen years old and weighed less than the chest did.

But he had carried water past the willows twice a day for a year, and he had learned the thing the guards never understood: that the ones who carry are the ones who endure, and the ones who endure are the ones who arrive.

He was still resting his hands on the lid when he saw it — far downstream, where the river bent toward the narrows, a white running light moving against the current. Too fast for a fishing boat. Too disciplined for smugglers.

A patrol boat, coming upriver. Toward them.

Kofi didn't call out. Not yet. He stood very still in the dark and watched the light grow, and began, silently, to count the miles.
