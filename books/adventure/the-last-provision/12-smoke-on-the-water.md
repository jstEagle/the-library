## 12. Smoke on the Water

Marguerite smelled it before she saw it—burnt palm oil and wet ash riding the morning current like a warning that had arrived too late to matter.

They had left the village of Toma an hour after dawn, running easy in the channel's deep water, the chest lashed beneath tarpaulin in the hold where Bo had built it a false floor. Kofi was at the bow with the lead line, calling depths in a voice that had lost its adolescent crack somewhere between the locks and the marshes. Ines was below, boiling coffee on a stove she'd rigged to burn clean so the smoke wouldn't give them away. Bo sat at the engine controls with his hands resting on the throttles like a pianist between movements.

The smoke came from behind them. From upriver. From Toma.

Marguerite turned and watched the horizon assemble itself out of gray: first the column, black and fat at its base, leaning east with the wind; then the glow beneath it, a bruised orange that no sunrise produces; then the sound, arriving across the water as a soft, continuous roar, the way a distant crowd sounds, or rain on a tin roof, if rain could be hungry.

"Bo," she said. "Stop the boat."

He looked at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic—the fuel they'd burned, the hours they'd lose, the patrol schedule Reyes had given them folded in her breast pocket—and she watched him decide not to argue. The engine dropped to idle. The river went quiet enough that she could hear the fire.

"Is that—" Kofi started.

"Toma," Marguerite said.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Ines came up the companionway with the coffee pot still in her hand, and when she saw the sky she set it down on the deck very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands have stopped being entirely yours.

"They were fishing families," Ines said. "Old men. Children."

"I know."

"You told them we'd only be there one night."

"I know, Ines."

"Then why does it look like that?"

Marguerite didn't answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie or a confession, and she had spent three years rationing both. She raised the binoculars instead.

Through the glass, Toma resolved into what it had become: the stilt houses along the waterline burning down to their black skeletons, the drying racks with their silver harvest gone to char, the church roof collapsed inward like a rib cage. A patrol boat sat off the village's seaward side, gray and low, its deck gun traversing slowly back toward the shore as though checking its work. Figures moved on the beach—small, unhurried, methodical. Not fleeing. Walking among the ashes. She understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water entering her lungs, that they were looking for tracks. For footprints. For proof that strangers had slept there.

She lowered the binoculars. Her hands were steady. That was the worst part—that after everything, her hands had learned to be steady while the rest of her screamed.

* * *

Three years ago, in another life, on another river, Marguerite Okafor had believed there were rules. Not laws—laws were for people who could afford lawyers—but rules, the unwritten treaties that kept the salvage trade civilized. You didn't touch a wreck that held bodies without asking permission of the living. You didn't work a stretch of river without paying the villages that fed you. And above all, you never, never made civilians pay for what you carried.

Tomas had taught her that last one. They'd been running a contract up the Sere valley, carrying survey equipment for a mining concern, and a checkpoint had shaken them down for everything aboard. Tomas had wanted to dump the gear overboard rather than let the soldiers take it and use it to justify a raid on the next village downstream. *Equipment can be replaced,* he'd said, laughing, already unbolting the case. *People can't.* She'd talked him out of it. She'd been practical. Practical had seemed like wisdom then.

Now she stood on the deck of her own boat and watched a village burn because forty kilograms of paper had slept under its roofs for one night, and every practical instinct she owned curdled into something older and less useful.

"We go back," Kofi said.

It was not a question, and it was not a suggestion, and it came from the boy at the bow in a voice flat as a cleat. Marguerite looked at him. He hadn't turned from the smoke. His knuckles were white around the lead line, and his jaw worked once, twice, like he was chewing on words too large to swallow.

"No," she said.

"There are people in there."

"There are people in there who are dead, or who are hiding, and a patrol boat sitting between us and them with a gun crew that has already fired once this morning." She heard her own voice come out level, instructional, the voice she used underwater, and she hated it. "If we turn around, we die, and the chest goes to the bottom, and Toma burns for nothing."

Kofi finally turned. What was in his face was not anger. It was worse—it was recognition. She had seen that look before, in refugee camps, in the eyes of children watching adults calculate. Watching them do the math that said: *you are not worth the risk.* He was sixteen years old and he had done that math about himself so many times it no longer surprised him, and that was the thing that broke something open in her chest.

"The navy did this," he said. "Because of us."

"Because of what we carry," Marguerite said. "There's a difference, and I need you to understand it, because the difference is the whole reason we're doing this. They didn't burn Toma because we slept there. They burned Toma because they are afraid of what happens if the world reads those papers. We are just the fuse. The fire is theirs. It was theirs twenty years ago in Veltania, and it's theirs now, and the only question—the only one—is whether we put it out or carry it somewhere it can be seen."

Kofi stared at her. Then he coiled the lead line with two sharp, violent motions and said nothing more, and Marguerite knew she had not convinced him, only postponed him, and that some debts postpone badly.

* * *

They ran all day, putting distance between themselves and the smoke, and by late afternoon the sky behind them had gone back to pretending to be innocent. Bo found them a bend in the channel where a fallen fig tree made a natural screen, and they tied up under its canopy with the engine ticking as it cooled, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Ines cooked anyway. That was her answer to most things—to feed people whether or not they deserved it—and she laid out rice and dried fish and the last of the pickled peppers on the deck plates, and they ate sitting in a row like mourners at a wake, which, Marguerite thought, was not far wrong.

"He'll escalate," Bo said at last. He said it to his plate. "Reyes. This wasn't a checkpoint action. Burning a village takes authorization. Somebody above him signed off on it, which means either Reyes asked for it—which I don't believe—or somebody above him decided the chest is worth more than the rules, and Reyes is now following orders he hates." He pushed rice around with his fork. "I've served under men like that. They break. The question is which direction."

"Or," Ines said, "he breaks toward us, and it doesn't matter, because the man who signed the order sends someone else."

Marguerite set down her plate. "We're two days from the delta," she said. "Balogun's safehouse is on the south channel past the fish weir. If we run hard and run dark, we're there before anyone can organize another Toma."

"And if they've organized already?" Ines asked. "Ines, they burned a village eight hours after we left it. Eight hours. Someone is feeding them our position, and it isn't the radio, because Bo stripped ours down to receive-only after the locks."

Marguerite had been avoiding that thought the way you avoid a hole in a wreck's hull—knowing it's there, feeling the cold coming through it, refusing to look. Now she looked. Someone had known they'd slept at Toma. Someone had known fast. Either the navy's intelligence was better than any border force she'd ever evaded, or—

No. She wouldn't do it. She would not stand on her own deck and look at Ines and Bo and Kofi and start counting them as suspects. Tomas had died because of a chain of small failures, and she had spent three years learning that suspicion was a solvent: pour it on anything and eventually everything dissolved, including the things holding you up.

"We assume they're tracking us," she said instead. "All of us assume it. Which means from tonight, we travel like prey. No lights, no cooking smells after dusk, no engines within earshot of a settlement. We buy nothing, we speak to no one, we shelter nowhere with a roof. The river itself is our cover until the delta."

"And the villages between here and there?" Kofi asked quietly. "The ones who might offer?"

"We refuse them," Marguerite said. "However it's offered. However it hurts."

Kofi looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded slowly, and Marguerite realized he had been testing her—not whether she was right, but whether she would say it out loud, whether she would accept the cost with her eyes open rather than discover it later and pretend it was unforeseeable. He was sixteen and he had already learned the lesson she was still learning: that survival bought with other people's blood was just slaughter on an installment plan.

* * *

Night came down soft and warm, and they ran dark through a country of reeds and drowned forest, Bo steering by the stars and the feel of the current against the rudder, Marguerite standing beside him in the wheelhouse with the radio receiver pressed to her ear, scanning frequencies for voices.

Near midnight she caught one. A patrol channel, encrypted except for the call signs, but the traffic volume told her what she needed to know: boats moving. Not searching—not yet—but repositioning, converging on the lower channel in the unhurried way of a net being set rather than thrown.

She woke Reyes's gift from her pocket—the patrol schedule he'd passed her in the marsh cathedral, handwritten, deniable, career-ending. She compared it against the radio traffic. The convergence points matched. Every boat was exactly where his schedule said it would be, which meant the schedule was still good, which meant Reyes was still feeding it true—or meant it was bait now, poisoned at the source, and the man who had chosen truth over duty had been made to serve duty one last time without knowing it.

She couldn't tell. That was the cruelty of it. Trust, once broken anywhere, leaked everywhere.

She stood in the dark wheelhouse a long time, listening to the static breathe, and let herself think about Tomas. Not the accident—she was done rehearsing the accident; she had confessed it to Ines on this same deck and found, to her astonishment, that saying it aloud had not killed her—but the man. His terrible impracticality. His conviction that the river gave back what you put into it, in kind. *You carry bodies gently,* he used to say, *and the river remembers. You carry them carelessly, and it remembers that too.*

The river was remembering now. Forty people in a flooded hold, chained, and a village of fishermen, and a boy's parents whose names she still didn't know, and the ledger was growing, and every line of it was written in the same ink: the ink of people who had decided that some truths were too expensive to survive.

Well. She was done deciding that.

"Change of plan," she said to the darkness, and then, louder, waking them one by one because some things should not wait for morning. "We don't run for Balogun anymore."

Bo's head came up. Ines stopped halfway out of the cabin. Kofi sat up in his nest of rope with his eyes already fully awake—he slept like a sentry, always had.

"The delta gate will be watched. Balogun's safehouse will be watched. We show up in this boat, we deliver them a target with a fixed address, and whatever happens next happens to him and everyone within a mile of him." She heard her own voice and recognized it—recognized the tone underneath the calm, the one she'd spent three years drowning. "So we stop being a delivery. We become a broadcast. There's a transmitter relay tower at Punta Verde, forty miles short of the delta—an old colonial weather station, automated, but the building still has power and a working landline junction. Balogun told me about it months ago, back when this was hypothetical. If we can get the documents scanned and transmitted from there, it won't matter what happens to the chest, or to us. The story leaves the country at the speed of light, and no patrol boat can chase it."

"That tower will be guarded," Bo said.

"Probably lightly. Nobody guards weather stations."

"Nothing about us is weather-station business anymore, Marguerite."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing about us is quiet business anymore. That's the point." She looked at each of them in turn—at Bo with his engineer's frown already sketching solutions, at Ines with her arms crossed and her fear worn openly the way she wore everything, at Kofi with the firelight of Toma still reflected somewhere behind his eyes. "I spent three years learning how to disappear. I was good at it. I was so good at it that I let a government erase a country and forty people drown in chains and I never once asked what was in the wrecks I was paid not to ask about." She picked up the receiver and set it down again. "Toma ends that. They burned a village to keep me quiet, and I am finished being quiet. From here to Punta Verde, we are loud, we are fast, and we are done apologizing for what we carry."

Silence. The river breathed against the hull.

Then Kofi stood up, all elbows and certainty, and said, "Then let's not waste the night," and Bo reached for the throttles, and Ines began breaking down the camp without being asked, and Marguerite felt something shift in her chest that she had no better word for than *miles*—the old counting, the ritual that had measured her life since Tomas died, suddenly pointing forward instead of back.

Behind them, far upriver, the sky was still faintly stained. Ahead, the channel ran black and wide and waiting.

She opened the throttle.
