## 13. Sable's Bargain

Sable had learned long ago that the most valuable currency on any river was not money, not information, but the precise moment a person believed themselves unobserved. He collected those moments the way other men collected debts—patiently, with an accountant's pleasure. And so it was that he stood in the shadow of the customs shed at Porto Verde, a cup of terrible coffee cooling forgotten in his hand, watching Marguerite Okafor count coins into Kofi's palm and send the boy up the hill for diesel filters, and knew that he had his moment.

She watched the boy go. She always did; he'd noticed that much in the two days since their boats had shared anchorage, two days of careful, mutually performed ignorance. She tracked Kofi until he rounded the fuel depot, then she turned back to her engine cowling, and her shoulders dropped half an inch—not relaxation, exactly. More like a woman setting down a weight she intended to pick up again the moment anyone looked.

That was when Sable moved.

He crossed the plank road between the sheds at an unhurried walk, because hurrying announced intention, and intention was the one thing he never sold cheap. The *Marta-Kay* sat low in the brown water, working boat to the bone, dive gear coiled on deck like sleeping snakes, the whole vessel wearing its ordinariness like armor. He admired that. He had once owned a boat like that himself, in another life, on another river, before the war taught him that ordinary things were the first things taken.

"Senhora Okafor." He stopped at the rail, hands visible, empty, folded around nothing but his reputation. "You have a moment?"

"You're the broker." She didn't look up from the cowling. Her voice carried the flat courtesy of someone who had already decided what he was. "Sable. I've heard the name."

"All good things, I hope." He smiled, because smiling was free. "I hear you're doing survey work for the navy."

"I do salvage. Whatever they want found."

"Mm." He set his coffee cup on her rail, deliberately, an intrusion dressed as informality. "And what do they want found?"

"A boat."

"Just a boat."

"Just a boat." She said it twice, and the second time was the tell. People who were telling the truth said things once and let them stand. People who were lying repeated themselves, as though repetition could weld the lie shut. Sable had made a career of listening for the seam.

He leaned against the rail beside her, close enough to be familiar, far enough to be deniable. Below them the river slid past, carrying silt and secrets south with equal indifference. Somewhere upstream, he knew, a chest lined with lead sat hidden under fish crates or bilge plating or whatever clever place Bo had engineered for it—he'd paid three separate children on this dock to keep their eyes open, and children saw everything, especially what adults thought they hid.

"Let me tell you how I work," Sable said pleasantly. "I don't threaten. Threats are crude instruments; they bend in the hand. I deal in arithmetic. You are four people on a slow boat with a navy liaison who has been asking questions about you in three riverports. You are carrying cargo whose weight does not match its manifest. And you are, above all, a woman who has spent three years learning how to be invisible—which tells me everything, because invisible people only stay invisible by never touching anything that shines."

Her jaw tightened. He noted it without gloating. There would be time for gloating later, if he wanted it, which he usually didn't. Gloating was a tax paid by amateurs.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"No," he agreed. "But you will."

* * *

He took her to the riverside cantina because it was neutral ground and neutral ground favored him; every man in it owed him something, and owed men kept their eyes on their drinks. They took the corner table beneath a ceiling fan that turned with the enthusiasm of a dying animal, and he ordered two of whatever the cook claimed was rum, and waited until she had tasted hers and set it down untasted again.

"I represent certain parties," he began, and she cut him off.

"The Veltanian exile council."

The words landed between them like a dropped tool, and for a heartbeat Sable forgot himself entirely—forgot to smile, forgot to angle his body toward the door, forgot twenty years of practiced composure—and simply stared at her. Then he laughed, low and genuine, a rare sound he resented giving away.

"Well," he said. "Well. So you've done your homework too."

"I listen." She turned her glass a quarter-turn on the sticky wood. "Ines heard you in Porto Amarelo, asking after divers. Kofi saw your card. And you just told me I'm carrying cargo that doesn't match my manifest, so either you're fishing blind, or you already know what I have. Nobody fishes blind twice on the same stretch of river."

"Your crew is more dangerous than you are," Sable observed, meaning it as a compliment and knowing, even as he said it, that she would take it as a threat. Everything he said to this woman arrived as a threat no matter how carefully he wrapped it. It was, he suspected, why he liked her. Honesty of that caliber was almost impossible to buy.

"So. The council." He spread his hands on the table, palms down, a man laying out cards. "You know what Veltania was. A country that existed for sixty years and then didn't, erased in a single season—the borders redrawn, the name struck from the atlases, the archives sealed, the people scattered or worse. There are perhaps two hundred thousand Veltanians alive in the world, most of them old now, most of them poor, all of them officially stateless, which means officially nobody. No passports. No embassies. No graves with correct names on them." He paused. "No accounting."

"And your council wants an accounting."

"My council wants *evidence*. There is a difference, senhora, and the difference is worth more than you have earned in your entire career." He leaned forward. His voice dropped into the register he used for prices. "Do you know why the chest is lead-lined? Not to protect the papers from water. Water gets in everywhere eventually; any archivist knows that. Lead is to stop the papers being read by *scanners*. Someone aboard that courier boat was smuggling those documents past a checkpoint that x-rayed cargo. Someone believed those photographs mattered enough to shield them from the eyes of a state. That person drowned for the trouble. Chained in their own hold, if the stories I've bought are accurate."

Marguerite's face did something small and controlled, a muscle moving near the eye, and Sable understood suddenly that she had seen the hold herself. Had gone down into it. Had touched what the river kept. Something in his chest, some organ he had assumed was vestigial, gave a brief unwelcome twinge, and he continued before it could develop into anything inconvenient.

"The council will pay you four hundred thousand," he said. "Clean money, laundered through shipping accounts in three countries, delivered however you specify. Plus passage—real documents, real names, a border crossing arranged anywhere in the world. You could disappear properly. Not river-invisible. *Gone*-invisible. New life, new coast, new ocean if you want it. For a box of paper."

"That paper is evidence of a massacre."

"It is *leverage*," Sable corrected gently. "Evidence is what journalists call leverage when they can't afford it. My council has spent twenty years filing petitions, submitting testimony, begging tribunals to notice that two hundred thousand people were unmade. Do you know how many hearings they've gotten? Three. Three hearings, senhora, in twenty years, and each one ended with a finding of insufficient documentation. The orders in that chest are signed. Dated. Stamped. Photographs with coordinates. That is not insufficient documentation—that is a verdict waiting to be delivered. But verdicts require access, and access requires negotiation, and negotiation requires..." He gestured elegantly. "...assets."

"You'd sell them to the tribunal."

"I'd sell them to whoever advances the cause, yes. Possibly the tribunal. Possibly a government willing to recognize Veltania in exchange for the moral credit. Possibly a museum, which sounds obscene and pays best of all. The council is pragmatic. Dead nations cannot afford purity."

"And if I say no?"

* * *

This was the question he had come to ask, and now that it hung in the air between them, Sable felt the peculiar sadness of his profession settle over him, the way it always did at this point in a transaction—the moment where the arithmetic stopped being abstract and became a specific woman's specific life.

"If you say no," he said, "then I am obliged to inform you of your position, which is currently worse than you believe. Officer Reyes has requisitioned satellite passes over this reach of river. He has interviewed dockhands in four towns. Two days ago he paid a man in Porto Amarelo to describe your boat to a sketch artist, and the sketch is now circulating among naval intelligence with your name attached—your real name, Okafor, the one you've spent three years burying. Did you know that? Because I sold it to them."

He watched her absorb that. She absorbed it the way stone absorbs rain—nothing showing, everything changing.

"You *sold* me to them."

"I sell to everyone. It keeps me honest." He shrugged. "Here is the arithmetic, plainly stated. If you refuse the council, I must assume you intend to take the chest elsewhere—to the journalist Balogun, everyone assumes, because Balogun is the fashionable destination for conscience these days. If you take it to Balogun, the documents become public, and public documents embarrass my principals' negotiating position, and embarrassed principals withdraw funding, and unfunded councils dissolve, and two hundred thousand stateless people lose their last organized voice. So if you refuse me, I protect the council's interest the only way left to me." He smiled, and for the first time it was not a pleasant smile. "I tell Reyes where you sleep tonight."

Silence. The fan creaked overhead. Down at the dock, someone was arguing about fuel prices in a language Marguerite probably understood and Sable pretended not to.

"You'd hand a woman and a child to a navy that burns villages."

"The navy burns villages without my help. I merely choose whom it finds." He turned his glass, watching the light move through it. "Do not mistake me for a monster, senhora. Monsters enjoy their work. I merely understand that on this river, everyone is cargo, and every cargo has a price, and the only moral choice available is deciding whose freight goes overboard first. Your chest sinks my council. My information sinks your crew. Neither of us is innocent. We are merely differently armed."

"And if I accept? Four hundred thousand, and I vanish, and the chest goes to your council—and then what happens to it?"

"Then it becomes what it was always going to become: a bargaining chip traded between powers, until eventually, in five years or ten or twenty, some fraction of it surfaces in some archive, and some historian writes a monograph that eleven people read." He said it flatly, without apology, because flattery was wasted on her. "Versus the journalist, who publishes next month, and gets two hundred thousand people remembered for exactly one news cycle before the world moves on to the next massacre, and the governments involved issue statements, and form committees, and nothing changes except that everyone who signed those orders knows precisely who exposed them. You'd be spending the rest of your life looking over your shoulder. Assuming you survive the month. Reyes has orders, senhora. Recover at any cost. Those are the actual words. At. Any. Cost."

"You've read his orders."

"I wrote half of them." He smiled thinly. "Who do you think convinced the ministry that a civilian salvage contract needed such stringent secrecy clauses?"

For a long moment Marguerite said nothing. Sable let the silence work for him; silence was a tool like any other, and he had honed this one longest. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—it had gone somewhere interior, somewhere he couldn't follow, and he recognized with professional discomfort that he had lost control of the conversation without understanding when.

"How much of the four hundred thousand survives the council?" she asked. "Administrative costs. Security. Brokerage fees."

"Sixty percent reaches the claimants directly."

"And the other forty percent?"

"Funds operations." He inclined his head, conceding the hit. "Including mine."

"There it is." She set her glass down with a small, definitive click. "You're not selling me the council's future, Sable. You're selling me your commission. Forty percent of four hundred thousand is a hundred and sixty thousand reasons for you to make sure this deal closes. Which means every threat you've made—the sketch artist, the satellite passes, the orders—I can't verify any of it, because you need me frightened more than you need me rich."

Sable considered several responses and discarded them all, because she was right, and rightness deserved acknowledgment even in an enemy. Especially in an enemy.

"Partly true," he admitted. "But here is the part that isn't: the sketch exists. I held it. The satellite tasking exists; I've seen the requisition. Reyes is closing, senhora, faster than you think—you have perhaps four days before his patrol pattern pins you to this anchorage. My threats are commissions dressed as charity, fine. But my *information* is genuine, and genuine information is the only thing on this river I've never discounted." He rose, dropping bills on the table that would cover ten times the cost of the rum. "So consider it not an offer but a forecast. Four days. Decide by then whether you'd rather be rich, righteous, or alive. Most people on this river get to choose one. You might manage two, if you move quickly and trust no one—including, I would strongly advise, the lovely Ines, whose radio transmissions I have been reading for a week."

He watched that land. He watched it land badly—in her eyes, briefly, there was something raw and wounded, and Sable felt again that unwelcome twinge in the vestigial organ, because he had not meant to hurt her quite there, quite like that. But precision was precision, and he had never yet sold a softened truth.

At the door of the cantina he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at her sitting alone in the fan-stirred heat with her untouched rum and her four-day clock now ticking visibly behind her eyes.

"One more thing, senhora, gratis, because I admire you. The person who chained those bodies in that hold—the officer who scuttled that courier boat—he is still alive. Still serving. Wearing a very fine uniform with considerable gold braid. Your chest contains his signature." He tapped the doorframe twice, a habit, a superstition, a farewell. "Which means somewhere inside your box of paper bones is the name of the man Reyes answers to. Think about that before you decide who to trust with it."

Then he stepped out into the white glare of the dock road, whistling something old and Veltanian that he had promised himself he'd forgotten, leaving behind him the smell of expensive cologne and the exact weight of a debt that had not yet been named.

Because that was the final arithmetic, the one he hadn't spoken aloud, the one he kept in a locked ledger at the bottom of himself: Marguerite Okafor would stall—she would stall beautifully, buying her four days with silence and misdirection—but stalling was itself a transaction, and every transaction with Sable left residue. She now owed him the consideration of her decision. And consideration, in Sable's experience, was the first installment of obedience. By the time she understood that, the locks would be behind her and the marshes ahead, and he would already be moving to collect.

He touched the rail of the *Marta-Kay* in passing, lightly, the way a man touches a horse he intends to buy.

Four days.
