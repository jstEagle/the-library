## 14. Betrayal in the Locks

Bo had always trusted machines more than people, and the *Marta's Promise* had never once lied to her. The old riverboat spoke in temperatures and pressures, in the pitch of her engine and the tremor of her hull, and Bo had spent eleven years learning that language until it was more fluent to her than any she'd been born with. People were different. People said one thing and meant another, smiled while they calculated, called you family on Tuesday and sold you out by Friday.

So when the radio began whispering at night, Bo heard it the way she heard everything mechanical: as a fault.

It started three nights after Sable's men had tailed them out of Puerto Ceniza. The *Marta* was moored in a backwater of reeds two miles below the last navy checkpoint, running dark, running quiet, and Bo was up at 0300 because the port engine's cooling intake had been running hot all evening and she didn't trust it not to seize somewhere worse. She'd been under the deck plates with a flashlight clamped in her teeth, elbow-deep in the raw-water strainer, pulling weed and silt out of the grate, when she heard it.

A voice. Low, rhythmic, coming through the deck itself like a pulse in the boat's bones.

She froze, wrench in hand, and listened. Not the engine. Not the water. A human voice, murmuring numbers, rising and falling in the cadence of someone reading coordinates off a chart.

Bo slid out from under the plates without a sound. Eleven years of working engines had taught her how to move quietly around machinery; it turned out the skill transferred. She climbed the companionway into the wheelhouse, where the night air hung thick and green off the marsh, and there was Ines, standing at the aft rail with her back turned, the handheld radio pressed close to her mouth, transmitting in short, careful bursts.

The moon was down. Ines's face was only a pale suggestion above the collar of her jacket. But her voice carried across the still water with terrible clarity—numbers, grid references, a phrase in clipped Spanish that Bo caught only half of: *"...confirming position... will hold schedule..."*

Bo stood in the shadow of the wheelhouse door and felt something in her chest go cold and hard, like a bearing seizing.

She thought of Kofi asleep in the forepeak, sixteen years old and carrying a nation's dead in his head. She thought of Marguerite, who hadn't slept more than four hours a night since they'd raised the chest, who checked the padlock twice before every watch change. She thought of the photographs—the rows of faces, the camp with its wire and its watchtowers—and of Ines's own stories, told once, late, over rum: the war she'd walked out of, the things she'd seen done with paperwork and patience.

And she thought: *she's selling us.*

Bo withdrew down the companionway without a word. She finished clearing the strainer. She went back to her bunk and lay staring at the overhead until dawn came gray through the porthole, and she said nothing, because saying nothing was what she knew how to do. Machines didn't need confrontation. They needed diagnosis. And you didn't confront a fault until you understood it.

So for two days she watched.

She watched Ines take the middle watch alone, which wasn't unusual—Ines liked the quiet hours—but now Bo noted that she took it with the handheld radio clipped to her belt instead of left charging in its cradle. She watched Ines volunteer for shore runs at riverports, going alone to buy provisions, gone forty minutes, fifty, an hour. She watched her come back with vegetables and diesel filters and a lightness in her step that Bo might once have read as relief from the tension that had ridden all of them since Veltania's seal came up out of the dark.

On the second day, Bo did what she should have done immediately. While Ines was ashore at the fuel depot below the locks, Bo opened the radio's battery compartment, the way she'd open any housing, looking for the modification inside.

She found it in nine minutes. A small board spliced into the transmit circuit, aftermarket, soldered with a sure hand—a frequency extender. The handheld could now reach channels it was never built to reach. Military channels. Someone had converted Ines's radio into a weapon, or Ines had converted it herself, and either way the little green board sat in Bo's palm like a splinter of proof.

She put it back exactly as she'd found it and closed the housing, and her hands were shaking, and she hated them for it.

* * *

The locks at San Ferro were the only way through the ridge country—two concrete chambers cut into the river's throat, operated by a skeleton crew of lockkeepers who'd worked the same gates for thirty years and asked no questions of any boat that paid its fees. Above the locks, the river split: the main channel ran east toward the delta through open country the navy patrolled with gunboats; the old channel ran south into the marshes, shallow, unmapped, a labyrinth of drowned forest and floating meadow where a patrol boat drew too much water to follow.

Marguerite planned to run the marshes. Balogun's safehouse was deep in the delta, reachable from either route, but the marsh route bought them time and invisibility, and invisibility was the religion Marguerite preached even if she'd stopped practicing it.

They locked through at midday, the *Marta's Promise* sliding into the lower chamber between walls furred with moss, the great gates groaning shut behind them, the water beginning to rise. Bo stood at the engine controls in the wheelhouse, watching the gauges, watching the walls climb past the windows, and Ines stood beside her at the helm, hands resting easy on the spokes, humming something old and low.

The chamber filled. The upper gates parted. And in the sixty seconds of open water between the lock and the bend where the channels divided, Bo made her decision—or rather, the decision had been making itself in her for two days, and it finally finished.

"Ines." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Who are you talking to?"

Ines didn't startle. That was the first wrong thing. A guilty person startles. Ines simply went still, the way water goes still over a deep hole, and turned her head slowly, and looked at Bo with an expression Bo couldn't read at all.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The radio. At night. Three nights ago, 0300, you were transmitting coordinates off the aft rail. There's a frequency extender board in your handheld that didn't come from any shop I know." Bo kept her eyes on the channel ahead, on the brown water and the green walls of the gorge, because looking at Ines directly made the cold thing in her chest spread. "I've known you six years. You taught Kofi to swim. You held my hair back when I had fever in Puerto Verde. So I'm asking you once, woman to woman, before I ask it louder."

The silence lasted three seconds. It felt like three hours.

"It's Balogun," Ines said quietly. "I've been feeding Ade Balogun our position so he knows when to expect us. So his people can be ready at the delta gate with cameras, witnesses, protection. Bo—" and here her voice cracked open with something that sounded, God help her, like sincerity, "—you think I'd sell that boy? After what he told us about the camps? You think I'd sell any of you?"

Bo wanted to believe her. That was the trap of Ines, always had been—she was warm the way a stove is warm, and you forgot stoves burn houses down. Bo gripped the wheel spokes and stared at the water and tried to do the arithmetic of six years of shared watches against ninety seconds of overheard transmission, and the arithmetic wouldn't resolve.

"Then show me," Bo said. "Show me the messages. Show me the frequency."

"There's no time—"

"There's always time for that."

But even as she said it, Bo saw it: the flicker in Ines's eyes, downward and to the left, the half-second delay. And Bo had spent her life around gauges, and she knew what a needle looks like just before it pegs.

That was when the sound came.

At first it was only a vibration in the wheelhouse floor, a tremor Bo felt through her boots before her ears caught it—the particular heavy thutter of an outboard running wide open, more than one, coming fast from downstream. From behind them. From the direction of the lower lock, which they had cleared twenty minutes ago, which no one should have been following them out of.

Bo crossed to the stern windows and looked back down the channel they'd ascended, and her stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

Two gray boats, military hulls, coming up the gorge at speed, wakes white against the dark water. And behind them, further back, the blunt silhouette of a patrol craft settling into the mouth of the lower lock like a fist closing.

"They're here," Bo said. Her voice sounded very far away. "Ines. They're *here*. Twenty minutes behind us through the lock. How does a navy patrol know exactly which boat, exactly which lock, exactly this hour?"

Ines had gone white. Not guilty-white—there was a difference, Bo would swear to it later, though later seemed a luxury then—shocked-white, the white of a woman seeing her own miscalculation come home. "I sent Balogun decoy routes," she said rapidly. "I never gave them this channel, these coordinates—I *swear*—"

"Then someone else did."

The words arrived in Bo's mind fully formed, and with them the whole architecture of the thing laid bare: Sable at the riverport with his soft voice and his knowing smile. Sable, who had found them too easily. Sable, whose people had tailed them out of Puerto Ceniza. Sable, who had needed only to put a tracker on the *Marta's Promise*—in the hull, in the superstructure, anywhere a magnet would stick—and let the navy do the rest.

Or Ines had sold them, and the shock was theater. Bo couldn't tell. Standing there with the engines idling and the gray boats eating the distance between them, she discovered the true horror of betrayal: not knowing. Trust, once broken, doesn't distinguish between innocence and guilt. It just breaks.

Marguerite came up the companionway two steps at a time, Kofi behind her, and took in the scene—Bo's face, Ines's face, the boats in the stern windows—in about a second and a half. Three years of running salvage on rivers where being seen meant being robbed had given her a predator's economy of motion.

"Marsh channel," she said. "Now. Full ahead, starboard turn at the fork. Bo, give me everything the port engine has and don't be gentle about it."

"What about—" Kofi started.

"No questions. Move."

Bo threw the throttles forward. The *Marta's Promise* was forty years old and displacement-hulled, built for hauling cargo, not for running, but Bo had rebuilt her diesels with her own hands and loved her enough to have coaxed outrages out of her before. The old girl surged, bow lifting, the twin screws churning the lock outflow into foam, and Marguerite hauled her hard to starboard at the fork, away from the broad east channel, into the narrow throat of the old southern passage where the trees leaned over the water and the depth gauge began its nervous descent.

Behind them, a loudhailer crackled across the water, a voice flattened by electronics into pure authority: *"Vessel Marta's Promise. Heave to. This is a naval inspection. Heave to or we will fire."*

"Heave to," Marguerite repeated softly, with a laugh that had nothing of humor in it. "Into the marsh, Bo. Follow the deep water. Left at the split cypress, right where the reeds thin."

"I know the channel," Bo said, and realized as she said it that she was lying—she'd never run this water, none of them had, it was a rumor of a channel on a chart forty years out of date—and realized also that it didn't matter. The alternative was stopping, and stopping was surrender, and surrender meant the chest going back into the dark along with everyone who'd ever been photographed inside it.

The first shot came thirty seconds later. Not at them—at the water ahead, a warning, a column of white spray off the bow. Then a second, closer. The patrol boats couldn't follow into the shallows—they were already falling behind, their wakes dying among the trees—but the patrol craft back at the lock had a deck gun, and its range was longer than the marsh was deep.

"Ines!" Marguerite shouted over the engine roar. "Forward deck! Get the canvas off the chest, get it ready to move—we may have to abandon ship!"

And Ines went. No hesitation, no argument. She scrambled forward along the deck with shells cracking somewhere behind them and the branches raking the wheelhouse roof, and she knelt over the lead-lined chest lashed amidships and tore the canvas free, and Bo, watching her in the mirror, felt the arithmetic shift again, unbearably, both directions at once.

The third shell hit the water close enough to rock them. The fourth hit the *Marta's Promise*.

It took the stern quarter—tore through the bulwark, through the lazarette, and exited somewhere below the waterline, and Bo felt it in her feet before she heard it, the sudden wrongness in the hull's voice, the note changing the way a bell changes when it cracks. Water came in fast and black over the deck plates aft.

"We're holed!" she shouted. "Below the waterline, starboard quarter—she'll stay up ten minutes, maybe fifteen!"

"Then we use ten of them." Marguerite was already steering for a shelf of higher ground she must have spotted through the trees—a hummock, a spine of old levee standing above the flood. "Kofi, get the dinghy launched. Bo, kill the engines before they flood. We go ashore with the chest and we disappear. Everyone takes a bag. Nothing else."

Bo shut down the diesels with hands that were almost steady, and the silence that followed was enormous—just the tick of cooling metal, the gurgle of incoming water, the distant idle of the patrol boats holding off in deeper water, waiting, patient as spiders.

They got the chest into the dinghy. They got themselves into the dinghy. Kofi paddled them the last hundred meters through water the color of tea, under hanging curtains of moss, while behind them the *Marta's Promise* settled by the stern with a long, sighing groan, like a living thing exhaling, and Bo watched eleven years of her work go down into the marsh and could not spare one second to mourn it.

The hummock took them in. Mangroves and flooded grass, a dry spine of ground barely wider than the dinghy, invisible from the channel within twenty meters. Somewhere out on the water, voices called to each other across the marsh, and a helicopter—when had there been a helicopter?—beat the air far to the east, sweeping the main channel, looking for a boat that no longer existed.

They crouched in the mud with the chest between them, four people breathing as quietly as they could, and the day went on around them, indifferent, full of bird noise.

It was Kofi who finally spoke, barely a breath: "Was it Ines?"

And Ines, mud to the waist, shaking, looked at him and said, "No," and Bo, who had heard her lie once today already, or heard her tell the truth once today already, and could no longer tell the difference, said nothing at all.

Out in the failing light, a searchlight swept the channel where the *Marta's Promise* had gone down, and paused, and began, slowly, to sweep toward the hummocks.
