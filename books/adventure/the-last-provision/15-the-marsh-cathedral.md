## 15. The Marsh Cathedral

The church had drowned decades ago and no one had bothered to bury it twice.

Marguerite saw it first as a shape against the last grey light—a broken triangle of roofline rising out the marsh like a rib cage, its bell tower leaning at an angle that defied every law of masonry she knew. The flood had come for this valley long before the war finished with it, and the water had simply stayed, patient as anything that knows it will win. Now the nave lay open to the sky through a collapsed transept wall, and cypress knees stood in the pews where congregations used to kneel.

"Inside," she said, and Kofi didn't argue. He hadn't argued since the locks, since Bo's shout across the water and Ines's voice on the radio and the searchlights sweeping the reed beds behind them. He paddled the way he did everything now—without wasted motion, without complaint, a boy who had learned somewhere before Marguerite ever met him that survival was mostly silence.

They dragged the chest into the church on the last of their strength. It came up the flooded steps trailing river weed, lead-lined and stubborn, heavier than paper had any right to be. Marguerite wedged it beneath the altar, which was the only dry-ish stone left in the building, and then she sat down in what had once been an aisle and let her hands shake for exactly ten seconds. She counted them. It was a discipline Tomas had taught her—*ten seconds of fear, then back to work*—and it was one of the few things she'd kept from him that didn't hurt to hold.

Kofi lowered himself onto a pew, water sloshing around his ankles, and looked up at the vault of the ceiling where painted saints had faded to ghosts. "Do you think God minds," he said quietly, "that we're hiding stolen government property under His altar?"

"It's not stolen." Marguerite wrung out her sleeve. "It's returned."

He considered that with the seriousness he gave everything, and nodded once, and said nothing more. Outside, far off across the marsh, a helicopter made its slow circuit, its rotor beat arriving a half-second after the sound of it, the way sound always lagged behind violence here.

They waited.

* * *

The night came down over the marsh in layers—first the birds going quiet, then the frogs starting up in their thousands, then the dark itself, total and wet. Marguerite kept watch from the collapsed transept wall, where she could see the channel they'd come by and the black mirror of the flooded fields beyond. Twice she heard engines. Once, a searchlight swept the tree line half a mile east and slid away again, indifferent as weather.

The chest sat under the altar like a sleeping animal. She found herself looking at it the way you look at something you've promised to protect—not with affection, exactly. With obligation.

Three years of invisibility, undone in eleven days. She turned the thought over and could not make herself regret it, which frightened her more than the helicopters did. There had been a version of her—the one who signed the navy's contract, the one who counted miles instead of years—who would have handed the chest to Sable at the first riverport and been gone by morning, name changed, boat repainted, another mile logged toward nowhere. That woman was still in her somewhere. But she'd watched Kofi's face when he opened the photographs, watched a boy recognize his own history in a grainy deportation manifest, and something in her had closed like a door. You could not un-know a thing like that. You could only decide what it cost.

Her radio crackled at midnight. She nearly put a knife through it before she remembered: Reyes's radio, the one he'd pressed into her hands in the marsh cathedral's shadow of a cousin, a flooded granary two nights ago, along with the patrol schedule folded inside a waterproof chart case. *It ends my career,* he'd said, and hadn't looked at her when he said it, which was how she knew he meant it.

She keyed the transmit switch and spoke low. "Go ahead."

"Margo." Ines's voice, thin with static and something worse than static. "Are you clear?"

"For now. Where are you?"

"South channel, with Bo. He took shrapnel in the shoulder getting us away from Sable's men—it's through and through, he'll live, but he can't pull an oar for a few days. We're holed up in a fishing stilt-house. Margo—" A breath. "I need you to listen to me before you decide what I am."

Marguerite looked at the radio for a long moment. Across the nave, Kofi had come awake at the sound of the voice, and his eyes in the dark were wide and steady.

"I'm listening," she said.

"The coordinates I was sending—they weren't ours." Ines's words came fast now, tumbling. "I've been feeding Balogun a decoy route since the locks. Every position I transmitted was forty kilometers east of where we actually were. I told him we were running the old logging canal. That's why the patrol boats hit the canal at dawn—that's why they weren't *here*. Margo, I swear it on Tomas's grave, I never gave them a true bearing. Not one."

"You were radioing someone," Marguerite said. "At the locks. Bo heard you."

"I was radioing Balogun. Every night, same time, coded burst. I've known Ade twenty years—he pulled me out of a border camp myself, back when I still had a different name and a different country. When you told me what was in the chest, I called him before I called anyone else, because if we went down, someone outside needed to already know the documents existed." Her voice cracked and steadied. "I should have told you. I told myself it was operational security. That was a lie I told to make it easier. I'm sorry."

The frogs filled the silence for a while. Marguerite sat with it—in the betrayal-shaped space where trust had been, testing the edges the way she'd test a hull breach, pressing to see what held.

"Why the decoy route?" she asked finally. "If you trusted Balogun, why lie about where we were even to him?"

"Because Balogun is careful, but Balogun's sources aren't. And because I've been on the wrong end of a leak before." Ines exhaled. "I gave him a false route so that if *anyone* squeezed him—Sable, the navy, anyone—we'd have bought ourselves days. It wasn't a lie to you, Margo. It was armor. For all of us. I just forgot to tell you I was wearing it."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Somewhere in the flooded nave, water dripped from the ruined roof onto stone, keeping a rhythm older than any of them.

"Bo," she said. "How bad?"

"He's angry. He'll get over it or he won't, but he's asking for you both, and he's asking me to tell you the engine parts are salvageable—we can be moving in two days if we can reach the boat."

"The boat's at the lock."

"The boat is where I moved it," Ines said, and there was the ghost of her old grin in the static. "While everyone was shooting at each other, I towed her three miles down the south channel and hid her under a fish-drying rack. Nobody looks at fish racks."

Despite everything—despite the helicopters and the shrapnel and the three years of counting miles alone—Marguerite felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched. "You hid our boat under a fish rack."

"I hid our boat under a fish rack."

A pause. Then Kofi leaned toward the radio, and Marguerite tilted it so he could speak. "Ines. It's Kofi." His voice was young and certain. "I believe you."

There was a sound on the other end that might have been a laugh or might have been crying; with Ines it had always been hard to say. "Tell your sister-in-arms the next burst goes out at 0400 with your real position," she said. "Balogun's man in the delta will meet you at the mouth of the Serpent Channel, day after tomorrow, dawn. And Margo—" Another breath. "Thank you for listening before deciding."

"Don't thank me yet," Marguerite said. "Ask me again when we're all in the same boat."

She killed the transmission and sat back, and Kofi looked at her with an expression too old for his face. "You forgive her," he said. Not a question.

"I'm working on it." She checked the patrol schedule Reyes had given her, tracing the penciled routes by feel in the dark. "And she bought us the thing we needed most, which is time. That counts for something. Most things count for something, eventually."

* * *

Dawn came up green-gold through the collapsed wall, and with it came the sound of a single outboard motor, unhurried, approaching from the north channel.

Marguerite woke Kofi with a hand over his mouth and had the chest halfway to the rear sacristy before the boat rounded the cypress line. She recognized the silhouette at the helm before she recognized anything else—the way he stood, weight forward, the way he cut the engine early and let the boat drift, conserving sound the way other men conserved ammunition.

Reyes stepped out onto the flooded churchyard alone. No squad. No rifle slung. Just the sidearm at his hip and a canvas bag in one hand, and the morning light catching the grey that had come into his temples since the last time she'd seen him above water.

He stopped at the edge of the nave, where the floor became water, and looked at the leaning bell tower and the drowned pews and the saints fading off the ceiling, and then he looked at her.

"This is where you've been," he said. "Under my search grid for thirty-six hours. My men motored past this church four times."

"Your schedule said they would," Marguerite said. "That was rather the point of giving it to me."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "I keep telling myself I gave you that schedule because I wanted the documents recovered safely, without bloodshed. And then I spend every night since checking the grid to see if you're still alive, and I understand that I lied to myself." He set the canvas bag on a dry pew. "I brought food. And bandages. Your deckhand was bleeding when you scattered—I saw it from the patrol deck."

"He's fine. It was shallow." Kofi emerged slowly from the sacristy shadows, and Reyes's gaze went to him and softened in a way that Marguerite had never seen it do in the years she'd known him, before the accident, before the drowning that hadn't happened. Reyes had a younger brother. She'd forgotten that. Or she'd buried it with everything else.

"There's bread in there," Reyes said to Kofi. "And cheese that isn't technically legal to transport, so eat it before I have to arrest it."

Kofi hesitated, then crossed the flooded aisle and took the bag, and the small domestic act of it—bread passed between enemies in a drowned church—did something to the air that none of their arguments had managed.

Reyes turned back to Marguerite. "I need to see them," he said. "The photographs. Before I go back."

"You've seen the manifests. You intercepted Sable's inventory."

"Inventories are numbers." His voice dropped. "Margo. Three weeks ago I signed orders routing a patrol through the Veltanian borderlands. Last week I learned those orders were drafted in the same office that drafted the deportations. I have spent my career believing my uniform stood between people and the thing that happened to that country. I need to know—with my eyes—what I've been standing in front of."

Marguerite looked at him for a long time. This was the man who had loved her once, clumsily and honestly, across the gulf of her grief; the man who had believed her dead for three years and built a disciplined life on top of that grave; the man whose duty was, at this very hour, hunting her through the marsh with orders to recover the chest *at any cost*, amended by one concession scribbled in his own hand: *the diver comes in alive.*

She lifted the chest from behind the altar and set it on the driest pew, and opened it.

The smell rose out of it—old paper, damp metal, the faint chemical ghost of photographic fixative—and Reyes went very still. Kofi, unasked, began laying the contents out on the pew beside the bread: the manifests first, columns of names and dates in the neat bureaucratic hand of a state that had since been erased from maps. Then the orders, countersigned, stamped, initialed. Then the photographs.

Reyes picked up the first photograph the way a man picks up something he expects to burn him.

He was a long time with them. The frogs went quiet and started up again. Light moved a few degrees across the ruined ceiling. He worked through the stack methodically, the way she dove a wreck—no skipping, no flinching away, giving each image its full weight—and Marguerite watched his hands begin to tremble somewhere around the twelfth photograph and did not look away, because he deserved a witness and she was the only one present who understood what it was to owe the dead your full attention.

When he reached the photograph of the camp—Kofi's camp, though Reyes didn't know that, couldn't know that the boy sitting ten feet away eating contraband cheese had walked out of that exact frame at nine years old—Reyes set it down face-up, carefully, as if it were fragile, and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"My god," he said. It was barely a sound.

"That's what I said," Kofi offered quietly, from the pew. "First time I saw them."

Reyes looked up at him. Something passed between them that Marguerite could see but not name—an understanding crossing the gap between a boy who had survived a massacre and an officer who had spent his life serving the machinery adjacent to it.

"These signatures," Reyes said, tapping the orders with one finger. "I know this man. He dined at my academy. He gave the address at my commissioning. He talked about *the river as the nation's memory*." A short, airless laugh. "He was right. It just wasn't the speech he thought it was."

"What happens now?" Marguerite asked.

Reyes straightened. She watched him do it—the physical act of a man putting his uniform back on from the inside, knowing it no longer fit, wearing it anyway because taking it off in front of witnesses would solve nothing. "Now I go back," he said. "I report that I searched the church grid and found nothing. I buy you another day, maybe two, before they reassign the search and send someone who doesn't owe you a concession." He paused at the waterline where the nave began, and for a moment he was not an officer at all, just a tired man standing in a drowned church, looking at the woman he'd mourned. "Margo. When this is over—if it's ever over—you and I are going to have a conversation about three years of obituary. But not today. Today, take your crew and your chest and go give those people their names back."

He stepped into the water, wading toward his boat, and Marguerite caught his sleeve.

"Elias." She hadn't said his given name aloud in five years. It landed between them like a stone in still water. "Why? Why any of this—the schedule, the concession, today. Duty doesn't bend this far."

He looked down at her hand on his arm, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made the answer simple in a way it had never been simple before.

"Because I read the manifests," he said, "and I realized I've spent fifteen years saluting the people who wrote them. And because you're alive, and I have spent three years being sorry to a ghost, and I find I'd rather be useful to the woman." He freed his sleeve gently. "Both of those are true at the same time. I'm learning to live with that."

Then he was wading away, and the outboard coughed once, twice, caught, and the sound of it diminished down the north channel until the marsh took it back.

* * *

They left the church at dusk, paddling south with the chest lashed between two cypress trunks like a coffin being ferried, which in a sense it was—though Marguerite preferred to think of it as the opposite of a coffin. A cradle. Paper bones due to be raised.

Kofi navigated. He was uncannily good at it, reading the marsh the way she read current, and she suspected—though he had never said—that the camps had taught him this too, that a child learns the land he is trying to escape across with a precision no mapmaker ever matches.

Halfway down the channel, the radio crackled. Ines's burst, 0400 sharp, and beneath the code a single line in plain text, which she must have known would make Marguerite's heart stop and restart:

*Balogun confirms scans transmitted abroad 18 hours ago. Story holds until you arrive. Bo says hello. Fish racks forever. — I*

Marguerite read it twice and then passed it to Kofi, who read it and grinned, and for the first time since the locks the grin reached all the way up.

Ahead, the channel widened toward the delta gate, and beyond it, two days out, a journalist with a safehouse and a transmitter and twenty years of patience. Behind them, somewhere in the dark, a navy without a search grid and a fixer without leverage and a nation's buried dead, waiting to be named.

Marguerite dipped her paddle and pulled.

One mile, she thought. Then the next.
