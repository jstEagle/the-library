## 16. What Reyes Carries

The marsh cathedral had a bell tower that no longer held a bell, only a nest of herons and twenty years of rain rot, and Commander Emilio Reyes stood beneath it in the blue hour before dawn with his sidearm holstered and his career in his hands like something already broken.

He had come alone. That was the first decision, made in the dark of his cabin aboard the *Vigilante* while the patrol boat's engines idled against the current: he would go into the flooded church without Marines, without Sable's people, without the radio operator who logged every transmission out of this sector. He had told Lieutenant Amara he was conducting a shoreline reconnaissance ahead of the morning sweep, and she had believed him, because she had no reason not to, because for eleven years he had been the kind of officer who did exactly what he said he would do.

Eleven years. He turned the number over as he waded the last thirty meters through water warm as breath, his boots finding the submerged flagstones of what had once been a village street. Eleven years of doing what he said he would do, and it had taken him three days to understand that the man who gave the orders now was not that man anymore—or perhaps had never been, and Reyes had simply been too far away, too useful, too willing to read *necessary* wherever the word *unthinkable* should have been.

The church rose out of the marsh on its own island of stone, its roof half fallen, its walls furred green to the height of the old flood line. Light moved inside it—not electric light, not firelight, but the pale diffusion of a lantern shuttered behind cloth, the kind of light people used when they did not want to be seen but could not bear the dark.

Reyes stopped at the doorway, water at his knees, and knocked on the stone with the flat of his hand. Three times. The way you knock on a door, not a tomb.

Inside, silence. Then the whisper of movement, and Marguerite Okafor's voice, low and level, coming from somewhere above him in the ruined gallery.

"You're alone."

"I'm alone."

"If you're lying, Kofi drops the chest into forty feet of black water and we scatter, and your morning sweep finds nothing but eels."

"Then I'm glad I'm not lying." He looked up. He couldn't see her—only the faint geometry of the broken balcony against the graying sky where the roof had opened—but he could hear her breathing, controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a woman measuring the distance between them in terms of exits. "Marguerite. It's me."

"That," she said, "is exactly what worries me."

* * *

She came down eventually. She came down because she was Marguerite, and Marguerite had never once in her life let fear make her decisions for her—she only ever let it inform them, the way a diver lets the current inform the route without allowing it to choose the destination. She descended the rotted stair with a dive knife in one hand and the lantern unshuttered now, and when the light found his face she held it there a long moment, studying him the way she'd study a hull for stress fractures.

He knew what she was looking for. He looked for it in himself, standing there knee-deep in a drowned church with his cap in his hand like some schoolboy: the tell, the crack, the place where the officer ended and the man began. Three years ago she had known all those places. She had mapped them more thoroughly than she'd ever mapped any river bottom. Now she searched his face like a stranger's, and he understood that this was the price of the life he had chosen—the uniform, the chain of command, the slow accretion of duty over everything soft—and that he had been paying it for three years without knowing the account had already emptied.

"You look older," she said.

"So do you."

"I am older." She set the lantern on a ledge of stone above the waterline. Its light filled the nave in a soft amber dome, catching the heron nests in the rafters, the dark mirror of the floodwater, the far archway where a shape moved—Kofi, he realized, the boy from the survey crew, watching from the shadows with a paddle in his hands like a spear. "You came here to arrest me. Or you came here to warn me. I've spent two days deciding which, and I still can't tell, so I'm going to ask you plainly, Emilio, because plain is all I have left."

"Ask."

"Which man walked through that door?"

Reyes took a breath. The church breathed with him—the water lapping, the herons shifting overhead, the whole ruined structure settling around their voices like an old animal making room.

"The second one," he said. "I think he's been walking around in the first one's coat for a long time, and last night he finally noticed it didn't fit."

* * *

They sat on the gallery stairs, above the waterline, the lantern between them. She kept the knife. He didn't ask her to put it away; asking would have been an insult to both of them.

He told her about the dead drop. About Sable—arrested at the riverport two nights ago, or rather taken, because the men who took him wore no uniforms and drove a vehicle with diplomatic plates, and by morning the naval intelligence office in the capital was issuing orders with the Minister's own signature: recover the chest at any cost, silence all witnesses, treat the salvage crew as hostile assets. He told her about the interrogation summary that had crossed his desk marked *Eyes Only*, and how he had read it twice, looking for the crime, and found instead a list of crimes—the manifests, the transport ledgers, the photographs—and how the word *recover* in the Minister's order meant not *return* but *erase*, along with everyone who had touched it.

"I requested one concession," he said. "That you be taken alive."

"And they granted it?"

"They granted it because they think I want you alive to interrogate." He turned his cap in his hands. The band was damp with river air. "Let them think it. It bought you forty-eight hours. Maybe less."

Marguerite was quiet for a while. In the amber light her face was all planes and patience, the face of a woman who had spent three years teaching herself to wait out anything—weather, grief, the navy itself. But he saw her thumb move along the spine of the knife, back and forth, back and forth, the small restless motion of someone whose waiting had finally reached its limit.

"Tomas used to say the river keeps everything honest," she said at last. "You can lie to a client, lie to yourself, but the water tells you exactly what you are. How much air you've wasted. Whether your knots will hold." She looked at him. "What does the service keep honest, Emilio? Because from where I sit, it looks like it keeps everything except that."

"It used to keep me honest," he said. "Or I thought it did. There's a difference, and it took me twenty years and one order signed by a minister to see it." He reached into his jacket then, slowly, telegraphing every motion, and brought out the package he had carried through the marsh since midnight: a field radio, compact, military issue, its serial number ground away, wrapped in oilcloth with a bundle of papers bound in rubber bands. He set it on the step between them. "Patrol schedule. The real one, not the sanitized version they give the liaison office. Sector rotations, checkpoint windows, the gaps between the sweeps—they're wider than you'd think, if you know where to look. And the radio is tuned to the patrol net. Channel nine. You'll hear everything we hear."

Marguerite didn't touch it. She looked at it the way she'd looked at the chest, he thought—like something heavy, something that changed the displacement of a person's whole life just by being in the boat.

"This ends you," she said. Not a question. A measurement.

"When they trace the leak—and they will trace it, they always do—it ends me." He heard his own voice come out steady, and was distantly surprised. "I've made my peace with the arithmetic. Twenty years of service against—" he gestured, taking in the church, the marsh, the invisible river beyond, the boy in the shadows, the woman beside him, the chest somewhere in this ruin holding the names of ten thousand people the government wanted unnamed— "against that. It isn't close. It was never going to be close. I just needed someone to make me do the sum."

"Why?" The word came out of her harder than she probably intended. "Why now? Why not three years ago, why not last month, why walk into my operation, sign the contract, run the surveillance—why all of it, Emilio, if this was where you were going to land?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the one he had been carrying upriver like ballast since the night he'd first seen her name on the salvage manifest and felt the floor of his ordered world tilt.

"Because I thought you were dead," he said.

The herons shifted above them. Somewhere out in the marsh a frog went silent, then another, the quiet spreading outward like rings on water.

"Three years ago," he went on, "they pulled a body out of the Sera near Kilometer Forty-One. Diver's gear, no identification, a week in the water. They asked me to view the remains because I'd filed the missing-persons report—I was the closest thing to next of kin the system could find, since you'd listed no one." His hands were steady. He made them stay steady. "I identified you, Marguerite. From a bracelet. The one with the depth markings you never took off. I stood in that cold room and I signed the paper, and I buried you in my head, and I grieved you, and then I took the posting on this river because it was the last stretch of water you'd worked, and I suppose I thought I could stand watch over it. Over you. Like an idiot. Like a man keeping a lamp lit in a house that burned down."

Marguerite had gone very still. The knife lay forgotten in her lap.

"Then your name surfaced on a salvage contract, and I told myself it was coincidence, a common name, and then I saw the survey footage—just your hands, gloved, tying off a lift bag—and I knew. Nobody ties off a bag like that. Nobody in the world." He laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know what I did? I reported it. I confirmed the identity match and I filed it and I continued the surveillance, because that was the job, because the chest mattered, because the mission mattered. And every day since, I have watched you through optics and intercept logs and informant reports, and I have told myself that duty comes first, and every day the man saying it sounded less like me and more like the Minister."

"Emilio—"

"Let me finish. I've rehearsed this in the dark for two nights and if I stop I won't start again." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the far wall, a giant hunched over the water. "I can't come with you. I need you to hear that and believe it, because I know you, and you'll spend the whole run downriver thinking there's a chance I'll show up alongside you, and I won't. If I defect, they don't just catch you—they close every gap in the schedule, they burn Balogun's safehouse, they sweep the delta clean before you're within fifty miles of it. My usefulness ends the moment I'm on your deck. Out here, wearing this uniform, lying to these men, I'm worth ten boats to you. On your deck I'm worth one corpse."

"That's not—"

"It's arithmetic," he said. "Your arithmetic. River arithmetic. What's the load, what's the current, what's the safest line. I'm cargo you can't afford to carry."

She was silent. Above them the sky through the broken roof had gone from gray to the first thin gold of morning, and the light came down into the nave in a slanted column, touching the floodwater, turning it briefly to hammered bronze. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice he remembered from a different lifetime, from a rented room in a riverport with rain on the shutters, from before.

"You identified my body," she said. "From a bracelet."

"Yes."

"And you never told anyone you weren't sure?"

"I was sure." He closed his eyes. "That's the worst of it. I was sure enough to sign. I wanted it to be you, in some terrible way I've never admitted to anyone—because if it was you, then you hadn't left me. You'd only died. People forgive the dead, Marguerite. They don't forgive being left."

The words hung in the column of gold light. He heard her exhale, long and slow, a diver's exhale, emptying everything.

"I didn't leave you," she said quietly. "I left myself. There wasn't anything left over to leave you. Tomas died because of a call I made—a shortcut I took to save forty minutes, and the cable parted, and he was on the wrong end of it. After that I couldn't be a person anyone counted on. So I became nobody. Invisible. I figured if no one was counting on me, I couldn't kill anyone else." She picked up the knife, looked at it, set it down on the stone between them, handle toward him—an offering, or a surrender, or simply a woman tired of holding weapons in houses of God. "And then this chest came up out of the water with ten thousand people in it, and every one of them is counting on me now, and I'm terrified, Emilio, because I know exactly what happens to people who count on me."

"They live," he said. "Look around you. Bo is alive because you got him off the locks. The boy is alive. Ines is alive. The fisherman who sheltered you—he's alive because you moved on when you could have stayed. Whatever you think you owe the dead, you've been paying it forward to the living for three years and calling it nothing."

"You don't know that."

"I know you," he said. "I identified you from a bracelet, Marguerite. I've been identifying you my whole life."

* * *

They climbed to the gallery together, because she wanted him to see the chest, and he understood that this—more than the radio, more than the schedule—was the real transaction of the morning. Trust, shown and not spoken. She lifted the lid while Kofi watched from the stairwell, arms crossed, and Reyes looked down at the paper bones of Veltania: the transport manifests in neat bureaucratic columns, the camp photographs with their gray faces and numbered rows, the order bearing the signature of a man who was now a respected elder statesman with buildings named after him.

Reyes had read the summaries. He had not seen the faces.

He knelt on the rotted boards of the gallery and looked at the faces for a long time. One photograph showed a line of people at a rail yard, winter coats, children in front. In the corner, half out of frame, a woman held up a child so the child could see over the crowd—toward whatever camera, whatever clerk, whatever future she imagined might be watching. Reyes looked at that raised child for a long moment, and then he took off his cap, which was a foolish, formal, useless gesture, and did it anyway, because there was nothing else in his hands that was equal to it.

"My grandfather was Veltanian," he said. "Did you know that? Before the erasure. He came south in the second wave and never spoke the language again, not once, not to my mother, not on his deathbed. I used to think it was grief." He replaced his cap. "It was obedience. He was showing me how to survive the same country that did this. And I learned the lesson so well I grew up to enforce its silence." He stood. "Take it to Balogun. Take it past me, past the checkpoints, past the Minister himself if you can. And when the inquiry comes—when the story breaks and the tribunals start and they come looking for witnesses—put my name in it. Not as a hero. As an officer of a navy that burned a fishing village to hide paperwork. Let me carry that publicly. It's the only weight I've got that's worth anything."

Marguerite closed the chest. Her hand rested on the lid a moment, flat, like a blessing or a promise.

"The schedule has a gap tomorrow night," she said. "Kilometer Two-Ninety to the delta gate. Four hours, no patrols."

"You've already read it."

"I read fast."

Despite everything—despite the marsh and the morning and the wreck of everything he had built—Reyes smiled. "Bo will like you," he said. "Ines will pretend not to."

"And you?"

"I'll be on the *Vigilante*," he said, "calling in false contacts, logging phantom sightings, buying you every hour I can steal. And when they trace it back to me, I'll be standing on my own deck when they come, because I'm still, after everything, a man who does what he says he'll do." He looked at her across the chest, across the twenty years and the three years and the drowned church full of morning. "This is goodbye, Marguerite. The real kind. I need you to let me have it—to let it be clean. No promises to meet in the delta, no letters, nothing they could use. Just this: I loved you before you disappeared, and I love what you're carrying now, and both of those things are true at once, and I'm done pretending truth has to travel in single file."

She crossed to him then, and for a moment—one breath, two—she laid her forehead against his, the way divers do, the way river people do, a greeting and a farewell and a sharing of air all at once. Her hair smelled of marsh water and woodsmoke. Her hand gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise, and he let it.

"Channel nine," she whispered. "Don't get careless."

"Don't get caught."

Then she stepped back, and the distance between them became the distance it had always been destined to be—the width of a river, the length of a career, the span of a life measured in miles instead of years—and Reyes went down the rotted stair and out into the water and waded away from the marsh cathedral without looking back, because looking back was a luxury for men with lighter loads.

By noon he was aboard the *Vigilante*, cap squared, reports filed, lying fluently to Lieutenant Amara about the reconnaissance. By evening the patrol net carried his voice, calm and authoritative, vectoring his boats away from Kilometer Two-Ninety toward a phantom contact in the eastern channels.

And in his breast pocket, where the Minister's orders used to sit, he carried a single photograph taken from the chest's edge—copied, not stolen, though the distinction no longer mattered to him. The woman lifting the child toward the camera. He did not know their names. He intended to learn them, someday, from a newspaper, or a tribunal record, or a history book that would exist only because a salvage diver and her misfit crew had once paddled a lead-lined chest through crocodile water by night.

Until then, he would hold the channel open. He would hold the line. He would carry what he carried.

At 0300, the radio in his cabin crackled once—three clicks, then silence. The signal they had agreed on. *We're moving.*

Reyes keyed the net, reported all stations quiet, and began, with the steady hands of a man dismantling his own life plank by plank, to falsify the morning's patrol log.
