## 17. The Delta Gate

The river changed character a mile before the delta began, the way a man changes his walk when he knows he's being followed. The broad brown current that had carried them for two hundred miles began to fray at its edges, splitting into braided channels that wound through stands of mangrove and reed, each fork a question and none of them answered on any chart Bo owned. The water went from the color of old rope to something paler, milkier, thick with silt that had traveled from mountains Marguerite would never see. Somewhere ahead, past all those branching throats, the river would give itself to the sea.

Somewhere ahead, too, was Ade Balogun's safehouse — a stilt house on a channel Ines knew from her smuggling years, marked on no map, reachable only by water that looked like every other water around it.

Marguerite stood at the wheel of the *Magpie* and did not let herself think about how close they were. Close was a word that invited the river to laugh.

Behind them, according to the patrol schedule Reyes had pressed into her hand in the flooded church three days ago, came the gunboat *Vigilant*. Behind them, if Sable's network was half as good as Sable claimed, came whatever else the navy could scrape together. Ahead — she squinted into the haze where the channels braided apart — ahead was the unknown, which after everything was the only enemy she hadn't yet learned to fear properly.

"Engine's running sweet," Bo called up from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had been white once, in some previous life. "I'll say this for the old girl: she doesn't know she's supposed to be dying."

"Nobody tell her," Kofi said from the bow, where he crouched with a pair of binoculars he'd bought off a fisherman for a handful of batteries. He swept them across the mangroves, across the water, across the pale sky. "Nothing yet."

"Yet," Ines said, from the galley doorway, arms crossed. She'd stopped pretending to cook an hour ago. "There is always a yet with you people."

Marguerite almost smiled. Almost. Her hands stayed easy on the wheel because they had to be; everything below her wrists was clenched hard enough to ache.

The chest sat in the hold beneath a false floor Bo had built, wrapped in oilcloth, lashed to the keel line where the boat rode steadiest. Two hundred miles it had ridden there. Manifests. Photographs. Orders signed by men whose names had been scrubbed from history books but not from paper. Paper bones, Kofi called them, and Kofi would know, because Kofi had been one of the living things those orders had tried to make into bones.

Two miles. That was what Ines kept saying. Two miles of channel from the last navigable point to Balogun's stilt house, and then the *Magpie* couldn't go further — too shallow, too tangled with fish traps and the pilings of abandoned fish camps. The last two miles would have to be swum or paddled, chest and all, through water that Ines described, with characteristic delicacy, as "crocodile water, and I am not being picturesque."

But first they had to reach the last navigable point. And first, before that, they had to pass the delta gate.

Marguerite saw it before Kofi did, because she'd been watching the water ahead instead of the banks. A channel narrowed between two mangrove islands, maybe eighty meters across, and moored squarely across its throat — deliberately, insolently, the way a man parks a truck across a road — was a boat.

Not a navy boat. That was the first thing her eyes told her, scanning hull lines the way Tomas had taught her to scan wreck profiles: fast, once. A river cruiser gone soft, cabin windows smoked glass, a crane arm folded on its aft deck. Painted a gray so neutral it might have been any government's or no government's. It flew no flag. It didn't need to. Marguerite knew the silhouette the way you know a voice on the phone.

"Sable," she said.

Kofi lowered the binoculars slowly, the way you do when sudden movement might be the wrong message to send. "That's him? How did he—"

"He's been ahead of us since the locks." Since the marshes. Since maybe the beginning. She turned the wheel a few degrees, casual, a course correction that meant nothing, buying herself seconds to think. "He doesn't chase. He waits. Chasing costs money. Waiting makes it."

Ines came up beside her, low, keeping her body below the line of the wheelhouse glass out of an instinct older than any of their wars. "How many men does he have?"

"With him? Six, eight. Behind him?" Marguerite shook her head. "Everyone he's ever bought. Which is everyone."

"Options," Bo said, climbing up into the wheelhouse, and the word had the flatness of a man who already knew the list was short.

"We go back." Ines again. "Find another channel. There are dozens—"

"There are dozens of channels that dead-end in swamp, and one of them has Balogun at the end of it, and we don't have fuel to wander." Marguerite heard her own voice come out level and was briefly grateful to whatever part of her had learned, over three years and eleven thousand river miles, to speak from somewhere above the panic instead of inside it. "And even if we wandered, the *Vigilant* is behind us. Reyes got his schedule stolen, but the rest of the fleet still has theirs. We go back, we meet them head-on in open water where we can't run and can't hide."

"So we go forward." Bo said it quietly, looking at the blockade, doing arithmetic with his eyes — distances, speeds, angles, the geometry of steel meeting steel. "Through him."

"Through him," Marguerite agreed.

"Or," Ines said, "we stop. We talk to him. He offered to buy the chest, Marguerite. Men who offer to buy don't always shoot."

"Men who offer to buy are telling you exactly how much your silence weighs." Marguerite remembered the riverport, Sable's linen suit, his voice like warm honey poured over something with teeth underneath. *Name your figure, Captain. Everyone has one.* She had thought then that she'd won the exchange by walking away. She understood now that he had simply filed her answer under *pending* and moved on to plan B. "If we stop alongside that boat, we lose the chest, and probably Kofi, and probably ourselves. Dead men don't testify."

"Then we're really doing this." Kofi's voice cracked in the middle of it, fifteen years old and trying to be twenty-five, and Marguerite's chest tightened in a way that had nothing to do with fear for herself. "We're going to hit a boat."

"We're going to hit a boat," she confirmed.

* * *

She gave the order to prepare the way she imagined a surgeon gives the order to cut: quickly, clearly, without dwelling on what lay beneath.

Bo stripped the deck of anything loose — the dive tanks lashed down double, the galley stove shut off and braced, Kofi sent below to lie flat against the engine room bulkhead, which was the thickest steel on the boat and the farthest from the sides. Ines brought up the chest itself, and here Marguerite made the decision she'd been turning over since the church: the chest rode topside now, lashed to the aft deck in plain view, wrapped in a tarp.

"If they board us, they find it anyway," she explained, cinching the last knot. "But if they see it, they'll shoot to disable, not to sink. Nobody sinks the thing they've spent two hundred miles trying to recover intact."

"And if they don't care about intact?" Ines asked.

"Then we were dead either way, and I'd rather die moving."

Bo took the engine room. Ines took the bow with a fire extinguisher and the boat hook, which drew a sound out of her that might have been a laugh in a better century. Marguerite stood alone at the wheel, the way she had stood alone at the wheel of every boat she'd run since Tomas died, and felt the old loneliness rise up in her like floodwater — and then felt it break against something new. Because this time, when she looked around the wheelhouse, she wasn't alone. There was a boy below decks who trusted her with his life. There was an engineer who had rebuilt her engine twice without being asked. There was a cook with war in her past holding a boat hook like a lance because Marguerite had asked her to.

They had chosen her. The least she could do was get them through.

The gap closed. Eighty meters became sixty became forty, and details resolved out of the gray: men on Sable's foredeck, two of them, then four, one raising something long and dark to his shoulder. A loudhailer crackled to life, and Sable's voice came across the water, unhurried, almost fond.

"*Magpie*, Captain Okafor. You look tired, my dear. Come alongside, tie up, and let's settle this like professionals. You have my word — and I think by now you know my word is the only currency on this river that never devalues."

Marguerite picked up their own hailer, thumbed the switch, and found she had nothing clever to say. So she said the true thing.

"You know what's in the chest, Sable. You've known since the riverport. Which means you know it isn't cargo. It's testimony. And I'm not selling testimony."

A pause, just long enough to be theatrical. Then, gently, almost sadly: "Captain. Everyone sells. Some of us just get a better price than others. Last chance. My associates are not sentimental, and neither, I'm afraid, is the gunboat behind you. Check your wake."

She didn't check her wake. She didn't need to. She could feel it now, faintly, through the hull — a deep-throated vibration arriving through the water itself, the way you feel a train before you hear it. The *Vigilant*. Reyes. Coming up the same channel at flank speed, because somewhere behind him an admiral had decided that the cost of one salvage boat and five lives was cheaper than the cost of the truth.

Twenty meters. Fifteen. On Sable's foredeck, the man with the long weapon shifted it from pointing-at-her to pointing-beside-her, and Marguerite understood: warning shots first, then the engine, then boarding. They wanted the boat alive. They wanted her compliant. They had time to be patient, because they thought she had time to be afraid.

She put the wheel hard over and brought the throttle to the stops.

* * *

Later, she would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory, she decided afterward, is a coward; it blurs exactly the moments that define you.

What remained sharp was this: the *Magpie* surging up onto plane, the tarp-wrapped chest straining against its lashings, Ines flattening herself behind the bow rail. The gray cruiser growing in the windshield, filling it, Sable's figure on its upper deck tilting slightly as he registered — a half-second too late — that the little salvage boat wasn't slowing down. The loudhailer barking something, her name maybe, or an offer, or a threat, swallowed whole by the roar of her own engine.

And Reyes. Because at the last moment, in the corner of the windshield's edge, she saw it: the *Vigilant*'s bow coming around the bend behind them, close enough now to see the gun mount, close enough to see figures at the rail — and among them, unmistakably at three hundred meters, a posture she would have known drowned, burned, or blind. Reyes, standing very still, watching her aim her boat at another boat, and doing nothing. Not yet. Holding his fire the way he had held it in the marsh cathedral, mercy coiled inside the hunt, waiting to see what she would become.

She rammed the blockade.

The impact was less a collision than a change of religion. One instant the world was engine-noise and spray; the next it was a white detonation of pain and noise, the wheel kicking against her palms hard enough to bruise bone, the *Magpie*'s bow climbing Sable's port quarter with a shriek of tearing steel that she felt in her teeth, in her spine, in the fillings Tomas used to tease her about. Glass went everywhere. Something heavy — the extinguisher, the boat hook, a piece of the wheelhouse itself — struck the deck beside her. For one endless suspended moment the two boats were locked together, grinding, the *Magpie* riding up and over the cruiser's gunwale like a dog mounting a fight it had no business picking, and through the shattered windshield Marguerite looked directly into the face of one of Sable's men, three meters away, eyes wide, holding a rifle he had suddenly forgotten how to use.

Then the boats tore apart, and gravity remembered them both.

The *Magpie* fell off the wave of the collision and slammed back onto the river, and Marguerite went down with her, knee cracking against the deck, hand finding the wheel by pure animal habit. The engine note changed — she heard it the way a mother hears a child cry differently in the night — a new rattle underneath the roar, a rhythm stumbling where it had been smooth. Steam, maybe. Water in places water shouldn't be. Bo's voice came up the engine tube, stripped of everything but function:

"We're holed! Above the waterline, I think — I've got steam, I've got—" A clang, a curse. "Rudder's answering. Rudder's answering. Go, go, keep going!"

Keep going. She hauled the wheel over and the *Magpie*, bleeding, listing slightly to starboard, obeyed. Behind them the gray cruiser wallowed, its port bow crumpled inward like a dented tin cup, men scrambling along its rail, and someone aboard was firing now — she heard the cracks, saw the water jump in white stitches off their stern, closer than comfort, farther than death. From the *Vigilant* behind: nothing. No gunfire. Just that deep diesel thunder, closing.

"Ines!" she shouted. "Report!"

"Everyone breathing!" Ines hauled herself back along the bow rail, hair whipping, alive and furious about it. "Kofi?"

"Here." Small voice from below. Alive. Good.

"The channel forks in four hundred meters," Ines shouted, dropping into the wheelhouse, blood on her cheekbone she didn't seem to have noticed. "Left goes to Balogun. Right goes to the fish camps and then nowhere. Tell me you can steer."

"I can steer." What she couldn't promise — what she could feel rising through the deck plates with every second, the engine's stumble worsening, the vibration going from rattle to shudder — was for how long.

Three hundred meters. Two hundred. The fork appeared out of the murk, the left channel narrowing between walls of mangrove root, dark water, low branches. Too narrow for the *Vigilant* to follow at speed. Maybe too narrow to follow at all. Maybe — and this was the prayer she didn't say aloud — too shallow for anything bigger than them to bother with.

The engine coughed. Properly coughed, the way a man coughs when the blood is coming up.

"Bo?"

"Working on it!" Faint, furious, muffled by steel. "Give me one more minute of throttle, that's all I need, ONE MINUTE—"

She gave him the minute. She stood at the ruined wheel with glass in her hair and blood starting down her shin from somewhere she'd catalogued and dismissed, and she fed the dying engine every revolution it had left, and the *Magpie* ran the last two hundred meters of its life like a wounded animal making for tall grass — staggering, smoking, magnificent.

The mangroves swallowed them. The gunfire fell astern, muffled by green walls. The channel bent, and bent again, and narrowed until branches scraped both rails at once, and then, with a sound like a long sigh, the engine died.

Not slowed. Died. Total, absolute silence except for the tick of cooling metal and the lap of water and, distantly, behind them, the *Vigilant*'s engines idling somewhere beyond the green — big boat, deep draft, stopping where the channel stopped, unable or unwilling to follow.

Marguerite let go of the wheel. Her hands wouldn't straighten. She looked down at them, curled like claws around air, and laughed once — a short, ugly, honest sound.

"Everybody out," she called. "This is as far as the boat goes."

Bo emerged from the hatch black with grease, one sleeve scorched, holding a wrench like a scepter. "Coolant line's sheared and the raw-water intake's cracked. I can fix it. Tomorrow. With parts. And a machine shop." He looked around at the mangroves, the brown water, the two miles of crocodile country between them and safety. "So. Today, we swim."

"Not swim," Kofi said.

They all turned. The boy had come up on deck, and there was something in his face Marguerite had never seen there before — not the careful grown-up mask he wore, not the flicker of the camp survivor. Something older than both. He was looking at the water the way other people looked at home.

"Poles and paddles," he said. "Fish traps mean stakes — there'll be stake fences all through this channel, and between them the water's shallow enough to stand in, mostly. My grandmother poled a dugout through water like this when I was six. She carried me on her back." He met Marguerite's eyes. "You carry the chest. I'll take us there. Two miles. Maybe three. But nobody puts a foot in deeper than their waist unless I say."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Ines started pulling paddles from the locker, and Bo started unshipping the spare oar from under the gunwale, and Marguerite went aft to untie the chest, and the *Magpie* settled on the silent water behind them like a faithful animal lying down at the end of a long day.

Two miles. Through crocodile water, poling a dead boat, with a gunboat behind them and Sable's men somewhere ahead or beside or God knew where, and a boy of fifteen leading them home through channels only his dead grandmother had ever known.

Marguerite put her shoulder to the pole, felt the mud take it, and pushed.
