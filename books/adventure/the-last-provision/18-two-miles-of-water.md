## 18. Two Miles of Water

Kofi had learned to swim before he learned to read.

That was the way of the delta camps — water first, everything else after, if there was an everything else at all. The older children taught the younger ones in the brown shallows behind the barracks, holding their bellies on their palms while they kicked, counting strokes in a language half of them were forgetting. *One. Two. Breathe.* You breathed when you were told or you drowned, and nobody wrote your name down anywhere, so nobody would even know to grieve you. Swimming in that place was not a sport. It was a form of obedience, and then, later, when the soldiers came to the riverbank with their lamps and their dogs, it was a form of escape.

He was nine years old the first time he swam through dark water with something precious held above his head. It was his sister's school notebook, wrapped in oilcloth, and he crossed two hundred meters of channel with it while the camp burned behind him, and he did not stop until his hands found the roots on the far bank, and he did not open the notebook for three days because he was afraid of what it would mean if the pages were dry.

He was seventeen now, and the water was colder, and the thing above his head weighed as much as a man.

*One. Two. Breathe.*

The engine had died with a sound like a struck bell — one deep metallic note that ran the length of the hull and then nothing, no second note, no third, just the river slapping against the dead boat and Marguerite's voice cutting through the dark: *Out. Now. Take the chest.*

Sable's boat sat across the channel like a closed fist. His men had spotlights but not many, and the burning hulk of the patrol launch downstream threw enough orange light into the smoke to make shadows everywhere and nowhere. The navy was coming from behind them; Kofi could hear it, a low diesel thrum under the water's noise, patient as a heartbeat. Two miles of open water between this spot and Balogun's safehouse, two miles of channel the maps called crocodile country, two miles with a dead engine and a lead-lined chest full of paper bones.

There was no vote. There wasn't time for one. There was only Marguerite stripping off her boots, Ines lashing the chest to the inflatable salvage float with the quick, brutal knots she used for everything, and Bo saying, in his flat engineer's voice, "I'll stay with the boat."

"Bo—" Marguerite started.

"Somebody has to make them think we're still aboard." He was already moving to the stern, already pulling the tarp over the deck gear, arranging shapes in the dark that might, from a hundred meters, look like people crouched behind cover. "Run the light on the battery. Make it flicker. They'll think we're crippled and fighting, and they'll come slow, and by the time they board her she'll be empty." He looked at Kofi then, and in the flare of the emergency lamp his face was very calm, the way it always went calm right before he did something enormous. "You know these waters better than any of us, boy. Get them there."

"I'm not a boy," Kofi said, because he said it every time, and because saying it was easier than saying what he actually felt, which was terror, which was gratitude, which was *don't die, don't die, don't die.*

"No," Bo agreed. "You're not."

Then the lights came off the water and the four of them slid over the gunwale like spilled mercury, and the river took them.

* * *

The chest floated. That was the first miracle — Ines had sealed it in salvage bags and lashed it beneath the float so that only its shadow rode the surface, a low black shape that broke the current into a long V. But a floating thing is a visible thing, and every few minutes Sable's searchlight swept the channel, and each time it did, Marguerite's hand pressed down on Kofi's shoulder and all of them sank, all of them together, down into water the temperature of a held breath, hanging on the float's lines while the light crawled over the place where their heads had been.

Kofi counted for them. He didn't decide to; his body just did it, the old camp rhythm rising out of him like silt. *One. Two. Breathe.* Down meant hold. Up meant stroke. He tapped Ines's arm once for up, twice for down, and she passed it along to Marguerite, and Marguerite to Reyes — because Reyes had come, Reyes had abandoned his own patrol boat in the smoke and shown up swimming, and none of them had time to be astonished about that yet — and the whole chain moved on the count like one animal with four hands.

That was the thing Kofi would remember afterward, in the years when he told this story and did not tell other stories: not the cold, not the fear, but the moment the crew stopped being four people and became one body. It happened somewhere in the first mile, without anyone naming it. Ines stopped swimming beside him and started swimming as part of him, her kick finding his rhythm so their wakes braided into a single line. Marguerite's breathing fell into the count without being asked. When the current shoved them toward a snag of drowned trees, six hands found the float lines at the same instant, corrected, released, all in the same half-second, no words, no wasted motion, the way a single body corrects its balance without consulting itself.

His sister would have understood it. Ama had talked once about the work crews in the camp, how the women who dug the drainage ditches moved like that after a season — not coordinated but *communal*, one shovel's rhythm passing down the line until forty backs bent as one back. She'd said it was the only beautiful thing the camps made. People who had nothing left to lose except each other.

The crocodiles were real. Kofi never saw more than eyes — amber coins riding the black water, here, gone, here again, thirty meters off, then fifteen — but he had grown up with them the way city children grow up with traffic, and he knew what they were telling him. They were telling him they were curious. Curious was survivable. What you couldn't survive was splashing, panic, blood. So he kept the count slow, kept the entries silent, kept everyone's limbs inside the float's shadow, and when the biggest one surfaced forty meters ahead and lay there like a log with opinions, he simply steered around it, wide and patient, the way you'd steer around a sleeping dog, and Marguerite followed his hand without question.

She trusted him. That kept arriving in him in waves, almost funny at the worst moments: *she trusts me.* Three months ago he'd been a dock kid stealing fuel line, and now a woman who had survived things he only half-knew about was putting her life on his count, stroke by stroke, in crocodile water, at night, hunted by two boats. Whatever else happened — whatever the navy did, whatever Sable did, whatever the morning brought — he would keep that. Nobody could take it out of him. It was the first thing anyone had given him since Ama died that he intended to keep forever.

*One. Two. Breathe.*

Halfway across, the shooting started.

It came from behind them — from the boat, from Bo — short, disciplined bursts, three rounds at a time, spaced out like a man who knew exactly what he was doing and wanted everyone to know it too. Muzzle flash strobed the smoke. Somewhere out on the water Sable's men shouted, and a spotlight swung away from the channel toward the dead boat, and Kofi felt the whole swimming body of them go still beneath the surface, listening, while Bo bought them another minute with noise.

"He's drawing them," Ines whispered against Kofi's ear, her lips almost touching skin, her voice smaller than he'd ever heard it. "He's drawing them off us."

"We keep moving," Marguerite said. Her voice was iron laid over something shaking. "He knows the plan. We do not waste his plan."

So they moved. And the shooting moved with them, walking them down the channel like a shepherd's dog, bursts to the east whenever the spotlight drifted west, bursts to the west whenever it drifted east, and Kofi understood with a cold clarity that Bo was spending his position to buy theirs, trading away his own safety meter by meter, and that the arithmetic of it was the most frightening kindness Kofi had ever been inside of.

Then the burst that came was different. Longer. Closer. And it ended with a sound Kofi knew from the camps, a sound he had hoped never to hear again in this life — the flat crack of a round finding flesh, followed by a silence that had a person-shaped hole in it.

The radio at Marguerite's belt crackled. Static. Then Bo's voice, strained and strange, almost conversational, the way hurt men talk when they're holding themselves together with sentences:

"Float's yours. Don't come back. That's an engineering order."

* * *

They did not go back. Bo had built the order into the plan, and they honored it the way you honor a load-bearing wall — because the whole structure stood on it. But Kofi heard the sound Ines made when the radio went quiet, a sound with no name, a sound he'd heard from women in the camp at night, and he reached through the black water and found her wrist and held it, and counted, and pulled.

The last mile was the hardest, though nothing shot at them. Adrenaline drains a body like a bilge pump, and the cold got into their muscles and turned every stroke into a negotiation. Marguerite's shoulder was torn open where a splinter had caught her during the ramming — she hadn't mentioned it, of course, because she was Marguerite — and by the final eight hundred meters she was swimming on will alone, her kicks going shallow, her breath ragged against the count. Reyes took the float line from her without asking. She let him. Kofi watched that happen and understood it was bigger than it looked: Marguerite Okafor letting someone carry her weight, letting herself be part of a body instead of insisting on being the whole one. Three years of invisibility, of carrying everything alone, dissolving one shared kilogram at a time in a crocodile river.

The safehouse showed itself as a smell first — woodsmoke, frying oil, the green rot-smell of stilt houses over backwater — then as a single kerosene lamp hung high on a pole, exactly where Ines had promised it would be, burning steady as a promise kept. Balogun's people waded out to meet them, silent men with poles and torches doused, and they took the chest between them like pallbearers, and Kofi's feet finally found mud, real mud, river-bottom mud, and he stood in it shaking while the count in his head wound down and stopped.

*One. Two.* 

Breathe.

He breathed, and it came out as a sob, and nobody pretended they hadn't heard it, and nobody made anything of it either. Ines put her arm around his shoulders and they stood like that, two orphans of the same war, watching the black water they'd just crossed.

Marguerite collapsed onto the stilt steps and started laughing — the cracked, disbelieving laugh of a woman who has just understood how close the margin was. Reyes crouched beside her, dripping, saying nothing, his hand hovering near hers without landing, a man learning a new kind of patience. And then the radio in Marguerite's belt hissed again, weak, wet-sounding, and Bo's voice came through like a man talking through a door:

"Still floating. Took one in the side — through and through, I think, left of the ribs. Sable's boys have the boat. I'm in the reeds, half a mile downstream of where we split. I can see the lamp." A pause, long enough for fear to fill it. "I can't walk it. Just so we're honest with each other."

Kofi was already turning to the silent men with the poles. "We need a boat," he said. "Small. Quiet. Now."

Marguerite looked at him — at this seventeen-year-old giving orders on a stranger's stilt landing — and whatever she saw in his face, she didn't argue. She just nodded once, and started pulling her boots back on over soaked feet, and said the sentence that ended the night and began whatever came next:

"Nobody we carried gets left in the water. Not tonight. Not ever."

Downstream, in the reeds, a man was bleeding and keeping watch over nothing but his own courage, and Sable's boats were still out there, sweeping their lights along the banks, closing like a hand. Two miles they had won. Half a mile remained, and it was the kind of mile that killed.

Kofi stepped into the borrowed boat and took up a pole, and began to count.
