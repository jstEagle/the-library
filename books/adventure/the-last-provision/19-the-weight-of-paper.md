## 19. The Weight of Paper

The safehouse was a stilt house at the end of a drowned lane, half-swallowed by the delta's slow green water, and it looked like nothing at all — which was, Marguerite understood, precisely the point. A fisherman's shack, maybe. A place where nets dried and nobody asked questions. Ade Balogun had chosen it the way he chose everything: invisibly.

They came in through the reeds with the chest lashed to a salvaged outrigger, paddling the last hundred meters because even an outboard's cough could carry too far across flat water at dusk. Kofi took the bow. Ines took the stern. Marguerite knelt in the middle with her hand flat on the lead-lined lid, feeling the cold of it through her glove like a pulse that beat too slowly to be alive.

Two miles back, the *Mirenne* sat dead on a sandbar with her engine flooded and her hull holed below the waterline where Sable's boat had clipped them in the channel. Two miles of open water behind them, and behind that, somewhere in the dark, Reyes's navy closing at patrol speed. They had made it. Barely. The word *barely* had become the whole shape of their lives.

Balogun met them at the ladder with a lantern hooded by his own hand, and up close he was smaller than his byline suggested — a compact man in his fifties with reading glasses pushed up into gray-threaded hair and the forearms of someone who still hauled his own water. He looked at the chest first, then at each of their faces, one by one, the way a doctor reads symptoms.

"You're hurt," he said to Kofi, who had a rope burn wrapping his palm.

"It's nothing."

"Inside. All of you." He paused at the top of the ladder and said, quietly, so only Marguerite heard: "Where is your engineer?"

Marguerite's stomach dropped as if she'd stepped off a ledge in dark water. "Bo stayed with the boat. Someone had to—"

"No." Balogun's voice didn't rise, but it changed register, the way a river changes when the bottom falls away beneath it. "Bo is not with the boat. Bo came ashore an hour ago at the fuel dock two channels east. My people saw him taken. Three men, a gray skiff, no lights. Sable's men."

* * *

The interior of the safehouse smelled of kerosene and old paper, which Marguerite would learn later was simply the smell of Balogun himself — a man who had spent thirty years turning other people's catastrophes into ink. There was a generator, a shortwave rig, a satellite phone wrapped in a cloth like something sick, and a folding table where the chest was set down with more reverence than any of them had ever shown it, though they had bled for it twice.

Ines lit the lamp. Kofi sat on the floor with his back against the wall and his burned hand cradled against his chest, and he did not cry, but his breathing had the ragged edge of a boy holding himself together with both hands.

"They took him because of us," Kofi said. "Because we wouldn't sell."

"They took him because Sable is losing," Marguerite said. "There's a difference. Losing men do desperate things, and desperate things are loud. Loud things make mistakes." She heard herself say it and recognized the voice — Tomas's voice, the old dive-brief cadence, *plan the dive, dive the plan* — and felt the familiar grief pass through her like cold water through a wetsuit. She let it. Grief was a tool now. It kept her hands steady.

Balogun had already opened the chest. He worked fast, gloved, photographing each document against a black cloth background before he so much as read it, and while he worked he talked, low and continuous, the way some men pray.

"The manifests are the spine of it. Deportation transports, Veltania to the eastern camps, dated, signed, countersigned. The photographs are the flesh." He held one up briefly — rows of men in a yard, wire visible at the frame's edge — and Kofi turned his face to the wall. "But the orders are the soul. Signed directives. Not regional excesses, not officers running rogue — ministry stationery, ministerial signatures. Your government didn't lose control of this, Captain Okafor. Your government *authored* it."

"I know what my government authored," Marguerite said. "I've been carrying it downriver for three weeks."

"Yes." Balogun set down the photograph with terrible gentleness. "And I need you to understand what happens in the next hour, because everything after depends on it. I can transmit these scans tonight. Encrypted relay, three hops, ending in newsrooms on three continents. By morning the story exists outside this country's borders, in a thousand copies no one can drown. But transmission takes time — ninety minutes, perhaps more, and during those ninety minutes every man with a gun who knows what you carried will be trying to reach this room." He removed his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt, unhurried, a man who had been shot at before and had made peace with arithmetic. "So. Do we transmit?"

"We transmit," Marguerite said.

"Captain—" Ines started.

"We transmit," Marguerite said again, "and then we deal with what's coming. One thing at a time. That's how you survive deep water. You don't solve the whole dive at once. You solve the next meter."

Ines held her gaze for a long moment, and whatever she saw there — the exhaustion, the guilt, the thing that had passed between them in the marsh cathedral and again in the locks — she nodded once and went to check the shortwave's antenna run without another word.

Kofi hadn't moved from the wall. "What about Bo?" he asked, and his voice cracked on the name, and Marguerite thought: *he calls him brother already, of course he does, this crew has been a family since the night run and none of us ever said it aloud.*

"Sable wants the chest," Marguerite said. "As long as he thinks the chest is the prize, Bo breathes. The moment he learns the chest doesn't matter anymore—" She stopped herself. Looked at Balogun. "How long until the first hop completes?"

"Forty minutes to the first mirror. Ninety to full redundancy."

"Then for forty minutes, the chest is still worth something." She stood, rolled her shoulders, felt every bruise from the ramming report itself along her ribs. "Which means for forty minutes, I'm worth something too. Because I know where it is, and Sable doesn't trust anyone else's knowledge. He'll want me. And I'm going to give him exactly what he wants."

* * *

She went out onto the platform alone, into the mosquito-thick dark, and keyed the handheld radio they'd taken from Reyes — his stolen gift, his career-ending parting present — and called the frequency Sable had left burned into her memory since the riverport, since the first time that charming voice had told her, over warm beer and warmer smiles, that he knew what she carried.

"This is Okafor."

Static. Then, smooth as oil on still water: "Captain. You've been difficult to reach. I hear your boat is dead on a bar. I hear you've been swimming with crocodiles. You really must stop making my job so dramatic."

"You have my engineer."

"I have a large man who keeps apologizing to my men for the state of their noses. He's fine. Uncomfortable, but fine. He sends regards, and also he'd like me to tell you that under no circumstances should you trade anything for him, and also that he's sorry about the engine, which he says was not his fault, and honestly, having seen the damage, I believe him." A pause, silk over steel. "You have something of mine, Captain. I have something of yours. This is the oldest conversation in the world. Let's have it like adults."

"Come to the safehouse yourself. Alone. No skiffs, no guns I can see. You come, we talk, you get your paper, I get my man."

"And why would I agree to terms that favor you?"

"Because you don't know where the safehouse is," Marguerite said, "and I'll never tell you otherwise, and because every hour you spend hunting costs you and your exile council money and exposure you can't afford. Because I'm offering to end this tonight. And because, Sable — you're not going to shoot the only person who can tell you whether the documents are real."

Silence on the line, long enough that she counted her own heartbeats against it. Then the laugh, soft and genuine-sounding, which was the most frightening thing about him.

"There she is," Sable said. "The woman who raised a wreck from a river everyone pretends is empty. Give me an hour. One skiff, one lantern, one very charming broker. Don't disappoint me, Captain. I've invested so much hope in you."

He cut the line. Marguerite stood on the platform with the radio against her chest and the delta breathing around her — frogs, water, the far-off diesel mutter of a patrol boat on a channel she couldn't see — and she let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she was about to do. Then she counted to ten, the way she counted everything now, in miles and meters and breaths, and went inside to lie to her family.

* * *

"You're not going alone," Ines said.

"I am."

"That's not negotiation, that's suicide with extra steps."

"Ines." Marguerite took her by both shoulders. In the lamplight she could see every year the war had put on the older woman's face, and every year the river had added since. "Listen to me. If Sable comes here with men, we fight, and fighting means shooting, and shooting near a kerosene lamp and a table full of the only irreplaceable papers in the world ends with all of it burning. If I meet him on the water, alone, it stays small. Small is survivable. I've lived small for three years. I'm good at it."

"You were done living small," Ines said. "That's what the marsh was. That's what Reyes was. You told us — you chose truth over silence. So choose it now. We go together or—"

"If I don't come back," Marguerite said, "transmit anyway. Everything. Names, faces, signatures, all of it. Promise me."

Ines stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes — the war, always the war, the thing she never spoke of except in fragments — and then she did the last thing Marguerite expected. She hugged her. Hard, brief, smelling of diesel and woodsmoke and the particular salt of a woman who had decided years ago she would never cry in front of anyone again.

"You come back," Ines said into her ear. "I've buried enough people. I'm not burying you. Swear it on Tomas."

The name hit like cold water in the ears. Marguerite closed her eyes. "I swear it on Tomas."

Kofi pressed something into her hand at the ladder — his good hand — and she opened her fingers to find the boy's diving knife, the cheap one, the first tool he'd ever owned, the one he'd used to cut himself free of a fence he'd never described in full. "Bring it back," he said. "It's got my whole life on it. You don't get to lose my whole life."

"I'll bring it back," Marguerite said, and meant it the way you mean oaths: completely, and knowing you might break it.

* * *

The meeting point was a drowned copse a half-mile east, dead trees standing in black water like the masts of a fleet that had sunk standing up. Marguerite arrived first, in the little pirogue, and tied off among the trunks where the shadows were deepest, and waited, and did the thing she had done at the start of every dive for fifteen years: she planned it. Exits first. Then threats. Then the single sentence she would build everything else around.

Sable came exactly on the hour, one skiff, one lantern hung at the bow, and he was indeed alone, or appeared to be, which in Sable's grammar amounted to the same thing. He wore white linen despite the mosquitoes, because of course he did, and he brought a bottle of something amber and two tin cups, because the performance was the man.

"Captain Okafor." He killed his engine and drifted the last few meters, hands visible, smiling. "You look terrible. River life disagrees with you."

"Where's Bo?"

"Ashore, fed, unharmed, currently lecturing my men on torque specifications. I confess I like him enormously. He reminds me of a younger, larger version of myself, before commerce softened my edges." He lifted the bottle. "Drink?"

"I'll stand."

"Everyone stands, at first." He set the bottle down untasted, which told her more than the smile did: he wanted his hands free too. "Shall we conduct our business? The chest, Captain. Where is it?"

"On dry land. Somewhere you'll never find it, and somewhere that stops mattering the moment you take me to Bo."

Sable sighed with theatrical disappointment and produced, from inside his linen jacket, not a gun but a folded sheet of paper, which he held up to the lantern. Even at distance she could read the letterhead. Ministry stationery. A signature she'd spent three weeks staring at.

"You think I want the paper," Sable said. "Captain. Please. The paper is worth money, yes — the exile council will pay a fortune for it, certain governments will pay more, collectors will pay obscene amounts for the signature alone. But paper can be copied. Paper can be forged. Paper can be *discredited*. What cannot be copied, forged, or discredited—" he tapped his own chest, twice, gently, "—is the witness. The woman who pulled it from the water. The diver whose testimony makes it true. My council doesn't want your documents, Marguerite. They want *you*. Alive, articulate, and grateful. You testify at the tribunal they intend to force into existence, you give the massacre its face, and they will make you wealthy beyond anything salvage will ever pay. Or—" the smile thinned, showing the wire beneath, "—you refuse, and I deliver you to Officer Reyes instead, and we discover together how much your navy pays for traitors. Either way, Captain, you come with me. The only question is whether you come as an asset or a prisoner. I strongly recommend asset. The food is better."

Marguerite stood in the pirogue among the drowned trees and felt the trap close around her with the slow, hydraulic patience of deep water — and felt, beneath the fear, the other thing. The thing she'd been gambling on since she keyed the radio. Because Sable had just told her, in his elegant, circumlocutious way, the one fact she needed: *he did not know.*

He didn't know about Balogun. He didn't know about the scans. He didn't know that in a kerosene-lit room half a mile west, an old journalist was feeding the massacre onto a relay that would carry it beyond the reach of any council, any government, any fixer in white linen. Sable still believed the chest was the treasure and the witness was the accessory. He had it exactly backward, and by the time he understood how backward, it would be too late for him to matter.

All she had to do was keep him believing it. Keep him talking. Keep him here, in the drowned trees, spending his hour, while the first mirror hop completed somewhere overhead in the indifferent dark.

"You want me to testify," she said slowly, buying seconds, watching the lantern flame bend in the river breeze. "For the exile council."

"For history, Captain. History pays better than you'd think."

"And Bo walks free either way."

"Bo walks free the moment you step into my skiff."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I make a radio call, and within twenty minutes this copse is full of men who are considerably less charming than I am, and we skip several pleasant stages of our negotiation." He glanced at his watch, a slim gold thing, absurd out here. "Though I'll note, Captain, that you haven't actually said no. You've been standing there doing arithmetic. I respect arithmetic. What are you calculating?"

*Whether ninety minutes have passed,* she thought. *Whether the story is already loose in the world, whether it's already a thousand copies wide, whether right now, at this moment, you've stopped being able to drown it — whether the weight of all that paper has finally shifted off my shoulders and onto yours.*

What she said was: "I'm calculating whether you're a man who honors his bargains."

"Captain." Sable placed one hand over his heart, wounded. "I have never once broken a bargain. I have merely renegotiated the terms retroactively, which is entirely different, and much more legal."

Somewhere beyond the dead trees, far off across the black water, a sound reached them — thin, high, unmistakable. A whistle. Not a boat's whistle. A signal whistle, three notes, repeated. Ines's signal. The one they'd agreed on in the marshes, in the cathedral, in the long nights of planning: *it's done. It's out. It's done.*

Sable's head turned toward the sound, and Marguerite watched understanding arrive on his face the way floodwater arrives in a low field — gradually, then all at once. His hand went inside his jacket, no longer performing.

"What," he said softly, "was that?"

Marguerite smiled at him — the first real smile she'd given anyone in three years, tired and fierce and entirely unafraid.

"That," she said, "was the sound of you becoming worthless."
