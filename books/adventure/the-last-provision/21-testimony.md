## 21. Testimony

The hearing room had once been a customs house, and it still smelled of river—old wood, old water, the ghost of cargo that had passed through and been taxed and forgotten. Marguerite noticed these things because noticing was easier than breathing. She sat at a plain table beneath tall windows streaked with salt haze, her hands flat on the wood, and waited for them to call her name.

They had given her a name to be called by. That was the strange part. For three years she had worked under variations of M. Okafor—Marge, Margot, the woman with no last name who took contracts nobody else wanted—and now a clerk with a reedy voice read out *Marguerite Adaeze Okafor* as though it had always belonged in an official record, as though it hadn't spent three years buried like everything else she'd tried to sink.

The room was fuller than she'd expected. Not packed—the government would never allow that—but full enough. Journalists in the gallery, their notebooks open before anything had happened, hungry. A cluster of families on the left side, people who had traveled from the interior on buses that took two days, holding photographs laminated against the humidity: faces of the disappeared, the deported, the drowned. Veltanians whose nation had been erased twice—once by decree, once by silence.

And among them, one face that made her chest tighten: a woman who looked like Kofi looked when he slept, which was to say like someone braced for the door to open.

Kofi himself sat behind her with Ines and Bo. Bo's arm was still in a sling from Sable's men, his shoulder stitched and angry-looking, but he'd refused to stay at the clinic. "I carried that chest two miles through crocodile water," he'd said. "You think I'm missing this?" Ines wore her good dress, the blue one, and had braided Kofi's hair herself that morning with the tenderness she usually spent only on food. None of them spoke. They didn't need to. They had become fluent in each other's silences somewhere between the locks and the delta, and this silence said: *we are here, we are not leaving, whatever you say in there will still be true when you come out.*

The commission itself sat at a curved table under the seal of a republic that had, until six weeks ago, insisted nothing had ever happened on that stretch of border river. Seven commissioners. Four lawyers for the state. One lawyer for her, a sharp-faced woman named Duval whom Balogun had arranged, who kept touching Marguerite's elbow and saying *you don't have to say more than you want to say*, and whom Marguerite kept gently shaking off.

Because that was the whole point. That was what Tomas had taught her without meaning to, in the last conversation they ever had, standing on the deck of the *Providence* while the fog came down the valley like something poured.

*You count the miles, Marguerite. But someday you're going to have to count the reasons.*

She had thought about that sentence every day for three years. She had built her whole life on not counting the reasons—invisibility as discipline, anonymity as penance, the ritual of logging river miles in a waterproof notebook so that distance could stand in for time and time could stand in for grief. The chest had ended all that. The chest had dragged her up into the light along with everything else the river kept.

"Commission calls Marguerite Adaeze Okafor."

She stood. Her knees held. She walked the eleven steps to the witness table—she counted them, because counting was what she did—and sat, and adjusted the microphone an inch closer, and looked out at the room.

The presiding commissioner, an old jurist named Halvorsen with a voice like gravel poured slowly, asked her to state her name and occupation for the record.

"Marguerite Okafor. Salvage diver." She paused. "Formerly of the *Providence*. Currently of the *Lantern*."

"And how long have you worked the rivers, Ms. Okafor?"

"Fourteen years. Eleven with my own crew."

Halvorsen nodded and let the pause stretch. She understood the technique; she'd seen enough interrogations from the wrong end of a contract to recognize a man giving her rope. But she wasn't going to hang herself. She was going to do something harder.

"I understand," Halvorsen said, "that your testimony today concerns two matters. The first is the wreck designated Courier Vessel 114, raised this spring on the restricted reach of the Serane River. The second—" he glanced down at his papers, and when he looked up again his gravel voice had gone quieter, almost gentle, "—is an incident three years ago involving the loss of the salvage vessel *Providence* and the death of one diver, Tomas Ferreira-Lindqvist."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. In the gallery, pens stopped moving. Behind her, she heard Bo exhale through his nose, slow and controlled.

Marguerite folded her hands on the table. She had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in the dark of the *Lantern*'s cabin, in the marsh cathedral while the search boats swept their lights across the flooded nave, in the hospital corridor outside Bo's room. Every rehearsal had failed her. Good. Rehearsed words were the navy's words, the government's words, the smooth language of people burying things. What she had instead was raw, and raw was what the room needed.

"Yes," she said. "Both matters. And I'd ask the commission's indulgence to take them in the order they actually happened, which is the reverse of the order you've listed them."

Halvorsen's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Proceed."

"Three years ago I was running a wreck survey on the lower Providence channel. Winter water, low visibility, current stronger than the forecast said. My dive partner was Tomas Ferreira-Lindqvist. We'd worked together six years. He was—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, deliberately, because smoothing it over would have been a lie of a different kind, "—he was the better diver of the two of us. Everyone who worked with us knew it. He was also the one who said we should wait a day for the current to drop. And I was the one who said the client wouldn't wait, and the money was short, and I was the captain, so we went."

She heard the small sounds a room makes when it stops pretending to be neutral. Somewhere a page turned. The woman who looked like Kofi had gone very still.

"The accident was mine," Marguerite said. "Not the river's. Not bad luck. Mine. I misjudged the current, I cut our surface interval too short because I wanted the job done, and when Tomas signaled that his line had fouled, I went after him instead of anchoring first—which is textbook, which is the first thing they teach you, and I knew it, and I did it anyway because I believed my own skill was a substitute for procedure. It isn't. It never is. He died forty meters below me while I fought a current I should never have asked him to enter. I got his body back. I couldn't get him back."

She stopped. Let it sit. The microphone carried the sound of her breathing into the speakers, and she did not apologize for it.

"Afterward I did what cowards do and called it discipline. I stopped using my full name. I took contracts no one would trace. I logged river miles instead of days because miles don't ask questions. I told myself I was honoring him by staying invisible, but invisibility isn't honor. It's just a smaller place to hide. And I want the record to show that I learned that lesson the hard way, this year, on the Serane, because hiding is exactly what the men who sank Courier 114 were counting on from everyone."

Halvorsen leaned forward. "We'll come to the courier vessel. But first—for the record, Ms. Okafor—do you wish to add anything regarding Mr. Ferreira-Lindqvist? The commission has his service file. It is, frankly, exemplary."

"He hated coffee," she said, and something that was almost a laugh moved through the families' section, quick and startled, like a bird crossing water. "He drank it every morning and complained about it every morning. He kept a photograph of his mother taped inside his mask case. He sang badly and constantly on decompression stops. He believed—this is the important part—he believed that the dead deserved to be found, that every wreck was a debt owed to somebody waiting on shore. That's why I became a salvager, and it's why I couldn't walk away from the chest when I found it. Everything I did this spring, I did in his debt. I am not asking this commission to absolve me. I am asking it to write down that the fault was mine, so that his name doesn't have to carry it."

For a long moment Halvorsen said nothing. Then he wrote something on his papers, unhurried, and Marguerite had the odd sensation of watching her confession become an official object, weighty and permanent, no longer hers alone to carry.

"The commission notes your statement," he said. "Now. Courier Vessel 114."

* * *

She told it the way she'd lived it, mile by mile: the liaison with the off-the-books contract and its secrecy clauses; the hull holed from the inside; the remains chained in the hold; the lead-lined chest with the crest of a country that no longer appeared on maps. She described the manifests, the photographs, the transport orders bearing signatures that matched, per Balogun's forensic analysis, four officials now sitting in senior positions in this very government. She watched those officials' faces as she said it—watched the fourth one, a deputy minister with damp palms, reach for his water glass twice and miss it both times.

She did not raise her voice. That surprised her most of all. She had expected rage; rage had carried her out of the burning village, rage had put her hand on the wheel when she rammed Sable's blockade. But here, under the tall windows, in front of the families with their laminated photographs, rage felt like a luxury. What came out instead was precise, and precision, she was learning, was its own kind of fury.

"When I opened that chest," she said, "I found the names of eleven thousand people. Not numbers. Names. Dates of deportation, camp assignments, execution orders initialed in the margins. Twenty years this government has said Veltania was a border dispute, a regrettable episode, a closed chapter. I am a salvager. I know what a closed chapter looks like. It looks like a hull on a riverbed with the hatches welded shut. This chapter was never closed. It was sunk. And the difference matters, because you can't grieve what you're told never existed, and you can't hold anyone accountable for a crime with no name. The chest gave the massacre back its name. All I did was carry it downriver. Any diver with a conscience would have done the same. Most would have done it faster."

A lawyer for the state—a sleek man named Corven who had spent the morning trying to establish that the chest's recovery violated maritime law—took his turn. "Ms. Okafor, you've admitted to concealing recovered material from a naval oversight officer. You've admitted to evading a lawful checkpoint. You've admitted to ramming a vessel in a navigational channel. How does the commission reconcile your lawbreaking with your professed commitment to truth?"

"Badly," Marguerite said, and waited out the ripple. "I broke laws. I'd break them again tomorrow, and I'll tell you why. There are two kinds of order in this world. There's the order that protects people, and there's the order that protects the people who burned a fishing village to keep eleven thousand corpses quiet. I chose between them at about two in the morning, running black through the rapids with a boy asleep on my charts who survived the camps those orders created. If that choice was illegal, then the law needs what's in this room today more than it needed my obedience. Write down my violations. Fine them. Jail me if you must. But put the orders in the record first, and put the signatures next to them, and then decide which of us endangered the republic."

Corven sat back down. He did not ask a follow-up question.

* * *

They broke at midday. Ines pressed a thermos of broth into her hands in the corridor—she'd made it from dried fish and river herbs, tasting of the *Lantern*, of home that had snuck up on Marguerite unawares—and Kofi leaned against her shoulder for exactly three seconds, which was his way of saying everything. Bo said nothing, just touched his sling in a little salute, diver to diver.

It was in the corridor, on her way back in for the afternoon session, that she saw Reyes.

He stood near the far window, out of uniform. That was the first shock. Commander Emil Reyes without his uniform was a lean, tired man in a grey jacket, hair going silver early at the temples, carrying himself like a man who had set down something heavy and hadn't yet learned where his hands went. Stripped of command—that was the phrase the newspapers used, and the word *stripped* had a violence to it that Marguerite suspected was accurate. He'd given her the patrol schedule and the stolen radio knowing it would cost him everything, and it had, and he had paid it without complaint, and now here he was, paying the interest.

"You came," she said. It came out smaller than she intended.

"I was subpoenaed," he said. Then, seeing her face, he almost smiled. "That's a joke, Marguerite. I came because I was asked to testify this afternoon. And because—" he looked down the corridor, at the families, at the doors of the hearing room, anywhere but at her for a moment, "—because I spent twenty years believing the navy was the river's keeper. Finding out what it was keeping the river *from saying*—I need to hear it said out loud. In a room like this. Where it counts."

"They'll take your testimony apart."

"They already took my command apart. Testimony is easy after that." He turned to her fully then, and she saw that the surveillance-sharpness was gone from his eyes, or transformed, the love in them no longer a lens but simply a light. "Duval tells me you named Tomas. Named yourself."

"There wasn't any other way to say it."

"No," he agreed quietly. "There never is. I spent three years grieving a woman I thought was dead, and then three months learning that the living can be harder to find than the dead. You were right there, the whole time, underwater." His mouth twisted. "I'm not here to ask you for anything. I want that on the record between us, since neither of us can get it into theirs. I'm here because when they strip away the uniform and the contract and the chest and the boat, what's left is that I loved you before the *Providence* went down, and I—"

"Don't," she said softly. Not a refusal. A request for patience.

He nodded. He understood patience; he'd been patrolling her silences for months. "Afternoon session starts in ten minutes," he said. "Walk in with me? People talk less when an ex-commander's scowling behind you."

Despite everything, despite the hearing and the sling and the eleven thousand names, Marguerite laughed—a real laugh, brief and rusty, startling both of them. They walked the corridor together, not touching, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and it felt like the first honest mile she'd logged in years.

* * *

Reyes testified after her. He confirmed the patrol schedule, the burn order for the fishing village, the chain of command that ran from the deputy minister's office to the gunboats. He did it in a flat, disciplined voice, and when Corven accused him of betraying his oath, Reyes answered, "My oath was to the republic and its people, sir. Not to the men who embarrassed it. I finally checked which one I was serving. I recommend the commission do the same." The gallery erupted and Halvorsen had to call order twice.

It was nearly dusk when Halvorsen called her back for one final question, the one she'd known was coming since morning.

"Ms. Okafor. The commission has heard your confession regarding the death of Mr. Ferreira-Lindqvist, and your account of the courier vessel. Before we adjourn: is there anything further you wish the record to contain?"

Marguerite looked out at the families. At the woman with Kofi's sleeping face. At the empty chair the Veltanian exile council had kept for the dead. At Reyes, standing at the back with his hands finally at rest.

"Yes," she said. "Two things. First: the names in the chest belong to the families in this room, and to every family that couldn't afford the bus fare. This commission should return them the way we returned the chest—not sealed, not redacted, not 'in due course.' Due course is where truth goes to drown. Second—" and here her voice did finally shake, and she let it, "—Tomas Ferreira-Lindqvist used to say that every wreck is a debt owed to somebody waiting on shore. I owe him a debt I can't repay to him. So I'm repaying it forward. Whatever this commission decides about my penalties, I'm asking—no, I'm telling you—that I will spend the rest of my working life finding the wrecks this government sank on purpose, and raising them in daylight, with names attached. Starting with the ones your own records say are still down there. That's not a threat. It's a schedule."

She stepped down. The room did not applaud—commissions don't permit it—but the families rose, all of them, silently, and stayed standing until she reached the door, and that was worth more than applause. That was a fleet saluting.

Outside, the river wind came up the street smelling of rain and diesel and evening. Kofi, Ines, and Bo were waiting on the steps, and beyond them, leaning against a lamppost as if he had nowhere else in the world to be, was Reyes, out of uniform, carrying a paper bag of coffee he would certainly complain about.

Marguerite descended the steps toward all of them, and found, to her astonishment, that for the first time in three years she was not counting miles at all.

She was counting the people.
