## 22. The Next Mile

The dry dock smelled of tar and cut timber and, faintly, of the river itself—the smell that had gotten into everything they owned and none of them wanted out. Marguerite stood on the planking beside the *Marta Cielo* with a mug of Ines's coffee going cold in her hand and watched Bo argue with a winch.

"You're over-torquing it," she said.

"I'm *calibrating* it," Bo said, without turning around. His arm was still in a sling from Sable's men—six weeks now, and he'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt, which Marguerite considered progress—but his hands worked the wrench one-handed with the same stubborn precision he'd used to hold an engine together through rapids at midnight with no lights. "There's a difference."

"There is not a difference."

"Then why do I have the wrench?"

She let him have it. There were arguments worth having and arguments that were just another way of standing next to someone on a morning you were glad to be alive for, and this had become the second kind somewhere along the way without her noticing when.

The boat looked different than she remembered her looking three months ago, before the locks, before the marshes, before the two miles of crocodile water that had nearly ended all of them. The navy's patrol boat had put a gash in her portside hull that Bo swore he could fix and then spent eleven days fixing, muttering the whole time about people who rammed blockades in vessels not built for ramming blockades. New planking, paler than the old, so the hull wore a scar of light wood like a bandage that hadn't yet weathered to match. Kofi had suggested painting over it. Marguerite had said no. Let it show, she'd said. A boat should be allowed to remember what happened to her too.

Kofi was up in the rigging now, sixteen years old and half spider, re-running the halyards while singing something in a language that was half his mother's tongue and half every riverport radio station between here and the delta. He'd grown since the inquiry. They all had, though only he seemed to have done it visibly, in inches, as if the story finally being told had given his body permission to catch up to itself.

Marguerite drank the cold coffee and counted.

It was a habit she'd thought she would keep forever: miles, not years. Three years since Tomas died she had measured her life in river distance because distance was honest in a way time wasn't. Time just passed. Distance you chose, or failed to choose, or fled. She had kept a small notebook, oil-stained, its cover soft as cloth from handling, and in it she wrote down every mile of water she put behind her, and for three years every mile had been a mile away from something—a wreck site, a mistake, a name she couldn't say aloud.

The notebook was still in her pocket. She checked it sometimes. But the last entry was different now, and she knew it by heart:

*Mile 4,112. Delta. Balogun's safehouse. All hands accounted for.*

She hadn't written anything since. Not because the counting had stopped, but because she hadn't needed to run from anything in four months, and the notebook had been waiting, patient as a diver at depth, to see what she would write when the miles weren't escape anymore.

* * *

The contract had come by post, which already made it strange. Everything else in her life came by radio, by rumor, by men like Sable appearing at railings with smiles priced to move. This came in an envelope with a university crest and a letter signed by a historian named Dr. Amara Voss, who wrote in the careful, slightly apologetic prose of someone who knew exactly how many salvage captains had thrown her previous letters away.

*Dear Captain Okafor,* it began. *You will know my name from the inquiry. I was the one asking the questions nobody wanted answered, and you were the one answering them anyway. I am writing to offer work that will not make you rich. I hope you will read to the end regardless.*

What followed was simple enough to fit on one page. The historical commission—newly funded, newly empowered, staffed by people who had spent twenty years being told the Veltanian deportations were a closed file—wanted the river surveyed. Every wreck. Every site where a boat went down carrying people or papers or both. Mapped, photographed, documented, and published. Openly. Under their own names. No secrecy clauses. No liaison officers. A clause explicitly stating that all findings belonged to the public record and that the commission would defend the crew's right to publish against any government, including their own.

Marguerite had read it four times. Then she'd laid it on the galley table and said nothing, because she'd learned in the marsh cathedral that a crew decides things together or not at all.

Bo read it twice and said, "They're paying survey rates? For open publication?" as if the concept were a mechanical part he suspected of being counterfeit.

Ines read it once, slowly, and set it down face-up, which from Ines was a speech. "Nobody's ever offered us honest work before," she said. "I didn't know it existed."

Kofi read it last, and longest, tracing the line about the public record with one finger. "It says the wrecks get names," he said. "The boats. The people on them." He looked up. "Like the camp got its name back."

"Yes," Marguerite said.

"Then we say yes," he said, and that was the vote, unanimous again, the fourth unanimous vote of a crew that had learned to count them like lucky coins.

* * *

The first survey site was forty miles upriver from the dry dock, a stretch of brown-green water that looked like every other stretch until you knew what lay beneath. The commission's records suggested three wrecks in the vicinity, unmarked, unvisited, their coordinates approximate at best because the original charts had been sealed and most copies burned in the years after Veltania was erased.

Marguerite went down herself on the first dive. Some habits deserved keeping.

The water was cold below ten meters, cold the way it always was, cold enough to make the world go quiet and simple. Her torch beam swept the silt slope and found the shadow of a hull at fourteen meters, listing hard to starboard, its superstructure furred with a decade's growth of weed. A river steamer, small, civilian. She swam the length of it slowly, reading the damage the way she'd taught Kofi to read it: where the metal bent inward, where it tore outward, what order things broke in.

There were no chains in this hold. Whatever this boat had carried, it had carried openly, and whatever had sunk it had been an accident—an old grounding scar, a sandbar's signature—and not a crime. She felt something loosen in her chest at that, some knot she hadn't known was tied. Not every wreck down here was a grave. Some were just boats that had bad luck, and those deserved recording too, honestly, without anyone flinching.

She took the photographs. She marked the position on the slate. And when she surfaced, Kofi was waiting at the ladder with the logbook open, and she called up the numbers, and he wrote them down in a hand that was getting steadier every week, under a heading he'd invented himself and printed across the top of the fresh page in careful capitals:

*SITES OF RECORD — OPEN CHART.*

"That's good," she said.

"It's true," he said, which was better.

* * *

Reyes came on a Thursday, in civvies, driving a borrowed truck, which was how he did everything now—stripped of command, stripped of uniform, a man learning to arrive places without an escort. The inquiry had cost him his career and everyone knew it, and everyone also knew he'd requested the transfer to the commission's security detail himself, a demotion disguised as a posting, guarding historians instead of hunting them.

He stood at the edge of the dry dock with his hands in his pockets and looked at the boat for a long moment before he looked at Marguerite.

"She wears her scar well," he said.

"So do I," Marguerite said, "most days."

He smiled—not the officer's smile she'd known first, nor the lover's smile she'd known after, but a third one she was still learning, quieter, unarmored. "Voss sent me with the updated charts for the upper reach. And—" he hesitated, and produced a flat package wrapped in brown paper, "—this. It came through the decommissioning inventory. It shouldn't have. Someone filed it wrong on purpose, I think."

Inside was a framed photograph, old, salt-faded: a courier boat at a dock, young crew laughing on deck, and in the corner, half-turned toward the camera, a man with Tomas's exact grin.

Marguerite sat down slowly on the planking.

"He served on her," Reyes said quietly. "Before your time. Before mine. The commission found the service records when they opened the files. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought you should have it. I thought maybe the boat should have it. Whichever wants it more."

Marguerite looked at the photograph a long time. Tomas, twenty-something, sunburned, alive, standing on a deck doing work he loved with people he trusted. She had spent three years unable to say his name and six months learning to say it, and here he was grinning at her from a frame, younger than her grief, older than her guilt, simply a man on a boat.

"The boat," she heard herself say. "It goes in the wheelhouse. Where the chart table can see it."

Reyes nodded. He didn't push. That was the thing she was learning about him now that duty had cracked open and something truer had come through: he knew how to wait. He'd waited three years believing she was dead. He could wait a season more for whatever this was becoming, this slow approach of two people who had hurt each other in the ordinary ways and forgiven each other in extraordinary ones.

"Voss wants the upper reach done before the rains," he said, businesslike again, gently. "Six weeks of good water. She says if anyone can map it, it's the crew that ran it blind at night."

"The crew that ran it blind at night," Bo called from inside the hull, "would like it noted that we are never doing that again."

"Noted," Marguerite called back, "and ignored if necessary."

Laughter moved through the dry dock—Bo's bark, Ines's low chuckle from the galley where she was already cooking lunch for five because she cooked for five now automatically, the way she'd once cooked for ghosts. Kofi whooped from the rigging. Reyes laughed too, awkwardly at first and then properly, and Marguerite watched him laugh and thought: *there he is.* Whoever he'd been underneath the uniform all along.

* * *

That evening they ate on deck, the four of them plus Reyes, plates balanced on knees, the river going bronze and then violet beyond the dock pilings. Ines had made the fish stew she made on nights that mattered, heavy with pepper and lime, and there was bread, real bread, bought ashore like people who lived ashore, which none of them did and none of them pretended to.

"To the Marta Cielo," Bo said, raising his cup. "May her new planking rot slower than her old."

"To the sites of record," Kofi said. "May there be a lot of them."

"To honest work," Ines said, and the word *honest* landed among them like a coin dropped in a quiet room.

They looked at Marguerite, because they always did, not because she led—they'd voted that down implicitly long ago—but because she was the one who counted, and they knew it, and she knew they knew it.

She lifted her cup and held it a moment over the water.

"For the mile ahead," she said.

It was the toast she'd settled on weeks ago, after the notebook, after she'd finally understood what the counting had become. Because the miles hadn't stopped. She still felt them, still tallied them in the back of her mind the way a diver tallies depth—one meter, two, the body never quite letting go of the arithmetic that keeps it alive. But the arithmetic had changed sign. These were not miles fleeing from. They were miles chosen, mile by mile, contract by contract, dive by dive, in company.

After the plates were cleared, she took out the notebook, sat alone at the stern for a while with the lamp turned low, and wrote the new entry. Her hand was steady.

*Mile 4,113. Upper reach survey begins tomorrow. Crew of four, plus one who waits on the dock. Wrecks to find: unknown. Names to give back: as many as the river will surrender. Reason: all of the above.*

She closed the book and looked upriver, where the water ran dark toward country she'd never charted, toward wrecks with no names yet and stories with no tellers yet, and felt the feeling she had once mistaken for loneliness settle into what it had actually been all along, waiting for her to recognize it: readiness.

Behind her, Kofi was teaching Reyes a card game and cheating at it shamelessly. Ines was humming. Bo was already asleep sitting up, sling and all, the way engineers sleep when the engine is finally quiet.

Marguerite stayed at the stern a little longer, watching the first stars come out over the water, and then she went forward to join them, because the mile ahead was long, and no one should start it alone.
