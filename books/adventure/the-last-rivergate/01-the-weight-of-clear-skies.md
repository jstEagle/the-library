## 1. The Weight of Clear Skies

The storm came up out of the southwest like something with a grudge, and Petra Underhill drove toward it the way other people drive away.

She'd been doing this long enough that fear had worn down to a kind of arithmetic. The cell on her radar app was a bruise-colored smear forty miles wide, crawling northeast across the salt flats at thirty miles an hour, and somewhere inside it was a hail core the size of her fist and wind shear that could flip a truck like a coin. The Vane Syndicate paid hazard rates for verified intercepts—photographic proof of funnel structure, barometric data logged at ten-minute intervals, hail samples if you were brave or stupid enough to collect them. She was both, on alternating days. The pay was good. The pay had to be good, because she owed the Syndicate eleven thousand four hundred dollars, and every dollar of it came with a smile attached.

She did the math the way she always did, one hand loose on the wheel, the flats unrolling gray-green to every horizon like the world's largest billiard table. This run, if the storm held together and she got clean structure shots plus a full data log, would clear maybe six hundred after fuel. Six hundred against eleven thousand four. Nineteen runs. Nineteen more times driving into the teeth of something that could kill her, for men who would send flowers to her funeral and itemize them on her account.

"Cheerful thought," she said aloud, because the cab was empty and the truth sounded less ugly with a voice on it.

The truck was a '09 flatbed she'd bought back when she still believed in things like equity and five-year plans. It had two hundred sixty thousand miles on it, a transmission that groaned in second gear like an old man getting out of a chair, and a hail-dented roof she'd stopped noticing years ago. The check engine light had been on so long she'd named it. Ambrose. Ambrose glowed amber on the dash now, steady as a votive candle, and Petra told him what she always told him: *not today.*

The storm agreed to be negotiated with, for a while.

She caught it on the western edge of the flats where the road dissolved into packed alkali and the sky went the color of a healing bruise. There's a specific light that lives under a supercell's shelf cloud—a green-gray, submarine light, like looking up from the bottom of a pool—and when it fell over the truck she felt the old lift in her chest, the thing that had made her choose this life before the debt made the choice for her. Underneath all the arithmetic there was still that. The sky doing something enormous and alive, and her small rig pointed at it like a question.

She pulled off a quarter mile from the road, dropped the tailgate, and started the log. Wind at her back came in gusts that smelled like rain hitting hot dust—that petrichor punch, wet stone and ozone. The pressure gauge on her handheld read 981 millibars and falling fast. Good numbers. Bankable numbers. She set the camera on its tripod mount and framed the wall cloud as it began to lower and rotate, slow as a potter's wheel, and felt the particular satisfaction of a woman watching money form out of vapor.

That was when the lightning started.

Not the polite kind—the distant flicker-and-count stuff, the kind you can put a number to. This was close lightning, the kind that arrives with no gap between flash and thunder, the kind that makes the air itself feel solid for a half second, like the whole atmosphere has clenched its jaw. The first strike hit maybe three hundred yards out, and the crack of it went through the truck bed and up through her boots and into her teeth. The second was closer. She couldn't have said how much closer, only that afterward her ears rang in a thin pure tone and the hair on her forearms stood up and stayed up, which every chaser knows is the sky's way of clearing its throat.

*Get in the truck,* she told herself. *Get in the truck, you idiot.*

But the data. The log needed another twenty minutes to be worth anything, and twenty minutes of hazard-rate time was real money, and real money was the difference between nineteen runs and eighteen, and—

The third strike hit the flats maybe eighty yards from her, close enough that she saw the channel itself, white-hot and branching, close enough that the ground kicked a spray of alkali dust into the air like the earth flinching. The thunder wasn't a sound anymore; it was a physical event, a fist against her sternum. She dropped behind the tailgate with her hands over her head and stayed there, heart slamming, tasting copper, while the rain arrived all at once like a door slamming open overhead.

She crawled into the cab soaked through, laughing the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. Her hands shook so badly she had to grip the wheel with both of them just to hold it still.

"You're fine," she said to herself. "You're fine, you're rich, that's a thousand dollars of data sitting right there."

It wasn't, quite. But it was close, and close was the best thing in her life right now, so she took it.

* * *

The storm moved on east, trailing its anvil across the sky like a bride's train, and Petra sat in the cab eating a gas-station sandwich and watching the light come back. That was the part nobody who hadn't chased understood—the silence after. The way the flats went gold and enormous and empty, the way your own breathing sounded loud. She'd chased with crews once, early days, a whole convoy of them, radios crackling, someone always singing badly. Now it was just her and Ambrose and the ringing in her ears, and she'd stopped telling herself that was temporary. Some things calcify.

She opened the ledger app. Not the Syndicate's ledger—hers. The private one, the one where she kept the count like a prisoner scratching marks on a wall.

Balance: $11,412.60. Interest accrued this month: $86.40. The interest was the part that made her want to put her fist through the windshield, quietly, without drama. She could outrun a storm but not compound arithmetic. Every month she stood still cost her nearly nine hundred dollars of chasing, just to stay exactly where she was. It was like being tied to a treadmill that also happened to be on fire.

She'd borrowed the original eight thousand four years ago to rebuild after a flood took her shop—the survey business, the legitimate one, the one with a sign and a lease and a name painted on glass. The Vane Syndicate had found her the week the insurance didn't. They'd sent a man named Doss Merrick, who wore linen suits in weather that ruined linen suits and smiled like the sun coming up, and he'd explained the terms so clearly, so patiently, twice, that she'd almost been grateful. No collateral. No co-signer. Just a friendly rate and a friendly face and a handshake that had felt, even then, like being fitted for something.

Four years later she understood what the handshake had been. A leash doesn't hurt going on. It hurts at the far end.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number, local prefix. She almost didn't answer—unknown numbers lately meant either the Syndicate's collections desk or scammers, and some days she genuinely couldn't tell the difference—but the storm was done, the day was done, and answering was free.

"Underhill."

"Petra! Hey. It's Doss." Of course it was. The universe had timing like that. "Saw your intercept post online. Beautiful structure on that cell. You've still got the eye."

"I've still got the debt," she said. "Same thing, these days."

His laugh came down the line warm as bathwater. "You know, that's actually why I'm calling. I've got something. An opportunity. Real opportunity, not the usual runaround. Can you come by the office tomorrow? Say, ten?"

"What is it?"

"It's better discussed in person. Bring your appetite—I'll have lunch in." A pause, and then, cheerfully, the way he said everything: "And Petra? Congratulations on the intercept. Genuinely. It's good work. We like good work."

He hung up before she could ask anything else, which was his way. Doss Merrick never answered a question he could defer, and never deferred one he could answer with a smile.

Petra sat with the phone in her lap and looked out at the flats going violet at the edges, and felt the feeling she always felt after talking to him, the one she had no better name for than *the cold*. It wasn't fear exactly. Fear had a shape, a direction, something to point at. This was more like standing in a room and realizing the walls had moved while you weren't looking.

An opportunity. From the Syndicate. Generosity from the men holding her leash.

She'd learned the hard way what that meant. When the Vane Syndicate offered you something for nothing, the nothing was the price, and you paid it later, with interest.

* * *

The truck died eleven miles from town, on the causeway road, with the last light draining out of the sky and the first stars showing through the torn clouds.

It didn't die dramatically. That was the insult of it. No smoke, no bang, no final heroic shudder. Ambrose's light flickered, the engine stumbled twice like a man losing his footing on ice, and then simply quit, and the steering went heavy, and Petra coasted onto the gravel shoulder with the calm of someone who has practiced this exact maneuver in her head more times than she'd admit.

She turned the key. Click. Turned it again. Click-click, and then not even a click, just the small dead silence of a machine that has decided, finally and completely, to stop.

"No," she said. "No, no—not today, Ambrose. Not today."

She popped the hood anyway, because that was the ritual, and stood in the cooling dark with a flashlight clamped in her teeth looking at an engine she knew as well as her own hands. Battery terminals: corroded but connected. Belt: intact. She tried the ignition again and got one weak cough, one sputter of life, and then nothing, and the smell that came up through the vents was electrical, sharp and wrong, the ghost of the day's lightning still living somewhere in her wiring harness.

Maybe it was coincidence. Maybe the near strike had cooked something deep in the truck's nervous system, a control module, a ground wire, something expensive. Or maybe two hundred sixty thousand miles was simply two hundred sixty thousand miles, and everything ended eventually, trucks and debts and patience alike.

Either way, the tow alone would be a hundred eighty dollars. A hundred eighty dollars out of a six-hundred-dollar intercept, before she'd paid for the fuel and the sandwich and the data plan overage. She sat on the tailgate in the dark and ran the numbers until they stopped moving, and the numbers said: *this run nets you maybe two hundred. The repair says four figures, minimum, if it's the module. The repair says you're not chasing for a month.*

A month of not chasing was $2,400 of interest-bearing stillness. A month was the treadmill speeding up.

She laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, not really a laugh at all—more like the sound the truck had made. Because here was the joke, and it was a good one, a genuinely well-constructed joke: she had spent the whole day calculating how many storms stood between her and freedom, and the sky had nearly killed her for free, and now the sky's leftovers were going to cost her more than the storm had earned. She couldn't afford the bad luck. She couldn't afford the good luck either. Good luck, it turned out, had a deductible.

Out on the flats, a coyote started up, high and lonesome, and got no answer.

Petra pulled her jacket tighter and called the only towing outfit that worked nights, and while she waited for them she opened the ledger app one more time and looked at the balance, and then, because she was who she was, she opened the folder of today's photos instead. The wall cloud filled the screen, green-black and rotating, gorgeous and indifferent, the most beautiful dangerous thing she'd seen all season. She'd sell those images. They were good. They might even be great.

Under them, the app showed her balance like a scar: $11,412.60, accruing.

Nineteen runs. She'd tell herself nineteen tomorrow, after the mechanic called with the damage. Tonight, sitting on a dead truck in the middle of nowhere with her ears still ringing and her savings nonexistent, she let herself feel it for exactly one minute—the exhaustion, the real kind, the kind that lived underneath the bravado like water under ice. One minute of admitting that she was tired all the way down, tired in the place where hope used to be stored.

Then the minute was up, because minutes like that were a luxury, and she couldn't afford luxuries.

Headlights appeared on the causeway road, still miles off, crawling toward her. Petra slid off the tailgate, brushed the dust off her jeans, and arranged her face into the expression that had kept her alive this long—easy, unbothered, a woman who laughed at lightning. The mask went on smooth as paint. It always did. That was the trick nobody warned you about: the mask gets easier every year, until one day you can't remember whether there's anything under it.

Tomorrow, Doss Merrick wanted to buy her lunch and offer her an opportunity.

Tonight, the tow truck was coming to carry her truck away, and she was going to ride in the cab of it like a passenger in her own life, watching the flats go by in the dark, counting what she owed.

The headlights grew. Somewhere far off, past the eastern horizon, new clouds were already building—tomorrow's weather, next week's weather, the sky forever cooking up something enormous and indifferent while a woman on a gravel shoulder did arithmetic against it.

"Okay," Petra said to the night, to the coyote, to the dead truck, to nobody. "Okay. What do you want?"

The night, characteristically, didn't answer. But ten o'clock tomorrow would.
