## 2. An Offer from Cheerful Men

The tow truck cost Petra one hundred and forty dollars she did not have, and the mechanic at Crooked Bend Auto—a woman named Ruthie with forearms like dock rope—threw in a diagnosis for free: *your alternator's dying, honey, and so is your fuel pump, and honestly I don't know how that truck got you here at all.*

"Stubbornness," Petra said.

"That'll do it." Ruthie wiped her hands on a rag already black past saving. "You want me to look at the transmission while it's on the lift? No charge for looking."

"No," Petra said, because looking was how they got you. Looking was free the way the first drink was free. She paid with the last of her hazard pay envelope and walked out into the heat of the flats with her pockets weighing nothing at all.

The Vane Syndicate had an office in Crooked Bend, which told you everything about the town. It wasn't an office the way banks had offices, all marble and hushed voices. It was a storefront between a bait shop and a shuttered dentist, with a hand-painted sign in cheerful yellow letters: **VANE & ASSOCIATES — SOLUTIONS FOR EVERY SEASON**. The windows were clean. That was the thing people noticed first and understood last. In a town where everything wore a skin of dust and river grime, the Syndicate's windows gleamed like eyes.

Petra stood across the street for eleven minutes by her watch, working up the particular courage it takes to walk toward something you dread. The storm she'd chased yesterday still lived in her body as a low hum, a static charge lodged somewhere behind her sternum. The lightning strike had missed her truck by maybe forty yards—the scorch mark on the flats was visible from the road, a black fern of burned earth—and she'd sat in the cab afterward with her hands shaking on the wheel, laughing, actually laughing, because what else do you do when death misses you by a length and your first thought isn't gratitude but arithmetic? Forty yards from a strike, and all she could think was: *at least I won't have to pay for a funeral.*

She crossed the street.

* * *

The man behind the desk was Doss Merrick, and he looked up from his paperwork with the delight of a host whose favorite guest has just arrived.

"Petra Underhill." He said her whole name like it was good news. He was a broad, soft-spoken man in his fifties with silver at his temples and reading glasses pushed up into hair that was more silver than not, and he dressed the way a retired teacher dresses on Sundays—open collar, pressed slacks, loafers you could see yourself in. There was a bowl of peppermints on his desk. There was always a bowl of peppermints on his desk. "Sit down, sit down. You want a mint? They're from Harrow Ford. Little shop off the courthouse square. Best peppermints in three counties, and I don't say that lightly."

"I know, Doss. You've told me."

"And yet you never take one." He pushed the bowl an inch closer, a small ceremonial gesture, like a priest offering communion she kept refusing. "One of these days you will, and that'll be the day I know we're real friends."

"We're not friends, Doss."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, and opened a folder. "We're something better. We're *honest* with each other."

The office smelled like paper and lemon polish and, underneath it, faintly, the river. There was a framed photograph on the wall behind him of a barge fleet at dawn, all those flat gray hulls sitting patient on brown water, and beneath it a smaller frame containing what appeared to be a child's crayon drawing of a sunflower, signed *For Mr. Doss*. Petra had never asked about the sunflower. She suspected that was the point of it.

Doss turned the folder around and slid it across the desk. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet was a number, and the number was hers. Fourteen thousand, two hundred and six dollars. Principal, interest, the late fees from the two seasons the storms hadn't come and the hazard contracts hadn't paid out, the processing fees, the *administrative review fee*, which she'd once asked about and Doss had explained with such gentle patience—*it covers the cost of us reviewing whether we can waive other fees, Petra*—that she'd almost apologized for asking.

"You've had a hard run of it," Doss said. "I read the field reports. That lightning yesterday came within a whisker of you."

"Forty yards."

"Forty yards." He shook his head slowly, and the terrible thing, the thing that made her stomach drop every single time, was that he seemed genuinely moved. Whatever else Doss Merrick was—and Petra had heard stories, everyone had heard stories, about the man who smiled through a kneecapping in Harrow Ford, about the debtor who swam out into the reservoir one night and was never found, stories that might have been true or might have been the Syndicate's best advertising—he was not performing his kindness. It was real. That was what made him frightening. A man who performed cruelty you could predict. A man who was sincerely, warmly, deeply kind while ruining your life was a weather system. You didn't negotiate with it. You just tried to know which way it was blowing.

"So here's what I've got," Doss said, and laced his fingers over his belly, settling in. "And I want you to hear the whole thing before you say anything, because I think when you hear it, you're going to be as pleased as I am."

Petra said nothing. Outside the clean window, a kid rattled past on a bicycle with a fishing pole across his handlebars, headed for the bend, headed for water that belonged to nobody.

"A client of ours," Doss said, "an old fellow named Elias Wrenn—maybe you've heard the name, prospector, been working these river systems since before either of us was born—is undertaking an expedition. Upstream. Far upstream." He tapped the folder. "He needs a guide. Not just any guide. Somebody who reads water like you do, somebody who can keep a crew alive when the river turns on them, and—this is the part I mentioned to him specifically—somebody who knows how to be *discreet*. Old Elias is a private man. Very private. And this is a private expedition."

"What kind of expedition?"

"The kind that pays well." Doss smiled. "The kind that pays *very* well. But here's the better part, and I mean this, Petra, I sat up half the night working the numbers because I wanted to bring you something fair." He pulled a second sheet from the folder, laid it beside the first. "Guide fee, top rate, plus hazard multiplier, plus completion bonus. And—" he drew a line in the air with one finger, a little flourish, a magician revealing the card—"half your balance. Forgiven. Gone. Not deferred, not restructured. *Forgiven.* You finish this job, you walk out of my office fourteen hundred miles downstream of debt, and you and I never have to have another conversation unless it's over coffee, as friends, talking about the weather."

The number sat there on the page. Half. Seven thousand dollars, gone, like it had never existed. Petra felt the offer land in her chest the way a strong current takes a boat—not pulling, exactly, just holding you, making the idea of fighting it seem suddenly exhausting and strange.

"How much is the guide fee?" she asked, because her mouth was dry and specifics were ballast.

Doss told her.

It was triple her best season. It was, she calculated with the cold quick part of her brain that never slept, enough to fix the truck, replace the pump, buy two seasons of gear, and still put money under the floorboard against the winter. It was generous. It was so generous that some animal part of her, some old flat-country instinct older than arithmetic, lay back its ears.

"Why?" she said.

Doss's smile didn't move, but something behind it adjusted, the way a room adjusts when a door opens somewhere in the house. "Why?"

"Nobody forgives seven thousand dollars out of the goodness of their heart. Your heart is good, Doss. I've never doubted that. It's also not why the Syndicate keeps you." She nodded at the folder. "So why?"

For a moment he just looked at her, and she watched him decide something—watched the decision happen behind his eyes like weather coming over the flats, no drama to it, just a shifting of pressure. Then he laughed, warm and easy, and took off his reading glasses, and polished them on his shirttail, and became if anything more avuncular than before.

"You want the truth?" he said. "Here's the truth. Old Elias Wrenn has been a client of this firm for thirty-one years. He's never missed a payment. Not once, not even the year the fever took his wife. And now he's dying—I'll spare you the details, but it's weeks, not months—and he came to us and said, *Doss, I've got one last thing I need to do, and I need a guide who won't ask questions and won't quit, and I'll pay whatever it costs.* And I thought of you." He set the glasses down and spread his hands, palms up, a man offering nothing but openness. "Because you're the best guide on this river, and because you're honest, and because I would *like*, Petra—purely selfishly, I admit it—to see you clear of this debt. You've paid on time through two bad seasons. People notice that kind of thing. *I* notice that kind of thing."

It was a beautiful answer. Every piece of it was plausible, warm, human. And it was assembled, Petra thought, the way a lawyer assembles a defense—true parts arranged to hide the shape of the whole. Because there was no version of the Vane Syndicate, in her experience or in any story she'd ever heard, where money went out the door without more money planned to come back in. Generosity from the Syndicate was not generosity. It was investment. The only question was what they expected the return to be.

"When does he want to leave?"

"As soon as you can provision. The dry season's starting early this year—your hydrologist friends are saying the almanac's going to be wrong by a week, maybe two." He said it lightly, but she caught it, filed it: *they knew the river forecast before she did.* "There's a launch point up at Crooked Bend, obviously, and he wants to go upriver, into the high country. Past the last marked channel."

Past the last marked channel. Nobody ran past the last marked channel except smugglers and fools, and the Syndicate was neither, usually.

"Who else is on this crew?"

"He's hiring his own people. Cook, mechanic, that sort of thing. You'd be guide and safety lead. Full authority on the water—that's written into the contract, he insisted. Once you're underway, nobody outranks you on the river. Not him, not anybody." Doss paused, and for just a moment the warmth thinned, and she saw briefly through it to the thing underneath, the way you see the current under a smooth stretch of brown water, invisible until it takes your boat sideways. "That clause matters to him. I'd take it seriously if I were you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no." Doss shrugged, vast and unbothered, and reached for the peppermint bowl, and ate one himself, crunching it cheerfully. "And we go back to the schedule we've always had. Monthly payments. Late fees. Nothing changes, nothing gets worse. I'm not in the business of punishing people for declining an opportunity, Petra. This is an *opportunity*." He said the word like it tasted good. "But I'll tell you what I told Elias when he asked me who to hire. I said, get me Underhill, because Underhill's the only guide on this stretch who treats the river like it's a person. Everybody else treats it like a road." He leaned back. "Elias liked that. Said, *then she's the one.*"

The office hummed around her—the tick of a wall clock, the whisper of the window unit struggling against the heat, somewhere in the back the murmur of another employee on a phone saying *of course, of course, we can absolutely help with that* in a voice like syrup. Petra looked at the number again. Seven thousand dollars, forgiven. Triple-rate pay. Full authority. A dying client who insisted on discretion and wanted to go where maps stopped.

Every instinct she owned stood up at once and said *no*. And every one of them was broke, tired, forty yards from lightning, driving a truck with a dying alternator and a dying pump and a transmission Ruthie hadn't even looked at yet.

"I need to see the route," Petra said. "Before I sign anything. His map, his plan. I don't guide blind."

Something flickered across Doss's face—there and gone, quick as the lightning's afterimage—and then the warmth closed back over it like water over a dropped stone.

"Now, that's the guide in you talking," he said approvingly, "and I respect it. But Elias was specific. Route stays sealed until you've signed. He's a careful old man. Trusts the wrong things and doesn't trust the right ones, if you follow me." He slid a pen across the desk, a nice heavy pen, the kind that writes smoothly, the kind that makes signing feel like an event. "Sign today, meet him tomorrow morning at the hospice in Harrow Ford. He's got things he wants to say to you himself. Man's got a speech prepared, I think. Been rehearsing it." Doss chuckled fondly. "Old men and their speeches."

Petra looked at the pen. She thought about the scorch mark on the flats, the black fern of burned ground where the sky had reached down for her and missed. She thought about Ruthie saying *I don't know how that truck got you here at all*, and about the number on the page, and about the word *discreet*, sitting in Doss's mouth like a peppermint he hadn't crunched yet.

The terms were generous. That was the whole problem. On this river, when the water rose too fast and too smooth, when the current came on easy and welcoming, that was the eddy that fed the undertow. Every drowning she'd ever witnessed started with calm water.

But she was so tired. And the number was so large. And Doss Merrick watched her with the patient, genuine kindness of a man watching weather he had already forecast.

"One condition," Petra said. "Mine. Written in. My crew comes home. All of them, every hired hand on this expedition—if I say the trip's done, it's done, and nobody—nobody from your side—comes looking for us mid-river with questions. I've seen your survey barges work. If I so much as see a Syndicate hull upstream of me, I turn around, and the deal's off, and you eat the lost season."

Doss considered her for a long moment. Then he laughed—a real laugh, head back, delighted—and clapped his hands together once, softly, like a man applauding a good hand of cards.

"There she is," he said. "That's exactly what I told Elias. *She'll negotiate,* I said. *Watch her.*" He pulled the contract back, uncapped the pen himself, and wrote in the margin in neat, schoolteacher script. "Done. Crew safety, full authority, no Syndicate traffic above your position. Initial there." He tapped the margin. "See? Honest. Like I said."

She initialed. Then, at the bottom, above the line that waited for her name, she hesitated one last time—one breath, in and out, the smell of lemon polish and the river underneath—and the clock ticked, and the window unit hummed, and outside the kid on the bicycle rang his bell at somebody, bright and far away.

Petra Underhill signed.

"Wonderful." Doss capped the pen and stood and offered his hand, and his grip was warm and dry and strong. "You won't regret this, Petra. I mean that personally." At the door he stopped, and turned, and his face was all kindness. "Oh—one more thing. Elias may mention a place. A name. When he does—" the smile widened by a degree, no more, "—you'll let me know what he says. As a courtesy. Between friends."

The office door closed behind her with a soft click, and Petra stood on the sidewalk in the white heat of the afternoon, her signature still drying on a contract for a job she already feared, and realized she had just sold the one thing she had left.

Not her truck. Not her gear.

Her silence.

Upstream, past the last marked channel, somewhere in the high country, a dying man was rehearsing a speech. And tomorrow morning she would hear it—and she understood, standing there with the peppermint taste of the whole arrangement in her mouth, that whatever Elias Wrenn was going to say to her in that hospice room, Doss Merrick wanted to hear it too.

Which meant it was worth hearing.

Which meant, she thought, walking toward Ruthie's garage to tell the truck the news, that she had just made the most expensive bargain of her life, and hadn't even been shown the merchandise.
