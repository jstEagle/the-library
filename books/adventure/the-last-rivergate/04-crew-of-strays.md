## 4. Crew of Strays

Petra made her list on the back of a fuel receipt, in the parking lot of a truck stop outside Harrow Ford, while the ink of Doss Merrick's contract was still fresh enough to smear if she licked her thumb and dragged it across the page. She didn't. She'd read the thing three times instead, sitting in the cab with the heater ticking, looking for the trap in the generous terms the way you'd probe a sore tooth. Half her debt forgiven for one season's guiding work. The other half deferred until Elias Wrenn's expedition came back downriver. No interest accruing. No collateral beyond the boat.

That was the trap. There was always a trap. But a list was still a list, and the first name on it had been there before Doss ever slid the folder across his desk.

*Juno Okafor*, she wrote, pressing hard enough to dent the receipt against the steering wheel. Then she sat with it a while, watching moths batter themselves against the sodium light above the diesel pumps, and tried to remember why she was doing this to someone who had never done anything to her.

Because Juno could read water like scripture. Because nobody else would hire her. Because Petra needed somebody who'd tell her when the river was lying, and Juno was the only hydrologist in three counties whose word she trusted more than the gauges.

She drove out at dawn.

* * *

Juno lived in a converted grain elevator at the edge of the floodplain, a concrete tower she rented cheap because everyone else said it hummed in high wind like a struck bell. Petra climbed the exterior stairs with coffee going cold in her hand and knocked on a door patched with sheet aluminum, and when it opened, Juno stood in the gap wearing a bathrobe over waders, hair cropped short as a crew cut, eyes already narrowed.

"You're not selling gutters," Juno said.

"No."

"Then you're either collecting a debt or offering one." She stepped back to let Petra in anyway, which was its own kind of answer. The inside of the elevator was all vertical: a ladder to a sleeping loft, bookshelves bolted to concrete, and every horizontal surface buried under charts. River charts. Stage graphs. Bathymetric surveys annotated in four colors of ink, some of them years out of date, corrected by hand with a patience that looked, from a distance, a lot like penance.

Petra set the coffee on the least buried surface and told her the shape of it. A dying prospector. An expedition upriver, past the Narrows, into country the almanacs barely bothered with. Six weeks, maybe eight. Hazard pay plus a bonus, both routed through her, both real—she'd checked that much herself, because she wasn't going to bring anyone onto a job built on vapor.

Juno listened with her arms crossed, and when Petra finished, she didn't ask about the money. She asked: "Who's the client?"

"Elias Wrenn."

The name landed somewhere behind Juno's eyes and stayed there. She turned to her charts, ran one finger up the river past the last surveyed bend, and stopped where the paper stopped. "He wants the Gate," she said. Not a question.

"He didn't say so."

"He didn't have to." Juno's finger tapped the blank space twice. "Nobody goes up there anymore because the channel died. Dry season's coming early this year—I've been watching the gauge data from Crooked Bend since March, and the recession curve is steeper than anything in the record. You'll be dragging boats over gravel by week three." She finally looked at Petra directly. "You know what I lost my license over?"

"Everyone knows. The Belltown assessment."

"I signed a report saying the levee upstream was sound. It wasn't. Or—" her jaw worked, "—it was sound until somebody wanted it otherwise. I found the seepage data didn't match the inspection logs, and I said so out loud, and eleven years later I'm living in a grain elevator correcting other people's maps for beer money." She picked up the coffee, drank it cold without flinching. "You're asking me to go back to the water."

"I'm asking you to come work. That's all. I don't need your history, and I'm not going to pretend I understand it."

"That's the most honest pitch anyone's given me in a decade." Juno almost smiled. It was a small, weathered thing. "One condition. When we're on the river, I call the hydrology. If I say a channel's dead, we portage. No arguing hazard pay at me from the stern."

"Done."

"And Petra." She set the cup down. "If Wrenn's map leads where I think it leads, there are people who've spent forty years making sure that valley stays blank on the paper. People with boats. You want to tell me who's funding this?"

Petra held her gaze and lied, cleanly and without practice, which frightened her more than the lie itself. "An old man's savings and a research grant."

Juno studied her a long moment—the way she'd study a gauge reading that didn't match the visual—and then let it go. "Pack list by Thursday. I'll bring my own instruments."

* * *

Teodor Vasic found her, which was how Teodor did everything. She was eating alone at the Blue Heron diner on Route 9 when he slid into the booth across from her uninvited, carrying two plates he hadn't ordered for himself, and set one in front of her: braised something-or-other over polenta, smelling of paprika and slow hours, in a town where the house special was a hamburger.

"You look like a woman who hasn't eaten a good meal since spring," he said. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, forearms scarred with old burn marks, gray coming in at the temples like frost. "Eat. Then tell me why you've been asking about me at three different kitchens this week."

"Word travels."

"In a valley this size, word doesn't travel. It teleports." He watched her take a bite, and whatever crossed her face made him nod once, satisfied, like a doctor confirming a diagnosis. "You want a cook for an expedition."

"How'd you know?"

"Because you're the third person this year, and the first two offered me half what the job is worth." He leaned back. "I fled Sarajevo, then Belgrade, then a fishing boat out of Trieste that I will not discuss. I have been in this country nine years cooking for truckers who think black pepper is adventurous. I am bored in a way that is becoming medically dangerous." He pointed a fork at her. "So. Where are we going?"

"Upstream. Past the Narrows."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "Past the Narrows. In dry season."

"We'll manage the water."

"We will manage the water because you will hire the right people, and I assume that means the woman in the grain elevator, which means you have at least one brain on board." He finished his own plate in about four bites, wiped his mouth with a napkin he folded into a precise triangle, and pushed it aside. "My terms. One: I cook what I decide to cook, and no one complains or they carry their own stove. Two: I get a bunk away from the engine. Three—" and here his voice changed, dropped its performance, went quiet and level, "—you do not lie to me about danger. I have run from enough of it to smell it through a hull. If this trip has wolves in it, I want to know the breed."

Petra thought of Doss Merrick's smile, of the survey barge she'd seen hauled out at Crooked Bend with new sonar skids bolted to its flanks, of Elias Wrenn's hands shaking around a teacup in a hospice room that smelled of rain. She thought about how much truth a person could afford.

"There may be people following us," she said. "Competitors. Interested parties. I'll handle them."

Teodor regarded her for a long moment, and she got the distinct feeling he was tasting the sentence the way he'd taste a stock, checking it for something off. Whatever he found—or decided not to find—he swallowed it.

"Interested parties," he repeated. "Fine. I have fed men through sieges. I can feed them through your interested parties." He stood, gathered the plates, and paused. "I start provisioning tomorrow. Real food. Your crew will hate how spoiled they become."

* * *

Sable she found by accident, or told herself she did, though later she'd wonder whether some part of her had been circling the marina all along, drawn to the sound of a small engine running too smoothly for its age.

The girl was under a skiff on blocks in the far shed at Crooked Bend, sixteen maybe, wrists thin as oar handles, a socket wrench moving with a fluency that made Petra stop walking. Nobody that age worked like that. Nobody that age worked like that *alone*, on a boat that wasn't theirs, in a yard where the owner charged by the hour and watched from his office window like a man waiting for a reason.

Petra crouched at the edge of the shade. "That carburetor's from a '94 Johnson," she said. "They stopped making parts for it before you were born."

"They didn't stop making parts," the girl said, without coming out. "People just stopped looking. There's a machine shop in Kestrel that'll turn anything you need from bar stock if you bring them a drawing." She slid out on the creeper, and Petra got her first full look: sharp-boned face, dark hair hacked short with what had clearly been kitchen scissors, grease to the elbows, eyes the flat wary gray of a sky deciding about rain. "You're Petra Underhill. Everybody at the yard talks about you. Storm chaser. Broke."

"Both true."

"Then you can't afford to pay me, and I can't afford to care, because Mr. Halloran's about to figure out I've been using his lathe." She sat up, wiped her hands on rag already more rag than cloth. "What do you want?"

"A deckhand who can fix anything. Six weeks, maybe eight. Room, board, and a cut of the bonus."

"Where to?"

"Upriver."

"That's not a destination, that's a direction."

"It's the only one I've got right now."

Something flickered across the girl's face—not suspicion exactly, but recognition, the look of someone hearing their own native language spoken after years abroad. She glanced toward Halloran's window, then back. "I ran away from a group home in March," she said, matter-of-fact, the way you'd report the weather. "They'll have stopped looking by now. Probably. If I go upriver with you, nobody finds me, and nobody asks questions I can't answer."

"Sable," Petra said—it was on the work order Halloran kept for her odd jobs, the only name anyone had.

"Yeah."

"I don't ask questions I don't need answered. But I do make promises. Everyone eats. Everyone gets paid. And if things go bad, I bring everybody home. Those aren't negotiable, and they're not conditional."

The girl looked at her for a long time, wrench turning slowly over in her fingers, and Petra understood she was being weighed on scales calibrated by every adult who'd ever made her a promise and let it rot. Whatever the reading came out to be, Sable nodded once, short.

"I'll need to bring my tools," she said. "All of them."

"Bring the lathe drawings too."

For the first time, the girl smiled. It changed her whole face, made her look briefly, dangerously young. "Kestrel charges by the ounce," she said. "You sure about that budget?"

"No," Petra said honestly. "But I'm sure about everything else."

* * *

They loaded at Crooked Bend on a morning so clear the river looked poured rather than flowing, glass-flat between the gravel bars, holding the sky upside down in its slack water. The boat was a thirty-foot shallow-draft freighter canoe with a mud motor and a jury-rigged canopy, and by seven they had her riding low: fuel drums, provision crates stacked and lashed by Teodor with a rigidity that bordered on religious, Juno's instrument cases padded in wool blankets, Sable's tool chest taking the spot of honor amidships like cargo worth more than the fuel.

Elias Wrenn came down to the bank in a wheelchair pushed by a home-health aide who'd been paid to look away for an hour. He was thinner than the maps had made him seem, if maps could make a man seem anything, his skin the color of old paper, but his eyes were still river-colored, still moving, reading the current the way Juno did—as text.

"So this is the crew," he said.

Petra made the introductions. Juno shook his hand like a colleague. Teodor bowed slightly, an old-world courtesy that seemed to please the old man enormously. Sable hung back until Elias beckoned her close and studied her face with an intensity that made her flush.

"You fix things," he said.

"Yes sir."

"Good. This trip will require it." He turned to Petra, and for a moment, just a moment, the cheerfulness dropped and something underneath showed through—urgency, or fear, or the specific gravity of a man carrying forty years of silence. "Captain Underhill. My map says we have a season. Your hydrologist says less. Listen to her."

"I intend to."

"And the others?" His voice dropped. "Do they know?"

"They know the direction. They don't know the destination."

Elias closed his eyes briefly, as if in prayer or exhaustion, and when he opened them the cheerfulness was back, fitted on like a coat. "Then keep it that way as long as you can. Secrets age badly on rivers. Like milk." He gripped her wrist, his hand shockingly strong for all its frailty. "Two promises, Captain. Bring them home. Keep silent. Everything else is weather."

The aide wheeled him back up the ramp. Petra watched him go, then turned to her boat, her strange cargo of strangers: a disgraced scientist correcting the river's grammar, a cook who'd outrun three wars, a kid who trusted machines more than people, and herself, a debtor with a contract folded in her breast pocket and a promise in her mouth that was starting to taste like iron.

"Cast off," she said.

Teodor handed up the last crate. Juno took the tiller. Sable crouched at the bow, watching the water with the fixed attention of someone learning a new language, and the Current slipped free of the bank and swung its nose upstream, into the narrowing channel, into the country the maps forgot.

Behind them, at the yard office, Halloran stepped out onto his porch and watched them go. Then he went inside and picked up a phone.

Downriver, in a trailer with air conditioning and excellent coffee, Doss Merrick's phone rang, and he answered it smiling.
