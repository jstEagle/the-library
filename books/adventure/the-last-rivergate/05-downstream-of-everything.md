## 5. Downstream of Everything

Juno Vasquez had learned to read water the way other people learned to read faces, and the first thing the Crooked Bend told her that morning was that it had been lying to somebody for a long time.

The river came around the bend in a long, muscular curve, brown-green and patient, carrying last week's rain from mountains none of them would see. Willows leaned over the near bank like gossips. On the far side, a heron stood in the shallows with the stillness of something that had never once been in a hurry. It was beautiful in the way rivers always were at dawn—generous, indifferent, already forgetting everything that floated into it.

Juno stood on the gravel ramp with a clipboard she didn't need and pretended to check load manifests while she actually watched the current. Six knots on the outside of the bend, maybe four along the inside seam where the channel deepened against the cut bank. Good push for loaded boats. She could feel the old arithmetic waking up in her chest, the numbers she'd sworn off three years ago in a conference room in Meridian City while men in good suits explained to her, kindly, publicly, exactly what happened to hydrologists who published inconvenient data about levee maintenance schedules upstream of poor towns.

She'd been right. That was the whole joke of it. She'd been right, and being right had cost her the license, the career, the apartment with the good light, and most of the people who'd claimed to know her. What remained was a woman with forged credentials under a name one letter off from her own, standing on a boat ramp at six in the morning, working for a storm chaser who owed money to cheerful men.

"Juno." Petra's voice carried across the ramp, low and even, the voice of someone who'd learned to talk over wind. "You want to tell me why you're glaring at the river like it owes you money?"

"Everything owes someone money," Juno said. "It's the natural condition."

Petra came down the ramp with two coffees from the thermos and handed her one without asking. That was a thing about Petra Underhill that Juno was still deciding how to feel about: she noticed things. She noticed that Juno took her coffee black and bitter enough to strip paint. She noticed when Teodor's hands shook before he started cooking and made a point of asking him questions that required answers instead of silence. She noticed Sable's hood coming up whenever adults got loud, and lowered her own voice accordingly. Noticing things was either kindness or surveillance, and after the Syndicate, Juno had stopped believing there was much difference.

"Load order," Petra said. "Elias rides in the middle boat with you. He's having a bad morning—I can hear it in his cough."

"He's having a bad decade," Juno said, and immediately regretted it, because Petra's jaw did the thing it did when she was swallowing something sharp.

"Yeah," Petra said quietly. "He is. So we keep him comfortable and we keep him moving, and we don't talk about decades. Load order."

* * *

They got the boats in the water by seven-forty, which by any professional standard was a small miracle, because the crew was a disaster assembled out of spare parts.

Teodor's galley boat went first—he'd insisted, in his accented and immovable way, that a hungry expedition was a frightened expedition, and he'd spent the night before turning the little cabin into something that smelled like paprika and toasted garlic and made even Elias sit up straighter. Then the supply boat with Sable at the tiller, the girl's hands moving over the engine controls with an intimacy that unsettled Juno slightly; sixteen-year-olds were not supposed to know what a fuel-air mixture screw was for, let alone how to tune one by ear. Then the passenger boat: Elias Wrenn wrapped in blankets in the stern like a king too old for his throne, and Juno beside him with the charts, and Petra at the helm up front, scanning water the way Juno used to scan data.

"Channel's left of the mid-river bar," Juno said, pointing. "Stay off the inside of the bend until we're past the willow point—the current'll try to shove you into the cutbank."

"You've run this stretch?" Petra asked.

"No." Juno sipped her coffee. "I'm reading it. There's a difference."

"There is," Petra said, and something in her tone suggested she meant more than the river, and Juno decided not to examine that.

They pulled away from Crooked Bend in a loose line, engines idling down to a work-song thrum, and the town fell behind them in stages: the rusted loading crane, the shuttered bait shop with its hand-painted sign half gone to weather, the grain elevator standing over everything like a tombstone for an economy. Then just the river, and the sky going from pewter to pale gold, and the strange, suspended feeling of being committed—past the point where turning back was cheaper than going on.

Elias slept against the gunwale with the map tube strapped across his body like a bandolier. He'd tried to give it to Petra again at the ramp and she'd refused it again, and they'd done this little dance with the tenderness of two people who both knew exactly how the story ended and were pretending otherwise. Juno had watched them and felt something complicated move through her chest, something she refused to name.

For the first hour, it was almost easy.

* * *

The trouble, when it came, didn't come as trouble. It came as data.

They stopped at noon below the old rail trestle to take on water and let Teodor feed people, and Juno climbed onto the trestle pilings with her binoculars mostly to be alone, which was a habit she'd kept from the years when being alone was the only safe configuration. From up there the river unspooled downstream in long silver loops, and upstream—

Upstream, maybe two kilometers back, hugging the far bank in the shadow of the bluff, sat a barge.

She wouldn't have given it a second look except that her eyes, trained by a decade of survey work, catalogued it automatically, the way a musician hears a wrong note. Flat-decked, low-slung, with a mast amidships carrying instruments she recognized the silhouettes of: a current profiler housing, a weather station, a mast-mounted LiDAR unit with its distinctive cowl. A survey barge. A serious one. The kind that cost more per day than Juno's entire former salary.

And it was holding station. That was the detail that put ice in her stomach. A working survey barge moved. It ran transects, it stopped and sampled, it crept along its study reach taking measurements. This one sat in the slack water under the bluff like a man leaning against a wall watching a door—and every few minutes, when Juno watched through the glasses, it eased forward a little. Stopped. Eased forward again.

Pacing them. Two kilometers back, invisible from water level, keeping pace with their convoy the way a shadow keeps pace with its owner.

Juno lowered the binoculars and stood very still on the pilings, feeling the river's breath move past her, doing the math she didn't want to do.

A survey barge on this stretch of river made no commercial sense. There was nothing here to survey—no planned bridge, no dredging contract, no flood study she'd ever heard funded. And nobody ran a LiDAR mast and a current profiler for fun. Somebody was mapping this river. Somebody was mapping it now, this season, this exact window of falling water, and somebody was doing it behind them, at a discreet distance, with the patience of an institution.

The Vane Syndicate owned dredging companies. She remembered that from the trial coverage, from the years after, when she'd read everything she could find about the men whose levee contracts had intersected her career like a knife. They owned barges. They owned the kind of instruments bolted to that mast. And they owned Petra Underhill's debt, which meant they owned, in every way that mattered, this expedition.

Unless it was coincidence. Unless some state agency was running a routine hydrographic survey and had chosen, of all the rivers in the territory, this one, this month, to hug the far bank and shadow four boats full of strangers heading toward a place that wasn't on any map.

Juno had built her whole ruined life on knowing the difference between coincidence and pattern. Pattern won. Pattern always won; that was the curse of her particular mind.

"Hey!" Sable's voice from below, cracking slightly with the effort of sounding casual. "Food! Teodor says if the beans go cold it's a personal insult!"

Juno pocketed the binoculars and climbed down, and said nothing, and hated herself for saying nothing, and understood perfectly well why she was saying nothing.

Because here was the shape of it: if she told Petra, Petra would have to decide something, and every decision available was bad. Turn back and the debt ate her alive. Confront the barge and confirm they'd been seen noticing. Say nothing and keep running blind toward whatever the Syndicate wanted so badly that they'd spend this kind of money following it. And if Juno told her, and Petra chose wrong—or chose right but too late—then Juno would have been the one who broke the fragile, improbable thing that was starting to form among these four strangers and one dying man, this accidental family assembling itself out of wreckage, and Juno had already destroyed one community by telling the truth at the wrong time in the wrong room to the wrong audience.

So she held it. She climbed down off the pilings with the observation folded up small inside her, and she ate Teodor's beans, and she laughed at Sable's impression of Doss Merrick's smile—that wide, warm, entirely empty smile—and she watched Petra across the fire ring of stones they'd made on the gravel bar, and thought: *you don't know yet. You're driving toward it and you don't know.*

The guilt of that sat in her like swallowed stone.

* * *

They ran the afternoon in golden light, and it should have been enough to make anyone believe in the trip. The river widened and slowed, braiding itself around islands of cottonwood, and egrets lifted ahead of the boats in white flurries, and Elias woke briefly and pointed out a bluff formation and called it "the old man's profile" with such certainty that Sable spent ten minutes finding the face in the rock and then refused to admit she couldn't unsee it. Teodor produced cinnamon rolls from somewhere that defied physics. Petra stood at her helm with the wind pulling her hair sideways and, for one unguarded moment, smiled at nothing at all, and Juno saw in that smile the person Petra might have been if the world hadn't put a price on her.

And through all of it, every time Juno checked—and she checked constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth—the barge was there. Two kilometers. One and a half. Holding the far channel. Once, near dusk, she caught the glint of glass on its mast: someone up there with optics, looking downstream. Looking at them, or pretending to look past them, which amounted to the same thing.

At the evening camp, on a high shelf of gravel above a slow eddy, Juno found herself beside Petra at the fire while the others argued cheerfully about star names. Sable had opinions. Teodor claimed a different constellation system altogether and defended it with the ferocity of a man defending his grandmother.

"The boats are running well," Juno said. Neutral opening. Probe.

"Better than well." Petra fed a stick to the flames. "Sable tuned the middle engine and it's burning cleaner than when it was new. I half suspect the girl of witchcraft."

"Mm." Juno turned her cup in her hands. "Anything seem off to you? About today?"

Petra glanced at her. Those gray eyes, quick as weather. "Off how?"

"Just—" Juno shrugged, casual, a performance she despised herself for. "River traffic. We've seen almost none. For a corridor this size, this time of year, I'd expect more barge work. Dredges, surveys, that kind of thing."

Something crossed Petra's face—fast, quickly smoothed, but Juno was watching for it. "Rivers dry up same as towns," Petra said lightly. "Everything downstream of everything else. When the money stops, the traffic stops." She looked back at the fire. "Why? You see something?"

There it was. The door, open one inch. All Juno had to do was say *there's a survey barge pacing us with a LiDAR mast and I think it belongs to the people who own your life*, and the trip changed tonight, in this firelight, with everyone listening whether they appeared to or not.

She looked at Elias instead. He sat propped against his pack with the map tube across his knees, asleep sitting up, his breathing wet and labored, his hands curled loosely around the cardboard like a man holding the last of something. Whatever was in that tube had killed him slowly for forty years, and he was spending his final weeks hauling it upriver to show them, and he trusted Petra Underhill—a stranger, a debtor, a woman with a bounty on her future—to carry it the rest of the way.

If Juno spoke now, badly, half-cocked, with no proof but binocular impressions, she might shatter that trust in ways it couldn't recover from. Or worse: she might be wrong. She hadn't confirmed ownership. She hadn't seen a name on the hull. She was working from pattern, and pattern had burned her before—not because the pattern was wrong, but because she'd presented it to people who needed it not to be true.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she'd find a way to get closer, or get proof, or at least get Petra alone somewhere the others couldn't hear the floor fall out of the world. Tomorrow she'd figure out how to say it so it landed as protection instead of panic.

"Nothing," Juno said. "Quiet river. I like it."

"Liar," Sable said comfortably, from across the fire, not even looking up from the engine part she was cleaning. "You've been making your thinking-face since the trestle. You get a line right here." The girl tapped the space between her own eyebrows. "Right there. Like the river's giving you bad data."

Laughter went around the fire, warm and easy, and Juno joined it because refusing would have cost more than joining, and underneath the laughter she felt the crack open a little wider—the first hairline fracture in the thing they were building, the doubt sliding into it like water into stone.

Later, on her watch, she stood at the edge of the camp and looked upstream into the dark. Far off, impossibly faint, she could just make it out: a single white running light, hanging motionless under the black shoulder of the bluff. Station-keeping. Waiting for morning to resume the patient arithmetic of pursuit.

Two kilometers back, and closing by inches, the Syndicate's eyes were on the water, and Juno Vasquez stood between them and the sleeping camp with a secret getting heavier in her hands, and no idea yet how much it was going to cost her to set it down.
