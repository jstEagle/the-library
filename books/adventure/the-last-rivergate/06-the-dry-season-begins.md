## 6. The Dry Season Begins

The river began lying to them on the third morning out of Crooked Bend.

Petra noticed it first as a sound—or rather, the absence of one. The Kettlewater had been talking to them for days, a low constant mutter against the hull, the kind of voice you stopped hearing until it changed. Now the voice had gone thin. Reedy. The way a man sounds when he's trying to fill a room with less breath than he has.

She stood at the bow of the *Marta June* with her coffee going cold in one hand and Juno's depth gauge printout in the other, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since dawn.

The almanac said the dry season would take the river down six inches a week. The gauge said they'd lost eleven inches in three days. The sandbars that had been safely submerged when they launched were surfacing like the backs of animals waking up. And somewhere ahead—she could feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar—there was a stretch of channel the old-timers called the Throat, where the water went shallow and mean all at once.

"Petra." Juno came up beside her, squinting downstream. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting something and the numbers are losing."

Petra snorted and handed her the printout. "Eleven inches in seventy-two hours. Almanac says we should've seen four."

Juno read it twice, lips moving slightly, and Petra watched the hydrologist do what Juno did with water: go quiet, go inward, go somewhere behind her own eyes where rivers made sense. When she came back she looked older.

"That's not seasonal drawdown," Juno said. "That's upstream diversion. Somebody's pulling headwater." She folded the paper with unnecessary precision. "Could be irrigation. Could be mining. Could be—" She stopped herself.

"Could be the Syndicate," Petra finished. "Drying up our escape route."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to."

Behind them, Teodor's voice rose from the galley in his usual morning liturgy—a running commentary in three languages, none of them directed at anyone in particular, about the moral failings of instant coffee and the spiritual superiority of real butter. Sable was already awake; Petra had heard her in the engine bay before sunrise, the small rhythmic clank of a girl who talked to machinery the way other people talked to saints.

Elias sat wrapped in two blankets in the stern chair, watching the water with the patience of a man who had already made peace with how much of his life he'd spend waiting. He'd lost weight since Crooked Bend. His hands, when they weren't under the blankets, moved constantly—folding, unfolding, tracing invisible lines on the armrests. Tracing the map, Petra thought. Even asleep, he was probably tracing the map.

"How bad?" Elias asked without turning around. He always knew when she came to stand near him. Some old prospector's sense, or maybe just the sound of someone carrying bad news.

"River's dropping faster than it should."

"Mm." He nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Kettlewater does that. She gives with one hand and takes with the other. My father used to say the river forgives everything except impatience."

"Well," Petra said, "I'm about to be very unforgivable."

* * *

They made forty miles that day by Petra's reckoning, which should have felt like progress but didn't, because the whole time the banks were getting taller relative to the waterline. Mud skirts showed along the shorelines, pale bands of exposed earth like rings on a draining bathtub. Driftwood lay stranded on shelves of mud that had been underwater a week ago. Juno marked each one in her log with a date and a height, building a record of the river's retreat the way other people built case files.

By late afternoon the channel had narrowed until the willows on both sides leaned over them like an audience. The current picked up—which would normally be good news—but Petra didn't trust it. Fast water over shallow bottom was just a different way of dying.

"We'll want to stop soon," Juno said quietly. "Before the light goes. If we ground out in the dark—"

"I know."

They rounded a long bend and there it was: a gravel bar, broad and pale as a bleached whale, sitting in the middle of the channel with maybe fourteen inches of water sheeting over its lower edge. Upstream of it, the river split into threads. Downstream, it gathered itself again into something deep enough—at least for tonight.

Petra brought the *Marta June* in slow, backing down against the current, and felt the hull kiss gravel with a sound like a held breath released. Not aground. Just... resting. She got the bow line out and Sable waded ashore in her boots to make fast around a cottonwood stump, and the expedition settled onto the bar like a bird folding its wings.

"Two days," Petra announced to no one and everyone. "Maybe three. We wait for whatever's happening upstream to either stop or finish."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about this crew she was still getting used to—they didn't argue about the things that mattered. They grumbled about coffee and card rules and whose turn it was to dig the latrine, and then when Petra said *we stop here*, they simply did it, the way a body simply obeys the mind.

Teodor was the first one off the boat, and he wasn't carrying gear. He was carrying a cast-iron skillet, and he was looking at the gravel bar the way Petra had once seen him look at an open market in a border town: like it was a problem that food could solve.

"Gravel," he announced, crouching, rubbing some between his fingers. "Good gravel. Clean. You know what lives under clean gravel?"

"Fish?" Sable guessed.

"Crayfish. Mussels. Maybe lampreys if we're unlucky." He stood, cracking his knuckles. "Also—I saw willow on the bank. Willow bark tea. And if God loves us, which is debatable, there are catfish holding in the deep hole below the bar, waiting for exactly one foolish cook to come find them."

"You're going swimming for catfish?" Sable said.

"I am going *noodling* for catfish," Teodor corrected with enormous dignity. "There is a difference. Noodling is a sacred art. Also I will probably get bit. This is also part of the art."

* * *

That first evening on the bar was the kind that made you forget you were stranded.

The sky did its slow western trick, burning orange to violet, and the midges came off the water in soft drifting columns that caught the last light like smoke. Teodor waded into the tail-out below the bar with his arms submerged to the elbow, making noises that were half prayer and half profanity, while Sable sat on the gravel sorting pump parts out of a tackle box with the concentration of a surgeon laying out instruments.

Petra watched them both from the fire ring she was building, and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was tight.

It had been years since she'd belonged to anything she hadn't paid for. Crews before this had been transactions—hazard pay split four ways, everyone watching everyone else's hands near the cash box. But these people had signed on for a destination they didn't know, for wages Elias Wrenn was paying partly in promises, and now here they were stuck on a rock in a shrinking river, and instead of turning ugly, they were—

Sable held up a piece of pipe fitting to the fading light. "Petra, do we have any thread sealant? Or am I improvising?"

"You're improvising."

"Great. Love that for me."

"—they were improvising," Petra murmured, and fed another branch to the fire.

Teodor emerged from the water twenty minutes later soaked to the ribs and triumphant, holding a catfish the size of a forearm by the jaw, blood streaming from three parallel scratches on his other hand where the fish had objected to its future.

"Dinner!" he bellowed, with the joy of a man announcing a coronation. "And also, I require the good towel and possibly a blessing!"

* * *

The catfish, dusted in cornmeal Teodor had been hoarding like a dragon, cooked over coals in the skillet with wild onions he'd dug from the bank, and it was—Petra closed her eyes on the first bite—the best thing she'd eaten in two years. Maybe because of hunger. Maybe because of the company. She suspected it was both, and that the ratio was closer than she wanted to admit.

They ate around the fire with their boots off, feet toward the flames, and the talk went easy and low. Sable told a story about the first engine she ever rebuilt, a lawn mower she'd taken apart at age nine and reassembled with two parts left over that turned out to be decorative. Juno, to everyone's visible surprise, told one back—a story about graduate school, a flooded gauging station, and a professor who'd taught her that a river is just a debt the land is always paying down. She laughed at her own ending, a short startled sound, like she'd surprised herself by remembering how.

Petra found herself laughing too, really laughing, and caught Elias watching her across the fire with an expression she couldn't name. Not pity. Something more like recognition—an old man seeing a younger version of himself learn the same lesson late.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing." He pulled his blanket tighter. "Just thinking my father was right about rivers."

"Which part?"

"The forgiving-everything-except-impatience part. But also—" he gestured at the fire, the crew, the pale sweep of the bar, "—rivers strand you in strange company, and sometimes that's the whole point of them."

* * *

The second day was harder.

The water dropped another five inches overnight. By noon the *Marta June* was high and dry on her keel, tilted slightly, listing like a tired horse, and the channel below the bar had shrunk to a ribbon you could have jumped across in places. Juno walked the whole length of it with a sounding pole, coming back grim.

"Eighteen inches at the deepest point. We need thirty. Minimum." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "If this keeps up, we don't float for a week. Maybe longer. Maybe not at all this season."

Nobody said the obvious thing—that Doss Merrick's boats drew less water than theirs, and that whoever was pulling water out of the headwaters might be doing it precisely so that heavy boats couldn't run the river. Nobody had to say it. Petra watched them all think it anyway, together, in silence.

So they worked. Because that was the other thing about this crew: when the news was bad, they reached for tools.

Sable spent the morning building a pump out of parts that had no business becoming a pump—a bilge impeller, a length of garden hose, a car battery, and a check valve she'd machined down with a file and terrifying patience. By early afternoon it was moving water, a thin steady arc of it, and she stood over it with her hands on her hips like a general reviewing troops.

"It's ugly," she admitted, "but it moves nine gallons a minute, which means if we dig a relief channel through the low end of the bar, we can deepen the riffle by hand. Slowly. Very slowly."

"How slowly?" Petra asked.

"Mathematically? Three days. Emotionally? Don't ask."

So they dug. All of them—even Elias, who insisted on holding the lantern the first evening until Petra gently took it away and gave him the job of supervising, which he accepted with the grace of a man who understood exactly what mercy looked like when it wore work clothes. They dug in shifts, waist-deep in the channel, passing buckets, prying boulders loose with a come-along and a lot of swearing. Teodor narrated the entire operation like a sports announcer. Juno surveyed each hour's progress with her level and gave quiet corrections that saved them hours of wasted effort. Sable kept the pump alive through two breakdowns and one small electrical fire that she put out with her boot and her dignity simultaneously.

By the second sunset, the riffle ran ten inches deeper than it had that morning, and everyone stood in the shallows looking at it, filthy and exhausted and absurdly proud.

"It's not much," Juno said.

"It's ours," said Sable.

"It's dinner," said Teodor, and went to catch another catfish.

* * *

Petra took the middle watch that night, as she always did, sitting up by the banked fire while the others slept in a row of bedrolls like a litter of puppies. The moon was down. The stars over the valley were thick enough to swim in. Somewhere upstream, miles away, the river made a sound like a man clearing his throat, deciding what to say next.

She thought about her ledger. The Syndicate's ledger. The neat columns of what she owed, compounding weekly, cheerful interest, friendly terms, Doss Merrick's warm handshake. She had spent two years thinking of that number as the truest fact about herself. The sum of her. What she was worth, minus what she owed.

But sitting here, watching Sable sleep with a wrench still in one hand, listening to Teodor murmur recipes in his dreams, knowing that Juno had rerouted them through country she'd sworn off and hadn't complained once, feeling Elias's faith resting on her shoulders like something warm and heavy—

She thought: *I would trust these people with my life.*

Not because they were skilled, though they were. Not because she had no choice, though she mostly didn't. But because when the river took something away from them, they had built something back with their bare hands, together, without being asked. That was worth more than any collateral. That was the only credit that had never once failed her.

The realization landed quietly, the way big things do. No thunder. Just a settling, like ballast finding its place in the hold.

*You'd trust them with your life,* she told herself. *So stop acting like the only thing you're protecting is your own neck.*

The fire popped. Downstream, far off, something else popped too—once, twice—and Petra's head came up, every nerve suddenly awake.

A sound like a rifle shot carries a long way over water at night. So does a boat engine, if you know what to listen for. And beneath the ordinary night-noise of the Kettlewater—frogs, wind in the willows, the tick of cooling gravel—Petra heard something new and wrong.

An engine. Idling. Somewhere upstream in the dark, holding position, patient as a spider.

She sat very still and counted her crew's breathing, four rhythms, steady. Then she looked north, toward the black seam of the channel, and did the math she hated most: whoever was out there hadn't come to fish.

They knew where the *Marta June* was. And come morning, the river would decide whether Petra had bought enough time—or whether the dry season had been working for the other side all along.
