## 7. What Elias Won't Say

Sable had learned to sleep in pieces. Three years of couch-surfing and bus depots and the floor of a mechanic's garage in Dellwater had taught her how to fold herself into whatever dark corner a night allowed, how to wake at the smallest wrong sound—a door, a footstep, the particular quality of silence that meant someone was moving who shouldn't be. On the expedition boat, moored in the willow shadow of a bend the maps called only Mile 212, she'd claimed a sleeping spot under the aft gunwale, wedged between the tool locker and the hull, where she could hear everything and be seen by no one.

So when the match flared at two in the morning, she was already awake to see it.

She didn't move. That was the first rule of being small and seventeen and nobody's: don't move until you know what you're looking at. She lay with her cheek against the cold aluminum and watched through the gap between the locker and the gunwale rail, a slit of night about as wide as her hand.

Elias Wrenn sat at the stern bench with a tin cup of river water he wasn't drinking and a leather journal open on his knees. The match had come from a box of storm matches they kept in the galley dry-box, and he'd struck it against his thumbnail like a man who'd done it ten thousand times, in wind, in rain, in places where a flame was the only warm thing left in the world. The light made a small orange room around him. It caught the hollows of his face and made them deeper, made him look like a map himself—ridges and canyons and dry washes where water used to run.

He tore a page from the journal. He held it to the flame.

Sable's first thought, absurdly, was *fire hazard*. Teodor would have a fit; the man kept the galley organized like a surgical theater and treated open flame on the boat like a personal insult. Her second thought came slower and colder: *he's destroying it*.

The page caught, curled, went to ash in the tin cup. Elias fed in another. He was unhurried about it. That was the part that made the skin on Sable's arms prickle. People burned things in panic—she'd seen a man in Dellwater burn a whole box of letters in a trash can at three a.m., sobbing, feeding the fire too fast, smothering it, feeding it again. Elias burned the way he did everything else: slowly, deliberately, like a man paying out rope. Like a man who had planned this burn for a long time and was only waiting for the right dark.

She counted the pages. She couldn't help it—counting was what her brain did instead of sleeping, instead of feeling. Four. Six. Nine. The cup filled with gray flakes that looked, in the matchlight, like moths. Elias's lips moved as he burned. Not reading—she'd seen him read, and he read with his whole face, frowning, tracking with a cracked thumbnail. This was different. This was *reciting*. Or maybe praying. She couldn't tell the difference at that distance and wasn't sure there was one.

The matchbox sat by his knee. Eleven matches gone, she estimated, by the little drift of spent sticks. He'd been at this a while before she woke. Which meant he'd chosen this night, this bend, this hour—when Petra slept like the dead in the bow because she always took first watch and then second watch too, because Petra Underhill did not know how to stop working, and Juno slept with her headphones on and her back to the world, and Teodor snored like a friendly landslide.

Everyone accounted for. Everyone asleep. Except the girl under the gunwale, who was not supposed to exist.

* * *

She should have gone back to sleep. That was the other rule: see nothing, know nothing, want nothing. Wanting things was how you got hurt. Knowing things was how you got *used*—she'd learned that from a foster father in Corrick County who'd found her diary and read it aloud at the dinner table like it was a comedy routine, and she'd learned it again from a shop owner who'd discovered she could strip an alternator blindfolded and suddenly had opinions about where she slept and who she talked to. Knowledge was currency, and currency attracted thieves.

But she lay there, and she watched, and she wanted to know.

The last page he burned was different. She could tell because he hesitated. He held it up to the matchlight first, the way you'd hold a photograph, and his thumb moved across it—once, twice—like he was touching a face. Then it went into the flame and he watched it all the way down, and when it was ash he closed the journal, and the journal was *thin*. Sable's mind did the math without being asked: the thing had been fat as a brick when they'd launched from Crooked Bend eleven days ago. She'd noticed it because she noticed everything he carried—the canvas satchel, the journal, the map case that never left his chest. Now the journal had the slumped look of something emptied out. Half a brick. Less.

He sat for a while after, just breathing. Then he did the thing that turned Sable's blood from cold to *interesting*: he took out the map.

The map case was oilcloth, tied with a leather cord, and he wore it against his sternum like a breastplate. Petra had seen the map—Petra had sworn her oath over it, whatever that oath was; Sable had heard the shape of that conversation through a hospice door she'd been fixing the latch of, because fixing latches was her job and eavesdropping was her hobby and the two overlapped more than anyone suspected. The map was Elias's. Elias's map, Elias's hand, drawn—so the story went—in his younger years, when he'd prospected the upper river and found the place he called the Last Rivergate.

Except.

Except that Elias unrolled the map now, in the matchlight, and Sable—whose eyes worked in the dark the way other people's worked in daylight, a gift from three years of reading by flashlight under blankets—Sable saw the paper was *wrong*. It wasn't expedition paper. It wasn't the waterproof stock Petra used, or the greasy chart paper Juno had aboard, or even the cheap spiral notebook pages Teodor wrapped around the dry goods to keep them from rattling. It was old. Old the way church paper was old, heavy and soft-edged, the kind that went brown at the corners like a leaf. And it was covered in a hand that was not Elias's hand.

She knew his hand. She'd spent eleven days watching him mark up Juno's river charts with a carpenter's pencil—big looping letters, practical, a little shaky now, the *g* always open at the bottom like a fishhook. The writing on the old map was small and upright and ruled like a schoolmaster's, ink gone the color of dried blood. And there were *annotations* in Elias's hand over it, pencil over ink, decades of pencil, layers of it—the way you'd annotate a thing you'd been studying your whole life. Not a thing you'd made. A thing you'd *found*.

The map was older than he claimed. Older than him, maybe. And it hadn't come from his prospecting days. It had come from somewhere else, and he'd built his whole story on top of it like a house on a foundation nobody was supposed to see.

Sable lay very still and felt her curiosity do the thing it always did, the thing she both loved and hated about herself: it leaned forward. It wanted more. It was a dog on a leash and she was holding the leash and the leash was winning.

* * *

He didn't see her. That was the strange part, looking back. Elias rolled the map, tied the cord, stowed the case against his chest, and sat a while longer looking at the river, and then he gathered the matchbox and the tin cup and went below to his bunk with the careful slowness of a man conserving his last coins of strength, and he never once looked toward the gunwale. Either he hadn't seen her or—she considered this in the dark, turning it over like a stone—he'd seen her and decided she didn't matter. Both possibilities stung. She preferred not being seen. She *hated* not mattering.

In the morning she was up before anyone, which was normal, because Sable slept four hours a night and had since she was twelve, and she went about her normal work—checking the bilge pump, greasing the winch, re-taping the frayed spot on the fuel line that Petra kept saying they'd replace at the next cache—while the boat woke up around her. Teodor got the stove going and the smell of coffee and frying dough filled the hull like a blessing. Juno emerged with her headphones around her neck and her hair doing something structural. Petra came out of the bow already talking, already planning the day's water, the day's miles, the day's everything.

And Elias came up into the light thin and gray and smiling, and his journal was in his satchel, and it was half a brick, and nobody noticed.

Nobody but Sable.

She started watching him properly after that. Not obviously—she was good at not obvious; she'd had a childhood of practice at being furniture—but constantly. She watched him at meals, where he ate half of what Teodor put in front of him and pushed the rest around like a man rearranging furniture in a house he was about to leave. She watched him on the water, where he sat in the bow seat Petra gave him because it was the smoothest ride, and watched the riverbanks with an attention that had nothing casual in it. Everyone else looked at the river. Elias looked at the *land*—at the cutbanks, the side channels, the old fence posts standing in the shallows with their wire still strung between them like the river had drowned a farm and forgotten to mention it. He was checking. She realized that on the third day of watching: he was checking the map against the world, and the world was answering, and every time it answered right his mouth did something complicated that wasn't quite a smile.

And she watched him at night.

He burned pages twice more that week. The second time she was ready—she'd positioned herself under the gunwale before he ever came up, and she watched the whole ritual from the first match, and this time she caught the words. Not all of them. His voice was a dry whisper, more breath than sound, and the river made its opinions loudly underneath. But the river paused sometimes. Rivers do; they go quiet between riffles like a held breath. And in one of those pauses, Elias held a page to the flame and read it through once before it burned, and Sable heard him say:

"—and the water came up out of a clear sky. Marta says the church bell rang all night under the water. She says you could hear it if you put your ear to the ground. She says the men who did it are still—"

The page caught. The rest went to ash.

Sable lay with her heart going like a trapped bird. *The men who did it.* Did *what*? The flood—Petra had let one crumb slip on the fourth day, when she thought Sable was asleep in the bow: *the valley flooded, one night, forty years back, whole town gone under.* Sable had filed it away the way she filed everything, in the big dark drawer that never filled up. But now the crumb had grown teeth. *The men who did it.* Floods didn't have men behind them. Floods were weather. Unless they weren't.

And *Marta*. Who was Marta? Not his wife—he'd never mentioned a wife, and the hospice smell of him, the monkish neatness of his gear, said a man who'd been alone a long time. A sister? Someone from the drowned town? Someone who'd *been there*, the night the water came up out of a clear sky?

The third burning was two nights later, and by then Sable had done something about it. She'd gone through his satchel.

She wasn't proud of it. She was also not sorry. There was a difference, and she'd stopped pretending otherwise around the time she turned fifteen. The satchel was under his bunk and he slept like the dead now, deeper every day, his breath going thin and rattling in a way that made Teodor frown and Juno, quietly, without telling anyone she'd noticed, start leaving the electrolyte powder where he'd find it first. Sable lifted the flap with two fingers and used her phone's dimmest light, cupped, and went through it the way she'd go through an engine: fast, thorough, everything back in its place, torqued to spec.

The journal was there. She didn't read it—no time, and its remaining pages were few and dense, and she'd have had to take it, and taking it would be a line she wasn't ready to cross. But under the journal, flat against the satchel's bottom, was an envelope. Old, soft, resealed so many times the flap had gone to felt. On it, in the schoolmaster hand she'd seen on the map, was written: *For the one who comes after. If the water takes me first, tell them where.*

Inside was not paper. Inside was a photograph, and Sable looked at it for maybe four seconds, and those four seconds rearranged the whole trip.

It was a town. A real town, photographed from the ridge above it—main street, church with a steeple, a grain elevator, houses with porches, laundry on lines. Forty years of river silt had not been kind to the colors; everything had gone amber and brown, the way old things do. But it was unmistakably the valley. She knew it the way you know a face you've dreamed about: the shape of the ridgelines on either side, the notch in the eastern wall where the water would come out, the limestone arch far downstream, small in the photograph but there, unmistakable, the Gate itself standing over a town that was alive. That had *been* alive. Streets. People. A bell.

And on the back, in pencil, in Elias's hand but pressed so hard the paper had dented, someone had written a list. Not a list of places. A list of *names*. Five of them. Four had lines through them, struck out with the violence of a man who wanted the names to stop existing. The fifth was circled, and the circle had been traced so many times the pencil had nearly cut through.

She couldn't read the circled name in the cupped light. She put the photograph back exactly as she'd found it, envelope under journal, flap down, satchel zipped, and she lay back under the gunwale and stared at the hull above her and listened to Elias breathe his thin rattle ten feet away.

*The men who did it are still—*

Still what. Still alive. Still out there. Still *looking*.

Because that was the piece that slid into place last, the piece she'd been circling for days without letting herself land on, the piece that turned curiosity into something with a colder name. Elias hadn't just found the map. Someone had given it to him, or left it to him—*for the one who comes after*—and that someone had known the valley's story from the inside, had been Marta or been Marta's kin, and had spent however many years afraid. And Elias, dying, had inherited the fear along with the map. And the fear had a shape, and the shape was a list of five names, four of them crossed out, one of them circled and circled and circled.

And Sable thought about the boat that had shadowed them out of Crooked Bend—the survey barge with its too-clean instruments and its too-casual crew, the one Juno had watched through binoculars for a full hour before saying nothing to anyone. Sable had seen Juno watching. Sable watched everything. And she thought about how Petra had come back from her meeting with the cheerful Syndicate men with half her debt forgiven and a smile that didn't reach her eyes, and how generous the terms had been, and how nobody generous had ever wanted anything from Sable Underhill except the thing itself.

The Rivergate wasn't a legend Elias was chasing. It was a place somebody had drowned on purpose, and somebody had made a list of the men who'd done it, and one of those men was still alive, and still had money, and still had boats, and had maybe—probably—*certainly*—been looking for the valley for a very long time.

And they were guiding a dying man straight to it, up a river that was dropping by the day, in a boat that everyone upstream could see.

The match flared again at the stern. Sable didn't look this time. She didn't need to. She lay in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the hull, feeling the cold river move underneath, and she counted Elias's spent matches by their smell—sulfur and ash riding the night air—and she made herself a promise, quiet and hard, the kind she was good at keeping because she'd never had anyone to keep promises *for* before this boat, this crew, this ragged accidental family she was starting, against all her rules, to love.

Whatever Elias was burning, she was going to know what it said. And whatever was coming up that river behind them—

She heard it before she understood it: a sound too low for the others, threaded under the water and the wind. An engine. Not theirs. Far upstream, around the bend, running dark, no lights, moving slow the way you move when you're looking for something and don't want it to see you first.

Sable lay still in the dark, seventeen years old and suddenly the only one awake, and listened to the hunters come.
