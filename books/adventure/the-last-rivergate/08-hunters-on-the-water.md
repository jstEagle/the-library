## 8. Hunters on the Water

The fuel cache was supposed to be a rumor.

That's how Elias had described it back in the hospice room, tapping one yellowed fingernail against the map's lower margin: *Old Corley stashed diesel at the mouth of Sutter Creek. If it's still there after forty years, God bless him and his paranoia.* Forty years of rain and rot, and yet when they rounded the creek mouth that morning, there it was—three steel drums sunk to their shoulders in a gravel bank, tarped under a slab of corrugated tin weighted with river stones, as if Corley had expected the water itself to come looking for them.

Petra waded ashore first, because that was the job. The drums were half-full, sealed well enough that the diesel smelled like diesel instead of like swamp, and she stood there for a long moment with her hand flat on the cold metal, feeling something dangerously close to gratitude.

"Boss," Sable called from the boat, "you're smiling. It's freaking me out."

"Don't get used to it." She turned, shading her eyes against the white glare off the water. "We fuel up, we eat, we push on before the heat kills the current. Two hours, no more."

Two hours. She'd learn later how expensive those two hours would be.

* * *

Teodor cooked anyway. He always cooked, no matter what Petra said about schedules, and by now she'd stopped fighting it because the truth was that his cooking held the expedition together better than her navigation did. He'd rigged a grate over a driftwood fire and was doing something criminal to canned beans with dried chilies he'd been hoarding since Crooked Bend, and the smell rolled down the gravel bar like an apology for everything else—the heat, the dropping gauge on Juno's staff, the way the river had gone thin and glassy overnight, running maybe eight inches below where the almanac swore it should be.

Juno crouched at the waterline taking measurements she didn't share, which was fine. Petra didn't want numbers right now. Numbers were just different shapes of bad news.

Elias sat propped against a drum, wrapped in two blankets despite the ninety-degree morning, watching the creek mouth with the fixed attention of a man who had spent four decades looking over his shoulder. His breathing had a whistle in it now. Petra heard it every time the wind died, and every time she heard it she did the arithmetic again—not of debt this time, but of miles. How many left. How many he had in him.

"Eat," she said, handing him a tin cup of beans.

"I will."

"You said that an hour ago."

"And I meant it then too." But he took the cup, and he ate three spoonfuls before his appetite quit on him, and Petra pretended not to count.

Sable was underneath the second boat—their spare, lashed atop the gear raft—tightening something and swearing at it in a vocabulary she'd clearly assembled from three continents. Teodor drifted over to supervise, and the two of them argued companionably about torque while Petra walked the perimeter without making it look like walking the perimeter. That was another part of the job nobody put in the brochure. You learned to scan a treeline the way you used to scan a sky. Both could go black on you in minutes.

The river ran low and quiet. Too quiet. In her old life, quiet weather had always made her itch—the atmosphere holding its breath, stacking its argument somewhere over the horizon. Out here the same instinct fired, but the storm it warned of wore boots.

She was halfway through her own bowl when Sable went still.

Petra saw it before she understood it: the girl's head tilting, wrench frozen mid-turn, whole body cocked toward the downstream bend like a deer's. Then, quietly, without looking up from her work: "Engine. Two of them. Coming slow."

* * *

They came around the bend at a tourist's pace, all polish and patience, and Petra's stomach dropped through the gravel into the bedrock of the world.

Airboats. Not the Syndicate's heavy survey barge she'd half expected—something smaller, faster, dressed down. Matte hulls, canvas covers over the cargo decks, men in fishing shirts that had never seen a fish. The lead boat swung wide in a courteous arc and idled up to the gravel bar, and the man who stepped off onto the stones did it lightly, hands visible, smiling like Sunday.

Not Doss Merrick. Doss didn't travel; Doss received. This was one of his instruments—a broad, pleasant-faced man in his forties with sun-bleached hair and the relaxed shoulders of someone who had never once been surprised by anything. Behind him, two more men stayed with the boats. One of them checked a rifle action with the absent-mindedness of a man cleaning his nails.

"Well, hell," the pleasant man called, spreading his arms. "People! Out here I mostly see herons, and frankly the conversation's thin. Y'all got coffee?"

Petra set down her bowl. She made herself smile, and she felt the smile click into place the way a mask does—slightly wrong, slightly late, but on.

"We've got beans," she said. "Coffee costs extra."

"Then beans, and my undying respect." He came up the bank with an easy gait, stopping a polite ten feet away, close enough to read the patch on her jacket if he cared to, far enough that grabbing him would be a decision rather than a reflex. Smart. "Name's Calloway. Ray Calloway. Working the upper drainage for Vane Hydrographic—survey season, you know how it is." He said the Syndicate's name the way other men said *the weather*, casual, ambient, everywhere. That was the point of it. "You folks running commercial?"

"Private expedition," Petra said. "Scientific. Water quality sampling for a university consortium." The lie came out smooth and boring, which was the only way to tell one. Boring lies don't invite follow-up questions.

"Couple hundred miles upriver for water samples." Calloway whistled softly, shaking his head in admiration. "Academics. God love 'em." His eyes moved past her, unhurried, cataloguing: the boats, the gear, the tarp over the drums. They rested on the drums exactly one beat longer than on anything else. "Mind if I ask your route? We log traffic for the company. Insurance thing. Nobody reads the reports, but somebody gets paid to file them, so."

"Sutter Creek to the Middle Fork, then we'll reassess depending on levels." She shrugged. "River's dropping fast. Might turn around at the Fork if it keeps up."

"Might turn around." He nodded gravely, as though she'd shared something profound. "Smart. Water's the whole game out here, isn't it? Too much or too little, never just right." He smiled again, and this time it reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Funny thing, though."

Here it comes, Petra thought, and kept chewing.

"Funny thing is, our aerial run Tuesday picked up a party matching your description—" he gestured loosely at the camp, at all of them, at the shape of their lives laid out on a gravel bar "—and they weren't headed for the Middle Fork. They were past it. Heading up toward the old Wrenn country." He tilted his head. "And here you are, sitting on Elias Corley's cache like you had the combination. Which, I mean—respect. That man hid supplies like a squirrel with a grudge. But it does make a person curious."

Behind her, Petra heard Teodor's ladle stop moving. Sable hadn't looked up from the boat, but her wrenching had gone silent too, and silence from Sable was louder than any alarm.

"Aerial survey," Petra repeated. "Your company photographs random canoes two hundred miles from anywhere?"

"Company photographs everything." Calloway spread his hands, all innocence, all teeth. "Land, water, mineral rights, old claims, new claims. Especially old ones. You'd be amazed what turns up when the river drops, Ms.—"

"Underhill."

"—Ms. Underhill." He said her name gently, tasting it, and she watched him decide whether he recognized it. She couldn't tell if he did. With these people you never could; they filed everything, and they spent nothing until the interest was right. "Anyway. No trouble. Just neighbors being neighborly." He dug in his shirt pocket and produced a card—actual cardstock, out here, because the Syndicate believed in theater—and held it out. "If you see anything interesting upriver. Old structures, survey markers, records, that kind of thing. Company pays finders' fees. Generous ones. Some of the old settlements out this way had paperwork worth real money, and the families that owned it are mostly dead, so it's all just... sitting there. Waiting for somebody with initiative."

Petra took the card. Her hand didn't shake. She'd have paid money for it to shake—at least a tremor would have been honest.

"We'll keep it in mind," she said.

"You do that." Calloway rocked back on his heels, satisfied, and for a moment his smile softened into something almost human, almost kind, which was the most frightening expression he'd worn yet. "One more thing, free of charge, neighbor to neighbor. River's due to drop hard inside the week. Harder than the almanac says—we've got gauges upstream the government doesn't know about. After Thursday, there's stretches up there a loaded boat can't float. Whatever you're looking for?" He tapped the side of his nose. "Find it quick."

* * *

They watched the airboats recede downstream until the sound of their engines dissolved into the heat-shimmer, and then nobody spoke for a full minute. The fire crackled. The river whispered over its own shrinking bones.

"He knew the cache was Corley's," Sable said finally, sliding out from under the spare boat, wrench dangling. "He wasn't guessing. He said the name like he'd rehearsed it."

"He wanted me to know he knew," Petra said. "That's the message. The questions were just packaging."

Juno came up from the waterline, staff in hand, face carefully blank in the way Petra had learned meant her mind was running at full boil. "Their hull draft was shallow even for airboats. Modified. Those aren't survey platforms—they're interdiction craft. Somebody built them to chase people up shallow rivers." She paused. "Somebody built them recently."

"They know roughly where we're going," Teodor said. It wasn't a question. He'd gone very still, the way he did when old fear surfaced, and his accent thickened around the edges of the words. "They photographed us. They let us pass once already, at the bend, and I thought—I told myself it was coincidence. Now they come smiling with cards." He spat, delicately, into the fire. "In my country, when men smile like this, afterward there are fewer people."

"So we talk about it," Petra said. "All of it. Right now."

She told them. Not everything—she kept Elias's name out of it, kept the promise's exact terms out of it—but she gave them the shape of the truth: that the Syndicate had financed this trip's forgiveness, that the destination was chosen by their dying employer, that the men in the fishing shirts were not hydrographers and never had been. She watched each of them take it differently. Juno went cold and precise, already calculating. Teodor went grim and unsurprised, a man adding one more entry to a long ledger of reasons. Sable just looked at her, and the look had a crack in it, young and raw: *were you ever going to tell us, or only when it was too late?*

"The card," Juno said. "Give it here."

Petra handed it over. Juno turned it in the light, sniffed it, held it edge-on. "Stock paper. Nothing hidden in the stock. Phone number's a Harrow Ford exchange." She looked up. "It's not a business card, Petra. It's a leash. They want us to call when we find whatever we find. Which means they believe there's something to find."

"Do you believe that?" Petra asked. "That there's something up there?"

Juno was quiet a moment. The river ticked over the gravel, patient, lowering.

"I believe," she said slowly, "that forty years ago something happened in this valley that everyone who lived through it agreed never to discuss. I grew up on stories about towns that drowned. My grandmother's cousin lost a claim up here. And I believe that a dying man paid a debt-forgiving loan shark to smuggle him home to it." She met Petra's eyes. "So yes. I believe there's something up there. And I believe Ray Calloway believes it too, or he wouldn't have burned fuel coming to smile at us."

* * *

They broke camp fast after that, faster than the two hours she'd promised herself, and the work had a new texture to it—quicker, tighter, everyone moving without being asked twice. Fear, Petra reflected, was a hell of a project manager. By early afternoon they were loaded and pushing upstream against the thinning current, and the valley walls had begun to lean in around them, older and darker, the forest going from scrub to cathedral.

Elias waited until they were underway, until the engine noise made privacy, and then he beckoned her close with two fingers, an emperor's gesture from a ruin of a man.

"You lied well," he rasped.

"Not well enough. He'll report the boat description. They'll match it to my name eventually—Doss knows who's driving this circus."

"Yes." Elias coughed, wet and long, and stared up at the limestone starting to show itself in the cutbanks, pale ribs of the earth. "They know where we're bound. They've probably known since the day I signed the papers. Doss doesn't lend money, girl—he buys information, and I was the information." His hand found her wrist, grip shockingly strong for all its frailty. "Which means everything from here is a race. Them to us. Us to the truth. The river to both of us."

"Then we win the race."

"Look at it," he whispered, and nodded at the water sliding past the hull, low and clear and getting shallower by the hour, boulders surfacing like sleeping animals waking. "Look at what we're racing *on*."

Petra looked. The river that had carried them this far was turning into a suggestion of a river, a rumor of wet rock, and somewhere ahead of them—she could feel it the way she'd once felt storms building past the horizon—the channel was pinching down toward whatever gate Elias had spent his whole life trying to reach.

The promise had been a rope when she took it. Standing at his shoulder, watching the water die, she understood it had become a cage instead—one whose bars were made of her own word, and whose lock was a dying man's last wish, and outside it the Syndicate's hunters were already paddling patiently toward the door.

Ahead, around the next bend, something glinted white in the afternoon glare. Limestone. A lot of it.

"Hold course," Petra heard herself say. "And somebody wake me if the river disappears entirely."
