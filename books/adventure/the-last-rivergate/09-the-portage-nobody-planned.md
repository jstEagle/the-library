## 9. The Portage Nobody Planned

Teodor had learned to read rivers the way other men learned to read faces—slowly, painfully, and always a season too late to avoid the worst of what they meant.

The channel ahead of them was saying something now, and it was not saying anything good.

He stood at the bow of the lead boat with a wooden spoon still tucked into his apron pocket out of habit, though he hadn't cooked in three days of running water, and watched the river do a thing rivers were not supposed to do. It bent hard to starboard around a bluff of red shale, and where it should have come back—where every map Juno owned, every chart Elias had memorized, said it came back—the water simply stopped. Not slowed. Stopped. A wall of collapsed bank lay across the channel like a fist slammed down on a table, forty feet of hillside sheared loose and slid into the current, dragging trees with it, whole oaks standing upright in the mud like drowned men who'd changed their minds about going down.

Behind that fist, the channel was gone. Dry gravel gleamed in the morning light for a quarter mile, maybe more, before the river found itself again in a thin silver thread at the far end.

"Almanac says this bend holds six feet in August," Juno said from the stern. Her voice was flat, which Teodor had learned was her voice for *I am frightened and will not admit it*. "It's holding zero."

"Almanacs are written by optimists," Petra said. She stood hip-deep at the boat's edge, testing the gravel with a pole, her braid dark with spray. "All right. Nobody panic. We portage."

The word landed on the crew like weather. Teodor felt it settle on his shoulders before anyone touched a pack.

* * *

They had two boats, four people, one dying old man, and somewhere north of nine hundred pounds of gear, fuel, food, and Elias's crates—that last item never discussed, always guarded, lashed under oilcloth like a coffin riding passenger. The gravel bar between living water and dead channel was soft in places, quicksand-soft, the kind of ground that took a boot and asked polite questions about your ankle. The sun came up over the shale bluffs and turned the whole portage route into a kiln.

Teodor organized it the only way he knew how: by feeding people first.

"You do not carry a boat on an empty stomach," he told Sable, who was already strapping herself into a harness made of rope and stubbornness. "You carry a boat on eggs. Sit. Eat. Argue with me after."

"I don't argue, I comply sarcastically." But she sat, cross-legged on a dry patch, and ate the eggs he fried in bacon fat with dried chilies, because Teodor cooked the way he'd crossed three borders—with intensity, improvisation, and total refusal to acknowledge any authority but hunger. He'd been a cook in a hotel in Trieste until the hotel's owner turned out to be moving money for men who broke fingers. He'd been a cook in a roadhouse in Maramureș until the roadhouse burned with questions attached. He'd been a cook on a freighter until the freighter's manifest stopped matching its cargo. Three countries, three exits, and everywhere the same lesson: keep your hands busy, keep your mouth shut, keep everyone fed, and maybe the world will let you stay a while.

Petra's expedition was the longest he'd stayed anywhere in eleven years. He did not examine why. He just cooked bigger portions.

Elias ate sitting up in the stern of the second boat, wrapped in blankets despite the heat, his hands trembling around the bowl. Teodor had started making his food soft and salt-heavy, the way you fed men whose bodies were closing their accounts. The old man ate everything and thanked him like it was a banquet.

"Chef," Elias said, as Teodor collected the bowl. "This gravel. I've walked it before."

Teodor paused. "Before the collapse?"

"Before there was a channel here at all." Elias looked downriver at the dead stretch of gravel with an expression Teodor couldn't name—grief, or recognition, or both wearing the same coat. "Rivers change their minds. People should be allowed to as well." He said nothing else, and Teodor, keeping his mouth shut per the oldest rule, carried the bowl back to the wash bucket.

* * *

The first boat went easy. That was the trap of it—everything went easy at first, and then the world remembered you.

Four of them hauled the lead boat up onto rollers of cottonwood logs, Sable calling the rhythm because she had the best ear for it, Petra and Teodor pulling, Juno steering the runners off soft patches. They made four hundred yards the first hour and Teodor let himself believe the day would be long but survivable.

Then the ground opened.

Not dramatically. That was the cruelty of it. No crack, no warning rumble—just a patch of gravel that looked exactly like every other patch of gravel, and Teodor's leg sank to the knee, and his body did what bodies do, which is grab the nearest solid thing with all its stupid desperate strength. The nearest solid thing was the gunwale of the boat they were hauling. The boat, burdened with eight hundred pounds of itself and their lives, swung on its log rollers with the slow indifferent momentum of a planet.

He got his arm over the gunwale instead of under it. That was the whole difference between a bruise and a break, and he owed that difference to a dockhand in Constanța named Pavel who'd lost two fingers teaching him. But the shoulder took the swing anyway. Something in it went—not tore, exactly, more like a rope slipping a knot—and the pain arrived a half-second later, white and total, and Teodor sat down hard in the gravel and discovered he was making a sound he didn't recognize as coming from himself.

"Off it. Off it—" Petra was there, hauling the boat's weight off him, Sable bracing the hull, Juno suddenly very close and very calm, her hands checking his arm with a clinical detachment that he later understood was how she kept from panicking.

"Dislocated?" Sable asked.

"Subluxed," Juno said. "Partially out, partially back. He'll live. He won't pull worth anything for a week."

"A week," Teodor managed, through his teeth, "we do not have."

"No," Juno agreed quietly. "We don't."

They splinted it with a sling of his own apron, which struck him as either funny or holy—he couldn't decide which—and then the arithmetic of the portage changed, the way arithmetic does when a man goes down. Nine hundred pounds, four carriers, minus one.

Minus one, plus a seventeen-year-old who hadn't been asked.

* * *

Sable didn't ask. That was the thing about her Teodor understood best, because it was the thing about himself. Kids who'd been left learned to volunteer before anyone could choose them for anything. You carried double so nobody could put you down for carrying single. You fixed the pump before someone noticed it was broken. You made yourself necessary because necessary things got kept.

She rigged a tumpline from a pack strap and a piece of firewood, braided extra loops into the harness ropes with her quick scarred fingers, and when Petra tried to cut the loads lighter, Sable said, "Don't. Just don't. I'm the strongest pound-for-pound person on this crew and we all know it. Give me the heavy end and stop doing math at me."

Petra looked at her for a long moment. Teodor saw something pass over the older woman's face—guilt, maybe, or the specific ache of watching a kid be harder than she should have to be.

"Double loads," Petra said finally. "And you rotate out when I say."

"And when you say is when I say," Sable said, and hoisted the fuel drum onto her back like it had insulted her cooking.

So they moved. All afternoon, in the kiln-light, they moved. Teodor walked with his arm slung and his good hand steadying the boats' stern lines, useless in the way that made him want to scream, useful only in the way of a man who could talk. So he talked. He told stories in three languages, mostly nonsense, anything to keep the rhythm going—about the hotel chef in Trieste who'd made him cry with a beurre blanc, about the border guard in Hungary who'd traded passage for a jar of pickled peppers, about the freighter cook who'd taught him that rice forgives everything if you're patient. Sable laughed at the right places even with two hundred pounds cutting her shoulders into grooves. Petra pulled like a woman paying off a debt with her spine. And the gravel bar unrolled ahead of them, endless, glittering, indifferent.

By late afternoon the sun had beaten the color out of everything and Sable had stopped talking, which frightened Teodor more than his own shoulder had. She was stumbling on the flat ground now. The fuel drum rode her back like a sin.

"Sable." He caught the stern line in his good hand and leaned on it. "Sit. Now. That is not a request, that is a menu—you sit, I feed you, those are the options."

"I can make the far water before—"

"The far water will still be wet tomorrow." He said it gently, in the voice he used for dough that needed resting. "Everything important keeps. Sit down, child, before your body files a complaint I have to answer for."

She sat. She drank the electrolyte brew he pushed into her hands and ate the salt pork and hardtack he'd been hoarding against exactly this hour, and within four minutes she was asleep in the shade of the overturned boat, mid-sentence, one hand still curled around a carabiner like a rosary.

Petra crouched beside him at the fire ring, rubbing the grit from her palms. "That kid's going to kill herself being useful."

"Yes," Teodor said. "I know the disease. There is no cure. There is only feeding them and lying about how much they rest." He nodded toward the sleeping girl. "Watch her hands when she thinks no one sees. They shake. Not from fear. From the effort of never putting anything down."

Petra didn't answer, but she looked at Sable a long time, and Teodor thought: *good. Let it cost you something too.*

* * *

It was Juno who solved the river.

Teodor noticed it happening the way you notice weather changing—a pressure shift, a silence with a different texture. While the rest of them slept the hot dead hours, Juno had walked the length of the collapsed channel alone, and now she came back along the shale bluff with her boots in her hand, barefoot on the hot rock, and stood looking at the dead water below the slide with an expression Teodor had seen once before, years ago, on a man looking at a door he'd sworn never to open again.

"There's water in the oxbow," she said.

Everyone looked up. Even Elias.

"Old meander, cut off decades ago. It's choked with cane and it doesn't connect to the main channel anymore—not on any survey I've ever seen." She said *any survey I've ever seen* in a strange careful voice, like handling glass. "But the gradient's there. If we cut the cane plug at the downstream end, the seep water pooled in the oxbow will drain toward the channel and give us maybe eighteen inches of flow for a few hundred yards. Enough to float the empty boats through the worst of the gravel. We portage the cargo light, run the hulls light, save our backs half the distance."

"That's hydrology," Sable said slowly, awake now, squinting at her. "That's, like, actual professional-grade hydrology."

Juno's jaw tightened. "It's ditch-digging."

"It's rerouting a river," Sable said.

"It's reading land that doesn't want to be read," Juno said, and there it was—the door, the old country of her competence, the thing she'd buried. Teodor had pieced it together over campfires and silences: a university, a flood, a levee board that had needed someone to blame, a career drowned in a single bad report. She'd sworn off rivers the way he'd sworn off kitchens with crooked owners, and here she was, barefoot on a shale bluff, doing the forbidden thing brilliantly.

Petra studied her. "Can you find the plug?"

"I've already found it." Juno pulled her boots back on, and her voice steadied as they laced, as if the act of moving forward reassembled her. "Two hundred yards upstream. Cane mat over a gravel sill. Machete work, an hour, maybe two. Then we open it and pray the water agrees with my math."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I was wrong," Juno said, "and I've been wrong before, and the world continued." She picked up the machete. For a moment she stood there, and Teodor saw her take a breath like a woman stepping into cold water. Then she walked out onto the dead channel, and it seemed to Teodor that she walked differently than she had all trip—less like a fugitive, more like a woman reporting for a job she'd never actually resigned from.

They cut cane until the light went copper. Teodor held bundles with his good arm and passed water and kept everyone fed on dates and jerky, and when the plug finally gave way it did not gush, it *exhaled*—a brown tongue of water sliding out of the hidden oxbow, finding the old scars of the channel, threading through the gravel, gathering itself, widening, deepening, until by full dark there was a foot and a half of moving water where that morning there had been none, and the sound of it running was the sweetest sound Teodor had heard since his mother's kitchen.

Sable whooped. Petra laughed—actually laughed, short and rusty, like a hinge unused. Elias, propped in the boat, murmured something Teodor couldn't catch, but his face in the firelight looked ten years lighter.

Juno stood ankle-deep in the water she'd called back from the dead and said nothing at all. But her shoulders had come down from around her ears, and when Teodor handed her a plate of beans and rice—because victory, in his experience, tastes best immediately and with salt—she took it and said, quietly, almost to herself:

"I forgot I was good at this."

"You did not forget," Teodor said. "You hid it. Those are different rooms in the same house." He tapped his own chest. "I keep mine here. The cooking. Everyone thinks a cook is harmless. Harmless is the best disguise there is."

Juno looked at him across the fire, and for the first time since Crooked Bend, she really looked, person to person, exile to exile.

"What did you bury?" she asked.

Teodor considered the question the way he considered a sauce—what it needed, what it could bear.

"A name," he said finally. "Mine, mostly. The one from before." He smiled, and it cost him less than he expected. "But that is a story for a night with better wine."

* * *

They ran the boats down the resurrected channel at first light, hulls hissing over eighteen inches of Juno's math, everyone wading alongside to lighten the load, and it worked, it *worked*, and Teodor felt something in his chest that he refused to name because naming things was how you lost them. By noon they reached living water. By two o'clock the current had them again, the real river, wide and brown and going somewhere.

It was Sable, high in the bow rigging she'd improvised, who saw it first.

"Smoke," she said. "Downstream. Two points of it. Camp smoke—or engine smoke."

Teodor's shoulder throbbed in time with his heartbeat, and he found he already knew, the way you know weather in an old scar, what kind of smoke waited downriver, and whose.

Petra raised the binoculars. She was quiet for a long moment.

"Strike the awnings," she said at last, and her voice had gone smooth and pleasant, which Teodor had learned was her voice for war. "Nobody waves unless I wave first."
