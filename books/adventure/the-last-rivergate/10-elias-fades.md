## 10. Elias Fades

The fever came back with the moon.

I knew it before I opened my eyes—knew it the way a man knows weather through the wall of a tent, by the pressure of it, the weight of it settling on his chest like a hand. The nights had turned cold upriver, and my body had turned colder still, burning and freezing in the same hour, as if it couldn't decide which way to leave me. Forty years I'd carried this map folded against my heart, and now it seemed the carrying had cost more than I'd budgeted for. That was always the trouble with rivers. They never tell you the price until you're already on the water.

I lay in my bedroll at the stern of the *Wrenn's Folly*—a joke of a name for a joke of a boat, though Teodor had patched her hull so lovingly she was beginning to deserve better—and listened to the expedition breathe around me. Juno's even rhythm from the bow, where she slept like a woman who had made peace with being awake too much of her life. Sable's restless turning, the girl dreaming in machinery, hands twitching over some imagined engine. Teodor snoring in bursts, apologetic even in sleep. And Petra, last to lie down as always, first up, her breathing shallow and counted, the breath of a woman doing arithmetic in the dark.

I knew that arithmetic. Debt math. I'd done it myself once, long ago, in a valley that no longer appeared on maps.

The fever lifted me out of myself for a while. When I came back down, the stars had wheeled westward and someone had draped an extra blanket over me that smelled of woodsmoke and river mud. Petra sat beside me on an overturned crate, wrapped in her coat, watching the black water slide past. She wasn't sleeping. She never really slept anymore; I'd watched her go hollow-eyed and bright-voiced, running on fumes and will, and I recognized the condition because I had invented it.

"Elias." She said it quietly, not knowing if I was under or above the surface. "You're burning up again."

"I'm aware," I said. My voice came out like gravel poured through a sieve. "It's the only fire I've got left. Let me have it."

She didn't laugh. That was the thing about Petra Underhill—I could count on one hand the times I'd seen her truly laugh, and every one of them had surprised her, caught her off guard like a wave from behind. A woman who treated joy as an expense she hadn't authorized. I knew that woman too. I had been that man, forty years ago, before the water took everything except the truth.

"Teodor wants to stop at the next landing," she said. "Get you to a doctor."

"There are no doctors between here and the Gate that aren't Syndicate men."

"Then we turn around."

"No." I said it harder than I meant to, and the effort of it started a cough that went on far too long, deep and wet, the kind of cough that carries news. When it finally released me, she was leaning close, her hand on my shoulder blade, steady as a brace. Her palm was hot from the campfire or from worry; I couldn't tell and didn't want to.

"We're not turning around," I said when I could speak. "And you're going to sit there and listen to me now, Petra, because I don't know how many clear nights I've got left, and this is one of them, and I've been rationing the truth like a miser rations candles. But a man can't take it with him. Believe me. I've checked the luggage allowances."

She settled back onto the crate. Above us the arch of the sky was salted with stars, and somewhere ahead, invisible in the dark, the river ran toward the place where all of this would end—or begin, depending on how you kept your books.

* * *

"The valley flooded in a single night," I told her.

I said it plainly, without ceremony, because ceremony would have made it easier for her to file away as an old man's story, and I needed it to land like a hammer on wet wood.

"One night. Not a season of rising water, not a slow drowning anyone could have walked away from in time. One night in October, forty-one years ago, the levee at the head of Belltown Valley failed—all at once, all along its length, in a way levees don't fail unless they're helped. The water came down the valley floor at better than twenty feet high. It took the town of Harrow Bend in eleven minutes. Eleven minutes, Petra. I've timed it against the church clock they pulled out of the silt, the one that stopped at 2:47. Eleven minutes to erase four hundred people, their homes, their mines, their claims, their records. Everything the water touched, it kept. Everything it took, it hid."

Petra was very still. Storm chasers know how to be still; it's part of the trade, reading a sky without disturbing it. But I saw her jaw work once, twice, the way it did when she was holding a question behind her teeth until it was worth asking.

"You were there," she said finally. Not a question either. We were two professionals trading in the same currency.

"I was nineteen," I said. "A surveyor's apprentice, green as new copper. My uncle held claims in the valley—good ones, silver and something else in the quartz that the assayers wouldn't name out loud. The night it happened I was camped on the ridge above town because I'd quarreled with him about wages and gone up to sulk like the young do. From the ridge I heard the levee go. It doesn't sound like thunder. People always say thunder, but thunder is round and distant. This was a crack—a sound like the world's spine breaking—and then a roar that went on and on, and then, worst of all, silence. Water doesn't scream. It just arrives."

I had to stop. The fever and the memory had gotten tangled together, and for a moment I was nineteen again, standing on that ridge with my lantern guttering, watching the lantern-light catch on the flood plain below where the lights of Harrow Bend had been and weren't.

"Forty-one families," I managed. "I've said that number so many times it's worn smooth as a river stone. Four hundred and six souls, if you count the ones the church register counts and the ones it doesn't—the claim jumpers, the drifters, the women whose names the priest wrote small. The water kept them all. And here is the thing nobody wanted known: the levee didn't fail. It was dynamited. Cut in nine places along three hundred yards, cut by men who knew exactly what they were doing, because the valley's claims stood in the way of a consolidation. Somebody wanted every independent holder in Belltown Valley gone—not ruined, not bought out, *gone*, with their paper drowned and their testimony buried, so the whole district could be refiled clean under new names."

"And those new names," Petra said slowly, "became—"

"The Vane Syndicate." I watched her face in the starlight. She didn't flinch, but something behind her eyes moved the way ice moves on a river—slowly, massively, with a groan you feel in your feet. "Three men signed the first consolidation charter eight months after the flood. Aldous Vane, who lent money. Corliss Merrick, who handled disputes—you'll have met his grandson's style by now, all smiles and soft questions. And a third man, a surveyor named Grady Finch, who drew the new maps. Maps, Petra. He redrew the district so thoroughly that Belltown Valley simply ceased to exist on paper. Every chart since has shown that reach of river running through open range. The water did the killing, but the maps did the burying. You can drown a town in a night. Erasing it takes a cartographer."

"That's why the ledgers matter," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman handling something live. "The church vault. If the records survived—"

"They survived." I let myself smile, and felt how thin my lips had gotten, how little of me the smile had to work with. "That's the terrible mercy of it. The flood that killed them preserved them. Cold water, no air, silt sealing everything like wax. The church of Harrow Bend kept the district's true registry—every claim, every deed, every payment, every bribe taken and bribe refused, and the coroner's preliminary notes from the week before the flood, when a boy found drill holes in the levee face and reported them and was told to forget what he'd seen. The boy's report is dated nine days before 2:47 in the morning. Nine days. That's how long the warning lasted. That's how long somebody chose to let four hundred people keep living."

Petra put her face in her hands. I hadn't expected that. She struck me as a woman who wept privately if at all, who filed grief in some interior warehouse and paid the storage fees without complaint. But there she sat, shoulders shaking once, twice, and then mastered, and when she looked up her eyes were dry and enormous.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why any of us? You could have hired anyone. Why give this to a debt-ridden storm chaser the Syndicate already owns half of?"

"Because the Syndicate owns half of you," I said. "Don't you see? They think they do. They think everyone does—that's their whole religion, that every person has a number, a balance, a price. I needed a guide they'd underestimate. And I needed—" I stopped, and the cough tried to interrupt, and I beat it down. "I needed someone who understood what it costs to owe. Because that's the trap they build, Petra. Not the debt itself. The *forgetting*. You pay and pay and pay, and somewhere in the paying you forget what you were before you owed. You start thinking the ledger is the truth of you. It isn't. It's just the newest page. There are older pages."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and cold, the river steaming where the night's chill met the current. Teodor cooked without being asked—of course he did; the man answered fear with food the way other men answered it with prayer—and the smell of coffee and fried dough drifted through the camp like an argument that everything might still be all right. Sable was already elbow-deep in the pump she'd rigged, muttering to it in a language of clicks and curses she seemed to share only with machines. Juno stood at the rail reading the water, reading it the way priests read scripture, and I saw her glance upstream twice with an expression I recognized: the look of a scientist who has noticed something wrong with the data and hasn't yet decided whether saying so will help or hurt.

But that was later. First there was the gray hour, just past first light, when Petra came and sat on the crate beside my bedroll again, and I knew she'd barely slept, and I knew this was the moment I'd been saving like the last match in the box.

"I need you to promise me something else," I said. "Not the map promise. Something underneath it."

"I'm listening."

"When I'm dead—and I mean dead, Elias Wrenn dead and buried or burned or scattered, not dying, not sick, *dead*—the valley stops being mine. Whatever's down there belongs to whoever it was stolen from. Four hundred and six people get a say in what happens to their own history, and since they can't speak, the speaking falls to whoever's holding the paper." I reached into my coat, into the inner pocket where the map lived against my heart, and pressed my hand flat over it. "I won't tell you what to do with it. Hear me? I've thought about this every night for forty years and I still don't know what's right. Sell it, spend it, burn it, shout it from a courthouse roof—it's yours to weigh. But I will tell you this one thing, and I need you to carry it the way you'd carry a child across a river."

"Say it."

"Some truths are heavier than debts." I looked at her until she looked back, really looked, none of her bravado between us. "You've spent years believing the opposite—that the debt is the heavy thing, that everything else must be light enough to fit around it. That's how they want you. A person carrying a debt will set down anything to protect it, even the truth, especially the truth. But it's a lie they built into the arithmetic. Debts are numbers, and numbers end. Truths don't end. Truths just wait. That valley has been waiting forty-one years, Petra, patient as water. It can wait a little longer. But someday—and I pray it's soon, and I pray it's you—somebody sets it down on the right side of the scale, and everything the Vanes and Merricks and Finches of this world built on top of it comes down. Not because of rage. Because of weight."

Her throat worked. "Elias—"

"I'm not finished." I was tired now, tired down to the marrow, but the words were coming clean for the first time in weeks, the fever burning off the fog, and I intended to spend every coin I had. "You asked why you. Here's the rest of the answer. Because you promised to bring them home. All of them—Juno, Teodor, the girl, yourself. That promise is the only thing on this river heavier than the ledgers, and I gave it to *you* because I watched you calculate hazard runs to feed a debt and never once calculate past your own crew. You count them in every equation. I've seen you do it. A woman who puts other people inside her arithmetic isn't owned by a ledger, whatever the ledger says. She's just temporarily confused about which column she's in."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The river talked instead, the way it always did, that low continuous syllable beneath everything, older than any of our languages.

"I hear you," she said at last. Quietly. No bravado anywhere in it. "Some truths are heavier than debts."

"Say it again."

"Some truths are heavier than debts."

"Again. Until it's yours and not mine."

She said it a third time, and something in her voice changed on the third saying—the way a knot changes when it finally slips tight—and I closed my eyes and let myself believe I'd done the one thing left worth doing.

* * *

The fever took me properly by midmorning. I remember pieces after that, the way you remember a storm from inside it: Teodor's hand cool on my forehead, murmuring something in a language I didn't know but understood anyway; Sable rigging a shade over my corner of the deck with a tarp and three oars, engineering me a little piece of mercy; Juno's fingers on my wrist, counting my pulse with scientific tenderness, then looking up at Petra and saying nothing, which said plenty.

And Petra. Petra everywhere. Steering, hauling, feeding me sips of water boiled with willow bark, sitting beside me through the worst hours with her hand on my arm like an anchor rode. Once, half-dreaming, I heard her talking to the crew in a low voice, telling them we'd make the Gate in three days if the water held, and I heard the word *water* hang in the air a moment too long, and I heard Juno answer, carefully, that the water was dropping faster than any almanac she'd ever trusted.

Faster than the almanac. I turned that phrase over in the soup of my mind and it tasted familiar. Rivers that run ahead of their schedules. Valleys that appear on no charts. Men who smile too easily and ask questions that aren't questions. Somewhere downstream, I was nearly certain, the Syndicate's boats were already moving, patient as their founders had been patient, forty-one years ago, on a night in October when the levee cracked like the world's spine.

I tried to lift my head to warn her. I couldn't find my body's cooperation. So I did the only thing left to a fading man: I caught Petra's sleeve as she passed, and pulled her down, and put my mouth near her ear, and gave her the last of my matches.

"They're coming," I whispered. "They've always been coming. Finish it faster than they do."

Her hand closed over mine, calloused and warm and absolutely steady.

"Sleep," she said. "We've got you."

And because I had given her everything, I believed her.
