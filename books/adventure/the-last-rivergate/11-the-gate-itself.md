## 11. The Gate Itself

The river had been shrinking for three days, and by the morning we rounded the last bend it had grown shy of its own banks entirely—pulled back into a channel no wider than a wagon road, sliding over pale stone with the sound of someone whispering an apology.

I stood at the bow of the lead boat with one hand on the painter's line and the other shading my eyes, and I told myself I was just looking for snags. That's what a guide does. She reads water. She looks for the deadfall and the shallow bar and the seam where current goes wrong. She does not stand in the cold gold light of early morning feeling like a thief outside a church.

Behind me, Sable was tinkering with the pump intake for the fourth time since dawn, muttering to it like it was a stubborn mule. Teodor had the stove going somehow, even under way—the smell of coffee and scorched sugar drifting up from the galley box, absurd and holy all at once. Juno crouched at the stern with her gauge, calling depths in a voice gone flat and careful, the way she got when the numbers were worse than she wanted to say aloud.

"Two feet under the keel," she said. "Maybe two and a half."

"And holding?" I asked.

"For now." A pause. "The almanac says this stretch should be running six feet in October."

"The almanac," Teodor called from below, "also says October is a month."

Nobody laughed. We'd been living inside that joke for a week now, wearing it smooth as a river stone.

Elias sat wrapped in blankets at the center of the boat, propped against the gear crates, his breathing a thin whistle that I tried not to count. He'd stopped eating much. His eyes hadn't stopped working. They were open and fixed forward, fixed upstream, with an intensity that made my neck prickle every time I glanced back at him. Forty years he'd carried whatever was up this valley. Now that we were almost there, he looked less like a dying man than a man being dragged toward something he'd sworn never to see again—and couldn't stop wanting.

"How far?" Sable asked, wiping her hands on a rag.

Elias didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice came out rusted. "Around the bend. You'll know it when you see it."

"You've said that about four bends now," she said gently.

"And I'll say it four more if the river makes me." But there was no humor in it. His knuckles were white around the blanket edge.

Juno rose from her gauge and came forward to stand beside me. Up close I could see the fatigue printed under her eyes in fine gray lines. She'd been running numbers all night—I'd heard her pencil scratching in the dark like a mouse in the walls.

"Petra." Her voice was low, pitched under the engine noise. "There's something wrong with this valley. The gradient's off. The whole drainage profile is wrong for the geology. It's like—" She stopped, chewed the inside of her cheek. "It's like the maps were drawn by someone who wanted them wrong."

"Or like the valley wants to stay lost," I said.

She gave me a look. Juno's looks could strip paint. "Valleys don't want things. People do." Then she went back to her gauge, and I pretended I hadn't heard what I'd heard, because pretending was becoming a professional skill of mine.

* * *

The bend came at midmorning, and it came the way Elias said it would—we knew it when we saw it.

The river swung hard left around a shoulder of red sandstone, and the current grabbed our hulls and slung us sideways, and for ten ugly seconds I was all hands and shouted corrections, Sable hauling the line, Juno reading the seams, Teodor braced against the crates with the coffee pot clutched to his chest like an infant. Then the boats came around, straightened, steadied—

And stopped. Not the boats. Us. Everything in us that kept moving.

The arch stood across the river like a sentence nobody had finished.

Limestone, bone-white and streaked with rust-red veins, rising out of the water on both sides and meeting overhead in a span so wide I couldn't take it in without turning my head. It must have been two hundred feet across, maybe more, and the river ran through it in a single braided ribbon, shallow and bright, glinting like spilled wire. Swallows nested in the high crooks of it; their calls came down to us thin and metallic, echoing off the stone. Beneath the arch the water darkened, deepened, gathered itself, as if even the river hesitated at the threshold.

"That's not on any map," Juno said. Her voice cracked on *map*. "That is not on any map I have ever seen."

"It's on mine," Elias whispered.

We drifted through beneath it. I don't think any of us breathed. The shadow of the arch slid over the boats like the cool palm of a hand laid flat on the water, and for a moment everything went dim and echoing and enormous, our voices bouncing back at us doubled and strange, and then we came out the other side into light—

Into the valley the maps erased.

* * *

I have chased storms across eleven states. I have stood close enough to a tornado to feel it pull the spit off my lips. I have watched a twister walk through a town and unmake it in ninety seconds, and I thought I knew what it meant for the land to be changed by violence.

I knew nothing.

The valley opened ahead of us like a held breath finally let go. Miles of it. The river widened as it entered, spreading slow and glassy between terraced banks, and the dry season had pulled the water down so far that the land beneath was surfacing—emerging inch by inch from forty years of drowning, like something rising in a dream you can't wake from fast enough.

Rooftops broke the waterline.

That was the first thing. Rooftops—dozens of them, black shingle and weathered tin and one church spire, standing up out of the shallows at angles, some collapsed inward, some intact, all of them furred with silt and algae so they looked less like buildings than like the backs of sleeping animals. Further in, where the water still lay deeper, we could see the ghost-shapes of streets beneath the surface: straight lines of drowned fence posts, the ridge of a main road, the corner of a brick wall showing through the murk like a tooth through gum.

A bell tower leaned out of the water near the center, tilted fifteen degrees, its bell still hanging. Still hanging. Forty years underwater and the bell hung on.

"God above," Teodor said quietly. He'd come up from the galley and stood with the coffee pot forgotten in his hands. In three countries' worth of flight, I thought, he must have seen towns die before—but not like this. Not preserved. Not *kept*.

Because that was the horror of it, or the wonder, and standing there at the bow I couldn't tell which. The flood hadn't destroyed Belltown. It had pickled it. The receding water was unveiling the place the way a museum unveils an exhibit—slowly, deliberately, everything exactly where it had been on the night the water came. A wagon sat in what had been a street, its bed full of river mud, its wheels still round. Laundry lines? No—not laundry lines. Wire fences, garden fences, running yard to yard, and here and there the pale shapes of chairs on porches that were themselves half-submerged, facing the water, facing each other, as if the whole town had been sitting down together when the end arrived.

Sable made a small sound. I turned. She was gripping the gunwale with both hands, fourteen years old and trying very hard to look like she saw drowned towns every day, and failing completely. "People lived here," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation aimed at the world.

"People lived here," I said.

"Where'd they go?"

Nobody answered. Elias answered, eventually, in a voice like ash settling. "Some downstream. Some not. The water came at night, in the spring, after the rains. There wasn't time for most of them to be anywhere but where they were."

Juno had her notebook out. I watched her write one line and then stop writing, pen hovering, because there was too much to record and recording felt like a kind of theft. She looked up at me, and her face had gone the color of the limestone. "Petra. This flood pattern—it's not natural. Look at the scour lines on the bluffs. The water came fast and it came *level*, all at once, like a reservoir let loose. Rivers don't do that. Dams do that. Or—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. I'd been carrying the shape of it since the hospice room, since Elias's rain-smelling silence, since the word *Rivergate* first landed in my lap along with a debt I couldn't pay. Somebody had drowned this valley on purpose. And somebody had spent forty years making sure the maps forgot it.

"Keep your notes," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "But keep them quiet."

Juno's eyes narrowed. I watched her decide—watched the question form behind them, *what aren't you telling us, Petra*—and then watched her fold it away, unasked, and nod once. That nod frightened me more than the Syndicate's hunters had. Trust freely given is a debt with no schedule. I was accumulating those faster than I could count.

* * *

We made camp that evening on a gravel shelf above the old ferry landing, where the water had fallen back far enough to expose the landing's pilings, black and barnacled, sticking up out of the mud like the fingers of a buried hand. Teodor built the fire small and cooked without being asked—river fish he'd caught at dusk, corn cakes, canned peaches warmed in their syrup because he said cold peaches were an insult to the dead. Nobody argued. Eating felt like a ceremony none of us had been trained for.

Elias wouldn't eat. He sat wrapped in his blankets facing the water, watching the last light go copper on the rooftops, and when I brought him a cup of coffee he took it in both hands like something warm was the only proof left that the world was real.

"I was here," he said. "Before."

I sat down beside him on the gravel. Behind us the crew moved quietly, pitching tarps, rigging lines, giving us the wide berth people give the dying and the confessing.

"You don't have to tell me anything," I said. "You paid for silence. I remember the terms."

He laughed—a dry click, barely a sound. "Terms. Yes. I bought myself a guide who keeps terms." He drank, grimaced at the chicory, drank again. "My brother had a claim here, Petra. A good one. Copper, maybe silver—the assays were real, I've seen them. Half this valley owned pieces of it. Farmers. Schoolteachers. The widow who ran the boarding house. Small people with small stakes, and then men came from downstream with ledgers and smiles, and the small stakes started changing hands. Paper debts. Foreclosures. And the ones who wouldn't sell—" His hand trembled; the coffee shivered. "The ones who wouldn't sell were drowned with the rest of it."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't break the oath I'd already sworn, and besides, he wasn't telling me. He was telling the firelight, the rooftops, the leaning bell tower. I was just the nearest thing with ears.

"They called it progress," he said. "They called it flood control. A levee project, upstream, official stamps on every page. And one night in April the levee failed—all at once, all along its length, in a season with no rain. Forty years I've known why. Forty years I've known who signed the orders and who got rich and which families downstream still eat off that money every single day." He turned and looked at me then, and his eyes in the firelight were wet and furious and terribly, terribly tired. "You're going to see it tomorrow. The records. The town kept records, Petra. Churches do. Banks did. The vault is under the courthouse foundation, and the courthouse is the tall building past the bell tower, and the water has fallen far enough now that you can reach it."

"Elias—"

"No." He raised a hand, weak as a bird's wing. "Don't promise me anything else. You've promised enough. Just—remember, when you're standing in it, that some truths are heavier than debts. Heavier than yours. Heavier than mine." He looked back at the water. "Mine's almost done weighing."

I stayed beside him until his breathing went long and whistling and even, until Teodor silently appeared with another blanket and draped it over the old man's shoulders with a cook's tenderness, until the fire burned down to coals and the drowned town disappeared into the dark except for the sound of the water moving among its streets.

Then I walked to the edge of the camp and stood alone looking out at the valley, and I let myself feel what I'd been holding off all day.

Awe. That was the honest name for it. Awe with a splinter in it. Because the same thing that made the breath catch in my throat—the vastness of it, the patience of it, a whole human world held underwater for forty years like a message waiting to be read—that same thing made me feel like a trespasser. Like a burglar in a tomb. Every rooftop breaking the waterline was a door somebody had expected to come home to. Every chair on every porch belonged to a name, and the names were down there in the vault, in ink, in triplicate, filed by men who believed the paper would outlast the crime.

It had outlasted the crime. That was the problem.

Somewhere behind me, Sable's tarp rustled; she was still awake, and I wondered how much of Elias's confession had carried on the still air. Somewhere downstream, days away, Doss Merrick was smiling at somebody over a contract. And somewhere under the brown water past the bell tower, a courthouse waited with its throat full of truth, and tomorrow I was going to have to decide whether to open it—or seal it, and lie to the only people on this river who'd ever trusted me.

The swallows had gone quiet. The arch stood black against the stars, holding up nothing, holding up everything.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow the valley tells us what it kept.

I just didn't yet know what I'd tell the crew.
