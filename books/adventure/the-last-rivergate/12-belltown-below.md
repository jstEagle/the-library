## 12. Belltown Below

The water gave the town back a foot at a time.

Juno stood at the rail of the *Wrenn's Mercy* and watched it happen—watched the receding river peel itself off the rooftops like a bandage drawn slowly from a wound. First the shingles, dark and slick as seal skin. Then the eaves, the gutter-iron, the weathervanes still turning in a wind that had no business reaching them down there. By midmorning a whole street of chimneys stood above the surface, and by noon she could see window glass, green with forty years of silt, catching the light and throwing it back in coins across the water.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

She had spent eleven years studying rivers—their chemistry, their tempers, their long bureaucratic memories written in sediment cores and flood-stage tables—and she thought she had stopped being capable of awe somewhere around the year her career died. She'd been wrong. Awe was still in her. It had just been waiting down here, drowned, preserved, patient as everything else in this valley.

"Belltown," Elias said from his blanket in the stern, and his voice was so thin it barely crossed the water. "That's what they called it. On account of the church."

Juno turned. The old man was propped against the gunwale with his map spread over his knees like a comforter he no longer needed, and his eyes were fixed on the rooftops with an expression she couldn't read—grief, or guilt, or something older than either.

"The bell's still up there," he said. "In the tower. They never got it out."

"Got it out of what?" Sable asked. She was crouched on the foredeck with a wrench in one hand and grease to her elbows, because Sable was constitutionally incapable of standing still, but even she had gone quiet.

Elias didn't answer. He closed his eyes instead, and Petra, who had been watching him with that careful look she wore whenever the old man said too much or too little, touched Juno's arm and nodded toward the shallowing shore.

"We'll make camp above the highwater line," Petra said. "Then we go down on foot while the level holds. Teodor stays with Elias."

"I stay with nobody," Teodor said, coming up from the galley wiping his hands. "I go where the town goes." He said it lightly, the way he said most things, but Juno had learned to read the second sentence underneath his first, and this one said: *I have seen towns die before. I want to see how this one did it.*

So they went down—all of them, even Elias, half-carried between Petra and Sable, wrapped in two blankets and insisting the whole way that he could walk fine, thank you, he'd walked these streets before they were streets under water.

* * *

The mud took them to the shins and held them like a slow animal deciding whether to swallow.

Juno went first with a surveyor's pole, reading the bottom the way she'd once read it for a living: gravel bar here, old channel scour there, the telltale fan of silt where a street drain had emptied into whatever passed for Belltown's waterfront. The town hadn't been built on the river. That was the first thing she understood, and understanding it made her stomach tighten. It had been built behind it—behind a levee, a long earthen spine that must have run the valley's northern edge, because she could see its ghost even now, a raised line of coarser ground running parallel to the shore, breached in three places like a throat cut three times.

"You're frowning," Sable said beside her. The kid moved through muck better than anyone Juno had ever seen, light-footed, weight distributed like she'd been born on a marsh. "You do your hydrologist face."

"This is my hydrologist face."

"No, your *worried* hydrologist face. There's a difference. I've been keeping notes."

Juno almost smiled. Almost. But her pole had come down on something flat and squared, and when she bent and worked her fingers into the silt around it, it came up as a door—a wooden door, painted a deep institutional green, still hanging in its frame, still shut, the brass knob bright beneath forty years of river.

It was lying in the middle of what had been a street.

"Doors don't float," Sable said quietly.

"No," Juno agreed. "They don't."

They walked the drowned avenue together, and the town assembled itself around them out of the murk the way a photograph develops. Porch posts. A picket fence, complete, leaning like a drunk against nothing. A child's tricycle, chrome still shining under the silt like it had been polished yesterday. A general store with its windows intact and its shelves visible through the glass, cans and jars ranked in neat rows, waiting for customers who had never come back. Everything was arranged. That was the thing that crawled under Juno's skin and stayed there. Floods scattered. Floods tore roofs off and stacked cars against churches and left trees growing out of second-story bedrooms. This water had risen gently, gently, gently—and then it had simply stayed, a lake wearing a town like a coat, until the dry season came along forty years later and started unbuttoning it.

Preserved. That was the word. She hated how accurate it was.

"Look at the waterline," she said, stopping at the corner of the store. She pointed with the pole at a stain on the brickwork, a tidemark running clean and horizontal, unbroken, around the building and away down the street as far as they could see. "One event. One rise. No drawdown cycle, no seasonal fluctuation—see how sharp the line is? The valley filled in hours. Maybe less." She heard her own voice go flat and clinical, which was what it did when she was frightened. "A river doesn't do that. Not naturally."

Sable looked at her. "Then what does?"

Behind them, Elias coughed, deep and wet, and Petra called for them to keep moving, keep him moving, the sun would be off the valley in two hours and the cold came up out of that water like something alive.

* * *

The church stood on the only high ground in the valley, a knoll of limestone that had stayed above the floodline except for its lower story, and even now, with the river shrunk to a third of its wet-season self, they reached it dry-shod across a causeway of cracked pavement. It was a plain building—white clapboard gone gray, a steeple like a lifted finger—but it had been built by people who expected it to last, and it had taken their expectation personally. The roof was whole. The doors were whole. Even the paint on the signboard beside the steps had survived, faded to a whisper:

**FIRST BELLTOWN CHURCH OF THE COVENANT — ALL ARE WELCOME — RECORDS OFFICE BELOW**

"Records office," Juno read aloud.

"Below," Sable read.

They looked at each other. Then, because neither of them was the kind of person who could walk past a sentence like that, they went around the side, where a separate stair led down past a foundation wall to a low door set into the earth itself—stone lintel, iron hinges, a lock plate green with age but, when Sable put her hands on it, sound as the day it was forged.

"Churches kept their vital records down in vaults like this," Juno said. Her voice echoed strangely off the stone. "Baptisms, marriages, deaths. Land transactions, sometimes. Deeds. In territories without courthouses, the church *was* the courthouse." She was lecturing. She knew she was lecturing. It was what she did instead of admitting that her heart had begun to hammer. "If Belltown ever existed officially—if anyone ever filed a claim, sold a parcel, registered a birth—"

"It's all down there," Sable finished. "Under a lock I can open in about ninety seconds."

"Sable."

"What? I'm saying I *could*. I'm not saying I will." The kid grinned, and then the grin faded, because Elias had come hobbling around the corner of the church with Petra hovering, and he was looking at the vault door with an expression that Juno would carry for the rest of her life.

He wasn't surprised. That was the terrible part. He looked at the door the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"Elias," Petra said carefully. "What is this place?"

"My church," he said. "When I was young. Before." He put a hand flat against the stone above the lintel, the way you'd touch a horse you were about to ask something terrible of. "My father helped build this vault. He said a town is only as honest as its paperwork." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh. "He was righter than he knew."

"Whose paperwork?" Juno asked. "Elias. Whose records?"

The old man slid down the foundation wall until he was sitting in the dead leaves, breathing hard, and for a moment Juno thought he wouldn't answer at all. Then he looked up at her—not at Petra, at her, at the disgraced hydrologist, and she would wonder later why he'd chosen her—and said:

"Everyone's. That's the point of a covenant church. You write it down or it never happened." His hand found hers and gripped it with a strength that startled her, that a dying man had no business having. "And girl—it all happened. Every bit of it. What they did to this valley, it's all written down, and it's all true, and that's why nobody has come back for it in forty years except men who wanted it to stay buried."

"Who's *they*?" Sable asked.

But Elias's grip was failing, and Petra was already kneeling, already easing him back, murmuring about rest and warmth and tomorrow, and the question hung in the cold air unanswered while the river muttered against the stones below them like a congregation that couldn't stop talking.

* * *

They made camp on the knoll that night, above the fog that rose off the shrinking water, and Juno lay awake in her bedroll listening to the town breathe below her.

That was the only word for it. The valley breathed. Water moved through drowned rooms and out again with each shift of the current, a slow tidal sigh, and every so often something shifted down there—a timber settling, a shelf giving way after four decades of patience—and the sound carried up through the fog like a house clearing its throat.

At some hour past midnight she gave up on sleep, took the lantern, and went down alone.

She told herself it was science. She told herself she just wanted to confirm the flood signature, take one core sample from the levee breach, establish the rise curve—that this was the work she'd been trained for, the work that had been taken from her, and here it was laid out in the largest, strangest field site of her career. All true. None of it the truth. The truth was that Elias had said *it's all written down*, and Juno Varga had built her entire ruined life on the conviction that things written down could not be entirely erased, and she needed—needed the way drowning needs air—to know if it was still there.

The vault door opened easier than it should have. The lock was old but the mechanism was simple, and Sable had shown her the trick with the tension wrench months ago on the *Mercy*, laughing, saying *everyone should know one illegal thing, Auntie Juno, it keeps you humble.* The hinges swung without a sound. Cold air came up out of the dark, mineral and clean, the smell of stone that had never learned about rot.

Her lantern showed her a room the size of a modest kitchen. Stone walls. Stone shelves. And on those shelves, row upon row, tin boxes—document cases, the kind law offices used, the kind churches used—each one labeled in faded copperplate ink. Each one sealed with wax.

*Belltown Covenant Registry, 1908–1919.*
*Parish Rolls, Complete.*
*Land Claims & Mineral Rights, North Valley.*
*Levee District Assessments.*
*Correspondence, Confidential — R. Vane, Trustee.*

She stood very still.

*R. Vane.*

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that shouldn't have existed underground, and Juno felt the cold come up through her boots, through her knees, into the place where she kept the last of her professional detachment, and she watched it go out like the flame.

She opened the box marked *Levee District Assessments* because her hands were shaking and shaking hands need a task. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job perfectly, were assessment rolls in a clerk's beautiful hand—property values, acreages, claim numbers—and clipped to the front page, a letter. She read it twice. Three times. The words refused to behave like words; they behaved like weights.

...the undersigned parties, being holders of majority interest in the North Valley claims, have determined that continued litigation regarding title cannot be resolved in our favor within any reasonable term, whereas the cost of certain works upon the river remains modest...

Certain works upon the river.

She climbed the stairs out of the vault on legs that belonged to someone else, and she stood in the moonlight on the knoll with the letter in her hand, and below her the fog rolled off the drowned town, and the chimneys stood up out of the water like mourners, and she understood at last why Elias had made Petra swear silence, why the Syndicate's cheerful men had smiled their way upriver behind them, why the maps had erased this valley as thoroughly as the water had covered it.

They hadn't flooded by accident. They had been murdered—every house, every claim, every name in those parish rolls—by men who then founded a lending house on the proceeds and called it enterprise. The Vane Syndicate hadn't been born in some countinghouse. It had been born here, in this valley, in the space of one gentle, monstrous night, and its founding documents were sitting in a tin box twenty feet below where Juno stood.

Footsteps on the pavement behind her. She didn't startle; some part of her had known she wouldn't be alone down here for long.

Petra came and stood beside her, arms crossed against the cold, looking down at the rooftops. For a long time neither of them spoke. Then Petra's eyes dropped to the paper in Juno's hand—to the wax seal she'd broken, to the copperplate she couldn't read at this distance but clearly recognized anyway—and Juno watched her captain's face do something complicated and final, like a lock turning.

"How much did you read?" Petra asked quietly.

"Enough," Juno said. "Petra—the founders. The names in that letter. They're *Vanes*."

Down in the fog, far off toward the southern bend, a lamp flickered to life on the black water—small, steady, deliberate—and began to move upstream toward them.
