## 13. The Choice in the Vault

The vault door was iron, and it had been waiting.

That was the thought that kept circling Petra as she stood in the flooded dark of the church basement with a lantern held high and water to her shins. Everything else down here had surrendered—pews rotted to lace, hymnals bloomed into soft gray coral, the plaster saints faceless and dripping. But the vault door had outlasted forty years of flood the way some men outlast every storm that should have killed them: stubbornly, and without apology.

Juno's hand was on the wheel. The girl—no, not a girl anymore, Juno was fifty-two and carried her disgrace like a stone sewn into her coat lining—worked the mechanism with the patience of someone who had spent a career being told she wasn't allowed to touch things. The dial clicked. Clicked again. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, they could hear Sable's voice calling measurements to Teodor, the two of them mapping the drowned town street by street, cataloguing doorframes and wellheads like archaeologists who hadn't been asked to be archaeologists.

"Last tumbler," Juno said. Her breath fogged in the cold air that seeped around the door. "You want to do the honors?"

Petra almost said no. Almost said *you found it, you open it*. But something in her chest—the same animal instinct that made her count lightning strikes between flash and thunder—told her that whatever came through this door belonged to her now. Elias had put it on her. Not in words. In the way he'd gripped her wrist at the last camp and said *some truths are heavier than debts*, his eyes already half-gone somewhere below the river.

"Open it," she said.

The wheel turned with a groan that traveled up through the water and into their bones. The seal broke with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go—a hiss of trapped air, forty years old, tasting of dust and iron and something faintly sweet, like paper left too long in a closed room. The door swung inward six inches and stopped against silt.

Inside, dry.

Not damp-dry. *Dry*-dry. The kind of dry that had no business existing at the bottom of a drowned valley, because whoever built this vault had built it to outlast exactly what had happened to Belltown. A lead-lined chamber set back into the limestone, sealed with wax and pitch, raised on a stone plinth above the flood line even though no one could have known where the flood line would be. Except someone had known. Someone had known the night the water came.

Petra lifted the lantern over the threshold.

Shelves. Rows of them, cut from cedar gone silver with age. Ledgers stacked spine-out, hundreds of them, their leather bindings cracked but whole. Tin document boxes with stenciled labels: *BELLTOWN MERCANTILE — ACCOUNTS, 1931–1962*. *RIVERGATE LEVEE DISTRICT — MINUTES*. *ST. BRIGID'S PARISH — BAPTISMAL & PROPERTY RECORDS*. And one box, smaller than the rest, set apart on its own shelf at eye level, unlabeled except for a single word burned into the tin:

*PROOF.*

"Petra." Juno's voice had changed. It had gone quiet in the specific way quiet goes when a hydrologist stops looking at water and starts looking at what people did to it. "Come look at this."

She'd pulled a ledger from the second shelf and opened it across her forearm, careful as a surgeon. The pages were ruled in faded green ink, columns of figures in a clerk's precise hand. But it was the marginalia that made Juno's jaw tighten—annotations in different ink, different years, a conversation conducted across decades in the margins of other people's money.

"This is the levee district's assessment ledger," Juno said. "See these entries? Coal shipments. Blasting powder. Detonation wire." She turned the page. "Forty tons of powder purchased by the Rivergate Levee District in the spring of '62. For *maintenance*." Her finger stopped under a line of figures. "And here—this entry, three weeks before the flood. Payment authorized to a contracting concern. You know what the company name is?"

Petra looked. The name was written in brown ink going black at the edges, and she knew it the way you know a scar on your own body, because she'd been reading it for two years on payment schedules and threat letters and the cheerful pink invoices Doss Merrick liked to leave tucked under her windshield wiper.

*Vane Consolidated Holdings.*

"They were the district's contractor," Petra said. Her voice came out flat, which was how her voice went when the bottom dropped out of something. "So what? They did levee work all over the valley."

"Levee work." Juno laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "Petra. I spent eleven years studying this watershed before the university took my name away. I know this valley's hydrology better than I know my own hands. There is no configuration of maintenance blasting that puts forty tons of powder into a levee. That's demolition tonnage. That's *bring-the-thing-down* tonnage." She turned another page, faster now. "And look at the assessment records from after the flood. Look at who filed claims on the drowned parcels."

Petra took the ledger from her. The lantern light trembled—not her hands; her hands never shook—but the flame itself, drinking air that hadn't moved in forty years.

The claims ran down the page like a guest list. Vane Consolidated Holdings. Vane Timber & Water. Merrick Brothers Transport. Name after name, parcel after parcel, the whole valley floor bought for pennies on the dollar within eighteen months of the night the water rose—and every purchase signed off by the same three men whose signatures Petra knew from the backs of her own debt instruments. Aldous Vane. Raymond Croft. And a third name she'd never seen before, a man named Hollis Wrenn.

Hollis Wrenn.

"Elias," she said aloud, before she understood why.

Juno looked at her. "What?"

"Nothing." But it wasn't nothing. Elias Wrenn. Hollis Wrenn. The dying prospector who traded a map for a promise, who wouldn't say what the valley held, who burned pages of his journal every night while Sable pretended to sleep. Brothers. Father and son. Something. Blood, anyway—the same blood that had signed away a town and then spent forty years trying to get back to it before the river gave it up entirely.

The lantern hissed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind them, patient as a clock.

Petra set the first ledger down and opened the small tin box marked PROOF.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were photographs. Black-and-white, curling at the corners. She lifted them into the light one at a time, and each one landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Men standing on the levee with blasting equipment. The same men, shaking hands in front of a survey office with a VANE CONSOLIDATED sign overhead. A telegram, pasted to cardstock: *NIGHT WORK APPROVED — AW — CONFIRM RECEIPT.* And the last photograph, the one she held longest: a row of houses along a street called Belltown Main, taken from the water, taken *after*, rooftops just breaking the surface, chimneys standing up out of the flood like the fingers of people reaching for a rope that never came.

Nobody had drowned in the flood. That was the thing Elias had never said and the photographs didn't need to—the town had been emptied first, bought out, threatened, run off, a community dissolved on paper before it was dissolved underwater. No bodies. Just theft so complete it wore the costume of an act of God. *Flood damage,* the insurance settlements had said. *Natural disaster.* And the men who turned the key on forty tons of powder had walked away owning the valley.

Until the river got low enough to give it back.

"Petra." Juno's voice again, very careful. "Do you understand what we're holding?"

She understood. That was the problem. She understood it all the way down, in the place where she did her arithmetic—the arithmetic that never stopped, that ran underneath everything like groundwater. Two hundred and fourteen thousand dollars owed to Vane Consolidated Holdings, compounding monthly, cheerfully, relentlessly. Eleven more hazard runs if the weather cooperated and she lived through them. Or—

Or this. A tin box full of dead men's signatures. Proof that could end the Syndicate with a phone call to any magistrate in the territory. Proof that could also be sold back to the Syndicate for more money than two hundred lifetimes of storm chasing would earn, because men like Aldous Vane would pay anything to make this room go back to sleep.

Both doors were open. Both led somewhere. And only one of them required breaking a promise to a dying man who had trusted her with his brother's—or father's—name.

"Juno," Petra said. "What else did you find down here? Tell me everything you saw. Every label. Every date."

Juno told her. The parish records. The town council minutes, including the final meeting, adjourned eleven days before the flood, with a note in the margin: *Mr. Vane assures the council the levee will hold.* The mercantile accounts showing buyout payments—small, insulting payments—to families who signed relocation agreements or else. Juno recited it all in her flat field-survey voice, and Petra listened, and the vault seemed to grow smaller around them, the air thicker, the lantern smaller, until it felt less like a room and more like a held breath.

Above them, boots crossed the church floor. Sable's voice, muffled through stone: "—so if the waterline keeps dropping we'll have the whole main street exposed by Thursday, Teo says the bakery's ovens are still standing—"

They sounded happy. That was the detail that undid her. Up there in the gray daylight, her ragtag crew of strays was laughing about bread ovens in a drowned town, and down here in the dark Petra Underhill was holding the only object in the world that could free them all or destroy them, depending on which way she turned it in her hands.

*Bring everyone home.* That was the oath. Elias had made her swear it in a hospice room that smelled of rain, and she had sworn it because she had nothing left to lose, and she had meant it, and meaning it had gotten easier every mile upstream as the strangers became a crew and the crew became something she didn't have a name for yet but recognized the way you recognize family—by the fact that losing them was unthinkable in a way debt never was.

But Elias had sworn her to a second thing. *Tell no one what the valley holds until I'm dead and gone.* And he wasn't dead yet. He was lying in the tent on the gravel bar with maybe a week left in him, burning journal pages, holding his secret like a coal in both hands.

If she told the crew—if she carried these ledgers up into the light and laid them on the camp table—they would do what honest people do with proof. They would argue. Juno would want justice, loud and immediate. Teodor would smell danger the way he smelled spoiled stock, and start packing. Sable—Sable would look at Petra with those eyes that had already been abandoned by every adult who ever promised her anything, and ask the only question that mattered: *are you telling me the truth?*

And Petra would have to answer while Elias was still alive to hear himself betrayed.

No. Not yet. Not like this.

"Seal it," she said.

Juno turned. "What?"

"We're putting it back. All of it. Exactly how we found it." Petra heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was, how reasonable, how much it sounded like Doss Merrick's cheerful reasonableness, and she hated that, and she kept talking anyway. "We reseal the door, we mark nothing, we tell the others we found flooded cellars and ruined records. Paper doesn't survive forty years underwater, Juno. Everyone knows that. It'll be believable."

"Believable." Juno stared at her. "Petra, there are men downstream right now with rifles who work for the people in these ledgers. This isn't just proof. This is *leverage*. We could walk out of this valley debt-free. All of us. You, me, Teodor, the kid—we could buy our lives back with one phone call."

"I know what it's worth."

"Do you?" Juno stepped closer, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the shelving. "Because I watched you do arithmetic on that gravel bar for two days straight. I've seen you calculate odds since Crooked Bend. Don't tell me you haven't already run the numbers on this."

Of course she had. She'd run them before Juno finished opening the second shelf. Sell it: freedom, wealth, the Syndicate paid off and the crew rich and safe and bought—which was just another word for owned, and hadn't the Syndicate taught her that lesson in monthly installments for two years? Expose it: justice, ruin, war—the Syndicate would burn the valley and everyone in it before they let a magistrate read page one, and Doss Merrick's smiling men were already upstream, already asking questions, already too close.

Or keep the promise. Hold the secret. Carry it out the way Elias intended, in his time, on his terms—and trust that a dying man's timing was better than hers.

"You don't understand what he asked me," Petra said. "Elias. He didn't ask me to sell it and he didn't ask me to publish it. He asked me to bring everyone home and keep silent until he's gone. That's the whole oath. That's all of it."

"And when he's gone?"

"Then it's mine to decide." She met Juno's eyes. "Not before."

Something passed over Juno's face—anger, yes, but underneath the anger, recognition. A woman who had lost her career for telling a truth nobody wanted to hear, being asked to bury the biggest truth of her life in a lead-lined box. Petra watched her wrestle with it and didn't blame her for the wrestling.

"If you're wrong," Juno said quietly, "if the Syndicate finds this room before we come back—"

"They won't. The river's dropping. By the time anyone else could reach this cellar, we'll either be gone or—" She stopped. She didn't finish the sentence, because the end of it was *or dead*, and saying it out loud in front of Juno would make it a plan instead of a fear.

They worked in silence. Juno replaced the ledgers in their exact order—she had a surveyor's memory for arrangement, and Petra was grateful for it. Petra rewrapped the photographs in their oilcloth, slid the tin box back onto its shelf at eye level, and stood a moment with her palm flat against the cool metal, the way you'd rest your hand on the flank of an animal you were asking to wait for you.

*Hold on,* she thought. *I'm coming back.*

Then she swung the iron door shut and spun the wheel until the tumblers caught, and the sound of the lock closing was final as a grave, and she felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders where it would live for the rest of the journey.

They waded out through the flooded nave, past the faceless saints, up the stairs into the gray afternoon. Sable was waiting at the top, perched on the church steps with her notebook, grease to her elbows, grinning.

"Well? What's down there?"

"Flooded cellars," Petra said. The lie came out smooth and warm and easy, and she hated how easy it was, hated that two years with the Syndicate had taught her this fluency, this cheerful fluency in the language of cheerful men. "Rotted pews, some parish registers gone to mush. Forty years of water gets into everything. Nothing worth carrying out."

Sable's grin flickered—just barely, just at the corner, like a candle in a draft—and Petra saw it, and told herself she'd imagined it, and knew she hadn't.

"Okay," Sable said, and wrote something in her notebook that Petra couldn't read upside down, and hopped off the steps to go tell Teodor the news.

Juno looked at Petra sideways. Said nothing. Walked toward the boats.

That evening the camp on the high bank above the town smelled of Teodor's stew and woodsmoke, and Elias lay propped in the tent watching the arch catch the last light, and Sable laughed at something Teodor said, and everything was warm and ordinary and fine.

And Petra sat apart from the fire, turning her wedding ring around her finger out of habit though there'd been no ring in nine years, doing the only arithmetic that mattered now: how many days until Elias died, how many miles of river between here and safety, and whether a secret this heavy could be carried through closing rapids by a woman who had just learned, in a lead-lined room beneath a drowned church, that loyalty and lying could wear each other's clothes so perfectly that not even she could always tell them apart.

Downriver, faint on the evening wind, she heard an engine.

More than one.

* * *

She was on her feet before the sound finished registering, scanning the black ribbon of the river. Two sets of engines, running slow, running quiet, the way men run engines when they don't want to be heard. She counted seconds between the sound and the bend, doing the math she was born doing.

"Sable," she said quietly. "Kill the fire."
