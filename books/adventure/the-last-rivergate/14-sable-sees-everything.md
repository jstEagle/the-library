## 14. Sable Sees Everything

Sable had learned early that adults lied best when they thought no one was watching. Her mother lied with her back turned, phone cupped to her ear, voice going soft and syrupy in a way it never did for Sable. The caseworkers lied with clipboards and kind eyes. The foster father in Dell County lied with a belt. So Sable watched. She watched the way other people breathed—watched without meaning to, the way some people couldn't stop tapping a foot—and by seventeen she could read a room the way Juno read water: current, undertow, the place where the surface said one thing and the depth said another.

Which was why she'd followed Petra down into the valley that morning instead of staying at camp like she'd been told.

Not because she didn't trust Petra. That was the strange part, the part Sable kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it. She *did* trust her—had trusted her since the gravel bar, since the night the river dropped out from under them and Petra stood hip-deep in the cold pushing the boat free while Teodor shouted encouragement from the shore and nobody slept. Something about a person who gets in the water first. You can't fake that. Sable knew fakes. She'd been raised on them.

But trust wasn't the same as blindness, and Sable had never once in her life been blind when it mattered.

So when Petra slipped away from the fire before dawn, moving quiet through the drowned town's streets with a headlamp hooded low, Sable counted to sixty and followed.

* * *

The fog off the receding water was thick as wool. Sable kept thirty feet back, barefoot in her river shoes, stepping where Petra stepped, learning the route off the backs of her heels. Down the flooded main street of Belltown, past rooftops breaking the surface like the spines of sleeping animals, past the church with its steeple leaning drunk against forty years of standing water. The whole town smelled like cold stone and old wood and something sweeter underneath—preserved things, paper and varnish and the ghost of somebody's Sunday dinner, all of it held in the floodwater's grip like flies in amber.

Petra went inside the church through a window someone had already pried. Sable waited three breaths, then followed the sound of her footsteps down into the undercroft, where the air changed—drier, older, tasting faintly of dust and iron.

Sable crouched on the stairs in the dark and watched through the banister rails.

The vault door was already open. Juno must have found it yesterday; Sable had seen the look on Juno's face when she came back to camp, that gray, scraped-out look, like she'd touched a live wire. Nobody had said anything over dinner. Sable had noticed that too. Four people eating beans and not talking is louder than four people shouting.

Inside the vault, Petra stood among shelves of ledgers and deed boxes and oilcloth bundles, her headlamp beam moving slow across the spines. She picked one up—a ledger bound in cracked black leather, swollen with damp but whole, the pages inside still legible, still sharp-edged after four decades underwater. Sable saw her open it. Saw her lips move as she read. Saw her face do something complicated: first the hunger of a woman reading the price of her own freedom, then something colder settle over it, like frost coming up a window.

Petra read for a long time. Then she took a photograph of a page with her battered phone—Sable saw the screen flash—and put the ledger back exactly where it had been, squaring the spine with two fingers like a librarian.

Then she closed the vault door and turned the wheel lock until it clanked home, and wrapped the hasp with wire, and twisted the wire with pliers until the twist was tight and ugly, and stood there a moment with her hand flat against the door like she was taking its pulse.

Then she prayed. Or something like it. Sable couldn't hear the words, just saw her shoulders move once, hard, the way a person's shoulders move when they're swallowing something too big.

Sable backed up the stairs without a sound. She'd gotten good at that, once. It used to be survival. Now it was just habit, and the habit had just handed her the biggest secret in the valley.

* * *

She didn't confront her right away. That was the discipline Sable had—the patience of someone who'd learned that catching an adult in a lie too fast only got you punished for snooping. You waited. You let them come to you, or you picked your ground.

Her ground was the evening, the far end of camp, the rock shelf above the water where Petra went to be alone with her maps. Sable climbed up carrying two cups of Teodor's coffee, which was terrible and perfect, burnt dark and sweetened with condensed milk he hoarded like treasure. An offering. Adults trusted you faster if you brought them something.

Petra was sitting with her knees up, chart case open, not really looking at it. The arch was visible downstream, a black stone bridge across the last gold light, and beyond it the valley going blue and shadowed. Somewhere below, the drowned town sat in its shrinking bath, keeping its secrets under wire.

"Hey," Sable said, and held out a cup.

Petra took it. "You're supposed to be inventorying the fuel."

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." Sable sat down close—not touching, but close enough that the silence between them had weight. She looked out at the water. "I followed you this morning."

She felt Petra go still. Not dramatically. Just a small cessation, the way a river goes flat over deep water. Petra didn't deny it. Didn't spin. She set the coffee down on the rock between them, deliberate, like putting a card face-up.

"How much did you see?"

"All of it." Sable kept her eyes on the valley. Easier to say hard things sideways. "The vault. The ledgers. The photo. The wire." A beat. "The part where you prayed."

Petra huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "That obvious?"

"You've got church shoulders."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Below them the river muttered over its stones, lower every day, the dry season eating it bite by bite. Somewhere across the water a loon called, that sound like a question being asked forever.

"So here's what I know," Sable said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Juno found records down there that made her look like she'd seen a ghost. You went down alone before dawn and sealed them behind wire. You took a picture of one page, which means whatever it says matters enough to keep and not enough—or too much—to show. And you told us at dinner we found nothing but mud and roof beams." She finally turned her head. "I'm not asking you to tell me everything. I'm asking you not to lie to me again. I can tell the difference now. I've had practice."

Petra looked at her for a long moment. In the last light her face was all planes and shadows, tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix.

"You want the truth or the truth-shaped thing?" Petra asked quietly.

"The real one. Even if it's a piece."

Petra nodded slowly. She picked up her coffee again, held it in both hands like something warm to hold onto.

"There are documents in that vault," she said. "Forty years old. They say who flooded this valley, and why, and they name names. Names you'd know if you'd grown up anywhere near a fuel pump in this territory." Her jaw worked. "The men who founded the Vane Syndicate drowned this town. On purpose. To bury claims and debts and people who were in their way. It's all written down in ink, in a church basement, and it's been sitting here waiting ever since."

Sable let that land. It landed heavy. She thought of Doss Merrick's easy smile, his cheerful men, the way kindness wore a uniform in that world.

"And Elias knows," Sable said. "That's why the map. That's why the promise."

Something flickered across Petra's face—there and gone, a fish under ice. "Elias made me swear to bring everyone home and keep silent about what's down here until he's gone. Both promises. At the same time. And those promises are currently trying to kill each other." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "That's the piece I can give you tonight. There's more, and I'm not giving it yet, and I need you to be okay with that."

"Why?" Sable asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking. "Why not give it? Why seal it? If those papers could burn the Syndicate down—"

"Because they could also buy us out of it." Petra's voice dropped. "Full forgiveness. Every debt. Mine, maybe yours someday if you ever sign anything stupid, which you will, because everybody does. One delivery, one handshake, and we're free." She stared at the water. "Or we take them to a magistrate and start a war we might not survive, with hunters upstream and the season dying and Teodor's shoulder torn up. Or we burn them and none of it ever happened and Belltown stays dead twice." She shook her head slowly. "I don't know yet. That's the honest answer. I sealed the vault because while I don't know, nobody else touches them. Including you. Especially including you, because you're seventeen and whatever happens, I am not letting this land on you."

Sable looked at her. Really looked. And here was the thing, the hinge, the moment she would think about for years afterward, turning it over in the dark of tents and trucks and long rivers: every adult who had ever held power over her had told her a version of the truth shaped exactly like what they wanted her to believe. Smooth. Complete. Comfortable. Lies with all the rough edges sanded off.

Petra had just handed her something rough. A piece. An admission of not-knowing. *I don't know yet.* Adults didn't say that. Adults said the confident thing and let you find out later it was hollow.

And Petra had said one more thing, almost under her breath, and Sable caught it because she caught everything:

*I'm not letting this land on you.*

Nobody had ever tried to keep a weight off her before. People added weights. That was what adults did—they handed you their messes and called it family until it wasn't convenient anymore.

"You took a picture," Sable said. "One page. Insurance?"

Petra's mouth twitched. "You see everything, huh?"

"It's my whole personality."

"If it goes wrong—if the Syndicate takes them, burns them, loses them—that page proves the vault exists and what's in it. Enough to send someone back. Enough that this valley can't be erased twice." She met Sable's eyes. "That's all I'll say about that."

"That's the part you're not telling Juno."

"Juno wants to fight. Teodor wants to survive. You—" Petra paused, and chose the word carefully, and Sable heard her choose it. "You deserve to grow up without this deciding who you are. So yeah. That part stays between us."

Sable turned her cup in her hands. The coffee had gone cold. Out on the water the last light was failing, and the arch was just a shape now, a doorway full of dusk.

Here was the arithmetic she ran, fast and silent, the way she fixed engines: This woman owed money to wolves. This woman was lying to her crew, or mostly lying, or lying by omission, which was the same animal wearing a different coat. This woman had sealed a vault full of freedom and wouldn't say why. Every indicator Sable had ever used to spot a liar was lit up like a dash panel.

And underneath all of it, steady as a groundwire: *she got in the water first. She pushed the boat free while we stood on the bank. She's carrying this alone so it doesn't land on us.*

Trust wasn't believing someone was telling you everything. Trust was deciding, on incomplete data, that they were worth betting on anyway. It was a repair job done without the manual. You couldn't wait for certainty; certainty was for people who'd never been abandoned, and Sable had stopped waiting for that around age nine.

"Okay," Sable said.

Petra blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. I won't push. I won't tell Juno or Teodor what I saw. But two conditions." She held up fingers. "One: when you decide—you, specifically, not the ledgers, not Doss, not Elias's ghost—I get told first. Before the crew. Before anybody. Because I saw the vault, so I carry it now too, and carriers get told first. That's just how ballast works."

A ghost of a smile crossed Petra's face. "Ballast works. Fine. Condition two?"

"Two: you don't decide alone. Whatever you're carrying, you carry it near me. Not on me—near me. I'm not asking to steer. I'm asking to be in the boat when you do."

Petra was quiet for a while. The loon called again, farther off now, the question drifting downstream. When she answered, her voice was rough at the edges.

"When I was your age I made deals with people like the Vanes because I thought I had no other move. I promised myself I'd never make anybody else feel that cornered." She looked at Sable sideways. "You drive a harder bargain than Doss Merrick does, you know that?"

"He's got worse coffee."

"He's got *no* coffee." Petra picked up her cup and tapped it against Sable's, a small ceramic click in the big dark. "Deal."

They sat there as the stars came out over the arch, two silhouettes on a rock shelf, holding cold coffee and a secret apiece. Sable felt the strange new weight of it settle in her chest—not lighter, exactly. Chosen. There was a difference between a burden dropped on you and one you picked up on purpose. She'd spent her whole life with the first kind. This was the first time anyone had ever asked her to take the second, and then stepped back and left the choice actually hers.

That was the thing she decided, there in the dark, deliberately, with her eyes open: *I trust her. Not because she's earned it completely. Because she's the first adult who left the choice to me.*

Faith, it turned out, wasn't a feeling. It was a decision you made and then kept making, morning after morning, like bailing a boat.

* * *

They came down the rock shelf together, and the campfire had been built up high, Teodor cooking something with onions in it because onions meant good news or bad news, he never said which. Juno looked up from her charts with that sharp hydrologist's gaze, and Sable watched her watch them come down the slope together, watched her clock the closeness, the shared silence, the way Petra's hand brushed Sable's shoulder once at the bottom like a sentence being finished.

Juno's eyes narrowed, just slightly. She glanced toward the dark line of the valley. Then at Sable. Then at Petra.

Sable held her gaze, calm, and gave her nothing. *Not yet,* she thought. *Whatever you're building in that head, not yet.*

Petra announced an early start, said the river gauge numbers worried her, said they'd want the cold hours for the shallows. All true, probably. All reasonable. And underneath it, Sable heard the other frequency now, the one she'd have missed a week ago: Petra was counting days. Counting water. Counting how long she could hold a sealed vault and a crew full of questions in the same boat.

Later, in the tent she shared with Sable, Petra lay awake a long time. Sable pretended to sleep, the way she had in a hundred houses, listening to an adult breathe through a problem. Near midnight Petra murmured something into the dark—half prayer, half plan, words Sable filed away without meaning to:

*"Two weeks of water. Maybe less. Then it's now or never."*

Sable lay still, heart ticking, and understood that whatever Petra decided, it was coming soon. And out in the blackness, far downstream, she imagined she could almost hear it—an engine note on the wind, low and patient, climbing the river toward them through the dark.

Doss was coming. Sable had known it since the fuel cache, since the smiles that asked questions. Now she knew Petra knew it too.

She closed her eyes and held onto her decision like a rail in rough water.

*Whatever comes up that river,* she thought, *we go in the boat together.*
