## 15. Doss Comes Upstream

Petra heard the engines before she saw them—two of them, running rough and rich, the sound of motors that had never once been asked to be quiet. She was on the gravel bar below camp, rinsing coffee grit from a pot in the shallows, and the water told her first: a long shiver moving upstream against the current's grain, wrong in every way a river could be wrong.

She set the pot down slowly.

The morning had been the kind the dry season made—high haze, heat standing on the water like a man waiting to be introduced, the river so low it had begun to whisper over its own bones. Upstream, past the last bend, the air shimmered. And through the shimmer came the boats: two aluminum-hulled skiffs with outboard cages and gunwales scuffed silver from hard use, riding high and empty-looking, which meant they were carrying exactly what they wanted her to think they weren't.

Doss Merrick stood in the bow of the lead boat with his hands folded in front of him like a man arriving at church.

She had known this moment was coming since Crooked Bend. She had rehearsed it in the dark hours between watches, lying in her bedroll listening to Sable breathe and Teodor mutter recipes in three languages, turning the conversation over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath. But rehearsal was a poor substitute for the actual weight of Doss Merrick's smile when it swung toward her across forty yards of dying river.

"Miss Underhill." His voice carried the way voices carry over water, flattened and intimate at once. "You're looking well. The river agrees with you."

"Doss." She didn't wave. Waving invited approach, and approach was a currency she wasn't ready to spend. "That's a long way from anywhere, for two boats."

"It is." He let the skiffs drift the last stretch, then killed the engines himself—a courtesy, or a performance of one—and they slid up onto the gravel with a grinding hiss that set Petra's teeth on edge. Four men besides Doss. She knew two of them by sight from the fuel cache at Miller's Eddy: the big one with the sunburned neck whose name she'd never learned, and the thin one who smiled too much and was called Prewitt as though it were a joke everyone else was in on. The other two were new, younger, watchful. They stepped ashore without being told, spreading along the bar with the loose, unhurried geometry of men arranging themselves around something.

One of them sat down on a rock and began, casually, to check his rifle. Not brandish it. Check it. Breech open, bore glance, magazine seated, breech closed—the whole liturgy performed with the absent devotion of a man going through his rosary. Then he set it across his knees and looked out at the river, admiring the view.

Petra kept her hands visible and her voice level. "We're low on everything. If you've come to trade, I can offer coffee grounds and an argument."

"I've come to offer you something considerably better than that." Doss walked toward her alone, boots crunching, and stopped just inside conversational distance—the distance at which you don't have to raise your voice, the distance at which everything said becomes a secret whether you want it to or not. He was a broad, comfortable-looking man, gray at the temples, with the tan of someone who spent his days outdoors doing nothing that would show on him later. He smelled of cedar shaving soap and engine oil. He had once shaken her hand at a Syndicate function in Harrow Ford and told her she had the best hazard instincts he'd ever financed, and she had gone home and hated herself for being flattered.

"Then say it," she said, "and go back downstream before my crew wakes up cranky."

"They know I'm here?" A flicker of genuine interest. That was the thing about Doss—he listened. Most dangerous men talked; Doss collected.

"They know engines when they hear them."

"Mm." He turned half toward the water, giving her his profile, which was somehow more disarming than his face. "I'll be honest with you, Petra, because I think we've earned each other's honesty. We know where you are because the river tells us. Low water narrows a man's options. There are only so many channels deep enough to float a loaded expedition, and you're in all of them eventually. It's not surveillance. It's arithmetic."

"And the arithmetic says?"

"The arithmetic says you're close." He paused, letting the word sit there, fat and patient. "Closer than anyone's been in forty years. Closer than the survey barges got before the season beat them back. And Elias Wrenn—" he said the name gently, the way you'd handle a borrowed tool, "—is a man who has spent four decades telling everyone who'd listen that the Rivergate is a fairy story, while quietly buying up every scrap of paper that ever mentioned it. Deeds. Claims. Court filings. A whole drawer full of Belltown, if you'll forgive me using a word I have no business knowing."

Petra felt the ground tilt under her, very slightly, the way it did after a lightning strike—not the world changing, just her understanding of it. *Belltown.* He knew the name. Which meant the Syndicate hadn't been guessing. Which meant they had always known, and the question had never been *where* but *whether* anyone would live long enough to come back with proof.

"You've been reading my mail," she said.

"I've been reading *Elias's* mail. Yours we couldn't afford—you'd have spent anything we paid you on truck parts." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners, and behind him the young man with the rifle turned a round over in his fingers and let it drop back into the magazine with a small brass click that carried across the water like a knuckle rapping on glass.

* * *

They walked while they talked. Doss suggested it himself—"stretch our legs, no sense standing in the sun"—and Petra understood it for what it was: moving her away from the camp, away from witnesses, away from the possibility that Juno or Sable might come down to the water and hear something that would require killing them. So she walked, matching his pace along the gravel margin, keeping the river on her left and her eyes on the middle distance, cataloguing everything. The two new men had drifted to either end of the bar. The sunburned one stayed with the boats. Prewitt had produced binoculars and was studying the cutbank above her camp with the idle thoroughness of a man pricing real estate.

"Here's the shape of it," Doss said. "The Syndicate forgives your debt. All of it. Every dollar, every point of interest, every creative fee we tacked on when you weren't looking—which, between us, was most of it. Zero balance. Clean slate. Your truck comes off the lien, your name comes out of the book, and you never see another cheerful man in a nice shirt again unless you invite him to dinner." He spread his hands, presenting the offer like a dish. "In exchange, whatever Wrenn's expedition turns up comes to us. Intact. Unread, ideally—I'll be candid, paper that old is fragile—but intact. You hand it to me, I hand you a receipt marked *paid in full*, and everybody goes home lighter."

"That's it," Petra said. "No finder's fee. No bonus."

"Forgiveness *is* the bonus, Petra. Ask anyone in our book." He laughed softly at his own joke, and she watched the laugh fail to reach any part of him that mattered. "Though I take your point. You're a negotiator. Fine. Ten percent of appraised value on top, payable after authentication. There. Now it's a deal worth signing."

She made herself walk ten more steps before answering, because the terrible truth—the thing she would hate herself for at two in the morning, and did already hate herself for in the daylight—was that some animal part of her brain had done the math instantly and liked the answer. Zero balance. Her name out of the book. No more runs across the flats chasing storms that paid less than the diesel cost, no more envelope under the door with numbers in it that grew while she slept. Forty seconds of conversation and the animal part of her was already reaching for it with both hands.

And underneath the animal, colder and older, was the memory of Elias's hand on hers in the hospice room, light as a bird, making her swear. *Bring them home. Tell no one.* Two things. He had asked her for two things, a dying man with nothing left to give but a map, and she had promised, and promises were the only currency she owned that the Vane Syndicate couldn't print.

"Say the number," she said.

Doss's eyebrows rose, pleased. "Which number?"

"My debt. Say it out loud. I want to hear what my freedom costs."

He considered her for a moment, and something in his face shifted—not softer, exactly. More honest. It was worse. "Eleven thousand, four hundred and six dollars," he said. "Plus the accrued penalties since March, call it thirteen even. Thirteen thousand dollars, Petra. That's what you've been drowning in. That's what a drowned town is worth to us." He shook his head, almost sadly. "You want to know the funny part? The founders would've settled this whole valley for less than that, back in the day. Men have always been cheap about other people's towns."

* * *

The wind came up the river then, hot and dry, pushing a lacework of dust across the bar, and Petra used the interruption to look at the sky instead of Doss's face. High cirrus, streaked east to west like combed wool. Nothing building. The dry season held, and would hold, and every day it held the river dropped another finger's width, and every day it dropped, the Syndicate's arithmetic got better and hers got worse. They could wait. Waiting was free for men who owned the book.

"Elias is dying," she said.

"I know."

"He could die tomorrow. Or tonight. What happens to your offer then?"

"Nothing happens to it. The offer isn't attached to Elias. It's attached to the paper." Doss stopped walking and turned to face her fully, and for the first time he took his hands out of their polite fold and let them hang at his sides, and that small change frightened her more than the rifle had. "But I'll tell you what does happen, Petra, because you've earned straight talk and I'd rather give it now than have you learn it ugly. If that paper reaches Harrow Ford—if it reaches a magistrate, a newspaper, a courthouse steps, a *campfire gossip*—then the debt stops being a number. Debts are business. Business is civilized. What comes after business is not, and I won't be the one administering it, because that's not my department, but I will be the one who has to tell you it's coming, and I'd genuinely rather not. I like you. You're the best guide this outfit ever rented, and I mean that professionally and personally both."

Behind him, unhurried, the young man with the rifle worked the action one more time. Open. Shut. The sound rolled down the gravel like a stone skipping.

"So," Petra said. Her mouth had gone dry, and she made herself speak anyway, slowly, tasting each word before she spent it. "Delivery by season's end. Whatever we find, brought to you intact, and the book closes on me."

"Season's end," Doss agreed warmly. "First week of October. Harrow Ford, or the fuel cache at Miller's Eddy, whichever suits your route. I'll bring the receipt myself. I'll even bring a pen you'll like—it's a good pen, heavy, writes wet."

"One condition." She held up a finger, and was distantly amazed at how steady it was. "Your boats stay downstream of the bend until then. No shadowing. No survey barges. No cheerful men checking rifles on my gravel. My crew works scared enough as it is, and scared crews make mistakes, and mistakes sink boats, and sunk boats lose your paper. You want delivery? Give me room to work."

Something moved behind Doss's eyes—calculation, quick and total, the kind she imagined went on inside slot machines. Room to work meant time. Time meant risk. But pressure meant mistakes too, and Doss Merrick had built a career on knowing exactly how much rope a person needed to feel free.

"Done," he said. "Downstream of the bend. You have my word."

His word. She thought of the levee, forty years gone, and the town behind it, and the men whose signatures were probably drying somewhere in a vault she hadn't seen yet, and she thought: *his word is the cheapest thing on this river.*

"Then we understand each other," she said, and put out her hand.

He took it. His grip was dry and warm and perfectly calibrated, firm enough to respect, gentle enough to reassure, and she hated how easily her own hand fit into it, hated the muscle memory of trust, hated herself for the small bright flare of relief she felt when he said, "Thirteen thousand dollars, Petra. One envelope of old paper. Best trade you'll ever make," because some traitorous part of her believed him, wanted to believe him, the way a drowning woman believes in the surface.

* * *

They pulled out twenty minutes later. The engines caught on the second try, coughing blue smoke, and the skiffs backed off the gravel and swung downstream in a wide, courteous arc, Doss lifting two fingers off his cap in a little salute as the bend took them. The sound faded. The river healed over behind them, closing its skin, and within a minute the water ran smooth and low and innocent again, whispering over its bars as though it had never carried anything heavier than rain.

Petra stood on the bank until the last echo died.

Then she bent, picked up the coffee pot she'd abandoned, and found her hands shaking—not with fear, she realized, but with disgust, a deep chemical revulsion aimed entirely inward. Because the lie had formed itself in her mouth without effort, without friction, sliding out smooth and shaped and ready: *delivery by season's end.* She had promised it with a handshake and a steady voice and she had never meant a word of it, and the truly damning part, the part that would keep her up tonight staring at the stars through the mosquito netting, was that it hadn't even been hard. Lying to Doss Merrick had felt like breathing. Like a skill she'd had all along and simply never needed.

*This is what he does,* she thought, watching the empty river. *This is what they all do. They make the lie feel like the honest option.*

Up at camp, a pan clattered. Teodor's voice floated down the cutbank, calling something about eggs, about eating while they were hot, and Sable answered, and Juno's laugh followed, brief and unguarded, and the ordinary sounds of her strange little family went on above her, unaware, trusting the river to keep its secrets.

Petra climbed the bank toward the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions, composing her report as she went—*fuel runners, headed downstream, nothing to worry about*—and hating, with a clean and growing fury, how easily that lie formed too.

Behind her, far downstream, faint as a heartbeat, she could still hear the engines. They weren't leaving.

They were idling. Just below the bend. Exactly where his word had put them.
