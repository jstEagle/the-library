## 16. Mutiny at the Fire

The fire was Teodor's doing, and he regretted it before the first log caught.

He had built it too big. That was the truth of it, the small domestic failure that would come to feel like an omen. They were camped on a shelf of stone above the river, in the lee of a fallen cottonwood, and the night had come down cold and wet off the water, and his hands had wanted warmth, so his hands had built a fire the size of a funeral pyre. He told himself it was for morale. He told himself a cook was allowed one extravagance per expedition, and this was his: real flame, real smoke, the smell of green wood burning instead of the stingy blue whisper of their camp stove.

But standing back from it, wiping his palms on his apron—he still wore the apron, out here, because a man could lose everything except his habits—Teodor looked at the fire and thought: *this is a fire you build when something is ending.*

He had seen fires like this before. In Varna, the winter the docks burned. In Trieste, on the quay, the night the smugglers celebrated a run that half of them didn't survive. Big fires meant big feelings, and big feelings meant somebody was about to say the thing that couldn't be taken back.

He ladled out the stew anyway. That was his answer to most of life's unanswerable questions: feed people first. It was what his mother had done through two wars and one divorce, what her mother had done through worse. You could not shout over a full mouth. You could not storm away from a bowl you hadn't finished. Food bought time, and time was the only currency Teodor had ever learned to handle well.

The stew was venison and roots and the last of the dried mushrooms Sable had found under the portage rocks, and it was good, and nobody said it was good, which was how he knew the evening was going to go badly.

Petra ate sitting apart. Not far—ten feet, maybe twelve—but apart, with her back against the cottonwood's root ball and her bowl balanced on her knees, watching the dark line of the river the way she'd been watching it all day. She'd been watching it since they came down out of the valley. Since Belltown Below. Since whatever it was she and Juno and Sable had found down there among those drowned rooftops that none of them would talk about.

Juno ate fast, the way she did everything, her spoon moving with the mechanical efficiency of a woman refueling a machine. She kept glancing at Petra. Then at the tree line. Then at the canvas duffel that had ridden in the center of the second boat all day, wrapped in oilcloth, guarded by silence.

That duffel. Teodor had carried it up the portage himself, one-armed, shoulder screaming, and even then—even with his rotator cuff singing hymns of agony—he had noticed the weight of it wasn't right for survey equipment. Survey equipment was awkward but honest. This was dense. This was *archived*. This was paper, reams of it, bound and boxed and heavy as sin.

Sable sat closest to the fire, knees drawn up, chin on them, her thin face lit orange from below. She was seventeen and she had eyes like a customs officer. She watched everyone the way she watched engines: listening for the knock, the rattle, the sound a thing makes just before it fails.

Elias lay in his tent, breathing in the long rattling rhythm that had become the soundtrack of their nights. In and in and in, then a pause long enough to pray in, then out. They had all stopped remarking on it. That was its own kind of knowledge—the kind you don't speak because speaking might make it true faster.

Teodor collected bowls. He always collected bowls. It gave him something to do with his hands while the air pressure changed.

It was Juno who broke.

She set her bowl down on the stone—not handed it to him, *set* it, with a click of ceramic on rock that cut through the fire-crackle like a pistol shot—and said, "No. I'm done. I want to know what's in the bag."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, a night bird said one syllable and thought better of it.

"We've been running your errand for three weeks," Juno went on, and her voice had that flat, precise quality it got when she was frightened, the hydrologist's voice, the voice she used for data. "We've hauled boats over a mountain because of this errand. We've been shot at—or nearly; don't tell me those men at the fuel cache were fishermen, I have a degree, I know what a rifle sling looks like. We are camped four miles downstream of a town that is not on any map printed in the last forty years, and yesterday we went into it, and today nobody will say its name." She turned to Petra. "So. What's in the bag."

Petra didn't move. The firelight moved on her instead, sliding along the planes of her face, and Teodor, who had spent a lifetime reading faces across kitchen counters and border checkpoints, saw her do the arithmetic. He saw her arrive at the number she didn't like.

"Sable," Petra said quietly. "You want to tell them? Since apparently you already have."

Sable didn't flinch. "I didn't tell them anything. I said there was a vault. I said it was sealed. That's not telling, that's observing."

"You're seventeen."

"I'm the one who carried the boxes up the hill," Sable said, "while you stood in the doorway looking like a woman deciding whether to drown a litter of kittens. Somebody had to carry something besides the secret."

*There it is*, Teodor thought, and reached for the coffee pot because his hands needed work. *There is the crack in the hull.*

* * *

He poured coffee into tin cups and distributed them the way you distribute things in a minefield: carefully, without sudden movements. Juno took hers without looking at him. Petra took hers and held it in both hands and did not drink.

"Fine," Petra said.

The word came out small and enormous at once, the way words do when they've been held too long.

"Fine. You want to know. Here's what I know, and I'll thank you to remember as I say it that I know more than I'm saying, and the part I'm not saying isn't mine to give." She looked around the fire, at each of them, one by one, and Teodor felt the look land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "Elias hired me to guide him upriver to a place called the Last Rivergate. Not to fish it. Not to chart it. To find it. He paid me partly in money I desperately need and partly by forgiving nothing, because the debt forgiveness came from the Syndicate, and if you want to know why Doss Merrick smiles like a man handing out candy at a parade, it's because he thinks he sent us where he wants us to go."

"The Syndicate knows about the valley?" Juno said.

"The Syndicate's founders *made* the valley," Petra said. "Forty years ago. There was a town up there—Belltown, they called it, though I doubt anyone called it that politely. Farms, a church, a school, claims registered and recorded, everything a town needs to prove it existed. And one night in the spring of—" she paused, and Teodor watched her reach for a fact and find Elias's voice instead, "—one night, the levee upstream failed. Catastrophically. The whole valley flooded between midnight and dawn. Forty-one people got out. Eleven didn't. And every record of who owned what, who'd filed what claim, who'd been swindled by whom—it all went under the water and stayed there."

"And the Syndicate," Juno said slowly, "dynamited the levee."

"I'm telling you what's in the ledgers," Petra said. "Which is to say: I'm telling you what I read in about ninety seconds before I ordered the vault sealed. Yes. The names that founded the Vane Syndicate are in those books, and what they did is in those books, and the books survived because the flood that killed the town also buried it deep enough and cold enough and silted enough to preserve it. Paper lasts underwater if the water keeps its promises. Juno, you know this better than anyone."

Juno's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth. Teodor watched her set it down with exaggerated care, the way you put down a knife before an argument.

"That's why my name was mud," she said. "That's why no university will touch my grant applications. The flood studies. My dissertation was on the Kestrel Valley inundation event. I called it an engineering failure. I called it *silt load and structural fatigue*. I built my whole career on the official story, and the official story has forty years of very expensive lawyers behind it."

"Now you know why Elias picked you," Petra said. "All of you. None of this crew is an accident."

The fire shifted. A log collapsed inward and threw up a fistful of sparks, and for a moment everyone's faces were fully lit, and Teodor thought: *remember this. Whatever happens next, remember their faces exactly like this.*

"So the ledgers," Juno said. Her voice had changed. Teodor had heard that change before too—in committee rooms, in funding meetings, in the voices of people who had just realized the world owed them something. "The ledgers are proof. Documented, dated, witnessed proof that the men who own the boats chasing us committed mass murder and fraud and then drowned the evidence. We are holding the only copy of the only account that can destroy them." She laughed, one short note with no humor in it at all. "Do you understand what that's worth? Not abstractly. *Literally.* Doss offered you debt forgiveness for it. Debt forgiveness! Petra, they'll pay seven figures for those books. Seven figures each, maybe. We could all walk away. We could buy boats. Real ones. We could—"

"Burn them."

Everyone turned. Teodor was surprised to discover the words had come out of his own mouth, in his own voice, in English, without passing through the usual committee of caution he kept between his heart and his tongue.

He set down the coffee pot. His shoulder throbbed in time with his pulse, and maybe that was why the words kept coming, plain and hot, the way pain talks.

"You burn them," he said. "You burn them tonight, in this fire, and we paddle out tomorrow and we never speak of Belltown again."

"Teodor—" Petra started.

"No, let me—please. Let me say it, because I am the oldest person at this fire and I have earned the right to be the fool." He stood, and his bad shoulder made him stand crooked, and he let it. "I have watched men trade other men's deaths for money my entire life. In Varna. In Trieste. In the camps outside Patras where I cooked for people who had lost countries. Every single one of those transactions began exactly like this one. Someone says: this is worth a fortune. Someone else says: but think of the good we could do with a fortune. And the dead stay dead either way, but the living learn the lesson. The living learn that everything has a price, and after that, they never stop selling." He pointed at the black water below the shelf, toward the valley upstream, toward the eleven who hadn't gotten out. "Those people in that town were killed so that four men could get richer. If we sell the proof, we make ourselves the next link in that chain. The Syndicate drowns a town; we sell the receipts. No. Burn them. Burn them and starve honestly."

Silence, except the fire.

Then Juno, softly, terribly reasonable: "And the next town? Because there will be a next town, Teodor. Men like that don't stop. They'll drown another Belltown, and another after that, and there won't be a magic vault under this one. Those ledgers aren't just history. They're a weapon pointed at the future. Burning them doesn't protect anyone. It disarms everyone."

"They're not yours to spend," Teodor said. "They belong to the dead."

"The dead don't need money," Juno said. "The living do. I've been living dead for six years because of these people. Don't you dare tell me my redemption isn't worth—"

"It's not redemption if you invoice it!"

The words cracked out of him louder than he intended, and the echo came back off the far bank, and somewhere in the dark Elias's breathing hitched and steadied, and everyone glanced at the tent, and the glance was the only thing they all agreed on.

* * *

It was Sable who ended it, or redirected it, which with Sable amounted to the same thing.

"Petra," she said. Just the name. She said it the way she'd say *hey, listen to this engine.*

Petra looked at her.

"What did Elias actually ask you for?"

And Teodor saw it happen—the flinch, small as a heartbeat, the truth trying to surface behind Petra's eyes and being pushed under by a promise. He had spent three weeks learning this crew's tells. Juno's tell was precision. Sable's tell was stillness. Petra's tell was that she answered questions slightly faster than she needed to.

"Not mine to give," Petra said. Too fast.

"Okay." Sable nodded slowly, and Teodor understood that she was filing the lie away, not swallowing it. Seventeen years old and abandoned by every adult who'd ever held authority over her, and she had developed the specific genius of knowing which lies to accept and which ones to keep. "Then here's what I think. I think we're arguing about the wrong question. Juno wants to sell them. Teo wants to burn them. Both of you are talking about the ledgers like they're ours." She looked around the fire. "They're not ours. They're the town's. And Elias—the man who's dying ten feet away from us—spent forty years finding them, and he gave them to *her*." She jerked her chin at Petra. "Not to a vote. To her. So the question isn't sell or burn. The question is: does this crew trust her or not? Because if we trust her, we shut up and follow her judgment, whatever it turns out to be. And if we don't trust her, we should say that now, tonight, out loud, because I have been on crews—well. I've been on *families*—where nobody said it out loud, and it rotted everybody from the inside."

The fire crackled. Nobody spoke.

Teodor found that his hands had gone still, which almost never happened. He looked at Petra—at this hard, broke, brave woman who had fed him when he arrived at Crooked Bend with two bags and a forged reference, who had carried the portage rope across her own shoulders when his gave out, who lied beautifully to armed men and badly to children—and he made his choice the way he made all his important choices: not with his head, which had betrayed him in three languages, but with the older instrument, the one that smelled rain coming.

"I trust her," he said. "Even when she lies to me. Especially then, maybe, because I know what it costs her." He sat back down. "I still think they should burn. But I'll carry them wherever she says, and I'll cook for whoever she defends, and that's my vote, such as it is."

Juno stared into the fire for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and level again, the data voice, and somehow that was worse than the shouting.

"I trust her too," Juno said. "That's what frightens me. I've watched trust be used as a tool my whole career. But fine. Fine. I'll hold my position and I'll row your boat and I won't touch the bag. But Petra—if we get out of this, if we live, you owe this crew the whole truth. Every clause. Even the ones that aren't yours." She picked up her cold coffee and drank it like medicine. "Because secrets kept *for* people curdle into secrets kept *from* them. I've seen it. I've *been* it."

Petra looked at the three of them—at the family assembled against her will and against all odds—and something moved through her face that Teodor couldn't name, some weather front passing over high ground.

"Get some sleep," she said. "First light, we're breaking camp. Doss's boats are a day behind us, maybe less, and the river's dropping."

They scattered to their bedrolls. Teodor stayed by the fire to bank it, as he always did, feeding it down from blaze to ember, from statement to memory. As he worked, he heard Sable's low voice at Petra's tent, and Petra's answer, too quiet to catch, and Sable's silence afterward, which was louder than anything Juno had shouted.

He pressed the last coals together and covered them with ash, and the fire went from orange to red to a dull pulsing glow, like something alive sleeping under a blanket.

Above the shelf, the stars were coming out hard and cold over the valley that maps had erased. Downriver, faint as a rumor, he thought he heard an engine—once, twice, then nothing, maybe wind, maybe not.

Teodor stood a moment longer with his ruined shoulder and his empty pot, and then he went to check on Elias, because someone had to, and because the old man's breathing had gone strange—too regular now, too calm, like a river that has finally decided where it's going.

"Elias," he whispered through the tent flap. "Are you awake?"

And from inside, in a voice thin as paper but perfectly clear, the dying man said:

"Heard every word, cook. Come in. It's time somebody in this crew knew the whole map."
