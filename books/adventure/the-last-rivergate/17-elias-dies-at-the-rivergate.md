## 17. Elias Dies at the Rivergate

The pain woke him before the light did.

It came the way it always came now—not arriving so much as being discovered already there, deep in his chest like a stone that had been sinking for months and had finally found the bottom. Elias Wrenn lay still in his bedroll beneath the arch and let it have him, because struggling only made it worse, and because he had learned, in these last weeks on the water, that there was a strange courtesy in pain if you stopped fighting it. It was honest. It never pretended to be anything but what it was.

Above him, through the gap of the limestone arch, the sky was going from black to a color he had no name for—a gray with a green thread in it, the color the river turned just before dawn touched it. The arch itself was invisible against the dark, but he could feel it overhead the way you feel a hand resting on your shoulder without pressing down. Forty years since he'd last stood under this stone. Sixty years since he'd first seen it as a boy, when his father poled them up through the narrows and told him, *Everything past this gate belongs to the water, and the water keeps what it's given.*

He had come back to give something back.

* * *

He heard Petra before he saw her. He always did now; his eyes were failing faster than his ears, which felt like a bad joke the river was playing on him—taking the sight of things first, leaving him the sound of them. Her boots on the gravel bar made a particular crunch, lighter on the left foot where she favored an old ankle injury she'd never mentioned and he'd never asked about. Some things you didn't need to ask. You watched a woman walk for three hundred miles and you knew her feet better than her face.

"You're awake," she said. Not a question. She crouched beside him, and he smelled woodsmoke on her jacket, and under it the iron-and-mud smell of the river at low water.

"Barely," he said. "Is that coffee I'm smelling or are you just happy to see me?"

"Teodor's making it. He says if you die before your second cup he'll take it personally."

"Tell him I'll do my best." Elias smiled, and the smile cost him more than he expected, so he lay back against the folded tarp they'd propped behind his shoulders and looked up at where the arch must be. "Petra. Come here where I can hear you properly. My ears work better close up."

She shifted closer. He could feel the warmth of her, and the careful way she held herself—the held-breath quality of people who've been waiting for someone to die and hate themselves a little for how practiced they've gotten at the waiting.

"How long?" she asked quietly.

"Till what?"

"Don't make me say it."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on longer than he wanted it to, and when it finished he felt lighter somehow, emptied out, the way a channel feels after the flood has taken everything loose from its banks. "Till the end," he said. "Today. Maybe tomorrow if I'm stubborn, and we both know I am. But today feels right. Today feels—" He searched for the word and found it floating nearby, easy as driftwood. "Clean."

"Dawn's coming," she said. "You wanted to see it under the arch."

"I wanted more than to see it." He reached out and found her wrist, closed his fingers around it. Her pulse was steady under his thumb, quick and strong, a young woman's pulse, a river-runner's pulse. "I wanted witnesses. And I've got the best ones money can't buy."

* * *

They brought him coffee and he drank half of it, which was half more than yesterday. Teodor sat on his heels nearby with his big hands wrapped around his own cup, saying nothing, because Teodor understood that silence could be a meal too, and he served it generously. Sable perched on a rock with her knees drawn up, seventeen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who had seen men die before, and failing, and Elias loved her for the failing. Juno stood a little apart, facing the water, because Juno faced the water the way other people faced their problems—directly, professionally, with instruments if you gave her any.

And Petra sat closest of all, cross-legged in the gravel, her shoulder almost touching his.

The sun came up the way it did in the valley: all at once, like a door opening. Light poured through the arch and laid a long gold stripe across the shrinking river, and the drowned town below them caught fire with it—rooftops breaking the receding waterline one by one, shingles and chimney bricks and the bell tower of the church standing hip-deep now where it had stood chest-deep a week ago. Belltown. The water was letting go of it slowly, reluctantly, the way a fist opens finger by finger around something it has held too long.

"There she is," Elias said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it crack. "Forty years I carried her. Forty years of maps and lies and half-truths, and every night telling myself I'd come back when it was safe, when the Syndicate forgot, when I was ready—and the truth is I was never going to be ready. Ready is a story cowards tell." He glanced sideways at Petra. "Present company excluded."

"I'm not brave," Petra said. "I'm broke. There's a difference."

"No, girl. Broke is what you are. Brave is what you keep doing anyway." He took a breath, and the breath whistled a little in his chest, and he pressed on before anyone could fuss. "I want to say my piece while the saying's good. So nobody talk. Just listen. That's all I ever needed from this crew—to be listened to once, by people who weren't after my map."

Nobody talked. The river ran thin and bright over the shallows. Somewhere downstream, faint as memory, a bird called out its three-note question and got no answer.

* * *

"I was twenty-two years old the night the levee went," Elias said. "I know I've told pieces of it. Juno's heard the hydrology. Sable's heard about the dynamite. But I've never told it whole, because whole is heavier than any of you signed up to carry, and because whole has a name on every page, and names are dangerous things." He paused. "But I'm done carrying it. So here it is, whole, one time, and then it's yours whether you want it or not.

"The Vane Syndicate didn't start as lenders. They started as claim-jumpers. Four men—Vane himself, and Corliss, and the Brandt brothers—they'd staked claims all through this valley, copper and silver, enough to make ten fortunes. But the claims were tied up in Belltown's registry, in the church vault, in deeds and affidavits sworn by miners who'd never sell. So the four of them did the arithmetic no decent man would do. They figured out what the valley was worth dead versus alive, and dead came out ahead.

"They blew the levee on a night in October, during the high water, and they did it knowing the flood would drown the town and everything in it—records, claims, and forty-one people who hadn't finished evacuating. Forty-one. I counted them afterward. I was the one who counted them, because I was the one who survived, because I was up on the ridge checking a survey line when the water came, and I watched my father's house go under from two hundred feet above it, and I could not even hear it over the noise of the river eating everything."

His voice had gone flat and even, the voice of a man reciting coordinates, and he knew it, and he let it, because feeling it fully would stop his heart right there on the gravel bar, and he had one more thing to say.

"The ledgers in that vault prove all of it. Dates, payments, the dynamite order signed by Vane's own hand. Juno found them; I know she did; I saw her face when she came up out of that church. And I know what's been happening around my fire at night—arguments, whispers, Sable watching everyone with those sharp little eyes. Sell them, burn them, trade them. I've lain here listening to my family tear itself apart over paper." He turned his head, slow as a dredge swing, and looked at each of them in turn. "So I'm going to settle it. Right now. While I can still raise my voice, which won't be long."

* * *

"Petra." He found her hand again. "Come up here where I don't have to shout."

She leaned in, and he spoke against her ear, quiet enough that the others got only the shape of the words and not the words themselves—which was deliberate, because some trusts are private or they're nothing.

"I never put it in writing, because writing gets found. But you remember the hospice room. You remember I said two things. Bring them home, and keep silent until I'm gone. You swore both. Now hear the rest of it, the clause I kept in my mouth for six hundred miles: the ledgers aren't yours to sell and they're not mine to burn. They belong to the forty-one. To the town. Their job isn't to make anybody rich or clean anybody's conscience. Their job is to testify. Same as a witness on a stand—you don't buy a witness and you don't bury one. You put them in front of the truth and you let them speak."

"And what do you want me to do?" Petra whispered. "Doss is coming. We both know it. He'll offer me everything."

"He will. And you'll be tempted, because you're tired and broke and you've been drowning in that man's arithmetic for years. So I'm not going to tell you what to do." He pulled back far enough to look into her face, and his failing eyes made her blur into a shape of dark hair and river-light, and somehow that was easier—talking to the shape of her, the essence. "That's the whole point. I spent forty years deciding things alone in the dark, and look what it got everybody. I'm not handing you another order, Petra Underhill. I'm handing you the weight and stepping off. Finish it your way. Whatever your way turns out to be—that's the only version of this ending I trust. Not the ledgers. Not the law. Not even the valley. *You.*"

Her jaw was working. She wasn't crying—he suspected Petra Underhill treated crying the way she treated debt, as something to be paid privately, in installments, where no one could see—but her eyes were shining wet in the new sun, and her hand in his had gone very still.

"Elias," she said. "I don't know if my way is good."

"Good ways are like good rivers," he said. "You don't know them till you run them."

* * *

The morning warmed. Teodor fed everyone breakfast without being asked, because that was how Teodor prayed. Sable came and sat by Elias and fixed the buckle on his pack strap that didn't need fixing, just to have her hands busy, and he let her, and told her the strap had never been straighter in its life. Juno waded out to check the gauge on the staff she'd driven into the gravel and came back frowning at her notebook, muttering numbers—storm cells building somewhere upstream, she said, pressure falling, the dry season maybe breaking early. Elias filed that away without understanding it, the way you file away weather you won't live to see.

Through the middle of the day he drifted. Sleep and waking had gotten mixed together in him like silt in current, and he surfaced sometimes to find the light changed, the shadows swung around, one of the crew sitting near him keeping watch without being asked. Once he surfaced to find Petra reading aloud from his own journal—the old pages, the ones he hadn't burned—his younger self's handwriting describing this same arch sixty years ago, and his own voice coming back to him in her roughened runner's accent, and he thought: *there it is, that's the whole trick of it, that's what carrying a story means. Somebody else's throat.*

Near evening the pain came back and stayed. He didn't hide it. There was no one left to perform for.

They gathered around him as the light went long and gold and then rose-colored, and the arch above them turned the color of an old wound healing. Below, the rooftops of Belltown stood black against the bright water, patient as gravestones, and the church bell tower caught the last sun and held it.

"Everybody's here?" he asked.

"Everybody's here," Petra said.

"Then I'll say the short version, since the long one wore me out." He smiled, and this time the smile was free; the pain had burned off whatever was left to be afraid of. "I spent my life thinking the Rivergate was a place. It's not. It's a crossing. You go through it carrying what you carry, and on the other side you find out what you actually had. I carried guilt for forty years and found out what I had was this—four strangers who became the family I thought I'd lost. That's the treasure. The ledgers are just receipts."

His breath was getting shorter between sentences. He could feel the pauses stretching, the river inside him slowing toward its pools.

"Petra. Finish it your way. That's all. That's the whole will and testament." His fingers tightened on hers, once, with everything he had left, which was more than he expected and less than he needed. "Bring them home. You already did. Look around. You already did."

The sun went down behind the western rim and the valley filled up with blue shadow, and the first stars came out through the arch like sparks off a wire. Elias Wrenn lay beneath the stone his father had shown him, listening to the river run thin and cold over the bones of Belltown, and he found that he was not afraid, and that this surprised him, and that the surprise was the last interesting thought he ever had.

He died sometime in the small hours, between one breath and the next, so gently that Sable—who was keeping watch—didn't realize for several minutes, and then realized all at once, and went and woke Petra without saying a word, because some news travels best in silence.

* * *

They buried him at dawn on the rise above the gravel bar, where the ground was dry and the view ran clear through the arch to the drowned town. There was no preacher, so Petra spoke, and then Teodor said something in the language of whichever country he'd fled third-to-last, and Juno read the gauge readings for the day aloud, which sounded strange until Elias, wherever he was, surely laughed—because what is a eulogy, really, except proof that the water kept rising after you stopped measuring? Sable put one of his burnt journal pages in the cairn they built, the one corner that hadn't burned, and on it, in his handwriting, a single line: *Tell it true.*

They stood there a while after. Nobody moved to break camp. The river ran. Far downstream, so far it was only a suggestion, a smudge of engine-smoke hung over the water where no engine had any business being—and Juno saw it first, and lowered her binoculars, and said quietly:

"They're coming. Doss is coming, and we've got maybe a day."

Petra turned from the cairn. Her face in the morning light was the face of a woman who had just been handed a weight and decided, finally, how to carry it.

"Then we'd better figure out how fast this river can run," she said.
