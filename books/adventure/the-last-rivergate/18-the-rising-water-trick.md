## 18. The Rising Water Trick

Juno Voss had spent eleven years learning how rivers lied, and the last three learning how people did, so she knew a desperate plan when it started building itself behind her eyes. This one arrived the way storms did—not all at once but in pieces, a pressure she felt before she understood it, a heaviness in the air of her own skull.

She was crouched at the river's edge below the arch when it finished forming.

Dawn had come up gray and bruised over the valley, the limestone gate still holding its shadow like a mouth refusing to speak. Behind her, the camp was quiet in the wrong way. Teodor had cooked nothing. Sable hadn't touched the outboard since yesterday. Petra stood at the tree line with her back to all of them, watching the downstream reach where the Syndicate's boats sat at the narrows like a hand closing around a throat—three hulls, a tarp shelter, the orange ember of a cook fire that never went out. Doss Merrick's people had been there two days now, patient as ticks, and every hour they sat there the water dropped another inch, and every inch the water dropped made the narrows shallower, and the shallower the narrows got, the more certain it became that no boat carrying four living people and a church vault's worth of dynamite-paper was going to slip past them.

The river was dying in front of them. That was the whole problem. That was also, Juno realized, standing there with her boots in the wet gravel, the whole answer.

* * *

She had been a hydrologist once. She had to keep reminding herself of that, because for six years she had done her best to be no one at all—someone who cooked rice in a galley kitchen and said little and knew less, someone whose laminated credentials had been mailed back to a state university with a letter she still couldn't reread. The Narrows Bend failure. Fourteen homes. A levee she had signed off on because a county commissioner with a soft voice and a hard handshake had shown her the numbers twice, and she had been tired, and the numbers had looked fine, and the river had looked fine, right up until the night it didn't.

She had sworn she'd never model a flood again. She'd sworn it in a motel room with her clothes still damp, watching the news footage of rooftops going under, and she had kept the oath the way most people keep oaths—by never testing it.

But Elias was dying under the arch, and Petra was carrying the ledgers like a stone in her chest, and Doss Merrick's boats were sitting in the narrows like a verdict already written, and Juno Voss had spent two days doing the one thing she'd promised herself she'd never do again.

She had been running numbers.

* * *

The storm was out there. She'd felt it for three days now in the way the wind came off the western rim in long, warm, wet breaths instead of the dry season's usual knife-gusts. She'd felt it in the barometer Sable had rigged from a salvaged altimeter and a strip of bicycle inner tube—the needle sagging lower each morning, the way a tired animal's head sags. The almanac called this the last window. Out on the flats where Petra used to chase lightning for hazard pay, the season's final cells would be building right now, stacking themselves tall and dark, feeding on the heat the dry season had left pooled over the broken country like oil in a pan.

And a storm, Juno thought, crouched at the waterline with a stick in her hand, is just a debt the sky has to pay back to the river.

She started drawing in the gravel. The channel below the arch, the narrows two miles down where the Syndicate sat, the long braided reach beyond that where the river spread thin as spilled tea. Then she drew the tributary—the one the maps had half-forgotten, the one Juno had noticed on their second day in the valley because noticing water was the one habit six years of exile hadn't burned out of her. It came down off the western rim through a canyon so narrow the sun touched it an hour a day. On the old survey sheets it was marked as seasonal. Intermittent. A blue dashed line the cartographers had drawn with the same bored confidence people used for anything they didn't expect to matter.

But Juno had walked its mouth yesterday, ostensibly gathering firewood, actually because her feet had carried her there the way her feet always carried her toward water, and she had stood in its dry gravel bed and felt the ground humming.

Not water. Not yet. But the alluvium was saturated. She'd pushed her hand a foot down into the gravel and it came up dark and cold. The whole canyon floor was a sponge already wrung to its limit, holding everything it had been given and waiting for one more reason to let go.

The storm would give it that reason.

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the rim and laid its light across the drowned town's rooftops—the copper church spire green as pond weed, the shingles furred with forty years of silt and weed—Juno had filled six square feet of gravel with arithmetic.

Here was the shape of it. The storm would come in from the west, the way they always did this time of year, riding that warm wet wind. The canyon tributary had a catchment of—she estimated, pacing it out with her memory of the survey sheets—maybe forty square miles of rim country, steep, bare, drained by a channel that dropped nine hundred feet in six miles. When the cells hit that rim, the runoff wouldn't arrive as rain. It would arrive as a wall. Flash crest, the old textbooks called it. In a canyon like that, with ground that saturated, the water would come down all at once, a brown cataract that would hit the main river and keep hitting it for the better part of a day.

The main channel below the arch was running at maybe eleven percent of its seasonal volume. She'd measured it—Sable's pump intake, a float stick, three readings a day, because Juno couldn't stop measuring things any more than she could stop breathing. Eleven percent. But the channel itself, the old riverbed, was still there under the gravel, carved by a century of spring floods, wide and deep and waiting. The narrows where Doss sat were the pinch point—fifty yards of rock throat that in flood season ran ten feet deep over green water.

If the crest came down the tributary in the next forty-eight hours, the river below the arch would rise. Not a trickle. A rise. She figured four feet at the narrows inside six hours of the first cell hitting the rim, maybe five, maybe more, and the beauty of it—the terrible, elegant beauty—was that the Syndicate's boats were sitting in the shallows on purpose. Grounded. Anchored to gravel bars they'd chosen because the water couldn't touch them.

Until it could.

A rise of four feet would float Doss's shallow-draft skiffs off their bars, sure. It would also turn the narrows from a rock garden into a chute, and it would do it fast, and the current through a fifty-yard throat carrying the whole western rim's runoff would run—she did the division in her head twice, then a third time because she didn't trust herself to want the answer—somewhere north of twelve knots. Nobody held a boat in that. Nobody held a boat in that on purpose, and nobody held a boat in that by accident either, because the water would take the choice away from you before you finished deciding.

The Syndicate would have to pull their boats or lose them. Either way, the narrows would open for a window—six hours, maybe eight—and a boat that knew the channel, that had a hydrologist reading the water and a mechanic who could keep an engine alive through silt, could run it in the flood crest's leading edge, riding the same brown wall that was flushing the wolves off their perch.

And downstream, past the narrows, past the braids, the river gathered itself again and ran two hundred miles to Harrow Ford. And in Harrow Ford there was a federal courthouse. And in that courthouse, if the river was kind and the engine held, there was a magistrate who had never heard of the Vane Syndicate and owed Doss Merrick nothing.

* * *

She waited until evening to say it, because she'd learned that desperate ideas, like wild animals, were best approached slowly and in good light.

The camp sat in the arch's long shadow. Teodor had finally made food—rice and the last of the dried fish, cooked without commentary, which from Teodor was its own kind of alarm. Elias lay in the tent, breathing in the shallow, spaced rhythm Juno had last heard from her father in his final week, and nobody said that out loud either. Sable sat cross-legged on a crate, turning a wrench over in her hands without seeing it. Petra stood apart, arms crossed, watching the downstream dark where the Syndicate's fire glowed orange through the trees.

Juno set her stick down. Took a breath. The air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"The storm's coming," she said.

Petra's head turned. Of everyone, Petra knew what weather meant to Juno—knew that Juno read the sky the way other people read faces, and that she'd been reading this one with her jaw getting tighter for three days.

"Tomorrow night," Juno went on. "Maybe the night after. It's big. Last cells of the season, and they're stacking up over the western rim right now."

"Weather's weather," Teodor said quietly. "We cannot drink it."

"We can ride it." Juno crouched and pulled the stick through the gravel, drawing fast, the lines coming out of her hand cleaner than she expected, as if the six years of silence had been storing them up. "The tributary. West canyon. You all crossed its mouth on the wood-gathering runs. It's dry now, but it drains forty square miles of rim and it drops nine hundred feet, and the ground under it is already saturated—I checked, I put my arm in the gravel up to the shoulder. When the storm hits, that canyon isn't going to trickle. It's going to fire. All of it, at once, straight into the main channel two miles below us."

Sable had stopped turning the wrench.

"The river's been dying for a month and everyone's been treating that as the trap," Juno said. "It's not the trap. The trap is Doss's boats sitting in the shallows of the narrows, anchored where the water can't reach them. But the water can reach them. In forty-eight hours the water can reach *everything*." She drew the crest line with a hard stroke. "Flash crest comes down the canyon, hits the main stem, and the river below us rises four, maybe five feet, inside six hours. The narrows turn into a chute running twelve knots. Nobody can hold a boat in that—not Doss's people, not anyone. They'll have to pull out or get flushed. And in that window, we run."

Silence. The kind of silence a room makes when it's deciding something.

"Run it how?" Petra said. Her voice was careful. Juno recognized the care; it was the voice you used when you wanted to say no and hadn't found the reason yet.

"Ride the leading edge. The first rise lifts us off the gravel before the current peaks—we float in water that's rising under us, fast but not yet murder. By the time the crest really hits, we're through the narrows and the Syndicate's boats are either wrecked or hauled out on the banks watching us go." Juno looked up. "And we don't stop at the fuel cache. We don't stop at Crooked Bend. We run two hundred miles to Harrow Ford, to the federal courthouse, and we put these—" she nodded at the drybag where the ledgers lived, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than any cargo they'd carried— "in front of a magistrate who doesn't take Doss Merrick's phone calls."

"That's not what we promised," Teodor said.

The words landed the way Teodor's words always landed—without cruelty, which made them worse.

"We promised Elias we'd bring everyone home," Juno said. "We promised him silence until he was gone. I'm not proposing we sell them, Teo. I'm not proposing we burn them either. I'm proposing we give them to the one person in two hundred miles who can make what's in them *matter*. Because I've done the math on the other options, and I did it for two days, and I'm going to say the ugly part out loud." She made herself look at each of them. "If we sit here, Doss comes upriver when the water's too low to run. If we try to sneak past the narrows at eleven percent flow, we ground out in the shallows and they walk up to us in ankle-deep water and take the ledgers and probably take us. There is no version of quiet that ends well. The river took away every small option. The storm is the only big one left."

"You want to outrun a flood," Sable said. Not mocking. Weighing.

"I want to *be* the flood," Juno said. "For about eight hours. Then I want to be a boat on a big slow river with a head start."

Sable looked down at the wrench in her hands. Juno watched the girl's mind working the way she watched water working at a cutbank—something shifting underneath, something about to let go. "The intake pump will choke on flood silt," Sable said finally. "I'd need to rig a strainer. And the prop—silt and gravel will eat it. I can shim the mounting, raise it two inches, but we lose thrust."

"We'll have thrust," Juno said. "We'll have the river doing the work. The engine just has to keep us pointed."

"And if the crest comes down the canyon while we're still loading," Petra said. "If we're on the gravel and the wall arrives early."

Juno had an answer for that too, and it was the answer she hated most, because it was the one that cost the most. "Then we hear it coming. A crest like that makes noise—you'll hear it two miles off, a sound like a train in the canyon. Sable's already got the boat packed to ninety percent. We load the ledgers first, then Elias, then us. We'll be on the water before the storm's first rain reaches the rim."

Petra looked at the downstream dark for a long time. The Syndicate's fire glowed and guttered. Somewhere out there, men who smiled for a living were sleeping next to boats that in two days would either be their coffins or their embarrassment.

"Elias," Petra said at last. "He made me swear to keep the valley's secret. Not to sell it. Not to give it away."

"He made you swear until he was dead and gone," Juno said, as gently as she knew how, which was not very. "Petra. He's dying under that arch right now. And what's in those ledgers isn't his secret. It's the town's. Belltown's. Forty years of people at the bottom of this water, and the men who put them there are the men who own my debt and yours. Elias didn't keep that vault sealed to protect them. He sealed it because he wanted it to land right. To land *heavy*." She looked at her own hands, gravel-dusted, steady. "I spent six years not saying what I knew about water, and fourteen families paid for my silence. I'm done paying that way. I'd rather drown in the narrows than carry it another mile quiet."

Nobody spoke. The river murmured over its gravel, low and thin, a voice worn down to a whisper, and Juno thought: *not for long.*

"Twelve knots," Sable said at last, half to herself, and Juno realized the girl had been doing the math too. "Through the narrows. In the dark, probably, if the cells come in overnight." She looked up, and there was something in her face Juno hadn't seen before—something lit from behind. "I can steer that. I've been wanting to steer something like that my whole life."

"Teo?" Petra said.

The cook was silent a long moment, turning his rice paddle over like Sable's wrench, like everyone in this camp turned some object over when the truth got too heavy to hold bare-handed. "In my third country," he said finally, "there was a flood. I carried my brother on my back through water to my chest. We lost everything we did not carry." He set the paddle down. "So I know what water can take. But I also know—" he tapped his chest, twice, "—what a person can carry, if the water is rising behind them. We will need food for two hundred miles. I will cook on a moving boat. It will be terrible food. We will eat it."

A sound went through the camp that was almost a laugh and was mostly relief, and Juno felt something unclench in her own chest that had been clenched since Narrows Bend, since before Narrows Bend, maybe since always.

Petra crouched at the edge of the gravel map. She put her finger on the drawn narrows, then on the drawn river beyond, all the way to the small X that was Harrow Ford, and Juno watched her commander do the thing she'd watched her do in every crisis since Crooked Bend: stop being afraid long enough to be exact.

"Timeline," Petra said.

"Storm hits the rim tomorrow night, near midnight if the cells hold their line," Juno said. "Crest reaches the tributary mouth one to three hours after first heavy rain—steep catchment, fast response. We launch when we hear the canyon start to roar. We'll have the leading edge at the narrows by dawn."

"Then we spend tomorrow getting ready," Petra said. "Sable, the boat. Teo, water and food for three days, everything sealed. Juno—" and here Petra looked at her with something that was not quite a smile and was much more valuable, "—read the sky."

Juno nodded. Then, because she was who she was, because six years of silence had not actually burned the habit out of her but only buried it alive, she walked to the water's edge and looked west.

The rim stood black against the last gray light, and above it, stacked like masonry, the first towers were already building—tall, blue-black, silent, lit from within by flickers too far away to hear. The wind came off them warm and wet and smelling of ozone and old rain, and it moved through the dead grass of the valley and out over the drowned rooftops of Belltown, and Juno stood in it with her eyes closed and did the thing she'd sworn off doing, the thing that had cost her everything once: she believed the water.

* * *

She woke once in the night to a sound she hadn't heard in a month, and lay still in her bedroll listening to it fall soft and steady on the tent canvas, on the gravel, on the river, on the whole waiting valley.

Rain. The first rain of the last storm. Gentle, patient, the opening argument.

By the time she crawled out into the dark, the whole camp was already up. Petra stood at the tree line with the ledgers' drybag slung across her back. Sable had a lantern lit and the engine cover open, her hands moving fast and sure. Teodor was lashing down the food boxes with the calm of a man who had fled three countries and knew exactly what you could carry and what you had to leave.

And from the west, from the black canyon of the tributary, two miles off and getting louder, came a sound like a train that had never had any tracks—deep, growing, inevitable—the river coming home to take back everything that had been taken from it.

Juno put her hand on the gunwale and said, "Get in the boat."
