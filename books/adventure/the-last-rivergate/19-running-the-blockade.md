## 19. Running the Blockade

The storm came up the valley like something summoned.

Petra felt it before she saw it—the pressure dropping so fast her ears popped, the river going glassy and strange in the last light, every bird on the waterline lifting off at once. She stood at the bow of the *Wrenn's Promise* with Juno's barometer in her hand and watched the western sky turn the color of a bruise healing backward: green to yellow to a deep arterial purple, stacked cloud boiling over the ridge tops like smoke from a fire underground.

"Two hours," Juno said beside her. "Maybe less. It's moving faster than I modeled."

"You modeled it to save our lives. Give it some credit."

"I modeled it with data from forty years ago and a teenager's weather radio." Juno's voice was flat, but her knuckles were white on the rail. "It'll do."

Behind them, moored across the narrows below the arch, sat three of Doss Merrick's boats—running lights on, generators humming, men visible on the decks in the long dusk like figures on a stage waiting for their cue. They weren't hiding anymore. There was no need. The dry season had dropped the river until the shallows below the Gate were barely knee-deep on the bars, and no loaded boat could cross them. The Syndicate didn't have to chase anyone. They only had to wait for the water to finish dying.

Which was exactly why the storm was a gift.

Petra turned and looked at her crew. Teodor sat on the engine housing flexing his bad shoulder, a bandana tied around his forehead to keep the sweat out of his eyes, a pot of rice and beans already cooked and lashed down because he refused to let any of them run a rapid hungry. Sable crouched at the helm controls with a wrench in one hand and grease to her elbows, running through the checklist they'd built together over two sleepless days—bilge pumps, fuel lines, the patched weld on the prop shaft that Sable swore would hold and then swore again, louder, when Petra raised an eyebrow. Elias lay in the shelter of the cabin, wrapped in blankets, breathing in the shallow, careful way of a man rationing what was left of him.

And in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth inside a steel ammunition box inside a flour sack, sat the ledgers of Belltown.

Forty years ago, men had dynamited a levee in the dark and drowned a town to steal its claims. Those men had founded the Vane Syndicate. The ledgers proved it—names, dates, payments, the whole architecture of the crime written in a dead bookkeeper's careful hand. And Doss Merrick wanted them badly enough to forgive everything, to smile while his men checked their rifle bolts, to sit out there in the dark below the arch like patient wolves at a den mouth.

"He'll expect us to run at first light," Juno said. "Standard thinking. Wait for visibility."

"He'll expect us to run when the surge peaks," Petra said. "That's the problem. Peak surge is when the rapids are at their worst."

"It's also when his boats can't maneuver in the narrows without wrecking." Juno tapped the chart she'd drawn by hand, the old skills she'd buried and dug back up for this one desperate job laid out in ink: the channel, the bars, the standing waves, the arch itself. "There's a window. Two hours after the front hits, the river comes up fast but hasn't crested. Enough water over the shallows to float us. Not enough to make the crossing easy. But enough."

"And after the crest?"

"After the crest it's a lake for a day, and then it drops, and we're trapped until next season." Juno met her eyes. "It's the window or nothing, Petra."

*The window or nothing.* That had been the shape of Petra's whole life since the lightning killed her truck on the flats—every choice a narrowing channel, every option a rock. But standing here now, with these people, she felt something different underneath the fear. Not calm. Nothing so kind as calm. More like clarity. The terror was still there, big as the storm, but it had stopped being about her debt, her neck, her ruin. It was about getting them through. All of them. She'd promised a dying man, and she'd promised herself, and for once those promises pointed the same direction.

"Sable," she called. "How's my boat?"

Sable looked up, and there was something in her face Petra hadn't seen before—not the guarded wariness of a kid who'd learned adults always left, but a fierce, settled readiness. "She's ugly and she's mine," Sable said. "And she'll hold."

"Then we go at the window. Everybody hear me?" Petra raised her voice. "We ride the surge down the narrows, we shoot the shallows where the chart says there's a lane, and we do not stop for anything short of God himself standing in the river. Doss will shoot. He'll shoot a lot, and some of it will be close. Nobody returns fire. We are not fighters—we're water people tonight, and water people win by being somewhere else. Questions?"

Teodor raised his hand. "What's for dinner tomorrow?"

"Whatever you cook, chef."

"Then we cannot die," he said solemnly. "I have a duck."

Laughter went around the deck, thin and bright and necessary, the way laughter does right before the worst thing happens. Even Elias smiled from under his blankets, and caught Petra's eye, and gave her a small nod that carried more weight than any speech. *My way,* he'd told her at dawn. *Finish it your way.*

Her way was downstream, through gunfire, into the truth.

* * *

The front hit at 9:40 p.m., and it hit like a door slamming.

Rain came sideways, a solid gray wall of it, and the wind stripped the valley bare of every loose thing not tied down. Petra stood at the bow in her slicker with the rain hammering her hood and watched lightning walk down the ridgeline in stuttering columns, close enough that the air tasted of copper and ozone, and thought: *I've chased you my whole life. Carry me now.*

The river answered within the hour. They could hear it—a low, rising roar from upstream, deeper than wind, the sound of a watershed remembering what it was for. The water level crept up the gauge stake Sable had planted: six inches, a foot, two feet. Juno called the numbers against her chart, voice shouted flat against the gale, and at 11:15 she grabbed Petra's arm.

"Window. Now. Before it crests."

Petra looked once more at the Syndicate boats below the arch. Their running lights burned steady through the rain. Men moved on the decks—she could see the shapes of them, hunched, watching the water rise with what she imagined was satisfaction. Let it come, they'd be thinking. Let it drown the valley and seal the tomb. They had no idea the rising water was the door.

"Cast off," she said.

Sable took the helm. Of course Sable took the helm—Petra had decided that two days ago, watching the girl's hands move over the controls with a sureness none of the rest of them had. Seventeen years old, three months runaway, hands steadier than anyone's. "Engine's warm," Sable said. "Tell me when."

"Now."

The *Wrenn's Promise* swung out into the current and the rain swallowed them whole.

The first minute was pure blindness—headlights useless in the downpour, the world reduced to Juno's shouted bearings and the feel of the current taking the hull. Then the lightning flashed and Petra saw everything at once, frozen silver: the narrows ahead, black water standing up in ridges, the limestone arch looming ahead like the jaw of the earth, and beyond it, faintly, the running lights of the blockade.

"They see us!" Teodor bellowed from the stern. A moment later came the sound Petra had been dreading and expecting all at once: gunfire. Flat cracks rolling up the narrows, muzzle flashes strobing under the arch, bullets slapping the water around them with little white plumes that the lightning kept revealing, one after another, like the river blooming.

"Down! Everyone down except Sable!" Petra flattened behind the bow rail. "Sable, armor plate, now!"

"Already did it yesterday!" Sable yelled back, and Petra remembered the scrap steel the girl had welded across the helm station and blessed her name forever.

The boat bucked through the first standing wave and came down hard enough to knock teeth together. Water sheeted over the bow. Somewhere aft, Teodor was singing—actually singing, some drinking song in a language Petra didn't know, bellowing it into the storm like defiance made audible—and absurdly it helped. You couldn't be entirely afraid while a man sang about wine and bad women at the top of his lungs in a hailstorm.

They shot under the arch.

For half a second the world was stone—the arch overhead blotting out sky and lightning both, the roar of the river compressed and doubled by the walls, spray hanging in the air like breath—and then they were through, into the lower narrows, and the blockade was two hundred yards off the port bow and closing.

"Doss's boat is moving!" Juno screamed. "He's coming after us!"

Of course he was. Petra risked a look over the rail and saw the Syndicate's lead boat swinging out into the current, its searchlight spearing the rain, men crouched along its gunwales. Behind it the second boat followed. The third held position at the shallows—the smart one, Petra thought wildly, the one crew who understood what was coming.

The shooting intensified. Rounds cracked past overhead, and then a burst raked the stern and Teodor's song cut off.

"TEODOR!"

"I'm fine!" A pause. "Mostly fine! My arm disagrees!"

"Graze?" Petra crawled aft through the pitching dark, found him pressed against the engine housing, blood running bright down his forearm where a bullet had opened a furrow along the meat of it. Ugly. Not deadly. He grinned at her through the rain, teeth flashing.

"A souvenir," he said. "From cheerful men. Bandage me and keep singing, captain—I lost my place."

She wrapped the wound one-handed while the boat slammed through wave after wave, and all the while Sable steered, reading the water by instinct and Juno's shouted numbers, finding the lane through the shallows that existed for maybe ninety seconds in a hundred years. The hull scraped once—a horrible grinding shudder along the keel—and freed. Scraped again, longer, sparks of terror up Petra's spine—and then the river rose another inch beneath them and they floated free into the widest reach of the lower narrows.

Behind them, Doss's boat ran onto the bar.

Petra heard it before she understood it: a change in the engine note, a shriek of metal, the searchlight swinging wild. The Syndicate's pilot had pushed his boat into water that his own employers' levee-blasting had spent forty years silting shallow—water that the flood maps in Belltown's sealed vault described precisely, if anyone had bothered to read history. The boat struck the gravel shoal at speed and slewed sideways, and men went into the current, and the searchlight spun crazily across the rain like a lighthouse gone mad.

Doss Merrick stood in the wreckage up to his knees, and even at this distance, even through rain and night and gunfire, Petra could have sworn he was looking straight at her. She couldn't read his expression. She wasn't sure she wanted to.

The second boat turned back. The third never moved.

"We're through," Juno said. She said it quietly, almost to herself, staring astern at the chaos fading into the rain. "Petra. We're through."

Petra put her hand on Sable's shoulder at the helm. The girl was soaked to the skin, shaking—with cold or adrenaline or both—but her eyes were fixed forward, downriver, into the dark where the floodwater ran.

"Take us home, Sable."

"I'm not home yet," Sable said. "But I know the way."

* * *

They ran all night.

Below the narrows the river widened and gentled, the storm surge carrying them on a brown, muscular current past country that slowly remembered it was inhabited—fence lines surfacing out of the flood, a barn roof, a county road bridge with water licking its pilings. Toward dawn the rain thinned to a drizzle and the eastern sky went the color of pewter, then pearl, then a soft bruised gold, and Petra stood at the bow and watched the world come back.

Teodor slept propped against the cabin with his bandaged arm cradled like a sleeping child. Juno had not slept at all; she sat amidships with the ammunition box between her boots and one hand resting on it, keeping watch over the ledgers the way a shepherd keeps watch over one sheep among many. Elias breathed. That was all she could ask of him now—breath by breath, hour by hour, a candle burning down in a draftless room.

Sable steered.

Somewhere past the second bend, as the sun finally broke through the torn clouds and laid a road of gold across the water, Sable spoke without turning around.

"They're going to come for us again."

"Yes," Petra said. No point lying to her. Lying to Sable was like lying to the river; it just flowed around the lie and kept going.

"But not today."

"Not today. Today we're faster than they are, and smarter, and wetter."

Sable almost smiled. "Harrow Ford is how far?"

"Juno says thirty miles. Magistrate's office opens at eight." Petra checked her watch. "We'll beat it there by an hour."

"Good." Sable adjusted the wheel a degree, eyes on the channel. "Because I've been thinking. About what's in that box." Her voice stayed level, but her shoulders had gone stiff. "You don't have to tell me what's in it. I get that. But whatever it is—it better be worth Teodor's arm. It better be worth all of it."

Petra looked at the back of that stubborn head, at the set of those young shoulders, and felt the promise she'd made in a hospice room press against her ribs like a hand. *Tell no one what the valley holds until he's dead and gone.* Elias was not dead yet. But he was dying, and they both knew it, and the secret was a weight she could not carry alone much longer.

"It's worth it," Petra said. "And soon—you'll know why. I swear it."

Sable nodded once, accepting the promise the way she accepted everything: provisionally, watchfully, with her escape routes mapped. But she nodded.

Ahead, the river bent toward Harrow Ford, and the first church bell of morning rang out thin and clear across the water, and the *Wrenn's Promise* ran on the flood toward the testimony that would end everything—or fail trying.
