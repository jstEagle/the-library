## 20. Testimony

The rain found Harrow Ford two days before we did, and it hadn't stopped since. It came down in long gray sheets that turned the town's single main street into a river of its own, running brown and fast over the cobblestones toward the real river below—the one we'd come down, the one that had tried to kill us and mostly failed.

I stood on the magistrate's steps with the ledgers in a waterproof satchel against my chest, and I could not make my feet climb the stairs.

They were heavier than they looked. That was the thing nobody told you about proof. You'd think a thing like that would weigh nothing at all—paper, ink, forty years of waiting. But every step toward those doors felt like carrying a drowned man up out of the valley. Elias was in the satchel. So was Belltown. So was every name in those church records who'd signed a claim and lost it to a wall of water that somebody had paid for.

Sable stood beside me under the awning, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets. She didn't say anything. She'd been not saying anything for two days, which from Sable was its own kind of speech. Teodor was back at the dock watching the boat, arm in a sling, refusing help with anything. Juno had gone ahead an hour ago to find the magistrate's office and confirm it was real—that there actually was a federal man in this wet little town who'd listen, who wasn't bought, whose name wasn't in anyone's ledger.

Juno had come back pale and certain. "His name is Aldous Crane," she'd said. "He's been filing complaints about the Vane Syndicate for six years. Nobody upstream will give him jurisdiction." Her voice had done something strange then, something almost like hope. "Until now. Until somebody walks in holding the thing he's been asking for."

So here I was. Holding it.

The rain drummed the awning. Down the street, a dog shook itself under a porch roof and looked at me with the flat patience of animals.

"You're stalling," Sable said finally.

"I'm assessing."

"You've been assessing for ten minutes."

"Ten minutes is cheap," I said. "I used to assess by the hour."

She snorted. Then she went quiet again, and when she spoke her voice had dropped into the register she saved for things that mattered, the one that made her sound older than seventeen. "You don't have to do the talking part alone. If you don't want to."

"I know."

"And you're going to anyway."

"I know that too."

She nodded slowly, like I'd passed some test she hadn't announced. Then she pulled her hood back, letting the rain hit her face, and squared her shoulders the way she did before crawling into an engine compartment. "Okay," she said. "Let's go end these guys."

* * *

The magistrate's office smelled like wet wool and lamp oil and old paper, and it was smaller than I expected—a room barely bigger than Teodor's galley, shelves sagging with case files, a stove ticking in the corner, and behind a scarred desk a narrow man with gray at his temples and eyes that had the particular hunger of someone who has waited a long time for something and doesn't quite believe it's coming.

Aldous Crane stood up so fast his chair skidded.

"You have them," he said. Not a question. His gaze went straight to the satchel like a compass finding north. "You have the Belltown records."

"How do you know what's in this bag?"

"Because I've spent six years reconstructing what happened in that valley from fragments." He came around the desk, stopped himself, held his hands at his sides like a man keeping them off a table of food. "Flood hydrology that doesn't match any natural event. Land transfers filed within weeks of the flood by three men who shouldn't have known the valley was coming available. A levee breach on a clear night in a dry season. I have theories, Ms. Underhill. Beautiful, damning theories. And not one piece of physical evidence, because the evidence is forty miles upriver behind rapids no one runs twice, in a town the state survey erased from the maps." His voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he covered it by clearing his throat. "Who gave you the location?"

"A dying man named Elias Wrenn."

Crane went very still. "Wrenn. There was a Wrenn in the old claims registry. A boy, then. Listed as surviving heir of a claim in the third district." He sat down slowly, all the urgency draining out of him into something heavier. "He was a child when it happened."

"He was." My throat tightened. "He spent his whole life walking back to it. He got there last week. He died under the arch four days ago, at dawn, with the river coming up around him." I set the satchel on the desk. The sound it made was small. It should have been thunder. "He asked me to bring everyone home and to keep silent until he was gone. He's gone. So here I am."

Crane unbuckled the satchel with hands that weren't quite steady. When he opened the first ledger, the smell of it rose up—damp stone, candle wax, time—and he made a sound low in his chest that I'll remember as long as I live. Something between a sob and a laugh.

"Sealed vault," I said. "Church records office. The flood put it underwater before anyone could burn it. Forty years of cold, dark, and silt, and the ledgers are legible. Every claim. Every signature. Every payment."

"To whom?" Crane asked, though his finger was already moving down the page.

I said the names. The three names. The founders. The men who signed the land transfers, the men whose cheerful descendants now ran loan offices in four counties and smiled while they did it. The men who had looked at a valley full of other people's property and solved the problem with dynamite and a dry-season night.

Crane wrote nothing down. He just listened, lips moving, and when I finished he closed the ledger with exaggerated gentleness, like closing a coffin.

"Ms. Underhill. I need your testimony. All of it. Formally, on the record, sworn. It will take hours. Possibly days. During which the Syndicate will learn where you are, and they will send people, and those people will be pleasant right up until they aren't." He looked at me over the ledger. "Do you understand what you're starting?"

"I've understood since a hospice room in March," I said. "The question was never whether. It was whether I'd survive the trip."

"Did you?"

I thought about the gravel bar, the portage, the gunfire on the surge, Teodor's shoulder, Sable's hands on the tiller, Juno reading water like scripture. I thought about Elias's face at dawn, peaceful in a way he hadn't been in forty years.

"Not all of me," I said. "But enough."

* * *

Testimony, it turns out, is a kind of storm chasing. You can't control it. You can only read the conditions, hold your position, and keep talking while the pressure drops around you.

We went through it in Crane's cramped office with the stove ticking and the rain still falling, hour after hour. I gave him the map. I gave him Elias's name and the terms of the oath and the reason I'd kept it. I gave him the route, the fuel caches, the survey barge that had shadowed us from Crooked Bend, the smiling men at the cache with their casual rifles. I gave him Doss Merrick's offer on the water—full forgiveness for the ledgers—and the exact words Doss had used, because Juno had written them down that same night in her logbook, verbatim, because that was the kind of mind Juno had.

Crane's clerk transcribed everything. Twice Crane stopped the session to send telegrams: to the federal marshal in Kestrel Crossing, to the land office, to a judge whose name he said like a password. Each time he came back to the desk he looked older and lighter at once, like a man setting down stones he'd carried so long he'd forgotten they weren't part of his body.

It was near midnight when he asked the question I'd been waiting for.

"Why you?" he said. "You owed these people money. By your own account, a great deal. They offered you freedom. All you had to do was hand over paper. No one would ever have known—not the crew, not the valley, not me. Why throw away the easiest exit of your life?"

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the dark window.

"Because I did the math," I said. "For two years I did nothing but math. How many storm runs to clear the interest. How many jobs to clear the principal. What my truck was worth, what my gear was worth, what I was worth if you sold everything and it still wasn't enough." I turned my cup on the desk. "Debt like that isn't money, Mr. Crane. It's weather. It follows you. You start making decisions inside it without noticing—you take the job because the debt says take it, you stay quiet because the debt says stay quiet. The Vane Syndicate doesn't collect payments. It collects people." I looked up. "Elias knew that. He'd watched them do it to a whole valley. He handed me the ledgers and said, some truths are heavier than debts. I didn't understand him then. I understand him now. You don't pay a debt like theirs. You end the lender."

Crane sat back. For a long moment he said nothing at all.

"When I was a young attorney," he said finally, "my father lost his shop to a Vane note. Same smile, different generation. He died believing he'd failed at arithmetic." He pressed both palms flat on the ledger. "I have waited twenty-six years for someone to walk through that door and say what you just said. I want you to know that whatever happens next—whatever they send, whatever it costs—I will see this through to indictments or I will die at this desk."

"That won't be necessary," I said. "But it's nice to hear."

* * *

They came for us on the second day, of course. I'd have been insulted otherwise.

I was at the boarding house window, watching the rain, when the car rolled up the street—long, black, mud to the axles, moving too carefully for the weather. Two men got out first and took positions with the unhurried competence of professionals. Then the rear door opened and Doss Merrick stepped out into the rain without an umbrella, tilted his face up into it for a moment like a man enjoying a fine morning, and walked toward the magistrate's office, where I'd been sitting all day giving testimony he could probably recite along with.

I met him on the steps. Crane objected; I told him I wanted to hear it myself. Sable hovered inside the doorway, arms crossed, radiating teenage fury like a small stove.

Doss climbed the steps and stopped three feet away, rain streaming off his jaw, and smiled at me the way he always did—warm, easy, genuine-seeming, which was the whole horror of him.

"Petra Underhill," he said. "You look terrible. River does that."

"Doss."

"I'm going to be brief, because I know you're busy, and because I respect your time even though you clearly don't respect mine." He spread his hands, rain dripping from his cuffs. "Full forgiveness. Principal, interest, penalties, the works. Signed, sealed, burned. Your truck debt, your boat debt, your crew's silence fees—gone. Plus a finder's consideration, generous, in cash. All you have to do is walk back into that office and tell the magistrate you were mistaken. Grief-addled. Saw some old church paperwork, couldn't read it, panicked. Happens all the time. Nobody blames a woman who's been through what you've been through."

"And if I don't?"

He sighed, genuinely regretful, and glanced out at the rain. "Then a lot of unpleasant machinery starts turning. Testimony gets impeached. Witnesses get nervous. Jurisdictions get argued about for years while you wait in towns like this one, watching doors." He looked back at me, and for just a moment the smile thinned and I saw the actual man underneath, the one who counted people the way I used to count storm cells. "Petra. I like you. I've liked you since the day Merrick senior sent me to your kitchen table. You're the best guide I ever placed. Don't make me be what I am."

I let the silence sit. Rain hammered the steps between us.

"You know what's funny, Doss?" I said. "Forty years ago, three men stood above a valley full of people who trusted them, and they did the math, and the math said drown it. And it worked. It worked perfectly. Nobody went to jail. Everybody got rich. Their kids got richer. And the only cost was that every one of them had to become the kind of man who could sleep after doing it." I stepped closer. "You're the third generation of that arithmetic. You're standing on a drowned town right now and you don't even feel it under your feet. That's not evil, Doss. That's just bookkeeping."

Something moved behind his eyes. I'll swear to this: it wasn't anger. It was recognition—the flicker of a man hearing his own eulogy read aloud and finding it accurate.

"The founders will be indicted by Friday," I said. "Your choice is which side of that paper your name lands on. You were at the fuel cache. You made the offer on the river. Juno wrote down your exact words. You can testify about orders you were given, or you can own them. One of those paths has a future in it."

Doss looked at me for a long time. Rain ran off his chin. And then he did the last thing I expected: he laughed, short and real, shaking his head.

"Goddamn," he said softly. "Elias Wrenn picked well." He turned and descended the steps, and at the bottom he paused, not looking back. "Tell your hydrologist her transcription is good work. Tell the kid the carburetor trick on our boats was clever—I noticed." He raised one hand, almost a wave. "See you at the trial, Petra. I'll be the one pleading out."

The car pulled away through the flooded street, and Sable appeared at my elbow.

"What was that?" she demanded. "What did he mean, see you at the trial? Is he—"

"Cooperating," I said. "Or performing cooperation. Either way, it's over. The rest is paperwork."

She stared after the car, then at me, and slowly the fury drained out of her face into something else—something young and stunned, like a person seeing a mountain they'd only ever heard described.

"We really did it," she said. "We actually—we ran the rapids and we did it."

"We did it," I said. "Well. Elias did it. We just carried the paper."

* * *

Friday came gray and clean, the storm finally spent, the sky scoured to a hard blue. Crane met us on the courthouse steps in a suit that fit him better than anything else in Harrow Ford, and beside him stood a federal marshal and a prosecutor flown in from the capital, and behind them, through the tall doors, I could hear the sound of a courtroom filling up.

The warrants named three men. The charges ran to eleven pages. Levee destruction. Fraudulent conveyance. Claims fraud. Conspiracy across three generations of signatures, and every signature matched a page in a sealed church vault beneath a limestone arch.

Inside, they seated us in the front row. Teodor wore his one good shirt with the sling hidden under a jacket and sat like a man guarding a door. Juno had her logbook on her knees, tabbed and flagged, ready to be called. Sable had braided her hair tight and sat with her spine straight, watching everything, missing nothing, the way she always did.

And when the clerk read the names of the founders, and the three of them rose in their expensive coats with their lawyers murmuring around them like attendants, I felt it happen—the thing I'd been chasing for two years without knowing it was a thing you could chase.

The weight lifted. Not all at once. But the arithmetic changed. The number that had followed me through every kitchen-table meeting and every hazard run and every sleepless calculation stopped being mine. It transferred, in that moment, onto the men in the expensive coats, where it belonged, where it had always belonged, and the marshal stepped forward and the courtroom went quiet and I breathed out forty years of someone else's drowning.

Afterward, on the steps, blinking in the blue light, Juno put her hand on my shoulder.

"They'll want statements," she said. "Days of them. Weeks maybe."

"I know."

"And then what? You've got a bounty coming and no debt. First time in years you're free." She squinted at the horizon, where, far off over the flats, new clouds were already stacking themselves up, white and towering and patient. "So what's the plan, guide?"

I looked at the storm building on the edge of the world, and I found I was smiling.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said. "Right now I want to go find a boat worth buying."
