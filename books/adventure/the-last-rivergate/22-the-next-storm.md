## 22. The Next Storm

The truck sold for less than it was worth and more than it deserved.

Petra stood in the gravel lot behind Harrow Ford's only dealership and watched a man named Culpepper—wide through the shoulders, narrow through the imagination—count out bills against the hood of the Silverado. The truck had carried her through eleven years of storm seasons, through hail that sounded like God throwing gravel, through floodwater up to the door handles twice, once on purpose. It had died under her at the end of a chase run with lightning still hanging in the air like a held breath, and she had hated it a little ever since, the way you hate anything that fails you when you can least afford it.

But standing there now, watching Culpepper's thick fingers fold the money into her palm, she found she couldn't hate it anymore. The truck had gotten her to Crooked Bend. To the launch. To the Rivergate. Everything after that, it had no part in.

"Runs rough when it's cold," she said, because some part of her still believed in honest dealing even when the other party had lowballed her by four hundred dollars.

"Everything runs rough when it's cold," Culpepper said cheerfully, and drove it away in first gear, grinding something that used to be a transmission.

The bills made a soft brick in her jacket pocket. Not enough to matter, in the old arithmetic. Enough to matter enormously, in the new one.

Because for the first time since she was twenty-three years old, Petra Underhill owed nobody anything.

She stood in the empty lot a while longer, testing the thought the way you test a tooth that's stopped hurting—pressing on it, waiting for the ache, finding none. The Vane Syndicate was gone. Its founders were in federal custody awaiting trial, its ledgers sat in evidence lockers, its cheerful men had scattered like roaches when the kitchen light came on. Doss Merrick himself had been arrested at the fuel dock in Harrow Ford, and when they'd cuffed him he'd looked at Petra across the parking lot with an expression she was still deciding how to feel about—not hatred, not quite. Something closer to professional respect, as if she'd finally played a hand well enough to be worth playing against.

*You know what the funny thing is,* he'd said, before they put him in the car. *I never once thought you'd do it. And I never once thought you wouldn't.*

She hadn't known what to say to that. She still didn't.

The bounty for testimony wasn't a bounty, officially. It was "relocation assistance" and "cooperation compensation," and it came as a check from a government office with a name so bland it had to be fictional. But it cleared. It cleared, and it was more money than Petra had seen in one place since her father's estate had been eaten by creditors, and she had spent exactly one night staring at it on the motel desk before she knew what it was for.

Not land. Not a house. Not the clean slate everyone assumed she'd want.

A boat.

* * *

She found it forty miles downstream of Harrow Ford, hauled out on cribbing in a yard that smelled of creosote and river mud, and she knew within thirty seconds of seeing it that the search was over. Thirty-two feet of welded aluminum, deep-veed and heavy-boned, built originally as a survey vessel for a mining concern that had gone bust before it ever surveyed anything. It had a cabin you could stand up in, a deck built to carry weight, twin engines old enough to be honest about their limitations, and a hull scarred all down one side where something—the listing said "contact with submerged object"—had tried and failed to open it.

"It takes water in the forward compartment if you push it past eighteen knots in a chop," the yard owner said, scratching his jaw. "Weld's good but the frame's tired there."

"I'll have it re-welded."

"That's a month of work. Maybe six weeks."

"I've got time." She said it and then heard what she'd said, and had to turn away and look at the river for a moment, because she did have time. For the first time in her adult life, she had time. Time was the thing she'd been selling, piece by piece, season by season, to the Vanes and their cheerful arithmetic, and now here it was, sitting in her hands like water, running through them faster than she could hold it but hers, all of it, hers.

She named it nothing for two weeks. Boats needed names and she knew it, but every name she tried on felt like a costume. Then Juno came down to look at the re-weld work, stood on the deck with her hands in her pockets, and said, "You could call it the *Elias*," and Petra said, "No," too fast, and Juno said, "Yeah, I figured," and neither of them mentioned it again. Three days later, alone at the dock at dusk, Petra painted *Last Call* on the transom in letters the color of storm clouds, and if anyone asked—and Sable asked, immediately—it meant whatever they wanted it to mean.

Juno's survey proposal had come in the mail the same week as the settlement check, typed on actual paper because Juno distrusted anything that couldn't be burned. *Baseline hydrological survey, upper basin, five months, three crew positions.* At the bottom, beneath her signature, she'd written: *Position one is yours if you want it. I'm not asking as a favor. I'm asking because you're the best pilot on this water and we both know it.*

Petra had read it four times. On the fourth reading she understood that Juno had buried the real message in the middle: *I'm not asking as a favor.* Meaning: this isn't charity. Meaning: you don't owe me. Meaning: we are done with debts, you and I, all of us, forever.

She signed it the same day.

Teodor took position two without hesitation and immediately began a campaign to install a proper galley, which he won, because Teodor always won arguments conducted entirely in food. He cooked the commissioning dinner on the dock—river fish in a crust of cornmeal and something he refused to identify, greens wilted with vinegar, cornbread that Sable ate four pieces of while claiming she wasn't hungry—and halfway through, he raised his tin cup and said, "To the ones who aren't here," and they drank, and nobody said Elias's name, and everybody thought it.

Sable took position three over her own mother's objections, which took some doing, since Sable's mother had surfaced in Harrow Ford two weeks after the magistrate's ruling, full of tears and explanations and a new husband. Sable had listened to all of it with her arms crossed, the way she listened to engines diagnose themselves, and then said, "I already got a family. You're late." Petra hadn't heard it happen—Sable told her about it later, on watch, in the flat voice she used for things that mattered—but knowing it happened was like a coal in her chest, warm and terrible both. The girl had chosen them. Deliberately. The way Petra had once chosen a debt, and never once regretted either choice since.

They loaded the boat through the last week of dry season. Survey gear in the hold: current meters, sediment corers, a sonar unit Juno had talked a university into lending on the strength of her rediscovered reputation. Water tanks, fuel drums, Teodor's galley, which weighed as much as a person and was treated with more care. Charts of the upper basin, including—folded separately, kept in a waterproof sleeve in Petra's cabin—a chart of the valley above the arch, drawn in Elias Wrenn's careful dying hand, annotated in his cramped script: *soundings unreliable below the gate; trust the water, not the paper.*

The state had sealed the valley. Memorial status, pending legislation. No public access, no salvage, no development—the Syndicate's remaining lawyers had fought it and lost, spectacularly, in a hearing that lasted eleven minutes. But Juno's survey permit covered the watershed entire, and the boundary line ran just below the arch, and Juno had looked at Petra across the chart table and said, "We're not going in. But we can anchor below the gate and take readings, and the readings will be true, and maybe that's enough. Maybe that's what he'd want. The valley keeping its records, and us keeping ours."

And Petra, who had sealed a vault and lied to a crew and testified to a magistrate and paid a debt in the only currency that had ever really counted, had said yes. Because some truths were heavier than debts, and some were lighter than grief, and the difference between them was mostly a matter of weather.

* * *

They cast off from Harrow Ford on a morning so clear it felt like an accusation.

Dry season still held. The river ran low and glassy-green between banks bleached pale, exposing roots and old pilings and the ribs of boats other people had loved and lost. Petra took the helm alone for the first hour, letting the others sleep, feeling the *Last Call* settle into her stride beneath her hands—the way the hull spoke through the wheel, the particular vibration of each engine, the small corrections the current asked for and received. A boat was a truck's opposite in every way that mattered. A truck fought the ground. A boat negotiated with the water. You didn't win against a river. You reached terms.

By midmorning the others were up. Sable claimed the engine compartment and stayed there, emerging at intervals with grease on her forearms and reports delivered in her flat teenage deadpan—"port engine's running rich, adjusting the carb, don't make it weird"—and Petra didn't make it weird. Teodor produced lunch from nowhere, as he did. Juno stood at the bow rail most of the day with a notebook, logging banks and inflows, occasionally calling depths back to the helm in a voice that had lost the apology it used to carry. The disgraced hydrologist was disgraced no longer; the papers had printed her name next to words like *instrumental* and *key witness*, and Juno wore the rehabilitation lightly, like a coat she kept forgetting she had on.

They anchored the first night in a cut below an old loading ramp, and Petra took first watch, and the river moved past in the dark making its endless argument with itself, and she stood at the rail and let it argue.

This was the thing nobody warned you about, coming out the far side of a fight like theirs. Not peace. Peace implied an ending, and nothing ended; the trial would grind on for a year, the valley would wait decades for its legislation, Elias was dead and would stay dead, and the ledger of all of it balanced to nothing anyone could spend. What came instead was motion. Forward motion, unowned, unbought. Nobody upstream owned her mornings now. Nobody held paper against her future. When the sun came up tomorrow she would point the bow wherever the work pointed it, and the work pointed upriver, toward the basin, toward the gate, toward everything they'd survived and everything they'd saved.

It wasn't freedom the way the word was sold in magazines. It was smaller than that and harder-won. It was the absence of the leash, and the discovery that you could still feel its weight long after it was gone, and the slow daily work of forgetting it.

On the third day they passed the mouth of the Wrenn Fork, and nobody said anything, but everybody looked.

On the fifth day they rounded the long bend below the gate country, and there it was.

The arch stood against the afternoon sky exactly as she remembered it, limestone gone gold in the low sun, spanning the gorge like a sentence the river had been trying to finish for forty years. Below it, the valley lay quiet under its memorial silence, the drowned town's rooftops breaking the receded waterline—more of them visible now, in the dry season, whole streets of them, a town exhaling. From this distance it looked peaceful. From this distance it always would. That was the mercy and the lie of distance both.

They anchored below the gate as permitted, and Juno took her readings, and the instruments hummed and recorded, and the numbers that scrolled across her screen were true, and Petra watched her write them down and thought: *this is what he wanted. Not the vault opened. The record kept.*

They stayed two days. On the second evening, Sable climbed to the top of the cabin with a cup of tea and sat beside Petra at the rail, legs dangling, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they were past needing to.

"You ever think about going back up there," Sable said eventually. "Past the line. Just to look."

"Every day."

"Yeah." Sable sipped her tea. "Me too. But it's better as a thing we don't do. Like—" she searched for it—"like how you don't dig up a grave to check somebody's still in it. You just know they are."

Petra looked at her—at this kid who'd fixed a pump on a gravel bar, carried double over a portage, steered a boat through gunfire under an arch while grown men shot at her, and chosen trust once, deliberately, and been right—and felt the coal in her chest bank itself down to something she could carry for the rest of her life.

"Elias would've liked you," she said.

"He did like me. He told me I was the only adult on the boat." Sable grinned, quick and crooked. "He wasn't wrong."

* * *

They were three days downriver from the gate, working a wide braided reach where the survey grid required slow transects back and forth, when Sable came up from the engine compartment, wiped her hands, and pointed west without a word.

Petra followed the gesture and felt something in her chest unlock—that old recognition, older than debt, older than fear, older than the Syndicate and everything it had built on drowned ground.

Clouds. Building hard over the western mesa country, towers of them, cauliflower-headed and anvil-topped, going up in the superheated afternoon air like smoke from a fire the earth was finally allowed to have. Dry-season storms were rare and violent and short-lived, and this cell had the look of one that meant business: a greenish cast under the base, the light going strange and metallic the way it did before hail, a faint brown veil of dust kicking up along the gust front where the outflow was already eating the desert.

Juno appeared with her notebook, squinting, calculating. "That front hits the headwaters by tonight. We'd get a rise of—" she flipped pages— "a foot, maybe eighteen inches, twelve hours behind the rain. Nothing dangerous down here. But the tributaries will flash."

"How long do we have before the gust front reaches us?"

"Two hours. Maybe three."

Petra stood at the wheel and looked at the storm for a long time.

There was a version of her, not so long ago, for whom that cloud tower had been a paycheck. Chase runs across the flats, hazard pay, lightning walking the ground close enough to taste the ozone, the truck dying under her with the sky still charged. She had chased storms because she had to, because the Syndicate's arithmetic demanded it, because every mile driven toward danger was a dollar against the debt. The storm had never been the point. The storm had been the invoice.

And there was another version of her, further back, seventeen years old in her father's boat on a rising river, before any of it, before the Vanes and the Valley and the vault—who had chased storms for no reason at all except that they were the most alive thing the world ever showed her, and she had wanted to be near that aliveness, wanted it the way other people wanted music or God.

Both versions were in her, standing at the wheel. The one who'd been bought. The one who'd been free.

"Petra," Juno said carefully, "we should run for the lee of that bluff. Standard practice, with a cell that size coming downwind—"

"We will. After."

"After what?"

"After I get a look at it." She turned the wheel, and the *Last Call* came around in a long easy arc until the bow pointed west, into the falling light, into the path of the gust front, into the brown dust-veil and the green-black base and the flicker starting somewhere inside it like a thought forming. "Log a course correction. Heading two-seven-zero. We'll run out to meet the outflow, take our readings on the leading edge, and then we'll run ahead of it to the bluff and anchor in the lee. Sable—I'll want both engines breathing right before we do this. Teodor—batten the galley."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about a family built in the fire instead of born in the blood: nobody argued, because everybody understood. Sable dropped back into the engine compartment already talking to the port carburetor in her low coaxing voice. Teodor began lashing his kingdom down with the efficiency of a man who had fled three countries and knew exactly what you could save and what you couldn't. Juno wrote the heading in her notebook, and then, beneath it, in her small precise hand, she wrote something else, and showed it to Petra without saying it aloud:

*For fun?*

"For science," Petra said, grinning, and pushed the throttles forward.

The *Last Call* rose onto plane and ran west into the falling light, and the storm came east to meet her, and the space between them closed the way it always closes, steadily, inevitably, the way a river closes on the sea.

The wind arrived first—a wall of cool air rolling off the mesas, smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet and dust that had, ruffling the water to whitecaps in seconds, bending the cottonwoods along the bank all one direction like grass. The light went copper, then bruise-purple. Somewhere behind the cloud base, thunder cleared its throat, patient, enormous.

Petra stood at the wheel with the wind tearing at her hair and the whole burning architecture of the storm filling the windshield, and she laughed out loud, alone, where nobody could hear her, because it was all still there. All of it. The awe, the terror, the electric animal joy of being small and moving and *here*, on the water, in the path of the biggest weather the sky could build. They could take your truck and your credit and your years. They could drown a valley and burn a map and put a price on your future. They could not, it turned out, take this. This had never been on any ledger. This had never belonged to anyone but her.

She eased the throttles back, turned the bow northeast, and ran for the bluff with the storm roaring at her heels like a living thing that had recognized her and let her go—for now, this season, this run.

Behind them, the first rain hit the river in great gray curtains, marching downstream, chasing them home the long way.

Sable climbed up beside her at the wheel, ducking the wind, and looked back at it.

"Same time next year?" the kid asked.

Petra checked the chart, set the heading, and answered the only way that mattered:

"Every season. As long as the water runs."
