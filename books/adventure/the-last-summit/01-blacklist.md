## 1. Blacklist

The permit office in Puerto Andino closes at noon, and by twelve-fifteen I'm already at the bar of the Cantina del Farol, which is either discipline or a disease, depending on who you ask. I ask the bartender, Tomás, who has known me for three years and still can't decide.

"Rejected again?" he says, setting down the glass before I've asked for anything. That's the kind of regular I am — my order precedes me, like a reputation.

"Rejected again." The clerk didn't even look up this time. He stamped the form with the enthusiasm of a man swatting flies and slid it back across the counter, and I read the word DENEGADO through a red stamp that had gone slightly crooked, as if even the bureaucracy was tired of denying me. Fourth permit this season. Fourteenth since the blacklist. I'd stopped reading the reason line years ago because it never changes: *applicant ineligible per Resolution 4471-C.* A number. That's what I am to the ministry now. Not a name, not twenty years of routes logged and clients carried home safe — a number on a denial stamp.

Tomás pours. "You could appeal."

"You could marry the widow Delgado," I say. "Same odds."

He laughs, because he's required to, then goes back to polishing glasses that don't need it. The cantina is empty except for an old man playing dominoes against himself in the corner and a dog asleep under table four like he's paying rent. Outside, the border town does its afternoon business — smugglers' trucks idling at the crossing, tour buses disgorging Germans who want their mountains pre-packaged, a kid selling lottery tickets with the conviction of someone who has already seen the winning numbers and knows they aren't mine.

I count my debts instead of drinking slowly. This is a habit now, like checking knots twice. There's the room behind the ferretería, two months behind. There's Tomás's tab, which he swears doesn't exist but which I keep in a notebook anyway, because a person should know exactly what she owes and to whom; it's the only ledger I've ever kept honestly. There's the gear in the storage locker that the owner keeps threatening to auction — ropes I trust more than most people, a tent that has stood up to weather that would make grown men weep, crampons with more miles on them than some trucks. All of it held hostage against money I don't have.

And there's the other debt, the one that isn't measured in pesos. Three years ago a client named Marcus Webb died on a mountain whose name I won't say out loud in public anymore, though privately I say it every night, the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts. It always hurts. The inquiry found negligence. The press found a villain, conveniently female and conveniently foreign enough. The guild found a scapegoat and struck my name from the rolls with the same efficiency the ministry uses on permits. Whether they were right — whether I chose the summit over the storm warning, whether Marcus Webb would be alive if I'd turned us around at the col — that question lives in me like a splinter worked so deep into the flesh that removing it would take half my hand with it.

The old man in the corner loses a domino match to himself, curses, resets the board. Even he gets to start over daily.

I'm halfway down the glass when she comes in.

I noticed her first because of how she entered, which is to say badly — hesitating in the doorway like the light itself might charge admission, scanning the room with the expression of a woman looking for something she's been told no longer exists. Black dress, good quality, older than mourning by a few seasons. Silver hair pinned up with the severity of a woman who made decisions about her appearance decades ago and saw no reason to revisit them. She crossed the cantina floor past the empty tables, past the dog, past me, and stopped when she reached my corner, and I knew — before she said a word, before Tomás's rag went still — exactly why she was there.

Because everyone comes to me eventually now. Not for my skills. For my price. Or rather, my lack of one. A disgraced guide will do things a licensed guide won't, and everyone in this town knows it, and most of them are decent enough to be ashamed of asking.

"Beatrix Villalobos," she said. Not a question. She pronounced it carefully, all five syllables, as though she'd practiced.

"Depends who's asking."

"Inés Solano." She pulled out the chair across from me without waiting for permission, which I respected immediately. "You were recommended."

"By whom?" Nobody recommends me. People warn others about me, which is a form of advertising, I suppose, but nobody *recommends* —

"By a man in the capital who owed me a favor and no longer wishes to be associated with this conversation." She folded her hands on the table. Her knuckles were large, working hands despite the dress — hands that had kneaded bread or washed floors or done whatever widows do to survive between the death and the paperwork. "So we will not speak his name either. There seems to be a great deal of not-speaking going around this country."

Tomás drifted over. She ordered coffee, black, and when it came she wrapped both hands around the cup like it was January, though it wasn't.

"Señora Solano—"

"I'll come to it quickly, because I know how you people charge by the hour." A flicker of humor, dry as chalk dust. Then it was gone, packed away, and what remained was something rawer. "Three months ago my husband led six men up the Río Blanco valley. Into the reserve. The closed reserve — yes, that one, don't bother telling me what the signs say. He had permits that were issued and then revoked, in writing, within eleven days. The government's position, stated in three letters from three different ministries, each letter contradicting the others, is that no such expedition exists. That no Aurelio Solano filed any application. That my husband is not missing, because he was never anywhere, because none of it happened."

She drank her coffee like medicine.

"The men who drove him to the trailhead have signed statements saying they drove him nowhere. His bank records show withdrawals that the bank says it cannot confirm. One of the mothers of the six young men received a visit from a government man who explained, very gently, over tea, what happens to families who ask questions. She stopped asking. I am not her."

I set down my glass. "What do you want from me?"

"To find him. Or find what happened. I'm not particular anymore about which." She reached into her bag and produced an envelope, thick, bound with a rubber band gone brittle. "Coordinates. His last radio transmission, transcribed by the operator who shouldn't have kept it but did. Maps — real ones, not the ones where the upper valley has been left blank out of official modesty. And this." A photograph, creased white at the folds: seven men grinning into sun too bright for them, gear that looked borrowed, a hand-painted sign behind them reading *EXPEDICIÓN RÍO BLANCO* with the second O drawn as a smiley face. My husband drew it, she said, or her face said, though her mouth had moved on to other words.

"They say he was a dreamer. Aurelio. They say he chased ghosts. But my husband did not chase ghosts. He chased things that other people had paid to make disappear, and there is a difference, and the difference is buried up that valley, and I need someone to go get it. Or him. Or the truth, which may be the same thing, or may be worse."

The envelope sat on the table between us like a live thing.

"I can't guide legally," I said. "You know that. Everyone in this town knows that. If I set foot in that reserve, I'm not just trespassing, I'm — "

"A criminal. Yes. As opposed to your current situation, which is what, exactly? Respectable poverty?" Her eyes were sharp, unkind in the way honest things are unkind. "Señora Villalobos. I have made inquiries. I know what you are. I also know what you were, before. Twenty years of routes. A reputation for getting people home. I know about the Webb thing too, before you flatter yourself that I don't. I know what they say you did and I know what you say you did and I notice those are not the same story. I don't care which one is true. I care that you're the only guide in two hundred kilometers who will say yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find someone less careful and he'll die up there with my husband's bones, and I'll bury two griefs instead of one." She said it without drama, the way you report weather. "But I don't think you'll say no. Because I watched you count your debts while you waited for me. You think you were being subtle. You weren't. You counted them the way a drowning woman counts the distance to shore."

The dominoes clicked in the corner. The dog sighed. Somewhere outside a truck hit its horn at nothing, at the whole town.

"How much?" I asked, because I was going to say yes, and we both knew it, and the only dignity left to me was pretending the decision was economic.

She named a figure. I laughed — actually laughed, a sound Tomás later claimed he'd never heard from me — because it was absurd, it was a year of guiding compressed into a single number, it was the storage locker and the tab and the room behind the ferretería and then some, it was everything I owed plus a margin I hadn't seen since before Marcus Webb fell. Half up front, in cash, tomorrow.

"There's a condition," she said.

Of course there was. "Which is?"

"My son goes with you."

I stared at her. "No."

"He hasn't asked yet. He'll say no when I tell him, loudly, possibly with furniture involved. But he'll come. Elias blames his father for everything — for the money Aurelio spent, for the obsession, for the way his mother grew small waiting. He intends to drag that expedition home by force, whoever's force it takes. He's twenty-six and angry and strong and completely unsuited to high altitude, and he will follow you up that valley whether you agree to it or not, because he's mine, and stubbornness skips a generation in our family, or perhaps it doesn't skip at all." She finished the coffee. "Better he goes with someone who knows what the mountain does to angry people. I'd prefer that someone be you."

"Why me? Truly. Past the money."

Inés Solano looked at me for a long moment, and in that moment I understood that she had rehearsed this answer too, and discarded it, and chosen honesty instead because she'd run out of time for anything else.

"Because a woman who has already lost everything once is the only kind of guide worth hiring for a job like this," she said. "Everyone else still has something to protect. You'll do what needs doing. That frightens me. It also reassures me, and I've learned to hold both at once."

She stood, buttoned her coat, left money on the table for coffees neither of us finished properly. At the door she paused, silhouetted against the hard border-town light.

"Tomorrow morning. The house on Calle Obispo, the blue one. Bring nothing but yourself and your gear list. And Señora Villalobos — the river keeps what it takes. Go and make it give some of it back."

Then she was gone, and the envelope sat there, and Tomás appeared with the bottle and refilled my glass without being asked, which he had never done in three years, and looked at the door, and looked at me, and said:

"So. The Río Blanco."

"Yeah," I said, and drank. "The Río Blanco."

Outside, evening was coming down off the peaks, and somewhere upstream, in a valley closed by decree and erased by paperwork, a river ran cold and patient toward a town full of people who had agreed, for money or fear, to forget where it began. Tomorrow I would start walking against it.

Tonight I finished the drink, paid my tab — all of it, every peso in the notebook — and started counting forward for the first time in three years.

It didn't feel like hope. It felt like a rope coming taut.
