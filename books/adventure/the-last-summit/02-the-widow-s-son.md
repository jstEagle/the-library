## 2. The Widow's Son

I spent the morning of my departure buying the mountain's opinion of me one item at a time.

The hardware man in Puerto Ceniza wouldn't sell rope to a blacklisted guide — not the good rope, anyway. He sold me the coil from behind the counter, the one with a faded price tag and a story he didn't bother hiding: *returned by a party that turned back*. I bought it. I bought the stove with the dented base, the fuel that smelled like it had wintered in someone's garage, the boots two sizes too large because they were the only pair in stock that hadn't been resoled for tourists. Every transaction came with a look. Not hostility exactly. Pity has its own particular flavor, and this town served it generously.

"You're really going up there," said the woman at the provisions stall, weighing rice like she was weighing my chances.

"I'm really going."

"Alone?"

"Apparently not." I'd told Inés Solano I worked better solo. She'd smiled the way grandmothers smile when children insist they can pour their own milk, and said her son would be joining me at the trailhead. Her son. She'd said it the way you'd say *the weather will do what it does*.

So I provisioned for three weeks instead of four, because a second mouth changes the math, and I provisioned cheap, because a fortune promised is not a fortune held. I've been stiffed before. Once by an oil company that wanted a route surveyed into a condor nesting ground; once by a man who paid his deposit in a currency that collapsed before we reached basecamp. Debts are patient animals. They wait by your bed each morning and ask what you've brought them.

The Río Blanco trailhead sat at the end of a road that gave up pretending to be a road about six kilometers early. I hired a truck as far as it would go and walked the rest, pack riding heavy on shoulders that remembered every kilometer of routes I was no longer permitted to guide. There's a particular grief in walking toward a mountain you know intimately and being unable to say so out loud. I knew the Río Blanco country the way you know a face in old photographs — the bend where the canyon narrows, the scree field that sings underfoot, the moraine where the light goes strange at four in the afternoon. Four years since I'd been allowed near it. The mountains hadn't missed me. That was the humiliating part. They don't miss anyone.

He was waiting where the trail left the river, and I saw him before I saw anything else about him, because he was standing wrong.

Elias Solano stood with his weight forward, arms crossed, planted on the rock like a man guarding a door rather than starting a journey. Late twenties, maybe thirty. City clothes worn once and never again — the jacket still had a crease down the sleeve where it had lived folded in a drawer. His pack was new, expensive, and packed by someone who had read about mountaineering rather than done it. Sleeping bag strapped outside where it would get soaked. Water bottles loose. No rope visible anywhere on him.

"Beatrix Villalobos," he said. Not a greeting. An identification, like a customs officer reading a passport he already intended to stamp *denied*.

"Elias."

"My mother says you're the best guide in the province."

"Your mother is generous."

"She also says you got a man killed." He said it flatly, no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty you can answer. Fact just sits there between you, breathing.

"That's the official version," I said. "It's also roughly accurate."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, maybe, that I hadn't defended myself. People expect the disgraced to argue. We make such good theater of our innocence.

"And yet she hired you."

"And yet."

He looked past me then, up the canyon, where the river ran white over stones the color of old bone. When he spoke again his voice had changed registers — lower, more careful, the voice of a man handling something fragile against his will. "My father led eleven people into that valley eight months ago. The government says seven. The government says the expedition was geological survey. The government says a great many things." He turned back to me. "I'm not here to find evidence. I'm not here for truth or justice or whatever my mother thinks is buried up there. I'm going to find my father and bring him home, and if he won't come, I'll carry him. And you are going to get me there alive, because that's what I'm paying you for, and it's the only thing I'm paying you for."

"Understood," I said.

"Do you understand? Because guides have opinions. My mother warned me you'd have opinions."

"I always have opinions. You're paying for my judgment, which occasionally agrees with them." I shrugged my pack higher. "First opinion: repack your bag. Your sleeping bag goes inside, dry. Second opinion: those boots will destroy your feet by tomorrow night, and blistered feet on day two becomes dead weight on day five. There's a shop back in town—"

"I'm not going back to town."

"Of course you're not." I set my pack down, opened it, and dug out the tape, the needle, the moleskin, the spare laces — the small inventory of a life spent fixing other people's mistakes at altitude. "Then we start with your feet. Sit."

To his credit — and I want to note this, because it became important later — he sat.

* * *

We made twelve kilometers the first day, which was ambitious, and I let him believe it was my ambition rather than his stubbornness. The trail followed the river through a gorge so narrow the sky became a rumor. Elias walked well enough for a city man — better than well, actually, with a rhythm that suggested his body remembered things his biography didn't mention. I filed that away.

He talked the way people talk when they're trying not to think. About the capital, where he taught engineering at a university that had recently asked him to stop teaching certain subjects. About his mother, whom he described with a tenderness wrapped in exasperation, like wire around fruit. About his father, Tomás, whom he described not at all, except in the negative spaces — the absences, the expeditions, the maps spread across the dinner table like a rival family.

"He was gone eleven months of the year, some years," Elias said finally, when the silence demanded tribute. "When he was home, he wasn't home. He was rehearsing leaving."

"That's a hard way to love someone."

"It's not love. It's a man who loved a river more than us, and everyone pretended otherwise, including him, especially him." He kicked a stone off the trail with unnecessary force. "And now my mother has given everything we have left to a stranger to go find him, and I'm supposed to be grateful."

"Nobody's asking you to be grateful."

"No. You're asking me to walk."

"Yes," I said. "That part of the job description is non-negotiable."

We camped where the gorge opened onto the first broad valley, and I watched him fail to pitch his tent with the quiet dignity of a man refusing help, and then accept help with the quieter dignity of a man whose fingers were numb. Over the stove he asked the question I'd been waiting for since the trailhead, asked it sideways, watching the flame instead of me.

"The client. The one who died. What happened?"

I could have lied. It occurred to me, briefly, how easy it would be — a weather story, a tragedy, poor Marco Reyes, gone too soon. But I'd learned something in four years of drinking alone in cantinas: the lie is heavier than the truth. You carry it uphill. It gets no lighter with altitude.

"His name was Marco," I said. "Twenty-six. First big climb. He wanted the summit so badly it was like watching someone hold their breath. We had a window, and the window closed, and the storm warning came at two in the afternoon, and I made the call to push for the top because we were ninety minutes out and the mountain owed me nothing and I decided I was smarter than the sky." I fed another twig to the stove. "We summited at 4:40. The storm hit at 4:50. Marco fell on the descent. Not because he was careless. Because I used up his margin spending mine."

Elias was quiet for a long time. The river talked to itself in the dark.

"They took my license," I went on. "They were right to. Not because I broke a rule — rules get broken every season and nobody dies. Because I confused the summit with the duty. The client doesn't pay for the peak. He pays for my judgment, and judgment means turning around, and turning around is the hardest thing a guide ever does, harder than any climb. I failed at the easiest hard thing there is." I looked at him across the fire. "So understand what you're hiring. If I say we turn back, we turn back. If I say we wait, we wait. If your father is up there and everything in me says go down, I'll say go down, and you can hate me for it, but you'll hate me alive."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll tie you to a tree and come back for you after the weather. It's been done before. The tree complained less than you will."

He almost smiled. It was the first thing about his face that looked like his mother's.

* * *

On the third day the valley narrowed again and the river changed its mind about being gentle. We crossed where an old flood had stacked the boulders into a rough staircase, water running fast and glass-green between them, cold enough to ache through boot leather. Elias went first because I made him — I wanted to watch how he moved when the mountain stopped cooperating.

He moved fine until the fourth boulder, which was slicker than it looked, which boulders always are, which is why you test them. His foot shot sideways and for one long half-second he was a man balanced entirely on hope, arms windmilling over green water moving at a speed that forgives nothing. I had his wrist before his brain finished deciding to panic. I hauled him back onto the stone and held him there while he found his breath, both of us crouched low, spray hitting our faces.

"Test every step," I said, close to his ear, over the water. "Every step. The river doesn't care that your father's up there. Neither does the mountain. Care is the only thing we bring that they don't already have."

He nodded, wet-faced, shaken, furious mostly at himself. But when we crossed the rest of it, he tested every step, and on the far bank he waited for me instead of walking on, which was new.

That evening, camped under the first real view of the high country — peaks coming up over the ridge like teeth — he sat beside my fire without being invited and said, "My father kept journals. Dozens of them. My mother burned most of them after he disappeared, or said she did. But there was one page I found tucked in a book, and I've been trying to decide whether to show you, because showing you makes all of this real."

"What did it say?"

He pulled a folded paper from inside his jacket, where he'd been keeping it dry the way I'd told him to keep the sleeping bag. He handed it over without letting go of one corner, the way you surrender a weapon.

The handwriting was small and precise, an engineer's hand. One line, written and struck through and rewritten beneath:

*The river remembers what we are forbidden to.*

Below it, a date from eight months ago. Below that, coordinates — higher than any survey point, higher than the mapped headwaters, up where the maps went blank and stayed blank on purpose.

"Higher than the source," I said.

"Or the source isn't where the maps say it is." Elias took the page back and folded it away. "Which means either my father was lost, or the maps are lying, and I've met the men who draw the maps." He looked up at the peaks going violet in the last light. "How far to these coordinates?"

"Four days. Five, if the weather votes."

"Then we walk fast."

"We walk smart," I said. "Fast is how people die up here. Smart is how they arrive."

He didn't argue. That frightened me more than arguing would have, because I recognized the silence. It was the silence of a man deciding that the summit — his summit, whatever shape it took — mattered more than the cost of reaching it. I'd heard that silence in my own head once, at two in the afternoon, with a storm building and ninety minutes to go.

Somewhere upstream, in a canyon the maps refused to name, voices were waiting that knew people's names. I didn't know that yet. All I knew was that the river ran white ahead of us into the dark, and that I had just agreed — silently, by walking beside him — to follow a grieving son into country that had already swallowed eleven men, and that the only thing I trusted less than my own judgment was how much I was beginning to want to prove it still worked.
