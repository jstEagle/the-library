## 3. Dead Air

The coordinates Inés Solano had pressed into my palm were written on the back of a church donation envelope, in her dead husband's handwriting, and they were wrong.

Not wrong enough to be useless. Wrong the way a drunk is wrong about where he lives — close enough to find the street, not the house. I'd spent two days reconciling them against my own maps of the Río Blanco drainage, cross-checking contour lines against the memory of a valley I'd only ever seen from the far side of a closed border. By the third morning I'd corrected them by four kilometers east and six hundred meters down, and when I showed Elias my work he looked at the envelope, then at me, with an expression I was beginning to recognize as his default setting: suspicion wearing politeness like a borrowed coat.

"My father wasn't sloppy," he said.

"No," I agreed. "He was being followed."

That shut him up for most of the morning, which I counted as a personal victory, though I'd have traded it for company that could carry on a conversation. Elias Solano walked like a man settling debts — shoulders forward, jaw set, each step an accusation against the trail itself. He carried too much gear because he didn't trust me to know what mattered, and he asked too few questions because he didn't trust himself to like the answers. We had been on the river three days and had exchanged perhaps forty sentences, half of which had been arguments about rope technique.

The Río Blanco ran milky beside us, glacier-fed and opaque, the color of bone ground fine. It's a strange thing, following a river you're not allowed to follow. The reserve boundary was a line on a map and a rumor on the ground — no fence this far up, just signs every few kilometers, shot through, rusted, their warnings worn down to punctuation marks. PROHIBIDO EL PASO. The government had closed the valley after whatever happened at San Rafael, and then it had closed the subject, and then it had closed the archives, and now the whole watershed sat behind its wall of silence growing wilder every year, the bridges rotting, the trails swallowing themselves.

Which meant, paradoxically, the best walking of my career. No permits, no clients, no liability waivers. Just me, a hostile widow's son, and a river running toward a truth somebody had paid a great deal of money to bury.

I tried not to think about how much that sounded like the kind of expedition that ends with someone's name on a memorial cairn.

* * *

We found the first sign of Tomás Solano's expedition on the afternoon of the fourth day: a tin cup, upright on a boulder, filled to the brim with rainwater.

Not dropped. Placed.

Elias picked it up and turned it over. There was a name scratched into the base — MERCADO — and beneath it, in different handwriting, smaller: *perdón.*

"Who's Mercado?" I asked.

"One of my father's men. A surveyor." Elias's thumb moved over the scratched word. "This isn't his cup."

"No," I said. "It's his apology."

I don't know why that bothered me more than anything else we'd found, but it did. People leave trash in the mountains all the time; the mountains are where people come to be careless. But nobody leaves an apology in a cup. An apology means someone knew something was coming. Or that something already had.

We camped that night under an overhang Elias found, and I lay awake listening to the river and doing arithmetic I didn't like. Six men had gone up this valley eleven weeks ago. The last radio transmission from Tomás Solano — the coordinates, the broken sentence Inés had played for me twice in her kitchen, her hands flat on the table as if holding down a map — had been nineteen days after departure. That left seven weeks unaccounted for. Seven weeks in which six experienced men had sent nothing, and from which, so far, one cup had returned.

"Your mother said your father was looking for the source," I said into the dark.

A long pause. Then, grudgingly, the way you'd pay out rope to someone falling: "That's what he told her."

"And what do you think he was looking for?"

Another pause, longer. "I think," Elias said finally, "that my father believed things were true before he checked whether they should be. And I think my mother mortgaged everything she had left to send him after one more thing that shouldn't be true but was." A breath. "So I'm going to go get him, and bring him home, and he can be right all he wants in his own kitchen."

It was the most honest thing either of us had said since the cantina. I decided not to ruin it by pointing out that nobody comes home from seven weeks of silence without a story, and stories like that don't fit in kitchens.

* * *

The coordinates resolved on the sixth day into a bench of flat ground above the river's confluence with a side creek, sheltered by a stand of aliso trees gone gold at the edges. From two hundred meters away I could see the tents, and from two hundred meters away I knew.

You develop an eye for it. A camp that's been struck looks like a camp that's been struck — stakes pulled, guy lines coiled, fire pit cold and scattered. A camp that's been abandoned looks different: things left mid-use, a boot by a door, a pot on stones. And a camp that's been *ended* looks like nothing else at all.

This one had been ended.

The tents stood — five of them, dome and tunnel styles, expedition-grade, the kind of money Inés had mortgaged herself into — but every one of them had been opened. Not torn by wind; slashed, long deliberate cuts from apex to ground, each tent peeled open like fruit. The contents were still inside, sorted into neat piles. Sleeping bags rolled. Stoves boxed. Food caches stacked and weighted under stones against animals.

Nobody loots a camp and then organizes it.

"Beatrix." Elias's voice had gone flat, which I was learning meant frightened. He was standing over the radio table. "Beatrix, look at this."

Three radios. Base station, two portables. All of them smashed — not dropped, not wet-failed, but smashed, methodically, each one beaten against the same rock until the casings split and the circuit boards glittered out like exposed nerve. The rock itself was stained dark in a way that rain hadn't fully washed.

And arranged around the radios, placed with the same terrible care as the piles and the cuts, were the batteries. Dozens of them, every one drained, laid out in rows. Counting them, I got to fifty-two before I stopped.

"They wanted to make sure," Elias said quietly. "Whatever they were making sure of."

I walked the perimeter while he went through the tents, because one of us needed to keep functioning and I've always been better at moving than thinking. The fire pit held ash from many fires, weeks of them. There was a latrine trench, properly dug, properly backfilled — disciplined men, right up until the end. On the trunk of the largest aliso, someone had carved a tally: groups of four strokes and a diagonal, row after row, seventy-one days' worth, and then the carving stopped mid-group, two strokes and nothing.

Day seventy-three, by my count against the dates, was when the last transmission should have gone out.

Except there was no last transmission. There was a transmission that ended mid-word — Inés had played it for me, Tomás's voice saying *hay gente en la quebrada, hay gente que—* and then static, and then nothing, and the government report calling it equipment failure in severe terrain.

Equipment failure doesn't slash five tents.

"Beatrix." Elias again, from the far tent. His voice had changed. "You need to see this."

* * *

It was a journal page, torn out, folded twice, and weighted under a stone in the center of the tent floor — placed, again, deliberately, the way you'd leave something for whoever came next. The paper was soft with damp. The handwriting was Tomás Solano's, tight and slanting, deteriorating across the page the way handwriting does when the hand is cold or afraid, and I suspected both.

Elias read it aloud, because neither of us suggested otherwise and because I think he needed to hear his father's voice, even secondhand, even in ink.

*"Day 68. The fog has not lifted from the canyon in nine days. Mercado says it is weather. I have stopped agreeing with him.*

*Last night the voices came again. They use our names. Not shouting — that would be easier. They say them the way you say a name at a funeral. Mercado heard his own name and would not sleep by the fire after. Ruiz swears it is echo, rockfall, wind through the narrows. But wind does not know that Paredes was born in Huaraz. Wind does not say 'Paredes de Huaraz, come home.'*

*I believe now that we were expected. I believe we have been watched since the confluence. I believe the source is guarded, and that the guards are not soldiers, though they move like them, and that they are the reason the valley was closed — not mining, not landslide, none of the reasons on the signs. Something happened here that the government buried, and something survived the burial, and it knows our names.*

*Ruiz wants to turn back. Mercado wants to push on. I am the leader and I cannot decide, because both of them are right. We came for the source of the river. I no longer know if the source is water.*

*If anyone finds this page: we are going up. All of us together, because splitting up is what the voices want. If we do not transmit again, it is not equipment failure. Tell Inés—"*

The page ended there. Torn.

Elias stood very still, holding it, and I watched a man discover that his father had been braver and more doomed than he'd spent years believing, and that both of those facts hurt in different places.

"He knew," Elias said finally. "He knew what he was walking into, and he went anyway, and he made Mom sell the house for it." His laugh came out wrong. "Voices in the canyon. Voices that know our names. What kind of—"

"Survivors," I said.

He looked at me.

"Think about it," I said, and my mouth was dry, because I'd been thinking about it since the cup, since the batteries, since the tally cut off mid-stroke. "Six men walk into a valley the government erased. Nobody comes out. But the tents weren't robbed, the food was left, the page was left for whoever followed. Soldiers who massacre a camp burn it. This was tidied. This was *kept.* Somebody wanted the next people to understand." I nodded at the journal page. "'They know our names.' You don't haunt people whose names you don't know. You warn them. And you can only warn people whose names you know if you've been here long enough to learn them — or if you were here first."

Elias looked down the valley, toward the white smear of the high country where the river came from, and I watched him arrive at the same arithmetic I'd done in the dark two nights ago: that somewhere upstream, the people who had ended this camp might still be there. That his father might still be there. That *voices that know our names* could mean ghosts, or could mean the living dead of something the world had been paid to forget.

"So we turn back," he said. It wasn't a question, exactly. It was a man testing whether he still owned the option.

"Do you want to?"

His jaw worked. He looked at the journal page, at the unfinished sentence — *tell Inés—* — and I saw him fold it along its old creases with hands that weren't quite steady, and put it inside his jacket, over his heart, like a letter.

"No," he said. "But I'm glad you asked."

We broke camp in twenty minutes, leaving the dead tents to the weather. As we climbed out of the bench toward the narrowing canyon, I glanced back once. The five slashed tents stood open to the sky in the gold light, patient as mouths.

And on the ridge above us — just for a moment, just long enough that I'd spend the rest of the day deciding I'd imagined it — a figure stood watching, still as the stone around it, and did not move until we looked directly at it, and then was simply gone, the way smoke is gone.

Elias hadn't seen it. I didn't tell him.

Some warnings, I figured, you deliver yourself.
