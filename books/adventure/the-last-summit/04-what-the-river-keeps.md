## 4. What the River Keeps

The river told us where to go, and the river lied about how hard it would be.

That was the thing I was learning about the Río Blanco. It looked like a promise from above — a bright thread stitched through the canyon, the kind of water they put on tourism posters — but up close it was all muscle and temper, cold enough to stop your heart if you fell in, loud enough that you had to shout to think. My father had followed this thread for thirty years of his life, off and on, and I was beginning to understand that following a river is not like following a road. A road wants you to arrive. A river just wants to keep going.

We'd been walking four days since basecamp, and Beatrix Villalobos had not said more than sixty words in any one of them. She moved like water herself, actually — around obstacles, over them, never wasting anything, never once looking back at me when I slipped, which I did often enough that I'd stopped keeping count out of self-respect. She'd point at a route with two fingers and keep walking. That was her whole vocabulary: *there*, and *not there*.

I hated it. I want to be clear about that. I hated her competence and her silence and the way she ate standing up, and most of all I hated that she was right about everything. When she said we'd need to cross at night because the glacial melt made the ford impassable by day, I argued for an hour and lost in eleven seconds when she walked me to the bank and showed me the water running brown and thick with rock flour, moving fast enough to bounce boulders the size of dogs.

So we waited for dark.

* * *

Night crossings are a special kind of stupid that only makes sense if you've done them. You can't see the bottom. You can't see the far side. All you have is a rope, a headlamp, and the sound of the water telling you, in no uncertain terms, what it thinks of your plan.

Beatrix rigged the line across a braided section where the river split into three channels around gravel bars, which she said cut the worst of the current. She went first, testing each placement, then came back for me. The stars were out in that brutal high-altitude way, so many of them the sky looked diseased, and the glacier upcanyon caught their light and threw it back down as a faint blue glow, like the mountain was dreaming.

"Stay angled upstream," she said. "Lean into it. If you fight it straight-on, it wins."

"I've crossed rivers before."

"Not this one." And there was something in her voice — not arrogance. Almost apology. "Nobody crosses this one twice the same way."

I went in at the first channel and it was fine, knee-deep, brutal cold but manageable. The second channel came to my thighs. By the third the river had decided it was tired of being polite.

I don't remember losing my footing. That's the part nobody tells you — there's no dramatic moment, no movie-slow tumble. One step the bottom was there; the next step it was a rumor. The current took me sideways and down before my brain finished forming the word *oh*. The pack filled instantly and became an anchor. The water wasn't cold anymore, it was nothing, it was a fact larger than temperature, and I remember thinking with absurd clarity: *my father drowned somewhere near here, metaphorically, for thirty years, and now I'm doing it literally, and Mom will get two funerals out of one man.*

Then the rope snapped taut and the world jerked sideways.

She'd braced it around a boulder and herself, and she hauled me in short, vicious pulls, not panicking, not shouting, just working, hand over hand, until my face broke the surface and I got a boot on stone and crawled out onto the gravel bar coughing up half the Río Blanco while she knelt beside me with her hand fisted in my jacket collar like I was a kitten she'd fished out of a drain.

"Breathe," she said. "Just breathe."

"I'm fine," I said, which is what everyone says, and we both knew it.

* * *

We didn't make the far bank that night. She wouldn't risk it after a swim, so we built a fire on the gravel bar, which felt illegal somehow, a little island of orange light in all that black rushing noise. She stripped my soaked outer layers, wrapped me in the emergency bivvy, and sat across the fire wringing out my socks with the expression of someone who has done this too many times for other people who said they were fine.

"You hesitated," she said finally. "At the third channel. You looked back at me instead of at the water."

"I was checking the route."

"You were checking whether I'd catch you." She fed the fire a stick. "That's how people die out here. Not from rivers. From outsourcing their survival."

"That's a charming philosophy. Did you learn it from the client you killed?"

The words were out before I could weigh them. Four days of silence and resentment and fear, and they came out sharp and cheap. She didn't flinch. That was almost worse. She just looked at me across the flames for a long time, and the firelight did something complicated with her face, and when she spoke her voice was flat and even, like she'd rehearsed this in front of a mirror for years.

"His name was Tomás Herrera. He was forty-six. He had two daughters, and every year he sent them postcards from whatever mountain he was on, and I know this because I read them, sometimes, when I can't sleep. He hired me to guide him up Cerro Tres Cruces. He was strong, cheerful, paid on time, and completely convinced he was going to summit, because I let him believe that."

The river roared. She talked under it, unhurried.

"There was a storm system coming up from the coast. Every forecast had it. The refugio radio confirmed it at midnight. We were camped at the col, twelve hours from the top, and I woke at three and watched the clouds building over Chile and I did the math every guide does — turn around and lose the window, or push and beat the front. Twelve hours. Maybe ten if we moved well. I wanted the summit. I'd turned back on that mountain twice before, and I was tired of telling clients stories about mountains I couldn't finish." She paused. "Tomás asked me if it was safe. I said the window would hold. It was even true, when I said it. Windows always hold right up until they don't."

"What happened?"

"We summited at 9:40 in the morning under a sky that still looked like a sky. The front arrived at 11:15. Whiteout, wind to a hundred and twenty, minus thirty with the chill. We should have been descending already. I pushed the turnaround too late because the top was *right there*, Elias, it was fifty meters away, and I have spent six years understanding that fifty meters is the exact distance between a guide and a murderer." Her hands were steady. Her voice was steady. Only her eyes weren't. "He fell on the descent. Roped to me. The anchor held. He fell forty meters and the rope went tight and I held him there, over the void, for eleven minutes, and then his harness failed. Or the rope burned through. The investigation couldn't say which. They said I chose the summit over the warning, and they were right, and the blacklist was the gentlest thing that happened to anyone involved."

I sat there dripping dry by a fire, alive because of her, having just been told she'd killed a man by wanting something too much.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you almost died tonight doing exactly what I did on Tres Cruces." She nodded at the river. "You knew the crossing was bad. You went anyway because you wanted to be across, and you thought the mountain would make an exception for you. Mountains don't make exceptions. Rivers don't. They just keep what you give them." She looked up at the glacier, blue and ancient in the starlight. "Your father understood that. It's why he's still up there, probably. Why he never came down."

"My father didn't die."

"No," she agreed quietly. "But he stopped coming home, years before he disappeared. There's a difference, and your mother knows it, and so do you."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth and found nothing in it. Because she was right, and we both knew that too — I'd spent the whole approach telling myself I was dragging him home, when really I was chasing the last version of him that existed before the river took his attention, his money, his letters, his eyes whenever he looked past us toward the cordillera.

"The wardens," I said. "You think they're real."

"I think voices in a canyon that know your names aren't ghosts." She poked the fire. "Ghosts don't slash tents. Ghosts don't take hostages. Someone up this valley remembers something, and my father—" she stopped, corrected herself, "—your father went looking for it on purpose."

"On purpose," I repeated.

"Think about it, Elias. A botanist doesn't fund eight years of expeditions to find a river source. The source of the Río Blanco has been on maps since before either of us was born — some glacier cirque, some coordinates, done. But the reserve above it has been closed since the massacre, closed so thoroughly that the government pretends the massacre didn't happen, and your father kept asking questions the government had paid to bury." She met my eyes across the fire. "He wasn't hunting glory. He was hunting evidence. And whatever he found, it was worth slashing tents over."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upcanyon, ice calved off the glacier with a sound like a rifle shot, and neither of us jumped, because we were both thinking the same thing, which was that the shot had come from the direction we were walking.

"Get some sleep," Beatrix said. "We cross at first light. And Elias—" she paused, and for a second she looked less like a blacklist legend and more like a tired woman on a gravel bar, "—when we find them, whatever we find, you don't hesitate at the third channel. Whatever it costs."

I lay back against my pack and stared up at the diseased, brilliant sky, and the river kept saying what it always says, which is nothing, forever, in the loudest voice in the world.

Above us, in the blue dark, something else was watching the fire. I didn't see it. But I felt it — the specific weight of being observed by someone who knows your name — and when I mentioned it to Beatrix in the morning, she was already gone from her bedroll, standing at the edge of the water, looking up at a figure on the far bank who had not been there yesterday.

A woman. Old, upright, unmoving, with a staff planted in the stones like she'd grown there.

"Come," she called across the roar, in a voice that carried like it owned the canyon. "Both of you. Before the river changes its mind."
