## 5. The Wardens

The rope came out of the dark like a judgment.

One moment Elias and I were picking our way through a canyon so narrow the river sounded like a crowd whispering behind stone, and the next there was a whistle — two notes, low, almost polite — and then the world went sideways. A weighted line snared my ankle and hauled. I went down hard on basalt, tasted blood and old iron, and by the time I'd spat, three figures had materialized out of the rock itself, as if the canyon had grown them.

They didn't shout. They didn't threaten. One of them — a broad man with a face like weathered adobe — simply took Elias's machete from his belt with the unhurried courtesy of a waiter clearing a plate, while another bound my wrists with a knot I recognized from a mountaineering manual and did not recognize from anywhere else. It was a guide's knot. Someone here knew ropes.

"Solano," Elias said, spitting dust. "We're looking for the Solano expedition."

The broad man looked at him for a long moment. "Everyone up this river is looking for something," he said. "That's why we take the looking away."

* * *

They marched us upstream through the night, blindfolded after the first hour, which I'll admit was a professional touch. You can't memorize a route you never see, and whoever ran this operation understood that better than most military outfits I've guided for. The trail under my boots climbed in long switchbacks, crossed the river four times on stones worn glassy by centuries of feet, and finally rose into air that smelled different — colder, cleaner, with that mineral bite you only get near ice.

When they took the blindfolds off at dawn, we were standing in a hanging valley I had no business being alive to see. Green meadows stitched with meltwater streams, walls of granite going up and up into cloud, and at the head of it all, half-hidden, a glacier hanging like a held breath. The source of the Río Blanco, or close enough to it that I felt the river's whole biography pressing down on me.

And scattered across the meadow: people. Maybe forty of them. Old men mending nets. Women sorting seed potatoes. Children who stared at us with the flat, unafraid appraisal of kids who have seen strangers arrive before and seen what happened to them.

"Not a camp," I murmured. "A village."

"There's no village here," said the broad man. "Not on any map. Not in any census." He said it without pride, the way you'd state the elevation. "The government says this valley is empty. We have found it useful to let them keep saying it."

Elias made a sound beside me, half laugh, half choke. "An entire town that doesn't exist."

"Entire towns that don't exist are the only kind that survive," the man said. "Come. She will want to see you."

* * *

She lived in a stone house at the meadow's edge, smaller than the others, with smoke curling from a chimney built at an angle that told me the builder had fought the wind and won. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke, dried herbs, and gun oil, an combination I found more honest than alarming.

Doña Rufina was small. That was my first mistake, expecting her not to be. She sat on a bench by the fire with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for bread to rise, and when she looked up at me, I felt the particular weight of being read by someone who has buried more people than she has planted.

"The guide," she said. "Beatrix Villalobos."

My spine went cold. "You know my name."

"I know many names." She nodded toward Elias. "His father's. His mother's. The names of eleven men who came up this river in March wearing red jackets and calling themselves scientists." Her eyes came back to mine. "I even know the name of the client you lost. Tomás Herrera. Forty-one years old. He fell on the north couloir of Cerro Espejo during a storm you were warned about."

The room tilted slightly. I put a hand on the doorframe. "Who told you that?"

"A dead man tells you nothing," she said. "But a living river tells you everything. Word travels upstream faster than water does, if you know how to listen. Sit down, guide. Both of you. What I have to say will go easier seated."

We sat. Elias's knee was bouncing; he pressed it still with his hand.

"You came looking for the expedition," Doña Rufina said. "Eleven men. Red jackets. Led by Andrés Solano." She watched Elias flinch at his father's name. "They are alive. All of them. They are also prisoners, which is a condition they volunteered for and then regretted, as men do."

"They're here?" Elias was on his feet before he finished the word. "Where? Take me to—"

"Sit down, boy. You've been sitting wrong your whole life; learn to do it right." There was no cruelty in it, only the authority of someone who has earned every syllable. He sat.

"They came in March," she continued, "with permits forged by a ministry official whose conscience caught fire too late. They told the valley they were surveying water quality. Your father, young man, is a terrible liar. By the fourth day he was asking about San Rafael."

The word landed in the room like a dropped stone. Even the fire seemed to lean toward it.

"San Rafael," I said carefully. "There's no record of—"

"No," she agreed. "There is no record. That is the entire point of what I am about to tell you, and once I tell you, you cannot un-tell yourself. So decide now whether you want to carry it."

Neither of us spoke. She took that as consent, because of course she did; she was the kind of woman who considered silence a signature.

"In 1989 there was a mining town at the foot of that glacier. San Rafael. Four hundred and six souls. The company wanted the mountain's copper, the government wanted the company's money, and the town wanted — well. The town wanted what towns want. To be left alone in their own lives." She unfolded her hands for the first time. "In November of that year, soldiers came to remove the obstruction. It took two days. When it was done, the mine shafts were collapsed with the dead inside some of them, the records were burned, the road was washed out in a convenient flood, and the maps were redrawn. Officially, San Rafael never existed. Officially, the reserve was created to protect 'pristine wilderness.'"

Her voice hadn't risen once. That was the worst part. It had the evenness of a story told so many times it had been polished smooth, like the crossing stones.

"And the survivors?" I asked, though I already knew.

"Walked up the river," she said. "Ninety-one of us reached this valley. The soldiers stopped at the tree line. Soldiers always stop at the tree line; they are issued courage by the meter and it runs out where the air thins." Something moved behind her eyes that might have been a smile in a harder life. "We buried our dead in the ice, because the ground here is thin and we could not dig deep enough for grief. And then we stayed. To remember. To guard. Because the evidence of what happened — the photographs, the ledger, the list of names — we hid it where only the mountain could keep it safe, and someone had to stay and make sure the mountain kept its promise."

"And my father," Elias said hoarsely, "came for it."

"Your father came for it," she confirmed. "He had spent nine years collecting what the burned archives left behind. Payroll records. A photograph of the town square taken the week before. Testimony from a company clerk who died believing his envelope would matter. He came to carry it out. To make the country look at what it paid to forget." She looked at him steadily. "He is a brave man. He is also a fool, because he believed the truth could simply walk out of these mountains in a backpack, and the last time truth tried that, it came back as soldiers."

"So you took them prisoner."

"We took their radios, their satellite transmitter, and their certainty," she said. "Prisoner is your word. Ours is *guests who may not leave*. Eleven men who now understand that the ledger stays on this mountain or everyone in this valley dies for it — the wardens, the children, the ones who already died once and cannot die again." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep lines of her face like contour lines on a map. "Now. Here is my question for you, Beatrix Villalobos, and think carefully, because I have heard what kind of guide you are."

I braced myself.

"Your reputation says you choose summits over people. That you were warned about a storm and went anyway, and a man named Tomás paid for your ambition." She said it plainly, without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "So I need to know: when the choice comes again — and on this mountain it always comes — which will you be? The woman who carries things out because the world demands it? Or the woman who understands that some things must never leave?"

Outside, the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic sound, like the mountain shifting its weight in its sleep. Somewhere in that ice lay a ledger with four hundred and six names in it, and somewhere between me and that ice lay eleven hostages, one desperate widow's hope, and a question I had been running from since Cerro Espejo.

"Doña Rufina," I said slowly, "what exactly would happen if someone tried to broadcast that ledger off this mountain?"

She went very still. For the first time since we'd entered her house, the old woman looked at me not as a prisoner, but as a problem.

"That," she said, "depends entirely on who is holding the transmitter."
