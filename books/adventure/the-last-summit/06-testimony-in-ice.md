## 6. Testimony in Ice

They came for us at dawn, which I suppose is the civilized hour for being taken prisoner.

Two wardens — the young one with the rifle slung loose like a fishing rod, and the old one who walked the way my father used to walk, like the ground owed him nothing — led Beatrix and me out of the warden camp and up a goat path that clung to the canyon wall by its fingernails. Nobody said where we were going. Beatrix, hands bound in front of her with the same kind of cord I'd seen her use to rig tarps, kept up a running commentary of glances that said, in her fluent language of eyebrows: *don't do anything stupid.*

I'd been planning to do something stupid since the night before. I was almost grateful for the rope.

The path climbed out of the river gorge into a world of gray stone and older gray stone, and then the glacier appeared above us, hanging in its cirque like something the mountain had swallowed and never finished digesting. The air had a smell I couldn't place at first — cold stone, wet iron, and underneath it something sweet and rotten, like a greenhouse left shut for years.

"Where are you taking us?" I asked, for the fourth time.

The old warden — Tomás, I'd learned, though he hadn't offered it; Beatrix had pried it out of him the way she pried everything — didn't turn around. "You asked to see what your father came to see."

"I asked where he was."

"You'll see both."

* * *

The cave mouth was hidden behind a curtain of icicles, and behind that, a slab of rock that two of them rolled aside with the ease of long practice. Inside, the cold was different — not the dead cold of altitude but a preserved cold, a cold that kept things, and I understood before anyone told me that this was the point, that everything in this cave existed because ice is the most honest archive there is. Ice doesn't take bribes.

They lit lanterns. And then I stopped understanding anything else for a while.

The cave went back farther than the light reached. Along its walls, in niches cut into the ice itself — cut, I realized, by hands, over years, decades — were the contents of other people's lives. Tin boxes. Oilcloth bundles. Photographs in waxed paper, dozens of them, laid out like the glacier was a family album.

Tomás lifted one out and held it into the lantern light, and I wished he hadn't.

A line of men against a mine headframe. Maybe forty of them. Day shift, by the light. Ordinary men in ordinary clothes, squinting, some of them smiling, one making a joke that had made the others laugh a half-second before the shutter fell. My father had a photograph almost exactly like it from the dam project — the crew, arms crossed, the foreman in the center. I'd looked at that photograph my whole childhood without seeing it, the way you look at anything that's always there.

"San Rafael mine," Tomás said. "October of the year the government says nothing happened."

"Nothing did happen," I said. "I looked. There's no record."

"There's no record," he agreed, and his voice was so level it made my teeth ache. "That is the record."

He turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in a dozen different hands, were names. Not the men in the photo — the men who'd written them. Wives. Mothers. Sons. A ledger of the missing, maintained by people who had no right to maintain anything, written on the only paper that couldn't be confiscated because it was already in their heads and on their walls of ice.

Beatrix had gone very still beside me. I'd learned in two weeks on the river that Beatrix had a whole vocabulary of stillness, and this one meant she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

Tomás set the photograph back in its niche. "Two hundred and six," he said. "We have names for a hundred and ninety-one. The ice has the rest." He nodded deeper into the cave. "The company flooded the lower workings when they closed. Said it was an accident. The men were still inside. Some of them were still — " He stopped. Recalibrated. "The company paid the government to call it an accident. The government paid the newspapers to call it nothing. And the families were paid nothing at all."

The sweet rotten smell. I finally placed it. Flowers. Someone had been leaving flowers in a cave at ten thousand feet.

* * *

They showed us everything, and I think that was deliberate. I think Tomás understood, the way my mother understood people, that the surest way to hold a man is to hand him something he can't put down.

A miner's ledger, its pages stiff as bark, in a careful clerk's hand: tonnage, wages, deductions — and then, in the last third, a different hand, hurried, the entries becoming a list. Names. Dates of hire. Dates of death. The dates of death clustered in a single week, and beside each one, in the margin, a single word repeated like a heartbeat: *inside. inside. inside.*

A bundle of letters, never delivered, that families had given the wardens for safekeeping because there was no one left to deliver them to and nowhere safe to keep them below. A child's drawing of a man descending into a mountain, done in the confident crayon of a kid who didn't know yet. A cassette tape in a plastic bag, labeled in ballpoint: *Doña Carmen, the names, second attempt.*

"Your father saw this cave," Tomás said.

I turned around. "What?"

"Eleven days ago. He stood where you are standing." Tomás's face in the lantern light was old the way the cirque was old, carved by weather into something patient. "He wept. I will tell you that, because you will want to know it and no one else will say it. A big man, a proud man, and he stood in front of the ledger and wept like the rest of us did the first time."

"That's not — " My voice cracked and I hated it. "He came here for the source of the Río Blanco. It's in his notes. First descent, headwater survey, the — "

"The notes were the map," Tomás said. "The man was the message." He studied me with an expression I was beginning to understand was pity. "You truly didn't know."

"I — " And then, because lying to a man who kept two hundred names in a glacier felt like a sin with witnesses: "No."

* * *

Here is what I knew about my father, before that cave: he was a man who needed summits the way other men need drink. He'd left for weeks, months. He'd missed my graduation for a river in another country. He'd spent my parents' savings on permits and satellite phones and the particular vanity of men who believe the world is unfinished until they've put their name on a corner of it. My mother had spent thirty years loving a man whose true wife was the horizon, and when he disappeared, I'd thought — God forgive me — I'd thought *finally she's free*, and then watched her sell the house to fund a search, and thought instead: *he's ruined her even from inside the mountain.*

Here is what I knew about my father now, standing in a cave full of other people's grief: his last expedition had no sponsors. His permits, the ones the government swore never existed — Tomás produced copies, taken from the man's own pack — had been applied for under a research clause so obscure it might as well have been a password. His satellite phone log, which the wardens had kept, showed calls not to newspapers or universities but to a human rights lawyer in the capital. Eleven calls. The last one, three days before basecamp went silent, forty-one minutes long.

He hadn't come to put his name on the map. He'd come to take the mountain's name — the names, all of them — out of the ice and into the light.

And my mother had known.

That was the part that sat in my chest like swallowed river water. Because I remembered the fight, two years ago, the one where he'd said *this one's different, Inés, this one matters* and she'd said *they all matter until they don't come back* — I'd thought it was the usual sermon. But she'd packed his cold-weather gear herself. She'd sewn a pocket into his jacket, and I'd teased him about it, and he'd just smiled. A document pocket. I knew that now. She'd known what he was carrying and she'd sewn the pocket shut with her own hands, and then she'd spent a year letting me believe he'd died for vanity, because the truth was a weight and she chose to carry it alone rather than hand it to me.

I sat down on the ice, which is not a thing you're supposed to do, and put my head in my hands, and the wardens, to their credit, pretended to be busy with their lanterns.

Beatrix crouched next to me. She didn't say *I'm sorry* — she'd told me once she thought those words were mostly a way of making other people's pain about yourself. Instead she said, "He got the ledger out. Did you know that? The original page. It's not in the cave."

I looked up. "What?"

"Tomás showed us the niche. Empty. Wax paper folded, box open. Whatever your father took from here, he took it out." She let that land. "The wardens took your radios because they thought he'd sent word ahead. They took you because they think the page is still on that mountain, or in that river, or in someone's pack. And they think *we* know where."

The cave was very quiet. The ice ticked and settled around us like a house at night.

"So the trade," I said slowly. "The hostages for the evidence. There's no evidence to trade."

"There's a page," Beatrix said. "And there are two hundred and six reasons it can't go back in the dark. And there's a woman named Doña Rufina who's spent her whole life learning that everything you love gets taken if you let it leave this mountain." She stood, and offered me a hand up, and her grip was steady and cold and absolutely certain. "Your father trusted the truth to travel. Rufina trusts it to stay. Somebody's about to be wrong, Elias, and the wrong one gets to decide how many people breathe tomorrow."

* * *

They walked us back down in the gray light, and I carried the cave with me — the photographs, the flowers, the word *inside* repeated down the margin like a pulse.

My father hadn't been running from us. He'd been running *toward*, and he'd sewn my mother into the journey without telling her the price, and she'd paid it, and now I was standing in his footsteps at the top of a river that remembered everything, about to inherit a debt I hadn't signed for.

At the camp, Doña Rufina was waiting by the fire, and she looked at me the way you look at a letter you've been expecting.

"Sit," she said. "We need to talk about what happens at the Last Summit."
