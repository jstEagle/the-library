## 7. Terms of the Mountain

They fed us first. That was the thing I couldn't get over, sitting in the longhouse with a bowl of quinoa soup going cold in my hands while armed men watched from the doorway — they *fed* us. Kidnappers with table manners. Hostage-takers who asked if I wanted more broth.

"You'll want your strength," Doña Rufina had said, as if she were sending me up a mountain instead of holding me prisoner. Maybe to her those were the same thing.

The longhouse smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hanging from the rafters in bundles that swayed whenever the door opened. Elias sat beside me, still damp from the river crossing two days back, his jaw set in that way he had when he was deciding whether to fight or sulk. Mostly he'd been fighting. With them, with me, with the fact that his father hadn't been lost — he'd been *kept*.

"They're not monsters," he said quietly, for maybe the hundredth time since we'd been brought down out of the trees at gunpoint.

"No," I said. "That would be simpler."

Simpler. Everything about this valley refused to be simple. The wardens were old men and older women with rifles slung on woven straps, farmers' hands on soldiers' hardware, and every one of them had a name stitched somewhere into the story of San Rafael — the mine, the massacre, the forty-seven people the government turned into a rounding error. I'd heard the number from Rufina herself, delivered flat as an elevation reading. Forty-seven. She'd been twenty-three years old. She'd carried her brother's body out of the adit on her back because the company trucks wouldn't stop for the dead.

Now she sat across the fire from me, small and upright, hands folded like a woman waiting for tea, and told me the terms.

"The evidence does not leave the mountain," she said. "Not in your pack. Not in your father's notebooks." Her eyes went to Elias. "Not in anyone's memory, if memory can be helped. It stays here, where it has stayed for thirty years, until it is safe to bring down. That is not negotiable."

"And the hostages?" I asked.

"Come with me tomorrow. All of you. You will see what your husband found, what he understood." A pause, the firelight moving across her face like water. "And then you will understand why we cannot let him carry it out. Not yet. Not while the men who paid for the silence are still alive and still listening."

Elias stood up so fast the bench scraped. "My father is sixty-one years old. He has a heart condition. You're keeping him in a cave—"

"We are keeping him *alive*," Rufina said, without raising her voice, which was somehow worse. "Do you know what happens to messengers, young man? Ask the last four who tried. Ask the journalist who came in '09. Ask the priest. The mountain keeps its dead, but we did not put them there. Others did. We only declined to join them."

I put a hand on Elias's arm. He shook me off. I put it back. He let it stay, which was progress, by the arithmetic of Elias Solano.

"Tell me the trade," I said.

Rufina looked at me for a long moment, the way you look at weather coming in. Then she nodded, once, to a man by the door — Tomás, the one who never spoke — and he left, and came back with a wooden box no bigger than a bread loaf, iron-banded, old.

"Here is the trade," she said. "You have a radio. Broken, yes? Salvaged from their basecamp. We know about it; we watched you carry it up the canyon like a relic." Almost a smile. "Tomorrow, at first light, my people will take the hostages down to the lower trail. Your expedition walks free. Every one of them. In exchange, the archive stays. The photographs, the ledger, the recordings — everything Tomás's father died carrying out of San Rafael, everything your husband spent three years finding. It stays in the ice where we keep it, and you go home, and you tell the world whatever story lets you sleep."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we are all still here next week," she said, "and the week after, and eventually someone comes up the river with better guns than ours, and everyone dies, and the mountain keeps everything anyway. The ice is patient. I am less so."

* * *

They let us walk after that. Under guard, but walking, along a path above the river where the water ran green-white with glacier flour and the air had that thin metallic taste of altitude doing its work on you. Elias stalked ahead. I let him. There are conversations you can't have side by side; you need the distance to say things honestly.

He stopped at a bend where the trail narrowed against a rock face, and when I caught up he was staring down at the water.

"She's right," he said. "That's the worst part. She's right about all of it. If Dad carries that ledger out and gets shot in some border town, it becomes a rumor. Another dead crank with a box of papers. But if it stays here—" He turned. "It stays buried. Forty-seven people stay buried. My mother spends the rest of her life not knowing, and I spend mine being the son who dragged him home to safety and buried the truth with him."

"That's not the choice you're being given."

"Isn't it?"

"No." I leaned against the rock, feeling the cold come through my jacket, feeling older than I've ever felt. "The choice is whether *you* decide, or whether you let a dying old woman with a rifle decide for you. Because those are different choices wearing the same coat."

He laughed, short and bitter. "Listen to you. The great Beatrix Villalobos, moral philosopher. Weren't you the one who—" He stopped himself. Good instincts, even furious.

"Go ahead," I said. "Say it. Everyone else has."

"Cerro Condor." His voice dropped. "The storm warning. You got the call at Camp Two and you kept climbing because the summit window was open and it might not come again, and Marco Reyes — twenty-six years old, first big mountain, trusted you with his life — he died in the whiteout on the descent. And they took your license, and everyone said you chose the peak over the person, and you never once denied it."

"I didn't deny it because it was true."

The words came out of me with no plan behind them, the way stones come loose. I'd said versions of it before — to investigators, to Inés, to myself at three in the morning — but never standing in a valley where other people's dead were stacked up like cordwood, where my one dead client suddenly looked very small and very large at the same time.

"I chose the summit," I said. "The forecast said twelve hours. I told myself twelve hours was enough. I did the math of ambition instead of the math of weather, and when the storm came early, Marco was roped to me, and I couldn't hold him, and the mountain took him, and I walked down alone because walking down was the only thing left I knew how to do." I looked at the river. "So don't ask me if truth is worth lives like it's a riddle you're hoping I've solved. I'm the last person qualified to answer. I already answered it once, wrong, and a family got a folded flag and a bill for the helicopter."

Elias was quiet for a long time. Below us the Río Blanco kept saying the only thing it knows how to say.

"So what do we do?" he finally asked. Not angry anymore. Just asking, the way you ask a guide.

And that was the moment I understood what Rufina had actually done. Not the trade itself — the *asking*. She'd handed the decision to me, the disgraced one, the blacklisted one, the woman whose judgment had killed a man, because she wanted the decision made by someone who knew exactly what it cost. She wasn't testing the evidence. She was testing me.

* * *

That night I sat up in the longhouse while everyone slept, turning the broken radio over in my hands. Its casing was cracked, the antenna snapped, the battery compartment full of green corrosion. Dead as anything. But salvageable — I'd thought so back at basecamp, and I'd hauled the extra weight up the canyon for reasons I hadn't let myself examine. Guides are superstitious about gear. We carry things.

The trade was clean. Take it, walk the hostages out, go home, surrender the truth to the ice and the patience of old women. Live with it. People do it every day; there are whole countries built on it.

Except.

Except I kept seeing Rufina's face when she said *the men who paid for the silence are still alive and still listening.* Except I kept thinking about a widow in a border-town cantina who'd offered me a fortune not to find a box of papers but to find her husband, and how the fortune was really just grief with numbers attached. Except I kept hearing Marco Reyes's mother on the phone, a year after, asking me — *asking me* — whether her son had suffered, and telling me the truth because lying to her would have been the second death.

People deserve the truth even when it costs. That was the lesson of Cerro Condor, inverted. I'd chosen the summit over a person. The wardens were choosing the truth over persons. Both were the same mistake: treating something abstract as worth more than the specific, warm, complaining human beings in front of you. The evidence wasn't the point. The hostages weren't the point. The point was that somebody, someday, had to stand on that peak and refuse both false choices — refuse to bury the truth and refuse to spend lives buying it.

I didn't have a plan yet. I had a cracked radio, a corrupt battery, and the beginnings of an idea so reckless that I almost laughed at it there in the dark, alone with the snoring wardens and the wind worrying the thatch.

Almost.

Because plans like that get people killed, and I was done making other people pay for my summits. Whatever I did, I would do it with my eyes open and my name attached.

Elias stirred in his blanket across the fire. "Can't sleep either?"

"No."

"You've got that face," he said. "The one from the trailhead. The one that means you're about to ruin everybody's careful arrangements."

"It's a good face," I said. "It's the only one I've got."

He sat up. Outside, far off, the glacier boomed like a door closing somewhere deep in the mountain, and I thought: not yet. Not this time. This door, we open.

"First light," I told him. "Wake Tomás. Tell him I accept the trade."

His eyes narrowed. "Beatrix—"

"But tell him we do it my way. On the summit. Where the whole world can hear it."

He stared at me, and slowly, terribly, he started to grin.
