## 8. Ascent

*Beatrix*

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

Doña Rufina stood at the mouth of the warden's cave and read the sky like a ledger. "It will hold until dark," she said. "Then it closes. If we go tonight, we go in the white. If we wait, we go in the deep."

I looked at the cirque above us, at the black seam of ice that led to the Last Summit, and I felt something I hadn't felt in six years: certainty. Not the arrogant kind — I'd buried that man-killer with Tomás Herrera. The other kind. The kind you earn by falling.

"We go tonight," I said.

Elias turned from the fire. "In the white? You said yourself—"

"I know what I said." I pulled my rope from the pack, coiled it, checked every meter of sheath with my thumbs the way a priest checks beads. "I said no one climbs this route in weather. But we're not climbing for a summit anymore. We're climbing so eleven people don't die in a cave while two governments argue about whose massacre it was. That changes the arithmetic."

Rufina studied me with eyes the color of river stones. "The last guide who led people up that face," she said, "did not bring them home."

"No," I agreed. "She didn't."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am." I cinched the rope across my chest. "That's the whole job description now."

* * *

*Elias*

She briefed us like a general who had learned humility the expensive way. Every knot demonstrated twice. Every hand signal repeated until the wardens — men and women who had spent thirty years reading snow, but never roped together — could answer without thinking. She paired the hostages with the strongest climbers. She put herself last, which I understood later was not modesty but strategy: the rear is where you catch the falling.

"You," she said to me, tapping my chest with one gloved finger, "are second. You call the pace when I'm out of sight. You do not pass me. You do not stop moving unless I say the word *piedra*. And if I say *caída*, you drop and anchor and you do not look up. Can you do that?"

"My father climbed for thirty years," I said. "I grew up on rope."

"Your father climbed for glory," she said, not cruelly, just as fact. "Tonight nobody gets glory. Tonight we get down. Or up. Whichever keeps everyone breathing." She held my eyes a beat longer than the lesson required. "Can you do that?"

"Yes," I said. And found that I meant it in a way that surprised me — not as defiance, not as proof, but as trust. Somewhere between the drowning and the archive cave, I had stopped trying to outrun her and started trying to keep up.

We stepped out of the cave into the blue hour before dark, twelve ropes of three, and the mountain took its first payment immediately: the wind came off the glacier field flat and hard, a wall of moving cold that made the hostages stagger. One of them, a young surveyor named Paredes, began to cry without seeming to notice he was doing it. Beatrix knelt in front of him, adjusted his goggles, and said something too quiet for me to hear. He stopped crying. We moved.

* * *

*Beatrix*

I knew this route. That was the cruelty of it. Every feature had a name in my mouth already, and most of those names were griefs.

The Ramp — where I had pushed for one more pitch with the barometer falling. The Gray Ledges — where Tomás had waited with the clients while I argued with the sky. The Couloir — where the storm had caught us like a fist closing, and where I had made the choice that unmade me: summit over warning. Up. When everything I knew said down.

Six years of disgrace had taught me what no certification course ever did: exactly how heavy a wrong decision is. So tonight I climbed with both hands full of that weight, and it made me careful in a way skill alone never had.

The storm closed at nine o'clock, right on schedule, and the world went to static. White wind, white snow, headlamps reduced to coins of light. I called the pace by feel — pendulum steps, rest-step rhythm, counting breaths because counting breaths was the only clock that worked up there. Behind me, forty-one people moved in the dark because I told them it was possible, and the terrible truth was that it was possible only because I told them so. Belief, on a mountain, is equipment.

At the Gray Ledges the wind found a gap in the rock and screamed through it sideways. Paredes slipped. Just a slip — one boot skating off wet stone — but his rope went taut and his partner went down to one knee, and for three seconds the whole night balanced on a nylon line.

"Hold!" I shouted, and dropped my axe into the snow, and the line held, and Paredes hung there making small sounds, and I talked him back onto the rock the way you talk anyone back from an edge: slowly, stupidly, one sentence at a time. *My name is Beatrix. Your name is Paredes. There is a ledge below your left boot. Find it with your toe. Good. Now again.*

Later, Elias asked me what I'd said to him at the start, in the wind, that stopped the crying.

"The truth," I said. "I told him I'd been the worst guide on this mountain once, and that the mountain let me live specifically so I could be here tonight. Whether or not it's true, it worked. Which on a mountain is the same thing."

* * *

*Elias*

Somewhere past midnight, on the long traverse below the couloir, I understood what she meant about belief being equipment — because mine ran out.

It happened after the second halt. My hands had gone from pain to nothing, which I knew was worse, and the storm had erased the difference between up and down so completely that I was navigating by the sound of her voice ahead of me, calling turns. *Piedra.* Step. *Piedra.* Step. And then her voice stopped, and the rope went slack, and for ten seconds — ten seconds that lasted approximately a geological age — I stood alone in the white with forty lives strung out behind me like beads on a broken necklace.

Then her voice again, closer than it should have been: "*Caída!*"

I dropped, anchored, did not look up. Snow hammered my back. And through the roar I heard her working above me — calm, methodical, cursing with great creativity — and then a grunt, and then her boots landing beside my head.

"Cornice collapse," she said, kneeling, breathing hard. "The route's cut. We go around by the seracs or we don't go."

"Doña Rufina's people know another way?" I asked.

"There is no other way. There's only the way I'm about to find." She stood, and in the coin of my headlamp light I saw her face — iced to the eyebrows, exhausted, absolutely lit from inside. "Elias. Listen. In an hour we reach the bergschrund, and after that it's the summit ridge, and after that it's done. Everything I have ever done wrong on a mountain has been preparation for tonight. Do you believe that?"

And here is the thing I will tell my children someday, if I ever have any, if any of us survive to have children: I did believe her. Not because she was certain. Because she wasn't. Because she stood there in the worst night of the mountain's year and admitted the route was gone and the plan was dead and she was going anyway — and that, I finally understood, was what competence actually looked like. Not the absence of doubt. Doubt, carried correctly.

"Yes," I said. "Let's go around."

* * *

*Beatrix*

The detour cost us two hours and nearly cost us Marta, one of the wardens, whose crampon snapped on the serac ice. We lashed her boot with cord and a tent pole and she climbed the rest of the way on a leg that would need a doctor and a story she'd tell for fifty years. The hostages, God bless them, had passed through terror into some strange collective calm, the way people do when the situation exceeds their capacity to process it and the body simply decides to walk.

At 4 a.m., in the deepest cold, we reached the bergschrund — the great split where the glacier tears itself away from the peak — and crossed it on a snow bridge that groaned under each person like a living thing being asked to forgive us. I crossed last. Standing on the far lip, I looked back down the route we'd come, and the storm had begun to thin, and below us the whole black world lay scrolled out under a sky going gray at the edges.

Tomás should have been behind me on that rope. That thought arrived without permission, the way it always did. Six years ago, on this same face, in weather almost exactly like this, I had been given a warning and chosen the summit, and the mountain had taken him instead of me, which was not justice but arithmetic, and I had lived six years doing sums with it.

"Elias," I said. He turned. Wind had died enough to speak without shouting. "When we get to the top, whatever happens — whatever they do, whatever I do — I want you to remember that your father didn't fail on this mountain. He got further than anyone ever has carrying something heavier than a pack."

He looked at me for a long moment. "Is that what you tell yourself? About Tomás?"

"It's what I know," I said. "There's a difference. It took me six years to learn it, and I learned it right here, and I am not going to waste the tuition."

Above us, maybe four hundred meters, the Last Summit stood against the graying sky — a black tooth of rock wearing its ice like a hood. And on the ridge below it, small in the dawn light, figures were moving. Wardens. Coming down toward us.

Rufina, beside me, went very still. Then she said, quietly, in the tone of a woman reading her own death warrant: "Those are not my people."

* * *

*Elias*

They came down the ridge fast, six of them, armed, faces wrapped against the cold — and Beatrix stepped forward off the rope with her hands open and empty, the way she does, the way that is either bravery or suicide depending entirely on the other party's mood.

The lead figure stopped ten meters away. Pulled down the wrap. A young man, hard-faced, frost in his beard, a rifle slung across his chest with the casualness of long habit.

"You're the guide," he said. Not a question. "The one they sent."

"Nobody sent me," Beatrix said. "I hired myself."

"They said you'd come." His eyes went past her, counting ropes, counting hostages, counting wardens, and stopping on me with something that might have been recognition. "Solano. You have his chin."

My father. This man knew my father. I opened my mouth and Beatrix's hand found my arm — *not yet* — and the young warden raised his voice against the wind:

"Then hear it once, because I won't say it twice. The transmitter at the summit is live. Has been since yesterday. They're broadcasting from the peak right now — someone got the old rig running — and every government on two borders is listening." He paused, and the wind filled the pause, and then he said the thing that rearranged the entire night:

"They're using your father's voice, Solano. Recordings. Testimony he recorded in the ice cave. It's playing on the radio right now, all the way down to the valley. And there are men coming up behind us who were paid to make sure it stops."

Beatrix and I looked at each other, and above us the summit stood black against the dawn, and somewhere on it, faint under the wind, I swear I could hear it — a voice, thin and crackling, saying words I had known since childhood.

My father's voice. Saying my mother's name.
