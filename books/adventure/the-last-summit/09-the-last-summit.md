## 9. The Last Summit

The transmitter weighed less than a dead bird. That was the joke of it — all this way, all these bodies on the mountain, and the thing that mattered fit in a stuff sack the size of a loaf of bread.

I'd pulled it from the wreckage of basecamp two days earlier, half-crushed under a collapsed tent pole, its antenna bent like a question mark. Elias had watched me test it by firelight and said nothing, but I'd seen him do the arithmetic in his head: one dying satellite unit, one chance, no second try. He'd stopped arguing with me somewhere around the icefall, which either meant he'd come around to my plan or he'd simply run out of breath for it. With Elias I'd learned you couldn't always tell the difference.

Now we stood at the top of the world's most expensive silence.

The Last Summit wasn't much to look at from below — just another shoulder of the cirque, white-capped, unremarkable. From up here it was different. The glacier fell away beneath us in blue terraces, and beyond it the whole Río Blanco watershed unspooled toward the horizon, a silver thread stitching together every valley the government had ever pretended was empty. Somewhere down that thread were towns. Schools. People who drank this water every day without knowing what had been poured into the ground at its source.

And in the ice cave forty meters below us, wrapped in tarps and guarded by a nineteen-year-old warden named Tomás who hadn't slept in two days, sat the evidence: photographs of men who'd been shot and stacked like cordwood in 1987. A ledger with names in a dead miner's careful hand. Cassette tapes of testimony recorded by survivors who'd walked out of the San Rafael mine and decided that if the world wouldn't come to hear them, they'd become the mountain's memory instead.

Doña Rufina stood beside me at the summit cairn, her hands folded, her face as weathered and patient as the rock itself. She'd carried this secret for thirty-six years. She'd buried friends in it. She'd let her whole life calcify around the weight of remembering.

"You're certain about this," she said. Not a question, really. A last door she was leaving open for me.

"No," I said. "But certainty is for people who haven't spent enough time in the mountains."

* * *

Here is what I knew about broadcasting from four thousand nine hundred meters in a storm window that was closing fast:

The transmitter had maybe twenty minutes of battery. The satellite pass — Elias had calculated it, because of course he had, with his father's almanac and his mother's stubbornness — gave us eleven minutes of clear line-of-sight to the relay. After that, nothing until tomorrow, and tomorrow the storm would eat us alive.

Eleven minutes. I'd spent longer than that deciding what coffee to order.

The hostages — six of them, the surviving members of Tomás Solano's expedition plus two government surveyors who'd wandered into the wrong valley at the wrong time — were gathered near the cairn, roped loosely, watched by wardens who looked less like captors than like people standing vigil. One of the hostages, a geologist named Ferrán with frostbitten fingertips and the eyes of a man who'd seen too much already, kept glancing at the ice cave like he was memorizing its location. I didn't blame him. I also didn't trust him. Both things could be true.

"Beatrix." Elias came to stand at my shoulder. His lips were cracked, his beard full of ice, and he looked, in that moment, so much like his father that my chest hurt. "The pass starts in four minutes. You should say something first. To them." He nodded at the wardens, the hostages, the mountain itself. "Before you turn it on."

"I'm not good at speeches."

"You're good at telling the truth badly," he said. "It's kind of your signature move."

I almost laughed. High altitude does strange things to a person.

* * *

I turned to face them all — Rufina and her wardens, gray-haired and young-faced, holding rifles they'd never wanted to need; the hostages, hollow-eyed, breathing thin air; the glacier behind us keeping its frozen archive like a heart that refused to stop beating.

"Thirty-six years ago," I said, and my voice cracked on the altitude, on the cold, on everything, "men came up this river and killed sixty-two people at the San Rafael mine. And then someone very powerful paid someone else very powerful to make sure none of us ever heard about it. No trials. No graves marked. No names in any paper. Just a river that runs past it every day, carrying the story downhill where nobody listens."

Rufina's eyes glistened but stayed dry. Wardens don't cry on duty. I understood that now.

"These people have held that truth alone for three decades. They've lost everything to it — their families, their futures, their names. They took hostages because they believed no one would listen otherwise. They were probably right." I looked at Ferrán, at the others. "You're not prisoners anymore. As of right now, you're witnesses. There's a difference. Prisoners get released. Witnesses have to live with what they know."

Ferrán opened his mouth. Closed it again. Nodded once, slow, like a man accepting a sentence he'd been expecting.

Elias handed me the transmitter. It was lighter than it had any right to be.

"Four minutes," he said softly. "Say it like they're listening. Because they are."

* * *

The red light blinked on. Eleven minutes of the world's attention, or whatever fraction of the world happened to be pointed at a satellite that didn't care about any of us.

I pressed transmit and started talking.

I told them about the massacre. I told them about the ledger, the photographs, the tapes. I read six names aloud from the miner's handwriting — slow, deliberate, letting each one land in the thin air like a stone dropped down a well. Aurelio Pacheco. Marisol Quispe. The brothers Endara. A boy of fifteen whose name the ledger only recorded as "el pequeño, hijo de Dolores." I said his name anyway, even though it wasn't really a name, because somebody had to.

Behind me, I heard Rufina begin to speak too — low, steady, in Quechua first and then Spanish, giving the testimony she'd rehearsed alone in the dark for thirty-six years. The wardens joined her, one by one, each adding their piece. The hostages listened. Ferrán wept openly, without shame, and I thought: good. Let the mountain see it. Let the satellite see it. Let whoever's listening understand that this isn't history. This is a wound that never closed.

Three minutes left. I described the location of the ice cave in coordinates precise enough to survive translation. I named the company. I named the officials. I said the word *massacre* out loud, over and over, until it stopped sounding like an accusation and started sounding like a fact.

One minute.

"Doña Rufina," I said, turning to her, holding out the transmitter. "Tell them anything you want. This is yours more than mine."

She took it in both hands like it was made of glass, or bread, or something holy. She spoke for fifty seconds. I didn't understand all of it. I understood enough.

Then the light went green, then amber, then dark, and the transmitter died in her hands with a small electronic sigh, and the wind came back in like it had been waiting politely at the door.

* * *

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then Rufina set the transmitter down on the cairn, gently, the way you'd lay down a sleeping child, and turned to her wardens. She said something short in Quechua. Tomás lowered his rifle. The others followed, one by one, until the guns hung at their sides like tools whose work was finally done.

The hostages stood uncertainly, ropes still on, waiting for permission to exist.

"Cut them loose," Rufina said. "They descend with us. All of us. Together." She looked at me, and something passed between us that I'll spend the rest of my life failing to describe — recognition, maybe, between two women who'd both chosen the harder thing over the safe thing, thirty-six years apart. "You found the third way, guide. I didn't think there was one. I've been on this mountain so long I forgot to look for it."

"There almost wasn't one," I admitted. "I got lucky."

"Luck," Rufina said, "is what the mountain gives people who prepare for every other possibility." She almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and I understood suddenly that she'd been beautiful once, and that she probably still was, in a way that had nothing to do with mirrors.

Elias appeared at my elbow, working the rope off Ferrán's wrists. His hands were shaking. So were mine. We didn't look at each other directly — some things are easier sideways — but our shoulders touched, and neither of us moved away.

"That was eleven minutes," he said quietly. "Maybe the most important eleven minutes anyone's ever spent on this pile of rock."

"Don't let the mountain hear you say that. She gets jealous."

He laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, ragged at the edges. Then he sobered. "My dad would have—"

"He did," I said. "That's why we're here. He just ran out of time to finish it." I squeezed his arm through two layers of frozen sleeve. "We finished it for him. Now we carry it down."

Below us, the wardens were striking camp with the efficiency of people who'd waited decades for exactly this morning. The hostages moved among them stiffly, unsure of the new rules. Ferrán approached Rufina and said something I couldn't hear; she listened, then pointed down-valley, toward the river, toward everything.

The storm front was visible now on the western horizon, a gray wall building itself out of cloud and intention. We had perhaps three hours of decent weather. Maybe four.

"Beatrix." Elias's voice had gone quiet and serious in the way I was learning meant he was about to say something true. "Whatever happens next — hearings, lawyers, the government trying to bury it again — it's going to be slow. Ugly. They'll fight it."

"I know."

"And you're going to be at the center of it. Your name, your record, the client you lost. All of it's going to get dug up and examined by people who've never been above sea level."

"I know that too."

"So why do you look happy?"

I looked out at the river, silvering in the distance, running downhill toward a world that was about to start listening. I thought about a man I'd failed on a mountain years ago, and how I'd spent every day since trying to figure out whether a person could come back from a thing like that. I didn't have an answer yet. But standing there, with the truth finally airborne and a widow's son beside me and an old woman's hand briefly gripping my shoulder before she turned to lead her people home, I thought: maybe coming back isn't a single act. Maybe it's a direction.

"Because for the first time in six years," I said, "I'm pointing downhill instead of up."

Elias smiled. Then he shouldered his pack, checked the bindings on his father's old crampons, and nodded toward the descent.

"Come on, guide," he said. "The river's waiting. And so is my mother."

* * *

We started down at noon, seventeen people roped in three teams, moving through the storm window like a prayer through a keyhole. Behind us the Last Summit stood silent, its secret gone, its burden lifted, its glacier free to be just ice again instead of a vault.

The transmitter stayed on the cairn. It seemed right, somehow. Let the mountain keep the machine. We'd take the words.

By the time we reached tree line, my satellite phone — the one Elias had insisted we carry, the paranoid, beautiful, practical man — buzzed against my hip with a signal I'd stopped believing I'd ever see: a text from a number I didn't recognize, forwarded through three relays, reading simply:

**WE HEARD YOU. GET DOWN SAFELY. HELP IS COMING UP THE RIVER.**

I showed it to Elias. He read it twice. Then he looked up at me with an expression I suspected I'd be seeing a lot of in the weeks and months ahead — hope, cautious and hard-won, like a flame cupped against wind.

"They heard," he said.

"The whole world heard," I corrected. "Now comes the hard part."

We kept walking. Downriver. Toward Inés, toward hearings, toward justice — slow, legal, and, for the first time in thirty-six years, real.
