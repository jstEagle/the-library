## 10. Downriver

The capital smelled like diesel and cut limes, which is to say it smelled like every city I'd ever been glad to leave. But I stayed. For the hearings.

They were being held in a government building that looked like someone had designed a wedding cake and then lost interest halfway through frosting it. Marble floors, brass fittings, and air conditioning set to a temperature that suggested the architects had never once been warm in their lives. Outside, the heat pressed down like a hand on the back of your neck. Inside, ministers in good suits said words like *alleged* and *pending verification* into microphones, and I sat in the third row with my mother's hand in mine and learned what justice actually looks like up close: slow, bureaucratic, and stubborn as water finding its way downhill.

My father's name was read aloud on the second day. Tomás Solano. Expedition leader. Deceased. The clerk pronounced it like an item on an inventory list, and something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was there — not grief exactly, because I'd already done my grieving for a man I thought I understood, but something adjacent. The sound of a story being corrected.

He hadn't gone up the Río Blanco looking for glory. He hadn't gone looking for the source at all, not really. He'd gone because Inés asked him to, because she'd grown up two valleys over from San Rafael and had a cousin whose name was never found, only inferred from a boot. He'd gone carrying his wife's dead in his rucksack alongside the rope and the stove fuel, and he'd died — or vanished; the wardens swore they buried him with honors above the treeline, and I believed them, because Doña Rufina did not lie about the dead — trying to carry the truth out for her.

I had spent eleven years blaming him for ruining us. For the debts, the absences, the way my mother went quiet as a closed shop whenever his name came up. It turned out he'd been quiet too. They'd both been sitting on a secret the size of a massacre, each protecting the other from its weight, and I'd walked through the middle of their marriage like a man walking through fog, seeing nothing.

"You're staring at me," my mother said during a recess, without opening her eyes. We were on a bench outside the chamber, sharing a bottle of water that tasted faintly of the plastic it came in.

"I'm allowed."

"You are." She opened her eyes. "It's strange, isn't it. Being seen."

"Terrible," I said. "I don't recommend it."

She laughed — actually laughed, a short bark of a sound I hadn't heard from her since I was nine — and then pressed her knuckles briefly against my cheek, the way she used to when I had fevers. "Your father would have hated these hearings," she said. "All this sitting. He'd have wanted them over in a day so we could go home."

"He'd have hated the waiting more."

"No," she said. "Waiting was the one thing he was good at. Eleven years he waited for me to be ready to tell you. That's patience, mijo. The rest was just stubbornness wearing patience's coat."

* * *

The hearings ground on. A mining company called Andesur — a name so bland it could only have been chosen by committee — sent lawyers with tans acquired in places no honest tan comes from. They did not deny the massacre. Denial required a position, and their entire strategy was to have none: they expressed regret for *events* in the region, acknowledged *difficult chapters*, and proposed a *process of national reflection*. One of them used the phrase *moving forward together* four times in a single statement, and by the fourth time I wanted to throw my water bottle at his head.

But the evidence held. That was the thing nobody had bet on, least of all me. The photographs from the glacier cave — grainy, silver-nitrate prints of men lined against a slag heap, of trucks backed up to a shaft mouth, of a foreman's ledger with dates and names and, in one terrible margin note, the word *resuelto* beside thirty-one entries — those photographs did not care about process or reflection. They just sat there on the projection screen, patient as stone, while men in good suits sweated under the lights and ran out of adjectives.

Doña Rufina testified on the fifth day. She came down from the mountain for it, first time off the reserve in forty years, wearing her black shawl and her warden's armband like a woman attending her own funeral and finding the eulogy satisfactory. When the chairman asked if she was afraid, she looked at him for a long moment.

"I buried my husband in 1987," she said. "I have been afraid every day since, and I have gotten up every morning since. Fear is not the problem, señor. Forgetting is the problem. I am here to be inconvenient."

The room laughed, and then stopped laughing, because she wasn't joking, and by the time she finished reading the names — all thirty-one, slowly, in her cracked mountain Spanish, letting each one sit in the air like a stone dropped in still water — half the chamber was crying and the other half was very busy taking notes.

Beatrix sat through all of it two rows behind me. She'd cut her hair short since the summit, badly, herself, with what looked like fishing scissors, and she wore the same canvas jacket she'd worn up the Río Blanco, and she took no notes at all. She just listened, arms crossed, like a woman auditing the world against a standard she now trusted herself to hold.

Once, during a recess, I caught her eye across the marble hall and she raised her chin slightly — acknowledgment, nothing more — and I felt it land somewhere below my ribs and stay there.

We were not anything yet. I want to be clear about that, because the newspapers had already decided otherwise — EL RESCATE ROMÁNTICO, one headline read, complete with a photo of us from the descent in which I looked concussed and she looked furious, which was accurate — but the truth was slower than the story. Two people who had nearly drowned together, who had climbed a storm wall at night holding hands with strangers' lives between them, do not simply become a couple. They become something else first: a habit of checking on each other. A shorthand. She'd say *your left* and I'd know she meant my blind side, the old shoulder injury, and I'd say *breathe* and she'd know I meant the panic, hers, the kind that lived in her chest since the client died, and we'd been trading these small currencies for weeks now without ever naming what they added up to.

Naming could wait. The mountain had taught us both that some things you carry down before you talk about them.

* * *

On the last day of the first round of hearings, Beatrix got a letter. I know because she told me, standing outside the building in the lime-and-diesel light, turning a cream envelope over in her hands like it might bite.

"Reinstated," she said. "Full license. Backdated apology. There's even a signature." She showed me: some director-general's looping scrawl above a stamp. "Eleven years of blacklist and they fix it in six weeks once the cameras arrive."

"What are you going to do?"

She didn't answer right away. She looked down the avenue, at the traffic, at the mountains visible past the rooftops on clear days like a promise the city kept forgetting to keep.

"There's a postbox on the corner," she said finally. "Come on."

I followed her. She bought a stamp from the kiosk vendor, licked it with the grim efficiency of a woman who had decided something, and addressed the envelope to the licensing authority in her own block capitals. Then she stood there holding it over the slot.

"You're sending it back," I said. Not a question. Her face had already told me.

"They gave it back because I broadcast a massacre from a mountaintop," she said. "Not because I earned it. If I take it now, I'm telling myself the same lie they told themselves for thirty years — that the paperwork is the point." She turned the envelope once more. "Besides. I've been thinking about what I'm actually good for."

"Which is?"

"Getting people up mountains and back down alive," she said. "That's it. That's the whole skill. Everything else — the permits, the reputation, the license — that's decoration." She dropped the envelope in. It made almost no sound, which seemed wrong for how loud it felt. "And I'm not doing it alone anymore either. So."

"So?"

"So." She faced me fully, and up close I could see the new scar at her hairline from the icefall, thin as a river on a map. "Elias Solano. Your mother has a house in the valley. You have your father's maps. I have a reinstated-then-surrendered license and no plans whatsoever, which is, frankly, the freest I've felt in a decade." A pause. The traffic roared past behind her. "Where does the river go next?"

I thought about it. Honestly, I thought about it longer than I needed to, because watching her wait was its own small pleasure, and because the answer mattered and I wanted to get it right.

"Down," I said. "All the way down. Through the reserve, past San Rafael, out to the coast where it meets the sea. Nobody's ever run the whole length. My father mapped the upper reaches; the lower gorge is blank on every chart. There are villages down there that lost people in '87 who've never once been asked." I shrugged, feeling my heart do something unprofessional in my chest. "Seems like someone should walk it. Ask. Write it all down before the forgetting gets there first."

Beatrix smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that started crooked and ended somewhere around her eyes.

"The whole river," she said. "Top to bottom."

"Somebody should."

"It'll take months."

"Probably a year."

"There will be leeches."

"Almost certainly leeches."

She stuck out her hand, formal as a treaty, and I shook it, and neither of us let go quite as fast as the joke required.

"Then we start Monday," she said. "First, though—" she nodded toward the café across the street, where my mother was already seated at a window table, pretending not to watch us and fooling no one, "—your mother wants to show me his maps. All of them. Apparently there's a box."

"There's always a box," I said. "With him there was always another box."

"Good," said Beatrix Brightwater, disgraced guide, cleared name, surrendered license, and — though neither of us said it yet, though we wouldn't say it for another hundred kilometers of river — mine. "I like boxes. Boxes mean somebody was planning to come back."

And we crossed the street together, downstream of everything, toward the water.
