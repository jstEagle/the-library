## 1. The Map That Costs

There are three rules I live by, and the first one is simple: never stop moving.

The second and third are variations on the first, if you must know. Never stop moving when the law is behind you. Never stop moving when the mountain is ahead of you. A train that stops is a target; an engineer who stops is a corpse with a good memory for grades and gradients. I learned this at twenty-two, standing in the wreckage of my first engine, watching men pick through the twisted iron for anything worth selling — including, briefly, me. I've been moving ever since. Twenty years of closed lines and dead stations and rails nobody official remembers exist. It's not a living so much as it is a habit that pays.

Tonight's line was the Kestrel Pass cut, officially abandoned eleven years ago when the mining company pulled out and left the mountain to its own devices. Unofficially, it was mine. I had the only complete map of it in existence, drawn in my own hand, corrected after every flood and rockfall, annotated in a shorthand I invented myself because I didn't trust anyone else to read it. The map rode in a oilskin case against my ribs, the way other people carry a Bible or a knife. In my experience it was both.

The night was the kind that makes you grateful for a good firebox — cold enough that the stars came out mean and sharp, clear enough that I could see the shape of the pass ahead by moonlight alone. My engine was the *Wren*, a rebuilt shunter I'd pieced together from three wrecks and a stubbornness that bordered on medical condition. She ran quiet for her age. That mattered tonight, because tonight I wasn't running empty.

I was carrying forty tons of untaxed silver ore for a man named Hobb, who paid me triple the going rate and asked no questions, which suited me fine, since my answers would have been incriminating for us both.

"Easy, girl," I murmured to the *Wren* as we took the long grade up toward the pass. She answered with a shudder through the floor plates, which is how she says *I know what I'm doing, woman*. We'd been arguing like this for six years. Neither of us has won yet.

The trouble started at Marker Nine, where the line threads between two walls of granite close enough that you could shake hands with the mountain on either side, if you were fool enough to open your hand while doing sixty feet per second. I knew there was a toll gang working the pass this month — word travels down the freight lines faster than any telegraph — but knowing they existed and knowing *where* are different animals entirely.

Toll gangs, for those fortunate enough never to have met one, are what happens when a railroad dies and its bones keep walking. Laid-off crews, mostly. Men who know every culvert and signal box on a stretch of closed line, and who've decided that knowledge is property. They don't rob trains, exactly. They charge them. A lantern swung across the track, a chain across the rails at a spot where you can't stop in time and can't see past, and then a figure stepping out with his palm open and a price written on it. Pay or derail. Most pay. The smart ones reroute.

I am, when I can manage it, the smart ones.

But the Kestrel Pass has exactly one route through it, and I was already committed to the grade, and forty tons of silver does not stop on a dime or even on a hundred dimes. So when the lantern came swinging out of the dark at Marker Nine — red glass, steady rhythm, the professional version, not some amateur waving a torch — I did the arithmetic fast.

Chain across the rails at the narrows. Speed: too high to brake. Options: none worth having.

Except one.

There's a maintenance spur off the main cut at Marker Nine, built back when the mining company still pretended to care about safety. It runs maybe four hundred yards along a ledge above the river gorge before it crumbles into gravel and wishful thinking. It's on my map. It is on no other map in the world, because I found it myself, seven years ago, by nearly dying on it.

I threw the switch lever as we passed it — a move that requires reaching out of the cab window into freezing air and hauling a rusted iron arm with your whole body while your engine tries to leave without you — and the *Wren* groaned, complained, and swung onto the spur with all forty tons of Hobb's silver swaying like a drunk leaving church.

Behind us, I heard the toll gang's chain snap taut across empty rail. Then I heard shouting, which is the sound of professionals realizing their revenue just grew legs.

"Hold together," I told the *Wren*, and held together myself, both hands on the reverser, eyes on the ledge. The spur was built for handcars and hope, not for a loaded engine, and every tie under us had opinions about the arrangement. Gravel sprayed off the edge into the gorge. Somewhere below, the river made its indifferent noise, the sound water makes when it doesn't care what falls into it.

Three hundred yards. Two hundred. The far end of the spur ended in a washout — a gap of eight feet where the flood of '09 had eaten the roadbed — but beyond the gap, the old line rejoined the main cut, and from there it was downhill all the way to the junction and Hobb's waiting wagons.

Eight feet of nothing.

I'd crossed it twice before. Both times at a walk, after bridging it with planks I'd hauled in myself over two days of labor I don't like to remember. The planks would still be there. Planks rot slower than promises in this country.

We hit the gap at speed.

I want to be honest here, since honesty costs me nothing on paper: there was a moment — half a second, maybe less — when the *Wren* was airborne with forty tons of silver and one engineer, and I understood with total clarity that I had bet my life on rotted timber and my own timing, and that this was, by any reasonable accounting, insane.

Then we landed, hard enough to knock my teeth together, and the old line caught us, and we rolled down out of the Kestrel Pass into the flat dark of the valley with the toll gang's lantern shrinking behind us like a bad memory.

I laughed. I always laugh, afterward. It's not joy. It's more like the body filing a complaint it knows will be ignored.

* * *

Hobb met me at the junction an hour before dawn, counting crates by lamplight with the fussy precision of a man who trusts arithmetic more than people. He paid in coin, counted twice, and looked at me the way he always does — like he's trying to work out whether I'm brave or broken and suspecting, correctly, that the answer is yes.

"You look tired, Dravenmoor."

"I look rich. There's a difference."

"There is." He weighed a coin in his palm. "Word is Vane's fleet is buying up salvage rights all along the coast. Whole stretches of line, whole towns' worth of wreck claims. Man's collecting disasters like other men collect stamps."

"Aldous Vane collects whatever he can put a price on," I said. "It's why I stay inland."

"Mountains have prices too."

"Higher ones." I pocketed the last of the coin. "That's rather the point of them."

He grunted and went back to his crates, and I went back to the *Wren*, and for a little while the world was exactly the size I liked it: one engine, one map, one stretch of empty track unspooling toward nowhere in particular. Dawn came up gray over the freight yards, all smoke and gulls and the smell of coal oil, and I sat in the cab with a tin cup of coffee strong enough to trot a horse across, and I let myself do the thing I only do when no one's watching, which is think.

Twenty years. That's what it came to. Twenty years of running lines that don't officially exist, carrying cargo that doesn't officially move, for clients who don't officially know me. I had money buried in three locations I trusted and a fourth I was starting to doubt. I had a reputation in perhaps nine hundred miles of territory, all of it whispered, none of it signed. And I had — I took inventory honestly, because the hour demanded it — no one. No one waiting anywhere. No one whose morning changed because I'd survived the night.

Never stop moving, I reminded myself. It's easier when there's nothing to move away from.

That was when the stationmaster's boy came down the platform looking for me, which was itself remarkable, since very few people know what I look like and fewer admit it. He was maybe fourteen, all elbows and urgency, clutching a telegram envelope like it might hatch.

"You the engineer off the night freight?"

"I'm the wind through the yard," I said. "What do you want?"

"Mister Corben said give this to the woman who runs the ghost trains." He squinted at me. "You her?"

"I'm afraid so." I took the envelope. He lingered, hoping for a story, and got a coin instead, which is the currency children understand best. He ran off satisfied.

The telegram was addressed to *O. DRAVENMOOR, CARE FREIGHTMASTER CORBEN, JUNCTION YARD*, which meant someone had spent real effort finding me, because O. Dravenmoor appears in no directory, holds no license, and exists on paper only as a rumor with good handwriting. I opened it with the caution the address deserved.

> **O.DRAVENMOOR STOP HAVE NEED OF RAIL MAN DISCREET STOP SURVEY PARTY MISSING SABLE REACH STOP WILL PAY DOUBLE YOUR YEAR EARNINGS QUOTED BY THOSE WHO KNOW STOP REPLY THIS OFFICE STOP C.ASHE**

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the cold with my coffee going bitter in my hand, because certain numbers demand verification.

Double my year's earnings. Whoever sent this hadn't guessed. They'd *quoted* — "by those who know" — which meant someone had talked, someone who knew what I charged Hobb or the ore brokers or any of the dozen clients who paid me off the books, and that was a problem I'd deal with later, because right now the number itself was doing something to my judgment that I recognized and distrusted.

Nobody offers double a year's earnings for a missing survey party out of grief. Grief hires criers and lawyers and men to stand in the rain holding umbrellas. Double a year's earnings buys silence, or speed, or discretion — the things I sell. Which meant either this Cora Ashe was desperate past sense, or she wanted something done that couldn't be asked for politely, or both.

And the Sable Reach. Everyone had heard of the Sable Reach, the way everyone had heard of shipwrecks and fevers. A drowned country of marsh and tide-flat at the end of the coastal branch lines, where the government had been running surveys for two years trying to prove the land could hold a rail extension, and where the land, in my professional opinion, had been winning. Tides there moved like weather. Half the old charts marked it *unsurveyable*, which in chart language means *we tried and the sea laughed*.

A survey team lost in the Reach. A widow, presumably — C. Ashe, and the telegram had the particular grammar of a woman writing with her jaw set. And a price that would buy me five years of running without taking another dangerous job, if I wanted out. If I ever wanted out.

I looked down the platform at the *Wren*, steaming gently in the gray light, patient as a dog tied outside a shop. Twenty years of never stopping. And here was a piece of paper offering me enough to stop, wrapped around a destination that promised the opposite of stopping.

"It's a trap," I told her. She vented steam, noncommittal.

The truth was simpler and worse: I was lonely. Not the dramatic kind — I'd have noticed the dramatic kind. The quiet kind, the kind that lives in the fact that a telegram was the most personal communication I'd received in a year, and it was from a stranger asking me to risk my life. And some traitorous part of me, the part that keeps the seat across from the firebox empty, read *C. ASHE* and felt something turn over, slow, like a cold engine catching.

I folded the telegram into my breast pocket, next to the map case, where important things go to be reconsidered.

"Corben!" I called down the platform. The freightmaster looked up from his ledger. "Wire back to that office. One word."

"What word?"

"*Coming.*"

He wrote it down without curiosity, which is why I use Corben. But as I climbed back into the cab and began coaxing the *Wren* around onto the coastal branch — south now, toward the Reach, toward tides and trouble and a woman who paid double for ghosts — I caught myself checking the telegram again, reading the name at the bottom like it was a timetable for a train I hadn't decided to board yet.

Double a year's earnings. Six hundred miles of drowned country. A missing team that the government had already stopped looking for.

Whatever Cora Ashe was buying, it cost too much for grief alone.

I meant to find out what it actually cost. I just didn't yet know that the price would be mine.
