## 2. The Widow's Bargain

The house sat at the end of a lane that the sea was eating one yard a year, which told me something about the woman who lived in it before I ever met her. People who stay in a house like that aren't holding on to comfort. They're holding on to a position. A widow who won't retreat from a losing shoreline is a widow who means to make somebody else pay for the water.

I left the *Wren* on a siding above the village — a rail town that had stopped being a town when the fish stopped, all shuttered canneries and gulls arguing over nothing — and walked down. I could have driven the engine closer, but engines announce you, and I wanted to arrive before my reputation did. That's a small vanity. I've earned worse ones.

The lane was gravel and kelp-rot. The house was white paint gone gray at the salt line, a widow's walk that had never once watched a ship come home, and a garden fighting a rearguard action against the wind. A woman stood in the garden. Not waiting — waiting has a posture, and she didn't have it. She was cutting back rosemary with a knife that had seen work, and she didn't look up until my boots were on her gravel, and then she looked up the way you check a gauge: once, accurately, and back to business.

"Oscar Dravenmoor," she said. Not a question. She'd have had my description from the telegram exchange, and probably from other sources. Cora Ashe struck me as a woman who paid for other sources.

"Ma'am."

"You came."

"You paid half in advance. That's a powerful argument."

The knife went through a rosemary stem with a small green snick. "I half expected you to take it and vanish."

"That's what most people would do with money," I said. "I have a different relationship with it. It mostly passes through me on its way to coal and axle grease."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. A smile's understudy. "Come inside. I don't discuss business where the wind can hear it. The wind in this county works for other people."

I followed her in. The house smelled of rosemary and lamp oil and, underneath it, that particular stillness a house gets when it's been lived in by one person for too long. There were maps on the walls — not good ones, government issue, the kind printed by the thousand — and a photograph on the mantel of a man in surveyor's tweed with a face like a held breath. Careful eyes. The kind of man who checks a measurement twice and then a third time out of politeness to the first two.

"My husband," she said, without following my glance to it. "Nathaniel Ashe. Principal surveyor, Coastal Boundary Commission. He went into the Sable Reach eleven weeks ago with a team of six and a government contract, and the government has now officially concluded that they are, and I'm quoting the letter, *presumed lost to natural causes*."

"Natural causes," I said. "In a group."

"The Reach is very natural." She set the knife down on the kitchen table with a click that ended the rosemary portion of the conversation. "Sit, Miss Dravenmoor. Or do you prefer engineer?"

"Oscar is fine. Titles imply I answer to somebody."

"Then sit, Oscar." She poured coffee without asking, which I approved of. People who ask if you want coffee are giving you a chance to say no, and I've found that most of what goes wrong in my life starts with someone saying no to coffee. "You've run the Reach line. The closed sections."

"Ran it twice. Once before it closed, once after, which is the more instructive trip. The line's still sound through the cut country. The bridges are the problem. And the tide."

"The tide is the whole problem." She sat across from me and put her hands flat on the table, and I noticed her hands — a working woman's hands, calloused at the base of the fingers, ink-stained in the creases. She'd been a clerk, or a bookkeeper, or something that made her fluent in figures. That mattered. Grief doesn't do arithmetic. Bookkeepers do. "The survey site is on the tidal flats below Gray Harrow Point. Twice a day the water comes in over it. Twice a day it goes out. Right now, in this season, the low water exposes the site for roughly four hours. In six days, the spring tides begin, and after that the site is under nine feet of water until autumn."

"So whatever's down there gets a burial the Reach dug for free."

"Exactly." Her eyes came up and held mine, and I'll give her this: she didn't perform anything. No wet lashes, no trembling cup. She'd done her grieving somewhere private and brought me the ledger instead. "The government won't send anyone. The Commission has closed the file. But there are salvage fleets moving toward the Reach right now — I've counted three notices of maritime claim filed in the port registers, all of them claiming rights over wreckage in a region where the only wreck is my husband's survey boat, and a government boat isn't even salvageable property, which means the filings are nonsense, which means the men filing them know something I don't."

"Or they're betting the government won't come check."

"The government doesn't check. That's rather the theme." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, a bookkeeper's gesture, squaring the numbers. "I want you to go in ahead of them. Find the site. Find out what happened to Nathaniel and his team. And if there's anything there that explains why three separate salvage concerns suddenly believe the Reach is worth money — I want that too."

I drank the coffee. It was good, which surprised me, and then didn't — a woman this precise would not keep bad coffee in the house.

"Let me tell you what I see," I said, "and you tell me where I'm wrong. Your husband was a surveyor. Surveyors find things. Boundaries, mostly, but boundaries are just the polite word for *who owns what*. Eleven weeks ago his team found something in the Reach that made three salvage fleets file fraudulent claims to get to it before anyone official did. The government wrote the team off with a phrase so lazy it ought to embarrass them. And you — instead of hiring the law, which is expensive and slow and would tell you no — hired me, because I run closed lines, I don't file reports, and I have no institutional loyalty to betray."

She didn't blink. "You're wrong about one thing."

"Only one?"

"I didn't hire the law because I did hire the law. Four months ago. A magistrate in Port Calloway took my petition, my deposit, and my husband's last letter, and then wrote to me to say the matter was *under consideration*. That was ten weeks ago. I have since learned what *under consideration* costs in this county and who collects the fee." Her voice stayed level. Level the way a rail stays level, right up until it doesn't. "So yes. I hired you because you can't be bought, Miss Dravenmoor. Not because you're honest. Because you're already paid in the only coin you take."

"And what coin is that?"

"Being left alone." She said it without cruelty, which is the only way to say a true thing about a stranger. "You run lines nobody runs. You live in a cab and a ledger. You don't stay anywhere long enough to owe anyone. I am buying the services of a woman who wants nothing, because a woman who wants nothing can't be leveraged."

I set the cup down. It made less noise than her knife had, but I meant it to land the same way.

"You've done your reading."

"I did my husband's reading. He kept files on every contractor the Commission ever considered. You were in there. Three pages. The third page is a list of lines you've run that the Commission itself had written off." She reached under the ledger on the table — and yes, there was a ledger, I'd have been disappointed otherwise — and slid a paper across to me. "Double your year's earnings. Half now, half on delivery. And a bonus I'll discuss if you find my husband."

"Dead or alive?"

"Either. I want *him*, Oscar. Not a report about him. Whatever's left, whatever he found, whatever he was doing in the last hour of his last day. The government gave me a sentence. I'm asking you to give me a man."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the gulls were still arguing over nothing, and the sea was still taking the yard it took every year, and I sat in a stranger's kitchen doing the arithmetic I always do, the cold kind, the kind that keeps me alive: distance to the Reach, condition of the coastal branch, coal aboard, water aboard, the spring tides six days out, and the fact — the uncomfortable fact, filed under *later* — that three salvage fleets with fraudulent claims and armed crews were pointed at the same stretch of drowning country I'd just agreed to enter.

And underneath the arithmetic, the other thing. The thing I don't put in ledgers. That the kitchen was warm, and the coffee was good, and the woman across the table had read my file because her husband had kept one, which meant that somewhere in this world a careful man with held-breath eyes had once sat at this table and said her name, and now she said his the way you touch a rail to check if a train's coming.

Stopping costs more than danger. That's the rule. Danger is just weather — it comes, it goes, it kills the careless. But stopping, staying, letting a house and a table and a warm lamp get their hooks in you — that kills slow, and it kills everything, and I've watched it do it to better people than me.

"Half now," I said. "The other half on delivery, and *delivery* means I put the truth in your hands, not a story you'd prefer. If your husband ran off with a circus, you'll hear about the circus."

"He didn't run off with a circus."

"Ma'am, in eleven weeks I've heard better marriages do worse."

That got the smile's understudy back for a full second before it was called off. "Half now," she agreed. "One condition."

"Conditions are extra."

"This one's free, because it's mine and not yours." She stood and went to the mantel, and took down the photograph of the careful man, and held it a moment before setting it face-down, gently, the way you'd turn a patient. "Whatever you find — and I've imagined everything, so don't spare me — find out whether he was afraid. At the end. I need to know if he was afraid."

I looked at her and understood that this was the real contract, the one not in the ledger, and that no amount of money covered it, and that I was going to try to honor it anyway, because I am, despite considerable effort, a person.

"I'll tell you what I can prove," I said. "The rest I'll tell you honestly."

"That's more than the government offered." She wrote out the draft in a bookkeeper's hand, even and sure, and pressed it into my palm, and her hands were steady, and I thought: *there's the fear. She put it all in the hands so it wouldn't show in the voice.*

I was on the gravel lane walking back up toward the *Wren* when I heard the sea take another bite of the garden's edge, patient as a creditor, and I did my final sum. Six days to the spring tides. Six hundred miles of drowned country. Three fleets, one bought magistrate, one careful dead man, and one widow who paid double because grief alone doesn't move that kind of money.

Whatever Cora Ashe was really buying, I was now contractually obligated to find out.

I climbed into the cab, lit the fire, and was checking the water gauge when I heard it — a small scuff of a boot on gravel behind the tender, quick, then still, the way a sound goes when whoever made it realizes they've made it.

I keep a wrench on the cab floor for exactly this kind of conversation.

I picked it up, turned around, and said to the darkness between the coal boards:

"You can come out now, or you can come out the hard way. One of those options is free."

* * *

Nothing moved for three heartbeats. Then, from behind the coal board, a voice — young, cracked at the edges, working hard to sound like it had never cracked at anything:

"I'm not stealing anything."

"Everybody on a train at midnight is stealing something. Fuel, warmth, a ride, a future. Question is what."

A boy climbed out. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and hollows, with a canvas coat two sizes too big and the particular grimy competence of a kid who's been surviving on his own wits and finding the wits barely sufficient. He stood in the cab doorway with his chin up, which cost him something, and I gave him credit for the cost.

"You're going to the Reach," he said. "The widow's engineer. Everyone at the junction said."

"Everyone at the junction talks more than the junction works. What's it to you?"

He swallowed. I watched him decide whether to lie, watched the lie get as far as his teeth, and watched him — to his credit, or maybe just his exhaustion — spit it back out.

"My dad's on the survey team," he said. "Nathaniel Ashe's team. He's been gone eleven weeks and nobody's doing anything, and I heard the widow hired you, and I—" His voice did the cracking thing again, and he hated it, and I pretended not to notice, because that's a courtesy you extend to the young and the proud. "I'm getting on this train one way or another. I'd rather it was with your permission."

I looked at him a long moment. At the coat. At the boots with the soles worn through and resoled with tire rubber. At the eyes, which were careful eyes, held-breath eyes, and which I recognized, and which I did not say I recognized, because the photograph had been face-down on the mantel and this boy had never seen me look at it.

The Reach was no place for a child. The Reach was no place for me, either, and here I was, lighting the fire.

"Name," I said.

"Finch."

"Finch what?"

"Just Finch. It's the one that's mine."

I should have put him off at the junction. Any sensible engineer would have. I told myself I was going to, right up until I threw the reverser and took the coastal branch south with the boy riding the tender like he'd been born to ballast, and the spring tide clock running down behind us like water through a cracked gauge.

Six days. One stowaway. Three fleets. And a careful dead man whose eyes I'd seen once, on a mantel, in a gray house the sea is eating one yard at a time.

The *Wren* ran south into the dark, and the dark ran all the way to the Reach.
