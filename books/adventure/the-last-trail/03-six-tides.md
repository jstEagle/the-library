## 3. Six Tides

I did my best thinking at tables that weren't mine.

This one belonged to the Harborline Hotel in Graywater Landing, a place where the tea came in tins older than the town and the windows rattled when the freight boats blew their horns. I'd taken the corner table by the window because it had a lamp with a shade that didn't lean, and because from there I could watch the door. Old habit. On a closed line you learn to count exits the way other people count money.

The tide table lay open in front of me, and beside it Cora Ashe's telegram, and beside that a sheet of hotel stationery I'd covered in numbers that would have looked like madness to anyone who hadn't spent eleven years making a living off arithmetic nobody else wanted to do.

Here is what most people don't understand about water: it keeps books. It doesn't care about your grief or your deadline or how much money a widow is willing to pay. It rises when the moon tells it to rise, and it falls on schedule, and if you're standing where it means to be, that's your error, not its. The Sable Reach was the same as any other stretch of drowned coast in that respect — except that the Reach had teeth. Sandbars that migrated like herds. Channels that were six fathoms deep in spring and dry enough to walk across by autumn. A wreck site out there didn't stay put. It drowned, silted over, drowned again, and every cycle buried it a little deeper under clay that would hold a body — or a ledger, or a secret — until the sun burned down.

Which meant this job wasn't really a search. It was a countdown.

I'd been at the table since before dawn, working it through, and by the time the harbor fog started burning off I had the shape of it, and the shape of it was ugly.

* * *

Let me back up. The night before, after Cora Ashe's man left me standing on the platform with a bank draft in my pocket and a bad feeling in my chest, I'd done what I always do with a job I can't decide about: I went to work on it like an engine problem. You don't fix a locomotive by staring at it and feeling things. You open the housing. You look at what's actually there.

So: the survey team. Seven men, government commission, last confirmed position logged at a coastal beacon called Marker Nine, which sat — according to the charts I'd bought off a drunk harbormaster for more than they were worth — at the mouth of the Sable Reach proper. Beyond that, nothing. No radio contact in nineteen days. The government's official position, delivered via a clerk who couldn't have found the Reach on a map with both hands and a guide, was that the team had likely been lost to weather and that further expenditure on recovery was not warranted.

Translation: seven men walked into the water and the paperwork was cheaper than the search.

Except they hadn't walked into the water. Not all of them, not at once, not without leaving anything behind. And salvage fleets — three of them, Cora Ashe said, flying the colors of one Aldous Vane — don't spend coal and crew on a site unless somebody expects something valuable to still be there. Salvage men are the stingiest creatures God ever put on salt water. They don't chase ghosts. They chase cargo.

So either Vane knew something the government didn't, or Vane knew exactly what the government didn't want known, and either way I was going to be standing in the middle of it.

I turned the tide table toward the light and got to work.

* * *

The Sable Reach floods twice a day, but not honestly. The morning tide comes in mean and fast — four hours from slack to high, pushed by currents that funnel between the barrier shoals like water through a broken pipe. The evening tide is slower, lazier, but higher. And then there's the spring cycle. Every fourteen days the moon lines up with the sun and pulls the whole coast up another three feet, and everything that was merely wet becomes unreachable.

I checked the lunar tables. Then I checked them again, because I didn't like the answer the first time.

Spring tide. Nine days out.

That gave me a window — the low-water series before the springs, when the channels around Marker Nine would drop far enough to expose the wreck site's approaches. Six usable low tides. Six chances to get in, find whatever there was to find, and get out before the water took the neighborhood away for a fortnight.

Six tides. Twelve days, if you counted generously, and I didn't, because the last two lows would be compromised by the building spring — water too shallow-margin, channels too unpredictable. Call it five honest tides and one gamble.

Now the other side of the ledger. Distance. From Graywater Landing to the railhead at Cold Sallow Junction: two days' run on my engine, assuming the mountain section held, which it would, because I maintained it myself and I am not modest about that. From Cold Sallow to the coastal depot at Ternholt, where the old survey spur began: another day and a half, slower going, the line there being less a railroad than a rumor with ties. Then the crossing into the Reach itself — and that was the part that made me put the pencil down and rub my eyes.

There was no rail into the Reach. There was a causeway, built thirty years ago for a copper concern that had gone bust before it shipped a single ingot, and the causeway flooded at every high tide. It was passable on foot at low water, barely, in good weather, by someone who knew the timing to the minute. Which meant my engine — my beautiful, stubborn, irreplaceable engine — stopped at Ternholt, and everything past that point happened on foot, carrying everything I'd need, against a clock run by the moon.

I added it up. Travel: four days, maybe five if the weather argued. That left five tides on site, minus whatever time the actual searching ate. Find the camp, read the ground, figure out what happened to seven grown men who vanished off the face of a charted coast.

And do it all while three armed salvage ships worked the same water with better equipment and no interest whatsoever in my continued health.

I looked at the numbers for a long time. They didn't improve with familiarity.

* * *

Here's the thing about my rule — never stop moving — that I don't say out loud very often: it isn't really about danger. Danger is a professional hazard, like boiler scale. You manage it. You respect it. You don't let it make decisions for you.

No, the rule exists because stopping is where the quiet gets in. When I'm running a line at night, there's a clarity to it — the fire, the gauge needles, the rails unspooling ahead of the lamp — and there's no room in that clarity for anything else. But sit me at a table with cold tea and too much time, and the arithmetic changes character. It starts counting other things. Eleven years of empty cabs. A name list of people who used to telegraph me and gradually stopped. The particular silence of a hotel dining room at nine in the morning, when the only other occupant is a waiter pretending not to watch you drink tea straight from the pot because you haven't slept.

The numbers said the job was possible. Barely. Alone? I ran that calculation three times and got the same answer each time, and the answer was *no* — not because the work exceeded one pair of hands, but because the margins didn't allow for error, and I am good, but I am not a miracle. One sprained ankle on the causeway. One tide misjudged by twenty minutes. One patrol skiff with an itchy trigger finger. Any single failure and I'm a story the gulls tell.

I needed someone. And the list of people I trusted enough to stand next to me in rising water was short enough to memorize, and I had just done so, involuntarily, and it had been a depressing exercise.

* * *

The waiter refilled my pot without being asked, which I chose to interpret as pity rather than hospitality.

I spread the charts out again and made myself look at the part I'd been avoiding: the salvage fleet's position. Cora Ashe's information placed Vane's ships at anchor off Ternholt as of four days ago. Four days. Which meant they'd already had four days of low-water access to the site that I wouldn't reach for another five. If they were competent, they'd have found whatever was findable by now. If they were thorough, they'd have found it and destroyed it. And if they were Aldous Vane —

If they were Aldous Vane, then the survey team hadn't been lost to weather at all, and the thing I was racing toward wasn't wreckage. It was evidence. Evidence that somebody had already killed seven men to suppress, guarded by the kind of fleet that doesn't fly insurance-company colors, and I'd be walking into it with a rucksack, a revolver with five rounds because I'd never gotten around to buying a sixth box, and a tide table.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone at the table, and the waiter decided he had somewhere else to be.

Because here was the ridiculous truth underneath all the grim arithmetic: I was going anyway. I'd known it since the platform, since before the platform, probably since the moment I'd read the words *double your year's earnings* and felt not greed but relief — relief that there was finally a reason to stop moving that wasn't just stopping. Cora Ashe hadn't hired a salvage expert. She'd hired a woman who'd been running away from a quiet room for eleven years and would have eventually run in any direction at all. She'd just happened to point me at the one direction with murder in it.

But knowing I was going and planning to survive it were different problems, and only one of them yielded to stubbornness.

* * *

I spent the afternoon on logistics, which is the coward's way of saying I spent it avoiding the real question. Rations for eight days. Rope, lamps, the good waterproof wraps for the instruments. Ammunition — fine, I'd buy the sixth box, the universe could stop smirking. Charts copied onto oilcloth so the salt spray wouldn't turn them to porridge. And the tide tables themselves, transcribed from memory onto a card I could keep in my breast pocket, because the original was already too well-thumbed to trust.

By dusk I had a load-out list that weighed roughly what a small horse weighs, and a route plan with margins so thin you could shave with them, and a growing conviction that I was missing something obvious.

It arrived with the lamplighter.

Not him specifically — he was a boy of maybe fifteen doing a job badly — but the sight of him reminded me: the survey team had a support contract. Government surveys travel with a supply arrangement, a scheduled resupply from the nearest railhead. Which meant someone at Ternholt had been receiving — or failing to receive — shipments addressed to seven men who'd stopped answering their radio nineteen days ago. Someone at Ternholt had records. Manifests. Dates. Possibly even letters, if any of the team had written home through the depot.

And someone at Ternholt might remember whether those seven men had seemed afraid.

I added it to the plan: half a day at Ternholt, no more, talking to whoever kept the depot ledgers. Half a day I didn't have, spent on a question I couldn't afford to skip, because the difference between walking into an accident and walking into a trap was exactly the kind of thing a depot clerk notices and a tide table doesn't.

* * *

Full dark now. The harbor lights came up one by one across the water, and somewhere out in the black the Reach was doing what it always did — filling, emptying, keeping its books — and seven men were either in it or beyond it, and a woman in a mourning dress was sitting in a house full of unanswered questions, waiting on a stranger she'd hired with money she probably couldn't spare.

I thought about her face when she'd talked about her husband. Not grief, exactly. Grief has a softness to it. What she'd had was worse — the flat certainty of a woman who has already been told her husband is dead by people who smiled while saying it, and who knows, in the marrow, that they were lying, and who has been informed by every official channel available that her certainty is inconvenient.

You can't argue with that kind of certainty. Believe me, I've tried arguing with my own.

I folded the charts. I settled the bill, overtipping the waiter out of guilt for the laughing. And I stood at the window a moment longer, looking north toward the mountains where my engine waited in a rented shed, and I made the last entry in the night's ledger:

Five days' travel. Five tides on site. Three hostile ships. One witness, possibly, if the empty space where hope should go counted as a witness. Zero margin.

And one more thing, which I wrote nowhere but carried out the door with me: I could not do this alone, and the only person I could think of to ask was a widow with no reason left to trust anybody, and the only person she could think of had been me.

The math was ugly all the way through. But it pointed one direction, and I've never been able to resist a line that goes somewhere.

I'd catch the dawn freight out of Graywater, transfer at Miller's Halt, and be standing in front of Cora Ashe's door by tomorrow night with a plan, a deadline, and a request I was already dreading having to make — because the plan had a hole in it shaped exactly like a second pair of hands, and I had a terrible suspicion about whose hands I was going to end up needing.

Somewhere between the hotel door and the platform, it started to rain. Of course it did. The water keeps its books, and apparently mine were overdue.
