## 4. The Stowaway Boy

I left Saltmere Junction at four in the morning, which is the hour I like best, because it is the hour when nobody else is awake to make demands of me. The fog sat on the yards like a wet blanket on a sleeping animal, and my engine — the *Kestrel*, a narrow-gauge shay older than most governments and twice as stubborn — breathed steam into the dark in long, slow sighs, as if she too resented being roused.

Cora Ashe's money was folded into an oilskin packet under the firebox door, where I keep things I don't want to think about. Her husband's last coordinates were chalked on a slate wedged beside my throttle. Six tides. That was the arithmetic of it. Everything else was weather.

I ran her out of the yard slow, past the water tower with its rotted legs, past the signal hut where some long-dead signalman had carved his initials into the frame and where no light had burned in eleven years. The rails sang that low note they make when the frost is coming out of them, and I let myself have the small, private pleasure of hearing it alone. That is the other reason for the four o'clock departure. The road at dawn is mine. No dispatchers, no toll gangs, no voices asking questions. Just me and the iron and the country opening up ahead like a book nobody else has read.

Which is why it irritated me profoundly when, somewhere past milepost nine, I heard a cough.

Not from the engine. Engines cough, but this was a human cough, wet and young and trying very hard to be quiet, which is the loudest kind there is. It came from behind the spare coal bunker, back by the tender, where a person could wedge themselves between the water tank and the tool locker and stay dry and mostly invisible, provided the engineer never turned around.

I turned around.

He was maybe fifteen, sixteen at the outside, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with hair the color of wet sand and eyes that had the particular shine of a boy who has not slept properly in several days. He had a canvas satchel clutched to his chest like a life preserver, and he looked at me the way a man looks at a judge: braced for the sentence.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," I said. "How long you been back there?"

"Since Saltmere."

"That's eleven miles."

"Ten and a half," he said. "You stopped at the spring tank."

I will grant him this: he knew his business. He'd boarded during the one moment I wasn't watching, ridden the worst stretch of the old line without falling off, and hadn't made a sound until his body betrayed him. In another life, in a better economy, that's a railroad man.

In this life, he was dead weight on a job where dead weight gets people killed.

"Name?" I asked.

"Finch."

"Finch what?"

"Just Finch." He lifted his chin a fraction. "You're Oscar Dravenmoor. The map woman."

"I'm the woman who's about to stop this engine and put you off it."

"You can't stop here," he said, and pointed ahead through the fog with a certainty that annoyed me more than anything else he could have said. "There's a washout at the culvert past the bend. Bottom's gone soft. You want to be over it at speed or not at all."

I looked. He was right. There *was* a culvert past the bend, and the bottom *was* gone soft — I knew it because my map said so, in my own hand, in ink I'd mixed myself so it wouldn't run. But no stowaway who'd been hiding behind a coal bunker in the dark should know that.

"Who told you about my map?" I said.

"Everybody's told me about your map," he said. "Rail men, dock men, smugglers. My father used to say—" He stopped. His jaw worked once, and whatever the rest of that sentence was, he swallowed it whole.

"Your father," I repeated.

"He's with the survey team," Finch said. "Sable Reach. Thomas Finch. Assistant surveyor. He writes me letters every month, except he hasn't written since September, and everybody says they're lost, and the government says they're delayed, and the salvage fleets say they're salvage." His voice stayed level right up until the last word, and then it cracked, just slightly, like rail steel under too much weight. "So I'm going to find him."

* * *

Here is what I should have done: stopped at the next flat stretch, set him off with a warning and half my lunch, and kept moving. That is my rule. Never stop moving, never take passengers, never take on anybody whose trouble might stick to you. I've watched good engineers die of exactly that — of stopping, of turning back, of caring one degree too much about something on the tracks behind them.

But he'd known about the culvert. And he'd said *my father used to say* about my map, in the voice of a boy quoting scripture, and there was a letter in that satchel — I saw the corner of it when he shifted it — addressed in a careful draftsman's hand, the hand of a man who drew lines for a living.

So instead I said, "Ride as far as Harrow Junction. Then you're off."

"Then I'm off," he agreed, in the tone of a boy agreeing to nothing.

* * *

Harrow Junction is not a junction anymore. It's two platforms, a water crane, and a station house with a roof like a broken promise. The main line curves away south toward the coast; my line — the closed line, the Sable Reach spur — goes north into country where the rails disappear under gorse and the only traffic is foxes and weather.

I brought the *Kestrel* to a stand under the platform canopy, which leaned out over the track like a drunk over a fence, and I pointed at the southbound curve.

"There's a freight comes through at nine. Brakemen are decent enough if you keep out of sight. It'll put you in Corvess by nightfall, and from Corvess you can get anywhere."

"I don't want to get anywhere," Finch said. "I want to get to Sable Reach."

"The Reach is a graveyard with tides," I told him. "The survey team went in with government papers, government rifles, and government confidence, and none of those came out. Salvage fleets are circling the site right now with armed crews and lawyers. And in six tides the whole place floods deep enough to drown every answer down there for good. That's not a place for a boy with a satchel."

"My father's in it."

"Your father is probably not in anything," I said, and immediately wished I'd found a kinder way to say it, because the truth was already doing its work in his face. "Finch. Listen to me. If they're alive, they've moved inland. If they're not, then what's down there is evidence, and evidence needs someone who knows how to carry it out, not someone who knows how to grieve. Go home. Wait for word. That's not cowardice. That's the harder thing."

He stood very still on the platform, and I could see him deciding something, and I made the mistake of thinking I knew what it was.

"All right," he said quietly.

I took on water, checked the sand dome, walked the running gear with my lamp while he stood there being obediently still. And then I climbed back into the cab, opened the regulator, and ran her north onto the Reach spur, and did not look back at the platform, because looking back is how engineers end up carrying ghosts.

I got three miles before I felt it.

A shay like the *Kestrel* tells you everything. She's geared, she's slow, she talks to you through the frame — every pound of weight, every shift of load, translated up through the trucks into the cab floor into the soles of your boots. And three miles out of Harrow, my boots told me there were forty pounds of stowaway sitting on the rear truck beam, tucked up under the tender frame where the spray can't reach, riding the one spot on the whole engine where a small person can hang on through anything.

I could have stopped. I could have walked back and dragged him off and left him standing on the ballast with his satchel and his letter, three miles from anywhere, at seven in the morning, in November.

Instead I did the arithmetic. If I stopped, I lost twenty minutes. If I lost twenty minutes, I lost the tide window at the drowned cut. If I lost the tide window, Cora Ashe's money bought nothing but a longer list of names for a stonecutter. And if I threw him off hard enough that he broke something, then somewhere down the line of my conscience — which I keep, like the oilskin packet, in a place I don't look — there would be a mark that didn't wash out.

Also — and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is cheaper now than it used to be — also, he quoted his father about my map. And a man who raises a son who rides engines that well, and reads washouts that well, and holds his grief that straight-backed, is a man I would have liked to meet. Which meant I owed the son something. I just hadn't decided what.

So I reached back with one arm, rapped twice on the tender frame, and shouted over the gears: "If you're going to ride my engine, you ride in the cab where I can see you! Move!"

There was a scramble, a clatter, and then he swung up over the coal and dropped into the fireman's seat, breathless, triumphant, sand-haired, grinning like the sunrise had come up inside him.

"You knew I was there," he said.

"I suspected. Now I know. There's a difference, and the difference is that now you're my problem." I handed him the oil can. "Congratulations. Firemen work for their passage. Learn the injector before we hit the gradient, or learn to walk."

* * *

We ran north through the morning, and the country changed around us the way it does on the Reach spur — fields giving way to scrub, scrub giving way to black pine, the air off the marshes getting into everything, salt and rot and cold iron. I put him to work. He oiled what I told him to oil. He learned the injector on the second try, which is one try better than most grown men. He asked questions in bursts, the way boys do — *what's a reversing lever, why do you call her the Kestrel, have you really run the Cordillera line at night* — and then went silent for long stretches, holding his father's letter in both hands and reading it again even though he must have known it by heart.

Around noon, with the pines closing in and the rails running straight as a sermon, he finally asked the question he'd been sitting on since Harrow.

"Why are you doing it? The widow's job. Everyone says you don't take jobs. Everyone says you especially don't take jobs where people died."

I watched the rail ahead for a while. There's an art to answering questions honestly without giving anything away, and I've practiced it for years, mostly on myself.

"Because she paid double," I said.

"That's not it."

"It's a very good price."

"That's not it," he said again, with the fearless stupidity of the young. "My dad wrote that you turned down the Vane contract. Three years' pay to map their recovery routes, and you turned it down flat. Nobody turns down Aldous Vane. So money isn't the thing that moves you."

I'll admit the letter surprised me. Not that Vane had offered — Vane collects people the way other men collect stamps, and he'd wanted my maps for years — but that a surveyor's assistant in a government camp had heard about it, and passed it to his son, and the son had carried it like a coin in his pocket all this way.

"No," I said finally. "Money isn't the thing that moves me."

"Then what?"

"Stopping costs more than danger," I said. "That's the whole philosophy. A train standing still is a target. A person standing still is worse. Your father's team stopped, and something found them. Mrs. Ashe stopped, and grief moved into the house and won't leave. Me — I keep moving because the day I stop is the day I have to sit down with everything I've ever run away from, and I know for a fact I'd lose." I glanced at him. "You're the exception that proves the rule. You're the first thing in eleven years I couldn't talk myself out of stopping for."

He looked at me like I'd handed him something breakable. Then he folded his father's letter away, very carefully, along its worn creases, and picked the oil can back up.

"Harrow Junction," he said. "You tried to leave me there."

"I did."

"And I got back on."

"You got back on."

"So what happens next time you try?"

I fed a knot of pine into the firebox and watched it catch. Somewhere ahead, under the fog and the black trees, the old line ran on toward Ghost Station, toward the flooded cut, toward whatever was left of six men who had stopped moving at the wrong moment. And riding beside me in the fireman's seat was a boy with his father's handwriting in his pocket and no idea — none at all — what we were about to find.

"Next time," I said, "I won't try."
