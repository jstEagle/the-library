## 5. Ghost Station

The line into the Sable Reach had been closed for eleven years, and it wore its abandonment like a badge of honor. Brush grew up through the joints of the rails in green whiskers. A culvert had sagged until the track dipped through it like a spine with a bad vertebra. Somewhere around mile forty, a fallen pine lay across both rails at exactly the height of a man's chest, as if the forest had finally gotten around to posting a proper warning sign.

I ran her slow. Fifteen miles an hour, maybe twelve on the curves, with Finch riding the pilot and calling out obstructions like a boy calling out birds — "Log! Big one!" — as though I might somehow fail to notice a tree the size of a church pew.

"See it," I called back, mostly to keep him from leaning out any further than he already was.

He'd earned his seat, I'll grant him that much. Two days earlier he'd outwaited me at Halloway Junction, sitting patient as a fencepost on my own coal tender while I stood in the agent's office congratulating myself on having shed him. When I'd climbed back aboard and found him there, arms crossed, dust on his collar, he hadn't begged and he hadn't apologized. He'd simply said, "You're going where my father went. Either I ride or I walk, and you don't strike me as the kind who'd let me walk."

Which was true, damn him. I've got exactly one soft spot and he'd found it inside of four days, which was either talent or fate, and I didn't have time to figure out which.

So now there were two of us on the last closed line in the territory, chasing six tides and a dead man's trail, and I will confess that the engine felt less empty than usual. That should have comforted me. Instead it made me watchful, because in my experience the things you start caring about are the things the Reach likes best to take.

* * *

The station appeared out of the fog at half past noon, and it took us both a moment to understand what we were looking at.

It shouldn't have been there. My map said nothing but wilderness for the next nine miles — no siding, no water stop, no reason for a man to ever set foot off the rails. But there it stood in a clearing the forest had begun to reclaim: a single-story frame building with a platform, a signal mast, and a water tower whose legs had rotted down to shadows of themselves. Paint gone gray. Windows dark. The whole thing sitting in its clearing quiet as a held breath.

"That's not on your map," Finch said, sliding down from the pilot. He said it carefully, the way you'd point out that the sky had turned green. Not accusing. Just establishing, together, that we were both seeing it.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

My map was the only complete record of the closed lines in existence, and I'll say without false modesty that it was good. I'd walked or ridden every mile of it. If there'd been a station here when I surveyed this stretch, I'd have marked it. Which meant one of two things: either my map had failed me for the first time in its life, or this place had been built since.

Neither possibility sat well.

I brought the engine to a stop two hundred yards short — never park blind beside a structure you can't see into; that's not superstition, that's bookkeeping — and we walked the rest, Finch carrying the lantern though the sun was still up. Fog in the Reach doesn't wait for dark. It just arrives when it feels like it and stays as long as it pleases, and by the time we reached the platform edge we were moving through milk.

The station had no name board. It had no ticket window, either — just a plank door, unlocked, opening onto a room that stopped me in the doorway with my hand still on the jamb.

Someone had been living here. Recently.

There was a cot against the far wall, blanket folded square. A table with a tin plate and fork, washed and dried. A shelf holding cans of beans and peaches and condensed milk, their labels facing out, arranged by size. A stove, cold, but swept clean of ash. In the corner, a stack of firewood cut to even lengths, split faces all turned the same way, like soldiers at inspection.

Nobody lives like that. Nobody *can* live like that. Men who live rough develop a certain comfortable chaos — boots where they land, cans opened in the order hunger demands. This was different. This was order imposed from outside, or order kept by someone terrified of leaving a trace out of place.

"Oscar." Finch had drifted to the back of the room, to a second door I'd taken for a closet. He was standing very still. "You need to look at this."

* * *

The back room had been a freight office once, and it was where the survey team had lived, because their gear was everywhere — and that was the second thing that stopped my blood.

Their packs stood against the wall, five of them, upright, buckled, packed. Theodolite in its case, brass fittings bright with oil, tripod strapped and coiled. Transit books stacked with their spines aligned. A rifle rack — two rifles present, cleaned and slotted, and two empty slots. Ration crates stenciled with the government survey mark, lids nailed down, unopened. A man's spare coat hung on a nail. Another man's pipe rested in a dish on the table, alongside a pouch of tobacco folded over twice to keep it fresh.

Everything cared for. Everything ready. Everything waiting for its owner to come back from wherever he'd gone.

"They didn't leave," I said slowly, working it through aloud because saying things helps me think and thinking things alone has never once helped me sleep. "If they'd abandoned the post, they'd have taken the packs. You don't walk out of the Reach without your rations. And if they'd been driven out in a hurry—" I gestured at the pipe, the coat, the oiled instruments. "Nobody fleeing leaves their pipe."

"So they meant to come back," Finch said.

"They meant to come back within the hour." I picked up the transit book on top of the stack, and that was the third thing.

The page was dated nine days before Cora Ashe's telegram reached me. The entries were neat, professional, the hand steady: bearings taken, elevations logged, a note about weather, a note about mule feed running short. The final entry ended mid-sentence — not crossed out, not finished, just *stopped*, the way a clock stops. The ink wasn't smeared. There was no blot where a pen might have been set down hard. The nib had simply lifted from the paper between one word and the next, and whoever held it had never come back to finish.

Nine days. The team vanished nine days ago, and everything they owned was still here, still orderly, still *maintained*. Somebody had been coming back. Sweeping the stove. Squaring the cans. Oiling the theodolite so it wouldn't pit.

"Who does that?" Finch asked quietly. He was fourteen and he'd grown up around rail camps, and I could hear in his voice that he already suspected the answer and hated it.

"Somebody making sure the story stays tidy," I said. "Somebody who needs this room to look exactly the way it looked the day five men walked out of it."

* * *

We went outside and searched the ground, and the ground told us the rest.

Fog keeps prints better than sun does, and the clay around the station held them in plenty — my boot prints, Finch's smaller ones, the scuff of our own coming and going. Beneath those, older, half-fuzzed at the edges by damp: boot prints. Several sets. Heavy-soled, iron-heeled, the kind issued by shipping lines and salvage outfits, not government survey crews. They circled the station. They went in and came out. They converged on the platform and then, all together, they led away north along the old right-of-way, five men walking abreast in step, and then—

Nothing.

No drag marks. No blood. No signs anywhere of what happens when one group of men persuades another group of men to go somewhere they'd rather not. No tracks of the surveyors themselves leaving under guard, which is the detail that mattered most and which took me three circuits of the clearing to confirm beyond doubting. Whatever happened to Elias Wren's crew, it happened somewhere else, and these iron-heeled prints were the only traffic this station had seen since.

And the surveyors' own boots? Their boots were inside, lined up beneath their packs.

Five pairs. Neatly.

Men do not walk into the Sable Reach in their socks.

Finch stood at the edge of the clearing staring north down the vanished right-of-way, where the rails gave out into brush and the brush gave out into fog, and I watched him try to make his face do what mine was doing, which was nothing, which is harder than it sounds. His father had worn one of those pairs of boots. His father had written letters about manifests and wrongness, and now his father's boots were standing at attention under a pack in a ghost station while strangers' footprints marched off north in perfect formation, and there was not one mark on this earth to say where any of them had gone.

"We should burn it down," he said suddenly.

I turned. "What?"

"The station." His voice cracked and steadied itself. "If somebody wants it to look like nobody was ever here, then—"

"Then burning it tells them somebody was here," I said. "Ash is a message too, boy. A bigger one than dust." I put my hand on his shoulder, briefly, because he was shaking and pretending not to, and because I remembered being fifteen and learning what the world did to people who asked honest questions. "We leave it exactly as we found it. We touch nothing else. As far as anyone knows, nobody's been here but the wind."

He nodded. He didn't ask the question hanging over both of us, so I answered it anyway, because leaving it unsaid would be crueler.

"Your father walked out of here alive," I said. "Whatever happened next happened to all five of them together, quick, and somewhere we haven't found yet. And the people who did it have spent nine days dusting the shelves." I looked north, into the fog, where the iron heels had gone. "That's not a search party's work, Finch. Search parties want to find people. Somebody wanted five men *unmade* — erased so clean that even their boots stayed behind like they were coming back for supper."

The fog thickened around the clearing as if it agreed with me.

* * *

We made the engine by late afternoon and ran another six miles before the light quit, banking her in a rock cut with the fire banked low and the wheels chocked. Supper was beans and a argument about whether fog could freeze — Finch maintained it could, I maintained he'd feel differently about it at three in the morning — and then he rolled into his bedroll under the tender like a pup circling twice, and was asleep before his breathing settled.

I stayed up with the map.

Not my master map — that stayed hidden, as always, sewn into its oilskin, because a mapmaker without her originals is just a woman with opinions about geography. I worked from memory instead, tracing the line north from the ghost station in my head. Nine miles on, the right-of-way climbed toward the ridge above Graywater Sound. From the ridge, on a clear day, you could see the whole lower Reach — and if Cora Ashe's coordinates were honest, the wreck site lay just beyond it, in the shallows the tide tables said would drown for good on the sixth tide.

Five tides left now. Maybe five and a half, if the moon was feeling generous.

I sat with my back against the firebox's fading warmth and did the arithmetic I always did, the cold ledger of survival: distance, fuel, water, hours. And then, because the ghost station had knocked something loose in me, I did a different sum, the kind I usually refuse to do. I added up what we actually knew.

A survey team erased. A station maintained by unseen hands. Boot prints marching north in step. An empty rifle rack — two guns left, two guns gone, carried off by men who wanted witnesses removed but tools preserved. And somewhere ahead, a salvage fleet moving faster than honesty requires.

Every road of evidence pointed the same direction. North. Toward the wreck. Toward whatever the survey team had found that was worth five lives and nine days of housekeeping to bury.

Finch stirred in his sleep and muttered something — a word, half-formed, that might have been *Papa* or might have been nothing — and pulled the blanket to his chin.

I banked the fire to embers, checked the chocks a fourth time out of pure habit, and settled back against the warm iron.

That was when I heard it. Faint, far off, threading through the fog from the north like a needle through cloth: the deep, rhythmic thud of steam-driven winches, and beneath it, thinner, the whistle of a ship's boat carrying orders across open water.

Somewhere past the ridge, in the dark, Aldous Vane's fleet was already at work.

And we were two days behind it, on a dead line, with five tides left and a boy asleep under my coal who deserved better than what was waiting north in the fog.
