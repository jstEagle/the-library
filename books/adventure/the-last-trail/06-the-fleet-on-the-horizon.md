## 6. The Fleet on the Horizon

There's a particular kind of quiet that lives on a ridge above the sea, and it isn't the friendly kind. It's the quiet of a place that has been left alone so long it has forgotten how to be looked at. Finch and I climbed out of the gorse at first light, our boots wet with dew and something worse — the low fog off the Reach had soaked through everything by the time we'd walked an hour, and I was already missing my engine the way you miss a coat in winter.

The Sable Reach opened below us like a held breath.

I'd seen it on charts a hundred times. Charts lie. They draw the water as a flat blue nothing between the headlands, tidy as a ledger line. What they don't tell you is that the Reach is a drowned country — old roads, old farms, an entire valley given over to the tide when the government decided a shipping channel was worth more than the people living in it. At low water you can still see rooftops under the surface, pale as knuckles. At high water you see nothing at all, which is exactly how the government prefers it.

"Which one's the wreck?" Finch asked, squinting into the haze.

"There." I pointed to where a crane arm broke the horizon, thin as a heron's leg. "Three miles out, give or take. The *Meridian Star*." I said the name flat, because names are just handles, and this one belonged to a coffin.

He stared at it a long time. His father had stood on that deck once, or near enough. I watched the boy do the arithmetic every child of the lost does — the distance, the impossibility, the smallness of himself against all that water. Then he did what his father's letters would have wanted him to do. He squared up.

"They're working it already," he said.

"They're not working it." I passed him the field glasses. "Look at the cranes. Look at the barges. Salvage crews strip a wreck slow and greedy, like scavengers on a carcass. This lot's moving cargo *off* the site, not iron *up* from it."

He raised the glasses. I watched his shoulders change.

"That's not salvage," he said slowly.

"No," I said. "That's housekeeping."

* * *

We lay in the gorse another hour while the sun burned the fog off, and I made myself study the fleet the way I study any line before I run it: coldly, completely, looking for the flaw.

Three ships. The flagship sat closest to the wreck — a black-hulled steam collier converted for lifting work, big enough to carry a small town's worth of machinery and ugly enough to carry its reputation. Painted along her stern, in letters you could read from shore if you cared to: *VANE*. Below the name, the registration numbers had been freshly painted over. Not weathered. *Painted over.* Ships that intend to be identified don't do that. Ships that intend to be remembered only as a rumor do.

A second vessel was a flat-deck barge tug, riding high and empty, waiting to be filled. And the third —

The third was the one that told me everything.

She was a patrol skiff, maybe sixty feet, fast-lined, with a deck gun mount that had been fitted recently enough that the paint around the bolts hadn't faded to match. She ran a lazy figure-eight around the other two, wide circles, the way a sheepdog works a flock. Except there was no flock. There were no fishing boats in the Reach this morning, no coastal traders, no mail packet. Nothing else on the water at all.

An honest salvage operation wants witnesses. Witnesses mean legitimacy, means the insurance men and the admiralty court and the port authority all saw you doing honest work in honest waters. A salvage master who finds something valuable *wants* the world watching, because the world watching is what turns a find into a fortune instead of a lawsuit.

This fleet had cleared the water.

"You said Vane bought the salvage rights," Finch said.

"I said he filed them." I kept the glasses on the skiff. "Rights are paper. Paper needs a court to mean anything. What he's doing out there doesn't need a court. It needs the opposite of a court."

"What's the opposite of a court?"

"A clean horizon."

Finch was quiet a moment. Then, because he was fifteen and boys that age ask the questions grown men have learned to swallow: "You think they killed them. My father's team. You think it already happened."

I could have lied to him. Some part of me — the part that had been a person once, before the rails got into me — reached for a lie the way a hand reaches for a rail in the dark. But I'd learned the hard way that lies are just debts with better handwriting, and I was tired of carrying other people's.

"I think a survey team doesn't vanish leaving its gear stacked neat as a parade ground," I said. "I think a fleet doesn't paint over its own name unless it plans to stop being a fleet. And I think Aldous Vane didn't get rich by letting loose ends float." I lowered the glasses. "Yeah, kid. I think it already happened."

He nodded. He didn't cry. I found myself respecting him for it, and then catching myself, because respect is the first step toward caring, and caring about people is how you end up stopped somewhere, and my whole life was built on never stopping.

* * *

We came down off the ridge through a fold in the land where an old coast road used to run before the water took it. That was the plan, such as it was: skirt the shoreline east, keep to the dead ground behind the headlands, reach the survey camp's last coordinates by nightfall. The engine was cached two miles back in a cutting, hidden under netting and cut brush, because you don't bring a locomotive to a coastline patrolled by a man with a gun platform.

I want it noted that the plan was sound. I want it noted that I have run closed lines through blizzards, washouts, and one memorable incident involving a landslide and a wedding party, and I have never once been caught where I shouldn't be. I want it noted, because what happened next was not my fault, and history should record that clearly.

The dog barked.

It came from a fisherman's hut tucked into the cove below the old road — a hut that hadn't been on any chart I owned, which meant it wasn't supposed to exist, which meant whoever lived in it was either hiding from the law or employed by someone who was. The dog was a rangy yellow thing with a voice like a foghorn having opinions, and it announced us to the entire eastern seaboard.

"Move," I said, and we moved.

We were fifty yards along the road when the hut door banged open and a man stepped out in oilskins, and behind him, down in the cove, a boat engine coughed to life.

Not *a* boat. *The* boat. I knew the sound of that skiff's engine now — I'd heard it circling for an hour, learned its rhythm the way you learn a rival engine's exhaust note. It was already turning toward shore.

"He saw us on the ridge," Finch panted beside me. "Through the glasses. He's been watching the ridge."

Of course he had. Any competent patrol officer watches the high ground, because the high ground is where trouble looks from. I had committed the cardinal sin of my profession: I'd assumed I was the watcher. On my own lines, in my own country, I am always the watcher. Here, I was a stranger on somebody else's map, and somebody else's map always beats yours.

The road bent ahead around a shoulder of rock, and beyond the bend, I knew from the contours, it dropped toward a shingle beach — open ground, visible from the water, a killing floor. Behind us, the skiff was coming on fast, that engine note climbing.

Options. I run options the way other people run prayers.

Fight: two of us, one rifle I'd brought for snakes, against a gun platform. No.

Hide: the gorse was thin here, the ground open. No.

Run: they had an engine; we had boots. No — unless the running had a shape to it. Unless the running went somewhere their engine couldn't follow.

"The culvert," I said. "Back the way we came. Where the road crosses the stream. How wide is that pipe?"

Finch blinked at me. "What pipe?"

"The one you splashed through. Thirty yards back. Don't tell me you walked over a bridge without looking under it."

"It's — narrow. Round. Maybe four feet."

Four feet. I'm not a large woman, but I'm not built for four feet either, and neither was the boy. But four feet of corrugated iron under six feet of earth is invisible to a boat, uninteresting to a searchlight, and — most importantly — it comes out the other side.

"In," I said.

We went in. I'll spare you the details except to say that cold stream water in early spring is a religion, and I am now a convert, and corrugated iron has edges that a person remembers for weeks. We came out the far side into a stand of alder, dripping, filthy, and alive, and I pressed us flat against the bank while the skiff's bow ground up onto the shingle below and men spilled out onto the road.

There were five of them. Not navy — navy men wear their authority like a uniform. These wore plain canvas jackets and carried themselves like men paid by the result. One of them had a repeating shotgun slung across his back like it was a farming tool, which is the most honest thing I saw all day.

They spread out and worked the road with the efficiency of professionals. Checked the hut man's story — I caught fragments: *two of them, ridge, heading east* — and then fanned out along the bend. Toward the beach. Toward the open ground where two figures on foot should have been very visible.

One of them stopped at the culvert.

I put my hand over Finch's mouth before he knew he was breathing loud. Through the alder branches I watched the man walk to the stream, look at the water, look at the pipe. He stood there long enough for me to count my regrets, which took a while, because I discovered I had more than I'd budgeted for.

Then he pulled something from his pocket, and my heart stopped, and it was only a tin of tobacco, and he crouched on the bank and rolled a cigarette, and looked at the pipe, and looked at the water, and got up and walked away.

Because here is the thing about men who work for Aldous Vane: they are paid to find things, but they are not paid to wonder. Wondering is expensive. Wondering asks *why would anyone crawl through a drain?* and the answer is *because they know what we are*, and a man who lets himself think that thought starts asking what exactly it is he's being paid to protect, and men who ask that question don't stay on Vane's payroll long. They hire thorough hands and careful eyes, but they screen out imagination at the door.

The skiff shoved off twenty minutes later. We stayed in the alder another hour anyway, because I don't gamble on twenty minutes when an hour is affordable.

* * *

We walked east along the dead ground until dark, and we didn't talk much, and that suited me fine, because I was doing sums.

Here's what the morning had taught me, laid out clean:

Vane's fleet was armed. Not "carrying sidearms for sharks" armed — gun-platform, five-man search parties, painted-over-name armed. That's not deterrence. Deterrence is for rivals, and Vane had no rivals out there; he'd cleared the water himself. That hardware had exactly one purpose, and the purpose was making sure that anything that saw too much stopped seeing.

Vane's fleet was fast. Faster than any honest operation needs, faster than the tides require, faster than patience. Honest salvage waits for calm water and good light. Vane was working through weather windows like a man racing a deadline — and I knew his deadline, because it was mine. Six tides. Whatever he was pulling off that wreck, whatever evidence the survey team had died carrying, he wanted it gone before the site flooded and the sea closed the book for him permanently.

And Vane's fleet was thorough. A patrol skiff sweeping the shore within hours of two strangers showing on a ridge three miles away. Either Vane had spotters inland — likely, given the hut — or he simply operated on the principle that no witness is a live witness, and any silhouette on any skyline gets investigated as a matter of course.

Put those together and you get a simple sentence, and I said it out loud that night, sitting against the engine's warm flank with Finch asleep inside the cab and the stars doing their indifferent rotation overhead:

"He's not hiding the wreck from us. He's hiding the *reason*."

The survey team hadn't stumbled onto salvage. They'd stumbled onto the thing underneath the salvage — whatever the *Meridian Star* was really carrying, or really carrying evidence of, that made a man like Aldous Vane spend gun platforms and painted-over names to keep it drowned. The team had found it, written it down, and been erased. And now the erasure was being finished, load by load, before the tide could preserve the crime scene forever.

Which meant the race wasn't for the wreck anymore.

The race was for whatever the team had *found* — their instruments, their notes, their final camp. Because paper drowns slower than men, and Vane knew it, and that's why his skiff was sweeping the shoreline instead of standing over the cranes.

Somewhere between that ridge and the survey camp's last coordinates, there was a record of what happened out on that water. And somewhere between me and that record were however many men Vane could afford, all of them paid to make sure a woman with a map and a boy with a grudge never got close enough to read it.

I banked the fire low and looked at the sleeping boy, and I'll admit something I don't admit often: for about ten seconds, I considered putting him on a westbound freight at dawn and finishing this alone. Cleaner. Safer. For him.

Then I remembered him lying in the gorse with the field glasses, squaring his shoulders at a coffin three miles out, and I knew two things. First, that he'd only stow away again, and next time on something worse than my engine. Second — and this is the part I liked less — that I didn't actually want to send him away, and a woman in my line can't afford to start wanting things, because wanting things is how you end up stopped somewhere.

Six tides. Five and a half now.

At dawn we'd move again, east along the dead ground, toward the coordinates, toward whatever the survey team had died knowing. And somewhere behind us, patient as arithmetic, the skiff would be working the shoreline — because Aldous Vane had cleared his horizon once already, and he intended to keep it clear.

He just didn't know yet that the horizon had learned to crawl through culverts.
