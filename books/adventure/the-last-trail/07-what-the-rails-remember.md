## 7. What the Rails Remember

My father wrote letters the way other men wrote confessions — slowly, with too many crossings-out, and always ending a sentence or two before he got to the point.

I'd read them so many times I could recite them with my eyes shut, which was fortunate, because on the third night aboard Oscar Dravenmoor's engine, my eyes were mostly shut. Not sleeping. Nobody sleeps properly on a moving engine. You doze in fragments, wedged into the corner of the cab behind the firebox where the iron stays warm, and every time the wheels hit a joint in the rail you surface for half a second to check you're still alive, then sink again. It's like being rocked by a giant with a grudge.

Oscar drove through the dark like she was reading it. That's the only way I can describe it. She didn't watch the track so much as consult it, glancing at the rails the way a scholar glances at a page she's already memorized, just to confirm nothing had been edited overnight. Every so often her hand would move on the throttle without her looking at it, and we'd slow, and thirty seconds later there'd be a fallen branch across the line, or a washout, or a deer standing in the gauge with the particular stupidity deer reserve for oncoming trains.

"You could teach me that," I said once, around midnight.

"No," she said.

That was the whole lesson.

* * *

The spur showed up an hour after dawn, if you knew where to look. If you didn't know where to look, it didn't show up at all.

From the main line it was just a seam in the weeds — two rusted threads running off into alder scrub, swallowed by ten years of green. Any honest surveyor would have called it abandoned and any honest railroad had, officially, done exactly that. But Oscar slowed at the seam without slowing the train, the way you'd nod to someone you recognized across a street, and then she took us onto it at walking speed while the alders dragged their fingers along the cab roof like beggars at a carriage window.

"Maintenance spur," she said, when she saw me watching. "Built in '61 to haul ballast up the ridge. Closed in '68 when they rerouted the main grade. Never pulled the rails, because pulling rails costs money and leaving them costs nothing." She glanced at me. "Half the map of this country is things somebody left because removing them was more expensive than pretending they weren't there."

"Is that on your map? The spur?"

"Everything's on my map. That's rather the point of me."

I'd seen the map. Once, briefly, when she unrolled it in the cab to check a junction and let it fall open for maybe four seconds. It wasn't a map so much as a nervous system — every line drawn in her own cramped hand, annotated in three colors, with little symbols I didn't recognize and one or two I suspected she'd invented out of spite. There were lines on it that no government document admitted existed. There were gaps where lines officially existed and clearly didn't. It looked less like something you'd sell and more like something you'd die holding.

She rolled it up before I'd finished looking. She rolls it up before anyone finishes looking. I think that's the rule.

* * *

Here is what I know about my father, arranged in order of certainty:

He was a surveyor for the Coastal Boundary Commission. He was good at it — patient with instruments, patient with weather, patient with the slow arithmetic of land. He laughed at his own jokes before he finished telling them, which meant nobody else ever got the chance. He smelled of pencil shavings and pipe smoke and, in the last year, faintly of salt, because the Sable Reach work had him living aboard ships more than ashore.

And in his letters home, in the final months, there was a phrase.

Not in every letter. In three of them, tucked into the middle of paragraphs about tide gauges and supply delays, always lowercase, always almost casual: *something wrong with the manifest.* Then he'd move on to complaining about the coffee. As though he'd mentioned the weather.

The first time, I read past it. The second time, I underlined it. The third time, I sat in our kitchen with the letter flat on the table and understood that my father — a man who told jokes badly and completely — had put that phrase there deliberately, in the one place he could be sure it would survive even if he didn't. Letters get kept. Letters get read. A man who spends his life making records knows what kind of record lasts.

Something wrong with the manifest. Whose manifest? Wrong how? He never said. He never got the chance to say anything again at all, because the Sable Reach party stopped writing, and then the government stopped asking, and then everyone except my mother and me stopped remembering there had ever been eleven men with instruments walking into those marshes.

The government wrote them off as lost to weather. Weather doesn't take eleven men and leave their rations. I've thought about that sentence every day for eight months, and today, rattling along a dead spur toward the Reach with a woman who trusted nothing and a firebox full of stolen coal, I finally got to do something about it besides think.

* * *

We stopped at noon where the spur climbed through a rock cut, because Oscar said the engine needed water and I needed to stop pretending I wasn't seasick on dry land. While she worked the filler hose at a spring seeping from the cut wall, I walked ahead along the rails, because walking ahead is what I do. It's not bravery. It's just that standing still next to Oscar Dravenmoor makes you feel like furniture.

The cut narrowed. The rails here were older — pre-war iron, flaking orange, and between the ties the ballast had silted into something almost like soil. Grass grew in the four-foot. And in the grass, catching the light wrong, something pale.

Paper.

It was wedged under the third rail anchor, folded twice, weighted down by a stone that someone had placed on top of it. Not dropped. Placed. I know the difference now; I've learned to read stones the way Oscar reads rails. A dropped stone scatters. A placed stone sits square, deliberate, like a signature.

I picked it up with hands that had suddenly gone stupid.

It was a page torn from a survey logbook — the heavy ruled stock the Commission used, water-stained but legible, the tear ragged on the left edge where it had been ripped free in a hurry or ripped free carefully to look hurried. The handwriting was small and exact. Surveyors' handwriting always looks like it's afraid of taking up too much room.

Most of it was routine. Soundings. Bearing corrections. A note about a broken theodolite thread. My chest hurt reading it, because it sounded like my father — that same apologetic precision, the same habit of apologizing to the paper for existing.

Then, near the bottom, boxed in double-underline the way my father boxed things he wanted found:

*Manifest discrepancy confirmed. Vessel Meridian Star — cargo entered as salvage equipment, derricks &c. Actual: none. Holds empty at sailing. Insurance schedule lists full outfit. V. signed the schedule himself. This is fraud at scale. Copying evidence tonight. If anything happens to us, the proof is—*

And there it ended. Torn mid-word. The rest of the page, the rest of the proof, the rest of everything, gone wherever the rest of the team had gone.

The *Meridian Star*. I knew the name the way you know a name from a newspaper you skimmed — Vane's flagship, the pride of the salvage fleet, the ship that famously wrecked in the Reach two years back and generated the insurance claim that built the fleet that was now, allegedly, rescuing people. Ships wreck. It happens. But a ship that sails empty and wrecks insured isn't bad luck. It's arithmetic.

Vane hadn't lost a ship. He'd harvested one.

I stood in that cut with the page shaking in my hands and felt something turn over inside me — not grief, grief I'd had for eight months and knew all its rooms, but its angrier cousin, the one that moves in afterward and starts rearranging the furniture. Hope, sharpening into purpose. Because a torn page means someone tore it. Someone tore it and hid it under a stone on a dead spur, which means someone expected a reader. Which means someone expected a survivor of the reading.

Which means somewhere ahead, either a ghost or a man who refused to become one was waiting for exactly the kind of fool who walks down abandoned rails reading stones.

Hello. That's me.

* * *

Oscar read the page twice. She reads fast and shows nothing, which I'm beginning to suspect is a professional deformity.

"Meridian Star," she said. "Vane's flagship."

"That's what it says."

"I know what it says. I'm establishing that we agree." She held the page up to the light, tilting it, reading the watermark the way she read everything — for what it was underneath. "Commission stock. Genuine. And the stone was placed recently — moss hasn't taken. Days, not years."

"Days," I said. "The team vanished eight months ago."

"So either the page has been sitting somewhere else and got moved here recently—" she paused, and I watched her arrive at the same door I had, "—or someone placed it recently. Someone who's been alive out here for eight months, and has decided, recently, to start leaving messages."

"A survivor."

"A survivor who knows this spur exists." Her eyes came up to mine, gray and level. "This spur is on exactly one map in the world, Finch, and I'm looking at the person who owns it. So either your father's party knew these rails better than the government that employed them — possible; surveyors walk everywhere — or whoever left this has been watching us since the ridge."

The alders moved along the top of the cut. Wind, probably. I looked at them anyway.

"What do we do?" I asked.

Oscar folded the page along its original creases with a care that surprised me, and handed it back. To me. Not to herself. Back to me, the way you return something you recognize belongs to someone.

"We do what the page says," she said. "We keep moving, and we keep reading. Stones, mud, rust, weeds. Everything out here is a record, Finch. The country remembers what people do to it — rails remember weight, clay remembers boots, paper remembers hands. Your father knew that. It's why he became a surveyor instead of a preacher." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that on another face would have been a smile. "And it's why whoever killed them failed. You can murder eleven men. You cannot murder a record. Somebody always tears out one more page than you can burn."

I put the page inside my shirt, against my chest, where the firebox warmth had been keeping me alive for three nights. It seemed fair to give the warmth to something that deserved it more.

"Finch," she said, climbing back into the cab. "If we find who left this — they may be in no state to talk. Or worse than no state. Are you prepared for that?"

I thought about lying, because she'd believe a lie about as far as she could throw the engine.

"No," I said. "But I'm going anyway."

"Good answer," she said. "It's the only honest one anybody ever gives." And she opened the throttle, and the old spur took us on toward the Reach, two rusted threads through the green, carrying a torn page, a dead man's underline, and whatever was waiting at the end of the line —

— which, an hour later, announced itself as a smell. Salt, rot, and brackish water, rolling down the cut on the wind. Oscar stood up in the cab, looked ahead, and said one word, quietly, the way people say words they've been dreading:

"Flooded."

The rails ran straight into a mile of shining water, and the tide was coming in.
