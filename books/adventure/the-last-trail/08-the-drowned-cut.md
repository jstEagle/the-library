## 8. The Drowned Cut

The rails ended the way most things end in the Sable Reach — not with a bang or a ceremony, but with water.

I brought the engine to a stop a hundred yards short of where the map said the line died, because I'd learned long ago that maps lie by omission and the ground lies by erosion, and the truth is usually somewhere between them, standing knee-deep and smug. I set the brake, climbed down, and walked the last stretch on foot while Finch hovered at my elbow like a question that hadn't found its shape yet.

The cut opened ahead of us like a wound somebody had tried to stitch shut with granite and given up on. Two walls of rock, forty feet high, and between them the sea — not the open sea, not yet, but the sea's advance scouts, filling the channel in slow gray tongues that slid over the old railbed and withdrew again, leaving the ties glistening like something freshly drowned.

"How deep?" Finch asked.

"Depends on when you ask." I checked my watch against the tide table I'd copied into the margin of my field book, because paper you can lose and margins you can burn. "Right now? Shin-deep at the near end, waist-deep at the far. In four hours it'll be over your head and mine and the engine's stack, and it'll stay that way until tomorrow's low."

"So we cross now."

"So we cross now," I agreed. "And here's the part where you pay attention, because this is the only lecture I give for free." I crouched and put my palm flat on the water sliding over the nearest tie. Cold enough to ache. That was good. Cold meant current from the deep channel, which meant the cut drained properly at low tide instead of just pooling. "The water comes back faster than it goes out. Always does. It's got the whole ocean behind it pushing, and all we've got is gravity pulling. So when I say turn around, you turn around, and you don't argue, and you don't look back to see if I'm following. You'll know I'm following because I'll be swearing."

Finch nodded with the particular solemnity of a boy who intends to do exactly none of what he's told the moment it becomes inconvenient. I've met that expression before. I used to wear it myself.

* * *

The problem — and there is always a problem, the Sable Reach being a place that seems personally offended by the concept of an easy day — was the engine.

I want to be clear about something, because people who've never loved a machine don't understand it. The *Kestrel* wasn't transportation. Transportation is a thing you use. The *Kestrel* was eleven years of my life welded to a boiler and painted the color of stubbornness. Every mile of closed line she'd ever run, every toll gang I'd dodged, every night I'd spent alone in her cab with the firebox painting orange light on the gauges — that was the asset. Not the engine. The *having* of the engine. An engineer without an engine is just a person who knows things nobody wants to know.

And the drowned cut did not care about any of that.

I stood at the lip of the channel and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came back ugly. The *Kestrel* weighed more than forty tons. The railbed under the water was a hundred years old and had been taking the sea's opinion of it twice a day ever since. Somewhere along that quarter-mile of submerged track there was almost certainly a soft spot, a washed-out ballast pocket, a tie rotted to lace. If she went through, she went through for good, and no tide table in the world would bring her back.

"Leave her," Finch said quietly. He'd been watching me do the math on my face, and he'd read it right. "We could go around. On foot. Come back for her after."

"Come back for her after," I repeated. "After what?"

"After we find them. My father. Wren. Whoever's left."

"And how far is 'after,' Finch? Ten miles? Twenty? Through country where Vane's skiffs are running patrols, carrying everything we need on our backs, with Wren — if he's alive — too hurt to walk fast?" I shook my head. "No. If we cross, we cross with the engine. She carries our supplies, our shelter, and if it comes to it, our speed. Afoot out here, we're not travelers. We're cargo waiting for someone to collect us."

He looked at the water, then at me. "You're scared," he said. Not cruelly. Just observing, the way kids do, with that terrible accuracy they haven't learned to apologize for yet.

"Terrified," I said. "That's how you know it matters. Now get the waterproofing off the cab fittings and help me lash everything above the running board. We've got maybe three hours of usable tide and I intend to spend every minute of them moving."

* * *

Crossing a flooded rail cut with a locomotive is not driving. It's drowning slowly, in installments, and pretending each installment is fine.

I took her in at walking pace, first gear, wheels barely turning, with the water climbing the spokes like it had all the time in the world and every intention of using it. Six inches. A foot. Eighteen inches over the railhead, and the *Kestrel*'s axles disappeared into green murk, and I stood up on the footplate so I could see down past my own boots into the channel and read the bottom.

You learn to read water the way other people read print. Clear water over gravel says *trust me*. Brown water says *something moved recently*. And that particular milky swirl that curls up from below and spreads out flat — that one says *there's a hole down there eating your roadbed right now*, and you have about as long as it takes the swirl to reach you to decide whether you're going to be on this side of it or inside it.

Two hundred yards in, the swirl appeared.

It came up off the downstream side of the track, maybe thirty feet ahead of the pilot, a slow gray bloom like ink dropped in milk. I slammed the reverser full forward and opened the regulator, and the *Kestrel* lurched — forty tons going from a crawl to a lunge is not a graceful thing; it's a thing with opinions — and we hit the soft spot doing perhaps eight miles an hour instead of two, and the difference between those two numbers is the difference between a bad afternoon and a very short obituary.

The right-hand drivers went down. Not much. Maybe eight inches, the ballast giving way under the wheel with a sound like a giant biting through celery. The whole engine listed, and cold water came over the running board and across my boots, and for one long second we hung there, tilted, the sea holding us the way a cat holds a thing it hasn't decided about yet.

Then the left-hand drivers caught solid bed, and steam pressure did what steam pressure does, and we clawed out of it. The hole passed under us. The water shallowed. Ahead, the far wall of the cut rose up gray and dripping, and the rails climbed out of the channel toward daylight like a hand reaching up from underwater.

I didn't say anything for a while. Neither did Finch. Some silences are just silence, and some are two people agreeing not to discuss how close that was, and we were having the second kind.

Finally he said, "The water's coming back in."

"I noticed."

"It looks faster than before."

"It is faster than before. It's always faster than before. That's the deal with tides — they're not fair, they're just punctual." I glanced at the sky, reading the light off the clouds the way the old coastal pilots taught me, back when I still let people teach me things. "We've got time. Barely. Keep your eyes on the gauge glass and tell me if the boiler pressure starts acting strange, because if she swallowed more than a mouthful back there, she'll let us know by sulking."

"She can sulk?"

"Finch, that engine has more moods than a widow at a wedding. Watch the glass."

* * *

We came out of the cut with the sun already leaning west and the sea closing behind us like a door.

I stopped the *Kestrel* on the first dry ground past the channel — real dry ground, gravel and gorse, the kind you could kiss — and sat in the cab for a moment with my hands still on the throttle, feeling the tremor run through the frame. She'd taken water into the ashpan, probably into the smokebox. Her exhaust had a wet rattle in it now, a catch on every beat, like a cough that means more than a cough.

"You're limping," I told her. "Don't make it a habit."

"What happens if she gets worse?" Finch asked from the tender.

"Then I fix her. That's the arrangement. She breaks, I mend, neither of us mentions it again." I swung down and walked her length, checking, listening. The wheels were sound. The motion was sound. It was the boiler throat I worried about — salt water and hot iron are old enemies, and they settle their arguments in ways that show up days later, at the worst possible hour. But she'd hold. She'd hold because she had to, and because I would stand in the firebox doorway with a hammer and argue with her until she did.

From the top of the gravel bank, the country beyond the cut opened up: a long gray valley running down toward the coast, stitched with the ghost lines of the old survey roads, and at the far end, half-hidden in the sea haze, a dark notch in the headland that matched the coordinates in Cora Ashe's telegram. The survey team's final position. Or close enough to final to matter.

"No tracks," Finch said. He'd climbed the bank ahead of me and was standing very still, looking down the valley. "There should be tracks. People. Cart ruts. Something. There's nothing."

There wasn't. That was the thing about the Reach that had been crawling up my spine since Ghost Station — not the danger, but the tidiness. Abandoned places are messy. They collect debris, rust, bones, the leavings of everyone who passed through and couldn't carry it all away. This valley was clean. Swept. As though someone had come through after the survey team and erased them the way you'd erase a figure from a ledger — thoroughly, professionally, with an eye toward auditors.

"Somebody wanted this valley to forget them," I said.

"Or wanted us to think it forgot them." Finch had his father's letters out again, the ones I'd pretended not to notice him reading every night by the cab lamp. He held one now, folded soft as cloth from handling. "'Something wrong with the manifest,'" he read, though he knew it by heart. "That's what he wrote. Something wrong with the manifest. Dad didn't write letters like that. Dad wrote about weather and food and whether the cook had learned to make anything new. When he broke pattern—" His jaw worked. "When he broke pattern, it was because he was scared."

I looked at the boy, then at the valley, then at the dark notch in the headland where the coordinates ended, and I felt the job shift under me the way the ballast had shifted under the *Kestrel*'s wheels in the cut. Until this morning it had been a search. Missing men, grieving widow, salvage fleet circling like gulls. Sad, but simple.

Now it was a murder investigation wearing a search's clothes, and I was standing in the middle of it with a boy whose father might be in a shallow grave, a wounded engine, and a tide schedule that gave me exactly one window in and one window out.

"Get the rifle off the tender rack," I said. "And load it."

Finch looked at me. For once he didn't argue or ask why. He just moved, quick and quiet, and came back with the gun held wrong, and I corrected his grip without comment, and we started down the valley together as the first shadow of the headland reached out across the gravel toward us like a finger pointing the way.

Half a mile on, at the mouth of the notch, the gorse thinned and the ground turned to clay — and the clay was not empty.

I saw them before Finch did, and I put a hand on his shoulder to stop him, and I felt him understand anyway, because his breathing changed. Rows of low mounds, maybe eight of them, spaced with terrible neatness, each marked with a stake. Survey stakes, repurposed. The numbers stenciled on them were still legible.

Eight graves. The survey team had been nine.

One marker stood apart from the rest, and the clay around it was torn and churned, and scratched deep into the wood of the stake — fresh scratches, days old at most — was a pattern of notches I recognized from a lifetime of reading signals I wasn't supposed to see.

Somebody had crawled out of one of these graves. And they were asking, in the only language they had left, to be found.
