## 9. Marks in the Clay

The tide gave us ninety minutes. It always gives you exactly enough time to make a mistake and not enough to undo it — that's the arithmetic of water, and I've never once beaten it.

We came up out of the drowned cut with our boots full of the Sable Reach and our lungs full of salt, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about wading a flooded rail cut at low water: it isn't the depth that kills you. It's the current that runs along the bottom like a freight train with no schedule, looking for anything loose enough to take. The engine stayed on the high shoulder where I'd left her, nose pointed back the way we'd come, fire banked low. If the water took her while we were gone, then everything I am went with her, and Finch and I would be two ghosts arguing over whose fault it was.

She was still there when we crested the shingle on the far side. Small mercies. I've learned to count them because they're the only currency out here that doesn't devalue.

The survey camp sat on a shelf of clay above the tideline, tucked against a bluff that kept the worst of the wind off it. From a hundred yards out it looked almost peaceful. Canvas lean-to still rigged. A cook fire ringed with stones, ash long gone gray. A line strung between two posts with three shirts pegged to it, bleached bone-white by weather, sleeves hanging empty.

Empty sleeves. That's the image I want out of my head and can't seem to evict.

"Miss Dravenmoor." Finch had stopped walking. He does that now — stops when something's wrong before his eyes have even worked out why, some animal sense he inherited from a father who clearly taught him well right up until somebody made him stop teaching altogether. "There's no gulls."

I looked. He was right. The Reach is loud with birds this time of year — gulls, terns, those fat insolent cormorants that sit on every piling on the coast like they're owed rent. This stretch of shoreline was silent except for the water working at the stones below us.

Birds know. Birds always know first. My grandmother used to say a field with no crows means something's buried in it, and she said it about people too, though I was too young then to understand she wasn't talking about farming.

"Stay behind me," I said, "and if I say run, you run for the engine and don't wait to see if I'm behind you."

"You'd come," he said.

"I'd try. That's different, and the difference will keep you alive." I checked the revolver in my coat — six rounds, which is five more than I've ever needed and one fewer than I'd like today — and we walked into the camp.

* * *

It was orderly. That was the first thing, and the thing that turned my stomach before anything else did. Not ransacked. Not scattered by weather or animals. *Ordered.* Packs stood upright against the lean-to posts in a row, buckled shut. Instruments racked neat as church pews — a theodolite, two levels, a brass transit with its case latched. Ration tins stacked in pyramids of six. Somebody had squared all of it away after the men were gone, the way you tidy a room after a funeral, except nobody had held a funeral, and the tidiness wasn't grief. It was inventory. Someone had catalogued these men's lives the way you catalogue cargo.

Finch went straight to the packs. Of course he did. I watched his hands hover over them without touching, reading names stitched into the canvas collars the way other boys read adventure serials. "R. Okafor," he said quietly. "T. Marsh. J. Ashe."

He said the last one very softly, and then he stopped saying things at all, and I let him have his silence because there was nothing I owned that was worth trading for it.

Ashe. Cora Ashe's husband. James, presumably, from the way she'd said the name — the way you say a name you've said ten thousand times across a breakfast table and expect to say ten thousand more. A government man, a surveyor, decent pay, decent prospects, a wife waiting with a lamp lit. And here was his pack, buckled, upright, filed away like a returned library book.

"Nobody starved here," Finch said finally. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices do when they're carrying something breakable. "Rations are near full. There's fresh water barrels. They weren't lost, Miss Dravenmoor. Lost men eat their supplies."

Lost men also scatter. Lost men burn their packs for warmth, tear their tents for signal flags, fire their weapons until someone comes. These men had done none of it. These men had been *arranged.*

I found the graves on the seaward edge of the camp, where the clay shelf met the shingle, and I knew what they were before I knew what they were, if that makes any sense — the ground itself told me. Six long mounds in the clay, each maybe six feet by three, each covered with a scattering of beach stone pressed into the surface. Not dug deep. Clay holds a shape like that; it remembers what's been pressed into it. And these had been pressed shallow. Shallow as a secret. Whoever dug them hadn't been burying men so much as filing them — getting them under the surface just far enough to pass a glance, never far enough to pass a season. One hard winter storm and the Reach would have opened every one of them like envelopes.

Maybe that was the plan. Maybe the sea was meant to do the final edit.

I knelt at the nearest mound and pulled one stone loose, and underneath it the clay was stamped with a boot heel — square heel, iron-shod, size eleven or better, pressed deep by someone standing over the grave doing the work. I moved down the line. Same boot. Every mound. One man had dug six graves alone, working methodically, probably through a night, probably while the men who were going into them watched from wherever the watching happened.

Six graves. Seven men on the survey roster, according to the manifest Cora Ashe had shown me. I counted twice because I wanted to be wrong in the hopeful direction.

Then I counted the mounds again and got five.

* * *

Five graves, six stones-and-clay shapes — no. Let me get this straight, because I got it wrong twice standing there and I refuse to get it wrong twice telling it.

Six mounds. Five occupied. The sixth — set slightly apart from the others, the way you set apart the thing you're not sure about — was empty. Not collapsed, not washed out, not robbed in any ordinary sense. Empty and *finished*, its edges trimmed clean, its stone covering undisturbed, as if it had been prepared and then never used. As if the digger had run out of time, or run out of nerve, or run out of men.

And driven into the clay at its head, where the other markers were plain stakes with nothing written on them — plain, deliberately blank, blankness being its own kind of message — this sixth marker had been scratched. Not carved with a knife. Scratched with something small and desperate and repeated, the way you scratch when your hands are bound or nearly bound and you've got one shot at leaving a message and no second draft.

I brushed the clay dust off it with my thumb and leaned close.

Lines. Groups of short lines and dots, four groups of them, cut into the wood in a hand that shook but didn't quit. I know signal code the way I know rail gauges — it's the alphabet of my trade, passed along closed lines by men who weren't supposed to be talking. And what was scratched into that stake was live code. Current. Recent. The cuts were pale at the bottom, raw wood showing, days old at most.

*Signal active,* my brain said, in the flat voice it uses when it wants me to understand something before my stomach is ready. *Somebody scratched this recently. Somebody who knows code. Somebody who expected another code-reader to walk this shore.*

"Finch." I kept my voice level, which cost me something. "Come look at this. Tell me what you read."

He read it faster than I did. Young eyes, and a head that had apparently spent its boyhood memorizing exactly the same useless-wonderful alphabets I had. His lips moved through the groups and then went still.

"It's a location marker," he said. "Distance and bearing. It says—" he frowned, working it, counting on his fingers against the stake— "two hundred yards, bearing northwest. And then this last group is a condition flag. It means *wounded* or *trapped* — the old coastal version. My dad used it in his letters when he was joking about being stuck in some hotel." His voice caught on *my dad* and he pushed through it, jaw tight. "Miss Dravenmoor. This is fresh. This is days old. Who scratches their own grave marker with a rescue signal?"

"Somebody who dug the other five," I said, "and understood he was digging his own next."

Because that was the shape of it, once you let yourself see it. Vane's crew had marched seven men out here and put down six of them, and the seventh they'd left breathing — why, I could guess: leverage, insurance, a witness they might need, or simply one man who'd found a way to make himself useful. And that seventh man, in whatever hours or minutes he'd been given unwatched, had knelt at the marker of the grave dug for him and scratched a message into it in the only language his killers couldn't read, addressed to whoever came after.

Addressed, as it happened, to me. To anyone with my particular disease, the one that makes you learn dead languages of wire and whistle. He'd bet a life on there being one more person in the world who spoke it.

I intend to collect on that bet.

* * *

We followed the bearing. Two hundred yards northwest, along the base of the bluff, over shingle that announced every footstep like a town crier, past a fallen section of cliff where the recent storms had chewed the coastline ragged. Finch kept glancing back at the camp, and I understood why and let him. You don't leave your father's name in an untidy place without looking back. I've left enough names in enough places to know.

The cave mouth was exactly where the code promised — a seam in the bluff face, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging kelp and rockfall, breathing cold air outward. Caves breathe when they connect to the sea through some lower chamber; the tide pushes air in and pulls it out like the coast itself inhaling. A man who knew coasts would find it. A man who knew coasts had found it.

I struck a match and we went in bent double, and twenty feet along, the passage opened into a chamber the size of a depot waiting room, and the matchlight found what the chamber held, and I have seen wrecks and floods and one riot in a stockyard, and nothing has ever looked at me the way that chamber looked.

A blanket. A tin cup. Three fish skeletons arranged neatly beside a cold fire ring — neatly, always neatly, because order was the last thing this man owned and he was spending it carefully. And propped against the wall, a man.

He was thin the way driftwood is thin, hollowed and weathered to the grain. One leg wrapped in strips of shirt gone brown at the knee. His eyes found the match flame first and then my face, and he didn't flinch and he didn't beg, and I thought: *surveyor.* They train them to observe before they feel. Whatever was happening to him, some part of him was still taking measurements.

"You read the stake," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"We read the stake," I said.

"Then you're either the best luck I've ever had," he said, "or Vane's people have gotten cleverer than I gave them credit for." He tried to shift his weight and failed, and Finch was past me before I'd decided to move, kneeling, steadying the man's shoulder with both hands the way you steady something you're afraid will tip.

"My father was on the survey team," Finch said. "James Ashe. Was he—"

The man closed his eyes. Opened them. Did the arithmetic of mercy versus truth in about a second and a half, and chose truth, because a man who scratches signals into his own grave marker doesn't have any lies left to spend.

"They didn't suffer long," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm Elias Wren. I ran the instruments. And I need to tell you what happened here, all of it, quickly, because when the tide turns, the men who buried my crew are going to come back for the seventh grave, and I have been keeping myself alive for nineteen days for exactly one reason."

"What reason?" I asked, though I already knew. Some reasons you carry so long they announce themselves.

Wren reached under the blanket and brought out a oilskin packet, cracked with salt, tied with wire, and held it against his chest like a coal he'd carried through a flood.

"The ledger," he said. "Vane scuttled his own ship, Mrs.—"

"Dravenmoor."

"Dravenmoor." Something moved through his ruined face that might have been hope arriving late and exhausted. "Vane sank his own vessel for the insurance and killed my crew to bury the proof, and this packet is the proof, and you are the first person in nineteen days to stand in front of me who isn't holding a shovel."

Outside, far off across the water, I heard it — faint, unmistakable, the sound I'd been dreading since the ridge: a ship's whistle, two long and one short. Fleet signal. Coming this way.

Ninety minutes of tide, I'd told Finch. We'd used forty.

"Hold on to him," I said, and started counting what I had left.
