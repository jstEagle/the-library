## 10. The Survivor

The signal code scratched into the grave marker read like a love letter written by a man who'd forgotten how to write anything but coordinates. Three lines, shallow, cut with something dull — a belt buckle, maybe, or the edge of a ration tin. I crouched over it in the gray light while Finch held the lantern and tried not to breathe too loud.

"Three marks down," he said. "Then that squiggle."

"That squiggle is a bearing." I traced it with my thumb. "And the three marks are paces, or they're tide-heights, or they're nothing at all and some dying man was scratching at the world to prove he was still in it."

"You don't believe that last one."

"No," I admitted. "I don't. Men who are done fighting scratch their own names. This man left instructions."

The bearing pointed north-northeast, straight into the cliff line where the Reach's black rock folded itself into shadow. Between us and it lay two hundred yards of tidal shelf, slick with weed and littered with the bones of things the sea had finished with. Somewhere out on the water, gulls were arguing over something dead. It felt less like a landscape than an opinion about our chances.

We went anyway. That's the whole of my philosophy in one sentence: you go anyway.

* * *

The cave mouth didn't announce itself. It was a seam in the rock, no wider than a coffin stood on end, hidden behind a curtain of hanging kelp that stank like a fish market in August. If I hadn't been looking for exactly this kind of hole — because men who hide choose holes like engineers choose culverts, for function first and comfort never — I'd have walked past it twice.

I went in first. Not because I was brave. Because if there was a gun waiting inside, I'd rather it found me than Finch. There are things I'll do for a stranger's widow; there are fewer things I'll let happen to a boy who stowed away on my engine and then refused, with real ingenuity, every attempt I made to be rid of him.

The passage narrowed, turned, and opened without warning into a chamber the size of a chapel. Water pooled in the floor, black and still as poured ink. And against the far wall, half-lit by the thin gray thread of light from a crack in the ceiling, sat a man holding a broken surveying instrument the way other men hold a crucifix.

He had a pistol in his lap. He raised it. His hands shook so badly the muzzle described small, exhausted circles in the air.

"Don't," he said. His voice was gravel soaked in brine. "Don't come closer. I've killed four men this week."

"You have not," I said. "You've been in this cave all week. Your powder's wet. I can smell it from here."

A long silence. Then the pistol sagged, and the man put his face in his free hand, and made a sound that wasn't quite laughing and wasn't quite weeping and was, I think, both at once, which is what happens when a body has run out of room for either.

"Elias Wren?" I said.

"How do you know my name?"

"Cora Ashe sent me. Her husband led your team."

At that, Wren looked up, and what was in his face made me wish I hadn't spoken so plainly. Grief is a private country. I'd crossed its border uninvited.

"He's dead," Wren said. "They're all dead. I dug the graves myself. Six of them." He swallowed. "Seven markers. One's empty."

"I know. I've seen them. The empty one has your code on it."

"My code." He almost smiled. "I figured if they came back to check their work, they'd find the marker and think they'd missed me. And if anyone else came — anyone honest — well. Honest people read signals. Dishonest ones burn things." He gestured at the chamber with the ruined instrument. "You're the first anyone in eleven days."

Finch pushed past me before I could stop him, dropped to his knees on the wet stone, and stuck out a hand like we were meeting at a church social. "Finch Halloway. My father was Tomas Halloway. He was on your crew."

Wren stared at the boy's hand. Then he took it, and held on longer than a handshake requires, and when he let go his eyes were wet and he pretended they weren't, and Finch pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done in that cave all week.

"He talked about you," Wren said quietly. "Letters home. He kept saying he'd bring you out here someday, show you the Reach at low tide. Said you'd love it." A pause. "He was wrong about that. Nobody loves this place. You just survive it."

"Tell me what happened," I said. "All of it. Start wherever it started going wrong."

* * *

What follows, I set down as he told it, in the order he told it, though he stopped twice — once at the names, once at the fire — and both times Finch put a hand on his arm until he could go on.

The survey team had been hired, through channels Wren still didn't fully understand, to chart the Sable Reach approaches for a new coastal relief route. Standard work. Soundings, bearings, bottom samples. Their tender was provisioned for six weeks.

On the fourth day, they'd been hailed by a salvage vessel flying Vane colors — the *Meridian Star*, pride of Aldous Vane's fleet. Polite as Sunday. Vane himself came aboard, which Wren said was unusual enough to make the whole crew uneasy; owners of fleets don't call on survey dinghies. He'd wanted to know their chart area, their schedule, their employer. Ashe — Cora's husband, a careful man, a good one — had answered honestly, because there was nothing in the answers to hide.

Two nights later, the *Meridian Star* ran aground on the Reach shoals in clear weather and flat water. Or rather: she was *made* to run aground. Wren's team, camped ashore, watched it happen. Watched her take the shoal where any competent pilot would have given it a mile of room. Watched the distress rockets go up, theatrical and punctual.

And then, Wren said, they'd done what any survey crew would do. They took their instruments and their boat and they went out to render assistance, and while they were hauling survivors and salvage, Wren — whose job was noticing things — noticed the hull breach from the inside. Cut plates. Not torn, not stove-in by rock. *Cut*, with a torch, along seams chosen with an engineer's care, below the waterline, in a compartment that could be flooded without sinking her outright. Scuttled. Deliberately, professionally scuttled, in the path of her own insurance claim.

"She was old," Wren said. "Over-insured. I did the arithmetic right there in the flooded compartment, standing in somebody else's sabotage up to my knees. She was worth more dead than alive, and everyone in that fleet knew it except the underwriters."

Ashe had wanted to report it. Wren had agreed. Two others had hesitated — Vane paid half the coast's wages, directly or otherwise — but hesitation lost. They logged the evidence: photographs, plate samples, the cutting pattern, the manifest discrepancy that proved cargo listed as lost had been offloaded at a friendly port two days before the wreck. Ashe sealed it in a waterproof case and sent word to their government contact requesting a magistrate.

"The reply came back in nine hours," Wren said. "That's how I knew. Nine hours, from a station that takes three days to answer a supply request. Somebody read our message and moved fast." His voice went flat. "The night after, Vane's men came ashore. Twelve of them, maybe more. They didn't rob us. They didn't question us. They shot us where we stood around the cook fire, and then they buried us — buried *us*, mind, gave us graves, tidy ones, like they were closing out an account — and burned the camp and the boat and everything that would float."

He stopped. In the dark, water dripped somewhere with the patience of a clock.

"They missed me because I'd gone down to the tide line to re-set a gauge. I heard the shots. I lay in the shallows under weed like a dead thing and listened to them bury my friends, and I heard one of them say — I'll hear it till I die — 'Vane wants the ledger case found or confirmed destroyed. Search again.' They never found it. Ashe hid it too well. Hid it so well that even I don't know where, and I've had eleven days and nothing to do but look."

"Do you think it's still out there?" Finch asked.

"It's out there." No doubt in it at all. "Tommy Halloway hid it. He was the best field man I ever worked with, and he was frightened, and frightened careful men hide things in places only they would think of. But he never told me where, and now he can't." Wren's jaw worked. "Which means the proof of the worst crime on this coast is sitting in a hole only a dead man knew, and the man who killed him owns every lawman between here and the capital."

* * *

I sat with that for a while. The cave did its dripping. Out beyond the crack in the ceiling, the sea breathed in and out like something sleeping that might wake hungry.

Here is what I understood, sitting in the dark with a broken man and a fatherless boy: I had come looking for a mystery, and mysteries are comfortable. Mysteries have shapes. You worry at them like a knot and eventually they give. What I had found instead was not a mystery. It was a weight. A testimony worth killing twelve men for, carried by the thirteenth, hidden in the head of a fourteenth, and wanted — actively, urgently, at any cost — by a man rich enough to buy the law that should have been hunting him.

Every hour I stayed in the Reach, I spent my life at rates I couldn't afford. Every hour Wren stayed in that cave, he was a loose end with a pulse. And somewhere behind us, three ships full of men who expected no witnesses were methodically making sure there wouldn't be any.

"We leave tonight," I said. "Both tides from now, the cut floods past wading. After that, this whole coast is a cage with water for bars."

"Leave for where?" Wren asked. "There's nowhere Vane doesn't reach."

"There's one place." I thought of the magistrate outpost on the far side of the Reach, six days' hard travel by closed rail, reachable in two and a half if the drowned cut behaved and the trestles held and God had decided to take a personal interest in railroad engineering. "There's a federal magistrate across the Reach. If your testimony reaches him sworn and witnessed—"

"Half the magistrates on this coast drink Vane's whiskey."

"Then we'll find out if this one does. But we try the door before we decide it's locked." I stood. My knees objected; the tide shelf had opinions about cartilage. "You can walk?"

"I can limp convincingly."

"He's feverish," Finch said. He'd been quiet, doing his own arithmetic, and I caught the look on his face and recognized it, because I'd worn it once at fifteen, learning that the world keeps books too. "His wound's gone bad. I can smell it."

"You can smell it because you've been living on my engine with a boy who never washes," I said. "But yes. He's infected, and infection kills slower than Vane's men but just as certainly, and it makes bad decisions feel reasonable on the way." I looked at Wren. "Can you make it to the engine?"

"If I die on the way, bury me shallow and scratch a signal. Seems to work."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of Finch, which I counted as medicine.

* * *

We moved at slack water, Wren between us with an arm over each of our shoulders, limping across the shelf while the gulls screamed their commentary overhead. Twice he went down. Twice he got up, and the second time he said, through his teeth, "In the letters Tommy wrote, he mentioned a railroad engineer. Said if anything ever happened, find the one who runs the closed lines. Said she was mean as a crosscut saw and twice as reliable." He looked at me sideways. "He undersold the mean."

"Everyone does," I said. "It's how I get work."

At the cave mouth I paused and looked back. The chamber behind us was empty now, its black pool undisturbed, its eleven-day tenant finally evicted. Eleven days alone in the dark with the graves of your friends a quarter mile away and the men who dug them hunting the coast for you. Most men would have gone mad. Some would have gone into the sea. Elias Wren had spent the time sharpening his memory into a weapon and scratching signals on gravestones, and when help finally came, his first act was to offer it information.

There are different kinds of courage. I've seen the loud kind die young on mountain grades. But the quiet kind — the kind that sits in a wet cave and refuses, day after day, to become what the killers need it to become — that kind I'd follow anywhere.

"Hold on to that testimony," I told him as we came out into the wind. "Every name, every date, every measurement. Hold it tighter than you held that pistol."

"Why?"

"Because right now it's the most dangerous object on this coast," I said. "More dangerous than any gun Vane owns. Guns only kill what's in front of them. Truth kills everything behind a man, all the way back to the beginning, and Vane knows it. That's why he murdered a survey crew over a hull breach. That's why he'll spend a fleet to stop one wounded surveyor reaching a courthouse." I tightened my grip on his arm. "So we're going to spend him instead."

Ahead of us, the light was failing, and far out on the gray water, running lights were moving — three of them, in formation, coming up the Reach like wolves working a scent line.

The fleet had found the empty grave.
