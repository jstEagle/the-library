## 11. Vane's Offer

They came for us at the narrows above Graywater Bend, where the old line pinched between a cliff face and a drop that went down far enough to make your stomach file a complaint. I'd chosen the route myself — high ground, one approach, no cover for skiffs to land. Which is why it bothered me when I heard the engines before I saw them, coming up the *other* side of the ridge I'd thought was my back door.

"Oscar." Finch said it low, the way you say a word you've learned means trouble. He'd taken to reading my silences like weather.

"I hear them."

Three machines, not skiffs this time but tracked crawlers, the kind salvage fleets use to haul winch cable over ground that laughs at wheels. They crested the rise in formation and fanned out with a discipline that told me these weren't hired muscle on a weekend errand. These were professionals. Somewhere behind them, presumably, was the man who paid for professional.

I put myself between Finch and the lead crawler and did the arithmetic I always do: distance, weapons, ground, odds. The odds were bad. The ground was worse. And Wren was half a mile back in the sea cave with a fever and a testimony worth more than every ship in the fleet combined, which meant running wasn't really running — it was just dying somewhere else.

The middle crawler stopped twenty yards out. The other two held their flanks. A man climbed down from the lead machine, unhurried, the way men move when they own everything around them including the concept of hurry. Grey coat, good boots, silver at his temples arranged like it had been installed rather than grown. I knew him from the shipping pages and the wanted-adjacent gossip columns: Aldous Vane, in the flesh, looking less like a pirate king and more like a bank director who'd wandered off his yacht.

"Miss Dravenmoor." His voice carried across the gap without effort. "Or do you prefer Oscar? Your reputation prefers the surname. The rest of you can decide."

"Most people who come looking for me send bullets," I said. "You sent yourself. Either that's flattering or it's a trap with better manners."

"It's neither. It's an introduction." He stopped at what he clearly judged to be conversational distance — close enough to be respectful, far enough that I couldn't reach him if I decided to be impolite. Smart man. Or well-advised one. "Aldous Vane."

"I know who you are. I've read your insurance filings. Well. The parts that survived."

Something flickered behind his eyes — quick, controlled, gone. A man like that doesn't survive by letting things flicker. But I'd seen it, and I filed it where I keep all useful things: next to the grudges.

"You found Wren," he said.

"I found a lot of things. Shallow graves. Boot prints from your crew. A survey team your government has already written off as lost." I let that sit. "Lost implies nobody knows where they are. Somebody knew exactly."

Finch shifted behind me. I could feel him wanting to say something about his father, wanting it so badly it had weight, like cargo shifting in a hold. Not yet. Not here.

Vane didn't deny any of it. That was the first thing that genuinely frightened me. Guilty men deny. Innocent men get angry. Vane did neither — he simply adjusted, the way water adjusts around a rock, and kept coming.

"Here's what I know about you," he said. "You run closed lines nobody else will touch. You mapped the Sable Reach spur system down to the last rotted tie — work the government never paid for, that no company ever commissioned. You did it because—" he paused, savoring it, "—because you couldn't *not*. Am I wrong?"

"You're wasting daylight."

"I'm establishing common ground." He gestured, small and precise, at the ridge, the cut, the drowned country beyond it. "Do you know what I see when I look at that line? Waste. Miles of iron laid by dead men, rusting because some committee decided the Reach wasn't profitable. You look at it and see a living thing. So do I. We're the only two people in this whole grey estuary who do."

"There's a third," I said. "He's seventeen and he stole aboard my engine. He sees it too. Difference is, he hasn't figured out how to monetize it yet."

That got a laugh out of him — brief, genuine-sounding, and therefore more unsettling than anything else he'd said. "Finch. Yes. I know about the boy. I know about the widow who hired you. I know you pulled Elias Wren out of a sea cave two nights ago, and I know he can barely stand." He spread his hands. "I'm not here to threaten you, Miss Dravenmoor. Threats are for people who don't understand value. I'm here to make you an offer."

* * *

He made it standing in the wind on a ridge above drowned rails, and I'll give him this: it was the best-made offer I've ever heard, and I've heard con men work a station platform like a fiddle.

"Double what the widow's paying you. Triple. Name a figure and watch me not blink." He said it lightly, like discussing freight rates. "Beyond that — a position. Chief surveyor for the fleet. Your own crawler, your own crew, access to every chart room I own. Do you know what my chart rooms contain? Neither do I, entirely. That's the point. Nobody has ever been able to map what I operate at my scale. The government can't. My rivals can't. It would take someone who runs closed lines at night for the pleasure of it. Someone who treats a rail cut like a conversation."

"And what would I be mapping?"

"The future." He turned, taking in the horizon with a proprietor's sweep. "The Reach is going to be developed, Miss Dravenmoor. Not by dreamers — by capital. Dredge the channels, rebuild the trestles, reopen the spurs. In ten years this will be the busiest corridor on the coast, and the man who owns the accurate maps of it will own the corridor. I'm offering you the chance to draw that map. Your lines. Your hand. With resources behind it instead of against it."

It was a good pitch. That's what I want recorded, somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of such things: it was a *good* pitch, and part of me — the tired part, the part that had slept four hours in three days, the part that ate cold beans over an engine boiler while the world called her a scavenger — that part leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

Because here's the truth nobody puts in ballads: loneliness isn't dramatic. It's just long. And Vane was offering an end to it. Crews. Charts. Someone else worrying about fuel and toll gangs and whether the bridge would hold. He was offering legitimacy, which is the one currency a woman like me can't mint alone, no matter how good her maps are.

And underneath it, unspoken but load-bearing: safety. Wren dies without a doctor. Finch gets collected eventually — boys who steal aboard engines grow into men who hang from gibbets unless somebody buys them a future. Cora Ashe gets nothing but a grave she can't visit. Every problem I had, Vane was offering to dissolve, in exchange for one small thing.

My signature. And my silence.

"What happens to Wren?" I asked.

"He recovers or he doesn't. Nature decides." Vane's tone didn't change, and that was the answer. "What happens to the boy? He goes to school. A good one. What happens to Mrs. Ashe? She receives a settlement — generous, anonymous, more than her husband's salary for ten years. Everyone walks away richer than they came. Except five dead men, and Miss Dravenmoor, they were dead before either of us arrived. I didn't kill them. I failed to prevent them. There's a difference, and history is written by people who can afford the distinction."

"There isn't a difference."

"There's always a difference," he said, "to whoever's still standing at the end."

* * *

I looked at Finch. He was staring at Vane with an expression I recognized — the same look he'd given the empty grave with the signal code scratched into its marker. Hope and horror sharing a bunk.

"You killed his father," I said. Quietly. Not an accusation — a measurement. I wanted to see what the number looked like on Vane's face.

"I restructured a liability," Vane said. "Your employer's husband found evidence of an accounting irregularity aboard one of my vessels and chose to pursue it through channels that would have ruined two hundred honest families employed by my fleet. The men who handled it exceeded their instructions. I regret that. I also benefited from it. Both statements are true, and only one of them matters to anyone with responsibilities." He stepped closer, boots crunching on shale. "You're stalling, Miss Dravenmoor. You're very good at it. But the tide doesn't negotiate, and neither, ultimately, do I. So let me say the quiet part plainly."

He did. Plainly.

"Wren's testimony is a piece of paper. Paper burns, paper drowns, paper gets discredited by the right magistrate — and I own the right magistrate, you'll discover that soon enough if you haven't already guessed. The only thing keeping that paper alive is you, and you are one woman with a borrowed engine and a boy, standing on a ridge I could take in ninety seconds. I'm not going to take it. I'm offering to *buy* it. That is the most generous thing that will ever happen to you, and you should recognize it as such, because the next offer won't be an offer."

The wind came up the narrows and moved his coat, and for a moment he looked exactly like what he was: a man standing on other people's graves, offering to sell the view.

And God help me, some cold practical corner of my brain ran the numbers anyway. Take the money. Take the post. Keep Finch fed and Wren breathing and Cora Ashe paid. Let the dead stay dead — they were dead regardless. Wasn't a live boy worth more than a dead man's vindication? Wasn't that just arithmetic, and hadn't I survived this long by doing arithmetic?

But then I thought about the boot prints in the clay. About packs left in perfect order at a ghost station, rations stacked neat as a shop display, because someone wanted the world to believe those men had simply walked out of their own lives. About a marker scratched with a live signal code by a man too hurt to dig himself free, waiting in the dark for a rescue that logic said wouldn't come, sending his little light anyway.

Elias Wren had spent six days in a sea cave with a broken body and one working conviction. If he could hold his line with a fever eating him alive, what kind of engineer was I if I sold mine for a chart room?

"No," I said.

Vane exhaled through his nose. Almost respect. "May I ask why? Genuinely. I collect reasons; they help me predict people."

"Sure." I jerked my chin at the drowned country below the ridge. "You offered me the future. But I already know what your future looks like — I've walked it. It looks like a ghost station with everybody's gear left in perfect order. You build beautiful things on top of holes, Mr. Vane, and you're offering me a job filling in the maps so nobody falls in. I'd rather fall in."

"That's not a reason. That's a temperament."

"It's the only kind of reason that holds in weather."

He studied me for a long moment, and I watched him revise something — his estimate of me, maybe, or his plan, or both. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone from his voice like sun off a slate roof. All that remained was the machinery underneath, and the machinery was very, very good.

"Then we understand each other," he said. "You have until the closing tide. After that, the Reach takes what it takes, and I take what's left. Including, I should mention—" he glanced past me, up the line, toward the sea cave, and smiled with nothing in it, "—whatever your wounded surveyor manages to write down between fevers. My men are thorough, Miss Dravenmoor. It's why I pay them. Unlike you, they took the offer."

He turned and walked back to the lead crawler. At the hatch he paused, half-turned, and delivered the last of it over his shoulder like a man tipping a hat.

"For what it's worth — your map work is the finest I've ever seen. That's not flattery. It's inventory. I now know precisely what I have to destroy."

The crawlers ground away down the far side of the ridge, engines fading into the wind, until it was just Finch and me and the grey water again.

Finch cleared his throat. "For a second there," he said carefully, "you were thinking about saying yes."

"I think about saying yes to lots of things," I told him. "Thinking's free. It's the signing that costs."

"So what do we do? Two of us, one engine, and he's got a fleet."

I looked north, where the clouds were stacking up dark and organized, the way weather does when it's got an appointment to keep. Then I looked at the boy whose father lay in a shallow grave under clay, and at the ridgeline Vane's crawlers had just crossed — and I started, quietly, to smile.

"We do what he just told us he fears most," I said. "We get clever with the map."

Finch frowned. "When did he tell us that?"

"When he said his men are thorough." I was already walking, fast, back toward the cave and the feverish man and the ticking water. "Thorough men follow routes, Finch. They don't make them. Come on — we've got a tide to weaponize."
