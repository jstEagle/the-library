## 12. Finch's Choice

They hit me with a belaying pin.

I want that on the record, because later — when people tell this story, and they will tell it wrong — I'd like it known that I did not go quietly aboard the *Corvid's Mercy*. I went loudly, swinging a boat hook I'd found under a tarp, and I cracked one of Vane's men across the shoulder before the second one came up behind me and introduced my skull to a piece of ship's rigging with real enthusiasm.

The world went white, then gray, then the inside of a hold that smelled of tar and old fish and money laundering.

When I could see straight again, my hands were zip-tied in front of me with electrical cable, which struck me as an insult. A salvage fleet this rich couldn't afford proper rope? I tested the ties anyway. Tight. Professional. These were men who tied up trespassers the way other men tied their shoes — without thinking about it, without caring whether the thing on the other end was a person or a parcel.

"Kid," said the man who'd pinned me, crouching so we were eye to eye. He had a beard like rusted wire and a voice like gravel settling. "You picked the wrong wreck."

"I pick everything wrong," I said. "It's a gift."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, and left, and a younger crewman sat outside the hold door with a rifle across his knees and the particular boredom of someone guarding nothing worth guarding.

* * *

Here's what happened, as best I can piece it together, because honestly some of it is still fog and ringing ears:

Oscar and I had split at the ridge line above the drowned cut. Her plan — and I use the word generously — was that she'd draw the patrol skiff east along the old quarry road while I circled back for Wren's signal lamp, which we'd cached in the rocks when the skiff first spotted our smoke. It was a good plan. It even worked, right up until the part where I came down the wrong gully and walked directly into a shore party from the fleet who'd been sent to sweep the beach for exactly the kind of evidence Oscar and I had been carrying around like idiots with a bomb.

Wren's testimony pages were sewn into my coat lining. That was the deal — Oscar carried the map knowledge, I carried the proof, and if one of us got taken the other could still finish it. So when they turned out my pockets and found nothing but a compass, half a tin of sardines, and my father's last letter, folded soft as cloth from a year of handling, I felt something I can only describe as relief wearing fear's clothes.

They kept the letter. Big mistake. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

The hold of the *Corvid's Mercy* was not what I expected, and I'd spent the whole skiff ride over expecting the worst. No chains bolted to bulkheads. No row of prisoners. Just cargo cradles, most of them empty, and one — aft, under a work lamp — occupied by a man sitting very still on an overturned crate, hands free, ankles free, reading a paperback novel like he was on holiday.

He looked up when the guard shoved me in. Fifty-ish, sunburned, surveyor's squint. And then he looked past me, at the door, at the guard, at the lamp, doing the same arithmetic I was doing, and I knew him before anyone said a name.

I'd seen his grave.

I'd stood over it two days ago, in the clay above the drowned cut, and read the marker scratched into a board: **T. HOLLIS — SURVEY PARTY**. One of seven markers. Six mounds. His mound was empty, and Oscar had knelt there with her fingers in the dirt and said, quiet as a prayer, *"Somebody climbed out."*

"You're Hollis," I said.

The man closed his book on his thumb. "And you're nobody I know, which means you're either very lost or very stupid."

"Both runs in my family."

Something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing a ghost of himself, ten years younger and twice as dumb. He glanced at the door again, and when he spoke his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"The letter in your pocket. The one the bosun took off you. Folded soft. You've read it a hundred times."

"It's my father's."

"Thomas Hollis wrote letters like that," he said. "Folded them soft. Carried them everywhere." He held my gaze. "Your father's name?"

"Finch. Samuel Finch."

The book slipped off his thumb. He caught it against his knee without looking, the way you catch things when your hands have done a job ten thousand times. For a long moment neither of us said anything, because there was a guard twelve feet away on the other side of a door, and grief is a loud animal, and we both had practice keeping it quiet.

"He talked about you," Hollis said finally. "In the camp. Every night, after readings. Said his boy could fix anything with wire and stubbornness." His jaw worked. "Said you'd come looking if the letters stopped."

"They stopped."

"Yeah." He looked at his own hands. "They stopped."

* * *

I should explain what I learned in that hold, in pieces, in whispers, between the guard's rounds — because it came in pieces, and I've had time since to fit them together, and the picture they make is worse than any single part.

Hollis hadn't escaped the massacre. He'd been spared. Deliberately.

The night Vane's men came to the survey camp — and it was Vane's men, Hollis confirmed it, six of them under a lieutenant named Corliss with a burn scar down his neck — they found what the team had found: the survey ledger cross-referenced against harbor manifests, proving the *Meridian Star*, Vane's own flagship before this one, went down on purpose. Ballast ports opened by her own crew. Insurance paid on a hull worth three times her scrap value, and eleven sailors who never got told they were ballast too.

My father's team had the proof. All of it. Signed, dated, witnessed.

So Corliss shot five of them and buried them shallow, and put a gun against Thomas Hollis's head, and then — because Aldous Vane does not waste assets — lowered it.

"One witness alive," Hollis told me, staring at the deck planking. "Kept comfortable. Kept fed. Kept *scared*. Because a dead witness proves nothing, but a living one who'll swear whatever Vane's lawyers need sworn — that's worth more than gold. If anyone ever drags this into court, Vane produces me, and I testify that the survey team fabricated the ledger for blackmail money. My word against a dead man's ink." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I've ever heard, because there was no humor in it at all. "And everyone in that courtroom will look at me and think: there's a man who sold his friends and lived. Which is true. Which is exactly what he wants them to think."

"You didn't have a choice."

"There's always a choice," he said. "That's the joke. I just made the one where I keep breathing." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Your father made the other one. Don't you dare waste it."

* * *

I don't know how long I sat in that hold. Long enough for the light through the loading hatch to change color twice. Long enough to learn the guard's routine — round every forty minutes, a smoke break on the weather deck every second round, and during the smoke break he propped the rifle against the doorframe rather than carry it, because the hold door locked from outside and he was guarding a teenager and a broken man, and boredom makes fools of us all.

Long enough to work the cable tie.

Not off. Off was impossible; I'd tried until my wrists bled. But the cable was cheap, and the crimp head had a burr on it, and if you twist your wrists opposite ways and grind the tie against the sharp edge of a cargo cleat — slowly, patiently, for hours, while smiling at your captor and asking politely for water — you can saw halfway through without it showing. The rest goes fast, if you don't mind pain. I minded. I did it anyway.

Hollis watched me do it and said nothing, and I understood why when I finally asked him, low, in the dark between rounds: "Come with me."

"No."

"Hollis—"

"Boy." He leaned close. "If I vanish, Vane loses his leverage and gains a blood hunt. He'll burn the coast looking for me, and he'll find your engineer woman, and he'll find Wren, and he will kill every one of you and sleep fine after. As long as I'm in this hold, breathing and compliant, Vane thinks his position is stable." He pressed something into my bound hands — small, metal, warm from his pocket. My father's letter, lifted back off the bosun's desk while the man slept. "Stable men get careless. Go be somebody's problem."

"But—"

"Tell him—" His voice cracked, once, and he mastered it the way you master a horse that wants to bolt. "Tell him the manifest was wrong. Tell him I checked it four times because I wanted to be wrong. Tell him—" He stopped. Started over, quieter. "Tell him I'm sorry it wasn't enough."

Then the lock turned, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

Getting off the ship was easier than getting on it, which tells you everything about how seriously the *Corvid's Mercy* took its security. The hold's loading hatch opened onto a lower deck stacked with salvage crates, the crates onto a maintenance ladder, the ladder onto a coal chute — disused, half-rusted, angled steeply down toward the waterline — and the chute spat me out onto a coaling platform slick with spray, thirty feet above black water, on the seaward side of the ship where the deck lights didn't reach.

Below me: the Reach, moving. Not calm. The tide tables Oscar kept reciting like scripture were turning, and the water in the channel had that muscular, restless look of something being pulled somewhere on schedule.

Above me: footsteps. Voices. Someone had found an empty hold and an empty crate and a guard who was about to have a very bad evening.

I had maybe four minutes.

And here is the choice. The one the whole thing comes down to, the one I've turned over every night since like a stone in the pocket.

East of the platform, maybe two hundred yards across open water, lay the shoreline rocks where Oscar would be waiting — if she was waiting, if she'd gotten clear of the patrol skiff, if she hadn't been taken too, if she hadn't decided I was dead and cut her losses the way smart people cut losses. Two hundred yards of cold, fast, tidal water, and I'm a strong swimmer but the current in the Reach doesn't care about strong swimmers; it cares about arithmetic, and the arithmetic says a fourteen-year-old boy in wet clothes has perhaps even odds of making that crossing.

West of the platform, twenty feet down a ladder, lay a moored ship's dinghy. Unwatched. Oars shipped. An easy, safe, sensible escape — row west along the coast, away from the fleet, away from everything, survive, run, live.

My father chose running once. I know that now. When the shooting started at the camp, when Corliss's men came out of the dark, Thomas Hollis says my father had a full ten seconds to reach the tree line and he spent them going the other way — toward Wren, dragging him upright, shoving him toward the sea caves, buying a stranger's life with his own. Ten seconds. That's all courage ever gets. Ten seconds and bad odds and no guarantee anybody will ever know.

I stood on that platform with salt spray hitting my face and my father's letter dry against my chest, and I thought about what he'd do with twenty feet of ladder and a dinghy and four minutes.

He'd take the dinghy. He was practical. But he wouldn't row west.

I went down the ladder, and I took the dinghy, and I rowed east.

* * *

The crossing nearly killed me, and I say that with the flatness of someone who has stopped finding death dramatic. The current grabbed the dinghy like it owed it money and dragged it south, and I rowed until my shoulders screamed and then past that, and I steered by the shape of the shoreline rocks against the sky, the ones Oscar had pointed at a hundred times with that finger she used like a conductor's baton. Twice I thought I heard engines behind me — the fleet waking up, the hunt starting — and both times I rowed harder instead of looking, because looking costs strokes and strokes were life.

I grounded the dinghy on the shingle below the rocks, fell out of it, and lay there gasping like landed fish while the tide sucked at my boots.

Then I crawled up the rocks, found the cache — Wren's signal lamp, right where we'd left it, untouched — and started working the shutter.

Three long. Three short. Three long. Our code, invented by Oscar on the train, mocked by me, memorized anyway: *alive, here, coming.*

The Reach answered with wind and water and nothing else.

I flashed it again. And again. My arms shook. Somewhere out in the dark, a ship's engine turned over — big, deep, deliberate — and searchlights began to swing along the waterline like slow white scythes, and I kept flashing, because the alternative was stopping, and stopping was the one thing my father had never taught me to do.

Then, from high on the ridge — a single answering flash. Sharp. Impatient. Unmistakably hers.

And below it, faint but carrying, a voice with that dry edge that meant she was furious and relieved in equal measure and would express both as sarcasm:

*"Finch! If that's you, you've got two minutes to explain why there's a search party between us and the cut — and if it isn't you, I'm very sorry to whoever I'm yelling at!"*

I stood up on the rocks, waving both arms, laughing and crying in a way I'd deny forever, holding the lamp high.

It was me. I was alive. I had the letter, I had the location of Vane's leverage, I had a plan forming in the back of my skull that was either brilliant or suicidal — with Oscar, historically, those were the same thing.

Two tides left. And now the fleet knew exactly where to look.
