## 13. Two Tides

The sea cave smelled of salt rot and kelp and the copper tang of Wren's blood, and I stood in it doing arithmetic that would have made a banker weep.

Two tides. That was what the tables in my head said, and my head had never once lied to me about water. The next low tide came at half past four in the morning. The one after that — the last one before the Reach swallowed the drowned cut for good and took every route out of this coast with it — came tomorrow evening, a little after six. After that, the spring tide would pile into the cut like a debt collector and stay for three days.

Three days was two days longer than Vane needed to make us all disappear.

Wren lay against the cave wall where Finch and I had dragged him, propped on a coil of old netting some fisherman had abandoned years ago. He was forty if he was a day, built like a man who'd spent his life leaning over surveying instruments in bad weather, and he'd spent the last week bleeding slowly from a wound in his side that he'd bound with strips of his own shirt and a paste of mud and moss that probably deserved a patent. His fever had a shine to it. I'd seen that shine before, on men I'd pulled out of wrecks, and I knew what it meant: the body was spending its reserves to keep the lights on.

"Say it again," I told him. "Slowly."

"Vane scuttled his own ship." His voice was gravel dragged through seawater. "The *Meridian Star*. Eleven months ago, off Grayhaven Shoal. He drilled her below the waterline himself — well, he had it done, but he signed the order, I saw the order. Then he filed the insurance claim. Forty thousand dollars against a hull worth twelve, and cargo he'd already quietly sold off in Port Mercy under a second manifest." He coughed, wet and long, and Finch flinched like each cough was a physical blow. "My team found the second manifest. In the customs house at Sable Landing. We were supposed to be surveying the channel. Instead we found a paper trail, and Vane found out we'd found it, and—" He stopped. Looked at the empty grave we'd told him about. At the marker scratched with his own signal code, because he'd scratched it himself, before they came back to finish the job.

"Six men," he said. "I buried five of them. The sixth one they took alive. Hollis. They keep him aboard the flagship as — as insurance. A witness they can produce or bury, depending on how the wind sits."

Finch had gone very still. I knew why. Hollis wasn't his father — his father was Marsh, the third name on the survey roster, the one whose letters had talked about *something wrong with the manifest* — but grief doesn't check names. It just checks faces. And Finch had been carrying his father's face around in his chest since the ghost station, since those packs sitting in perfect order with nobody coming back for them.

"Alright," I said. Because somebody had to say it, and neither of them was going to. "Here's what we have, and here's what we don't."

I crouched down and drew in the sand with a stick, the way I plan everything, because paper can be taken off you and sand can't. Not by Vane's men, anyway. The tide takes it for free.

"We have Wren's testimony, which is worth more than gold and about as durable as gold leaf right now. We have the signal code, which means somewhere between here and the magistrate's outpost there may be evidence caches Wren's team planted — papers, the manifest copy, instruments with serial numbers Vane's people can't explain away. We have my engine, sitting on the far side of the drowned cut, God willing and the water level willing. And we have two tides."

"What don't we have?" Finch asked. He'd learned to ask the question straight. I liked that about him. Most adults want you to soften it first.

"We don't have a magistrate who isn't bought," I said. "We don't have any law within a hundred miles that Vane's lawyers haven't already gotten to, or will get to before we do. We don't have weapons worth the name. We don't have a doctor, and Wren needs one. And we don't have time, which is the one thing that makes all the others survivable, because a man with no time makes mistakes, and Vane has been making money off mistakes his whole life."

Wren laughed. It turned into another cough, and Finch held a canteen to his lips while I pretended not to notice how carefully the boy did it, how he'd learned tenderness somewhere and kept it hidden like contraband.

"You're Dravenmoor," Wren said when he could talk again. "The map woman. My team used your charts. Marsh — Finch's father — swore by them. Said there wasn't a closed line on the coast you hadn't run in the dark."

"Your team also died following government coordinates," I said. "So maybe swear by something else."

But something went through me when he said it. Something I didn't have time to examine. A man dead a week, praising work I'd done, and his son sitting ten feet away not knowing that was the closest he'd ever come to hearing his father's voice. I put it away in the compartment where I keep things I'll feel later, when later is safe.

*

The plan, when I finally laid it out in the sand, had exactly three moving parts, because plans with more than three moving parts are just wishes wearing a uniform.

"Part one," I said. "We cross the drowned cut on the morning low tide. Half past four. That gives us maybe ninety minutes of walkable water, less with Wren's leg. My engine should still be on the spur above the cut — she was on solid rail when I left her, and the water hadn't reached her axles. If she runs, we go north along the maintenance line to Junction Nine, then east on the coastal branch to Harrow Point."

"Harrow Point," Wren said. "That's where the federal magistrate sits."

"That's where the federal magistrate *sat*, eight months ago, when the schedules were printed. Whether he still sits there, whether he's Vane's man now, whether his office even has a roof — I don't know. But it's the nearest federal authority, and it's across the Reach, which means Vane's fleet has to sail around the point to reach it while we can cut straight across on rails. That's our only advantage: his ships are faster than us on open water, but water bends around coastline and iron doesn't. I intend to make the geography argue on our side."

"And part two?" Finch asked.

"Part two is the evidence. Testimony alone gets a magistrate shrugging — no offense meant, Mr. Wren, but dying men say a lot of things. We need paper. The manifest copy, the scuttling order, anything with Vane's signature on it that isn't his lawyer's. Wren, your team planted caches. Where?"

He was quiet a moment, working it out behind eyes gone glassy with fever. "Sable Landing customs house. Behind the third-floor archive, loose brick above the window frame. And — the signal station at Cape Morrow. The old one, decommissioned. There's a floor cache under the lamp mount. Instruments and the logbook originals."

"Cape Morrow is on my map," I said, and felt the small cold satisfaction of it. "It's four miles off the maintenance spur. We can hit it on the way north without losing an hour."

"And part three?" Finch said.

"Part three is the part I haven't solved yet." I looked at him, because he deserved the truth and because lying to him had stopped being possible somewhere around the ghost station. "Hollis. The man in the empty grave's spot, alive on Vane's flagship. Every hour we run toward Harrow Point, Vane holds a live witness who can be coached, coerced, or killed to suit whatever story his lawyers tell. If we testify and Hollis contradicts us — or worse, if Hollis dies conveniently before the hearing — the whole thing wobbles."

"So we rescue him," Finch said. Flat. Certain. Fifteen years old and ready to board an armed flagship with a stick.

"No," I said. "We *can't*. Not with two tides and one engine and a dying man. And I need you to hear me say that plainly, Finch, because I know what you're carrying, and I know it's got a shape very much like Hollis's shape right now."

"It's my father's shape," he said quietly.

"I know." I let that sit. Out past the cave mouth the water was turning, the first whisper of the ebb beginning its long pull. "Here's what I can offer instead. Vane needs Hollis alive — a dead witness proves nothing except guilt. Which means as long as we're moving, Hollis breathes. And once testimony lands with a federal magistrate, Vane's leverage evaporates, because a coerced witness walks free the moment the law arrives. We don't save Hollis by going to him. We save him by making him too expensive to keep."

Finch stared at the sand drawing for a long time. I watched him do the arithmetic — not the tide kind, the other kind, the kind where you weigh a living man against a dead father and find both weights unbearable. Then he nodded, once, and I understood I was looking at someone deciding who he was going to be, and that it was going to be someone better than me.

"Okay," he said. "Then let's not waste the tide arguing."

*

We moved at three in the morning, in that black hour when the sea sounds loudest because everything else has shut up. I carried the medical end of Wren — such as it was, a shoulder and a lot of encouragement — and Finch carried the kit and the lantern with its shutter down to a coin of light. Wren walked. I'll give him that; he walked, on a leg that should have been in a splint, biting down on every step so hard I could hear his teeth from behind.

The cut was waiting for us the way I knew it would be: a long stone throat with maybe eighteen inches of water running out through it, smooth as poured glass in the dark, and the walls rising sheer on either side. Somewhere ahead, my engine sat on her spur like a promise I'd made to myself and couldn't remember signing.

"Ninety minutes," I said. "If the water starts coming back before we're through, we climb. There are ledges. Cold, wet, miserable, but above the flood line. Nobody argues with the schedule. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Finch.

Wren just set his jaw and stepped in first, which told me everything about what the last week had done to his sense of risk.

The crossing took fifty-one minutes. I counted. The water stayed at our shins, then our knees, then — for twenty yards near the middle, where the cut deepened over a washout — our waists, and Wren went gray as ash and leaned on Finch and cursed with a fluency that would've embarrassed a dockhand, and we kept moving. Twice I stopped us to listen, because sound carries strangely in a cut and I wanted the slap of returning water long before I saw it. Twice I heard nothing but the sea breathing behind us.

And then we were through, and up the shale ramp, and there she was — my engine, my stubborn, ugly, irreplaceable girl — sitting on dry rail with her boiler cooling and not so much as a gull perched on her, and I put my hand flat against her flank like a fool and felt her warmth and thought: *not yet. You don't get to be a memory yet.*

"She runs?" Finch asked.

"She runs." I was already in the cab, opening valves, checking water, listening to the pressure gauge tell me the night's news. "Get him up on the tender. Pad him with everything soft. And Finch —"

"Yeah?"

"When we move, we move fast. Faster than I like and faster than is strictly wise. The maintenance line hasn't been inspected since before the war, and I'm going to be asking it for favors it may not want to give. So hold onto something, and hold onto him, and don't ask me to slow down."

He climbed up beside Wren without another word. I watched him settle the man against his shoulder, watched Wren's fever-bright eyes close, watched the boy keep watch over a stranger because his father would have. Then I opened the regulator, and the old line unspooled ahead of us into the dark, and we went north.

*

Cape Morrow came up an hour later, a broken finger of basalt with the dead lighthouse squatting on top of it like a hat somebody forgot. The signal station huddled below it, shuttered, salt-eaten, exactly as forgotten as the government had intended. I brought the engine down the access spur slow, wheels singing on rail that hadn't heard song in years, and while Wren slept fever-dreams against Finch's shoulder, the boy and I went inside with the lantern.

The cache was where Wren said. Under the lamp mount, a floor panel cut flush and waxed against damp, and inside it a oilcloth packet containing the original survey logbook, a folded duplicate of the second manifest with initials on the margin that matched Wren's, and a scuttling order bearing a signature I recognized from newspaper engravings: *A. Vane*, written in a hand that signed other men's death warrants without lifting the pen.

Finch held the order under the lantern light for a long moment. His face did something complicated — grief and fury and a strange, settling calm, all fighting for the same ground.

"This is him," he said. "This is really him."

"That's really him." I wrapped the packet back in its oilcloth and stowed it inside my coat, against my chest, where I'd feel it if anyone tried to take it. "Paper burns, though. Paper drowns. Right now it's the most perishable thing on this coast, including Wren. Which means we don't stop again. Not for weather, not for breakdowns, not for anything short of the rails themselves giving out. Harrow Point by tomorrow evening's tide, or none of this ever happened."

I climbed back into the cab. And that was when I saw it — far out across the black water, past the point, where the Reach opened toward the shipping lanes.

Lights. Three of them, strung in formation, moving with the patient confidence of something that owns everything it looks at. The fleet. Not searching the coast anymore. Turning.

Turning toward Harrow Point.

They knew where we were going. Of course they knew — the magistrate was theirs, and Vane didn't need to catch us to kill us, he only needed to arrive first and own the room we were running toward. Two tides, I'd promised myself. Two tides to beat an armed fleet to a bought courthouse.

I put my hand on the regulator and pushed it further open than I ever had on this line, and the old engine answered like she'd been waiting all her life for me to stop being careful.

"Hold on," I said, to Finch, to Wren, to nobody. "We're about to find out what these rails remember."
