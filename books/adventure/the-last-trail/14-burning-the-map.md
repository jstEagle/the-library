## 14. Burning the Map

There's a particular kind of quiet that comes before you do something unforgivable. I'd heard it twice in my life — once before I signed the papers on my first engine, and once now, standing at the edge of the drowned cut with a tin of lamp oil in one hand and twenty years of my life folded into oilcloth under my arm.

The second quiet was louder.

Finch sat on a fallen tie, watching me with that look he'd been wearing since the cave — half awe, half worry, like he couldn't decide whether I was brave or simply broken in a way he hadn't catalogued yet. Give him time. He'd catalogue it.

Wren was asleep against the engine's driving wheel, fevered but breathing, his testimony scratched into three pages of survey paper tucked inside my coat where it rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Two tides left. Maybe less, if the storm Wren kept muttering about arrived early, and storms have never once had the courtesy to be late when I wanted them to be.

"You don't have to watch," I told Finch.

"Yes, I do," he said. "You watched for me. At the junction." He meant the night I tried to leave him behind and he stood on the platform in the rain, refusing to board the local, refusing to blink, until I gave up and took him aboard because some arguments you lose just to end them. "Fair's fair."

Fair's fair. The boy had his father's sense of accounting, apparently. I wondered if Elias Wren kept ledgers too, or if that was just something the Reach did to people — taught them to count everything, because everything cost.

I unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

You should understand what that map was, because I'm not sure I can explain it properly even to myself, and I was there for all of it.

It wasn't a map the way maps are maps. It wasn't printed. It wasn't filed anywhere. It existed in exactly one copy, drawn in my own hand on linen-backed paper, corrected in pencil over ink over pencil, layer after layer of revision going back to when I was nineteen years old and still signed my work with someone else's name because no railroad would hire a woman to run their dead lines. Every abandoned spur, every washed-out trestle, every tunnel that ate men and every cut that flooded on a schedule only I had bothered to learn. Twenty years of night runs. Twenty years of walking track that hadn't felt a wheel since before the war, tapping rails with a hammer and listening for the note that meant rot underneath.

People called me the woman who knew where the railroads went to die. They paid me obscene sums to get things through places no honest crew would enter, and they paid it because of that map, and they trusted me because of that map, and if I'm being honest — which I try to be exactly once per chapter — the map was more me than I was. My hands had made it. But somewhere along the way it had started making me back.

And Vane's trackers were reading it over my shoulder.

Not literally. Literally, they were three days behind us and closing, following the trail we'd left through country only that map could navigate. But a man like Aldous Vane doesn't need your actual map. He needs your *route*. Every mile we ran, every junction we chose, every flooded cut we crossed told his pursuit ship exactly how my mind worked, and my mind worked along lines that could be learned. That was the flaw in my legend nobody ever mentioned: a legend can be studied. A route can be predicted. And a predictable Oscar Dravenmoor was a dead Oscar Dravenmoor, with a dead stowaway and a dead surveyor and a testimony that died with us in the water.

So there was really only one move left, and it was the kind of move you make once.

"I need you to understand something first," I said to Finch, because apparently I was going to make a speech, and when an old woman starts making speeches you let her, out of decency. "This isn't bravery. Bravery is cheap. This is arithmetic."

"Everything with you is arithmetic."

"Everything with everyone is arithmetic. Some people just lie about it." I held up the map so he could see it in the last of the daylight — the whole Sable Reach rendered in my cramped, obsessive hand, every secret I owned laid out in graphite and grease. "Vane's people are good. Better than good. Given enough of our trail, they'll reconstruct this. Not all of it — they'll miss the details, the trap lines, the washouts — but enough. Enough to corner us at the magistrate's door, enough to know where we'd run, enough to turn every choice I make into a door they've already opened."

"So burn it, then. Problem solved."

"No." I shook my head slowly. "Burning it doesn't solve anything. Burning it means *they* can't solve it either. Including the parts I need." I looked at him until he got it, and I watched it land — watched the boy realize what I was actually saying, watched his face go through the stages the way faces do. Confusion. Then the arithmetic. Then something that looked a lot like grief on behalf of another person, which is the purest grief there is.

"You won't have it anymore either," he said quietly.

"There it is."

"But you've got it memorized. You said—"

"I've got the big lines memorized. The trunk routes, the main cuts, the tide tables. What I don't have — what nobody has but the paper — is twenty years of small corrections. Which culvert dropped two inches in the spring flood. Which trestle lost its middle span and looks fine from the approach. Which line Vane's lead ship will take because it *looks* safe, right up until it isn't." I smiled, and I felt it come out crooked, the way my smiles do when there's teeth behind them. "That last part, though. That part I'm keeping up here." I tapped my temple. "The rest goes in the fire."

* * *

I want to tell you I hesitated. It would make a better story, wouldn't it — the trembling hand, the long moment, the near-change of heart. Novelists love a trembling hand. But the truth is my hand didn't tremble, and that frightened me more than any patrol skiff ever had, because it meant some part of me had already done the grieving in advance, quietly, sometime between the cave and the cut, and had simply waited for the rest of me to catch up.

I poured the lamp oil slow. I'm not a wasteful woman; even in destruction I keep good form.

The linen took the flame the way old paper does — reluctantly, then all at once. Twenty years went up in about ninety seconds, which is either a lesson about impermanence or a lesson about oil, depending on your temperament. I stood there and watched the Sable Reach curl and blacken. The drowned cut went first, then the ghost station, then the little maintenance spur that only my map knew, the one Finch and I had walked while he talked about his father's letters and the wrongness of manifests. The fire ate the trap lines. It ate the safe houses. It ate the notes in the margins in my own voice — *rot, do not trust*, *span gone 3rd pier*, *water lies here* — twenty years of a woman talking to her future self, and her future self burning the conversation.

Finch didn't flinch. I'd told him he didn't have to watch, and he watched anyway, and he didn't flinch, and I don't know why that undid me more than the fire did. Maybe because flinching would have made it a tragedy, and not flinching made it a decision, and decisions are things you can live inside of. Tragedies just happen to you. Decisions you have to carry.

When it was down to embers I stirred them with a stick until nothing remained but ash that might have been anything — a campfire, a signal, a cook's carelessness. Nothing that said *here burned the only copy of the most dangerous document on the coast.*

"Well," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "That's that."

"What does it feel like?" Finch asked. Not *are you okay*, mind you. The boy had learned quickly that I answered honest questions and deflected kind ones.

I considered lying. I considered saying it felt like freedom, which is what the ballads would say, or like losing a limb, which is what the tragedies would say. Neither was true. Here's what was true:

"It feels like being unarmed," I said. "For twenty years, whenever anything went wrong, I had a way out in my pocket. Every bad situation, every cornered moment — I always had one more line nobody else knew about, one more door. Now—" I spread my empty hands. "Now the doors are all in here, and memory is a poor mapmaker. It rounds corners. It forgets which pier is rotten. From tonight on, I'm running on a legend instead of a fact, and legends have killed better engineers than me."

"But Vane's running on less."

"Vane's running on nothing." I crouched and ground the last ember flat under my boot heel. "He's got trackers reconstructing a route from our wake. By tomorrow he'll think he knows where we're going. And tomorrow night, I'm going to teach him what happens to a man who thinks he knows where I'm going." I stood, brushed ash off my knees, and found Finch still watching me with those cataloguing eyes. "What?"

"Nothing. It's just—" He hesitated, which he almost never did. "My dad used to say you could tell what a person loved by what they'd give up for other people. He said most folks never give up anything, so you never find out." He shrugged, suddenly awkward, fourteen years old again instead of whatever ancient thing he'd been becoming out here. "I guess now I know what you loved."

"That's a terrible thing to say to a woman ten minutes after her funeral."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be ready." I nodded toward the engine, where Wren shifted and murmured in his fever. "Because what comes next, we only get one try at, and I've just spent my safety net buying it. If I'm wrong about the trap line — if my memory rounds one corner wrong, forgets one rotten pier — then we don't fail. We *vanish*. Same as the survey team. Same as everybody Vane buries."

Finch picked up his pack. When he slung it over his shoulder he said, without looking at me, "You won't be wrong."

"And if I am?"

"Then I'll say I told you so on the way down."

Despite everything — the ash, the unarmed feeling, the storm building somewhere out past the Reach like a debt coming due — I laughed. Actually laughed. First time since the widow's parlor. There may be a lesson in that too, but I've had quite enough lessons for one chapter.

* * *

We ran that night with no map and no margin, just me and the dark and twenty years of ghosts telling me where the rails remembered being safe. And here is the thing I discovered somewhere around mile forty, hammering a joint and listening for the note: the map had been a crutch after all. A beautiful, irreplaceable, twenty-year crutch. The knowledge was still mine. It had always been mine. Paper was just where I'd kept it because keeping it in paper meant I never had to trust myself completely.

No more of that. From here to the magistrate, from here to Vane's throat, I was the map.

Near dawn, with Wren shivering under every blanket we owned and Finch riding the footplate learning the fire like his father had probably once learned it, I felt it before I saw it — far off across the flats, a smudge of smoke moving wrong for weather and right for a ship. Vane's lead pursuer, running the coastal line, exactly where my burned map said a smart tracker would expect us to flee.

Exactly where I wanted him.

"He's taken the bait," Finch said, glass raised.

"He's taken what he thinks is bait." I handed the glass back and put my hand on the throttle, and I will confess that in that moment, unarmed and mapless and down to my last two tides, I felt more dangerous than I had in twenty years. "What he's actually taken is an invitation. There's a trestle six miles ahead that's been missing its middle span since the flood of '09, and every chart in existence says it's intact."

Every chart except one. And that one was ash.

I opened the throttle, and the last trail began.
