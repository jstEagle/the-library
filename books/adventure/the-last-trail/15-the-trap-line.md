## 15. The Trap Line

There's an old saying among line runners: the map is the territory. It's a lie, of course. The map is a promise you make to yourself that the world will hold still long enough to be known. I'd spent eleven years making that promise and burning my master copy had not, as it turned out, freed me from keeping it. Because here was the ugly truth of it: I didn't need the paper anymore. Eleven years of night runs over closed lines had written themselves into me — every grade, every culvert, every rotten trestle between the Sable Reach and the magistrate's outpost at Graywater Junction. You can burn a map. You cannot burn what it made of you.

Which is why, on the morning after the burning, with two tides left and Vane's lead pursuit ship crawling up the coast road like a spider that had finally admitted it was hunting, I sat on the footplate of my engine and did something I hadn't done since I was a girl learning her father's trade.

I planned a murder.

Not of a man. Of a ship. Though watching Aldous Vane's face when it happened, I suspect he'd have told you there wasn't much difference.

* * *

Finch found me studying nothing — which is to say, studying everything, all of it held behind my eyes where no tracker could read it.

"You haven't slept," he said. He'd learned to read me faster than most grown men ever managed. Fourteen years old and already fluent in the language of a woman who communicated mostly in silences and sarcasm. His father would have been proud. His father, if Elias Wren's account held true, had died face-down in clay with his boots still laced, because Aldous Vane needed a ledger to disappear more than he needed six honest men alive.

"Sleep is for people whose enemies sleep," I said.

"Vane's men are sleeping right now. Saw their campfire from the ridge."

"They're sleeping because they think they know where we're going." I let that sit. "Graywater Junction. The magistrate. It's the only road out of the Reach that makes sense, so it's the only road they'll watch."

Finch frowned. "But we *are* going to Graywater."

"We are," I agreed. "Eventually. By a road they don't believe exists anymore."

Wren lay in the cab behind us, wrapped in every blanket I owned, feverish but lucid in the way dying men sometimes manage — as though the body, knowing it's spending its last coin, spends it carefully. He'd dictated testimony into Finch's notebook until midnight. Insurance fraud. Six murders. A scuttled ship called the *Meridian Star* and a salvage claim built on a wreck its own owner had made. Enough to hang Vane twice over, if anyone honest ever heard it.

"The old coast spur," Wren rasped from under the blankets. "You're thinking about the old coast spur."

I turned. "You know it?"

"I surveyed it. Eight years ago, before the washout." His eyes were bright with fever and something else. "The Kestrel Trestle. Hundred and forty feet of timber over a gorge the sea fills twice a day. The flood took out the middle span. They never rebuilt it. Officially, the line doesn't exist past mile marker nine."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it exists exactly as much as it always did. The rails are still there. The ties underneath them are mostly gone." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man who had watched his colleagues buried in shallow graves and had been waiting eight years for someone like me to come along. "A ship can't tell the difference from the water. But a locomotive can."

* * *

Here is what the salvage fleet understood, and what I intended to turn against them: Vane's lead ship — the *Iron Meridian*, three hundred tons of armored arrogance with a bow gun and a crew of forty — could not follow us inland. No ship could. So they tracked us the way all hunters track prey that moves along fixed paths: they watched the corridors. The coast road. The river mouths. The one rail causeway where the Sable Reach narrowed to a throat and any engine heading for Graywater would have to cross within sight of water.

They knew I'd burned my map. Vane's trackers had found the ashes — I'd left them conspicuously, because a burned map tells a hunter two things: she's desperate, and she's now running blind. Both lies. A blind runner sticks to the obvious route. A blind runner can be predicted, intercepted, cornered at the drowned cut or the narrow causeway where forty men with a bow gun end the story in ten minutes.

So I gave them a blind runner.

At dawn I ran my engine loud and visible down the valley road, smoke standing up straight in the still air like a signal flag, exactly the way a woman without a map runs — cautious, hugging the low ground, doubling back once for show near Miller's Bend where I knew — remembered — a patrol skiff liked to idle. Let them see me hesitate at the junction where the coast line diverged. Let them see me take the wrong branch, correct, take the wrong branch again. A woman groping through fog toward the only door she knows.

Finch worked the fireman's side in silence, feeding the boiler, watching me perform. After an hour of it he said, quietly, "That's the best lying I've ever seen, and you're not even talking."

"I'm always talking," I said. "This is just the first time anyone's listening."

* * *

The Kestrel Trestle stood where Wren said it would, and it stood the way old things stand in my memory — half-ruin, half-ghost. From the ridge above the gorge I glassed it through my father's brass spyglass while Finch held the engine quiet under steam, ticking like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

The middle span was gone. Forty feet of open sky where timber should be, the broken ends of the trusses jutting out like snapped bone. Below it, the gorge floor was a shelf of black rock and tidal pool, and beyond the gorge the sea lay flat and silver, waiting for its afternoon appointment with the tide tables.

And there, anchored two miles offshore in the haze, sat the *Iron Meridian*, black hull, white superstructure, patient as a debt.

"She can't see the gap from that distance," Finch said. "Can she?"

"Not the gap. Not from seaward, with the sun on the water and the trestle reading as just another gray line against the cliff." I lowered the spyglass. "What she *can* see is a locomotive crossing that line. What she can hear, on a calm day like this, is my whistle echoing off the gorge walls. And what her captain knows — because Vane will have told him — is that the only way to Graywater runs across this trestle."

"So he'll move in close. To watch us cross. To catch us halfway."

"To catch us halfway," I agreed. "Or to shell us off the span if we look like we might make it. Either way, he has to come inside the headland. Inside the headland, the water shoals fast and the current runs contrary to the tide, and a three-hundred-ton ship drawing nineteen feet has exactly one channel available to her." I tapped the air, tracing a line only I could see. "And that channel runs directly beneath the washed-out span."

Finch looked at the gap in the trestle. Then at me. Then back at the gap.

"You want him under the hole," he said slowly, "when the tide comes in."

"I want him under the hole when the tide comes in *and* when a certain amount of very heavy timber comes down." I nodded at the eastern approach, where the trestle's landward spans still stood, sound enough to walk on, unsound enough to fall. "Eight years of salt rot, Mr. Finch. Those trusses aren't holding anything up anymore. They're just waiting for permission."

"How do you give a trestle permission to fall?"

I looked at him until he got it. He was a smart boy. It took him maybe four seconds, and then his face went pale and set at the same time, which is, I've found, the exact expression courage wears before it's tested.

"You're going to drive the engine across," he said. "Right up to the edge of the gap. And the weight—"

"The weight of forty tons of locomotive on trusses rated for twenty, rotted down to twelve, will bring the eastern span down into the channel. Right on schedule. Right on top of whatever happens to be moored beneath it." I shrugged. "Assuming my memory of the load ratings is right. Assuming the rot is where Wren says it is. Assuming the tide behaves."

"That's a lot of assumptions."

"That's engineering, boy. Everything else is just arithmetic with prayers attached."

* * *

We spent the middle of the day preparing, and I will be honest with you, since this account is the only honesty I have left: my hands shook through all of it. Not from fear. Fear is a clean feeling, and this wasn't clean. This was the deliberate unmaking of the one thing I had sworn my whole life to protect — the engine, my father's engine, the *Perseverance*, forty tons of riveted iron that had been my home, my income, my identity, and, if I'm cataloguing honestly, my only companion for eleven years of empty stations and closed lines.

I'd already burned the map. Now I meant to burn the thing the map was for.

"You could jump clear," Finch said. We were packing the firebox extra dense, banking the pressure high, because a dead engine rolls farther and hits harder than a live one. "Last thirty feet. Jump, and let her go in alone."

"And lose forty tons of falling iron's worth of certainty? No." I checked the gauge. Two hundred and ten pounds. Good. "She goes all the way across or not at all. That was our deal, her and me. Never stop moving. I'm not going to teach her new rules on her last run."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing with me around chapter four of our acquaintance, which showed a wisdom rare in teenagers and rarer still in adults.

Wren insisted on being propped up in the cab to watch. "I surveyed this line," he said, when I told him to lie down. "I signed the report that closed it. Least I can do is watch it do some good." Fever-bright, wrapped in blankets, he looked like a ghost attending his own funeral, and I decided that was appropriate luck and stopped fighting him.

* * *

We made our approach at half past three, an hour before high water, running slow and loud and lost.

I played it perfectly, if I say so myself, and I do, because nobody else will. I brought the *Perseverance* down the coast grade in plain view of the water, whistling at the curves the way a nervous runner whistles — too often, too bright. I stopped once, visibly, at the old mile marker nine, walked the length of the engine pretending to inspect her, and stood a long time looking at the trestle the way a woman without a map looks at a bridge she's only heard about. I even took out the spyglass and studied the far shore with theatrical thoroughness.

Two miles out, the *Iron Meridian* weighed anchor.

"There she comes," Finch breathed.

Through the glass I watched her come on — steady, unhurried, supremely confident, a predator that had been told the trap was already sprung and was merely walking over to collect the animal. She stood in past the headland, following the deep channel with the lazy precision of a captain who'd charted these waters from the comfort of a chart room. Her bow gun was manned. Men lined her rail. And on her upper deck, beside the wheelhouse, stood a figure in a gray coat who needed no glass to identify, because evil of that particular vintage carries itself a certain way.

Aldous Vane had come to watch me drown or surrender. Possibly both. He struck me as a man who enjoyed options.

"He sees us," Finch said. "He's coming to the rail."

"Good. I want an audience." I climbed back into the cab and put my hand on the regulator. "Because what happens next only works if he understands it. Not the crew. Him. He's the one who reads maps."

I opened the throttle.

The *Perseverance* gathered herself and rolled onto the trestle at a careful walking pace, rods turning, drivers biting, forty tons of iron announcing itself to eighty-year-old timber with a groan that traveled up through the footplate and into my teeth. The sound was terrible. It was the sound of a structure telling the truth about itself, and the truth was that it had been dead for years and was only now getting around to mentioning it.

Fifty feet onto the span. The trusses sang a high, splintering note. Dust sifted up between the ties like the bridge exhaling.

A hundred feet. Out on the water, the *Iron Meridian* had slowed, holding station a quarter mile off the headland, well inside the channel now, her bow pointed at the gap in the trestle as if she meant to sail through it. Vane stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind him, watching me cross, and I imagined him doing the arithmetic he thought he was doing: *she's committed, she's mid-span, she can't turn, my gunners have all the time in the world.*

Two hundred feet. The eastern approaches began.

Here is the moment I will remember on my deathbed, whenever some honest doctor gets around to scheduling it: the last hundred feet of track I would ever run. The rails ahead of me gleaming in the low sun, running out to the torn edge of the span and then simply ending, the way all lines end eventually, in either a destination or a drop. My hand on the regulator, steady now, the shaking gone, because there is a particular peace that arrives when the decision is finally behind you and only the consequence remains.

"Oscar," Finch said softly. Not a question. Just my name, the way you'd say it at a graveside.

"Now," I said, and opened her all the way.

Forty tons surged forward. The trestle screamed. Behind us, span by span, the eastern trusses began to go — not all at once but in sequence, a domino run of structural failure, each cracked member passing its burden to the next weaker one, the whole rotten argument of the bridge collapsing inward toward the point of greatest load, which was me, which was us, which was the engine standing on the last intact span with her cowcatcher hanging over open air and the gorge yawning below.

I slammed the reverser into emergency and we stood there, brakes screaming, the *Perseverance* shuddering on a span that was actively disintegrating beneath her, and I felt the exact moment the eastern approach let go — a lurch, a roar, four hundred tons of timber and iron railing peeling away from the cliff and dropping into the channel in a single catastrophic exhalation.

The splash reached us as spray. The sound reached us as thunder.

And out on the water, the *Iron Meridian* — caught in the narrowing channel with the tide shoving her stern and the shoals guarding her flanks, with nowhere to go but forward and the forward way suddenly blocked by a floating barricade of hundred-year-old timber — rang her engines full astern and began, ponderously, catastrophically, to swing broadside onto the rocks.

* * *

We jumped, Finch and I, at the last workable second, onto the solid rock of the western abutment, and we pulled Wren after us, and the three of us stood on the cliff edge and watched the *Iron Meridian* die by inches.

She didn't sink. That was almost the worst part. She grounded — hard aground amidships on the channel shelf, listing fifteen degrees, her keel laid open on rock that her charts swore wasn't there, taking water with the methodical patience of the sea settling a very old bill. Her crew swarmed her decks. Boats went down. Somewhere aboard, a man was shouting orders that no longer mattered.

And on her tilting upper deck, alone amid the chaos, Aldous Vane stood perfectly still.

Even at four hundred yards, even through salt haze, I could feel him find me on the cliff. Feel the moment his eyes — a man who read water the way I read rail — traced the collapsed span backward to its cause, and understood. Not that the bridge had failed. That it had been *used*. That the blind woman with the burned map had known every rotted inch of this coastline better than his charts, better than his captains, better than his money could buy, and had walked his flagship into the one place on earth where all that knowledge could be dropped on his head.

He raised one hand. Not a fist. Not a curse. Almost — and this is what chilled me — almost a salute. The acknowledgment of one professional to another, across a widening gap of water and wreckage.

Then he turned and started giving orders, and even from the cliff I could read the shape of them: save the ledgers first.

"He'll get off her," Finch said. "Ships like that don't sink before the important man gets a boat."

"I know." I watched the list deepen. Somewhere below us, my engine hung off the ruined span, nose over the gorge, dead or dying, and I made myself not look at her. Not yet. Grief keeps; vengeance doesn't. "Vane will be ashore by dark with dry boots and a grudge, and everything he couldn't save off that ship will be ashore with him. Including his patience."

Wren, propped against a boulder, feverish and grimly satisfied, said, "You've crippled his lead ship and stranded his fleet inside a closing tide. Do you know what he does to people who cost him money?"

"I'm counting on it," I said.

Finch looked at me. "Counting on it how?"

I turned east, toward Graywater Junction, where the last open road to a bought magistrate ran along the shore — along the shore, past a grounded, sinking, furious man's entire reach. "Because a mortal enemy," I said, "is predictable in ways a cautious one never is. He'll come for us himself now. No more ships, no more hired guns at arm's length. Him. And a man like Vane, coming personal, makes mistakes a fleet never would."

The tide was turning. Behind us, the *Perseverance* groaned against her broken cradle of timber, and I finally allowed myself one glance back at her — one — before shouldering Wren's arm over my shoulder and starting down toward the shore road.

One tide left. One enemy ashore. And somewhere between here and Graywater, the last trail of all.

"Keep him moving," I told Finch. "We've got a magistrate to embarrass."
