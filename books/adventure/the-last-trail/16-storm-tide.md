## 16. Storm Tide

I have kept a surveyor's log for twenty-two years, and in that time I have recorded landslides, flash floods, a bear in the instrument tent, and one memorable occasion when my assistant ate a week of rations in a single night and blamed the tide. I mention this to establish my credentials as a man who knows what weather means. So when I woke in the bottom of that rocking railcar with my teeth chattering like a telegraph key and the smell of rain coming through the boards, I did not need a barometer to tell me the arithmetic had gone wrong.

The storm was early. The storm was a day early, which in the business of tides is the same as being a day late for everything else.

"Oscar," I said, or intended to say. What came out was more of a croak, the kind of sound a frog makes when it has opinions about the weather but no authority to enforce them. I tried again. "Oscar. The barometer."

She was already there, crouched in the dark with a lantern turned low, her face all angles and shadow. Behind her the boy — Finch — was wrestling the canvas flap closed against a wind that had opinions of its own.

"I know," she said. "Fell four points since midnight. Fell four more since you started snoring."

"I don't snore."

"You narrate," she said. "Whole surveys. Names of elevations. It's very dull."

I would have laughed, but laughing cost breath and breath was currency I was running short of. The fever had been building for two days, ever since the cave, ever since the salt water got into the gash on my thigh and set up housekeeping. Now it had invited friends. My leg had gone from aching to singing, and the song it sang was not a good one. Somewhere under the fever I understood, with the cold clarity fever occasionally grants, that the wound had turned. That the thing in my blood was now a more reliable killer than Aldous Vane, and Vane was very reliable.

"The tide," I managed. "If the storm surge stacks on top of the spring tide—"

"I've done the figures," Oscar said. "Twice."

"And?"

"And I got the same wrong answer both times, so I'm doing them a third time." She said it flat, the way she said most things, but I had spent enough days with her now to hear the seam in it. The figures were wrong in the specific sense that they meant the final tide would not be a deadline. It would be a wall.

* * *

Let me tell you what the truth weighs, since I was carrying it.

It weighs about as much as a ledger. A water-stained, salt-bloated ledger, forty pages of it, that I had pulled off Vane's own desk the night his ship *Meridian* went down in Sable Reach — pulled it off the desk of a ship that was supposed to be wrecked by weather, in a hold that had been opened from the inside, with seacocks that had been turned by a wrench I later found stowed in the captain's locker, wrapped in oilcloth, clean as the day it was bought. Nine men drowned that night. The ledger proved the cargo manifest was fiction, the insurance claim was fraud, and the wreck was a purchase. Vane had sold his own ship to the sea and sent the sea the bill.

I had carried that ledger out of the Reach on a broken leg, watched my six colleagues die for carrying less, buried them in clay I dug with a survey spade, and then crawled into a sea cave and scratched a signal code into a stone marker above a grave that wasn't occupied, because I figured if anyone ever came looking for the dead, they might also come looking for the one grave with nobody in it. Surveyors think in contingencies. It's the whole trade. You don't map the road; you map what happens when the road fails.

And now the truth was lying in a storm-battered railcar on a closed line, wrapped in oilcloth next to a clean wrench, inside a body that was cooking itself alive.

"You should take it," I told Oscar, somewhere past noon, when the rain had gone from drumming to hammering. "The ledger. Take it and the boy and go. I'll slow you down. I've been slowing you down since the cave."

Oscar didn't look up from the tide tables she'd spread on the floor, weighted with a wrench at each corner. "No."

"You don't even consider it."

"I considered it," she said. "For about a second and a half. It was a bad second and a half and I'm not proud of it, but it's over." She looked up then, and her eyes had that quality I'd started to recognize — the flat gray of a man who has read a river and knows exactly what it will do. "Here's the thing about you, Wren. You're not a man carrying a ledger. You're a man who *is* a ledger. Every figure in that book is in your head too. I've heard you recite cargo tonnage in your sleep. You narrate."

"I told you I don't—"

"Manifest numbers. Page by page. It's very dull." She folded the tables. "So even if I left you, Vane would still have to kill you, and I'd still have made a widow of the truth. I don't care for the arithmetic. So we don't split. We move."

The boy spoke up from the door, where he was watching the rain come off the ridge in gray sheets. "The magistrate's outpost. How far?"

"Two days' run if the line holds," Oscar said. "One and a half if I push her."

"And the tide?"

Oscar was quiet a moment, and I watched her decide whether to lie to him, and watched her decide not to. That, I thought, was the whole of her character in one gesture. "The tide closes the drowned cut behind us at tomorrow's high water, storm surge included. If we're not through it before then, we're not through it. And if the storm's still on us when we cross—" she didn't finish, which was answer enough.

The cut. The Drowned Cut. I'd surveyed it myself, years back, before the line closed. A channel the rails crossed on a low causeway, passable on foot at low water, a drowning trap at high. I had written the report that got the line closed, in fact. There's a joke in there somewhere, but I was too feverish to find it.

* * *

We moved at dusk, because Oscar said the rain would cover the engine noise and because waiting for the storm to end was a luxury the arithmetic no longer allowed. Getting me onto the engine's cab floor was a production. I am not a proud man — pride is heavy and I travel light — but there is a particular indignity in being carried up a ladder by a woman half your size while a fifteen-year-old boy pushes from below and the rain tries to drown all three of you for sport.

"Surveyors," Oscar grunted, hauling me over the lip, "are heavier than they look."

"We carry a great deal of data," I said, and then the fever took the joke and shook it and I was laughing and shivering at the same time, which alarmed the boy considerably.

Finch. I should say something about the boy, since the fever has made me sentimental and this may be the only testimony I get to give about anything. His father was Tomas Finch, rod-man on my crew, the best in the business, a man who could hold a level staff dead steady in a gale and who wrote letters home that read like love songs to a boy he missed so much it embarrassed the rest of us. They killed him in the camp on the third night, I think. I was already in the water by then, so I didn't see it, and I have spent every waking hour since wishing I had, so that at least one of us would know. The boy has his father's stubbornness. I recognized it the first day, when Oscar tried to put him off at a junction and he simply refused, in the quiet way of his, to have been put off.

He sat beside me through the run, one hand braced on my shoulder every time the engine lurched, which was constantly, because Oscar drove that night the way she did everything — at the exact edge of what the machine and the line could bear, no further, never an inch further, and never an inch less.

"Tell me about him," the boy said at some point in the dark. "My dad. You never tell me the same thing twice."

"That's because everything I tell you is true," I said, "and the truth doesn't repeat itself clean. All right. Here's one. Your father once argued with me for four hours about a benchmark. Four hours. He was sure my figure was off by two centimeters."

"Was it?"

"It was off by three," I said. "I never told him. He'd have been unbearable."

The boy smiled in the dark, and I thought: this is what the ledger is for. Not for the magistrate, not for the law, not for the abstract machinery of justice. For a boy on the floor of a locomotive, so that one day the shape of his father's death would not be a blank page.

* * *

The fever broke over me properly around midnight, and I will not pretend to remember that stretch in order. Fever is a tide of its own. It comes in, it takes things, it goes out and leaves the beach rearranged.

I remember dictating. I remember that clearly, because Oscar held the lantern for me and Finch wrote, in a surveyor's field book he'd found in my pack, and I remember being furious about the field book — *that's for data, boy, not for this* — and Oscar saying, in that flat gray voice, "This *is* data, Wren. The most important survey anyone's ever run on this coast. Keep talking."

So I kept talking. Page by page, figure by figure. The seacocks, the wrench, the manifest, the night the *Meridian* went down with her pumps dry and her crew locked below by orders that came from the flagship. The names of my six dead. I made the boy write the names twice. Names are the figures that matter most and the ones magistrates skip.

"Again," I said, teeth rattling. "The names. Again."

"They're written, Mr. Wren."

"Write them again. Ink fades. Grief doesn't, but ink does."

Somewhere in the small hours I remember the engine stopping — a hard, wrong stop, the brakes singing — and hands on me, and Oscar's voice very close and very calm, saying my name the way you'd say it to a man on a ledge. I had been walking, apparently. In my head I had been walking the causeway at the Drowned Cut, checking the water level, because that was my job, that had always been my job, and the water was coming and someone had to check.

"You're on the engine," she said. "You're on the floor. You're safe. The water's four miles back."

"It's coming," I told her. "It always comes. That's the whole thing about water, Dravenmoor. It comes."

"Yes," she said. "So do we. Lie down."

* * *

Dawn came gray and furious, the storm still on us, the rain moving sideways, the ridges gone to smoke. I woke lucid for the first time in a day, which I understood immediately was not good news. Fever is a storm; lucidity after fever is the survey of the damage. I took stock the way I'd take stock of a bridge. Leg: swollen to the knee, hot, the gash angry and weeping. Breath: short, wet at the bottom. Hands: shaking. Verdict: I was failing, and the failure had a schedule, and the schedule was running ahead of the tide.

Oscar saw me taking the survey of myself and knew what I was doing. She'd have done the same.

"How long?" she asked. No preamble. She doesn't believe in them.

"If I'm carried — two days, maybe. If I'm kept warm and dry and dosed with whatever's in your kit — three, at a cost. If I keep moving in this weather—" I shrugged, which was all the answer there was.

"The outpost is a day's run," she said. "Maybe a little more, with the storm on the line."

"Then the arithmetic favors us," I said, "which frightens me, because in my experience arithmetic that favors you is the first sign you've miscounted."

Finch came in from the tender, soaked through, carrying water. He'd taken over the water detail, and the wood detail, and the watching detail. The boy had appointed himself crew and none of us had had the heart or the sense to overrule him. He looked at my leg, then at my face, and I watched him do the same survey I'd just done, and I watched him arrive at the same figure and decide, the way his father would have decided, not to say it out loud.

"Mr. Wren," he said instead, "if the magistrate won't listen — if he's bought, like you think he might be — what happens to the book?"

"Then the book goes to someone who will," I said. "There's always someone who will. Corruption is cheap but it isn't free, and it doesn't buy everyone, because everyone costs too much. You just have to live long enough to find the one who isn't for sale."

"Then live long enough," the boy said, with his father's exact stubbornness, and went back out into the rain before I could answer, which was also his father's exact habit.

Oscar stood at the cab door a moment, watching the gray wall of the storm, and I saw her do something I hadn't seen her do before. She took a folded paper from inside her coat — a page, hand-drawn, dense with lines — and looked at it, and put it away. I knew what it was. Everyone in the Reach knew what it was, or knew the legend of it. The map. The map that was worth more than the woman who drew it.

"Dravenmoor," I said. "If it comes to it — if it comes down to the map or the book—"

"It won't," she said.

"But if—"

"It won't," she said again, and there was something in her voice I couldn't read, some figure she'd already run and wasn't sharing, and I was too tired to press. Surveyors know when a line of inquiry is closed. You mark it and move on.

* * *

We ran all that day through the teeth of the storm. I lay on the cab floor and listened to the engine work — a sound I know better than my own heartbeat, every knock and breath of her — and I listened to the line report on itself through the wheels. Rail joints like a drumbeat. Then, in the afternoon, a new sound underneath: a long, low groan from the trestle country, the sound of old timber taking on water it was never dressed for.

Oscar heard it too. She slowed the engine, which I had never once known her to do voluntarily, and stood in the doorway looking back along the line we'd come, and then forward along the line we were going, and I understood she was doing arithmetic again, and that the figures had gotten worse.

"Bridge at Miller's Run," she said, half to herself. "If the creek's up, it's under. If it's under, we go around by the coast road, and the coast road runs through—" She stopped.

"Through what?" the boy asked.

"The drowned cut," Oscar said. "It runs through the drowned cut."

Nobody said anything for a while. The rain filled the silence, and under the rain, far behind us, I could almost imagine I heard the other sound — the one we'd been running from since the cave. Salvage steam. Vane's fleet working the Reach, and his hunters working the land. They had lost us at the cut, but men like Vane do not lose things; they wait for things to be found.

I dictated two more pages that afternoon, because it was the only useful work my body could still do, and because I wanted the boy to have my voice in his ear while he wrote — the names, the figures, the wrench in the oilcloth, all of it — in case my voice stopped being available. He wrote fast and clean, and when he finished a page he read it back to me word for word, and I corrected him twice, and the second correction was my own error, which I admitted, because a surveyor who won't admit error is no better than an insurer.

Toward dusk the fever climbed again, and the world went soft at the edges, and the last thing I remember clearly is Oscar crouched over me with the lantern, feeding me water from a tin cup, her face drawn and gray and absolutely resolved.

"Storm's peaking at midnight," she said. "High water at the cut is dawn. We cross at first light, all three of us, or none of us."

"And if the line's washed out before Miller's Run?" I asked. "If we can't reach the cut at all?"

She was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her coat and took out the folded page — the map, or what remained of the legend of it — and held it over the little iron stove, and looked at me, and said:

"Then I'll need you to remember the way, Wren. Every figure. Every elevation. Because in about a minute, I'm going to stop being the only person alive who knows this coast."

And she let the corner of the page touch the coals.
