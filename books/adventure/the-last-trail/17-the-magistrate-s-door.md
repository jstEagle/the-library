## 17. The Magistrate's Door

The magistrate's outpost at Grayharbor was the kind of building that had been designed by a man who wanted you to feel small before you'd said a word. Grey stone, grey slate roof, a door of black oak with iron studs arranged in a pattern that probably meant something to somebody. A brass plaque beside the entrance read HONORABLE EDMUND CROWE, FEDERAL MAGISTRATE OF THE REACH DISTRICT, and beneath that, in smaller letters that had gone green with salt air: *By Appointment of the Crown Authority on Commerce and Salvage.*

I stood in front of that plaque for a long moment while Finch held Wren upright against my shoulder.

"Commerce and salvage," I said. "That's a comforting combination."

"You think that means something bad?" Finch asked.

"I think it means the man who owns three armed ships and half the shipping contracts on this coast has had lunch with whoever appointed him. I think it means nothing good comes out of a door with that word on it." I shifted Wren's weight. He was lighter than he should have been, and hotter than any living man ought to be. "But it's the door we have. So we knock."

Wren mumbled something into my collar. It might have been a prayer or a list of coordinates. With surveyors it was hard to tell the difference.

We'd made better time than I'd dared hope. The storm that Wren's fever had promised us arrived early, as storms do when they want to ruin someone's arithmetic, but it came in behind us instead of ahead, shoving us up the last stretch of coast road like a hand between the shoulder blades. We'd left the engine hidden in a quarry cut two miles back — she wasn't built for roads, and roads weren't built for her — and walked the rest through rain that came sideways and tasted of iron. Finch had carried Wren across the worst of it, a boy of maybe sixteen hauling a grown man twice his weight, and he hadn't complained once. He'd inherited his father's stubbornness along with his letters. The world takes things from us and hands us strange change back.

The outpost sat on a bluff above the harbor town, and from its steps you could see the whole of Grayharbor spread out below: fishing boats hauled up against the weather, the customs house with its flag snapping wet, and beyond the breakwater, the open grey water where, if you squinted through the rain, you could just make out the shapes of ships at anchor. Three of them. Riding high and empty, waiting for orders.

Vane's fleet had followed us. Of course they had. But we were hours ahead — I'd spent the whole walk doing the sums, and the sums said we had until mid-afternoon before Vane's lawyers could arrive by the coastal packet, and longer than that before Vane himself could bring his flagship around the point. Hours. In my experience, hours are either everything or nothing, and you don't find out which until afterward.

I knocked on the black oak door.

* * *

The clerk who answered was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the expression of someone who had been interrupted during something important, which, given the hour and the weather, was almost certainly his lunch.

"We need to see Magistrate Crowe," I said. "On urgent federal business. Witness testimony regarding the destruction of the vessel *Meridian Star* and the murder of a government survey team."

I'll give the clerk this much: he didn't laugh. He looked at me, then at Wren — who had chosen that exact moment to sway like a mast in crosswind — then at Finch, soaked to the skin and glaring with an intensity that would have been more effective if he'd been taller than the door handle.

"The magistrate is not receiving," the clerk said.

"He will receive this." I set Wren carefully against the doorframe, reached inside my coat, and produced the evidence ledger — oilskin-wrapped, salt-stained, and worth more per page than everything in the harbor below. "Surveyor Elias Wren, sole survivor of the Ashe expedition. His testimony names Aldous Vane in the deliberate scuttling of his own ship for insurance, and in the deaths of six government men sent to prove it. If your magistrate won't take it now, he can explain to the Crown Authority why he turned away a dying witness at the door."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes. Not conscience — I've learned to tell the difference, and conscience looks different, arrives faster. This was calculation. The slow arithmetic of a man weighing which master to disappoint.

"Wait here," he said, and shut the door in our faces.

Finch looked at me. "He's going to let us in?"

"He's going to go count something," I said. "Money, or seconds. Possibly both."

We waited. Rain ran off the eaves and pooled around our boots. Wren slid slowly down the doorframe until Finch caught him under the arms and propped him up again, and I watched the harbor and counted the shapes of ships and did not like what the counting told me. One of the anchored vessels had begun to move. Slowly. The way a thing moves when it wants to seem like it isn't moving.

The door opened. The clerk's expression had rearranged itself into something official.

"The magistrate will see you."

* * *

Edmund Crowe received us in a room designed to make visitors feel the weight of the law, which is to say there was a very large desk and a very small chair on the wrong side of it. Portraits of stern men lined the walls, all of them looking down with the particular disapproval of people who never had to make a difficult decision themselves. Crowe himself was round and pink and comfortable-looking, with small clever hands folded on the blotter and eyes that went over us the way a customs officer goes over cargo — quickly, thoroughly, and without interest in anything but value.

"Magistrate Crowe," I said. "Oscar Dravenmoor. This is Elias Wren, witness. And Finch—"

"Ashe," Finch said. "My father was Thomas Ashe. He led the survey team."

Something flickered in Crowe's face. It was quick, and it was gone, and I am sorry to say I recognized it, because I had seen the same flicker on the face of every man who has ever been paid to look the other way and then shown the thing he looked away from. It wasn't guilt. Guilt is heavier; it stays. This was just discomfort — the discomfort of a man discovering the price of his silence has come due in person.

"Dravenmoor," Crowe said. "The railroad woman. I've heard of you. They say you can run a train over track that hasn't existed for twenty years."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them exaggerate how much fun it is."

He smiled the way men smile when they've decided you're harmless, or purchasable, or both. "You've come a long way in bad weather, Miss Dravenmoor. What is it you want?"

"Justice," I said. "Or failing that, the law."

Crowe leaned back. His chair creaked with the contentment of well-fed furniture. "Then let's be precise, because the law is a precise instrument. You're claiming that Mr. Aldous Vane — a licensed salvager, a holder of Crown contracts, a man whose fleet employs four hundred souls in this district alone — deliberately destroyed his own vessel and murdered a government survey team. Is that correct?"

"That's what the ledger says. That's what Mr. Wren saw with his own eyes."

"And you, Mr. Wren. You can confirm this testimony?"

Wren lifted his head off Finch's shoulder. Fever had burned him down to wire and stubbornness, but when he spoke, his voice was clear as a struck bell. "I can confirm it. I marked the hull breaches myself. Cut inward, from the inside, below the waterline. I have the measurements. I have the manifest discrepancies. I have the names of the men who did the cutting, and the name of the man who paid them, and I watched that man's crew put six of my colleagues in shallow graves above the tideline." He drew a breath that cost him. "I am prepared to swear to all of it, Magistrate, before God and the Crown, and I would prefer to do it before I die of this fever, so if we could move along—"

"That will be quite enough," Crowe said.

It wasn't shouted. That was the worst part. It was quiet and tired and final, the voice of a man closing a ledger.

"Mr. Wren, your testimony is remarkable. Detailed. Convincing, even." He spread his small hands. "But testimony requires corroboration. Physical evidence. Chain of custody. And this ledger of yours — recovered how? From a wreck site? Found lying in the mud? Any competent lawyer will have it dismissed by luncheon. And the graves you describe — disturbed sites, no bodies produced, no photographs, no coroner's report. Do you know what I have instead?" He gestured at the window, at the harbor, at the three ships riding at anchor. "I have a salvage contract, properly filed. I have an insurance settlement, properly adjudicated. I have a missing survey team whose own government declared them lost to weather eight months ago. On paper, Miss Dravenmoor, Mr. Vane is the injured party here. Someone stole his ship right out from under him, apparently, and drowned it."

"The paper was bought," I said.

"The paper," Crowe said, "is the law."

For a moment nobody spoke. Rain ticked against the tall window. Somewhere below us, a foghorn moaned, long and low, like the coast itself clearing its throat before saying something unpleasant.

I had imagined this moment, on the long walk in. I had imagined banging the ledger down on the desk and watching justice happen — slow, bureaucratic, indifferent, but real. I had imagined clerks and affidavits and a trial somewhere in a clean courtroom far from the salt wind. I had even, God help me, imagined going home afterward. Instead I stood in a warm dry room watching a fat man in good wool explain to me, politely, that the door I'd carried a dying man to was painted shut from the inside.

"How much?" I asked.

Crowe's eyebrows rose. "I beg your pardon?"

"Vane's number. For this room. For you. I'm curious what a federal magistrate costs these days. Is it a yearly retainer, or does he pay by the case? Because I want to know whether I should feel insulted or flattered that he spent real money on you."

"Miss Dravenmoor—" Crowe's voice acquired an edge. "I will not sit here and be accused—"

"No," I said. "You'll sit there and be *known*. There's a difference. Accusing takes proof, and we've established you don't deal in proof." I picked up the ledger from his desk — he'd had the clerk lay it out, and I noticed his fingers hadn't actually touched it, the way a man doesn't touch a hot stove — and wrapped it back in its oilskin. "Here's what happens now, Magistrate. You're going to refuse this testimony. Formally. Loudly. You're going to have your clerk write it down, date it, and seal it, because in about two hours Aldous Vane's lawyers are going to come through that door and ask you what happened, and I want your refusal on record in your own hand, not theirs. Because when this breaks — and it will break, Crowe, it's already breaking, it broke the moment Vane started chasing witnesses instead of wrecks — the man who refused a dying federal witness at his own door is going to hang a great deal lower than the man who took the money."

The color had gone out of Crowe's pinkness. His small hands were no longer folded; they were gripping the edge of the blotter.

"You're threatening a magistrate," he said. "That's a crime."

"So is murder," I said. "So is arson, fraud, and conspiracy. The Reach is full of crimes today. I'm just choosing which ones to commit." I turned to Finch. "Ask the clerk for paper. We're doing this properly. If the law won't take the truth, the truth can at least get a receipt."

* * *

They wrote the refusal. Crowe dictated it in a voice gone thin and careful, and the clerk wrote it in his neat ink-stained hand, and I stood over them both like a foreman over a bad pour of concrete, making sure every word set. *The undersigned declines to receive testimony of the nature described, absent corroborating evidence satisfactory to this office.* Satisfactory to this office. As if the office had a palate, and the truth tasted wrong.

Wren signed nothing — he was past signing, past speaking, past anything but breathing in the chair by the window with Finch's coat around his shoulders. But I signed as witness, and Finch signed, and the clerk stamped it with the district seal because I asked him to and because, underneath the ink stains and the calculation, there was still a small dry core of a man who kept records, and records were the one thing in that building that hadn't been bought outright.

At the door, Crowe stopped me. Up close, without the desk between us, he looked older and smaller and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Dravenmoor," he said quietly. "You don't understand what you're carrying. That ledger isn't evidence. It's a death warrant — yours, the boy's, his. Vane won't send lawyers next time. He'll send the *Corvid*, and the *Corvid* doesn't carry lawyers." He glanced back toward the room where Wren sat. "Give it up. Take whatever he offers. Men have lived long comfortable lives on less."

"I know," I said. "That's what makes it tempting. Good day, Magistrate."

Outside, the storm had eased to a steady grey drizzle, and the light had changed the way light changes when the afternoon starts spending itself. I checked the harbor out of habit and stopped checking fast. Two of the three ships were moving now, standing in toward the harbor mouth with the unhurried confidence of things that own the water they float on. And at the customs house dock, a packet boat was tying up, and men in good coats were coming ashore under umbrellas, and even at this distance I could tell lawyers by the way they walked — like the ground owed them money.

"Hours ahead," Finch said beside me. "Like you said."

"Lawyers, yes." I settled the ledger inside my coat, against my ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat. "But look past them. The big one, standing in past the breakwater. That's the flagship. That's Vane himself, and he's not waiting for his lawyers' report. He's coming to see the ending personally."

Wren stirred against Finch's shoulder and managed, through cracked lips, three words: "The emergency channel."

I looked at him. He looked back at me with fever-bright eyes, and I saw it land in my head the way a rail lands in a spike hole — sudden, permanent, exactly fitting. Every ship in Vane's fleet, every port authority on the coast, every wireless shack from here to the capital, all of them tuned to one frequency, monitored around the clock, because that's what emergency channels are *for*.

"Crowe won't listen," I said slowly. "His masters won't listen. But the whole coast is listening to that channel every minute of every day."

Finch grinned through the rain. "So we don't knock on any more doors."

"No," I said, turning for the quarry cut and the engine and whatever came after. "This time, everybody hears us."
