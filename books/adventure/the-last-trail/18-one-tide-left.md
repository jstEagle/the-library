## 18. One Tide Left

The magistrate's door closed on us with the soft, expensive click of mahogany — and that sound did more damage than any bullet Vane had ever fired at me.

I stood on the veranda of the outpost at Grayharbor Point and watched the rain come in off the Reach like it had a grudge. Finch stood beside me, jaw set the way his father's must have been when he wrote *something wrong with the manifest*. Wren leaned against the doorframe behind us, fever-bright, one hand pressed to the bandage at his ribs like he was holding himself together by main strength.

"Well," I said. "That's that."

"That's not that," Finch said.

I looked at him. Seventeen years old, soaked to the bone, running on nothing but grief and stubbornness, and he was right. That's the trouble with boys like Finch — they haven't learned yet which walls are load-bearing, so they go on pushing all of them.

"Magistrate Corliss is bought," I said. "Bought men don't unbuy themselves because a boy glares at them."

"Then we don't need him."

I'll give the boy this: he'd learned arithmetic from watching me. Six tides ago I'd taught him that survival is subtraction — what you can afford to lose, what you can't. He was doing sums now.

"Every ship in Vane's fleet carries an emergency channel," he said. "Wren told us. Fleet-wide, hardwired, can't be jammed from the bridge without cutting power to the whole ship. Regulation. It goes to every port authority on the coast. It's for distress calls."

"And?"

"And Vane's whole operation runs on that regulation," Finch said. "His insurance, his salvage licenses, his contracts — they all depend on him being the man who answers distress calls. The man whose fleet is *equipped*. So what happens if his own emergency channel starts broadcasting a murder confession?"

I felt something turn over in my chest. Not hope exactly. Hope is warm. This was colder and better — it was the feeling of a switch throwing, a signal arm dropping, a route clearing where there had been none.

"Finch," I said, "remind me never to bet against you again."

* * *

Here's the problem with stealing a broadcast: you need a transmitter, you need power, and you need time. We had one of the three, barely, and it wasn't the one you'd want.

The magistrate's outpost had a coastal relay — a squat iron tower behind the station house, built for weather warnings and official traffic, humming to itself in the rain. Official traffic. Which meant Corliss's people would notice anyone touching it inside of minutes. But here's what twenty years of running closed lines teaches you: every system built by men who trust each other has a back door left open by men who got lazy. The relay shed's maintenance hatch faced away from the station house, toward the cliff, and its lock was older than Finch.

The harder problem was Wren. He could barely stand, and testimony whispered through cracked lips into a hand microphone isn't testimony — it's a rumor. Rumors drown. I needed him loud, clear, and undeniable, and I needed him that way before Vane's lawyers finished their tea and started asking Corliss harder questions about the ragged trio on his veranda.

So we did the thing I never do. We stopped moving.

We holed up in the old coastguard hut two hundred yards down the cliff path — abandoned, half-rotten, smelling of gull lime and rust — and I made Wren lie flat while Finch raided the relay shed's stores for anything with a charge in it. Batteries, mostly, and a hand-crank dynamo off a decommissioned signal lamp. Finch came back with his arms full and his eyes shining, and I realized I was looking at the first person in years who thought my life was an adventure instead of a sentence.

"Don't get sentimental," I told him. "Hand me the dynamo."

* * *

Vane found us, of course. I'd have been insulted if he hadn't.

It took him until well past dark, which told me Corliss had stalled as long as his conscience — such as it was — allowed. By then we'd spliced into the relay's auxiliary line, tested the carrier wave twice on frequencies no one monitors, and rehearsed. Wren had his testimony written out in surveyor's script, neat as a plat map, because a dying man's handwriting is the most honest document in the world. The evidence ledger — the manifest pages, the scuttling charges receipt, the logbook leaf naming Vane's own ship — lay wrapped in oilcloth beside him.

What we didn't have was a way to keep Vane's boarders off us long enough to finish the broadcast. A full reading, ledger entries included, ran forty minutes. Forty minutes is a geological age when armed men are climbing your cliff path.

They came at the third hour of the morning tide. I heard them before I saw them — boots on wet shale, the particular discipline of men who've done this before. Six of them, moving in pairs, lanterns shuttered. Below them in the black water, the running lights of Vane's lead ship sat offshore like a patient animal.

I put Finch in the relay shed with instructions I hated giving: if I fall, you finish the reading. Every word. Then you run the coast road east until your legs give out.

"I'm not leaving you," he said.

"You're not leaving *him*," I said, nodding at Wren. "There's a difference, and it matters more than either of us does right now. Go."

He went. Good boy.

I went out to meet the boarders with a signal lamp, a coil of wire, and the only weapon I've ever trusted: knowledge of ground nobody else has bothered to learn. The cliff path at Grayharbor Point crosses an old storm culvert — a stone throat, dry nine months of the year, running under the path itself. On a night like this one it carried a foot of rainwater and a current strong enough to break a man's ankle if he stepped wrong. There are exactly two places to cross it clean. I knew both. They didn't know one.

I won't pretend it was elegant. I killed their lanterns with a thrown wrench, let the dark do the first round of work, and listened to two of them find the culvert the hard way — the crack and splash and swearing of men learning geography at speed. The other four were smarter; they fanned out and came for the relay shed, which meant they came for Finch, which meant the plan was now running on its final margin.

Which is when I stopped being clever and started being audacious, because the margin was gone.

I climbed the relay tower.

* * *

Ninety feet of iron ladder in horizontal rain, with four armed men below me and a feverish surveyor below them, and my hands remembering every rung the way they remember throttle notches. At the top there was the auxiliary junction box, and inside it the fleet-standard coupling Vane's own engineers had installed when the company bought its coastal relay rights — because Aldous Vane never met a piece of infrastructure he didn't want to own, and ownership leaves threads, and threads can be pulled.

I coupled our line into the emergency band. I keyed the tower's master transmit. And then, ninety feet above the sea, in the worst weather the Reach had thrown at us all season, I spoke into a dead man's microphone.

"This is a priority distress transmission on the Sable Reach emergency channel. This is not a drill. This is testimony regarding the deliberate scuttling of the vessel *Meridian Star*, owned by Aldous Vane, and the murder of six government surveyors who documented it. All port authorities, all licensing boards, all insurers listening: what follows is read from original documents. Verify them or be complicit."

Below me, lanterns swung toward the tower. Someone shouted. Someone else fired, and the shot went wide and died in the rain — shooting at a relay tower in a storm is like shooting at the idea of distance.

In the shed below, Finch began to read.

He read Wren's testimony in a clear boy's voice that didn't shake once, and I have never been prouder of another human being and I told him so later, once, awkwardly, and he pretended not to care and cared enormously. Wren himself took the second stretch, fever and all, because a voice that sounds like it's dying makes men listen differently than a voice that sounds rehearsed. Between them, entry by entry, the ledger went out over the airwaves: the charges receipt dated eleven days before the *Meridian Star* went down. The insurance rider signed by Vane's own hand. The survey logbook page. The manifest discrepancy Wren's captain had flagged in his last letter home — *something wrong with the manifest* — now spoken aloud to every harbor office from here to the capital.

And then the ports started answering.

First one, then three, then a dozen: acknowledgment signals stacking on the emergency band, port authorities demanding confirmation, insurers waking their clerks, a federal marine board at Kestrel Narrows breaking protocol to transmit directly — *hold your position, maintain broadcast, assistance dispatched*. Vane's own machinery, built to make him untouchable, was now the thing pinning him to the wall. Every acknowledgment was another witness. Every witness was another nail.

The boarders stopped climbing. I watched from the tower as their lanterns converged, argued, and broke apart — because men paid to bury a story will not keep digging when the story starts shouting from every rooftop on the coast. One of them looked straight up at me. I looked straight back. Neither of us fired. There are moments when even hired guns understand that the money just changed sides.

By the time I climbed down, my legs shaking, the eastern sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the emergency band was a chorus. Wren lay in the shed doorway with his eyes closed, alive, spent, smiling faintly like a man who'd finally put down something heavy. Finch stood in the rain holding the oilcloth ledger against his chest, guarding it still, though it existed now in fifty places at once.

"It worked," he said. "It actually worked."

"Don't celebrate yet," I said. But I was smiling too, and he saw it, and that was worse for both of us than the rain.

Because I'd counted the acknowledgments, and I'd counted the hours, and I knew what Vane was doing while we broadcast. He wasn't listening. He was moving. His lead ship had turned from the anchorage, and she was making for the only place on this coast where a cornered man can still choose his own ending — the drowned cut, the flooded rail crossing, the last choke point between the Reach and the open sea.

My cut. My rails. My trap, whether I'd meant to build one or not.

One tide left. And Aldous Vane was sailing straight into it.
