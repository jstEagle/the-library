## 20. Testimony

The magistrates came on the morning tide, three of them, riding a gray government steamer that looked like it had been built by people who hated both boats and governments equally. I watched them come down the gangway in their long coats and their brass buttons, and I thought: so this is what the law looks like when it finally decides to show up. Late. Damp. And looking around like it hoped nobody would notice how long it had been gone.

Finch, that's me, by the way. In case you were wondering who's telling this part. Oscar says I've earned the right to narrate at least one chapter of whatever book eventually gets written about all this, and she only said it half-sarcastic, which from her is practically a medal.

I'd been awake since before dawn anyway. Sleep doesn't come easy when your body still remembers the sound of the closing water, that deep groan the drowned cut makes when the tide comes home like a landlord collecting rent. So I was sitting on the engine's footplate with a tin cup of something Oscar called coffee but which I suspected was mostly rust and regret, watching the steamer nose into the harbor, when the first thing hit me.

Not the magistrates. The smell.

Vane's flagship — or what was left of her — lay heeled over against the cut wall where the closing tide had pinned her, half her superstructure underwater, her name-plate tilted at an angle that seemed almost apologetic. And drifting off her on the morning wind came the stink of bilge and coal-smoke and something worse, something organic, the smell of a ship that had died with her secrets still aboard.

They pulled Aldous Vane out of the cut at six-forty in the morning.

I know because I made Oscar tell me the exact time twice, and she gave me the same answer both times, which for Oscar is a kind of tenderness. Six-forty. A deckhand from one of the smaller salvage ships went down on a line and came back up with the great Aldous Vane clamped under his arm like a drowned cat, coughing seawater and dignity in roughly equal measure. They laid him out on the quay and a woman knelt over him doing whatever it is people do to bring back the nearly dead, and I stood there watching his chest heave and thinking about my father, who had not gotten a deckhand and a line and a second chance.

My father got a shallow grave in the clay and a marker scratched with somebody else's survival code.

I want to be honest here, because Oscar says honesty is the only currency that doesn't devalue: for about four seconds, standing on that quay, I hoped they wouldn't bring him back. Four seconds. Then the fifth second came, and I was ashamed of the first four, and I've decided that's just what being a person is. You get the four seconds. What matters is the fifth.

* * *

The independent magistrates set up in the harbor office, which turned out to have a room big enough for proceedings and small enough that every word carried. Word had traveled ahead of them — Oscar's broadcast had seen to that, Wren's testimony and the evidence ledger read out flat and clear over Vane's own emergency channel until every port authority from the Reach to the capital had heard a dying surveyor name Aldous Vane as a murderer in a voice like gravel sliding downhill.

So there was a crowd. Fishermen, dockhands, salvage crews who'd worked honest contracts under Vane's flag and wanted to know if their wages were blood money. Cora Ashe sat in the front row in a black dress gone gray at the seams from travel, her hands folded in her lap like she was waiting for a train that might never come. She'd waited eleven months already. What was one more morning.

Wren testified first.

They brought him in on a wheeled chair, because he couldn't walk anymore — the fever and the wound and the sea cave had taken that from him — and I watched him take stock of the room the way a surveyor takes stock of terrain. Measuring. Marking. Deciding where the ground would hold. His voice was wrecked; he could manage maybe twenty words before the coughing took him, so they'd arranged it so a clerk read from his dictated testimony while Wren himself supplied the confirmations, nodding or lifting one finger, yes, yes, that is what happened.

And here is the thing I will remember until I die: he never once looked at Vane.

Vane sat at the defendant's table between two lawyers who smelled of expensive soap and quiet desperation, and he looked — I'll give him this — composed. Hair combed. Collar clean. A man who had been pulled from the sea six hours ago and had already reassembled himself into someone who belonged in a room with brass buttons. He watched Wren the way a man watches weather coming in off the horizon: seriously, but without any belief that it would actually reach him.

But Wren never looked at him. Wren looked at us. At Oscar, arms crossed by the window. At me, perched on a crate near the front. At Cora Ashe. He told the story of the survey team — my father among them — finding the manifest discrepancies, the patched hull plates, the insurance filings that didn't match the wreckage, and he told it to the people who had come looking, not to the man who had sent killers after them. Because testimony isn't for the guilty, I think. The guilty already know what they did. Testimony is for everyone else. It's a map drawn after the fact, so no one else walks into the same water.

The clerk read the ledger entries. Nine names. My father's was fourth. When the clerk said it — *Josiah Finch, chainman* — I felt Oscar's hand land on my shoulder, heavy and brief, and then lift away again, because Oscar's comfort comes in single doses, measured out like medicine.

* * *

Vane's lawyers fought, of course. That's what lawyers are for; even the bought ones earn their keep in the losing. They challenged the broadcast as hearsay. They challenged Wren's condition — a man that feverish, how reliable could his memory be? They produced a witness, some fleet functionary with a face like a closed ledger, who swore Vane had been ashore the night the survey team died.

It was going the way these things always go, slow and silted, when the magistrate — the middle one, a dry stick of a woman named Halloway who looked like she'd personally buried more corruption than most cemeteries — asked the question that broke it open.

"Mr. Vane. Your ship was found scuttled with patched plates below the waterline. Who authorized those repairs?"

Silence. The good kind, the kind that has weight.

"Repairs are routine," Vane said. "Maintenance."

"Routine maintenance," Magistrate Halloway repeated, "performed at night, undocumented, on a hull section that failed to match your insurance survey. And your chief engineer — Mr. Corrick, is it? — filed a deposition with our office two days ago. Would you like to hear what he says about who ordered those repairs?"

I didn't know then that Corrick had flipped. Nobody did, except maybe Oscar, who later admitted she'd guessed — *a man who builds his empire on other men's silence keeps a list of the silent, and lists can be seized.* But watching Vane's face when Halloway said the engineer's name, I saw the moment the weather reached him. The composure stayed on like paint, but underneath it something shifted, some load-bearing certainty giving way, and for one unguarded second Aldous Vane looked exactly like what he was: a man alone in a room full of witnesses.

He denied everything, naturally. Loudly, then coldly, then with a lawyer's hand on his sleeve. But denial is just testimony in reverse, and everyone in that room could read it. The fishermen nodded to each other. The dockhands stopped pretending they weren't listening. Even his own crew — the ones who'd refused to follow him into the closing water — stood along the back wall with their hats in their hands, saying nothing, which from sailors is a whole sermon.

The magistrates bound him over for trial in the capital. Full inquiry. Seized ledgers, seized manifests, Corrick's deposition, Wren's testimony, and — entered last, almost as an afterthought, though it wasn't one — the sworn statement of one Oscar Dravenmoor regarding the condition of the wreck site and the graves in the clay.

Oscar gave her statement in nine sentences. I counted. She could have talked for an hour and buried them in detail; instead she handed them nine sentences sharp enough to shave with, and sat down, and looked bored, which is how she looks when she cares the most.

* * *

Cora Ashe got her answer that evening.

Not from the magistrates — they were done with us, packed back onto their ugly steamer with their seized documents. From Wren. Oscar wheeled him down to the harbor wall where the light was going gold and left the two of them alone, and I hung back far enough to see but close enough to hear, because I am seventeen and grief makes eavesdroppers of us all.

"He didn't suffer," Wren said. "At the end. There wasn't time for suffering. There was only time for—" and here his ruined voice caught, "—time for him to say your name. We were running. He was ahead of me. He turned around to make sure I was following, and he said your name, like he was reminding himself why he'd come home. Then the shots came from the ridge."

Cora Ashe didn't cry. Or if she did, she did it the way Oscar does things — privately, precisely, in doses. She put her hand on the arm of Wren's chair and held it there a long moment, and she said, "Eleven months I've been writing letters to a government that had already decided my husband was a rounding error." Her voice didn't shake. "You kept him alive in a cave for nothing but the truth. I won't forget that."

"The truth," Wren said, "was all any of us had left of them."

Then she turned and saw me standing there pretending to inspect a bollard, and instead of being angry she beckoned me over, and she looked at me the way you look at a boy who has your husband's stubbornness and none of his years, and she said:

"He wrote to me about you. Josiah did. 'There's a boy on the rail lines,' he said, 'who reads tide tables for pleasure.' He thought you sounded like trouble." A pause. "He would have been proud of the trouble."

I said thank you, which was inadequate, and she said, "Yes, it was," which was somehow exactly right, and then she walked away down the quay toward the packet boat that would carry her home to a house with one too many chairs, carrying an answer eleven months late.

Bittersweet. That's the word Oscar used later, when I asked her what you call a victory that costs this much. Bittersweet. Though knowing Oscar, what she actually meant was: sweet enough to survive, bitter enough to remember.

* * *

That night we sat on the footplate, the three of us — Oscar, Wren propped on blankets in the cab, me — with a fire in the firebox and the harbor lights strung out across the black water. Somewhere behind us, Vane sat in a cell under guard, wet and stripped of his fleet, waiting for a trial in the capital. Somewhere ahead of us, whatever came next.

"They'll want to give you a medal," Wren said to Oscar. "Or a job. Governments always try to buy the people who embarrass them. It's cheaper than changing."

"I don't wear medals," Oscar said. "They snag on machinery."

"And the job?"

Oscar was quiet a while. The fire ticked. Out on the water, a buoy bell rang once, patient as arithmetic.

"I'm a mapmaker," she said finally. "Not a monument. Somebody has to keep drawing the lines nobody else will run." She glanced at me, quick, before I could pretend I hadn't been watching. "And apparently somebody has to teach the next fool to read tide tables for pleasure."

I grinned. Wren laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into Oscar thumping his back with exactly as much gentleness as she gives anything.

But late — much later, when Wren was asleep and Oscar was checking the engine's rods by lamplight the way she does every night, touching each bolt like an old friend — I climbed up beside her and asked the question that had been sitting in my chest since the quay.

"Do you think it was worth it? The map. Burning it. All of it."

She didn't stop working. She never stops working; stopping, she says, is how you die. But her hands slowed, just slightly, on the third rod.

"You're asking if I regret it."

"I'm asking what's left."

She straightened up and looked out at the dark water, and for a moment she looked less like the legend they write telegrams about and more like what she is — a tired woman who traded everything she owned for nine strangers' names and a boy she hadn't planned on.

"What's left," she said, "is what I can draw from memory. Turns out that's most of it. Turns out the map was never the paper." She snapped the lamp shutter closed. "Bed, Finch. Tide turns at four."

I went. But as I dropped down into the cab, she added one more thing, quiet, almost to herself:

"And what's left is that Vane faces trial, and Cora Ashe sleeps tonight, and you're still here. Some lines you burn. Some lines you keep."

I fell asleep under the engine, on the warm iron, with the buoy bell ringing out its patient count, and dreamed — for the first time since the cut — of open track.

In the morning there was a telegram waiting at the harbor office. Of course there was. There is always a telegram.
