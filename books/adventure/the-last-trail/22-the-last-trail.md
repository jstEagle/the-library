## 22. The Last Trail

The telegram found me in a place called Mercy Flats, which is the kind of name that tells you everything and nothing. Everything: there had once been a reason to call it that. Nothing: nobody here had received any in living memory.

It came by way of a mule driver who'd carried it forty miles because the telegraph line ended at a junction the railroad had forgotten before I was born. He charged me double for the trouble and triple for the story of how he got it, and I paid both without complaint, because a man who carries paper through bad country deserves his price, and because I have learned that stories are often worth more than the documents they arrive with.

I waited until dark to open it.

This is not superstition. Well — it is partly superstition. But it is also habit, and habit is just superstition that has survived long enough to call itself wisdom. Bad news reads better by firelight. Good news does too, if you're the sort of person who trusts good news about as far as she can throw an engine, which I am, and which I can't.

Finch was asleep under the engine when I struck the match.

He sleeps there more nights than not now, curled up on the work blanket in the shadow of the driving wheels, close enough to touch the steel. I told him once it was a strange place to sleep — cold iron, cinders, the smell of hot oil soaking into your dreams — and he looked at me like I'd asked him why he bothered breathing. The engine had carried him through the Reach. It had carried him past Vane's guns and across the drowned cut and out the other side of the worst six tides either of us ever hopes to see again. As far as Finch was concerned, the thing had earned its keep as furniture.

So he slept under her, one arm flung over a coupling rod, his boots still on, his father's survey compass tucked against his ribs where it wouldn't fall. Six months since the closing water. Six months since Vane chose the sea over the stand, and his crew stood on the bank and watched him go under and did nothing at all — which was, in its way, the most damning testimony anyone gave that whole season.

Six months, and the boy still slept with his boots on.

I understood that better than I let on. There's a particular kind of restlessness that gets into you after a thing like the Reach. You lie down at night and the quiet is so complete it feels like a held breath, like the world is waiting for you to stop moving so it can finally catch up with what you owe it. Boots stay on. Maps stay within reach. You learn to sleep the way a sentry sleeps, in slices, ready to be up and running before your eyes finish opening.

They tell me it fades. They being people who have clearly never run a night train over a closed mountain line with a toll gang shooting at their headlamp.

I sat down by the fire with the telegram and turned it over twice before opening it, which is another habit, though I couldn't tell you what it defends against. Then I read it. Then I read it again, slower, the way you reread a sentence that has just rearranged the furniture inside your skull.

*TO O. DRAVENMOOR. SURVEY COMMISSION. NORTHERN DIVISION LINE DECLARED LOST 1891. TERRAIN UNCHARTED SINCE. GOVERNMENT OFFERS FULL AUTHORITY, EQUIPMENT, AND PAY TO MAP AND CERTIFY ROUTE. REPLY BY WIRE, MERCY FLATS STATION, WITHIN TEN DAYS.*

Full authority. Equipment. Pay.

I want to be clear about what this offer actually was, because the words are respectable and the thing underneath them was not respectable at all. The Northern Division Line was lost for a reason. It went into country that eats railroads — granite shoulders, river gorges that change their minds seasonally, passes that snow shut in October and stay shut until the birds come back. Two crews had tried to certify it before me. One quit. The other didn't quit so much as stop existing, which is a different way of saying the same thing.

And now the government wanted it mapped, certified, and opened, and they wanted *me* to do it, and they were offering authority and equipment and pay, which is the trinity every honest engineer dreams about at three in the morning, and which is also — I have learned this the expensive way — exactly how they get you.

Because here is what the telegram did not say. It did not say why now. It did not say who wanted the line open, or what freight they intended to push through it, or whose land lay along the right-of-way, or whose water would get muddied, literally or otherwise. It did not say whether the magistrate who'd refused Elias Wren's testimony had any cousins working in the survey commission. It did not say a single word about Aldous Vane, whose trial was still crawling through the courts like something wounded and mean, and whose salvage fleet — what remained of it — had been bought up piecemeal by men whose names I did not yet know but fully expected to.

Ten days to reply. That was generous, by the standards of people who send telegrams. Which meant they expected me to say yes fast, or no fast, and either answer served them. A fast yes meant I was theirs. A fast no meant they'd find someone cheaper and worse, and then whatever happened on that line would happen without me anywhere near it to see.

I fed the fire and thought about it for a long time.

* * *

Here is the inventory of my life, six months after I burned my master map:

One engine, older than sin and twice as stubborn, running well enough that I'd stopped apologizing for her. One apprentice, fifteen going on sixteen, who could now strip a valve assembly in the dark and had opinions about boiler pressure that I pretended to find annoying. One copy of a map — redrawn from memory, minus the lines I'd rather keep closed, which is to say maybe sixty percent of what I used to carry. A tin box of survey instruments that had belonged to Cora Ashe's husband, which she'd pressed on me at the trial with a look that permitted no argument. And a reputation, rebuilt from ash, that had somehow gotten larger instead of smaller, because that is what happens when you burn a legend in front of witnesses: they don't remember the burning. They remember the fire.

There had been offers. There are always offers. The government post came first, formal and flattering, with a desk and a salary and a title, and I declined it in a letter so polite that the clerk who read it probably framed it. Then the private offers started, and those were less polite, and some of them I declined in person, and one of them I declined by leaving town at two in the morning with the engine already hot, which is the most eloquent refusal I know.

And underneath all of it, quieter than all of it, was the question I hadn't let myself ask out loud: *what now?*

Because that was the true cost of the burned map, the part nobody warned me about. Not the money. Not the safety. Those I could rebuild; I'd been broke and hunted before, and I'm good at both. The cost was the shape of my days. For eleven years I had known exactly who I was. I was the woman who knew the closed lines. I was the map. Take that away and what remained was a person with an engine, a boy, and a great deal of skill at moving fast through country that wants her dead — which is a fine set of tools and no job at all.

I had been drifting since the trial. Honest work, small runs, hauling what needed hauling between towns too poor to attract trouble. Good months, even. But drifting. Sleeping in slices. Keeping the boots on.

And now this telegram, sitting in my lap like a lit fuse, offering me the one thing I'd sworn off: a trail nobody else would run.

* * *

"You're going to take it."

I nearly put the fire poker through the dark. Finch was sitting up under the engine, hair full of cinders, eyes wide awake, which meant he'd been awake the whole time, which meant the boy had learned to fake sleep the way I'd learned to fake patience, and I decided not to be annoyed about it because imitation is the sincerest form of apprenticeship.

"How long have you been listening?"

"Since the mule man." He crawled out, brushed off the blanket, folded it with the care he always showed toward anything that belonged to the engine. "You read it four times. You only read things four times when you've already decided and you're looking for reasons to change your mind."

"I do not."

"The manifest page," he said. "In Wren's logbook. You read it four times on the ridge. And the tide tables. Four times each." He held up fingers. "It's a pattern."

I looked at him — this kid who eighteen months ago was a stowaway stealing rides toward a dead father, and who now read me like a gauge — and felt the specific mixture of pride and dread that I suppose is what parents feel, if parents are anything like what I've accidentally become.

"And what do you think I've decided?" I said.

"That you're taking it." He sat down across the fire from me, cross-legged, hands around his knees. "But you're scared of it."

"Scared."

"Not of the line." He shrugged. "Of what it means. If you map it, you're back. Really back. The map lady. And last time—" He stopped himself, which was considerate, and then didn't stop himself, which was honest. "Last time cost you everything."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a coyote made its opinion known about the general state of the world.

"It cost me a piece of paper," I said.

"You know that's not—"

"Finch." I leaned forward. "Let me tell you what I figured out while you were pretending to snore. That map — the master map — it wasn't my livelihood. Livelihoods can be earned again; I've earned three since the Reach. It wasn't even my identity, whatever the newspapers liked to print. It was my *excuse*. Every year, the map gave me a reason to keep moving. Never stop, never settle, never let anybody close enough to lose. Eleven years of perfect alibis, bound in oilcloth." I tapped the telegram. "And now some clerk in the capital has handed me a fresh excuse, official seal and all, and the frightening part isn't the granite or the snow. The frightening part is how relieved I am."

Finch was quiet for a moment. Then, carefully: "Is that bad?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was the most honest sentence I'd produced in months. "Ask me in another six hundred miles."

* * *

We spent the next day doing what we always did before a decision: work. There is no question so large that it can't be postponed productively by greasing axles. I pulled the Northern Division surveys I'd collected over the years — fragments, rumors, a dead crew chief's marginalia I'd won in a card game from a man who didn't know what he had — and spread them across the engine's boiler while Finch ran the numbers on coal and water stops.

The picture that emerged was ugly in the way I love best. Ninety miles of uncharted grade. Three river crossings that the old crews had argued about in pencil. A pass that the dead chief had marked, in handwriting gone shaky near the end, with a single word: *maybe.*

"She'll do it," Finch said, patting the running board like a horse's neck. "She's done worse."

"She hasn't done worse. We have done worse. She's been a willing accomplice, which is different, and don't let her hear you volunteering her."

But my hand was flat on the boiler too, and we both knew what that meant.

At dusk I walked to the Mercy Flats station — a shed, a wire, a clerk who doubled as the postmaster and possibly the mayor — and dictated my reply. I kept it short. Short replies leave less surface area for lawyers.

*DRAVENMOOR ACCEPTS. CONDITIONS FOLLOW. ONE: MY ROUTE, MY JUDGMENT, NO COMMITTEE OVERRIDES. TWO: ALL FINDINGS PUBLISHED IN FULL, INCLUDING WHAT THE LINE CROSSES AND WHO IT DISTURBS. THREE: ONE APPRENTICE ON COMMISSION ROLLS, PAID. FOUR: PAYMENT HALF IN ADVANCE, IN GOLD, BECAUSE I HAVE MET YOUR MAGISTRATES.*

The clerk tapped it out without expression, which I took as professional respect.

Walking back to the fire, I passed the notice board outside the station, and stopped. Pinned there among the livestock listings and a reward poster for a horse thief with excellent taste was a newspaper clipping, weeks old, someone's scissors work. The headline was about the Vane trial — the verdict was still pending, the article said, but the fleet's remaining ships had been seized as collateral, and *investigators continue to trace the ownership chains behind the salvage consortium*, and beneath that, in smaller type, a name I didn't recognize attached to a company I'd never heard of, buying up debt and hulls and harbor rights all across the northern coast.

I read it twice. Not four times. Twice was enough to know the shape of it.

Vane had chosen the water, but water leaves wreckage, and wreckage leaves claims, and claims leave men like Vane — patient ones, this time, ones who'd studied how the first man fell and meant to correct his errors. The Northern Division Line ran straight through the best deepwater harbor on that coast. A harbor currently owned by nobody, because nobody could prove access to it.

And the government suddenly wanted a railroad there. Within ten days of asking. Fast.

I tore the clipping off the board and folded it into my coat, because I have learned that stories are worth more than the documents they arrive with, and clippings are somewhere in between.

* * *

Finch was waiting with coffee that tasted like it had been brewed in the engine's ash pan, which, knowing him, it had. I sat down, drank it anyway, and laid the telegram on my knee.

"We leave day after tomorrow," I said. "North, by the coast spur, then cut inland where the old chief's notes say there's a shelf road. Ninety miles of nothing, then a pass marked *maybe.*"

"Coal?"

"Half a bunkered load and a purchase in Millbrook that will require diplomacy or theft. We'll decide when we meet the seller."

"Water stops?"

"Two certain, one probable, one theoretical. Pack extra canvas."

He nodded along, pulling a stub of pencil from his pocket, writing it all down on the back of an old timetable, and watching him do it — quick, sure, already thinking two steps ahead the way I used to think two steps ahead before the Reach taught me to think five — I felt the drift go out of me. Just like that. Like a knot coming loose.

Some people are built for houses. Some for harbors. Some for desks in warm rooms where the worst weather is a difficult letter. I had spent eleven years telling myself I was built for the closed lines, the lost trails, the country that eats railroads — and maybe that was true, but standing at the edge of this new route, I finally understood it wasn't the whole truth. What I was built for was this exact moment: the fire, the boy, the impossible offer, the blank space on the map with my name quietly written across it in pencil.

The road goes on. That's not a philosophy. It's just what roads do, if you're paying attention.

"Oscar?" Finch said, around midnight, when the fire had gone down to a red heart and the stars over Mercy Flats were thick enough to steer by. He was settling back under the engine, boots still on. "The pass marked *maybe*. You think it's real?"

I looked north, where the dark was deepest, where ninety miles of uncharted country lay waiting with its granite teeth and its shifting rivers and whatever patient new Vane was already circling it.

"No," I said. "But it's real enough to try."

He grinned in the firelight, and above us the coyote sang again, and somewhere out past the hills a train whistle blew — long, lonesome, going somewhere — and I lay back with my boots on and started planning the route.
