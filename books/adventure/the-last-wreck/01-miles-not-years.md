## 1. Miles, Not Years

The storm sat over the Serrado like a fat cat that had no intention of moving, and I flew around it, because I am a professional and professionals do not argue with weather. We argue with weather constantly. We just lose politely.

"Passenger's stable," Juno called from the back, over the drone of the engines. "Sleeping. Or dead. Fifty-fifty."

"He's breathing."

"Then he's sleeping. Lucky man." Juno leaned forward between the seats, and I caught the smell of engine grease and the lemon candy she sucked to keep her ears popping. "You want to tell me why we're routing east? Direct line puts us forty minutes ahead."

"Direct line puts us under that cloud."

"That cloud is twenty miles wide, Ada. We've flown through worse."

"We've flown through worse," I agreed, and banked east anyway.

Juno sat back. She didn't push it, because Juno understood the arrangement better than anyone alive, and the arrangement was this: Ada Almeida would fly anywhere, at any hour, in any condition, so long as the route did not cross the Corvina gorge, the lower Vireo basin, or — and this was the part I never said out loud, so it was really more of a clause in a contract only I had signed — so long as nobody asked her to think about rivers as anything other than water going somewhere else.

Seven years of that arrangement. It had held. It had held the way a dam holds, which is to say everyone downstream felt fine right up until they didn't.

The patient was a rubber tapper named Ovidio who'd taken a branch to the chest when a storm came down early, and who had, according to the note the mission station sent, "a cracked rib, a punctured lung, and a wife with strong opinions about hospitals." I liked him already. Men who get hurt doing quiet work in beautiful places and complain only about their wives are the backbone of this coast, in my experience. The coast runs on them the way the Leech used to run on diesel and prayer.

The Leech. I hadn't thought about that boat in — well, that was a lie, I thought about it every time it rained, which on this coast was a professional hazard, like bird strikes or fuel theft. I thought about her the way you think about an ex: with affection, with resentment, with the firm understanding that going back would be a mistake and the less firm suspicion that the mistake might be worth it.

"Cloud's building over the ridge," Juno said. "If we're crossing the Vireo headwaters we should do it now."

"We're not crossing the Vireo headwaters."

"We're crossing them in twenty minutes or we're crossing them in an hour, in rain."

I adjusted the trim. "Then in twenty minutes."

Juno made a sound that in a less disciplined woman would have been a groan. In Juno it was just a long exhale through the nose, a full sentence's worth of opinion compressed into nothing. She'd been my mechanic for five years, which meant she'd been listening to me not-talk about the river for five years, and she had learned that the fastest way through one of my silences was around it.

"Ovidio's wife is coming to the strip tomorrow," she said instead. "Mission says she wants to thank us personally."

"Send her a fruit basket."

"She'll want to hug you."

"People who want to hug me can be redirected to you. You're more huggable. You have that whole — approachable grease thing happening."

"I'm going to put that on my headstone. *Approachable Grease.*" She unwrapped another lemon candy. "You know, most pilots would've said yes to the hug by now. Most pilots have people. A whole network of them. It's very common. There are pamphlets."

"I have people. I have you."

"I'm a mechanic."

"You're my best friend who happens to be a mechanic, and if you say that's sadder I'll turn this plane around."

"It's not sadder. It's just — " She let it go, the way she always did, and I loved her a little for it, and hated myself a little for making her do it. "Ridge in two minutes. Hold onto something, Ovidio."

From the back, a faint, wheezy voice: "Holding onto my wife's opinions."

I laughed. It surprised me, the laugh, the way it always did — arriving uninvited, real, gone again before I could decide whether I'd earned it. That was the other part of the arrangement. I could have these moments. I just couldn't let them stack up into anything. A laugh was a laugh. It was not a life.

The Vireo headwaters passed beneath us in twenty minutes, silver-green, harmless-looking from four thousand feet, a snake seen through smoked glass. I looked at it exactly as long as the flying required and not one second longer. Somewhere down there the water narrowed. Somewhere down there it turned brown and fast and mean, and the walls came in, and there was a bend — the second bend, past the drowned marker tree — where the Corvina had gone down in ninety seconds with my father's hands on her wheel and half the salvage fleet's money in her hold, and where he had stayed ever since, in the dark, in the cold, in the one place I had sworn in front of six witnesses I would never go back to.

I did not look down. I flew.

* * *

The strip at Santa Rosa was a dirt scar with a windsock that had given up on ambition years ago and now pointed wherever it felt like, which was, honestly, relatable. I put us down soft — I always put us down soft, it was the one vanity I permitted myself, because everything else about my life was hard landings — and the mission's orderly came out with a stretcher and a face like a man who had been awake too long to be polite about it.

Ovidio was transferred, pronounced "sleeping," and carried off to the sound of his own muttered commentary. Juno started the post-flight ritual: tie-downs, covers, the long suspicious walk around the airframe that she performed like a priest inspecting an altar for heresy.

I went to find the mail.

There is a system on this coast, older than the radio net and more reliable, by which letters accumulate at mission strips and wait for whichever pilot is headed in the right direction. Most of what I carried was medicine and sutures and the occasional crate of schoolbooks. The letters were the cargo nobody paid for and everybody needed. I'd been running them for seven years, and in seven years I had developed a rule about my own mail, which was that I read it standing up, in daylight, with the engine still ticking — because a letter read sitting down has a way of becoming a conversation, and conversations have a way of becoming attachments, and attachments were the thing I'd given up along with the river.

There were four pieces for me. A fuel invoice, which was really a ransom note. A flyer for a harvest dance at Puerto Cielo, which I would not attend, and which I kept anyway, for reasons I declined to examine. A postcard from a med student I'd once flown out with a snakebite, now in the capital, now with a mustache, now apparently engaged — the postcard said so in a handwriting that had improved, which told me the fiancée was a schoolteacher.

And the fourth.

I knew before I turned it over. That's the thing nobody tells you about the past: it doesn't ambush you. It announces itself, politely, the way a storm announces itself — a pressure change, a smell, a weight in the air — and you have time to do something about it, and the fact that you don't is your own doing.

The envelope was heavy stock. The kind that survives river humidity. The address was in a hand I would have known upside down, in the dark, in a burning building: narrow, upright, economical, every letter doing exactly its job and not a stroke more. A hand that had never once written anything it didn't mean to profit by.

Marcus Vey.

I stood there on the strip with the engine ticking and the windsock pointing at nothing, and I did not open it. I held it at arm's length like something venomous, which was, in fairness, accurate.

Seven years. Seven years since the gorge, since the oath — *never again, not for money, not for blood, not for him* — since I'd watched Vey stand on the bank with his ledger face on and say the words *unfortunate* and *asset* and *we'll recoup* in the same breath, as if my father were a line item that had failed to perform. Seven years since I'd decided that the distance between me and Marcus Vey would be measured in miles, forever, and that I would keep flying those miles until the flying itself wore the memory smooth.

It hadn't worked. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the one cargo I carry for free. The miles hadn't worn anything smooth. The miles had just kept moving me past the same country, over and over, the way a fly keeps hitting the same window.

"Ada." Juno's voice, from under the wing. "You've been reading your mail for six minutes and you've read none of it. I can see the invoice from here. It's still folded."

"It's nothing."

"You're holding it like it's nothing that bites."

I looked at the envelope again. Heavy stock. River-proof. Which meant it had come upriver, which meant someone had carried it down through the basin, which meant Vey had gone to the trouble of routing a letter through a hundred miles of backwater to reach a strip he must have known I serviced, because Vey never did anything without knowing the odds first, and the fact that he'd spent effort told me the letter was not a courtesy.

It was a hook. And the thing about hooks is that they only work on fish that are already swimming toward the bait.

"Juno," I said, "what's our debt situation?"

"Beautiful. Why?"

"The fuel invoice. And the engine overhaul we've been postponing since — "

"Since the last overhaul. I know the number, Ada. I keep it written down and I look at it the way other people look at photographs of exes." She came around the wing, wiping her hands, and her eyes went to the envelope, and narrowed. "Who's it from?"

"Nobody."

"Nobody with expensive stationery."

"Juno."

"Ada." She held out her hand. I didn't give it to her. She nodded slowly, like a doctor confirming a diagnosis. "Okay. So. Here's what we're not going to do. We're not going to stand on this strip in the wind and open a letter from Nobody, and we're not going to fly home and open it at the table with the good lamp, which is what you do when you want to give something power. We're going to fly home, and I'm going to drink one beer, and then you're going to open it, and we're going to deal with whatever it is like adults with a debt."

"That's the same as the table with the good lamp."

"It has beer."

I put the letter in my jacket, inside pocket, over the ribs. It weighed nothing. It weighed a great deal. Both of these things were true, and I had learned to hold contradictions like that the way Juno held a wrench — firmly, and with a standing suspicion that something was about to strip its threads.

We flew home in the long gold light that comes before the monsoon, when the whole coast looks like a photograph of itself, too beautiful to be trusted. Juno slept in the right seat, or pretended to. Below us the rivers ran their courses, patient, indifferent, carrying everything they carried toward the sea, and I did not look at them, and I did not think about the gorge, and I did not say my father's name, and I told myself — the way I had told myself for seven years, in the voice of a woman giving directions to a stranger — that I was fine, that the arrangement held, that I measured my life in miles now, and miles were clean, and miles went nowhere in particular.

The letter rode against my ribs the whole way home.

I opened it that night, after the beer, at the table with the good lamp, because Juno was right and I hated that. Three paragraphs. An offer. Triple pay for a monsoon-season run upriver, cargo unspecified, manifests unnecessary, discretion expected and compensated. And at the bottom, in that narrow economical hand, the signature I hadn't spoken aloud in seven years — and beneath it, one line more, written smaller, the way a man writes when he knows exactly where the knife goes:

*I know what's in the Corvina, Ada. And this time I'm asking instead of taking.*
