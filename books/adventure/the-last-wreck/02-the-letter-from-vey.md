## 2. The Letter from Vey

The letter was waiting for me when I got back from the medevac run, which meant it had been waiting for two days, because the mail plane only comes to Corvo Strip twice a week and the pilot, a saint named Beatriz with forearms like dock rope, leaves things under the weighted stone by my fuel drums. I knew it was trouble before I touched it. Trouble has a particular kind of envelope — heavy stock, machine-stamped, addressed in a hand too careful to be honest. Nobody who writes like that wants anything good from you.

I stood there in the heat shimmer with my flight bag still cutting into my shoulder and read the return address three times.

*Vey Salvage & Recovery.* Port Meridian.

Seven years, and Marcus Vey had finally spent the price of a stamp on me.

I should have burned it right there. I want that on the record, wherever records go — the version of me who knew better, standing on the cracked apron with jet fuel fumes rising around her ankles like a bad idea made visible. She existed. She held the letter over the drum of waste oil. And then she thought about the number scrawled on the whiteboard inside the hangar, the one Juno kept updating in red grease pencil like a man keeping score at a funeral, and she put the letter in her shirt pocket instead, against her heart, where it sat all evening like something swallowed wrong.

* * *

Juno was under the Leech when I found him, which is where Juno lives most days, flat on the creeper with his boots sticking out into the aisle like an offering to whatever god repairs river boats. The Leech is ours — mine and his, though the debt on her is mostly his name on paper and mostly my fault in spirit. Thirty feet of riveted iron that handles floodwater like a drunk handles stairs: badly, loudly, but she gets there. We've hauled medicine, fuel drums, survey gear, once an entire wedding party including a goat, and never once — not once in seven years — have we taken a contract above the falls.

"Mail's here," I said.

"Mmf," said Juno.

"Beatriz says the wet season's coming early this year. Pressure's already dropping off the coast."

"Mmf."

"There's a letter from Marcus Vey."

The creeper stopped rolling. There was a silence with a specific texture to it, the sound of a man deciding whether he'd heard what he'd heard. Then Juno slid out backward into the light, blinking, a smear of grease across one cheekbone like war paint applied by someone with no artistic convictions.

"No," he said.

"I haven't opened it yet."

"You don't need to open it. It's from Vey. Whatever's inside is no." He sat up, took the rag off his shoulder, and pointed it at me the way other men point guns. "Ada. Say no with me. Ready? No."

"He hasn't even asked anything yet."

"He's asking. He's been asking for seven years, just quietly, from a distance, the way a shark asks a seal." He got up, joints popping in sequence like a lock being picked, and held out his hand. "Give it here. I'll burn it. I'm good at burning things, it's basically my whole skill set, ask anyone."

I didn't give it to him. That was the whole problem with me, really, distilled: I didn't give it to him.

We went inside instead, into the office corner of the hangar where we keep the desk with the wobbly leg, the kettle with the frayed cord, and the whiteboard with the number. I put the letter on the desk between us like a card I was afraid to turn over. Juno put the kettle on, because Juno's answer to every crisis since I've known him has been tea, and honestly it's as good a theology as any.

I opened it.

* * *

You have to understand what Marcus Vey's handwriting looks like before you can understand the letter. It's small, precise, upright — the writing of a man who has never once let emotion touch the paper without signing for it first. Seven years ago I watched him write cargo manifests in that same hand while my father argued with him about salvage rights, and even then, even at twenty-three, furious and grieving in advance for reasons I couldn't name yet, I remember thinking: *this man writes numbers the way other people write prayers.*

The letter ran four paragraphs. I'll give you the shape of it, because I've had seven years and one very long night to memorize it.

Paragraph one was pleasantries, which from Vey is a threat. He hoped I was well. He noted that the Leech was still registered, still surveyed, still crewed — he knew this because Vey knows everything that can be bought or looked up, and he'd clearly checked. He mentioned, in passing, that monsoon season was forecast to be severe, and that severe seasons were historically good for men with boats and bad for men without them.

Paragraph two was the offer. Triple rate. A cargo run upriver, above the falls, into the gorge country, during the first window of the rains. His launch would follow with the heavy equipment; the Leech would run ahead as pathfinder and light freight. Payment half up front, half on delivery, cash, no credit, no offsets against — and here he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over a scalpel — *outstanding obligations.*

He knew about the debts. Of course he knew about the debts. Somewhere in Port Meridian there was a file with our names on it, and the file was accurate down to the last interest payment, and that accuracy was itself a message: *I have been watching you the way you watch weather.*

Paragraph three was the terms. No questions asked about the cargo. No manifests filed. No stops at intermediate stations. Radio silence after the second checkpoint. The crates would be sealed and pre-loaded; we were not to open them; we were not to count them; we were, in the language of the letter, "conveyance only." Conveyance only. As if the Leech were a mule and I were a muleteer and the whole history of the river were just a route on somebody else's map.

And paragraph four — paragraph four was one line, and it was the line that did the damage, the hook set so gently I almost admired it:

*I think your father would have understood the arithmetic.*

* * *

I read that line maybe forty times that night. Juno read it once, went very still, and then said, with the terrible gentleness he saves for exactly these moments, "He's using your dad to sell you a coffin."

"It's just a sentence."

"It's bait, Ada. It's the exact right size and shape of bait for you specifically. He's known you since you were a kid. He knows what you carry." Juno wrapped both hands around his mug and looked at me over the rim. "Your father died up there. In the gorge. On a job that had Vey's fingerprints all over the financing, if you ever actually say that out loud, which you don't, which is its own problem. And now Vey wants you to take a sealed, unmanifested, triple-rate load back up the same water during the worst season of the year, and he signs it off with a note about what your father would have understood." He set the mug down hard enough to slosh. "That's not a business letter. That's a man testing whether the scar tissue holds."

"The money's real, though."

"The money's always real. That's how you know it's a trap. Traps are expensive. Nobody baits a snare with gravel."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and then we sat in the hangar office while the evening came down outside — the light going bronze, then violet, then that deep river-country blue that makes every silhouette look like a decision you haven't made yet. The moths arrived at the work lamp. Somewhere down the strip, Beatriz's plane ticked as it cooled. And the number on the whiteboard hung over everything, red and patient.

Here's what the number was. I'll tell you straight, because the whole shape of what happened later depends on it being told straight. We owed eleven thousand, four hundred and change. Engine overhaul on the Leech — she'd been running on a rebuilt block that Juno swore was fine and Juno, being honest, admitted was fine *for now*, which is mechanic for dying. Six months of back berth fees at Corvo. A medical bill of Juno's that he'd tried to hide from me for a year and that I'd found anyway, because I read his mail the way he reads my silences, and neither of us had ever said a word about it, which is its own kind of love. Interest accruing at a rate that meant the number grew faster than any honest freight schedule could shrink it.

Triple rate cleared it. Triple rate cleared it twice over, with enough left to rebuild the engine properly, pay the berth through the year, and still eat during the dead months when the river traffic thinned to nothing and the strip went quiet as a held breath.

"That's how he gets you," Juno said quietly, watching me do the arithmetic with my eyes, which I cannot hide, I've never been able to hide it, my father was the same. "Not with lies. With true numbers attached to a false thing."

"It's freight," I said. "Sealed crates. People move sealed crates all the time. Diplomatic pouches. Pharma cold-chain. Half the legitimate cargo on this coast travels under seal because of humidity alone."

"In monsoon season?"

"Crates get sealed *for* the wet season. That's standard."

"Above the falls?"

"Vey's launch goes above the falls. Someone has to run ahead."

"Radio silence after checkpoint two?" He spread his hands. "Ada. Name one legitimate cargo run on this river that goes dark for two hundred miles. Name one. I'll wait."

I named none. Because there weren't any, and we both knew it, and the knowing sat on the desk between us next to the letter like a third person at the table.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself in this account, and fairness requires admitting that I did due diligence. I did the diligence of a woman constructing a scaffold to hold a decision she'd already made. I called the harbormaster at Port Meridian — two hours on a bad line, most of it listening to static breathe — and confirmed that Vey Salvage & Recovery was licensed, bonded, current on its insurance. All true. I asked around the strip about anyone who'd worked a Vey contract recently. What came back was thin and strange: a barge pilot who'd run for him two seasons ago and now wouldn't discuss it; a loader at Meridian docks who said, and I quote, "Vey pays on time," and then looked at me with an expression I've thought about more than I'd like, an expression like pity wearing a workman's face.

Pays on time. Licensed. Bonded. Insured. Every box ticked, every box hollow. A shark is a perfectly legal animal. It's even, in its way, well-run.

On the third night I took the letter out to the apron and read it once more under the strip lights, moths orbiting my head like small confused moons, and I paid attention — finally, honestly — to the sentence underneath the signature. Not the one about my father. The one I'd skimmed, because it was small and administrative:

*Conveyance begins within the fortnight. The window will not reopen.*

The window will not reopen. I turned that over. A cargo run is a cargo run; windows close all the time; schedules are schedules. But this phrasing had hunger in it. This wasn't *we'd prefer* or *the contract allows*. This was a man talking about a door that was closing whether he liked it or not, and wanting through it badly enough to pay triple and buy radio silence and reach seven years back into the past to find the one lever marked *Almeida* and pull it.

My father used to say the river tells you what it's carrying if you watch the surface long enough. Whirlpools mean rock. Smooth green water means depth. And a wake that runs too straight, too fast, means something big underneath going somewhere with intent.

This letter was a wake running dead straight.

I knew that. Standing there in the moth-light, I knew it the way you know weather in your knees. And I signed anyway.

* * *

The signing was almost comically ordinary. That's the part nobody warns you about, the part I'd want some younger version of myself to understand: damnation doesn't arrive with thunder. It arrives with a pen that's slightly dry, so you have to scribble it awake on the margin first. It arrives at a wobbly desk, under a work lamp, with tea going cold beside you and your partner watching you with an expression that is not anger, which would be easier, but grief arriving early, the way locals feel earthquakes before the needle moves.

I wrote my name on the acceptance line. My hand was steady. I remember being angry at how steady it was.

"Conveyance only," I said out loud, to make it solid, to make it a thing in the air instead of a thing in my chest. "We're the boat. We're not the reason. Whatever's in those crates, it's his cargo, his risk, his manifest-or-lack-of-one. We run the water, we get paid, we come home, we fix the engine. That's the whole story."

"That's the story you're telling yourself," Juno said. "Stories are load-bearing. Be careful which ones you stack your weight on."

"Say the number, then. Out loud. You tell me how else it clears."

He didn't say the number. He looked at the whiteboard for a long time, and then he looked at the Leech out in her berth, black water holding her hull like cupped hands, and something in his face did the arithmetic too, because Juno is many things but he has never once been a hypocrite about money. We were both hungry. That was the truth under everything. Vey hadn't built the trap out of greed. He'd built it out of need, which is stronger stuff, and cheaper to source.

"One condition," Juno said finally. "We inspect the crates at loading. Not open — I'm not stupid, a seal's a seal, breaking it voids the contract and probably worse. But weight, dimensions, handling marks, how they're strapped, what the strapping tells us. Cargo talks even through a lid. Let me listen."

"Done."

"And if anything about those crates smells wrong — not suspicious, I mean literally wrong, chemicals, rot, whatever — we walk. Mid-contract, penalties be damned. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

He stuck out his hand. We shook on it, grease and all, and it should have felt like a bargain struck. It felt like two people agreeing on the terms of a mistake so that afterward, whichever way it broke, each could say to the other: *but we agreed.* I've thought about that handshake a lot since. It was the soundest unsound decision I've ever made.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Juno had gone home to his room above the chandlery and the strip was nothing but wind and the far-off mutter of the river — I took my father's old river atlas down from the shelf. It's a beautiful object, hand-annotated, every reach of the gorge country covered in his pencil: depths, snag lines, the little skull-and-crossbones he drew at the falls, the tiny neat notation at the gorge mouth that says, in his handwriting, *bad water, respect it,* which was as close as he ever came to fear on paper.

I hadn't opened it in seven years. I'd told myself that was discipline. Sitting there with the lamp low and the letter on the desk and my own signature drying on the acceptance line, I understood it was something else. Discipline keeps a door shut so you can function. What I'd done was brick it up and call the bricking discipline, and now Vey had come along with a crowbar shaped like a number, and the bricks were coming out whether I liked it or not.

I opened the atlas to the gorge.

His pencil marks were still sharp. Rivers don't change much; men's reasons do. I traced the reach above the falls with one finger, the water my father had died in, the water I had sworn off in front of six witnesses whose names I still carry like stones, and I noticed — for the first time, or maybe for the first time I allowed it — that the notation at the head of the gorge wasn't *bad water, respect it.*

It was *bad water, respect it — M.V. knows another way.*

M.V. Marcus Vey. My father had written it years before the end, back when they were partners, back when the word *partner* still meant something between them. Vey knew another way through the gorge. Had known it all along. And the expedition that killed my father had gone the long way, the public way, the way the charts showed — because someone had decided the short way was worth more kept secret than shared.

I closed the atlas. I sat in the dark for a while, listening to the river talk to itself two miles off, saying the same thing it always says, which is that it was here first and will be here last and does not care, even slightly, what we sign.

In the morning I wired confirmation to Port Meridian. *Almeida & Juno accept conveyance contract. Terms acknowledged. Loading date to follow.*

The reply came back within the hour, which told me Vey had been sitting on the transmission the way a fisherman sits on a line. Four words, in that small precise hand, transmitted by his office girl in his exact cadence:

*Freight ready Friday. Dawn.*

Friday. Three days. Enough time to provision, fuel, patch the starboard seam that had been weeping since March, and not nearly enough time to think, which I suspected was the point.

Juno read the telegram, folded it into quarters, and tucked it into his breast pocket, where he keeps the things he intends to survive.

"Dawn Friday," he said. "I'll get the Leech ready. You get some sleep." And then, at the hangar door, with the morning coming up gold behind him and the whiteboard number at his back: "For the record, Ada — I still say no. But I'm coming. Write that down somewhere. When this goes sideways, I want it on paper that I said no."

I wrote it down. I keep the note still.

Three days later, at dawn, a hired deckhand walked down the berth with a duffel over one shoulder and no name to give, and that — though I didn't know it yet — was the moment the freight stopped being freight, and the river started keeping score.
