## 3. What Was Sworn Off

Seven years is a long time to keep a promise you made while shaking.

People assume oaths are made in churches or courtrooms, with witnesses and good lighting. Mine was made at the edge of a gorge called the Throat, at four in the morning, in the rain, by six people who had just watched a crane cable snap and a man they loved go into the water without even time to shout. We swore on whatever we had — a machete, a bottle of rum, Juno's wrench, which she held out flat on both palms like an offering. No river again. No Corvina again. No Vey again.

Three rules. Simple enough to remember when your hands won't stop moving and your voice comes out wrong every time you try it.

I kept mine for seven years. I'd like to tell you that was virtue. Mostly it was arithmetic: the river had taken my father, and I did not intend to give it anything else of mine to hold.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Grief always wants to skip to the end. Let me start where it actually started, which was with a letter too — though that one I opened gladly.

* * *

The Corvina had been dead for eleven years when we went after her.

She was a coastal freighter, three hundred tons, that had come upriver during the last big flood year because her owner — a shipping magnate named Aurelio Sandoval, a name that meant nothing to me then and everything now — believed he could beat the dry season down to the delta and turn a profit on a cargo of machinery, medicine, and, rumor insisted, specie. The rumor was probably true. Rich men don't run rivers for fun.

She never made it. A rock shelf in the lower gorge took her keel, and the river took everything else, slowly, over a week, while salvage crews stood on the banks and argued about liability. Eleven crew drowned. The cargo went into the deep channel below the falls, into a reach so violent that no diver would take the contract at any price. The insurance paid out. The lawyers ate well. And the Corvina settled into the silt of the Throat like a coin dropped in a well, and everyone agreed she was gone for good.

Everyone except the men who kept buying up the old survey charts.

My father, Tomás Almeida, was one of those. He wasn't a rich man's gambler; he was a river engineer who had spent thirty years learning the interior's water the way other men learn scripture — obsessively, argumentatively, with marginal notes. He'd helped run the original rescue attempt at age twenty-five. He'd spent the rest of his life collecting hydrographic surveys, dam proposals, drought records, anything that told him what the Throat looked like underneath its temper. His theory was simple and, as it turned out, correct: every forty years or so, the dry season came down hard enough to drop the gorge's waterline by nine meters. Nine meters was not enough to raise the Corvina. It was exactly enough to walk to her.

He didn't want the gold. I want to be fair to him here, because later, when people needed a villain-shaped hole in the story, they tried to make him one. What he wanted was the cargo manifest and the ship's papers — proof, he said, of what really happened aboard her in that final week. Because there was a second rumor about the Corvina, quieter than the gold one, and it said the ship hadn't simply struck the shelf. It said there had been a boat launched from her stern in the night before she grounded, carrying two passengers and a strongbox of documents downstream, and that the boat had swamped, and that one of those documents was worth more than all the specie in the hold.

My father thought the quiet rumor was the true one. He wanted to be the man who proved it.

What he got instead was Marcus Vey.

* * *

Vey came to us in the dry season of that year with money, equipment, and a smile you could have used to cut glass. He'd been a rival of Dad's since the survey days — same generation, same obsession, opposite temperaments. Where my father loved the river, Vey audited it. He could tell you the tonnage capacity of any reach on the system, the going rate for dive crews, the depreciation schedule on a salvage winch. He spoke about water the way an accountant speaks about a difficult client.

"He's not so bad," Dad said, when I complained about him. "He's just a man who thinks everything can be counted."

"Everything can," Vey said, overhearing, entirely unbothered. "Some things just haven't been measured yet."

I was twenty-four. I'd grown up on boats and barges, doing medevac runs and mail runs and whatever else kept fuel in the tank, and I thought I knew what an expedition was. I signed on as pilot and deck boss because it was my father, and because the pay was real, and because some part of me — I can admit this now — wanted to see the Throat. You grow up hearing about a place the way you hear about a monster under the bed, and eventually you have to look.

There were six of us. My father. Me. Juno Reyes, then nineteen, already the best mechanic on the river and already incapable of saying a kind sentence when an accurate one was available. Old Baptiste, who'd dived the original wreck week and claimed he still heard the Corvina's whistle in storms. Two of Vey's hires, Ferro and a quiet man named Ostrer, who were supposed to be riggers and who spent a suspicious amount of time maintaining rifles nobody had mentioned needing.

And Vey himself, who stood on the deck of our barge the whole way upstream with his ledger book open against the rail, writing.

"What are you counting?" I asked him once, somewhere above the second confluence.

"Costs," he said.

"We haven't spent anything yet."

"We've spent plenty." He turned a page. "You're spending your father's luck. He's spending his life's work. I'm spending capital. The question is whether the return justifies the expenditure."

"And if it doesn't?"

He smiled at me with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse than the alternative. "Then someone will have learned something cheaply. Knowledge is never wasted, Ada. It's only ever mispriced."

I laughed at that, then. Twenty-four-year-olds laugh at men like that. We think we're seeing through them. We're actually just standing too close to see the shape.

* * *

The dry season came down like a verdict.

By the third week of the expedition the gauge at the upper falls had dropped past every mark on the board, and the Throat — which in flood season is a brown roar you can feel through the soles of your boots half a mile away — had shrunk to a green-gray corridor of pools and shingle bars, with the drowned channel running narrow and clear between black walls of rock. You could hear the falls differently, too. In high water they're a single voice. Low, they become many voices, each ledge and undercut talking separately, and my father stood on the bank listening to them with his eyes shut like a man at a concert.

"There she is," he said.

I thought he meant the sound. Then I followed his arm and saw it: a dark line below the surface of the deepest pool, long and straight and impossible, like a rib bone laid across the throat of the world.

We worked for nine days. Divers in shifts, Baptiste first because he insisted and because his nerve was real even if his stories weren't. The hull was intact along the starboard side, buried to the gunwale in silt, and the hold — the hold was everything the rumors promised. Crates stenciled with the Sandoval line's crest. Machinery gone to rust-lace. And in the purser's cabin, behind a door that had warped shut and sealed itself, a strongbox.

Dad came up from his first look inside the hold laughing. Actually laughing, treading water in the green light, holding a handful of silt like it was treasure. "Ada! Ada, the papers — the manifests are here, the whole correspondence, it's all—" He couldn't finish. I'd never seen him like that. I've never seen anyone like that since, except maybe grooms and new mothers.

That was day nine. That was the last good day.

* * *

Here is what I understood only afterward, and what I need you to understand now, because it's the whole reason this story exists:

Marcus Vey never cared about the papers.

He cared about the specie. He'd known about the strongbox in the purser's cabin before we ever left the coast — had known, in fact, that the quiet rumor was true, that the Corvina carried a fortune in minted silver in her double bottom, and that the Sandoval family's claim to it had lapsed into a legal fog that a man with good lawyers and better patience could sail straight through. My father's expedition was, to Vey, a survey. My father himself was, to Vey, a cost item. The manifests, the correspondence, the truth about what happened in the Corvina's last week — that was noise. That was sentiment. Sentiment doesn't appear on a ledger.

And the rifles? Ferro and Ostrer hadn't brought them against river pirates. They'd brought them in case anyone else reached the wreck first — and in case anyone who reached it with us developed inconvenient ideas about shares, or evidence, or telling the Sandoval heirs what their father's ship had actually been carrying, and whose signatures were on the transfer orders dated three days before she sailed.

I know all of this now. I didn't know it then. Then, I only knew that on the tenth morning, the winch cable sang a note I'd never heard it sing, and my father looked up from the strongbox inventory he was writing, and said, very calmly, "Get off the shingle," and the cable parted.

They say drowning is quiet, from outside. It isn't. There's the crack of the cable, which you feel in your teeth. There's the boom swinging, taking the derrick with it, taking the near edge of the shingle bar with it, taking him — he was closest, he was always closest to the work, that was the whole trouble with him — and there's the water closing, and then there's the silence afterward, which is the loudest thing I have ever heard.

The river took him into the deep channel. The same channel that holds the Corvina. In a way I suppose he got what he wanted: he's with her. I have never once found that comforting.

* * *

We came downriver in silence. Six of us left, one of us Vey, who spent the descent doing something I will hate him for until I die: he worked. Sat in the stern with his ledger open, entering figures, closing accounts, and when I finally crossed the deck and put my hand flat on his page he looked up at me with something that was almost sympathy, which is the cruelest thing a man like that can produce.

"The expedition failed," he said. "The loss is total. But the knowledge—"

"Don't," I said.

"—the knowledge is banked," he finished, gently, as if consoling me. "Nothing is wasted, Ada. It's only mispriced."

Juno broke his pencil. Snapped it in half and dropped both halves in the river, and he watched them float away with an expression of mild accounting inconvenience, and I swear to you that expression is why I believed, for seven years, that he was merely a cold man rather than a criminal one. Cold men don't forge things. They don't hire riflemen. They just write numbers.

It took me years to understand what he'd written in that ledger on the way downriver. Not costs. Assets. Which claims to the wreck were legally dead, which heirs had given up, which documents in that strongbox — the ones my father died reaching for — were worth destroying rather than recovering. He hadn't lost the expedition. He'd completed a survey. Everything after was just waiting for the price to be right.

* * *

We swore the oath at the Throat's edge because none of us could leave without marking the place somehow, and because grief needs a container or it spills everywhere. Machete, rum, wrench. No river again — meaning the gorge, meaning salvage, meaning the Corvina by any name. No Vey again. And the third rule was mine, though I said it quietly and only Juno heard the whole of it: no more letters with money in them. If a job ever came with too many zeros attached, it was him, it was always going to be him, and I would burn it unread.

Baptiste drank his share of the rum and poured the rest into the water, which Juno called a waste and I understood completely. You give some back. That's how the bargain works. The river takes, and you acknowledge it, and you don't come back asking for change.

Then we went home, and I sold my share of the boat, and bought a plane, and started flying medicine into places where the ground couldn't follow me. Seven years of valleys and cloud decks and landings on strips barely wider than my conscience. Seven years of keeping the third rule.

Seven years of being very good at not thinking about the fact that somewhere in the deep channel below the falls, in the dark, next to my father, a strongbox full of paper was sitting in a purser's cabin, holding a truth that a certain careful man with a ledger had once priced as worthless — and might, someday, reprice.

I burned the letter the morning it came. That's the rule.

I read it first.
