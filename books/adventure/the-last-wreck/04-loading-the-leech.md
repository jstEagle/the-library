## 4. Loading the Leech

There are two kinds of cargo that make a mechanic's stomach drop. The first is the kind that's too light — crates marked FRAGILE that rattle when you tilt them, which means either glass or something alive, and both mean paperwork. The second is the kind that's too heavy. Medicine doesn't weigh anything. Insulin, antibiotics, antivenin, plasma — you could carry a hospital's worth in one arm if you packed it right. So when I came down the dock at first light and saw six crates on the Leech's deck, each one big enough to ship a piano and sitting on the planks like they'd grown roots there, my stomach did the drop before my brain caught up.

"Juno," Ada said, from the wheelhouse door, coffee mug in hand like it was any other morning. "We need to talk about the manifest."

"We don't have a manifest."

"We have a piece of paper Vey's man gave me that says *general freight* in a handwriting so bad it might say *general regret*. That's not a manifest. That's a shrug."

I looked at the crates again. Six of them, strapped down under a tarp that was doing a poor job of pretending they weren't there. Each stenciled with a shipping mark I didn't recognize — some coastal outfit's code, all angles and no vowels — and each with a weight estimate chalked on the side that made me do arithmetic I didn't like. Two hundred kilos apiece, give or take. Fourteen hundred kilos of *general freight*, riding low in a boat whose hull I knew better than my own hands, because I'd rebuilt those hull planks myself three seasons back after she took a sandbar wrong above Serano Bend.

"The Leech can carry it," I said, because that was true, and because saying the other thing felt like opening a door I wasn't ready to walk through.

"I didn't ask if she can carry it." Ada drank her coffee and watched the river the way she always did, like it owed her money. "I asked what you think."

What I thought was: medicine runs pay in gratitude and diesel receipts, and gratitude doesn't cover the yard bill sitting in my toolbox like a tumor. What I thought was: triple pay is triple pay, and monsoon season upriver is only suicide if you assume you want to live to a ripe old age, which I had never been able to confirm about myself. What I said was:

"I think we should've charged quadruple."

Ada almost smiled. It was the kind of smile that means the argument's over and everyone lost. She went back inside, and I put my hand flat on the nearest crate and pushed. It didn't move. Not stubborn-heavy, not awkward-heavy. Dead heavy. The kind of weight that has no give, no shift, no life in it. Machine parts, maybe. Or metal. Or —

Well. Or the thing we'd all agreed not to say out loud, which was that Marcus Vey didn't get rich paying triple for insulin.

* * *

The deckhand showed up at seven, which meant he'd been waiting somewhere out of sight since six, because nobody arrives exactly on time unless they've rehearsed it.

He was tall in the way that makes boat work harder, all elbows and knees folded into spaces built for shorter men, with dark hair going gray at the temples early and a face that looked like it had been assembled from spare parts of several more expressive faces. He had a duffel over one shoulder and a canvas roll — tools, by the shape of it — under his arm, and he stood at the top of the gangplank for a full ten seconds reading our boat the way a man reads a document he intends to contest later.

"You the hired hand?" I asked.

"I'm looking for the Almeida run."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer I have." He came down the plank without waiting for permission, which I noted, and set his duffel down clear of the scupper line, which I also noted, and then he looked at the engine housing through the open hatch with an expression I recognized, because I wear it myself sometimes — the look of a man diagnosing something he already knows is terminal. "That's a Marlin forty-horse?"

"Marlin forty-two," I said. "Rebuilt."

"When?"

"Three seasons ago. Why?"

"Because her compression's uneven on the starboard cylinder and your fuel line's chafing against the bracket where the previous owner drilled a hole he shouldn't have." He crouched, peered into the hatch without touching anything, which earned him a point, and added, "You'll lose her above the second gorge if you don't reroute that line."

Now, here's the thing. I have spent eleven years keeping the Leech alive, and in eleven years exactly four people have ever correctly identified that fuel line problem, and three of them were dead. The fourth was me, last Tuesday, which is why there was a coil of new hose sitting on my bench with a note in my own handwriting that said FIX BEFORE MONSOON, IDIOT.

So I did what any professional does when a stranger walks aboard and reads my engine like a confession. I hated him immediately and completely.

"Who told you to say that?"

"Nobody. It's obvious."

"Nothing's obvious. Who are you?"

"Elias," he said, after a pause long enough to suggest the name had been selected from a menu rather than remembered from a life. "I'll work. I don't talk much. I'm cheap."

"Nobody's cheap who knows things like that."

"I'm cheap," he repeated, in a tone that closed the subject like a hatch, and picked up his duffel, and went aft to find a place to stow it, and that was that. First conversation, total duration ninety seconds, contents suspicious.

I found Ada in the wheelhouse and told her, "Our new deckhand diagnosed the starboard cylinder from the gangplank."

She didn't look up from the freight paper she was annotating with the specific brand of pessimism she reserves for documents she intends to survive. "Can he lift?"

"He can lift."

"Then he's hired until he gives me a reason otherwise." She tapped the paper. "Vey's man comes back at noon for the count. I want everything squared away before he shows. And Juno —"

"I know."

"You don't know yet, but you will."

That's the thing about Ada Almeida. Seven years I've worked for her, and she has this habit of being right about disasters roughly forty-eight hours before the rest of us get the memo, and she carries the knowledge around like a stone in her pocket, touching it now and then to make sure it's still there. I've learned to read the signs. The coffee mug held but not drunk. The manifests annotated twice. The way she looked at those crates like they were letters from someone she used to be.

* * *

Vey's man came at noon, and he brought friends.

Not laborers, either. I've loaded enough boats to know the difference between men hired to haul and men hired to stand. Haulers arrive sweating and complaining and immediately start asking where things go. These six came up the dock in step, in boots too clean for dock work, wearing the particular blankness of men whose job description is a number, and they spread out along the Leech's rail without anyone telling them where to stand, which is itself a tell, because untrained men cluster, trained men distribute.

Their leader was a broad, sunburned man with a ledger book under his arm, and he introduced himself as Corben, of Vey Salvage, and shook Ada's hand with the enthusiasm of a man testing grip strength.

"Count's simple," Corben said. "Six crates, fourteen hundred kilos, sealed as received. You deliver upstream, we offload, you're paid on return. No questions clause stands."

"No questions clause," Ada agreed. "Which means no questions about why a salvage outfit needs freight hauled past its own forward camp instead of running its own boats."

Corben smiled like a man enjoying a joke he'd heard before. "Fuel costs, Ms. Almeida. Seasonal scheduling. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Ada said, in a voice that meant she knew exactly how it was and would be filing it away for later use, possibly as evidence.

Here's what I noticed, though, while Corben and Ada performed their little duet of mutual distrust. While they talked, two of the standing men walked the deck. Casual-like. Except they weren't looking at the crates. They weren't looking at the tarp, or the tie-downs, or the engine hatch, or any of the things a man loading a boat looks at. They were counting.

I know counting when I see it. My father counted everything — fish, debts, the seconds between lightning and thunder — and he taught me the look. Lips still, eyes moving in that tick-tick-tick rhythm, a small nod at each unit completed. These two were counting the rifles slung across their companions' backs. Six men on the dock, five rifles visible, one doing something with his jacket that suggested the sixth rifle was present but shy. Then one of them glanced at the wheelhouse, at the flare locker, at the small arsenal of tools and odds we keep lashed by the stern winch, and his lips moved slightly, and I understood that we were also being counted. The boat. Us. Assets and liabilities, weighed together.

And then — and this is the part I keep coming back to, the part that sat in my chest all afternoon like a swallowed hook — Corben opened his ledger, wrote something, and I caught, upside down, in his blocky hand, a column of figures next to the word LEECH. Not a payment figure. A valuation. Our boat, our engine, our fuel, appraised the way you appraise something you might need to acquire, or dispose of, or leave behind.

He caught me looking. He didn't flinch. He just turned the ledger toward himself, pleasant as Sunday, and said, "Something wrong with your eyes, friend?"

"Sunlight," I said.

"Mm." He snapped the ledger shut. "Crate seals check out. We'll see you at the forward camp, day after tomorrow, weather permitting." He paused at the gangplank, one boot on the dock, and looked back at the Leech with that same appraising expression, and said, mostly to himself, "She'll do."

She'll do. Like we were equipment. Like the Leech and her crew were a line item that had passed inspection, and the inspection had been for something other than freight.

They went down the dock in step, in their clean boots, and the dock boards barely registered them, and I stood at the rail watching them go and thinking about the word *dispose*, which I hadn't actually heard anyone say, except that I had, inside my own head, in Corben's voice, and once a word installs itself in your head in another man's voice, you may as well believe it, because your gut knew it before your brain signed off.

* * *

We cast off at dusk, because Ada wanted the tide and I wanted to be gone from that dock, and Elias turned out to be worth his wage in the first hour — lines handled clean, no wasted motion, the economy of a man who's done hard work in places where wasted motion gets people hurt. He didn't talk. He did glance upstream every time the channel narrowed, quick flicks, like checking exits, and once, passing the mouth of the Serano cut, he said quietly, to nobody, "Shoal's moved half a cable east since the charts," and thirty seconds later the shoal was exactly where he said, showing its teeth in the last light, and Ada looked at him in the wheelhouse mirror for a long moment and said nothing, which from Ada is a paragraph.

I ran the engine and rerouted the fuel line by lantern light while we idled in the lee of the point, because I'd be damned if a stranger was going to be right about my boat twice without me fixing the thing he was right about. And while I worked, with my arms in the bilge and the diesel smell in my nose, I did my own counting, because apparently it was contagious.

One: six crates of dead-weight freight that no salvage company needs ferried past its own camp. Two: six men in clean boots carrying five and a half rifles between them, counting ours. Three: a ledger with our boat's name and a valuation beside it. Four: a deckhand who knows rivers that aren't on the charts and won't say where he learned them. Five: triple pay, which in my experience is the price tag attached to jobs where the real currency turns out to be something else.

Five items. In my father's system, five counts means you start moving toward shelter before the thunder arrives.

I wiped my hands, climbed out of the bilge, and found Ada at the wheel, upstream lights burning low, the gorge walls beginning to lean in around us like a crowd going quiet.

"Juno," she said, without turning. "How's the line?"

"Fixed."

"Good." A pause. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere ahead, thunder — or the falls; up here they sound the same. "Whatever you're counting," she said, "count faster."

I looked at her. She was watching the water, and her knuckles were white on the wheel, and she had known — she had known since the crates came aboard, probably since the letter, maybe since seven years ago — and she'd signed anyway, and she was steering us straight into the arithmetic.

"Ada," I said. "What did Vey hire us to carry?"

She was quiet long enough for the engine to answer for her, thumping its steady forty-two horses against the current.

"Freight," she finally said. "He hired us to carry freight."

And the way she said it — the way the word sat in her mouth like a stone she couldn't spit out — told me that whatever was in those crates, whatever waited upstream in the narrowing dark, it wasn't freight at all, and the first wall of rain hit the deck before either of us said another word.
