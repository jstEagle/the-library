## 5. Upstream

The Leech took the current like an old dog taking stairs — reluctantly, with much complaint, and only because she trusted the hand on her rail.

I stood at the wheel through the first morning and let the river do most of the talking. It's a mistake to fight water that big. You negotiate. You find the seam where the current folds back on itself, the slack water hugging the bank, and you slide your boat into it like a letter into an envelope. My father taught me that before I was old enough to spell *negotiate*, and seven years of flying had not untaught me. The hands remember. That's the trouble with hands.

Behind me, Juno had the engine hatch open and was talking to it in the low, profane murmur she reserved for machinery she loved and suspected of betrayal. The crates sat stacked amidships under tarps, riding low in the water — heavier than any medicine run I'd ever carried, but Vey's money was already spent against our debts, and a debt is a kind of cargo too. It rides below the waterline whether you load it or not.

The deckhand watched the gorge walls go by.

He'd given his name as Elias and nothing else — no surname, no port of origin, no story about a sick mother or a gambling debt, which is what hired deckhands usually offer when you ask why a man who reads books is hauling rope for triple-no-questions pay. He'd been aboard two days and hadn't once asked what we were carrying, which told me more than any question would have. Curious men ask. Men who already know keep quiet.

"Sandbar," he said, without raising his voice from where he leaned at the bow rail. "Two hundred meters ahead, starboard side. It moves after every rain."

I looked. The water there showed nothing — no boil, no discoloration, just the river being smooth and innocent. But I'd grown up on this water, and underneath the smoothness my eyes found it: a faint flattening of the current's grain, the way silk lies differently over a hidden knee.

I eased the Leech port and we slid past whatever slept there. A minute later the bar broke surface behind us, brown and glistening, exactly where he'd said.

Juno stuck her head out of the engine hatch. "How'd he do that?"

"Lucky guess," Elias said.

"Twice in one morning isn't luck," Juno said. "Three times is a pattern. You've done three."

Elias smiled at her — a small, closed smile, the kind you give children who ask about the lockbox on the high shelf. He didn't answer, and I decided I could live without answers for another day. The gorge was narrowing ahead, and narrow gorges deserve your whole attention. They take payment in hulls.

* * *

By afternoon the walls had come in close enough that the river ran green instead of brown, deep water moving slow between cliffs that rose sheer on both sides, streaked white with bird lime, hung with vines that trailed in the current like drowned hair. The air cooled ten degrees the moment we entered. Sound changed too — our engine noise came back off the stone and followed us, so we seemed to be pursued by ourselves.

It's beautiful country. I want to be honest about that, because everything else I have to say about this river is ugly, and it would be a lie of omission to leave out the beauty. The light comes down the gorge in slanted gold columns and lands on the water like something spilled from a rich man's pocket. Orchids grow out of cracks in the cliff face at heights no human hand will ever reach them. There are places where the rock has been carved by centuries of flood into shapes so like architecture — arches, buttresses, doorways — that you half expect the doorway to open.

My father used to say the gorge was the most honest church he knew. Then he went into it to pray, in his fashion, and it kept him.

I don't fly over it if I can help it. I especially don't look down.

"You've been here before," Elias said. He'd drifted aft to stand near the wheelhouse, close enough to talk over the engine, far enough to be polite. Or far enough to be deniable. With him it was hard to say which.

"Everyone who runs this river has been here."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Most people who run this river turn back at the falls. What's past the falls doesn't get run. It gets survived." His eyes stayed on the cliff walls, reading them. "You handle the wheel like someone who learned the words to a song she swore never to sing again."

I let the silence stretch long enough to be rude. "You handle rope like a man who's read about it in a book. We're all full of surprises."

That got a real laugh out of him, brief and surprised out of him before he could stop it, and I was annoyed to notice that I liked the sound. Annoyed, because liking things is how rivers get you. You admire the orchids, and then you're looking up instead of ahead, and then the sandbar has your keel.

"The dead reach starts in four kilometers," he said, conversationally, as if we were discussing weather. "There's a bend where the 1911 expedition lost two boats and eleven men. After the bend the channel splits around a midstream island; the left channel looks open but has a submerged timber jam from the old logging days. Take right, hug the cliff, and mind the eddy line — it'll try to spin you."

I looked at him properly then. Up close, in the slanted gold light, he was younger than his voice suggested and older than his face — one of those people assembled from mismatched parts, as if whoever built him had started with a plan and abandoned it halfway. There was a scar through one eyebrow, fine and old. His hands were wrong for a laborer: long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticles even now, after two days of rope work.

"And how would you know all that?" I asked. "The logging jam went in forty years ago. The men who saw it are dead or lying about it in bars."

"My father kept notes."

"Your father sounds like a careful man."

"He was a thorough one." Something moved behind his eyes when he said it, some large animal turning over in a dark room. "Thorough enough to write down where everyone died."

* * *

We took the right channel. Of course we did — I'm stubborn, not stupid, and the fact that his information proved perfect was its own kind of argument. The timber jam was there, black and slick under six feet of water, sharp as a fence of spears. We cleared it by a boat's length. Juno crossed herself with an oil-stained thumb, which is the closest she comes to prayer, and Elias watched me the whole time with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not smugness. Something more like grief wearing satisfaction's clothes.

At dusk we tied up in a fold of the cliff where the current went slack, under an overhang that would keep the rain off if rain came. Juno cooked rice with dried fish and too much chili, the way she cooks everything, on the principle that suffering should at least be flavorful. The gorge walls went purple, then black. Bats came out in their thousands and stitched the last light together overhead.

"So," I said, when Juno had gone forward to check the anchor lines. Elias sat on the crate stack, eating carefully, like a man who'd learned not to trust meals. "Triple pay. No questions. You signed on for a monsoon run up a river that kills professionals, to haul freight nobody will name, for a man you'd never met before this week. And you know this water better than my charts do."

"I know this water better than anyone's charts," he said. "Because I made some of them."

"From your father's notes."

"Among other sources." He set down his bowl. In the lantern light his face did something complicated — the large animal in the dark room turned toward the door. "Captain Almeida. Can I ask you something, since you're asking questions?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"Why does a woman who clearly loves this river hate it this much? I've watched you steer. You know every seam and eddy like they're letters from home. And every time we round a bend into somewhere beautiful, you flinch. Like the beauty is a trick being played on you specifically."

I should have thrown him off the boat right there. That's the honest assessment. A stranger who reads you that accurately on day two is either a friend or a threat, and I hadn't determined which, and the smart move was to determine it from a distance, preferably a distance measured in meters of open water.

But the gorge was dark and the rice was hot and he had said *letters from home*, and there is a loneliness that lives in people who avoid their own histories, and it answers to its name when called, even by strangers. Even by strangers with ink-stained hands and dead fathers' notebooks.

"My father died in this gorge," I said. "Seven years ago. On a salvage run. I was crew. I was at the wheel when it happened." I heard my own voice go flat and procedural, the way it goes when I'm carrying something heavy across a narrow plank. "The surviving crew swore an oath. Never again. I've kept it. Until this week."

Elias was very still. "What were you salvaging?"

"That's a no-questions job, same as yours."

"It was the Corvina," he said quietly.

The name went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. I kept my face still because I've had practice, but inside, everything tilted. Seven years I'd gone without hearing that word spoken aloud, and here was a stranger saying it in the dark like he was setting a match to paper.

"How do you know that name?"

"Because the Corvina belonged to my father." He reached inside his jacket and brought out an oilcloth packet, worn soft at the edges, and laid it on the crate between us without opening it. "Half a manifest. The other half went missing the week he died, along with certain papers that made me legitimate. Marcus Vey has them. Marcus Vey has always had them. And Marcus Vey has hired you — the daughter of the man who died trying to claim that wreck — to carry him up this river so he can strip it clean before the floods seal it forever."

The bats wheeled overhead. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstream, faint as a memory, thunder was deciding whether to become weather.

"You're telling me," I said, "that I've been paid triple rate to ferry the thief to the theft."

"I'm telling you that you've been lied to by a man who lies professionally." His voice was steady, but his hands weren't quite; he folded them in his lap like closing a book. "I stowed passage because I needed to reach that wreck, and because I needed to see what kind of person would take Vey's money. I wanted to know if you were like him."

"And now?"

"Now I've watched you read the water all day like it owes you an apology." He almost smiled. "Now I think you're someone who made a deal with her own worst memory, and I know exactly how that feels."

I looked at the oilcloth packet sitting between us. Half a manifest. Half a life, maybe. The thunder upstream was getting organized.

"If any of this is a performance," I said, "it's a good one. Which means I can't trust it. Which means—" I stopped, because the sentence was going somewhere I didn't want it to go, somewhere past the wheelhouse and the payroll and the plan.

"Which means you'll watch me," Elias said. "And I'll watch you. And we'll both be watching Vey. That's not trust, Captain. That's just three sets of eyes where before there was one."

Above us the first fat drops hit the tarpaulin, heavy as coins. Juno came aft at a trot, squinting up. "Rain's early," she said. "Season's coming in mean this year. I can feel it in the engine."

I looked upstream, into the black throat of the gorge, where the thunder had stopped deciding and committed. Somewhere up there, Vey's advance camp was burning lamps and counting rifles. Somewhere up there lay the ribs of a ship I'd sworn off, holding whatever it held.

"Get some sleep," I told them both. "Tomorrow we make the falls."

And beneath the drumming rain, Elias picked up his father's oilcloth packet and held it a moment against his chest, like a man holding a door shut against something on the other side — while I sat at the wheel of a boat named for a bloodsucker, carrying us all deeper into the debt.
