## 6. The Manifest

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like competence. I had been practicing it for three days.

I hauled rope when hauling was needed. I kept my hands busy and my mouth shut. I learned the Leech the way a thief learns a house he means to rob someday — the groan of her bilge pump, the particular complaint her engine made above two thousand revolutions, which crates Juno had lashed where and why. And all the while Ada Almeida watched me with those river-gray eyes that missed nothing except, thank God, the one thing that mattered.

She thought I was a hired deckhand who wouldn't give his name because he was running from something. She was half right. I was running *toward* something, which in my experience produces the same guilty posture and considerably worse sleep.

The truth is a document. It has always been a document, for me. I grew up in the offices of my father's shipping concern in Port Meridian, among ledgers and brass fittings and the smell of ink, and I learned early that paper outlives people — that a signature can kill a man as thoroughly as a river can, only slower, and with better legal standing. My father, Aurelio Serrano-Quist, owner of the *Corvina*, died believing his affairs were in order. He was wrong by exactly one forged page.

But I'm ahead of myself. On the third night upstream, wedged between the fuel drums and a crate marked MEDICAL SUPPLIES — DO NOT INVERT, which I had privately confirmed contained no medicine whatsoever, I decided the performance had run long enough.

* * *

It happened because of the rain, actually. The monsoon's first scouts had come through that afternoon — brief, violent squalls that turned the gorge walls to waterfalls and sent everyone scrambling for canvas. By full dark the air was a wet wool blanket and the river below us spoke in a continuous low roar, like an animal digesting. We'd tied off in a side channel behind a spit of gravel, and Juno had rigged a tarp over the cockpit that funneled the runoff into a bucket, which overflowed every twenty minutes, which Juno emptied without ever quite waking up, which I found genuinely admirable.

Ada sat under the lamp at the little chart table, mending a torn seam in the tarp with waxed thread, and I understood — watching her hands move, quick and certain, not even looking at the work — that this woman had done this exact thing a thousand times, in worse places, with worse company. There was a scar across her left knuckle I hadn't seen before. There were others, probably, under the sleeves.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit on this boat. Juno does it and look what it got him." She bit the thread clean. "Thinking's how people end up owing money."

"I'm not going to owe you money."

"No?" She finally looked at me. "Then what are you going to do, Elias? Because you've been paid nothing, you've signed nothing, and you haul cargo like a man who's memorizing the route rather than working it. Vey's men didn't hire you — I asked. So either you're a spy, a lunatic, or a ghost, and honestly at this point I'd take the ghost."

The rain filled the pause for me. I've given this speech to lawyers, to magistrates' clerks, to a tribunal clerk in Port Meridian who stamped my petition DENIED — INSUFFICIENT STANDING with the enthusiasm of a man killing a spider. I knew how it went. What I hadn't counted on was how it would feel to say it to her specifically, to the daughter of Tomas Almeida, whose death my father's ship had caused in a way nobody had ever bothered to explain to anyone.

"My name isn't Elias," I said. "Well. It is Elias. But it isn't *only* Elias."

Juno stirred under the tarp behind me, interested despite himself. The bucket overflowed.

"Elias Quist," I said. "My father owned the Corvina."

I have watched people receive shocks before. It's a lawyer's occupational hazard. But I had never watched anyone go as still as Ada Almeida went just then — stillness like deep water, everything underneath still moving. Her needle stopped. The lamp flame leaned in the draft and righted itself.

"The Corvina sank eleven years ago," she said finally.

"She did. In the gorge. With most of her cargo hold sealed and unrecorded, because my father was smuggling more than he was declaring, and because the men he trusted were stealing from him while they did it." I reached inside my jacket — slowly; she flinched, actually flinched, and I hated that I'd caused it — and brought out the oilskin packet I'd carried against my ribs for four years, since the night I'd broken into Vey's office and stolen back what was mine. "Half a manifest. My half. The other half is in Vey's safe, along with the will he forged to get it."

I laid the packet on the chart table between us. Didn't open it. Let it sit there, square and heavy as a confession.

"Aurelio Quist had one legitimate heir," I said. "Me. Born on the wrong side of a marriage that never existed, acknowledged late, resented universally. When he drowned — when his ship drowned, same storm, same season, though he wasn't aboard when she went down in the gorge, he lost her earlier, to debt and to Vey — when he died, his estate should have passed to me. Instead Marcus Vey produced a will, dated six weeks before my father's death, leaving everything to a business partner. Himself, effectively. The courts accepted it because Vey had papers and I had a mother who taught dancing lessons. That was nineteen years ago."

"Nineteen—" Ada started.

"The wreck claim came later. The salvage rights, the expeditions. Vey's spent two decades converting my inheritance into his balance sheet, and the last piece — the Corvina herself, whatever's still sealed in her hold — sits in that gorge waiting for the flood season to close it forever. He has the manifest that says where to cut. I have the manifest that says what's actually down there. And neither half is worth anything without the other, which is why he needs his expedition and why I need his boat." I spread my hands. "Or your boat, as it happens. I stowed aboard at Meridian Docks. Juno caught me within the hour and I bought his silence with the truth and my father's watch, which he tells me he intends to sell the moment we're ashore."

"It's a good watch," Juno said from the darkness, defensively.

Ada hadn't moved. "Tomas Almeida," she said. "My father piloted the salvage attempt. Seven years ago. He died in the gorge."

"I know."

"You *know.*"

"I've read every surviving record of the Corvina expeditions, Miss Almeida. Including the inquiry into the loss of your father's boat. Which concluded — I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have — that the wreck shifted due to floodwater scouring and took the mooring line with it. That conclusion was written by a surveyor on retainer to Marcus Vey." I held her gaze. "Your father did not die of bad luck. He died of a decision Vey made about risk arithmetic — the same arithmetic that let him forge a will, strip a dead man's estate, and hire your boat this season because you're the best pilot on this river and he assumed your grief had an expiry date."

Something moved through her face then — grief and fury braided so tightly I couldn't tell which was winning, and I don't think she could either. The needle in her hand bent. She noticed, and set it down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing you'd otherwise throw.

"Why now?" she said. "Why tell me now, three days in, instead of at the dock?"

"Because at the dock you'd have refused the job and I'd have lost my only way upriver. Because I needed to know if you were Vey's creature or your own before I handed you the only evidence of my life. And because—" I stopped. Reconsidered. Went on anyway, because lying to her had become exhausting somewhere around the second set of rapids. "Because you steered us through the Narrows yesterday with a hand on the wheel like you were holding someone's wrist. And I thought: whoever she's holding, she deserves the truth more than Vey deserves my silence."

The bucket overflowed again. Juno emptied it. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like furniture being dragged across a very large floor.

Ada picked up the oilskin packet. Turned it over once. Then she opened it, and read, by lamplight, the document her father had died within sight of — the manifest of the Corvina, in my father's own cramped hand, listing crate after crate of declared cargo and, in a separate column, initialed A.Q., the undeclared: documents, deeds, letters, the paper skeleton of a business empire, plus bullion enough to make a man stupid. Her eyes tracked down the columns. Stopped at one line. Went back to it.

"'Crate forty-one,'" she said quietly. "'Personal correspondence, sealed. Do not open.'"

"He wrote that for me," I said. "Forty-one was addressed to my mother. He was going to acknowledge me properly. Legally. He was going to fix the paper." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "He ran out of river first."

Ada closed the manifest. For a long moment she looked at me the way you look at a chart when the coastline doesn't match the memory. Then:

"Vey thinks you're dead?"

"Vey thinks Elias the deckhand is a competent pair of shoulders. Vey hasn't thought about Elias Quist in fifteen years — I made sure of it, changing my name, staying coastal, being small. He believes the Quist line ended in a courthouse."

"Then we keep it that way." She stood, folded the manifest back into its oilskin, and pressed it into my chest with both hands — firmly, like dosing a patient. "You carry it. You don't show it to anyone else. And Elias—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd boarded the Leech she almost smiled, a thin, dangerous thing, "—next time you stow away on a boat going into the gorge that killed both our fathers, introduce yourself *before* the rapids. Some of us would like to know whose funeral we're attending."

"That seems fair," I said.

She turned to go aft, then stopped. "One more thing. The crate weights Vey gave Juno — the ones that don't add up. They're not medicine and they're not salvage gear, are they. They're his cutting equipment. Hold-cutters. Diving kit."

"They're whatever lets him open the Corvina before the water closes," I said. "Yes."

Ada nodded slowly, absorbing it. Above us the rain thickened from scouts to skirmish, hammering the tarp, and the river rose a visible inch against the pilings in the time it took her to speak again.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we pass the falls. After that, everything Vey promised us is downstream, and everything he wants is upstream, and I intend to find out which direction he thinks *we're* going."

She killed the lamp. In the dark, Juno whispered, "So the watch. Still selling it?"

"Hold onto it," I said. "It may be the only thing my father ever gave me that Vey can't forge."

And beneath us, patient as paperwork, the river carried the Leech another mile toward the falls.
