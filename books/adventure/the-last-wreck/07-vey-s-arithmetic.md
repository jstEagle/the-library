## 7. Vey's Arithmetic

There is a particular pleasure in a ledger that balances, and I have spent my life chasing it the way other men chase God.

Marcus Vey. That is the name on the manifests, the deeds, the shipping bonds — Marcus Vey, salvage contractor, licensed and bonded and twice sued and never once convicted. My father left me nothing but his debts and his handwriting, which was beautiful and said nothing true, and I have spent forty years correcting both errors. The ledger is how. Every man a line item. Every risk a column. Every sentiment a rounding error to be audited out of existence before it costs you something you can't write off.

I learned arithmetic before I learned kindness, and only one of them has ever paid.

* * *

The forward camp sat in a bend of the river where the gorge pinched down to a throat of black stone, and I had chosen it myself two seasons ago, when the water charts still showed the Corvina's reach as a rumor. Twelve men under canvas. Two launches, one crane barge with a winch older than most of my crew's sins. A cook whose name I had never bothered to learn because cooks are interchangeable and grief is not, and I had buried enough of both to know the difference.

Rennick ran the camp for me. Big man, quiet, hands like mooring cleats. He kept the rifles oiled and the men bored, which is the two great secrets of managing hired muscle: keep the iron clean and keep them from thinking. Thinking men start asking what their employer's margin actually is, and the answer to that question has ended better partnerships than mine.

"Boat's two days out," Rennick said, crouching beside my camp table. "The Leech. Pilot's the Almeida woman."

"I know who it is."

"You want her turned away anyway?"

I looked up from the manifest — half a manifest, technically, though paper doesn't care about halves; paper cares about provenance, and provenance is whatever the last stamp says it is. "No. I want her brought upriver exactly as contracted."

"She'll know the wreck when she sees it."

"Of course she will." I smoothed the page flat. "That's not a flaw in the plan, Rennick. That *is* the plan."

He didn't understand yet. Rennick understands rifles and river currents and which of the men will run at the first sound of real gunfire — he does not understand assets. So let me explain it the way I explained it to myself, seven years ago, standing on a dock with wet boots and a dead man's chart case in my hands while the gorge swallowed the best pilot on this coast.

Ada Almeida is the finest river pilot between here and the headwaters. That is not sentiment; that is a line item. Her father was the finest before her, and the river took him for it, and the daughter responded by swearing off the water entirely — seven years of flying medicine into valleys no road reaches, seven years of keeping her name off every salvage registry, seven years of a discipline so total it looks, from the outside, like peace.

But here is what the ledger taught me, and it has never once been wrong: people do not abandon their gifts. They store them. And stored things rot toward use.

She would come. Not for the money — triple pay is bait for debt, and I'd made sure her debts were the right shape, but money alone wouldn't have moved her. She came because I asked her to pilot a boat up a river she had sworn never to touch again, and somewhere in that woman there is a girl who has spent seven years wondering if she could have saved him, and rivers are patient, and so am I.

One sentimental flaw. Every asset has one. You don't remove it — removal devalues the asset. You *aim* it.

* * *

Let me be honest about the Corvina, since honesty costs me nothing here in my own head, where no tribunal can subpoena it.

The ship went down in the gorge eleven years ago — driven onto the teeth by a storm her owner, one Aldous Renner, should have seen coming, because I had sold him the weather report myself. Cleaned up, that is. Adjusted. A man who trusts a broker's numbers deserves what the river gives him, and Renner trusted numbers the way saints trust prayer.

The cargo was insured. The cargo was also, per my adjusted figures, worth three times what Renner declared, because Renner was an honest man and honest men file honest manifests, and an honest manifest is simply a gift to whoever reads it correctly. I read it correctly. I have been reading it correctly for eleven years, through lawsuits and bankruptcies and Renner dying poor and furious with an illegitimate son nobody would confirm, through the flood seasons that sealed the gorge and the drought years that opened it like a wound.

And then there was Almeida. The father. The best pilot on the river, who put together an expedition seven years back when the low water showed the Corvina's spine, and who made the mistake of filing his salvage claim openly, like a man with a clean conscience. I bought his crew out from under him piece by piece — quietly, cheaply, the way you buy anything from people who need cash more than loyalty. By the time he reached the gorge he had a boat full of strangers and a claim I could contest in court for a decade.

He died in the narrows. I want to be precise about this, because precision matters in accounting: I did not kill him. I did not have to. I simply made certain that when the river offered its usual choice — turn back and lose everything, or push through and probably die — the man had no one left aboard he could trust with the truth of either option. The river did the rest. Rivers always do the rest. It is why I prefer them to partners.

His daughter saw the boat go into the narrows from the bluff above. I know this because I was on the same bluff, and I watched her watch it, and I filed the image away the way I file everything: under *leverage*, subheading *unspent*.

Seven years unspent. Tonight I intended to spend it.

* * *

The rain started after midnight, the monsoon's scouts, fat drops that hit the canvas like fingers testing a drum. I sat in my tent with the lamp turned low and did what I do every night of my life: I counted.

Crew: twelve, plus Rennick. Cost, including hazard pay they didn't know was conditional: manageable. Loyalty: four of them solid, five rentable, three already looking at the exits, which I noted in the mental column marked *disposable*. The three exitable ones would go upstream with the Leech's fuel and gear when the time came, doing the heavy hauling, and if the river wanted them, the river could have them at cost.

Equipment: adequate. The winch worried me — old cable, spliced twice — but cable is replaceable and time is not. The low water window had perhaps nine days left before the rains closed the gorge until next season, possibly forever, given the dam schemes crawling up every government desk on the coast. Nine days. Everything I had built toward in eleven years compressed into nine days, and I found the compression clarifying rather than frightening. Fear is for men with unbalanced books.

The manifest: half of it mine, purchased from Renner's estate administrator, who had accepted my figure without checking it against the original, because administrators are men who get paid either way and therefore check nothing. The other half — the original, the true one, the one that named the bullion and the documents and the little strongbox Renner kept his real fortune in, which was paper, not gold — was somewhere in the wreck or somewhere in the world, and finding it mattered less than reaching the hold first. Possession, as I have said before in courtrooms that believed me, is nine-tenths of the law, and the last tenth is just lawyers arguing about a corpse.

And Ada Almeida: asset, arriving in approximately thirty-six hours, carrying my freight up a river she had sworn off, piloting my plan into position with skills I could never buy outright and had no intention of trying to. You do not buy a thing like Ada Almeida. You arrange circumstances around her and let her excellent character walk itself into your pocket.

Triple pay. I could afford it. I could afford anything, this close to the end, because the end was the point at which all prior costs became irrelevant — that is the whole secret of ventures like this, the thing the moralists never grasp. Ethics are priced for men who expect to play again. I did not expect to play again. This was the final entry, the closing line, the sum beneath the sum, and when a man knows he is closing the book, every page before it becomes negotiable.

Which brings us to the flaw in my own ledger, the one line item I had never managed to audit out.

Her father's face, on the bluff. Not hers — his, afterward, when they pulled what the river gave back. I had attended the funeral. I stood at the back in a good coat, because I had owed him that much and because funerals are excellent places to learn what survivors intend to do next. And I had watched Ada Almeida, twenty-six years old and already harder than the rock they buried him under, swear her oath over the grave with six witnesses — never again, never the river, never the Corvina — and I had thought, even then, even grieving: *there is a woman who will keep that oath right up until keeping it costs her something.*

Everyone keeps oaths until they cost something. That is not cynicism. That is bookkeeping.

* * *

Rennick woke me an hour before dawn, which meant the sentry had seen lights on the water.

"She's early," he said.

"Pilots who are early live longer." I dressed, checked the lamp, checked the ledger box under my cot — habit, not necessity; the real ledger was in my head and had been for years. Outside, the camp smelled of wet canvas and kerosene and the river's green rot, and down through the trees I could see the Leech coming up the bend with her running lights doubled against the dark, riding low.

Too low.

"Rennick. What did we ship her?"

"Crates. Per your list."

"How heavy?"

He told me. I did the arithmetic in about four seconds, and the number was wrong — wrong by enough tonnage that the Leech should have been wallowing, not cutting the current the way she was. Which meant either my freight list had grown en route, or someone had added weight I hadn't ordered, and in my experience unexplained weight on a river is either smuggled goods or stowaways, and only one of those is interesting.

"Count heads when she docks," I said. "Every soul aboard, against the manifest of crew. And count the crates before anyone touches them."

"You think she's playing games?"

"I think," I said, watching the Leech swing toward my dock with a competence that made my chest tighten in a way I logged, briefly, as indigestion, "that everyone is playing games. The difference between me and them is that I know the stakes."

The Leech came alongside. Lines went over. And there she was at the wheel, Ada Almeida, seven years older and not one degree softer, looking down at me across the rail with eyes that had watched a boat die in the narrows and had apparently decided to spend the intervening years sharpening themselves on the memory.

"Vey," she said. No greeting. Good. Greetings are for people in relationships; we were in a transaction.

"Captain Almeida. You're ahead of schedule."

"The river was kind." Her gaze went past me, unhurried, cataloguing the camp — the launches, the crane barge, the rifles some fool of a deckhand hadn't bothered to cover. I watched her log each item. She was good. She was very good. For one unprofessional moment I regretted that the arrangement required her eventual disposal rather than her retention.

Then her mechanic appeared at the rail — the Juno woman, grease to the elbows, counting my men the way I counted everything — and behind her, half in shadow, a third figure. Tall. Unlisted. Watching me with an expression I couldn't price, which in thirty years of pricing expressions had happened perhaps four times.

"Your crew list said two," I said pleasantly.

"My crew list said two," Ada agreed. "The river provides."

I smiled, because smiling is free, and inside my head the ledger opened to a fresh page and wrote, in my father's beautiful lying hand: *Unknown variable, one. Origin unknown. Value unknown. Threat assessment: pending.*

I hate pending.

"Get her unloaded," I told Rennick. "Fuel her, feed them, and put them up in the east tents. They've earned rest."

"And after?" Rennick asked, low, as the gangplank dropped.

"After," I said, watching the unknown variable step onto my dock and set his boots down like a man arriving somewhere he'd been before, "we travel light."

By noon the siphon hose would be in the Leech's tanks, and by dawn the day after, my three disposable men would be gone upstream with every liter of it, and the finest pilot on this coast would wake up marooned above the falls with a monsoon coming down and a long walk ahead of her — unless, of course, the river provided.

It sometimes does.

For my enemies, it never has.
