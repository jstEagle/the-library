## 8. Stranded

I woke to the sound of the river doing something wrong.

You spend enough years on water and you learn its vocabulary — the mutter of a healthy current, the cough of a snag, the long vowel of a rapid working through its rocks. This was none of those. This was a silence where an engine note should have been, and beneath it a hiss like the river drawing breath through its teeth.

I lay in my bunk aboard the Leech for three full seconds before I understood, and I want it noted that those were the last three seconds of peace I got that day.

Then I was up, bare feet on wet planking, out into the grey.

The fuel line hung open over the side like a severed tendon, dripping nothing. The tank cap sat on the deck beside it, placed there deliberately — not lost, not forgotten, *placed*, the way you'd set down a teacup before leaving a house forever. That was the detail that told me everything. Vey's men hadn't been sloppy. They'd been courteous about it. There's a particular species of insult in a thief who tidies up after himself.

"Juno," I said, and my voice came out flat as slate. "Juno, get up."

She was already moving — I could hear her below, the clatter of her tool roll hitting the deck plates. She emerged into the dawn with a wrench in one fist and her hair doing something architectural, and she looked at the open fuel line, and then at me, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the tank gauge wasn't already saying better.

I checked anyway. Old habit. You check the wound even when you can see the bone.

Empty. Bone dry, all four hundred liters of it, plus the two reserve drums we'd lashed aft, which were gone entirely — cut loose with the lashings sliced clean, no hurry, no fear of being caught. Which meant they'd had time. Which meant they'd started hours ago, while Juno slept her watch off and Elias kept whatever vigil he kept and I dreamed, God help me, of getting paid.

Above us, maybe six kilometers upriver through the morning mist, a thread of engine smoke climbed off the water and bent east toward the falls. Vey's launch, hauling our fuel, our spare parts, most of the good rope, and both of the crates marked MEDICAL SUPPLIES that had never once contained anything medical.

"They took the winch cable too," Juno said. She'd walked the length of the boat twice by then, cataloguing losses the way other people count rosaries. "And the spare prop. And — Ada." She stopped at the stern and turned around slowly. "They took the anchor."

"The anchor."

"The anchor."

We looked at each other across the deck of a boat that was now, technically, a large wooden opinion about floating, moored to nothing above a set of falls that ate boats the way I ate regret: regularly, and without much chewing.

* * *

Here is what I want to say about betrayal, since I've now collected enough specimens to write the field guide: it doesn't feel like surprise. Surprise is for things you couldn't have predicted. Betrayal feels like recognition. It feels like meeting someone at a train station you'd already seen in a dream, badly lit, and thinking *oh. Of course. It was always going to be this platform.*

I'd known. Some part of me had known since the crates went aboard heavier than any medicine ever milled, since Vey's men counted rifles with the fond attention other men reserve for counting money, since the manifest I signed had more blank lines than filled ones. I'd known and I'd signed anyway, because debts are a kind of deafness, and triple pay talks very loudly.

Elias appeared from the forward hold where he'd been sleeping — or pretending to sleep; I was beginning to suspect the man did neither so much as he *waited* — and took in the scene with an expression I couldn't read. Not shock. Something closer to a man watching weather he'd forecast arrive on schedule.

"You knew," I said.

"I suspected."

"Suspected." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You stowed away on this boat. You knew half the manifest by heart before I'd heard of it. Did you suspect this too? That he'd strand us?"

"He needs this river more than he needs us," Elias said. "He always has. Men like Vey don't betray people, Almeida. They depreciate them. We were an asset with a service life, and the service life ended last night." He crouched at the open fuel line, touched the drip point, rubbed his fingers together. "Siphoned sometime after two. Two men minimum, forty minutes of pumping. Nobody woke."

"Nobody woke," I repeated. "On my boat. With my watch schedule."

"With your watch schedule," he agreed mildly, "which puts you asleep between midnight and four, Juno between four and eight, and me — officially — nowhere at all." He glanced up at me, and there was no apology in it, only arithmetic. "I don't sleep on rivers. I thought I mentioned that."

He had mentioned it, actually, back in the gorges, in that conversation that had been half confession and half dare. I'd filed it under eccentricity. Now it filed itself under something else.

Juno came back from her third circuit. "Fuel's gone, spares are gone, food's mostly gone — they left us the rice and the tins nobody likes, which tells you how long they think we'll need to survive. Tools I hid, so we've got tools. And—" she patted the engine housing, almost tenderly, "—the Leech herself. Which is dry as a sermon but structurally sound, if anyone's asking."

"Who would get us fuel?" I asked, though I already knew the shape of the answer, the way you know the shape of a stair in the dark.

"Vey's forward camp is upstream past the falls," Elias said. "His main depot's downstream, two days' run, if the boat ran. The nearest human beings who aren't employed by Marcus Vey—" he paused, enjoying it, the absolute bastard, "—are the mission at San Telmo, four days overland through the monsoon country. Assuming the trails exist. Assuming the bridges exist. Assuming the monsoon hasn't eaten them yet, which, given the sky—"

I looked at the sky.

I'd been avoiding looking at the sky, the way you avoid a bill on the kitchen table, but it deserved looking. All night the clouds had been building in the west, stacked like dirty plates, and now the whole western horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The air had that pressed, held-breath quality I remembered from seven years ago, from the last morning of the Corvina expedition, when my father had stood on the bank and said *rain by noon, girl, mark me,* and been right by eleven hours, and dead by twelve.

"First wall hits today," I said.

"Before noon," Elias said. "Maybe sooner. The pressure's wrong for later."

Juno sat down heavily on the fuel drum that wasn't a fuel drum anymore, just a hollow promise with a bung hole. "So let me say the quiet thing out loud," she said. "We're marooned. Above the falls. In a boat with no fuel, no anchor, and no reason to believe anyone's coming back for us, because the man who hired us has demonstrated his entire position on our continued existence, and it's *line item.*"

"That's the quiet thing," I agreed. "Thank you for saying it quietly."

"There's a louder thing," she said, "but I'm saving it for when the rain starts."

* * *

We worked. It's what I know how to do with dread — put it somewhere useful. Juno stripped the engine and found, miracle of miracles, that Vey's men had siphoned the main tank but missed the carburetor bowl and the sediment trap, which between them held perhaps a liter and a half of petrol. A liter and a half. Enough to turn the engine over maybe thirty seconds. She sealed it back up like a woman preserving a relic.

I took inventory honestly, which is to say cruelly. Rice, eight days if we starved politely. Tinned fish, six cans, all the brand Juno calls *cat decisions.* Water, plenty — we were moored in the middle of the largest freshwater argument on the continent, though boiling it would eat our fuel, so we'd be drinking river and gambling on our stomachs. Rope, forty meters of the cheap stuff. The good rope was upstream, doing Vey's work now.

Tarpaulin, one, which by afternoon was going to be the difference between miserable and dead, so I re-rigged it over the midships well and double-lashed every point, and then lashed them again, because redundancy is just prayer with knots.

Elias, meanwhile, stood at the rail and read the river.

That's the only way I can describe it. He didn't look at the water the way I do — as surface, as threat gradient, as a road with opinions. He looked at it like a man reading letters from home. His eyes tracked the current seams, the boil lines over the drowned bars, and once, for a long moment, he stared at a stretch of flat brown water maybe two hundred meters downstream and said, quietly, to no one: "There used to be a church there."

"A church," I said.

"San Roque de las Aguas. My father's survey party anchored off it in 'sixty-one. He wrote about the bell." His voice did something complicated. "It's forty feet down now. The whole valley. When the dam schemes died, they'd already moved everyone out, and then the water never came — except here, where the tributaries backed up. So the villages drowned without a flood. Just slowly. Nobody left to pull the bells down."

I didn't answer. There was a version of me that wanted to ask him everything — about his father, about the manifest, about why a man with a legitimate grievance chose to travel toward it folded into a cargo hold smelling of bilge — and a version of me that trusted him exactly as far as I could throw the Leech, which is to say not at all, being made of ironwood and spite.

Both versions agreed on one thing, though, and it was this: he'd known about the fuel. Or he'd known enough to be unsurprised, and in my experience unsurprise is just guilt wearing a cleaner shirt.

"You could have warned us," I said, coming to stand beside him at the rail.

"Could I have?" He didn't look at me. "You'd have gone to Vey. Confronted him. He'd have smiled, denied it, and moved the siphoning up a night, and we'd be having this conversation in the water instead of above it." Now he turned, and his eyes were the flat grey-green of the river in rainlight. "Or you'd have turned the boat around, and we'd have lost the gorge window, and Vey strips the Corvina next month with nobody to stop him. I chose the outcome where we're angry, alive, and still ahead of him. Forgive me the arithmetic."

"Forgive me," I said, "if I don't grade your arithmetic highly when I'm the variable you sacrificed."

"You weren't sacrificed. You were spent." He almost smiled. "There's a difference. Spent implies you bought something."

"And what did I buy?"

"Time," he said. "Look downstream."

I looked. And I saw it — or rather, I saw what he meant, because the river was low. Lower than I'd ever seen it, lower than the old charts marked, the drowned bars showing their spines like the backs of sleeping animals, the falls ahead of us running thin and mean instead of wide and white. The drought months had pulled the curtain back on a riverbed nobody living had seen. Including, presumably, the approaches to a certain gorge, and a certain wreck, sitting in water shallow enough to reach.

"He thinks he's ahead of us," Elias said. "He took our fuel so we couldn't follow. But he's hauling a loaded launch through water his own charts say is impassable below two meters, and the gauges at the mission station read one-point-six and falling. He'll make six kilometers today and lose a prop doing it. We're stranded — but so is he. The difference is he doesn't know it yet."

"And when the monsoon comes?"

Something moved behind his face then, quick and dark, like a fish under ice.

"When the monsoon comes," he said, "everything changes. For everyone. Which is why we have until it does."

* * *

The first drops hit at twenty past eleven.

Not a drizzle. The monsoon doesn't introduce itself. One moment the air was hot and holding its breath; the next, a single drop the size of a grape struck the tarpaulin with a sound like a knuckle on a door, and then the door opened and the whole sky came through it.

Juno got her louder thing ready. "TOLD YOU," she screamed over the roar, pointing at the heavens with the wrench, and I decided to count it as prophecy rather than complaint.

We battened down. There's a rhythm to it — hatches, lashings, gear bagged and bagged again, everything that floats tied to something that shouldn't — and the three of us fell into it without discussion, and I noticed, distantly, through the hammering rain, that we'd stopped being three separate agendas and become a crew somewhere in the last hour without anyone calling a vote. Elias worked the high lines because he was tallest. Juno sealed the engine housing with tar and canvas because she loved that engine more than she loved most people. I ran the lashings, because knots are the one prayer I trust.

By one o'clock the river had risen a hand's width. By three, two hands, and the color of it had changed — gone from brown to the yellow-white of churned clay, carrying branches, a dead pig, half a fence post with the wire still on it, the whole watershed's inventory coming down at once.

We sheltered under the tarp in the midships well, three people and a boat, riding at nothing on a rising river above a falling world, and I did the thing I never do, which was talk.

"My father died in that gorge," I said. The rain was loud enough that saying it felt almost private. "Seven years ago. Vey was there. Vey was *leading* the salvage, and when the Corvina's hold shifted, he called the retreat from the safe side of the gap, and my father was on the wrong side of it, and Vey's stopwatch said the window was closed." I watched the water climb the bank. "The surviving crew swore an oath on the crossing back. Never return. Never salvage. Never take Vey's coin. I've kept it for seven years, and it took me one letter and some bad bookkeeping to break all three clauses in a single week."

Neither of them spoke. Good. They were learning.

"So understand me," I went on, and here my voice did something I didn't authorize, something rougher than I intended. "When we get off this sandbar, however we get off it — I'm not following Vey to beat him to treasure. I'm not chasing his gold or your birthright or anybody's justice. I'm going up that gorge because he left me stranded above the falls with a dry tank and a stolen anchor, and I intend to return the favor at volume."

Juno reached over and squeezed my wrist once, hard, which in her language is a sonnet.

Elias just nodded slowly, rain streaming off his jaw, and said: "Volume. Yes. I can work with volume."

And that was when Juno, who had been staring downstream with the fixed expression of a woman watching a bad idea approach, said:

"Ada. What's that on the water?"

I followed her finger. Downstream, where the drowned church lay under its forty feet of quiet, the flood had torn something loose from the far bank — a shape, dark and angular, tumbling end over end in the current, growing larger.

It took my eyes a full ten seconds to resolve it against the rain, and when they did, my stomach performed the sick drop the whole day had been rehearsing.

It was a crate. Stenciled, roped, half-swamped — one of ours, torn from Vey's overloaded launch somewhere upstream, come all the way back down the flood to find us. And lashed to it, riding the crest like a passenger, arms windmilling, was a man.

Vey's man. Alive. Screaming something the rain shredded before it reached us, and drifting — fast now, faster — directly toward the falls.
