## 9. Rain Like Iron

I want it on the record that I patched that hull with my own two hands while the sky tried to murder me, and nobody has ever once thanked me properly.

The rain came at dawn on the second day — not rain as I understood it, which is a polite thing that falls from clouds and makes you wet, but a wall of water with intent behind it. It hit the Leech like someone dropping a warehouse on her. Ada had us tied up in a side slough behind a shelf of rock, and even so the boat bucked against her lines like a horse that smelled fire.

"Juno!" Ada shouted over the noise. "Tell me something good!"

"She's taking water through the port seam," I called back. "About a bucket a minute!"

"Is that good or bad?"

"It's bad if we don't fix it and good if you enjoy bailing!"

That's the kind of conversation you have in a monsoon. Poetry dies out here. What survives is plumbing.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about the Leech: she was old when I bought her, and she was lying about her age even then. Forty feet of river scow with an engine I'd rebuilt four times and a hull that had been patched more often than a fisherman's trousers. I loved her the way you love a dog that bites — loyally, and with a healthy respect for the teeth.

The port seam had been weeping since the falls. We'd been pumping her every hour and telling ourselves it was fine, which is the official religion of river mechanics everywhere. But now the monsoon found the weak spot the way water always does, and by mid-morning we were bailing in shifts and I was standing waist-deep in the bilge trying to think.

You can't caulk a seam in a moving boat. You can't even caulk one in a still boat if the rain's hammering the deck above your head hard enough to loosen your fillings. So we did the only thing there was to do: we beached her.

Ada found the spot — a gravel shelf where a drowned creek mouth met the main channel, half-sheltered under an overhang of red stone. She ran the Leech's nose up onto it at full throttle, which I want noted was *my* suggestion and executed with *her* usual delicacy, meaning we all went into the bow rail like laundry in a drum.

Then it was work. Real work. The kind where you stop noticing you're soaked because you were soaked an hour ago and the concept of dry has become theoretical.

The hull patch was a ugly masterpiece. I had a sheet of spare plating, a bag of roofing tar that Juno-the-boat — that is, me — had bought off a widow in Port Meridian for less than it cost to carry, and a roll of canvas that had been destined for awnings in a better life. We ground the seam clean, laid canvas soaked in tar over the split, pressed the plate over that, and weighted the whole mess with sandbags and the engine's spare flywheel until it set.

Elias held the plate. That was the first surprise of the day.

I'd had him pegged as a stowaway with a good tailor — soft hands, city voice, the kind of man who'd never held anything heavier than a fountain pen. But he held that plate against the hull for forty minutes in driving rain without a single complaint, bracing his boots against the gravel, water running off his chin in ropes, and when I told him he could shift his grip two inches left, he shifted it two inches left, exactly, no argument, no "are you sure," none of the helpful backseat engineering that kills most first-time deckhands.

"You've done this before," I said, when we finally let him drop his arms.

"I've done worse for longer," he said.

That was all. But I looked at his hands after — blistered, bleeding at two knuckles, steady — and revised my ledger on Elias of the mysterious surname. Soft hands can learn. It's soft spines you have to worry about.

* * *

Water was the other problem, which sounds like a joke until you're living inside it.

River water in monsoon season is soup — silt, dead things, and whatever the flooded villages upstream contributed. Drink it and you'll trade a thirst for a fever. We had maybe six gallons aboard in sealed cans, enough for three days if we were careful, and the rain showed no sign of being a short-term guest.

So I built a still. Well — I built a still out of the Leech's copper fuel line, a dented kettle, a length of rubber hose, and a condensing coil made from the cooling jacket of a compressor I'd been saving parts from for years. Sacrilege, technically. But a mechanic who won't cannibalize her own boat in a survival situation isn't a mechanic, she's a museum curator.

The theory is simple: boil dirty water, catch the steam, cool it, drink what drips. The practice is that everything is harder in the rain, including boiling water, because your fire keeps drowning and your condenser coil keeps getting rained on, which warms it, which ruins the whole point.

We solved the fire problem with a driftwood lean-to and a tarp. We solved the coil problem by burying it in wet gravel, which sounds backwards but works — the gravel stays cooler than the air, and the steam gives up its heat to the stones instead of the sky.

By evening of the second day the still was producing. A cup every twenty minutes. Thin, flat, tasting faintly of copper and regret, but clean. I sat there watching it drip into our one enamel mug and felt a pride so pure and stupid it nearly made up for everything else.

"Juno," Ada said, crouching beside me, rain sheeting off her hood. "Did you just build a moonshine rig out of my fuel line?"

"Your fuel line was redundant."

"It was not redundant."

"The engine runs rich anyway. She won't miss it."

Ada looked at the drip, drip, drip of the mug filling, and then she did something I hadn't seen in seven years of working with her. She laughed. Not the sharp bark she used to keep people at distance — a real one, helpless, rain-soaked, gone in five seconds but real.

"If my father could see this," she said, "he'd say we were finally doing salvage right."

She didn't say anything else after that. Neither did I. Some doors you notice are open and walk away from, out of courtesy. But I logged it. First time since the gorge that she'd said his name out loud like it was a fact instead of a wound.

* * *

The third discovery of the week was Elias and his maps.

Not paper maps. He didn't have any. He had the kind of map you can't lose overboard, the kind drawn on the inside of a man's skull, and it turned out to be better than anything the survey office ever sold.

It started small. We needed to know whether the slough we'd sheltered in connected back to the main channel downstream, or whether we were going to have to winch the Leech backward over the gravel bar come flood-out. Ada was tracing the shoreline with her eyes, frowning, doing the arithmetic of current and gradient that she does when she thinks nobody's watching.

"The channel rejoins below the red bluff," Elias said. "Half a mile down. There's a cut through the cane on the left bank, but it floods early — you'd want to take it before noon tomorrow or wait two days for it to drop."

Ada stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"There used to be a ferry crossing there." He said it mildly, the way you'd mention a bus route. "My father kept a launch at the landing above the bluff. I grew up running this reach."

"Grew up—" Ada's voice did something complicated. "Nobody lives up here. Nobody has lived up here in twenty years."

"No," Elias agreed. "They don't."

And then he told us, sitting under the tarp with the rain hammering down like applause for a terrible play, about the drowned villages.

I'd heard of them the way everyone had — the dam schemes, the surveys, the government men with their brass instruments and their promises, the whole valley condemned on paper and then un-condemned when the money dried up, leaving a string of settlements that emptied out slowly, family by family, until the river took the rest. Everyone knew the story's shape. Nobody knew its details.

Elias knew the details. He knew which village had the stone church and which had washed out in '09. He knew where the old ferry cable was still anchored under the water — he'd shown us, actually shown us, pointing at a stretch of brown flood and saying *there*, and when the current eddied just so, we saw the ripple over it, faint as a scar under skin. He knew that the bend past Kilometer Forty-One ate boats in high water because of a submerged grove of ironwood, and that the safe line was closer to the far bank than any sane pilot would choose, and that his father had run that line two hundred times and never lost so much as a plank.

"Why do you know all this?" Ada asked. Not hostile. Hungry, almost, though she'd have denied it.

"Because my father wrote it all down." Elias looked out at the rain. "Letters. Twenty years of them. He was the Corvina's owner, and after she went down he spent the rest of his life writing to people who'd stopped answering. Someone had to remember the river. It turned out to be me."

I'm not a sentimental woman. My feelings mostly live in my hands. But I'll tell you what I thought, watching him: *this man has been carrying a drowned world around in his head, alone, for years, waiting for someone to tell it to.* And the two of us — the exile and the mechanic — had just been drafted as the audience.

Whether we'd been drafted for anything else was still an open question. I wasn't naive. He'd stowed away on our boat with a secret agenda; that's not nothing. But he'd also held a steel plate against a leaking hull for forty minutes in a monsoon without complaint, and in my experience that tells you more about a person than any confession.

* * *

Night of the third day, the rain eased to a steady drumming and we got our first hot food in two days — rice and tinned fish cooked under the tarp, eaten in silence, the way food gets eaten when everyone's too tired for conversation.

It was Juno's watch first — mine, I mean, my watch — which meant I sat in the bow with the tarp around my shoulders and listened to the river and tried not to think about how much I missed my workshop, my kettle, my chair, my entire previous life.

Around midnight, Ada came forward and sat next to me without asking. That was new too. Seven years ago she'd have stood apart, arms crossed, radiating *don't*. Now she just sat, shoulder almost touching mine, both of us watching the black water slide past the hull.

"He knows the river better than my father did," she said quietly.

"Yeah."

"That should frighten me."

"Does it?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "No," she said finally. "That's what frightens me."

Down aft, Elias lay on the cargo hatch with his boots on, asleep or pretending to be, one hand curled loosely around the oilcloth packet he never let farther than arm's reach. Whatever was in it — papers, letters, proof of something — he guarded it the way I'd guard a carburetor in a famine: absolutely, and without apology.

"Juno." Ada's voice dropped lower. "When Vey's men stripped our fuel, they also took the manifest case from the wheelhouse. Did you see?"

"I saw."

"Vey doesn't leave paperwork behind. He takes it so no one else can read it." Her jaw worked. "Which means whatever Elias is carrying matters enough to steal blind. And it means Vey's already upstream, already ahead, and every hour we sit here fixing my beautiful rotten boat, he's getting closer to—"

She stopped herself. To the wreck. To the Corvina. To the thing she'd sworn off seven years ago and was now being dragged toward at the speed of a patched hull and a flooded river.

"We'll catch him," I said. "He's got two launches and a crew of men who follow money. We've got a still, a tarp, and a walking atlas of drowned villages. I like our odds."

Ada snorted. "You would."

"Somebody on this boat has to be optimistic. It's a heavy job but I'm qualified."

She almost smiled. Then her eyes went past me, downstream, to where the rain had thinned enough to show the shape of the gorge walls against the lesser darkness — and I watched the smile die and something older take its place.

Because that's when Elias spoke from the hatch, without opening his eyes, in the mild voice of a man remarking on the weather:

"The water drops tomorrow afternoon. If we take the cane cut before noon, we can make the narrows by dark." A pause. "And the day after that, weather holding, we reach the reach where the Corvina lies."

The rain ticked on the tarp. Ada didn't move. Neither did I.

"And how," she said at last, very evenly, "do you know *that*?"

Elias opened his eyes. In the dark they caught what little light there was, and for the first time since I'd fished him out of a crate, he didn't look like a stowaway or a deckhand or even an heir. He looked like a man arriving somewhere he'd been traveling toward his whole life.

"Because my father drew me a map," he said, "and I've been reading it aloud to you for three days. Tomorrow I'll show you where it ends."
