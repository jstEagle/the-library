## 10. The Drowned Village

The rain let up around noon the way a debt collector lets up — briefly, and only because it intended to come back with reinforcements.

We found the village by accident, which is to say Elias found it on purpose and let us believe it was luck. Juno had been steering us along the north bank for two hours, squinting at a shoreline that kept changing its mind about where it ended, when Elias said, quietly, "There. Past the dead fig tree. There's a lane under that water."

"A lane," Juno repeated.

"Under the water. It runs up to a church."

"There's no church."

"There was," Elias said. "Before they raised the dam level twice and drowned it anyway."

I should explain something about this stretch of river, since I've spent seven years flying over it and pretending it didn't exist. Back when I was young enough to think my father's plans were the same thing as my own, there was a scheme — one of many — to dam the upper gorge and flood the valley. Engineers came with maps and slide rules and the unshakable confidence of men who have never had to argue with a river. They surveyed villages. They drew lines on paper showing where the water would rise. Then the money dried up, or the government changed, or both, and the schemes went into drawers. But some of the villages had already emptied out in anticipation, families paid off or frightened off, houses abandoned mid-sentence. And then, years later, one of the half-built dams actually got finished downstream, and the river backed up into places nobody had drawn lines around.

So there are drowned villages in these reaches. Whole streets standing in three feet of water, doors opening onto nothing, roofs caved in like eggshells. My father used to call them the river's pawnshops — places where the water held things people hadn't come back for.

I hadn't thought of that phrase in years. It came back to me whole, in his voice, as we poled the Leech down what had once been somebody's high street.

* * *

It was stranger than I remembered, or maybe I'd just been younger and less willing to be unsettled. The water was tea-colored and still as glass, and beneath it you could see the cobbles, furred green, running straight as a hymn. Doorways stood open on either side of us. In one house a table still sat where someone had left it, and on the table — I swear this — a bowl, upside down, as if whoever went had washed up first. In another, a child's chair floated gently against the ceiling, tethered by nothing, turning slow circles like it was waiting to be asked to dance.

"Nobody took anything," Juno said, and her voice had gone careful, the way it does when she's working through something she doesn't want to feel yet.

"They were told they'd be back," Elias said. "That's the cruelty of it. Compensation committees don't say *leave forever*. They say *temporary relocation*. They say the water will stop at the line." He poled us past a window where lace curtains hung in the current, waving like weed. "People leave their photographs. Their grandmother's dishes. You can't carry a whole life on your back, so you fold it up small and tell yourself you'll unfold it again in six months."

"And then?" I asked, though I knew.

"And then the water stops wherever it wants."

We tied off at the church, or what the church had become: a stone shell with its roof gone, its bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested it had considered falling for thirty years and never committed. The floor was above the waterline, barely — knee-high dry ground, mud-slicked, but dry. Dry is a currency up here. We'd have camped in a coffin.

Juno got a fire going on a sheet of salvaged tin, because Juno can get a fire going on a sheet of ice if you insult her competence enough times. Elias disappeared into the vestry and came back with an armload of things he did not explain: a rusted lantern, a bundle of candle stubs fused together like coral, and a ledger — an actual parish register, swollen with damp, its pages soft as cloth.

"You're robbing a church," I said.

"I'm reading a church," he said. "Different crime entirely."

* * *

Here's what I knew about Elias Reyes by that point, which wasn't much, and most of it sideways. He was the son of Tomás Reyes, who had owned the Corvina — owned her on paper, anyway, in the way rich men own ships they never sail. He'd grown up rich and illegitimate, which is a particular kind of poverty. Vey held papers saying otherwise; Elias held half a manifest and a grievance the size of the gorge. He navigated drowned landmarks like a man who'd memorized a map of a country that no longer existed. And he read — I'd noticed this on the third night upstream — he read everything, fast, the way hungry people eat.

What I didn't know until that afternoon in the drowned church was why he'd brought his father's letters at all. Who carries letters on a salvage run? A man carrying ammunition, rope, medicine — sure. But a oilcloth packet of correspondence, forty years of it, wrapped and rewrapped and carried from the coast?

A man carrying proof, I'd assumed. Evidence for the tribunal he dreamed of. But sitting there on the church steps with the rain starting again on the broken roof — a sound like slow applause — he unwrapped the packet and said, "My father wrote to a woman here. Every month, for twenty years. Her name was Beatriz. She was the wife of the man who kept this church's records."

"The witness," I said. "The one whose grave—"

"Whose grave we're sitting next to, yes." He nodded toward the churchyard, where a row of stones leaned out of the water like bad teeth. One of them, the newest-looking, bore a name I now recognized from Elias's angry recitations: Anselmo Duarte. Keeper of records. Witness to a will. Dead eleven years, in a village that had been dead twenty.

"He came back," Elias said. "Everyone else stayed away. He came back to be buried here, next to her. My father paid for the plot and couldn't bring himself to make the trip. Too much water over too many roads." He turned a letter over in his hands. The paper had gone the color of weak coffee. "I never met him, you know. My father. Not really. I met him at Christmas, and at funerals, and once on a boat where he pointed at things and told me their names as if naming them settled his debts. These letters are the only place he ever talked."

Juno looked up from her fire. "You want to read them. Out loud. Here."

"I want—" He stopped. Started again, which I would learn was rare for him. "I've read them alone a hundred times. It doesn't work. They're not doing what they're supposed to do. So yes. Out loud. Here. Where they were written *to*."

And because the rain had pinned us down, and because the alternative was watching Juno repair the bilge pump for the fourth time, and because — I'll admit it — I wanted to hear it, we said yes.

* * *

He read badly at first. Stiff, formal, skipping the dates like they embarrassed him. The early letters were exactly what you'd expect from a shipping magnate writing to his bookkeeper's wife: cargo tonnages, complaints about weather, a dry joke about a parrot that had learned to swear in three languages. Beatriz, whoever she'd been, had apparently answered with recipes and village news and, judging by his replies, a talent for telling him when he was being a fool.

Then, somewhere around the tenth letter, the tone shifted. The parrot died. The tonnage dropped. And Tomás Reyes began to write like a man talking to the one person who wasn't trying to get something from him.

*"Beatriz — Anselmo tells me you called my last letter 'a merchant counting his coins in the dark.' He is right and I am furious and I am writing this immediately so that you know I noticed. You are the only person alive who says such things to me. Everyone else says worse and means them as flattery."*

Juno snorted. Elias glared at her. I bit the inside of my cheek.

The letters got longer. He wrote about the Corvina — not as freight, but as a woman he'd married too young and loved inconveniently. He wrote about the dam schemes with a bitterness that surprised me; I'd always imagined men like him profiting from them. *"They have redrawn the maps again. Beatriz, they have drawn a blue line through your kitchen and called it progress. I have signed nothing and will sign nothing, but understand that my signature is worth very little against men who have bought the ink."*

"That's not true," I said, before I could stop myself. "He signed. Eventually. Everyone says he signed the second scheme."

Elias looked at me across the fire, rain dripping off the ruined roof between us. "Did everyone also tell you what he signed?"

"No. Everyone just said the word *signed* like it was a verdict."

He passed me the letter. The paper was soft enough that I handled it like a sleeping animal. The relevant passage was near the bottom, in a hand gone hurried:

*"Anselmo has drawn up the instrument. I have signed what must be signed to keep the Corvina out of their consortium's hands — a trust, Beatriz, a shield, not a surrender. If I die before the water comes, everything goes to my son. All of it. Anselmo witnessed it. He has the copy in his keeping, in the strongbox, under the floor of the vestry where we buried the good brandy during the drought year. Tell him the brandy joke still makes me laugh. Tell him I am not afraid of the water, only of the men who pour it."*

I read it twice. Around us the village stood in the water that had been poured, and the vestry floor was ten feet behind Elias, and the strongbox —

"You already know it's not there," I said. "Vey's people stripped these banks years ago."

"I know what Vey's people found," Elias said. "Silver plate. A strongbox full of ledgers they couldn't read and didn't keep. What I'm telling you is that my father hid the *real* copy somewhere else, and Anselmo Duarte knew where, and Anselmo Duarte is buried thirty feet from where you're sitting, and his headstone has an epitaph on it that my father paid extra to have carved, and for eleven years nobody in my family has been able to afford the trip up here to read it."

The rain filled the pause. Juno had stopped pretending to work on the pump.

"So read it," I said.

* * *

We went out into the churchyard together, which felt right and also slightly absurd, three soaked conspirators wading between graves to conduct literary criticism of a headstone. The Duarte stone was granite, better cut than its neighbors, the lettering still sharp under a skin of moss. Elias scrubbed it with his sleeve like a man polishing a wound.

The epitaph was short. It was in Portuguese, and Elias translated as he traced the letters:

*Here lies Anselmo Duarte,*
*who kept the records*
*and told the truth about them.*
*What he witnessed is written*
*where the water cannot reach.*

"Well," Juno said. "That's helpful."

"It's a riddle," I said. "It's a riddle on a grave."

"It's a message," Elias said quietly. "From one stubborn old man to whoever finally made the trip. My father chose the words. Anselmo approved them — there's a letter about the argument; they fought over whether *told the truth* sounded boastful." He was still tracing the carving, slower now. "*Where the water cannot reach.*"

"The deep vaults in Port Meridian," Juno offered. "Bank cellars. Archive."

"My father distrusted banks the way sailors distrust calm seas — as a trap that works by making you comfortable." Elias turned to look at the church behind us, at the vestry, at the flooded nave where the water stood perfectly still over the pews. "And he loved this river, and he understood it better than the engineers did. Water rises. That's all water does. Everything above the flood line is temporary." His voice changed — dropped, went almost reverent. "Everything below it is permanent."

I felt it land before I understood it. Below the waterline. Under the flooded nave. Somewhere in the dark under our boat, under the drowned street, in a place the looters' grappling hooks had dragged and dragged and never thought to search because who hides documents underwater—

"The crypt," I said. "Under the altar. It floods every wet season and drains every dry one, and nobody robs a place that's underwater half the year because paper doesn't survive—"

"Oilcloth survives," Elias said. "Oilcloth and lead and wax. My father shipped cargo in the wet season his whole life. He knew exactly what water ruins and what it merely waits behind." He was already moving, wading back toward the church, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the anger that usually ran him, not the watchfulness. Something younger. Hope, maybe, or its dangerous cousin.

"Wait," Juno said. "Wait wait wait. The monsoon's peaking. The water's *rising*, not draining. You want to dive into a flooded crypt in a gorge that's about to become a lake, while Vey's crew is somewhere upstream with rifles and a head start—"

"Yes," Elias said.

"That's not a plan, that's a mood."

"It's both," I said, and surprised myself, and surprised Juno more. Because here was the thing I wasn't saying out loud: I had spent seven years measuring my life in miles flown, in distances kept, in the careful geometry of staying away from this river and everything in it. And now I was standing in a drowned village holding a dead man's letter, and the letter said *everything goes to my son*, and the son was waist-deep in his father's river asking for help, and some part of me that I'd boarded up years ago was pounding on the boards.

Because it wasn't gold. I'd known that since the letters started. Forty years of correspondence and not one word about bullion — it was all names, jokes, grievances, love, a parrot, a recipe for salt cod that Beatriz had apparently corrected twice. The inheritance was a story. The story needed a witness. And the witness's grave had just handed us the last page.

Elias stopped at the vestry door and looked back at us, water streaming off him, and asked — actually asked, this man who'd stowed away on our boat and lied to us and navigated us through hell — "Will you help me look?"

Juno groaned the groan of the reasonable damned. "If we drown, I'm haunting both of you separately and at length."

I picked up the lantern. "The altar's this way," I said, because seven years ago I'd flown supply runs over this valley and once, drunk on youth and my father's stories, I'd landed here. Once. I'd stood in this church when it still had a roof, and an old man had shown me the crypt steps and said, *remember where these are, girl, the river keeps what it's given.*

I remembered.

Behind us, far downstream, faint under the rain, came a sound I'd know anywhere: an engine, working hard against the current.

Vey was coming.
