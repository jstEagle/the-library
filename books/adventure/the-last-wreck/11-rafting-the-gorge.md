## 11. Rafting the Gorge

There is a particular kind of silence a man makes when he has just agreed to do something stupid. It isn't the silence of fear, exactly. Fear is loud. It's the silence of arithmetic — the moment after the sum comes out wrong and you decide, for reasons you'll defend later with increasing volume, to carry the one anyway.

I did my arithmetic on the shingle bank above the drowned village, in the grey hour before the rain came back, while Ada Almeida stood over the Leech's gutted hull with a pry bar and an expression I can only describe as funereal with ambitions.

"We take the hull apart," she said. "Lash the planks into a raft. Run the gorge at night, high water, no lights."

"Run the gorge," I repeated, because a man should hear a sentence like that twice before his body commits to it. "The gorge."

"You've been saying its name like it's a saint," she said. "It's a hole in the earth with opinions."

"I know what it is. I've read every account of it. Including the one that ends with your father."

She didn't flinch. I'd learned by then that Ada Almeida's flinch was a private transaction, conducted somewhere behind her eyes and settled in full, and what you got on the surface was a kind of polished patience that could have been mistaken for calm by anyone who hadn't spent nine days on a boat with her.

"I know what's in your file, Elias," she said. "Your father's letters. The survey notes. You've read about this gorge the way priests read about God — secondhand, and with more certainty than is healthy."

"And you've seen it firsthand," I said, "which is worse."

"Neither of those is a plan."

"No," I agreed. "The plan is the raft."

* * *

The raft took us the better part of a day, and I will say this for the Leech: she died generously. Juno pried her apart plank by plank with the tenderness of a man undressing a lover he's angry at, muttering an inventory of grievances under his breath — the seized pump, the rotten transom, the fuel lines Vey's men had left chewed open like something had been at them. Every third plank he held up to the light, found it sound enough, and said, "She'd have wanted it this way," in a tone suggesting that what she'd have wanted was to sink with all hands and be done with us.

We built her skeleton-first: the longest planks as runners, crossbeams lashed with rope and, when the rope ran short, with the steel cable Juno had salvaged from the winch. Two crates lashed amidships as a platform for what remained of our supplies. A steering sweep made from the rudder post. Ada directed the whole thing without once raising her voice, which frightened me more than shouting would have. I had come to understand that Ada's quiet was a load-bearing quiet. Things rested on it. You did not want to be the thing that made it shift.

By late afternoon we had a raft that Juno described as "technically buoyant" and Ada described as "adequate," and I described, honestly, as a suicide note written in timber.

"Say what you actually think," Juno said, lashing down the last crate.

"I think my father owned a ship that died in that gorge," I said, "and I've spent my whole life reading about it from the safe side of paper. It seems fitting that I should finish the story from the unsafe side."

"That's not what you think. That's what you'd put in a letter."

I looked at him. He was a small, square man with forearms like mooring line and the permanent squint of someone who has spent thirty years being lied to by engines.

"What I think," I said, "is that if we don't run the gorge tonight, Vey's crew comes back down this river with rifles in three days, and we are standing on a shingle bank with a raft and no options. So I think the gorge is the better of the two deaths, and I think I'd like to stop talking about it."

Juno nodded slowly, the way men nod when they've heard the truth and resent it. "Fair," he said. "For a stowaway."

"Passenger," I said. "I've been promoted."

"You stowed away on a river boat, Elias. There's no promotion from stowaway. There's only tenure."

* * *

We launched at dusk, into the first slack of the rain.

I want to be precise about that rain, because I have read a hundred descriptions of monsoon and they are all lies written by people standing under awnings. This rain did not fall. It arrived — horizontally, in freight quantities, with a sound like gravel poured on tin. Within a minute I could not see Juno, who was sitting three feet from me. Within two, I could not see the bank we had just left, and I felt something in my chest unmoor and drift, because that bank had been the last thing in the world attached to dry land, and now it was gone, and so were we.

Ada took the sweep.

I had expected her to give it to me. I had, in fact, prepared a small speech about my knowledge of the channel, the survey charts, the drowned landmarks — my inheritance of paper, my half a manifest, my father's letters with their careful annotations of depth and current. I had imagined myself at the sweep, guiding us through the dark on the strength of a dead man's handwriting.

Ada took the sweep and said, "Sit behind me. Call the water."

"Call the—"

"You read the river to me. I steer. That's the division of labor, and it's not a negotiation."

"Why not you reading and me steering?"

"Because," she said, without any cruelty at all, "I've steered this river since I was nineteen, and you've read about it. And because if we hit something, I'd rather it be my fault than yours."

There was no answer to that, so I gave none. I knelt behind her in the rain with my face an arm's length from the water and did the one thing I could do: I read the gorge the way my father had taught me to read, in his letters, in his margins, in the furious annotations of a man who loved a river that would eventually kill him.

* * *

Here is what I know about the gorge, and I knew all of it before we entered, which is both the whole of my usefulness and the entire architecture of my terror.

The Corvina gorge is not long — a mile and a half, perhaps two, at this season drowned and swollen — but it is a throat, not a channel. The walls narrow to less than a boat's length in the middle reach, where the old survey called the Squeeze. The current through it runs at eight knots in flood, and the water does not flow so much as it is expelled. There are three hazards with names: the Teeth, a shelf of rock that shears off the left wall just below the surface and shreds anything drawing more than a hand's depth; the Squeeze itself; and the Cauldron below it, where the river drops into a reversal and holds whatever it catches, turning it, politely, forever.

My father's ship did not die in the Teeth or the Squeeze. The Corvina went into the Cauldron with a hold full of cargo and a crew full of arguments about whose fault the weather was, and the river kept her there for a day, turning her, and then took her apart at her leisure. My father died in the water above it, trying to run a line to the bank. Ada's father died below it, in the same flood season, seven years ago now, trying to do the same thing from the other side. Two men, one rope, one river. The gorge has a sense of economy.

"First landmark," I shouted, and my voice came out smaller than I intended, so I shouted it again. "There should be a leaning fig on the right bank — dead now, drowned, but the trunk stands. My father marked it. It means the Teeth are forty yards past it."

"Right bank," Ada said. "Fig. Forty yards." As if she were taking an order for parts.

I found the trunk by the sound of the water breaking against it — a different sound, a hollow one, wood against current — and I called it, and Ada leaned on the sweep, and the raft swung left, and something scraped along our starboard runner with a sound like a file drawn slowly across the world's largest tooth, and then we were past it, and Juno said a word I will not repeat, and the rain took the sound away.

"Second landmark," I called. "There's a bend where the left wall has a split in it — a vertical crack, tall as a church door. The old pilots called it the Priest's Door. It means the Squeeze. You have to be dead center. Not near center. Dead."

"Dead center," Ada said. "Naturally."

"Ada." I put my hand on her shoulder. The rain was so loud I had to put my mouth nearly against her ear. "My father's notes say the current in the Squeeze pulls left. Everything he ever wrote about it says left, left, left. He lost a barge there in '09. He said you have to aim at the right wall and trust the river to bring you back."

"Trust the river," she repeated. "That's the plan."

"It's his plan."

"It's a dead man's plan."

"Yes," I said. "It's the only plan anyone's ever made about this gorge that came from someone who'd been through it."

I felt her weigh that. In the dark, in the rain, with the roar of the throat ahead of us growing from a sound into a presence into a fact of the world, I felt the whole of Ada Almeida's grief shift its weight from one foot to the other.

"Aim right," she said. "Trust the river."

* * *

I will not pretend I remember the Squeeze clearly. Memory in extremis is a liar with excellent penmanship; it writes you a clean account afterward and you sign it because the alternative is admitting you were nothing but animal for ninety seconds.

What I have written down, and what I believe: the walls came at us like the closing of a book. The crack in the left wall — the Priest's Door — passed so close I could have touched it, wet stone sliding past my shoulder, and then the current took us exactly as my father had promised, a vast leftward hand, and Ada was already leaning into the sweep with her whole body, and the raft went sideways, and we hit — I don't know what, a wave standing up off the right wall, a cushion of reflected water — and for a moment we were not on the river but inside it, and then we were through, spat out the far end into the relative calm above the Cauldron, all of us breathing like landed fish.

"Landmark," Juno gasped. "Tell me there's a landmark."

"The Cauldron's next," I said. "It's not a landmark. It's a destination."

"How do we miss it?"

"You don't miss it. You go around it. The reversal only holds the center. The edges are fast but honest. My father's notes say — " I stopped. I had read this passage a hundred times. I had read it the night before, by candle, in the drowned village, while Ada pretended to sleep and Juno actually did.

"Say it," Ada said.

"He wrote: *The Cauldron forgives nothing and remembers everything. Hug the right wall until you smell the pine on the far bank, then give the river your whole heart and let it throw you.*"

"Let it throw you," Juno said. "That's the plan. That's the actual plan."

"That's the plan," Ada said, and I heard something in her voice that I had not heard in nine days on the water — not fear, and not the polished patience, but something underneath both, older, almost eager. "Elias. The man who wrote that. Was he a good pilot?"

"The best on the river, by every account."

"Then he'd have made it," she said, "if the water had been lower. That's what I've never been able to make peace with. It wasn't the river that beat him. It was the season. It was the year we came too late." She rolled her shoulders once, like a woman settling a pack. "Well. We're not too late. Hug the right wall. Juno — hold on to something that's holding on to something."

* * *

We went around the Cauldron so close that I could look into it.

I want you to understand what the Cauldron is at flood, at night, in the rain. It is a hole in the river the size of a chapel, and it turns. Not flows — turns, a slow colossal wheel of standing water, and the rain fell into it and the river fed it and it took both and gave back nothing but a sound like the earth breathing through its teeth. Things floated at its rim. A tree, whole, with its roots still clutching a piece of bank. Something pale and long that I decided was a log and have decided since to keep deciding.

Ada held us against the right wall with the sweep buried to the collar, and the current there was fast and honest, exactly as the dead man had promised, and it carried us along the rim of that turning dark like a hand along a banister, and then the pine smell came — sudden, absurd, green — and Ada gave the river her whole heart, and it threw us.

We went forty yards in a breath. We went airborne, briefly, all of us, the crates, the sweep, the rain. We landed in the long wave-train below and the raft did everything a raft should not do and survived doing it, and when we finally came out the bottom of the gorge into water that merely moved instead of argued, Juno was laughing — a high, unhinged, entirely sincere laughter that I have never heard from him since and never want to hear again, because I understood even then that it was the sound a man makes when he has looked at the bottom of his own fear and found it furnished.

Ada stood at the sweep with the rain running off her chin, and she was not laughing. She was crying, I think, though the rain made it a private matter, and she was steering, and she did not stop steering, and I understood that I was watching a circle close. Her father had died below that wheel, in this water, in this season. She had just come through it alive, at the sweep, on a raft made of the bones of a boat named for a fish that feeds on the dead.

She didn't speak for a long time. When she did, it was only: "Pine on the left. There's an eddy behind the point. We'll make camp there."

* * *

We lost half of everything at the Cauldron's rim — one of the two supply crates tore loose in the throw, and we watched it go into the wheel and come apart, and the river distributed our rice, our lamp oil, our spare line, and one of Juno's beloved tool rolls with the even-handedness of a tax collector. Juno watched it go with an expression I can only call grief, and then he inventoried what remained by feel, in the rain, and announced that we had enough for two days if we ate like monks and three if we ate like liars.

But we came out the bottom of the gorge in one night what would have taken Vey's launch two days to descend from the north — the falls route, the long way, the way a man goes when he trusts his engines more than his pilot. We had bought thirty-six hours, and we had paid for them in rice and rope and, on my part, several years of whatever composure I had left.

We made the eddy behind the pine point as the sky went the color of wet ash. Ada shipped the sweep. Juno lay down on the crates and was asleep before he finished lying down. And Ada stood at the stern a while longer, looking back upriver at the throat we'd come through, black now against blacker, the rain erasing it behind us like a debt being paid.

"My father's notes end at the Cauldron," I said. "Everything after that is blank pages. He never got the chance to write the rest."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Then we'll write it for him," she said, "and the next fool who reads it will think we knew what we were doing."

I laughed — the first time I'd laughed in nine days, and it came out cracked, and it was true.

We beached the raft in the eddy and dragged it into the trees, and I was wringing out my shirt when Juno, half-asleep, said the thing that stopped my hands.

"Smoke," he said. "You smell smoke?"

I did. Thin, upstream, wrong — woodsmoke where no woodsmoke should be, on a bank where no one should be, thirty-six hours before anyone could possibly have followed us down.

Ada came out of the trees with her head tipped, listening the way she listened to water.

"Campfire," she said quietly. "Somebody's camped above us on the right bank. And there's only one kind of fool who'd light a fire in this rain."

She looked at me, and I looked at her, and we both arrived at the same name without saying it.

Vey hadn't taken the long way after all. He'd sent someone ahead — overland, ahead of us, down the portage trail on the far side. And whoever it was had a fire, and a view of the river, and nothing between him and us but a mile of dark water and the rain.
