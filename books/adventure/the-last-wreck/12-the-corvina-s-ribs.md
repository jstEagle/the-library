## 12. The Corvina's Ribs

The river told us before the sky did.

We'd been running on Elias's drowned landmarks for two days — a church steeple that was now a snag, a ferry crossing marked by a rusted signpost standing in six feet of water like a man wading patiently toward shore. Juno had patched the hull twice more with canvas and prayer, and the Leech rode low and sullen, taking water at a rate we measured not in gallons but in buckets-per-hour, which is the only honest unit when your bilge pump has opinions.

It was mid-morning when the current changed. I felt it through the wheel first — a stutter, a hesitation, the river gathering itself the way a dog gathers before it barks. Then the sound came: a low, grinding hush ahead, like the gorge clearing its throat.

"Shallows," I said. "Or worse."

"Not shallows." Elias stood at the bow, one hand on the rail, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear but older, the way grief is adjacent to love. "The river splits here. The eastern channel drops over the falls. The western one —" he paused, and I watched him make himself say it — "the western one runs past the wreck."

I brought us around into the western channel without answering, because my hands knew the way even if my mouth didn't. That's the thing about a river you swore off. It stays in you. Seven years of flying over everything else in the world, and my hands still remembered which side of the current to favor above the Corvina's grave.

The low water was Vey's doing, or the drought's, or the dam engineers' — nobody upriver would claim it, and downriver they blamed each other in official-sounding memos. Whatever the cause, the result was a river shrunken out of its own banks, exposing things it had spent years politely covering. Sandbars like whale backs. Deadfalls stripped of bark, bleached white as bone. And there, where the channel bent hard against the southern cliff —

Ribs.

I want to tell you I was calm about it. I want to tell you the sight of her hit me like scripture, solemn and clean. But the truth is uglier and truer: my first thought was *there she is*, the way you think of an ex-lover across a crowded room, and my second thought was *she's smaller than I remember*, and my third thought was a cold little arithmetic of cargo holds and salvage windows that sounded so much like Marcus Vey in my own head that I had to grip the wheel until my knuckles went white to shut him up.

The Corvina lay heeled over against the cliff wall, half-exposed, her starboard side rising out of the waterline like the hull of some enormous beached animal. Seven years of flood silt had scoured off her this season instead of burying her deeper. Her name was still legible on the stern, the gilt gone green, the letters soft-edged with corrosion: *CORVINA*. Below them, someone — a fisherman, a kid with a knife, the river itself — had carved a crude cross into the planking.

My father wasn't under that cross. I'd made my peace with that particular uncertainty years ago, or something shaped enough like peace that I could carry it. He'd gone into the gorge below the falls with the ship's boat and four volunteers, trying to run a line to the wreck before the flood took their window. The line parted. The river kept what it took. We buried his boots, because the crew needed to bury something, and I have never decided whether that was mercy or cowardice.

"There," Juno said from the engine well. She'd come forward without me noticing, which meant she'd been watching me watch the wreck, which meant she was worried. She pointed with her chin. "Hold's intact. Look at the seam lines — no breach above the waterline. Whole starboard hold could be dry inside."

"Dry after seven years?"

"Silt packs tight. Silt is honest. If she flooded slow, the mud sealed her like wax." Juno shrugged, but her eyes were bright, and I recognized the brightness. It was the same look she got over a stubborn engine, the look that said *I can fix this, I can open this, I can know this*. "Ada. That's a sealed room down there. Nobody's opened it since she went down."

Nobody's opened it since she went down. My father had died trying to reach that sentence, and here it was, floating in low water, waiting for us like a letter with our name on it.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the cliff, in water so still it held the wreck's reflection perfectly, so that the Corvina seemed to exist twice — once rotting in the air, once whole beneath the surface, and I couldn't decide which version was the lie.

Elias didn't wait for the skiff. He was over the rail before I'd finished tying off, swimming the thirty yards with long, unhurried strokes, and I swear the man swam toward that wreck the way other people walk into their childhood homes — knowing where every door was in the dark.

Juno and I followed in the skiff with rope, lamps, crowbars, and the growing silence of two people who have both done the math on how much trouble this is.

The deck was a garden. Seven years of river had planted it — moss in green velvet sheets, a stunted tree growing straight out of the companionway, its roots prying at the planks like fingers working loose a knot. We crossed it carefully, reverently, though I noticed Elias crossed it fastest, like a man late for an appointment seven years overdue.

The hold hatch was under the tree. Its padlock had rusted to lace. One blow of the crowbar and it crumbled, and the hasp lifted, and the smell came up out of the dark — river mud and old wood and something sweeter underneath, tallow and tar and time.

Elias went down the ladder first. I followed with the lamp, and the light swung across the hold, and I stopped breathing.

Not gold. Not first, anyway. Crates. Hundreds of them, stacked in the careful geometry of a conscientious mate, lashed and chocked and labeled in faded ink. And between the crates, packed in oilcloth and soldered tins, the smaller boxes — the ones my father's expedition notes had called "the specie," the bullion, the payroll gold of a mining concern that had never lived to see its mine.

But it was the labels that stopped me. Because they weren't just cargo manifests. Under the shipping marks, in a clerk's careful hand, were annotations — dates, signatures, transfer records. And Elias had gone straight to a specific crate near the hold's center, as if he'd memorized its position from a document he'd only ever seen half of, and pried the lid, and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and bound with tarred string.

He unwrapped it with hands that shook.

Papers. Dozens of them. Letters, ledgers, a leather folio swollen with damp but legible, and on its first page, in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea:

*Last will and testament of Augustin Reyes Ferro, owner, the steamship Corvina.*

Below it, a second signature block. A witness line. And a name I didn't recognize, dated three days before the Corvina sailed on her last voyage.

"That's him," Elias said quietly. "Tomás Braga. The witness. My father's engineer. He signed the true will aboard ship, three days out of port. The will that leaves everything — the estate, the shares, the salvage rights — to me." His voice did something complicated on the last word. "Vey's papers name a different heir. A nephew who never existed, invented with forged documents filed while the Corvina was presumed lost with all hands. While *I* was presumed lost."

"You were aboard?" I said. "When she sank?"

"I was eleven." He said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy. "My father sent me ashore at the last port with the cook, who owed him a debt and hated boats. I screamed the whole way down the gangplank. I wanted to sail with him." He looked around the hold, at the crates, at the dark water seeping slow and patient through the lowest seams. "The river decided otherwise. For twenty years I thought I'd been spared. Now I think I was left behind on purpose — the only copy of the inheritance that couldn't be forged."

I looked at the folio in his hands. The true will. The witness signature. And beneath those, when he turned the pages with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping child, correspondence — letters from Tomás Braga to a lawyer in Port Meridian, letters describing the forgery, naming the men paid to file it, naming—

"Marcus Vey," I read aloud, because my brain apparently wanted to hear it in the open air. "He was the filing agent. Twenty-two years old. He processed the false estate papers himself."

"He built his career on my father's grave," Elias said. "And then he spent twenty years buying up river salvage licenses, waiting for the water to drop far enough to let him back into this hold. He doesn't want the gold, Ada. The gold is the bait. The *papers* are the problem. Every document in this folio proves his foundation is fraud. He needs these burned more than he needs the bullion."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, Juno's boots crossed the deck, keeping watch, and the sound was the most comforting thing in the world.

I should have felt righteous. Instead I felt the cold arithmetic again, uninvited, whispering in a voice that sounded reasonable and sounded like mine: *bullion at today's prices, times the number of tins visible from here, minus the cost of a salvage crew, equals a life where you never have to fly a medevac through a storm-fringed valley again, equals a life where nothing hurts.*

Seven years I'd been measuring my life in miles flown, patients delivered, debts serviced. Here was a room full of enough money to buy back all the years at once. All I had to do was take it and not ask whose it was. All I had to do was be the kind of person who could stand in this hold and see treasure instead of a tomb.

Because that's what it was. I made myself look properly. The crates weren't just cargo. They were a dead man's plans — the mine that never opened, the payroll for workers never hired, the manifests in a clerk's hand for a future the river had cancelled. My father hadn't died chasing this gold. He'd died chasing the *claim* — the legal right to recover it honestly, for the families of the lost crew, per the articles he carried. He'd believed the paperwork mattered more than the metal. I'd spent seven years telling myself he was a fool for it.

Standing in the hold, holding the lamp on a dead man's will, I finally understood he'd been the only sane man on the river.

"Ada." Elias was watching me. He had that unnerving habit of seeing exactly which door in me stood open. "Whatever you're deciding — decide it out loud. I've watched people go quiet over money. Quiet is where it goes wrong."

"I'm deciding nothing," I said. "I'm carrying papers."

"And the tins?"

"The tins stay put until we know who's coming up that channel behind us." I turned for the ladder, then stopped. "Elias. Your father's will. Does it say anything about salvage crews? About the men who died trying to raise her?"

He was quiet a moment. "Article nine," he said. "Recovery costs first, then equal shares to the families of any crew lost in recovery." He almost smiled. "Your father read it to us at dinner, the night before he took the ship's boat into the gorge. He said it was the fairest clause he'd ever signed."

Of course he had. Of course my father had found the one honest paragraph in a hundred pages of lawyer's timber and made it his gospel.

I climbed the ladder into the light, and the river lay flat and silver around the wreck, and for one whole minute I allowed myself to believe we'd won the race.

Then Juno, at the bow, lowered her binoculars very slowly and said, in a voice carefully drained of everything:

"Smoke. Upstream bend. Cooking fire, maybe two. And a glint on the water that I'd bet my last wrench is a launch's windshield."

They'd cut the falls shortcut close, but Vey had never been a man to arrive second when he could arrive armed.

Behind us, down in the hold, the Corvina waited with her ribs full of proof and gold, and the monsoon clouds were building black over the gorge rim like a debt coming due.
