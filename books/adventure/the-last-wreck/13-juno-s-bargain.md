## 13. Juno's Bargain

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head, the one where every debt and favor is written in grease pencil — that I did not go looking for temptation. Temptation came looking for me, wearing a dry coat and holding a tin of biscuits, which in monsoon country is roughly equivalent to wearing a halo.

We'd made camp in the lee of a rock shelf above the drowned village, Ada sleeping like someone who'd finally set down something heavy, Elias sitting watch with his father's letters wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. The rain had eased to that steady whisper it does sometimes, as if the sky were only catching its breath before the next argument. And downriver, around the bend where the water went glassy and brown, I heard an engine.

Not our engine. Ours was a two-stroke with a personality disorder that I'd rebuilt four times and could identify blindfolded, by feel, by smell, by the particular way it cleared its throat before dying. This engine was healthy. This engine had been maintained by someone who got paid regularly. This engine belonged to Marcus Vey.

I should have woken them. That's the version of events I tell first, when I tell it at all: I heard the launch, I woke the others, we hid, we survived. But that's not what happened, and this chapter doesn't work unless I'm honest about the order of things.

What happened is that I stood there in the rain with my hand already full of signal flares — Vey's flares, left behind in the gear his men abandoned, red as sin — and I did arithmetic.

Here's what you need to know about me. I am not brave and I am not clever, not the way those two are. Ada steers by some internal compass that points at her dead father half the time, and Elias navigates by drowned villages and family grudges, and both of them can make suffering look principled. Me, I keep the Leech alive. I know the price of gaskets and the weight of fuel and exactly how many days of diesel we're short of getting home. I think in numbers because numbers don't lie to you, and because for eleven years they've been the only thing on that boat that never has.

And the numbers said this: the Leech was holed in two places I'd patched with canvas and prayer. We were out of fuel, most of our food had gone into the gorge during the rafting, and the documents Elias carried were worth nothing to me personally except the pleasure of watching a rich man lose. Meanwhile Vey was coming upriver with a launch, a winch, and — I'd counted, back at the loading dock, back when I still thought we were hauling freight — eleven rifles.

Eleven rifles versus one wrench. I've done that math before. It's how my last crew ended.

So when the voice came out of the dark — not Vey himself, one of his men, a big quiet fellow standing at the edge of the firelight with his hands visible like he'd read a book on approaching strange dogs — I didn't scream. I listened.

"Juno." He knew my name. Of course he knew my name; Vey buys information the way other men buy drinks. "Mr. Vey wants you to know he remembers good work when he sees it. Says a mechanic who kept the Leech alive seven years on scrap deserves better than scrap wages."

"I get paid," I said.

"You get paid in promises." He set the biscuit tin on a rock between us, and on top of it, a small oilskin pouch that clinked with the specific dull music of coin. "Third of the salvage. Cash, on delivery. All you have to do is nothing. Don't wake them. When we come for the wreck at dawn, be asleep. Or be awake and looking the other way — Mr. Vey's flexible, for a man who keeps ledgers."

"And them?"

The man shrugged, and the shrug said everything the contract didn't. *Held for the monsoon. Disposed of.* I'd heard Vey use that phrase at the forward camp and thought it meant paperwork.

He melted back into the trees. I stood with the pouch in my hand and the rain coming down and the engine noise fading upstream toward the wreck site, and I did the thing I'm least proud of, which wasn't taking the money. Taking the money was almost honest. The shameful thing was that I sat down by the cold fire and I *thought about it*. Properly. With lists.

A third of the Corvina's cargo. Even half of what the manifest promised, even after Vey's cut, that was more money than the Leech had earned in its whole rotten life. Enough to rebuild her from the keel up. Enough to buy a workshop with a roof that didn't leak, a lathe, a stock of parts, maybe enough to hire somebody young and teach them everything I knew so it wouldn't die with me. I could see it so clearly it hurt: clean benches, ordered shelves, an engine hoist, no more scavenging seals off dead boats in the rain.

That's how temptation gets you, if you want to know. Not through greed. Through tiredness. It comes to you when you're forty-one years old and soaked to the sternum and it doesn't offer you gold, it offers you *rest*. It says: you've patched enough. You've gone without enough. Somebody owes you, and here at last is somebody willing to pay.

I looked over at Ada, asleep with her mouth open, one hand curled near the knife she never actually slept without. Seven years I'd watched her measure her life in miles instead of years, flying away from anything that might hold still long enough to love her, all because of a wreck and an oath and a man named Vey who'd turned her grief into his business plan. And here I was, holding the price of helping him finish the job.

Elias stirred at his watch post. In the firelight he looked younger than he was, which I'd learned was a trick — the soft face of a man who'd grown up rich and then grown up hard, in that order. He was clutching those letters like scripture. Half a manifest, he'd said. Proof of a theft. A name he'd spent his whole life being denied.

And I thought, with a clarity that felt like a wrench slipping and skinning my knuckles: *Vey isn't offering me a third of the treasure. He's offering me a third of theirs.*

Because that's what it was, once Elias told us what he was. Not salvage. Not found money. A stolen inheritance, and we weren't salvors anymore, we were witnesses. And a witness who sells out for coin isn't a salvor either. He's just a second thief with better tools.

I got up. I walked to the river's edge, where the water was rising already, the monsoon's first real push, licking at the rocks like something tasting ahead to a meal. I held the pouch over the water.

And then — because I am who I am, because eleven years of keeping books in grease pencil leaves marks — I opened it first. Counted it. Forty coins, heavy gold, each one probably worth a month of Juno's wages. I counted them because some part of me wanted to know exactly what honesty was going to cost, and I'll carry that little piece of shame to my grave alongside the rest.

Then I threw the whole pouch, tin and all, arcing out into the brown flood. It went in without a splash worth mentioning. The river took it the way it takes everything, without comment, without gratitude. Somewhere downstream it's sitting in the silt next to a drowned village, and honestly? Good company.

* * *

The problem with throwing away a bribe at midnight is that dawn still comes, and dawn came with engines.

I woke the others — properly this time, hand on shoulder, no theatrics — and we got Ada's plan moving: hide the documents, scatter along the high shelf, let Vey's men take the empty camp. What I didn't do, what I stood there choking on while Ada marked sightlines with her eyes and Elias buried the oilcloth packet under a cairn of stones, was say anything about the night visitor.

You'd think after the river took the money I'd feel clean. I didn't. Throwing it away paid a debt to nobody but my own reflection. The fact remained: Vey's man had come to *me*, known my name, offered terms, and I'd sat alone in the dark doing sums instead of raising the alarm. Whatever I decided afterward, for one hour I'd been the kind of person who could be bought, and the price had merely come back too expensive.

Vey's launch rounded the bend at first light, riding low and mean, eleven rifles glinting along the gunwale like a row of black teeth. They took the camp in ten minutes, professional and bored, turning over our bedrolls, kicking apart the cold fire. Vey himself stepped ashore last, dry under an umbrella one of his men held, which tells you most of what you need to know about Marcus Vey: even his sheltering was somebody else's job.

He talked. Of course he talked. He offered Ada partnership with her father's name in his mouth like bait on a hook, and she refused him with a flatness I've only heard from her twice, both times involving graves. He ordered us held — tied at the rock shelf above the waterline, "for the monsoon to dispose of," and this time I understood the paperwork reference perfectly.

But here's the detail that mattered, the one I kept turning over while they lashed my wrists with wet rope that would only tighten: Vey never mentioned the visit. Never looked at me sideways, never gave the little nod that says *you took the deal*. Which meant either his man hadn't reported back yet — or I was still, officially, an open account. An asset with a price, waiting for confirmation.

An asset. Held captive, wrists bound, watching the river rise toward the wreck that killed my captain's father-figure, and the worst wound of the whole day was that word. Asset. Because he'd been right about one thing: I *had* considered it. The rope around my wrists was almost a relief, punishment pre-paid, the ledger balanced by force.

Ada worked her bonds loose within the hour — of course she did; the woman can't sit still even trussed — and started whispering escape geometry to Elias, who was feeding Vey's navigator false coordinates with the serene face of a choirboy selling indulgences. Neither of them asked me anything. Neither of them looked at me with anything but the ordinary trust of people who've rafted a gorge together, and somehow that was worse than suspicion. Suspicion I could fight. Trust I had to earn back, and they didn't even know I owed it.

Night fell. The rain came back like it had a grudge. And I lay there on the wet stone listening to the gorge fill up — the Corvina's ribs somewhere below us, Vey's winch groaning as his crew rigged the lift, the river climbing a hand's width every hour — and I decided.

Not to escape. Ada had that handled. I decided to talk.

When her fingers finally freed my hands — she went down the line, Elias first, then me, working by feel in the dark — I caught her wrist before she could move on. She went very still. Ada goes still like a cat does, all attention, no sound.

"Before we do this," I whispered, "there's something you need to know. About last night."

Elias shifted closer. The rain filled the pause.

"A man came into camp. Vey's. While you slept." My voice came out lower than I wanted, so I made myself say the rest louder, into both their faces, because a confession whispered is a confession you can later claim was misheard. "He offered me a cut. Third of the salvage, cash on delivery. All I had to do was stay asleep when they came for you."

Ada didn't move. Elias didn't move. The river moved enough for all three of us.

"I sat here for an hour," I went on, because apparently I'd lost all sense of mercy toward myself, "doing the math. Workshop. Dry roof. Rest. I want you to understand it wasn't a hard sum. It was easy. It was the easiest math I've ever done, and that's what scared me." I swallowed. "I threw the money in the river. Tin and all. But that's not the point. The point is he came to *me*, and I listened, and I didn't wake you, and I've been carrying it since midnight like a stone in my boot. So now you know. If you want to leave me tied, leave me tied. If you want to leave me here entirely—" my voice cracked, and I hated it, and I kept going anyway, "—I'd deserve it."

Silence. Rain. Somewhere below, the winch groaned again, hauling at a dead ship's bones.

It was Elias who spoke first, and what he said surprised me more than forgiveness would have. "Did you count it?"

"What?"

"The money. Before you threw it in." His eyes caught the faint gray of the water-light. "Did you count it?"

"...Forty coins," I admitted. "Gold."

Something that was almost a smile moved across his face. "My father used to say you can judge a man by what he counts. Vey counts everything and values nothing." He glanced at Ada. "She counts what matters. You counted it *before* you let it go. That's not a thief's arithmetic, Juno. That's a man pricing his own soul and finding it not for sale."

"That's a generous reading," I said.

"I'm a generous heir," he said. "Ask anyone. Well. Ask Vey."

Ada still hadn't spoken. She was looking at me the way she looks at an engine that's making a new noise — not angry, not afraid, just *listening*, running diagnostics. Then she reached out and gripped my forearm, hard, mechanic to mechanic, the grip that means *hold fast*.

"You woke us," she said. "Last night, when it counted — no. Before that." Her jaw worked. "Eleven years, Juno. You've never once lied to me about the boat. Not about how bad it was, not about what it would cost, not about when it was dying. I built my whole life on knowing one person who tells the truth about broken things." She squeezed harder. "Don't you dare stop tonight."

"I threw the bribe in the river," I said, because apparently I needed the last word even in my own redemption.

"I know," she said. "I heard the splash."

"You were awake?"

"I'm always awake when it matters." She let go of my arm and turned toward the water, toward the rising flood and the wreck beneath it and the window closing by the hour. "Now listen. The river gives us until mid-morning, maybe less. Vey's rigging his lift on the seaward side, which means his attention, his rifles, and his best men are all pointed down at the hold. Juno—" and here she turned back, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen since before the gorge, something that might have been the beginning of trust spending itself freely again, "—how long to kill that winch?"

I felt the question land in my chest like a dropped tool. Kill the winch. Sabotage. The one crime on this river that couldn't be rationalized, priced, or thrown in the water afterward. The one act that put me past bribery and straight into war.

I thought about the workshop with the dry roof. I let it go — really let it go, watched it sink the way the coins sank, no splash worth mentioning.

"Give me twenty minutes and something that passes for a crowbar," I said, "and that winch will never turn again."

Ada smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman with a plan, and the plan had teeth.

"Then let's go ruin Marcus Vey's ledger," she said, "permanently."

Below us, the river rose another inch, patient as arithmetic, and the Corvina waited in the flood with all her secrets — and somewhere on that wreck, I'd learn soon enough, waited the one thing none of us had bargained for.
