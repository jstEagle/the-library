## 14. Vey Arrives

I have always believed that a man's character is revealed by how he arrives. Some men arrive apologetically, hat in hand, already negotiating their own diminishment. Some arrive loudly, announcing themselves to the trees and the fish and anyone else who might be impressed. I arrive on time, with a ledger, and I have never once apologized for it.

The launch came around the last bend of the gorge at 0640, forty minutes ahead of schedule, which pleased me more than it should have. Punctuality is the one virtue I permit myself, and like all my virtues it is mostly arithmetic. The river was running brown and fast with the monsoon's first real week of rain, and the Corvina sat in the shallows below the cliff shelf like a whale that had died mid-sentence — ribs up, hold open, exactly where my charts and my patience had said she'd be.

And there, on her spine, three figures standing very still in the rain.

Almeida. The mechanic. And the boy.

Well. Not a boy. I'd read the file twice on the way upriver — Vey does not travel without files; sentiment travels free, information pays for its berth — and Elias Rennick-Cardoso was twenty-nine years old, illegitimate, educated past his station, and in possession of half a manifest that should have been ash twenty years ago. Half a manifest. That was the detail I kept turning over like a coin with a flaw in it. Forged papers burn. Originals have a way of surfacing, usually at the worst possible moment, usually held by someone with nothing left to lose, which makes them dangerous in inverse proportion to their net worth.

I stood at the rail while my men brought the launch alongside the wreck's exposed keel, and I watched Ada Almeida watch me, and I confess the old ledger opened itself unbidden to the page marked *assets*.

Seven years ago she was the best pilot on the coast and the second-best swimmer in her father's crew — the best being her father, whose record I now intended to break posthumously. She flew medicine into places that eat airplanes. She kept no partners, no debts of gratitude, no forwarding address longer than a season. In my trade we call that kind of person *clean*. Clean assets are rare. Most people are collateralized to the eyebrows — mortgages, marriages, mothers — but Almeida owed nobody anything, which meant she could be bought outright or lost outright, and either transaction would be tidy.

She had one sentimental flaw. I'll come to it.

"Marcus." She said my name the way you'd spit out a seed. "You're late for your own funeral."

"I'm early," I said. "For once, everyone else is punctual too. Look at us. A family reunion with better weather than the last one."

The mechanic — Juno, small, wiry, hands like a bundle of tools someone had tied together with rope — shifted her weight so she stood between me and the hold. Protective. Predictable. People protect what they've decided matters, and then they become legible. Almeida herself didn't move. She stood with the rain running off her chin and looked at me with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it on her father's face, once, across a campfire in this same gorge: not hatred. Assessment. She was pricing me.

Good. I respect a counterparty who reads the contract before signing.

"You siphoned my fuel," she said.

"I rationalized my fuel," I said. "There's a difference. You'd have spent it going downstream eventually, and downstream is where the money isn't. Consider it a forced investment. I'm told those build character."

"You left us above the falls in a leaking boat."

"I left you above the falls in a boat, during the dry week, with a mechanic capable of patching a hull and a stowaway capable of reading water. If I'd wanted you dead, Almeida, I'd have done it properly, and you know it. Dead crews generate questions. Marooned crews generate grudges, and grudges keep quiet if you pay them off." I gestured at the wreck behind her. "Speaking of which."

* * *

Let me tell you about the Corvina, since everyone in this story insists on telling me about her as though I hadn't financed two expeditions to find her before either of them was born.

She went down in '09 carrying — officially — machine parts and salt pork. Unofficially, per the manifest that half of exists and half of which I spent eleven years and four bribes reconstructing: specie, a strongbox of documents belonging to the Cardoso estate, and enough uncut stones to make a modest province change its mind about taxation. Her owner, Tomás Cardoso, drowned with her. His will, filed eighteen months later, left everything to a nephew with excellent penmanship and no blood relation worth mentioning. The nephew sold the salvage rights to a consortium. The consortium's managing partner was a younger man named Marcus Vey, who learned within a year that the will he'd purchased his fortune through was forged, and who made the entirely rational decision to treat this information as a business opportunity rather than a moral crisis.

A forged will is only a crime until you control the estate. After that it's a founding document.

The problem, historically, has been the gorge. The Corvina wedged herself into a slot that floods nine months of the year and lies in low water the other three, and every attempt to reach her has ended in drowned divers, snapped cables, and one very memorable lawsuit. But the dam schemes upstream have changed the arithmetic. This monsoon will be the last that leaves her reachable. Next year the gorge becomes a lake bed, the wreck becomes a rumor, and everything inside her becomes legally indistinguishable from nothing.

Which means the documents in her hold — the true manifest, Cardoso's correspondence, whatever proof the old man carried against his own murder-by-paperwork — become worthless as evidence and priceless as leverage. Burn them, and the forgery stands forever. Keep them, and they're a sword over the head of every heir and tribunal that might ever come asking.

I intend to burn them. Obviously. But I intend to *own* them first, because a thing you can destroy is only truly yours when you've decided not to.

All of which brings us back to the woman dripping on the wreck's spine, and the sentimental flaw.

* * *

Her father, Emilio Almeida, died in this gorge seven years ago trying to claim the Corvina under a salvage license I had quietly arranged to be revoked mid-expedition. I want to be precise here, because precision is the courtesy I extend even to people I ruin: I did not kill him. I made his claim illegal while he was standing on it, and the river did the rest, and I have slept adequately every night since. There is a version of events where that makes me a monster. There is another version where it makes me a man who understands that rivers don't need help killing people, and laws don't need help ruining them, and all a competent operator has to do is stand where the two meet and collect.

Emilio's flaw was ambition. He wanted the Corvina so badly he stopped checking his lines, his air, his weather. He wanted it the way I want things, which is to say completely, and the gorge collected him for it.

His daughter inherited the ambition and, being smarter, buried it. Seven years of flying medicine and refusing river work. Seven years of building a life designed so that nothing on it could be taken from her. I have watched her do it from a distance with something adjacent to admiration. It is the most expensive kind of safety — the kind that costs you everything you might have been — and most people lack the discipline to pay for it.

But she came upriver anyway. Triple pay, no manifests, and she signed. Which tells me the buried thing is still down there, alive, patient, waiting for exactly the shovel I have just handed her.

So I made the offer standing on her father's grave-water, and I made it well, because I meant it.

* * *

"Here's the shape of it," I said, and I stepped down onto the wreck's keel so we could speak without shouting over the rain, which is a small thing, but negotiations are built of small things. My men fanned along the rocks with their rifles slung — visible, not aimed. Theater. But theater works, or I wouldn't budget for it.

"The hold's intact. You've confirmed it, I can see it in your face. Inside is roughly four hundred thousand in specie, the stones, and a document cache that is worth more than both together to precisely one person." I nodded at the stowaway. "Him. To everyone else on earth, including the two of you, it's paper. Paper burns. Specie doesn't."

Elias Rennick-Cardoso had the look of a man doing arithmetic he hated. Good. Let him hate it. Hating arithmetic is how people end up poor.

"Split three ways, the take comes to something north of a hundred thirty thousand each, plus my compliments, plus the satisfaction of watching me walk away from the documents untouched." I let that land. "Or split two ways, minus my compliments, minus the satisfaction, minus your chances of surviving a monsoon in a patched hull with no fuel and no allies. I've done the numbers both ways. I prefer the first. I suspect you prefer living."

"And the documents?" Almeida asked.

"Burned. Sealed, witnessed, burned. The estate passes to whoever holds legal title, which after next month is a lake. Everyone walks away rich and the past stays dead, which is where it lives best."

"You'd forge the burning," Juno said. Flat. Certain.

I turned to her and gave her the truth, because the truth disarms people who expect lies. "Of course I would. I'd forge it beautifully. But here's what you're missing, mechanic: I don't need to. Once the specie is out and the gorge floods, the documents are unreachable by anyone, including me. Burning them is a gesture. Leaving them to drown is a policy. I'm offering you the policy."

It was, I want the record to show, a genuinely good offer. Fair margins, clean exits, no hidden clauses beyond the ones inherent in dealing with me, which any honest person prices in automatically. I have ruined perhaps forty careers and I have never once cheated a partner who read the terms. Cheating the illiterate is beneath me; there's no craft in it.

Almeida looked at me for a long time. Rain came down the collar of her jacket and she didn't flinch from it, and I thought: *there she is. Emilio's eyes, Emilio's stubbornness, none of Emilio's carelessness.* The gorge took the careless one. It would have to work harder for this one.

"No," she said.

Just that. No speech, no moralizing, no recitation of grievances, which I had budgeted five minutes for and would have enjoyed. Just no, delivered the way you decline a second helping.

"For the record," I said, "that's a bad decision, financially."

"It's not a financial decision."

"There it is." I almost smiled. "Your father said something similar, the night before the river took him. Word for word. *It's not about the money, Marcus.* And then he went down after the money, because it's always about the money underneath, and the ones who claim otherwise are simply worse bookkeepers." I shook my head. "He wasn't a bad man, Emilio. He was an ambitious man with sloppy risk management. The river doesn't distinguish. Neither do I."

"You're wrong about him," she said quietly, "and you're wrong about me, and you're wrong about what's in that hold. But mostly you're wrong about the river. It distinguishes fine. It just takes its time."

There was something in the way she said it — not defiance, not fear, *certainty*, the certainty of a person holding information like a card face-down — that made the ledger in my head flip forward several pages and flag a line item I hadn't priced.

They'd been at the wreck since yesterday. My charts said the hold's document chest was aft, under the collapsed spar deck, a six-hour job for a trained crew with proper tackle. They'd had maybe ten hours, exhausted, underfed, in the rain.

What if they hadn't waited?

What if the chest was already empty, and the oilcloth-wrapped something in the bottom of their patched boat was already gone, already hidden, already moving downriver in the belly of the flood where my launch couldn't follow and my divers wouldn't survive?

I looked at Almeida's calm face and understood, with the particular cold pleasure I reserve for discovering I've been outmaneuvered, that I was negotiating for a strongbox I might already own the location of — or might never see again — and that she knew I couldn't tell which.

"Search them," I said to Ruiz. "Gently. Then put them in the camp tent. Feed them — I don't starve assets, it degrades their judgment — and keep them under watch until the monsoon breaks."

"And after?" Ruiz asked.

"After," I said, "the river disposes of what I don't retain. We've discussed this. The gorge is generous that way. It's taken better people than these and asked no questions, and in three weeks there won't be a bank, a tribunal, or a witness within two hundred kilometers who can prove any of them were ever here."

Almeida heard every word. I made sure of it — I don't whisper orders in front of the condemned; it's cruel and it produces struggling, and struggling damages cargo. She heard it and her face did something I found genuinely interesting: nothing. Absolute stillness, the stillness of deep water, and behind it I could see the calculation running, fast and cold, and I realized with something close to professional delight that I had finally made a mistake worth making.

I had shown her the exit. And exits, in my experience, are only locked from the outside by people who've forgotten that the condemned have hands.

They walked them up to the tent between two riflemen, the mechanic limping, the heir looking back once at the wreck with an expression I chose not to interpret, and Almeida not looking back at all — which frightened me considerably more, though I logged it, as I log everything, under *miscellaneous*.

Then I turned to the Corvina's ribs, and the rain, and the work.

The hold was intact. The chest was gone.

I stood in the flooded dark of that ship for a long moment, water to my knees, my lamp finding nothing but the clean swept space where eleven years of my life had been stored, and somewhere overhead the monsoon leaned its whole weight on the gorge, and I began — calmly, methodically, the way I begin everything — to revise the numbers.

The ledger, I reflected, hates an empty cell. It will not rest until something fills it.

Neither will I.
