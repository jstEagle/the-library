## 16. The River Rises

The rain stopped being weather sometime in the night and became a fact of the world, like gravity, like debt. I woke to it hammering the tarp over our heads and lay there a moment doing arithmetic I didn't like: the sound had changed pitch. Water on canvas is one thing. Water on water is another.

I crawled out into the grey half-light of the drowned village and looked at the river, and my stomach did the thing it does when a patient's pulse goes from fast to wrong.

The Corvina was gone.

Not gone gone — nothing that heavy goes anywhere — but the low water that had exposed her ribs for three days had climbed her hull overnight, and now only the top strake of her starboard side broke the surface, black and streaming, like the back of some animal deciding whether to dive for good. The gorge had swallowed her to the gunwales while we slept. By tomorrow she'd be a rumor. By the day after, a story old men told badly.

"Ada." Juno stood behind me with two tin cups and the expression he wore when the news was bad but not yet fatal. "Water's up four feet since midnight. Maybe five."

"I can count."

"You can also sulk, and I'd rather you counted." He handed me a cup of the chicory substitute Elias had been brewing from his father's stores, which tasted like punishment but was hot, and hot mattered. "Elias says the flood crest comes through the narrows tomorrow night. After that, the whole lower gorge runs white to the coast for a week. Nobody rides it. Nobody."

"So we ride it tonight."

"So we ride it tonight," he agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing to his own funeral because the alternative was standing in line for it. "Assuming we have a boat, an engine, and a miracle. Current inventory: one boat, no engine, and Vey."

Vey. Right. I drank the chicory and let the bitterness settle me. Because that was the other item on the morning's ledger: forty yards upstream, on the shelf of rock where the camp had been built, Marcus Vey's crew was working a portable winch with the cheerful industry of men who could hear payday. They'd dragged their salvage barge down to the waterline in the night — a flat ugly thing with a crane arm folded like a mantis — and they were rigging slings down to the Corvina's hold. Every hour the river rose made their job harder and more expensive, which meant every hour made them faster and stupider.

"They started at dawn," Juno said. "Been hauling since. Two crates up so far. Silver plate, by the look of it going into the barge hold."

"Elias?"

"In the church. Reading."

I found him where Juno said, sitting on the steps of the flooded chapel with his back against the doorframe, the oilcloth packet of documents open across his knees and his father's letters stacked beside him in their oilskin wrapper. The water had reached the third step. He didn't look up when I sat down next to him, which was its own kind of courtesy; Elias had learned somewhere that silence could be a room you offered people.

"How long have those crates been down there," I said. It wasn't really a question.

"Since before the letters stopped." He turned a page. His hands were steady, but I'd noticed by then that his steadiness was a load-bearing structure — take one thing away and everything else came down. "My father wrote that the hold was sealed with pitch and tarpaulin before they abandoned ship. He thought he'd come back for it. Then the will happened, and the coming back became someone else's problem. Mine, apparently."

"The river's taking it tonight."

"The river's been taking things from my family for thirty years. Tonight it's just being efficient about it." He closed the packet and looked at me, and in the flat grey light his face had the same color as the water — patient, deep, moving faster underneath than anyone gave it credit for. "You're thinking about the winch."

"I'm thinking about a lot of things. The winch is the one with a plan attached."

"Tell me the plan."

"It has holes."

"Most true plans do. My father's had a hole in it big enough to sink a ship." A ghost of a smile. "Literally."

* * *

Here was the plan, such as it was, laid out on the chapel floor with a stick drawing in the silt while the rain came down like applause for something terrible:

The Leech still floated, lashed under the collapsed boathouse at the village's downstream edge, patched twice and holding like a promise made by a stranger. No engine — Vey's men had stripped the fuel line and taken the carburetor as insurance against exactly the escape we were planning, because Marcus Vey did not get where he was by leaving tools lying around for other people's plans. But the current through the village ran hard and clean toward the narrows, and the narrows ran toward the lower gorge, and the lower gorge, if you survived it, spat you out into the wide brown water below the falls where Vey's forward launch was moored, fat and fueled and unguarded, because everyone worth guarding against was currently up here playing winch.

So: no engine needed, if you didn't mind steering a leaking boat through a flood with paddles, prayer, and one very good mechanic's understanding of how water actually behaves. Which I did mind, enormously, and was going to do anyway.

The complication was the winch. Not because it threatened us directly — Vey wanted us alive-ish until the monsoon disposed of us, and his men were too busy to chase anyone — but because of what it was doing to the Corvina. Every crate they hauled out was a piece of the proof Elias needed, or a piece of the treasure that would keep Vey funded forever, or both; the manifest papers were in the strongbox in the master's cabin, and the master's cabin was already under water, and Vey's divers were working down to it in shifts.

"We don't have to beat them," Elias said, tracing the stick-drawing channel with one finger. "We have to make them stop beating themselves."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the winch is anchored to that shelf, and the shelf is limestone, and limestone under a rising river does what limestone always does." He looked up. "It lets go. Your mechanic knows this better than I do. Ask him what happens to a load-bearing anchor when the water gets under it."

I asked Juno. Juno got the particular light in his eyes that I'd learned, over seven years and one previous catastrophe, meant either genius or property damage, and historically the categories overlapped.

"The deadman anchors they've set are screwed into the shelf lip," he said. "Fine in low water. But the river's come up five feet, and the shelf's got a seam running right along the lip — I saw it yesterday when I was pretending to be too broken to work. Water gets in that seam, pressure does the rest, and the whole lip shears off under load. Takes the winch, takes the barge's crane arm if they've got it braced wrong, takes maybe a man or two into the drink." He paused. "Or it doesn't, and they haul everything out by dark and we've lost."

"So we help the river along."

"So we help the river along." He grinned, and it was not a nice grin, and I loved him for it. "One crowbar, one hour underwater on that seam, and geology does the rest. I've been worse employed."

"And while the winch is introducing itself to the gorge," Elias said slowly, "we take the Leech through the narrows."

"While the entire camp is watching their paycheck try to drown them," I said. "Yes."

There was a silence. Rain on the chapel roof. The river chewing patiently at the world.

"My father," Elias said at last, "would have hated this plan. He believed in ledgers. In things balancing." He gathered his letters, wrapped them, tied them. "I think that's why he lost. The river doesn't balance anything. It just decides." He stood. "Let's go be decided."

* * *

Juno went into the water at noon, in the worst of it, with the crowbar strapped across his back and a length of rope around his chest held by Elias and me from the boathouse rocks. We watched him go under between the barge and the shelf, a pale shape in brown water, and then we watched nothing at all, which is the specific torture of loving someone who works underwater: all the drama happens where you can't see it, and your imagination fills the gap with talent.

The winch kept running the whole time. That was the obscene part. Its cable hummed down into the flood, taut as a violin string, hauling up Vey's future one crate at a time while Juno worked below it in the dark, prying at a seam in the bones of the earth. Once the cable shuddered — a load shifting, a diver moving — and both Elias and I leaned so hard against the rope we nearly pulled ourselves in after him.

Four minutes. Five. Six. I counted the way I used to count in medevac, the way you count when counting is the only useful thing your hands can do.

At seven minutes Juno surfaced twenty yards downstream, gasping, grinning, bleeding modestly from a cut on his scalp, and treading water with the air of a man who has just left a tip for the management.

"Well?" I hauled on the rope.

"Well," he choked, as Elias dragged him hand over hand to the rocks, "the seam's open about a foot now. Packed it loose with rubble so it reads natural. When that cable takes full load again — and it will, they're greedy — the lip goes." He let Elias pull him up and lay on the stone, laughing at the sky and the rain together. "Best part? They'll blame the river. Nobody blames the river. River's got no fingerprints."

* * *

We waited out the afternoon under the boathouse, and waiting is its own chapter of hell. The winch ran. The crates came up. The water rose — six inches, a foot — and each rise was a door closing somewhere ahead of us, in the narrows, in the gorge, in the whole geography of getting out alive.

Elias sat with the Leech and prepared her like a man dressing a bride for a funeral: checking the patches, lashing down everything that could become a projectile, wedging the document packet inside his jacket where it made him look slightly barrel-chested and entirely determined. I checked the paddles, all four, and the bailer, and the length of cable we'd use as a drag-brake in the fast water, and I tried not to look upstream at the shelf.

It was near dusk when the river called in its marker.

The winch took a heavy load — we heard the engine note drop, strain, hold — and then there was a crack like the gorge clearing its throat, a sound I felt in my teeth before my ears, and the whole lip of the shelf sheared away. The winch went with it. The crane arm whipped down, kissed the barge amidships hard enough to stove in her rail, and the cable snapped back up through the rain like a living thing, whistling. Men scattered. One went into the water and was fished out screaming and mostly fine. The barge swung on its lines, listing, its cargo of silver plate sliding audibly from one end of the hold to the other like coins in a pocket.

And every man in Vey's camp turned to watch it happen.

"Now," I said.

We slipped the Leech out from under the boathouse into the flood, and the flood took us the way it takes everything — completely, without negotiation. The first hundred yards were pure surrender; the river had us by the keel and all our paddles could do was argue about the angle. Juno rode the stern as rudder-weight, Elias forward with the drag-cable ready, and I knelt amidships reading the water the way my father taught me before I knew he was teaching me: the V of a submerged rock, the smooth tongue of a channel, the place where the current lied.

The narrows took us at full dark.

I will not pretend I steered that passage so much as negotiated with it. The walls came down to boat-widths, the water stood up in places like it had changed its mind about being horizontal, and the noise was total — a sound with no edges, in which our own shouting was just another tributary. Twice we hit things. Once we spun. At the worst of it, in a stretch where the gorge pinched and dropped and the Leech stood on her nose like a diver, Elias turned around in the bow, soaked black, lit by nothing, and shouted something at me.

"What?"

"I said — my father's letters — say the narrows run left at the split—"

"Then pray they're right!"

They were right. Or the river was feeling generous, which amounts to the same thing. We came through the split hard to port, shipped half the narrows' water as ballast, and shot out the bottom of the gorge into the wider dark below, where the current gentled just enough to let us discover we were all screaming and stop.

For a while nobody spoke. We bailed. The rain kept on, softer now, almost apologetic, and the Leech wallowed downstream through the black with the patience of a thing that had survived worse owners.

"That," Juno said finally, "was the easy part."

"Don't," I said.

"No, genuinely. Easy part's done. Now we've got to pass the falls pool, steal Vey's launch, and outrun a flood." He wrung out his shirt with two hands, like strangling an old friend. "But the hard part —" he nodded back upstream, toward the gorge, toward the wreck, toward the sound of a ruined camp regrouping in the rain — "the hard part is that Vey's going to figure out pretty quick that his winch didn't die of natural causes. And when he does, he's going to want his launch back."

"Which is why," Elias said quietly, from the bow, "we need to reach it first. There's a moon coming up behind that cloud bank. When it does — look downstream."

I looked. Far down the black reach of the river, where the falls pool widened and went quiet before its long drop, something caught the coming light and held it: a pale shape at anchor, riding easy, fueled and waiting.

Vey's launch. Our way out. And between us and it, measured in hours now instead of miles, the last of the monsoon's patience running out.

"Put your backs into it," I said, and drove the paddle down into the flood.
