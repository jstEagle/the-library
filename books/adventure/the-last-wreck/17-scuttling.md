## 17. Scuttling

The rain had stopped, which was the river's way of clearing its throat.

I stood on the Corvina's exposed deck with water lapping at my boots and Vey's men closing from both ends of the gorge, and I thought: seven years of avoiding this place, and here I am, standing on my father's grave like it's a stage.

The wreck lay on her starboard side against the gorge wall, half out of the low-season water, her ribs black with a decade of silt and moss. From above she'd looked like something a giant had discarded — a fish skeleton, a fallen cathedral. Up close she was worse. Up close she was a ship, with a wheelhouse and a companionway and a name still legible under the grime, and every plank of her was a sentence in a story that had cost my father his life.

"Ada." Elias crouched at the cargo hold hatch, prying at the seal with a wrecking bar. "It's holding. Ten more minutes."

"We don't have ten minutes," Juno said. She was at the upstream end of the deck, watching the bend where Vey's launch would appear. Her voice was doing that thing it does when she's frightened and refuses to admit it — going flat, going precise, going full mechanic. "Engine noise. Two engines. Maybe three minutes."

"Then help me instead of narrating," Elias said.

They got the hatch open between them, and the smell came up out of the hold like a held breath finally released — river mud, old tar, rust, and underneath it all something sweeter, something that didn't belong to a drowned ship. Elias went down first with the lamp. I followed.

The hold was exactly as the letters had promised and nothing like I'd imagined. Not chests of coin glinting in the dark. Crates. Dozens of them, stenciled with the Almeida Shipping mark — my family's mark, though not my branch of it, though the ink had faded to ghosts — strapped down and sealed against the water that had never quite reached them. And bolted to the bulkhead, an iron case the size of a coffin lid.

Elias put his hand flat on it. For a moment he didn't say anything, and I understood that he was touching his father's handwriting through six inches of steel.

"The documents," he said. "And under the crates — the specie. Half the estate, if the manifest is honest. Which it is, because my father was many things but not a liar about money."

"Then we take the case and go," I said.

"And leave the rest for Vey."

I looked at the crates. I thought about what they were worth. I thought about what they'd cost.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't stay buried any better than treasure does. You can sink it in the deepest gorge you can find, weight it with an oath, mark the spot on no chart, and some man with a ledger will come along with a grappling hook and drag it back into the light, and there it'll be, exactly as heavy as the day you dropped it.

My father died forty meters from where I was standing. I know because I've done the arithmetic a thousand times, in the bad years, when sleep wouldn't take. He went into the water trying to free a fouled winch cable, and the current took him into the gorge throat, and we found his boat three days later and him eleven days after that, downstream, in a way I will not describe even now. The crew stood on the bank afterward — me, Juno, old Sefu, the others — and swore. Never again. No salvage, no return, no gold worth a second name on that list of the dead.

Sefu broke his oath first. Went back for a survey contract two years later, drowned in a routine crossing, as if the river kept accounts too. After that the oath stopped being words and became a load-bearing wall in my life. I built everything against it. The flying, the distance, the rule about rivers.

And now Marcus Vey had come along with a crowbar.

"Ada." Elias's voice, careful. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding where I live," I said. "Give me a minute."

* * *

Vey's launch came around the bend at 4:40 in the afternoon, riding low with armed men and salvage gear, and the first thing I felt wasn't fear. It was insult. He hadn't even hurried. He'd known we'd do the hard work of finding the hold and opening it, and he'd let us, because that's what Vey does — he lets other people pay the costs and then arrives to collect the profits. His whole life is other people's sweat with his signature on it.

He came aboard with four men and a shotgun he carried the way accountants carry briefcases — not to use, just to establish the terms of the meeting.

"Almeida." He smiled at me across my father's deck, and I want you to appreciate the craftsmanship of that smile. Twenty years of practice. "You found her. Your father would be proud."

"Don't," I said.

"He'd be proud," Vey went on, mild as Sunday, "because he understood what you refuse to understand. That wrecks aren't tragedies. They're inventory. He died because he was impatient, not because he was wrong. There's a difference." He gestured at the open hold with the shotgun barrel, casual, obscene. "Half a million in specie down there, plus papers worth twice that to the right buyer. We split it. You get your father's share plus interest. The boy gets his name in a tribunal record somewhere, which is all names are good for anyway. Everyone walks away rich except the dead, and the dead don't complain."

"You'd give Elias his inheritance?"

"I'd give him a cut. Inheritance is just a word for money with sentiment attached. I deal in the money."

Elias stepped forward, and I saw Vey reassess him — the ledger-mind recalculating, the asset reclassified from nuisance to complication. "You forged my father's will," Elias said quietly. "You've spent ten years pretending I don't exist."

"I spent ten years being the only one willing to do the work of finding this ship. Work has value. Sentiment doesn't." Vey turned back to me, and here it came, the sincere part, the part that made him dangerous — because Vey believed his own arithmetic the way priests believe scripture. "Ada. Last offer. Partner or passenger. Partner gets a quarter and a future. Passenger gets to watch the monsoon come in from wherever we decide to tie her up. Choose fast; the gauge says we have hours, not days."

I looked at the water rising green and patient around the Corvina's hull. I looked at the crates. I thought about my father's face the last time I saw him alive, sunburned and certain, saying: *the river gives back what it's ready to give, adaçinha. Everything else it keeps.*

Everything else it keeps.

"Juno," I said.

"Yeah?"

"How much cutting charge did we pack for the winch cables?"

There was a silence. Then Juno started laughing — that low, disbelieving laugh she does when one of my terrible ideas lands fully formed. "You're joking."

"Am I ever?"

"Constantly. It's your worst quality." But she was already moving toward the equipment bag, and Vey's smile was starting its long collapse.

"Almeida," he said. "Think about what you're—"

"I've thought about nothing else for seven years."

* * *

Let me be precise about what happened next, because tribunals ask, and because I want it on record that it wasn't rage. Rage is cheap. This was bookkeeping.

The Corvina's hull below the waterline was a century of compromises — riveted plates, patched seams, corrosion eating patiently at every joint. My father had surveyed her in '09 and pronounced her sound for one more season, and the season killed him, and the ship had been dying ever since, slowly, in the dark. All she needed was help.

Juno taped the charges to the lower seams while Elias and I hauled the document case up onto the tilting deck, and Vey's men — and this is the detail I'll keep forever — Vey's men didn't stop us. Not at first. Because they couldn't believe it. Four armed men watching a woman in a soaked flight jacket tape explosives to a fortune, and their faces said the same thing over and over: *she won't, she can't, nobody would.* Vey had taught them the world ran on greed. He'd never imagined someone might run it on something else.

"Last chance," Vey said, and his voice had lost the Sunday mildness. "Blow that hull and you blow your father's life work. Everything he came here for. Everything he *died* for."

"No," I said. "He died for a payroll and a promise to men who trusted him. The gold was always yours, Marcus. Yours and people like you. That's why he never made it home — because men like you make rivers into ledgers and then act surprised when the ledgers drown somebody." I held up the detonator. "This isn't destruction. This is a verdict. The court's been in session for ten years and you just arrived late."

"Ada—" Juno, warning. Water sounds. The gorge was filling, the monsoon's second wall coming down the valley like weather with an opinion.

I pressed the switch.

* * *

I have heard explosions in mountains and explosions in mineshafts and once, memorably, inside a fuel truck I should not have been standing beside. None of it prepared me for the sound a ship makes when she finally gives up. Not a bang. A sigh — a vast, structural, underwater groan, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep, and then the river moved.

The Corvina settled. Just that. Settled another meter into the gorge floor, and the green water poured in through the opened seams, and the crates began to shift, and the specie — half a million in coin and bullion, Vey's entire horizon — slid in its strapping and then broke loose and went down into the dark in a slow glittering avalanche, like the river reaching up and taking back its own.

Vey screamed. I'll say that plainly because it matters: he screamed, an honest animal sound with no craft in it at all, and then he dove.

Off his own deck, into floodwater rising toward the gorge throat, after sinking treasure, in the last light, with the monsoon coming — he stripped off his jacket and went over the rail headfirst, and his own men shouted after him, and none of them followed, because his men were hired with money and money was currently leaving the premises.

Elias caught my arm. "He'll drown."

"Probably."

"Ada."

I looked down at the boil of water where Vey had gone in. I thought about pulling rank on the river, about ordering a rescue, about the version of myself who would dive in after a man who'd left me marooned to die. That version existed once. She signed contracts without reading them and called it trust.

"He knew the risks when he bought the river," I said. "Everybody signs. Nobody reads."

But I took off my jacket anyway, and Juno threw me the line, and we rigged a throw-rope off the Corvina's rail because I could hate Marcus Vey with my whole history and still not watch him die on my father's deck without lifting a finger. There's a difference. He taught me that himself, in his terrible way. Inventory versus verdicts.

We got him on the third throw. He came up coughing half the gorge, clawing at the rope, empty-handed, absolutely empty-handed, and as Juno and Elias hauled him onto the tilting planks I watched him look past us — past the humans, past the rescue — down into the settling murk where his fortune was finishing its descent, and the look on his face was the truest thing I'd ever seen him wear. Not gratitude. Not shame. Arithmetic. A man watching his ledger balance to zero in real time.

"You insane woman," he rasped. "Do you know what you've done? Do you know what that was *worth*—"

"It was worth a man's life in 2016," I said. "Paid in full. The river doesn't extend credit, Marcus. You'd know that if you'd ever read anything but numbers."

His men were already backing toward the launch. One of them said something low and the others laughed — short, ugly, finished laughter — and Vey turned to shout orders at them and discovered, in real time, what happens to power when its currency devalues. They left him there. Wet, shivering, bankrupt in every sense his imagination could reach, on a sinking wreck in rising water, and the launch pulled away upriver without him because even mercenaries can read a balance sheet.

Which left the four of us — me, Juno, Elias, and a ruined man — on the corpse of the Corvina, with the monsoon's front wall visible now as a gray curtain swallowing the valley upstream, and maybe ninety minutes before the gorge throat became a chute no boat could survive.

"Well," Juno said, checking the sky with a mechanic's squint. "Anyone else got a terrible idea? I find I'm in the market."

Elias had the document case strapped across his chest like a child. He looked at me, and under all the fear there was something else, something that had been building since the drowned village since the gorge since the letters read aloud by lamplight. "The raft," he said. "Your father's route. Through the throat."

"In this?" Juno said.

"Especially in this," I said. "Floodwater runs straight through the throat — no eddies, no recirculation, just velocity. It's the only time the passage is survivable, and it's survivable the way a bullet is survivable: only if everything goes perfectly." I looked at the gray wall coming down the valley and felt something I hadn't felt in seven years, standing on the deck where my father died, choosing to run the water that killed him. It wasn't peace. It was better. It was *aim.*

"Get the raft," I said. "Leave him a line and a flare. What he does with them is his biography, not ours."

Vey laughed — a cracked, wet, disbelieving sound. "You're going through the throat. In a monsoon. In an inflatable."

"Learned it from my father's charts," I said, stepping into the raft. "He always said the river gives back what it's ready to give."

The first drops hit the deck like thrown gravel, and behind us the Corvina groaned her last agreement, and we pushed off into the flood.
