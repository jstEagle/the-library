## 18. What Vey Chose

I have always believed that a man's ledger tells the truth about him long before his mouth does. Mouths lie. Ledgers don't. A ledger is just arithmetic wearing a suit, and arithmetic has never once flattered me.

So let me be honest with the only witness I trust anymore, which is this account, written — or rather thought, because my hands are otherwise occupied and have been for some minutes now — in the last dry corner of my mind while the rest of me is underwater in the Corvina's hold, tangled in a cargo net with a dead man's gold pressing me toward a floor I will never walk on again.

The water is cold in the way that only deep river water is cold, a cold that feels less like temperature and more like opinion. The gorge has opinions about me. It has had them for seven years, ever since Almeida's father came down into it chasing the same numbers I am chasing now, and the river closed its hand around him like a fist around a coin. I watched from the ridge. I did nothing. I told myself at the time that there was nothing to do, which was true, and also that there was nothing I would have done even if there had been, which was truer, and I have spent seven years not thinking about the difference between those two statements.

Now the river is collecting.

Let me reconstruct, because reconstruction is what I do. When a venture fails, I sit down with the figures and find the exact line where the error entered, the way a surgeon finds the bleeder. Errors are never mysterious. They are always early, always small, always dressed as reasonable decisions at the time. So: where did mine enter?

Not when I hired Ada Almeida. That was sound. An asset with one sentimental flaw is still an asset; you simply price the flaw in. I priced it. I knew she'd balk eventually, and I budgeted for the balking — two rifles' worth of persuasion, a night's delay, nothing that wouldn't amortize. What I failed to model was the boy. Elias. The stowaway heir with half a manifest folded inside his jacket like a love letter from a dead man. He wasn't in my ledger because he wasn't on my manifest, and I have learned — am learning, at this precise moment, at four fathoms and sinking — that the things not on your manifest are the things that sink you.

Or perhaps the error was earlier. Perhaps it was the day I forged the will.

I want to be careful here, because forgery sounds like a word other people use about you, a word with a courtroom attached. In my own books I called it *asset consolidation*. Old Corvina — the owner, the dreamer, the man who bought a ship with poetry and paid for it with promissory notes — had an illegitimate son he meant to legitimize with paper. Half the estate, held in trust, contingent on a document that existed in exactly one copy. One copy. Any accountant will tell you that a single point of failure is not security, it is an invitation. I accepted the invitation. I burned the original, wrote my own, and told myself the boy would land on his feet somewhere, boys always do, and that the estate would do more good in competent hands than in the hands of a child raised on river stories.

Competent hands. Mine. That was the phrase. I have used some version of that phrase so many times in my life that it has worn smooth as a river stone, and I can no longer see the print of my own thumb on it.

The net tightens. Or I tighten against the net; at this depth the distinction is academic. Above me — I can tell by the light, a greenish trembling membrane far overhead, the surface seen from underneath, which is a view I recommend to no one — above me the monsoon is doing its work. The Corvina's ribs are singing. I mean that literally. Old iron hulls under stress make a sound, a low groaning note that travels through water better than through air, and the whole wreck is humming it now, one long dying chord, the ship's opinion of everything we are doing to her.

Ada opened her seams. I watched her do it. That is the part I keep replaying, the part my mind keeps returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth. She stood on the deck plates with a pry bar and she looked at me — looked at me the way you look at a figure in a ledger you've finally decided to write off — and she drove the bar under a seam plate and pulled, and the Corvina began to drink.

She chose drowning over dealing. With me. She would rather feed forty years of salvage to a gorge than shake my hand over a percentage.

Here is what I cannot get out from behind, even now, even here: I understood her choice perfectly. That's the humiliation of it. I could have written her decision myself, entered it in my own column, *asset refuses liquidation, prefers total loss* — I've seen it before, in men smaller than her, in deals gone sour, in partners who burned their own warehouses rather than sell to me at my price. I always filed those men under *irrational*. Irrational, sentimental, self-defeating. And every time, some quiet clerk in the back office of my skull wrote a second entry I never read: *they would rather lose everything than become you.*

I am reading that entry now. Underwater. Late.

The gold is the problem, physically speaking. There is a chest of it wedged against my legs — Corvina's bullion, or part of it, the portion that hadn't yet slid through the torn hold floor into the gorge's throat — and the chest is heavy in the way only gold is heavy, a moral weight as much as a physical one, and my boot is through the cargo net, and the net is through a rib of the ship, and the rib is moving. Everything down here is moving except the gold. Gold does not move. Gold waits. That is its entire genius, and I have spent thirty years admiring it without once noticing that waiting is all gold ever does, that it produces nothing, feeds no one, builds nothing, that it sits in the dark being itself while men drown around it, and that I have organized my life in the image of a metal that does nothing but wait to be dug up.

My lungs have opinions too. They are filing them urgently.

Let me be precise about the choice, since the chapter of my life demands a title and this seems to be it. There was a moment — there is always a moment, I teach this to junior men, *there is always a moment, Vey, find it* — a moment when I could have let go. Cut the boot loose, drop the chest, kick for the green membrane of light, live. My hands knew it. Hands are honest; they started to move before the rest of me authorized anything. And then the chest shifted, settled deeper into the silt, and some clerk in the back office said: *recoverable. Still recoverable. You're close. Sunk cost is not lost cost, Vey, you taught us that.* And I stayed.

I stayed for a chest of metal in a collapsing ship in a flooding gorge, because leaving it felt like admitting the whole enterprise had been — what? Not evil. Evil would at least be a shape, a thing with edges you could name. No: admitting it had been *empty*. Thirty years of ledgers balancing beautifully, columns marching, assets consolidating, and at the bottom of every page, in invisible ink, the same sum: nothing acquired that could not be taken away, nothing built that outlived the building of it, no one who would come looking for me if I went into a gorge, except creditors.

The light overhead is dimming. Rain on the surface, I think, or the gorge walls narrowing as the flood rises, or both. The ribs are louder. Somewhere above, steel gives way with a sound like a door in a very large house closing forever, and the current changes, and the wreck begins to settle for good, stern-first, the way her father's body must have settled seven years ago, and I understand suddenly — with the clarity that only suffocation grants, the terrible gift of the almost-dead, everything simple at last — that I am going to die in the exact shape of my life: holding on.

Except I don't. That's the joke. That's the punchline the river has been building to for seven years, and I want it recorded accurately, because if my ledger is going to balance it should balance honestly:

I let go.

Not nobly. Not in a flash of redemption. My hands simply ran out of argument. The fingers opened the way they open on a dead man, one knuckle at a time, and the chest slid away into the dark trailing its chain like a kite string cut, and I kicked, and the net tore — or I tore, the grammar doesn't matter — and I rose through black water toward a shrinking coin of green light with nothing in my arms, nothing in my hands, nothing anywhere, and I surfaced gasping in the rain like a man born at fifty-eight years old with no assets whatsoever.

The gorge took the Corvina in eleven minutes.

I know because I counted. Counting is what I do instead of praying; it has the same rhythm and none of the kneeling. I counted from the moment the stern went under until the last rib slipped beneath the flood, and I stood on the ledge below the camp — soaked, coughing river out of myself in humiliating increments — and I watched forty years of ambition fold into the water like a ledger being closed by someone else's hand.

My crew watched from higher ground. All nine of them. They had the winch cable in their hands when Ada opened the seams, and they dropped it the way men drop a rope when the load turns out to be a snake, and after that they mostly watched me. I could feel their eyes doing arithmetic. Crews are costs; I taught them that too, implicitly, by treating them that way for years, and now the market was repricing, and I could see the figures moving behind their faces. *What is he worth? What is he good for? How long do we stay tied to a falling asset?*

The answer, it turned out, was: until the rain got serious.

Dessalines went first. Of course Dessalines. The big one, the loyal one, the one I'd promoted twice for exactly the quality — call it appetite — that made him the first to understand that there was nothing left to be loyal *to*. He didn't say anything. He picked up his rifle, picked up his pack, looked at me once with something that wasn't hatred, which was worse — it was appraisal, final valuation, the look you give a lot at auction that didn't meet reserve — and he turned upriver toward the pass, and three others followed him within the minute, and then two more, and then it was raining hard enough that the departures stopped being decisions and became weather.

Four left. Then two. Then, by full dark, one — a boy named Ferro who stayed not out of loyalty but because he couldn't swim and the flood had eaten the downstream trail, and who sat under the tarp shivering and not looking at me, and whom I fed from the last of the stores because feeding him was the only transaction still available to me and I have never known how to be in a room without a transaction in progress.

That is the finding, then. That is what the audit returns, when the auditor is drowning and the accounts are finally forced open. Power — my power, the real kind, the kind I actually held all these years — was never power at all. It was hunger wearing power's clothes. Men followed me the way gulls follow a trawler: for the wake, for what spilled, for the offal. Take away the catch and the gulls go, instantly, without malice, without memory, wheeling off toward some richer boat, and the sea does not notice, and neither does the sky. I built nothing that needed me. I only moved things from one column to another and called the moving an empire, and tonight the columns are underwater, and the empire is a wet man under a tarp sharing hardtack with a boy who pities him.

Ferro asked me, around midnight, whether we were going after them. The Almeida woman and her people — they'd slipped the camp during the scuttling chaos, taken the raft, gone into the flood. Downriver. Toward the coast. Toward the tribunal, if they were smart, and the woman was smart, and the boy had documents sealed in oilcloth, and documents, unlike gold, weigh nothing and ruin everything.

"No," I said.

He seemed surprised. I was surprised. Every instinct I own says pursue, recover, litigate, retaliate — the four verbs of my entire biography. But I sat there in the rain with my hands empty and I found, searching myself the way I'd search a failing company for saleable assets, that I owned no verb for *begin again*. Thirty years of consolidating, and not one line item called *build*. The gorge had taken the treasure, the crew, the ship, the claim, the name. It had left me the one thing I'd never carried: nothing. And nothing, I discovered, sitting in the dark listening to the flood eat the banks, is astonishingly light. I kept waiting for it to hurt more. It hurt, God knows it hurt — but underneath the hurt was a silence I hadn't heard since childhood, the silence of a ledger with no entries, and some ruined clerk in me looked at the blank page and felt — I'll write it plainly, since plainness is all I have left — relief.

"Then what are we doing?" Ferro asked.

I looked downriver, into the rain, along the route the raft would have taken, toward the coast, toward Port Meridian, toward a tribunal room where a forged will was about to meet daylight and a dead man's son was about to be handed a name I had stolen and could never again afford to keep.

"We're going to listen," I said.

Ferro frowned. "To what?"

"To what happens," I said, "when the truth gets read aloud." Because I wanted — this is the strangest entry of all, the one I'd strike from any sane ledger — I wanted to be in the room. Not to fight it. To hear it. To watch the boy stand up in front of the tribunal with his oilcloth documents and his father's letters and his legitimate grief, and to hear some clerk read the words *forged in the hand of Marcus Vey*, and to sit there in the gallery with nothing, owning nothing, owed nothing, and finally learn what my life had actually purchased — by watching, from the cheap seats, what it had cost everyone else.

The rain eased near dawn. The flood kept rising. Somewhere downriver, a leaking raft carrying three people and a packet of papers was riding the brown water toward the coast, and I stood on the bank with a boy who couldn't swim and a rifle I no longer had any use for, and I started walking.

Downstream. Toward Port Meridian. Toward the reading of the will — the real one, the one I burned, which apparently survived anyway, because the dead are more careful archivists than the living, and because a man's true ledger always surfaces eventually, no matter how deep he sinks it.

I only hope they let me in.
