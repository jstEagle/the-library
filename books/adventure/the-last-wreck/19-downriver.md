## 19. Downriver

The river took us the way a dog takes a bone it's been waiting all day to steal — sudden, delighted, and with no intention whatsoever of giving it back.

I want that on the record, because Ada will tell you she steered us out of the gorge. Elias will tell you he read the water like his father's letters, every eddy annotated in some dead man's hand. Both true. But between Ada's hands and Elias's eyes there was me, flat on my belly in three inches of floodwater on the Leech's ruined deck, bailing with a fuel funnel and praying to whatever greasy saint looks after mechanics who have made one too many promises to inferior engines.

The Leech was not built for this. The Leech was built for sullen progress, for hauling cargo up lazy backwater channels at an insulted crawl. She was not built to surf. And yet there we were, riding the monsoon's spine downriver at a speed I have only previously achieved in nightmares, her hull groaning like a church organ played by an angry man, her patched seams holding by nothing but pine resin, canvas, and what I can only assume was spite.

"Juno!" Ada shouted over the roar. "How is she?"

"She's a boat!" I shouted back, which was the most honest answer available.

* * *

We came out of the gorge near dawn, or what passes for dawn under a sky the color of wet slate. One moment the walls were screaming past close enough to touch, white water standing up in columns where no column should be, and the next the river opened its hand and let us go — spilling us into a broad brown valley where the flood had swallowed the banks whole and the trees stood waist-deep, patient as drowned men waiting for a bus.

Ada killed what remained of the engine and let us drift. Nobody spoke for a long while. The rain had thinned to a fine grey whisper, the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang around you like a debt.

I did inventory, because inventory is what I do when I don't trust myself to do feelings. We had: one boat, holed twice and patched thrice; half a tank of fuel; a tin of biscuits gone soft as sponge; Ada's med kit, which I had guarded through everything with the devotion other men reserve for lovers or gods; the oilcloth bundle, strapped beneath my jacket against my chest, damp nowhere, because Elias had wrapped it the way his father must have wrapped things — triple-folded, waxed seams, tied off with a knot I've since tried to learn and failed at four times.

And we had each other, battered. Ada's left arm hung in a sling made from a shirt sleeve, the shoulder wrenched when the gorge spat us sideways off the wreck's deck two nights ago. She flew planes with that shoulder. She steered boats with it. She had not once mentioned it hurt, which meant it hurt enormously. There was a gash above Elias's eyebrow that I'd closed with six stitches from the med kit, my first ever on a living face, and if you looked at him straight-on you could see they were crooked, and if you mentioned it he said "it gives the scar character," and if you mentioned it again he threatened to give my face character too.

Me, I'd burned both forearms on the winch line during the sabotage, and my hands shook — not from fear anymore, just from having been afraid so long the body forgot how to stop. They'd stop eventually. Bodies are stupid but loyal.

"Port Meridian," Ada said finally, to the river more than to us. "Four days' drift, maybe five, if the flood holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Elias asked.

"Then we walk out of a swamp full of things that eat people, carrying documents nobody will believe."

"Cheerful," I said.

"I'm a pilot," she said. "We're paid to be cheerful about weather."

* * *

The strange thing about the days after — and I've thought about how to say this without making it sound like a sermon, because sermons make me itch — is how light everything got.

Not easy. My arms blistered and wept. Ada couldn't lift her arm past her ribs, so Elias and I rigged a block-and-tackle off the mast so she could haul herself upright, and she hated it, hated being hauled, hated needing it, and used it anyway, which I think is the most honest portrait of Ada Almeida anyone could paint. The biscuits ran out on day two and we fished with a bent hook and a bootlace and ate what we caught half-raw, and Elias turned out to know which ones were safe, because of course he did, because the man had apparently memorized an entire drowned country out of filial grief and stubbornness.

But light. That's the word. Every decision we made now was small and clean: steer there, patch this, sleep in shifts, keep the bundle dry. Compare that to the weeks before, when every choice had been a coin with Vey's face minted on both sides. Take the job, don't take the job, signal him, burn the signal. I still woke at night sometimes seeing that flare gun in my hand, aimed at the ridge where his launch would come from, my thumb on the trigger, Juno-the-clever weighing a private cut against two people asleep in the rain. I burned the cartridge instead. I confessed by morning. And Ada had looked at me a long moment and said, "You told me before I found out. That's the whole difference between a traitor and a man." Which was generous, because I'd been a traitor for about eleven minutes, but I've decided to accept the framing.

That's the thing about choosing rightly. It doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like putting down something heavy you didn't know you were carrying, and then your back hurts anyway, from having carried it so long.

* * *

On the third day we passed the drowned village again — the one with the witness's grave, where Elias had read his father's letters aloud while the rain came down like judgment. From the river it was just rooftops now, a few ridgelines of tile breaking the brown water, and the church bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding.

Elias stood at the bow looking at it for a long time.

"They'll drown it completely this season," he said. "The new dam height."

"You could mark it," Ada offered. "Come back after. Put a buoy on the grave."

"He wouldn't want a buoy." A small crooked smile, careful of the stitches. "He wanted to be believed. That's what the letters were for. That's what I'm for, now."

I kept my mouth shut, which for me is a heroic act, and thought about my own father, who had taught me engines and never once said anything as clean as that. Some inheritances are documents. Some are a feel for carburetors and a temper with cheap solder. Both count, probably. Neither fits in oilcloth.

* * *

Day four, the rain quit entirely, which felt suspicious, like a card player suddenly being friendly. The sky opened into a pale washed blue and the flood began its slow retreat, leaving mud lines on the trees like bathtub rings, and the current gentled until we were drifting rather than fleeing, and there was nothing to do, which was its own kind of terror.

Ada fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, in the afternoon sun. Just — out. Seven years of vigilance switching off like a breaker thrown. Elias and I sat very still so as not to wake her, and after a while he said, quietly:

"She hasn't slept like that since the gorge. I've watched her. She sleeps like a cat on a hot roof — one ear open."

"Well," I said, "she did just steer us through the place that killed her father and sink the treasure she spent seven years not thinking about. Let the woman have her nap."

"It's more than that." He was turning the oilcloth knot over in his fingers, not untying it, just touching it, the way you'd touch a door to a house you'd been locked out of for thirty years. "My father wrote that the Corvina's crew swore an oath never to return. He wrote it like it shamed them. I used to think it did — cowardice dressed up as grief. Now I think the oath was the only honest thing any of them owned. You can't carry a dead man and a fortune in the same boat. Something has to go overboard."

"That's very poetic for a stowaway."

"I had a lot of time to practice in a cargo hold."

Ada stirred, muttered something about flaps, and slept on.

* * *

We hit the tidewater on the fifth morning.

You can smell Port Meridian before you see it — diesel and fish and woodsmoke and the particular sweet rot of a harbor town that has made peace with its own corruption. The river widened, slowed, went tidal and grey-green, and the traffic thickened: barges, dugouts, a patrol skiff that circled us once at a distance, took in our scorched and patched and generally apocalyptic condition, and evidently decided we were somebody else's paperwork.

Ada woke for the approach and took the tiller herself, refusing help with a look that ended the discussion before it started. She brought the Leech in on the last fumes of the last fuel, threading the harbor moles with the same hands that had ridden the gorge, and set us against a rust-stained quay with a bump so gentle the fenders barely noticed.

A harbor official wandered over, looked at the boat, looked at us, and asked the standard questions: name, port of origin, cargo.

"Cargo," Ada said, "is documents," and produced a salvage declaration she must have drafted in her head somewhere between the falls and the sea, because it was written out in pencil on the back of a chart, signed, dated, witnessed by all three of us. Of course she had. Of course she'd been composing legal paperwork while bailing floodwater with a funnel. I have known this woman seven years and she still finds new ways to be terrifying.

The official squinted at it, stamped it mostly out of indifference, and waved us through.

We stood on the quay. Solid ground did its usual trick of feeling like it was still moving. My legs had forgotten land; I walked the first fifty yards like a newborn giraffe with opinions.

"So," I said. "Tribunal's tomorrow?"

"Day after," Elias said. "They'll want the documents authenticated. My father's letters, the manifest halves, the will." He touched the bundle at his chest, and I saw his hand wasn't quite steady, and understood that for him the hardest part hadn't been the gorge or the guns. It was this — handing the story over to strangers and trusting strangers to believe it.

"They'll believe it," Ada said. "Paper doesn't lie as well as people do."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." She looked out at the harbor, at the masts and cranes and the low gold light coming in off the sea, and something crossed her face that I'd never seen on it before — not peace exactly, more like the absence of a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton. "I need to send a wire," she said. "There's someone I have to see, before the tribunal."

"The grave?" I asked, gently, because even I can read a room occasionally.

She nodded once. Then, because she was Ada: "First, food. Real food. Juno, you're buying. Consider it hazard pay."

"Hazard pay," I said. "For the man who nearly sold us out."

"For the man who burned the flare," she corrected. "The ledger balances."

And that — that right there — was the strangest part of the whole voyage. Not the flood, not the guns, not watching the Corvina's ribs fold into the brown water with a fortune going down inside her like a heart stopping. The strangest part was standing in a harbor town at dusk, filthy and stitched and half-starved, eating fried fish off paper with fingers that still shook, and laughing. Actually laughing. At something stupid Elias said about my stitching, probably, or about the Leech, sitting out there on the quay looking like a refugee from a shipwreck museum, and how she'd gotten us here anyway, the ugly loyal old tub.

Exhausted grace. That's the only phrase I've got for it. Grace that costs everything and arrives smelling of fish.

* * *

It was late when we parted for the night — Elias to a boarding house he insisted on paying for, muttering something about finally spending money that was arguably his; Ada to the telegraph office and then, I suspected, to sit alone somewhere with whatever she was going to say to her father's headstone; and me to the boat, because somebody had to sleep aboard the Leech, and because after five days on the water I trusted her hull more than any mattress.

I was checking her seams by lantern light, running my hands along the patches like a doctor palpating a patient, when I heard footsteps on the quay.

Not Ada's. Not Elias's. These steps had a ledger behind them — measured, deliberate, the walk of a man who counts everything, including his own footsteps.

Marcus Vey came down onto the planks into my lantern light, and I will grant him this: he looked terrible. Grey-faced, hollow-eyed, salt-damp coat, the look of a man whose arithmetic has finally come due. His crew had scattered at the coast like spilled coins, the papers said later. His launch impounded. His accounts frozen by the first tribunal writs. Everything balanced to nothing.

He stopped a few feet away and looked at the Leech, and then at me, and for a moment I thought he'd come to threaten, or buy, or beg.

Instead he said, quietly, almost to himself: "Your stitching. On the deck patch. Resin and canvas over a split seam. Where did you learn that?"

I blinked. "My father. River crews. Why?"

"Because it held," he said. "Through the gorge. Through the flood. Nothing else I've bought in twenty years held like that." He looked at the water for a moment, and there was something in his face I hadn't expected and didn't enjoy recognizing — not menace. Envy. "The tribunal starts day after tomorrow. They'll summon me. I'll answer." A pause. "Tell Almeida—" He stopped. Started again. Failed. Finally he just shook his head, once, like a man closing a book he no longer liked, and walked away up the quay into the dark, counting his footsteps all the way.

I stood there a long time with the lantern, listening to the harbor slap against the pilings, and I decided — not tonight. Tonight we had earned our grace, and whatever Vey had come to say or not say could keep until morning.

But I'll tell you what I did do. I went below, took out the oilcloth bundle, checked the triple knots, checked the wax, checked it was dry — and then I sat down and wrote in the Leech's logbook, in my own bad hand, the full account of everything that happened upriver. Dates, coordinates, the flare I burned, the treasure we sank, all of it. Because Elias has papers, and Ada has testimony, but a logbook is a mechanic's kind of truth: boring, specific, and impossible to argue with.

Tomorrow we'd tell Ada about Vey's visit. Tomorrow we'd visit graves and tribunals and whatever came after.

Tonight, the logbook. Tonight, the truth, entered neatly, in ink.

Outside, the tide turned, and the harbor began, very softly, to breathe.
