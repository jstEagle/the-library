## 20. The Tribunal at Port Meridian

The courthouse at Port Meridian smelled of salt, mildew, and old varnish — the smell of a building that had been arguing with the sea for a hundred years and losing gracefully. I sat in the antechamber with my hands flat on my knees and discovered that a man can survive a monsoon, a betrayal, a gorge that eats ships, and still be undone by a wooden bench.

"You're going to crack your kneecaps if you keep doing that," Juno said beside me. She was in her one good shirt, which was her only good shirt, which was also her work shirt, laundered with such violence that the buttons had begun to surrender. "Breathe, heir apparent."

"I'm not an heir until they say so."

"Then breathe like a man who's about to be."

Ada stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the harbor through the warped glass. She'd been quiet all morning in the way she was quiet before a hard flight — not nervous, exactly, but pre-flighted, running through her own checklist of things she intended to say and things she refused to say. She wore the same clothes as always, which is to say clothes that looked like they'd been chosen by someone who considered clothing a logistical problem rather than a statement.

"They'll try to make you angry," she said without turning around. "Vey's advocate. That's his whole trade. He'll ask you questions designed to make you shout, because a shouting man sounds like a man with something to hide."

"I don't have anything to hide."

"No," Ada said. "You have something worse. You have a story he can't argue with and a face that looks honest. Try to look slightly less honest. It'll put them off balance."

Juno snorted. I decided to take it as advice.

* * *

The tribunal chamber itself was smaller than I'd imagined from the outside — a long room with a raised bench for the three adjudicators, a rail, benches for the public, and tall windows that let in the gray coastal light and, when the wind turned, the smell of fish. The public benches were full. Word travels fast in a port town, faster than justice usually does, and everyone wanted to see the Corvina affair settled: salvagers, merchants, two newspapermen pretending not to be newspapermen, and a scattering of river people who had come down on the mail boats just to watch.

Marcus Vey sat in the third row with his advocate, and I will give him this much: he looked better than he had any right to. The gorge had taken his money, his crew, and most of his certainty, but it had left him upright. He'd lost weight in the way men do when grief and arithmetic arrive together. His hands were quiet in his lap. He did not look at me. He looked at the adjudicators, the way a gambler looks at cards he has already counted.

I looked at him plenty. I'm not proud of how much satisfaction there was in it, and I'm not entirely sorry either.

The senior adjudicator was a woman named Corvane, dry as August grass, with spectacles she used less for seeing than for punctuation. She opened proceedings by reading the claim into the record — my claim to the estate of Aurelian Voss, owner of the steamship *Corvina*, lost with cargo in the Gorge of Saint Ambrose seven years prior — and then she looked at me over her spectacles and said, "You will state your name for the record."

And there it was. The whole question of my life, asked in four words.

"Elias Voss," I said.

My voice held. I'd rehearsed it against the hull of a leaking boat in the rain, saying it over and over to the sound of iron water, so a dry courtroom was hardly going to break me. But saying it aloud in a room where it would be written down — that was different. Names are small things until someone takes yours away, and then they are the only thing.

Corvane wrote nothing herself; a clerk did that. She simply nodded, as if I'd passed some preliminary inspection, and said, "Proceed."

* * *

They began, sensibly, with the documents. My half-manifest, water-stained and soft as cloth at the folds, went onto the evidence table. Then the letters — my father's letters, forty-one of them, which Ada had insisted we seal in oilcloth along with everything else, because Ada understood before any of us did that paper was the true cargo of the *Corvina*. The gold made the story dramatic. The paper made it true.

I read three of them aloud, at Corvane's direction, and I will confess that reading my father's voice into a courtroom was harder than rafting the gorge. In the boat there had been rain and terror to hide behind. Here there was only silence, and forty rows of strangers listening to a dead man explain himself.

*"To my son, if the law ever finds him: the manifest is half yours by blood and half mine by labor, and no arrangement of papers can make it otherwise. If Vey shows you documents saying differently, look at the ink. Look at the date. A man who must forge the past has already confessed the future."*

The courtroom was very still. Somewhere near the back a gull complained against a window ledge, absurdly, like a heckler.

Then came the forged will.

We had recovered it from the document cache aboard the wreck — the cache Vey had spent seven years and other men's lives hunting, guided at the end by coordinates I fed him like sugar to a horse. It was my father's actual testament, drafted the year before the *Corvina* sailed, witnessed by one Tomás Ferro, lighthouse keeper of the drowned village of Santa Roca. And beside it, Vey's version — the one that had transferred the estate to Vey's own holding company within a month of the sinking, the one that had kept me nameless and landless for my entire adult life.

The advocates argued ink. They argued dates. Vey's man produced an expert who testified, with great composure, that the Ferro signature on the original was "consistent with period practice," which was a lawyer's way of saying it might be real or might be clever, and who could say? It was Ada, called as witness, who ended the argument, and she ended it the way she does most things: directly, without decoration.

"The signature is real," she said, "because I've met the man who wrote it. Or rather, his grave. We sheltered in Santa Roca during the monsoon. Tomás Ferro is buried there, above the flood line, under a stone his neighbors cut for him. The stone gives his dates, his office, and the fact that he witnessed a testament for Aurelian Voss. His neighbors carved it because he told them to, before he died, and they told us the same story he would have told you: that Voss came to him in the spring, that the papers were signed in the lighthouse, and that Ferro kept a copy of the registry page in the lighthouse log because he didn't trust lawyers or rivers."

She set the lighthouse log on the table. Water-stained, salt-bitten, but legible. Ferro had been exactly the kind of careful man drowned villages produce — the kind who writes things down because everyone else lies.

"The registry entry matches the will," Ada said. "The ink matches the log's own entries from that season. And the drowned village doesn't lie, because nobody profits from a drowned village."

Corvane looked at the log for a long time. Then she looked at Vey, and I watched a man understand, in public, that the arithmetic had finally failed him.

* * *

They gave Vey the floor to answer. His advocate spoke first — smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man paid by the hour — and made the case you'd expect: that salvage rights were salvage rights, that the estate question was separate from the wreck question, that my parentage was asserted but unproven, that sentiment was not evidence.

Then, unexpectedly, Vey stood up himself.

The room leaned forward. Even his advocate looked surprised, which I suspect was the point.

"I'll speak to one matter only," Vey said. His voice was quieter than I remembered from the river, worn down at the edges. "The will. The forgery is mine. I commissioned it, I executed it, and I used it to divert the estate while the wreck was presumed unrecoverable. I believed the legitimate documents were lost with the ship. When they proved otherwise, I took steps I am not proud of, against persons who had done me no wrong." He paused, and for a moment the ledger behind his eyes showed through, adding up a final column. "I will not contest further. I ask only that the tribunal note that the crew of the vessel *Leech* acted throughout with more honor than I extended to them, and that Ms. Almeida in particular was offered partnership and refused it under conditions that would have tempted a saint."

He sat down. His advocate stared at him as if he'd sunk the firm's boat personally.

I had imagined this moment for years — rehearsed it on bad nights, scored it with music, given myself all the best lines. In none of those versions did Vey confess. In all of them, I did. And now that it had actually happened, I found I felt mostly the way you feel when a tooth stops aching: relief, and a strange hollowness where the ache used to live, and the discovery that you had organized quite a lot of your life around the ache without noticing.

Ada caught my eye across the rail. She didn't smile, exactly. She gave me the small nod she gave Juno after a landing neither of them had expected to walk away from. *It's done. Fly on.*

* * *

The adjudication itself took less than an hour, which seemed obscene after twenty-two years of waiting. Corvane summarized with the economy of a woman who billed by clarity rather than confusion: the original testament authenticated; the forgery entered into the record and referred to the criminal court; the estate of Aurelian Voss — lands at Voss Reach, the salvage claims, the residual estate — adjudicated to Elias Voss, son of the testator, upon proof of blood, which proof the tribunal accepted on the combined testimony of the documents, the witnesses, and, in Corvane's words, "the conspicuous absence of any other candidate."

"There remains the matter of the cargo," she said. "The bullion and goods of the *Corvina* were deliberately scuttled into the gorge by the claimant's own hand, in circumstances the tribunal has reviewed. Salvage law does not reward destruction. But the tribunal notes that the destruction prevented armed theft, that the claimant and his companions acted at mortal risk and personal loss, and that the estate's value survives independent of the bullion. The costs of the expedition incurred by the claimant and his associates will be met from the estate. The tribunal finds no grounds for penalty." She removed her spectacles, which I gathered was her version of applause. "You chose the documents over the gold, Mr. Voss. The estate agrees with your choice. Court stands adjourned."

* * *

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, the port did what ports do: it absorbed us. Juno shook hands with strangers who wanted to touch the woman who'd burned the signal flare. Someone pressed a beer into my hand before I'd agreed to want one. The newspapermen descended, and I answered their questions in sentences I barely recognized as my own, hearing myself described as if I were a character in someone else's serial.

Ada stood apart, at the top of the steps, looking out at the harbor the way she'd looked out the antechamber window — except now there was nothing on the water she needed to avoid.

"So," I said, coming to stand beside her. "You own me a debt of gratitude, apparently. Costs met from the estate. You're officially reimbursed."

"For a leaking boat, a burned engine, and seven years of avoidance interrupted." She turned the beer bottle someone had given her without drinking it. "Worth it."

"You could take more. Land, a share. I meant what I said on the river — the offer stands."

"I know it does." She looked at me then, and whatever was in her face wasn't refusal, exactly. It was more careful than refusal. "Ask me again when I've slept somewhere stationary for a month. Ask me again when I've decided who I am when nobody's chasing anything."

"That sounds like a yes wearing a coat."

"It sounds like a maybe with good boots," she said. "Don't rush it. Things that grow slow hold."

Down the steps, at the edge of the crowd, Marcus Vey stood alone, hat in hand, watching the harbor too. No one spoke to him. He hadn't waited for a verdict on his freedom — that would come later, in a colder room — but he stood there anyway, a man auditing a lifetime and finding the column empty. As I watched, he put his hat on, squared it with the old precision, and walked away down toward the water, unhurried, like a man leaving a job site that had never belonged to him.

"He'll be back," Juno said, materializing at my elbow. "Men like that always come back. Smaller, though. Each time smaller."

"Let him," I said. "I'm done measuring myself against him."

Ada laughed — short, surprised out of her. "Listen to the heir. One morning of owning land and he's a philosopher."

"One morning," I agreed. "Come to Voss Reach and philosophize at me properly. There's a house that hasn't heard voices in twenty years. It could use a mechanic. It could use a pilot. It could use—" I stopped, because I'd nearly said *a family*, and some words you shouldn't spend in public.

Ada looked at me for a long moment. Then she finished her beer, set the empty bottle on the step with the care of a woman making a decision, and said:

"All right. But I fly on Sundays. Whatever else happens — the garden, the house, the rest of it — Sunday mornings the plane goes up. That's not negotiable."

"Deal," I said.

And that was how, on the steps of a courthouse that smelled of salt and old arguments, with a gull heckling from the gutter and Juno already negotiating the price of a truck to carry our gear, I got everything I'd been denied — the name, the land, the future — and discovered that what I actually wanted was the two people standing next to me, and a house with its windows open, learning to hear voices again.

Ada was already halfway down the steps, talking engines with Juno, when she stopped and turned back.

"Elias. Your father's letters. The ones we didn't read at the tribunal."

"What about them?"

"There's one addressed to a woman in Port Meridian. We never delivered it." She held it up between two fingers, the envelope soft with age. "Twenty-two years is long enough for the last mile, don't you think?"

I took the letter from her, and read the address, and felt the day tilt gently into something new.
