## 21. Inheritance

The tribunal released us on a Thursday, which felt right. Thursdays were the day my father used to declare, with great ceremony and no legal basis whatsoever, that all salvage work was cursed and should be postponed until Friday. He said it every week of his life except the last one.

Port Meridian smelled of tar and frying fish and the particular green rot of a coast coming into monsoon's tail end. The tribunal building was a former customs house with a dome that leaked in three places, and the clerk had put buckets under the drips with little paper flags labeling them by severity: *minor*, *moderate*, and *structural*. I liked the clerk immediately.

Elias had dressed for the occasion in the only clothes he owned that hadn't been underwater, which were still stiff with salt and made him look like a man wearing a sail. Juno sat beside me in the gallery with her arm in a sling — the winch had taken its toll — and kept up a running commentary in a whisper pitched for someone sitting much closer.

"He's going to cry," she said, nodding at Elias, who stood at the witness table with his half-manifest laid out like a holy relic. "I give it four minutes."

"He's not going to cry."

"Three minutes."

He didn't cry. What he did was worse, or better, depending on your constitution: he read his father's letters aloud. All of them. Slowly. In a voice that started steady and frayed by degrees until the third letter, the one written from aboard the Corvina itself, when he stopped being a man testifying and became a son reading mail that had taken twenty years to arrive.

The magistrates let him finish. Even the one who'd been checking his watch.

Vey sat in the gallery two rows behind us, alone, in a suit that no longer fit him — not physically; it fit fine, but it hung on him the way clothes hang on a man who has recently discovered what he actually weighs. His ledger-mind was still working. You could see it behind his eyes, running numbers that came out to nothing no matter how he arranged them. He didn't look at us. He looked at the floor, where there was presumably some figure he could still count.

When the senior magistrate struck the table with her gavel and read out the adjudication — the estate of Aurelio Sandoval, vessel Corvina, cargo and papers and name, restored to Elias Sandoval Vey—

Wait. No. *Sandoval*. Just Sandoval. He'd dropped the other name years ago, I learned later, the way you drop a stone you've been carrying in your mouth.

—restored to Elias Sandoval, the gallery did that thing galleries do, which is nothing, which is silence, which is the loudest sound there is when everyone in the room understands what just happened.

Juno leaned over. "Told you. Four minutes. He cried during the letters."

"That wasn't crying."

"It was absolutely crying."

* * *

Afterward, outside on the customs house steps, with the buckets-and-flags clerk locking up behind us and the harbor doing its evening glitter, Elias turned to us with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the face of a person about to make an offer they've rehearsed.

"I want you both to understand what I'm about to say," he began.

"Oh no," said Juno.

"—and I want you to know that I've thought about it carefully—"

"She's going to say no," Juno told me conversationally. "But let him finish. It's good for him."

"—the estate includes not only the wreck's recovery rights, such as they are now, but land. Upland, riverfront, the old warehouse district near the falls—" He stopped, took a breath, started again with less rehearsal. "There's money. There's a great deal of money, actually, more than any of us can comfortably imagine, and I would rather distribute it badly among friends than correctly among lawyers. Ada. A share. A full partner's share. And ground — actual ground, if you wanted it. Somewhere to put down whatever it is you've been refusing to put down for seven years."

The harbor glittered. A gull screamed something litigious at another gull.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't want money. Grief has never once, in the history of human feeling, looked at a bank draft and said *yes, that settles it*. Grief wants somewhere to go. It wants a direction, a task, a place to set itself down at the end of the day the way you'd set down a load too heavy to carry uphill. For seven years mine had been circling, looking for a landing, and I had kept it airborne on purpose because as long as it was flying I had a reason to keep moving, and as long as I kept moving I never had to find out what I was without it.

Miles, not years. That had been the whole architecture of my life. Every flight a way of not arriving anywhere.

"I can't take the money," I said.

Elias opened his mouth.

"Not because I'm proud. I'm not proud, I'm broke, those are different things." I watched a fishing boat come in, riding low and satisfied. "And I don't want ground. Ground is a thing you inherit from the dead or buy from the living, and I've had enough of both kinds of transactions to last me."

"So that's a no," said Juno.

"That's a no to the share and the ground." I turned to Elias. "What I'll take is work. If you're rebuilding anything upriver — the warehouse, the village, whatever the estate turns out to owe the drowned places — I'll fly for you. Medicine, mail, parts. Regular runs. Not charter-whenever-someone-pays. Scheduled. The kind of run where you know what day it is because it's the day you fly."

Elias looked at me for a long moment. "That's not a partnership."

"It's better than one. Partners split things. This way I always know where I stand — which is in the air, going somewhere specific, and then coming back."

"To where?" he asked, gently, because he knew exactly what he was asking.

I looked at the harbor. At Juno, who was pretending very hard not to smile and failing in a way visible from orbit. At the customs house with its labeled buckets, minor, moderate, structural — everything broken named and tended to, nothing pretended dry.

"Here," I said. "For a while. We'll see about after."

Juno punched me in the good arm. "Scheduled runs," she said. "*Sundays off.* Do you hear yourself? You've become a person with a calendar."

"Don't ruin it."

"Too late. I'm buying a calendar tomorrow. A big one. I'm writing your name in all the squares."

* * *

It took me nine days to get up the nerve, which was eight days longer than it should have taken, and I'll tell you why: because visiting a grave is easy and telling it news is hard. A grave visit is ritual. You bring flowers or whiskey or nothing, you stand, you leave. But news — real news, the kind that changes the shape of a story — requires you to believe the dead are listening, and I had spent seven years carefully believing they weren't.

The cemetery sat on the hill above the river town where I'd grown up, terraced into the slope so every plot got a view of the water. My father had chosen his spot years in advance, joking that he wanted to be buried where he could keep an eye on the gorge, and the family had honored it, and so he lay there facing exactly the stretch of river that killed him, which either proved he'd had a sense of humor or that the universe did.

I brought coffee. Two cups. One for me, one poured slowly onto the grass beside the headstone, because he'd have called the whole libation business pagan and then drunk his anyway.

"Almeida," I said. "You absolute mule."

The headstone said ALMEIDA, T., and beneath it a line he'd dictated himself: *He measured rivers.* No dates, because he'd insisted dates were "clerical." The stonemason had charged extra and my mother had paid it and told the story for years afterward with the laugh she saved for stories about him.

"You're not going to believe what happened, so I'll tell it straight." I sat down in the grass. It was damp. I stayed anyway. "The Corvina came up. Well — part of it. Low water showed her ribs like a whale skeleton, and I stood inside her hold, Dad. I stood where you died trying to get to, and I'm sorry, because I know you wouldn't have died for gold, you'd have died for the *being right*, and you were right, the manifest was real, the will was forged, the whole rotten claim Vey built his life on was paper and lies. Your friend's son has it all back now. Elias. He reads letters out loud like they're scripture and he cries during the third one every time, and Juno says he cries, and he says he doesn't, and honestly it's the most alive argument I've heard in years."

A bird landed on the next stone over and regarded me with the frank insolence of birds everywhere.

"I scuttled it. The treasure. Most of it. I opened her seams and let the gorge take it back, and before you start — yes, I know what you'd say. I know you spent your whole life chasing that hull. But you chased it because of what it *proved*, not what it weighed, and the proof came home. The proof is in a courtroom record now with a clerk's flag on it saying *structural*, and no flood can take that back down."

The coffee steamed between us. Downriver, the gorge ran high and brown and closed for the season, holding whatever was left of the Corvina the way a fist holds a secret it has finally decided to keep.

"I flew medicine for seven years so I'd never have to stop moving," I said. "You knew that. You probably knew it before I did. You always could read my weather from a long way off." I swallowed. "I'm staying awhile. Port Meridian. Scheduled runs. Sundays off. Juno's buying a calendar."

The wind came up the valley the way it does in the evenings, warm and river-smelling, and moved through the cemetery grass, and I am not going to tell you it was him, because I don't believe that, and neither did he. But I let it be enough anyway, which I think is the whole trick of graves: not signs, just permission.

"I measure years now," I told him. "Not miles. You'd hate how slow it feels. You'd also love it."

I drank my coffee. I poured the rest of his out slow, the way he liked, and sat until the light went bronze and the fishing boats came home, and then I stood, and brushed off my trousers, and said the thing I'd flown seven years of valleys to be able to say:

"See you Sunday, old man. Same time. I'll bring the news."

* * *

The hangar at Meridian field had a leak in the roof directly over the Leech's engine hoist, which Juno declared "thematically appropriate" and fixed with a tarp and a great deal of swearing. We were three days into fitting out for the scheduled run — crates stenciled CLINIC, MAIL, PARTS, and one unlabeled crate Juno refused to explain — when Elias appeared in the doorway holding a document folder and wearing the expression of a man carrying something fragile across ice.

"This came for you," he said. "Forwarded from the coastal office. It's been following you around for weeks, apparently."

The envelope was travel-worn, stamped and re-stamped, addressed in a hand I didn't know. Inside, a single page, written in careful, formal English by someone for whom English was a second language, maybe a third:

*Mrs. Almeida — they say you fly where the roads drown. Our clinic up the Varra lost its nurse to the fever and we have no one. The rains come soon. I am told you do not always say yes. I am asking anyway.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you read something you intend to keep.

Juno drifted over, wiping grease off her hands. "Well? Charter or charity?"

I looked at the letter. At the rain-fringed mountains past the field fence, already gathering their weather. At the Leech's sister plane — my plane, the one I'd flown seven years of miles in — sitting fueled and ready on the apron, going nowhere in particular until now.

"Scheduled," I said, and folded the letter into my breast pocket, over the heart, where the important cargo rides. "First run of the season. Up the Varra."

Juno grinned and reached for the manifest.
