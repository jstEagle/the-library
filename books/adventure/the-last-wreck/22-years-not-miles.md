## 22. Years, Not Miles

The garden was Juno's idea, which is to say it was an argument I lost in April and have been losing gracefully ever since.

"You can't keep a person alive on tinned peaches and spite," she'd said, kneeling in the dirt behind the hangar with a packet of tomato seeds and the expression of a woman who had already decided how this was going to go. "I've seen your cupboards. There's geometry happening in there. The cans are organizing."

"They're stacked by weight," I said. "It's efficient."

"It's a cry for help."

So now there is a garden. Tomatoes that came in lopsided and furious, beans that climbed the fence wire like they were escaping something, a row of peppers Juno swears are ornamental and eats anyway. I water it most mornings before the first flight, which means I stand in one place for ten minutes at a time doing nothing but watching water soak into ground. Seven years ago that would have felt like drowning. Now it feels like weather.

It's strange, the things you measure when you stop measuring distance.

* * *

The medicine run hasn't changed much. Same valleys, same storm-fringed ridgelines, same little strips carved into river bends where someone waves you down with a shirt or a lantern or both. What's changed is me, mostly. I used to fly those runs like a man fleeing a fire behind him — fast, low over the trees, always arriving somewhere else before the past could catch up. Now I fly them like a woman going to work.

There's more time in that. Time to notice the way morning fog sits in the valleys like milk in bowls. Time to wave back at the kids who run alongside the strip. Time, once, to land early because old Marta at Kilometre Nine flagged me down not for medicine but because she'd made too much fish stew and her late husband had always said no pilot should fly hungry, and she'd decided to honor the dead by force-feeding the living. I ate two bowls. It was terrible. I'd have fought anyone who said so.

Juno flies half the runs now. She got her license in March, on the third try, and when the examiner signed off she walked out to the plane, patted the cowling, and said, "You're demoted, sweetheart," to the aircraft, not to me. She still calls the Leech "the boat" with a fondness neither of us examines too closely. Some scars you keep polished.

Elias has his name back — all of it, the estate, the house above Port Meridian with the widow's walk his father built and never stood on. He writes every few weeks, letters full of ledgers and lawsuits and, lately, a slow astonishment that the world will simply hand you things if you show up with the right papers and refuse to leave. Last month he wrote: *I stood in my father's study today and read his letters aloud to an empty room. It seemed like the sort of thing he would have wanted an audience for.* I wrote back that empty rooms are the best audiences; they never interrupt, and they never ask what happened to the gold.

He never asks about the gold. That's how I know he understood what we did out there, in the gorge, in the rain like iron. We didn't lose it. We spent it — on Vey's ruin, on the tribunal's verdict, on the right to say the Corvina belonged to a story instead of a strongbox. Elias got the documents, the name, the justice. The river got the rest. As far as I'm concerned, the river earned it.

Vey survived. Of course he did. Men like Marcus Vey don't drown; they just run aground somewhere shallower and start counting again. They say he's upriver still, running cargo, smaller operations, fewer rifles. They say he lost his crew's loyalty the day he surfaced from the gorge with nothing in his hands and started blaming the water. I don't think about him much. When I do, it's with the mild pity you'd give any man who traded everything for a chest that turned out to be full of paper — and then drowned trying to hold the paper down.

Sundays, though. Sundays are the real revolution.

I didn't plan them. They planned themselves, the way habits do when you're not looking. First it was just that the strip closed for maintenance one Sunday and I had nowhere to be and discovered, with genuine suspicion, that nowhere-to-be wasn't going to kill me. Then Marta started expecting me for lunch. Then Juno started bringing her radio and we'd sit in the garden listening to coast stations play music through static, arguing about whether a song was good or merely familiar. Then Elias started coming up on the mail boat when he could, sleeves rolled, city shoes ruined, learning to repot tomatoes badly.

A year ago I measured my life in miles flown. Miles were safe. Miles couldn't die on you in a gorge. But miles add up to nothing — you can't plant them, can't eat them, can't sit inside one while the rain comes down. Years, it turns out, are where the living happens. You have to stay put long enough to catch them at it.

* * *

The letter came on a Tuesday in October, which is monsoon's tail-end, when the sky can't decide if it's finished with us.

I'd landed late from Kilometre Twelve, delivering antibiotics and picking up a crate of oranges somebody's cousin insisted on sending us as payment, and there it was in the mail slot at the strip office — wedged between a seed catalogue and a bill, an envelope with no return address, addressed in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

I knew before I opened it. You learn to know. Seven years ago a letter with a certain kind of weight meant trouble dressed as opportunity, and my hands remembered the shape of that lesson even if my mind had filed it away.

But here's the thing about peace: it isn't the absence of trouble. It's knowing the difference between trouble that chases you and trouble that waits politely to be invited.

I didn't open it at the strip. I carried it home, set it on the kitchen table, went out and watered the garden — the beans needed it, and so did I, honestly, ten minutes of standing still — and then I made tea, and sat down, and opened it slowly, the way you open something you intend to finish reading rather than survive reading.

*Almeida,* it began. *You don't know me, but the river does.*

My tea went cold while I read. That's fine. Tea forgives.

The writer was a woman named Ondine Castellanos, writing from a settlement two days above the drowned village — a place I'd thought was empty, and mostly is, except apparently for eleven families who stayed after the dam schemes collapsed, farming the floodplain the engineers abandoned. No doctor within four days' travel. A child with a heart that beats wrong. A midwife getting older. And, she wrote, a wreck.

Not the Corvina. Something older, a trading barge from before the surveys, sitting in a side channel the floods only uncover some years. The villagers have known about it for generations. They call it the Grandmother. And last spring, she wrote, men came asking about it. Men with ledgers and rifles and a way of talking about salvage that made the word sound like a knife being sharpened. She didn't name them. She didn't have to. She described the leader — *a tidy man, counts everything twice, smiles like a contract* — and I set the letter down and looked out the window at my ridiculous, thriving, stubborn garden and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

Some rivers keep feeding the same fish forever.

She'd heard, she wrote, of a pilot who once flew into the interior when no one else would, who knew the gorges, who had — and here she hesitated on the page, I could feel it — *history with the water*. She wasn't asking me to come fight anyone. She was asking me to fly medicine, the ordinary kind, the kind I already carry. The rest — the wreck, the men, the Grandmother — she asked only that I know. *"Because,"* she finished, *"the ones who know the river's history are the only ones who can tell when someone's about to steal it."*

I read it twice. Then I folded it along its original creases, set it under the pepper pot on the windowsill — our system for Important Things, established by Juno, contested by no one — and went outside.

The rain had started again, soft, the polite kind. I stood on the step and let it hit my face and did the arithmetic I've gotten good at this year. Not miles. Not money. The other kind: how many days until Sunday. How many tomatoes left. How long a flight to a settlement two days above the drowned village, carrying nothing heavier than medicine and maybe, if the child's heart needed it, staying a night or two.

The wreck could wait. Wrecks are patient; it's their whole nature. The men with ledgers could wait too — or not, and if not, well. I'd met their kind before, and I'd learned the one thing worth knowing about them: they count everything twice, and they never count the people who love the river, and that omission is where they always, always come apart.

Juno appeared at the fence line, umbrellaless, unbothered, holding two cups of the terrible coffee she insists on making.

"You've got a letter face," she said.

"I have many faces."

"This is the one you make when the world's about to get interesting and you're pretending to deliberate." She handed me a cup. "So? Trouble?"

I drank. It was terrible. I'd have fought anyone who said so.

"Medicine run," I said. "Two days upriver. A sick kid, a village that stayed when everyone else left." I paused, and let myself smile, small and unhurried, the smile of a woman who has learned that adventure is not a thing that happens to you but a thing you agree to, on your own terms, with good company and a full tank. "And maybe a story about a wreck called the Grandmother."

Juno grinned. "When do we leave?"

"After Sunday," I said. "Some things you don't rush."

The rain kept falling, gentle, on the garden, on the hangar, on the whole green breathing valley — and for the first time in longer than I could measure, I wasn't counting the miles at all.
