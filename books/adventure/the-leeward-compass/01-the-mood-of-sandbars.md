## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Vellwater woke mean that morning, and Mirabel Nystrom loved it for that.

He stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with his boots planted wide on the deck plates, feeling the river's temper come up through the soles of his feet the way other men read weather in their knees. Dawn had come up the color of pewter, and a wind out of the northwest was combing the water into long gray ridges that broke white where they crossed the bars. Bad light for reading water. Good light for a man who didn't want to be seen.

"Two feet over the channel mark," he murmured to himself, watching a heron lift off the point at Miller's Bend. The bird flew low and heavy, wings still wet from fishing the shallows. A heron that lazy meant fish were running in the slack water off the bar, which meant the bar itself had shifted since the last meltwater pulse, which meant the channel he'd memorized three days ago was already a lie.

The river lied constantly. That was the first thing his father had taught him, standing on a deck not much bigger than this one, before everything else happened. *The river lies, boy. It shows you a smooth stretch and puts a snag under it. It shows you a deep run and fills it with silt overnight. Your whole job is to know when it's lying.* His father had known. Right up until the night he hadn't.

Mirabel put the memory down the way he put down all of them — carefully, face-down, like something that might still be alive.

The *Kestrel* ran light this trip: forty barrels of pitch resin bound for Corren's Ford, a crate of survey instruments he'd promised not to look inside, and eleven sacks of salted eel for a factor who paid cash and asked nothing. Small cargo. Honest enough cargo. The kind of run that kept a boat moving and a name quiet, and quiet was the whole architecture of Mirabel's life. Ten years on the Vellwater and its tributaries, ten years of building himself into a man nobody looked at twice — weathered, unremarkable, a working captain among hundreds of working captains, his papers clean because he'd paid good money for them to be clean, his debts scattered across three jurisdictions like seed corn, sprouting nowhere he intended to harvest.

Mostly.

He eased the throttle back a notch as the bend came on, letting the current do the work. The *Kestrel* answered him the way an old dog answers — not eagerly, but without argument. She was thirty years old if she was a day, patched at every seam, her paddlewheel singing its one cracked note, and he knew her hull the way he knew his own hands: every soft spot, every scar, every place the caulking wept a little in cold water and sealed itself again when the sun came out. She wasn't much. She was his. Well — she was mostly his. The bank in Graylock held a lien on her that was less a debt than a chronic condition, like a bad tooth you stopped naming after a while.

Ahead, the river split around a long sandbar that hadn't been there in spring. He could see it building even now, the current laying down its pale crescents of silt with the patience of a mason. By late summer it would be an island. By the calving floods next year it would be gone or moved or doubled, and some other shape would stand in its place, and the pilots who ran this stretch would argue about it in the taverns as though the river were capable of being argued with.

Mirabel took the eastern channel, wide of the new bar, and felt the *Kestrel* shudder once as she crossed the seam where two currents met and quarreled. Then smooth water. He allowed himself the small luxury of rolling his shoulders.

"Morning, Captain."

Deckhand. Boy named Tam Ossel, sixteen, all elbows and appetite, hired at Corren's Ford two runs back because he worked cheap and asked fewer questions than most cats. He came up the companionway with two cups of chicory and handed one over without ceremony, which was the main reason Mirabel kept him.

"Bar's moved," Tam said, squinting astern at the pale crescent sliding by.

"Bars always move."

"You knew it'd moved."

"I guessed. Guessing right is ninety percent of this work." Mirabel drank. The chicory was terrible. He drank it anyway. "The other ten percent is knowing when your guess is wrong fast enough to do something about it."

Tam considered this with the serious frown of youth confronted by wisdom it suspects might be nonsense. "My uncle says the river's got moods. Says you can tell what it'll do by how it smells."

"Your uncle's right, which should worry you, coming from a man who also swears by wearing copper against rheumatism." Mirabel nodded upstream, toward the gray wall of the morning. "Smell it? Green and sour, like a pond turned over?"

Tam sniffed. "Maybe."

"That's the sloughs breathing. Water sitting too long in the back channels, going stale. Means the meltwater pulse hasn't pushed through yet. Means the level's going to drop another hand-span by tomorrow, maybe two." He tapped the rail. "Means every bar between here and Graylock is going to grow teeth tonight. We make the Ford landing before dark or we tie up and lose a day."

"We'll make it," Tam said, with the beautiful unearned confidence of sixteen.

Mirabel almost smiled. "We'll see."

* * *

They made the middle reaches by noon, and that was where the trouble started — not river trouble, which Mirabel could handle, but the other kind, the kind that walked on two legs and wore a Survey badge.

He saw the inspection launch first as a smudge of smoke above the willow line at Tarnish Point, then as a shape: the Survey cutter *Verity*, white hull, brass fittings kept bright enough to insult every working boat on the river, running downstream at an easy patrol speed. Official colors flying. Two figures at her rail with the particular posture of men whose job was looking at other men.

Mirabel did not swear, though he wanted to. Swearing was noise, and noise carried.

"Tarp," he said quietly. "The crate marked Fennick & Sons. Canvas over it, rope tight, and then go sit on the bow and eat your lunch like a boy with no thoughts in his head."

Tam went. He was learning.

The problem was simple and unsolvable in the way most of Mirabel's problems were: the *Verity* was commanded, or at least staffed, by a port inspector named Cullo Marsh, and Cullo Marsh had seen Mirabel Nystrom's face at close range four years ago, in Graylock, on a night involving a bonded cargo manifest, a locked customs shed, and a quantity of Mirabel's own signature in places it ought never to have been. Marsh had been junior then, eager, and had chased him through three streets before losing him at the tannery sluice. Marsh would be senior now. Senior men remembered better. Senior men had time.

The *Verity* was holding midstream, and the channel here ran narrow past the point. There was no way past her that didn't bring him within hailing distance. So Mirabel did what he had trained himself to do for ten years, which was become slightly less present.

He took off his good coat — the one with the collar that sat right — and pulled on the oilskin with the torn shoulder. He dragged a thumb through the grease rag he kept by the engine hatch and marked his jaw with it, casual, the way a working man gets marked without noticing. He let his shoulders round. It was astonishing, the smallness of the transformation and how completely it worked: Captain Nystrom, master of the *Kestrel*, holder of papers, keeper of debts, folded himself away somewhere behind his own eyes, and what remained at the rail was just a tired man in a bad coat running resin downstream, the kind of man whose name you'd have to check twice and wouldn't bother.

The *Verity* grew. He counted her crew without appearing to: six, plus the two at the rail. One of the rail men raised a hand — routine hail, checking traffic, nothing more, probably, probably — and Mirabel raised his own hand back, slow, incurious, a man conserving energy for cargo.

"Ho, the boat!" The voice carried flat across the water. "Whence and whither?"

"Corren's Ford down from Tarnish mills!" Mirabel called back, pitching it right — a shade too rough, a shade bored. "Resin and salt eel!"

The pause stretched. On the cutter's rail, the second figure leaned and said something to the first. Mirabel kept his eyes forward, hands easy on the wheel, heart working like a pump at flood stage while his face did absolutely nothing at all. This was the trick of it, the only trick he'd ever mastered: the outside stays boring. Let the inside scream. The outside stays boring.

"Papers?" the voice called, finally, and there was a note in it Mirabel didn't like — not suspicion exactly, but the diligence of a man building a file.

"Bill of lading's on the board, per regulation!" Which was true; he kept it posted in the wheelhouse window, legible, dull, correct. Regulations were wonderful things. They were walls built by other people that a clever man could stand behind.

Another pause. Then the hand waved him on — *carry on, carry on* — and the *Verity* slid past to starboard close enough that Mirabel could see the brass buttons and the bored eyes and, for one half-second, the face of Cullo Marsh himself, older, heavier through the jaw, squinting at the *Kestrel* with the mild professional attention of a man cataloguing boats he'd catalogued a thousand times.

Their eyes did not meet. That was the whole art. Their eyes did not meet, and Marsh looked away first, at nothing, at the water, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

Mirabel held his course and his expression for a full mile. Then he exhaled, long and slow, and discovered his shirt was stuck to his back.

"Captain?" Tam, at the bow, around a mouthful of bread.

"Eat your lunch."

"Was that—"

"It was an inspection launch, and we're a cargo boat, and you were eating lunch." Mirabel turned the wheel a spoke. "Finish the lesson: what did you learn?"

Tam chewed, thought. "Don't look at 'em?"

"Don't be worth looking at." He said it lightly, and it landed heavier than he meant it to, the way true things do when you dress them up as jokes. He watched the *Verity*'s smoke dwindle downstream, toward Graylock, toward the landings and the offices and the ledger rooms where the valley's debts lived in neat rows like coffins in a crypt. Somewhere in those rooms was a page with his name on it. Several pages. He'd made sure of their dispersal the way a careful man scatters a fire risk.

Ten years. Ten years of this, and he was good at it, and being good at it was the loneliest skill he owned.

* * *

Toward evening the river changed its mind, the way it did, and gave them an hour of gold.

The light came low across the water and turned the Vellwater to hammered bronze, and the sandbars showed themselves plainly for the first time all day — pale spines laid along the channel like the backs of sleeping animals. Mirabel ran the last miles with the sun full in his eyes and the *Kestrel* humming under him, and for a little while the day forgave him everything. This was the payment, he supposed. This was why men stayed on the river when the river took their fathers and their names and their sleep. Not the money, which was thin. Not the freedom, which was mostly a rumor. But hours like this one, when the boat and the water and the man at the wheel agreed on a single purpose, and nothing in the world needed to be forgotten because nothing in the world was being remembered either.

Tam slept on the cargo hatch, mouth open, hat over his face. The banks slid by: alder and willow, a heron rookery loud as a market, one lit window far back in the trees where somebody's life was happening without him. Mirabel steered by habit and let his mind go quiet, and in the quiet, unwelcome as a stone in a shoe, came the thought that arrived most evenings lately:

*This is it, then. This is the whole of it. Running well, hiding well, dying eventually, owing everyone.*

He was thirty-four years old and he owned a boat that wasn't fully his, a debt that wasn't fully his — inherited, signed away by his father before Mirabel was old enough to hold a pen, a claim on their family's land traded for a season's survival and drowned with the man who'd signed it — and a talent for invisibility that had kept him alive and cost him everything else. Weary contentment, he'd heard a preacher call it once, though the preacher hadn't looked weary or content. Mirabel had both, in about equal measure, and neither one generously.

The landing lights of Corren's Ford came up ahead, strung along the bluff like a handful of coins spilled on dark cloth. Smoke from the kilns. The smell of tar and frying fish. Voices carrying across the water the way voices do at dusk, half-heard, half-invented.

He brought the *Kestrel* in on the last of the light, easy against the pilings, lines made fast, wheel lashed. Routine. All of it routine, and routine was the armor he wore, and the armor fit, and under the armor was a man who could not have said, if you'd asked him straight out, what he was keeping himself safe *for*.

"Fuel and water in the morning," he told Tam, shrugging into his good coat again, becoming, piece by piece, a person with a name. "Then we take the eel to the factor and we're done. Two days' layover, maybe three. Keep your money in your boot and stay off the far side of the market."

"Why the far side?"

"Because I said so." Because the far side was where the Survey kept its offices, and where lists were kept, and where a man with dispersed debts did not linger. "I've business at the tavern."

Which was true, in its way. The Gull's Rest sat on the near side of the landing, and Mirabel intended to sit in its deepest corner with a meal he hadn't cooked and a drink he could barely afford, and be invisible in a crowd for a few hours, which was the closest thing to rest his life offered.

He was halfway down the gangplank, collar up against the evening chill, when he saw her.

A woman at the foot of the dock, standing apart from the evening traffic, holding something wrapped in cloth against her chest the way you hold a thing that matters more than it weighs. She wasn't dressed like dockside women. Her coat was good wool gone thin at the cuffs, mourning-black, and she stood very still in the moving crowd, still the way people stand when they've been waiting a long time and have decided to wait however much longer it takes.

Her eyes found his across the planks — found him, picked him out of the dusk with unsettling precision, the way the compass in her arms would soon pick out a direction no compass should know — and she started toward the gangplank without hesitation, without doubt, as though she had been told his name and his boat and the hour of his arrival by someone who did not make mistakes.

Mirabel stood on his own gangplank, ten years of careful anonymity humming in his ears like a struck wire, and understood with a sinking, certain clarity that his quiet evening at the Gull's Rest was already gone.

She reached the bottom of the plank and looked up at him.

"Captain Nystrom," she said. Not a question.
