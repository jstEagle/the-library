## 2. A Widow at Graylock Landing

Graylock Landing smelled of tar and old rain, the way every river town did when the fog came up off the Vellwater in the hour before dawn. Mirabel Nystrom sat in the back corner of the Drowned Gull with his back to the wall and his face turned toward the window, which was to say he sat the way he always sat: visible enough not to draw notice, positioned enough to see the door.

The tavern was doing its slow morning business — barge hands nursing coffee thick as pitch, a pair of net-menders arguing over the price of twine, the landlady's boy sweeping sawdust in circles that never quite met. A stove ticked in the corner, burning driftwood that popped and spat salt. Outside, the Kestrel rode low at her mooring, her hold full of salted pork and bolt linen bound for Corren's Ford, and Mirabel was three days from being invisible again.

He liked that about the river above all things: it did not ask who you were. The Vellwater asked only whether you could read it, and reading was the one inheritance his father had left him that no debt collector could seize.

He was on his second cup and his first lie of the day — telling himself he'd sleep aboard tonight instead of taking a room, saving the coin — when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a gray coat.

She was not what he expected, though he hadn't known he was expecting anyone. He had learned long ago to expect everyone. But the woman who crossed the Drowned Gull's floor moved like someone who had dressed in the dark and gotten most of it right: good wool gone shiny at the cuffs, boots resoled twice, hair pinned with the grim efficiency of a person who had stopped caring how she looked and started caring only that she looked *finished*. She carried a canvas satchel against her hip the way other women carried children.

She paused inside the door. Let her eyes do one slow circuit of the room — the bargemen, the net-menders, the boy with the broom — and then found him in the corner as if she'd been told exactly where he'd be sitting.

Mirabel's stomach performed the small cold drop it always performed when a stranger looked at him too directly. Ten years on the river taught you that directness was the first symptom of trouble. Indirect people wanted cargo carried or fish bought. Direct people wanted *you*.

The landlady intercepted her. Low words. The woman shook her head once, firm, and pointed — not subtly — at his table.

"Captain Nystrom," she said, arriving. Not asking. Her voice was low and even, the kind of voice that had been trained by grief into a flat calm, like water over deep mud.

"Madam," Mirabel said, "you've mistaken me for someone with money."

"I've mistaken you for nothing." She set the satchel on the table between them but did not sit. "You're the Nystrom who runs the Kestrel. Thirty feet, green hull patched with whatever was cheapest, draws less water than anything else working this stretch. You'll take cargo nobody else will and ask no questions nobody wants answered."

"That last part isn't true," Mirabel said. "I ask plenty of questions. I just don't answer them." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the wood, a habit, a small ritual of control. "Whoever told you about me wasted their breath."

"Osket Brann's people told me about you."

And there it was. Brann's name dropped like an anchor into the conversation, and Mirabel felt the hook set. The smuggler queen of the Gorge Narrows did not hand out recommendations; she handed out debts disguised as favors. He made his face go pleasantly blank, the expression that had gotten him out of more trouble than any knife.

"Brann's people say a lot of things," he said. "Half of them are tests."

"This isn't a test." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, finally, and up close he could see what the flat voice had been covering: a tiredness so complete it had gone past the eyes and settled into the hands, which lay on the table as if they'd forgotten they were hers. "My name is Sela Voss. My husband is Aldric Voss. He went upriver with a government survey crew in the spring, and he has not come down."

The stove popped. Somewhere behind the bar a keg was tapped, a wet knock and sigh.

"Lots of men go upriver," Mirabel said. "The headwaters eat them. It's practically a trade."

"He wrote to me in the third week. Then nothing." She opened the satchel without hurry, without drama, and laid things on the scarred table one at a time, the way a merchant lays out goods she knows are sound. Letters, tied with string. A folded survey commission bearing the wax seal of the Department of Lands and Waters. And a small brass object wrapped in oilcloth, which she unwrapped with fingers that trembled — though Mirabel noticed, with the part of his mind that never stopped noticing, that the trembling was not weakness. It was the careful shaking of someone handling something precious and hated at once.

The compass was old-fashioned, heavy-cased, the kind surveyors used before the new instruments came in. Its glass was scratched. Its brass had gone the color of weak tea. Someone had loved it enough to keep it polished until recently, and then not.

"It stopped pointing north," Sela Voss said. "Three weeks after his last letter. I've had it next to two other compasses and a lodestone and it ignores all of them. It points upstream. Toward the glacier country." She looked up at him, and her eyes were brown and absolutely steady, which frightened him more than tears would have. "I want to hire you to take me up there and find him."

"No," said Mirabel.

He said it quickly, because he'd learned that quick refusals sounded like principle and slow ones sounded like negotiation. He stood, put coins by his cup, and gathered his coat.

"Double your rate," she said to his back. "Whatever your rate is. Double it."

"The rate doesn't matter, madam, because the answer is the same either way." He shrugged into the coat. "Nobody runs the upper reaches during melt season. The channels move daily. The gorge floods without warning. And the Survey has the whole upper valley closed — permits, patrols, gunboats. I don't carry government paper and I don't argue with government guns. Those are the two rules that have kept me alive, and your husband's commission is the second rule written in wax."

"Aldric didn't take that commission because he loved the government." Something moved under the flatness of her voice now, a current under ice. "He took it because it was the only way a man with our debts could earn forgiveness instead of interest. They send indentured men where free men won't go. You'd know something about that, Captain. Or should I say — *Nystrom*?"

The name landed on the tavern air between them, and Mirabel felt every ear in the room strain toward it without turning a single head. That was the trick of river towns: everyone listened while appearing to sweep.

He sat back down. It was, he would tell himself later, purely tactical — better to control the conversation than let it curdle in public. But some honest part of him knew that the name had done what her coin could not.

"You've been reading files," he said quietly.

"I've been reading everything I can find about the men who might say yes." She leaned forward. Up close her exhaustion had a structure to it, almost architectural, like a bridge built to hold far more than it was rated for. "There aren't many. The free captains won't go. The indentured captains can't leave their routes. And the ones crazy enough to try the headwaters mostly work for Brann, and Brann's price is a cut of whatever you find. I'm not offering a cut. I'm offering wages. There's a difference, and I think you're a man who knows the difference."

"There's a difference," Mirabel agreed, "and it's smaller than you think. Wages get spent. Cuts get counted. Either way, come winter you're where you started." He turned the cup again. "I'm sorry about your husband, Mrs. Voss. Truly. The river takes men, and the ice takes more, and there's no wage that buys them back. Find yourself a Survey clerk with a soft heart and file for widow's relief. It's faster than grief and pays better than hope."

"You think I haven't tried the clerks?" For the first time her voice cracked its casing, and what came through underneath was not hysteria but fury, banked and ancient. "They gave me a form. The form asks for a body. No body, no relief, no inquiry, no record. As far as the Department of Lands and Waters is concerned, my husband walked off the edge of the map and the map is not required to care." She drew a breath and got the flatness back, reassembled it piece by piece, and Mirabel watched her do it and recognized the labor of it the way one craftsman recognizes another's joinery. "So I'll stop wasting both our time. Here is what I can pay."

She took papers from the satchel. Contract papers — he knew the format instantly, the way a man knows the shape of his own cage. River indenture, standard issue, the document that turned a debt into a decade. She slid them across the table.

"His contract," she said. "When he took the survey posting, they converted his debt into service. If he's dead, the debt passes to me. If he's alive, we're both still in it. Either way—" she tapped the page— "it's mine to carry now."

Mirabel looked down at the paper, meaning only to glance, to confirm what he already knew, and then he saw it.

Lower left corner. Where the registry seal went.

A brand, not a stamp — burned into the fiber with a hot iron, the way livestock are marked, the way his own father's contract had been marked thirty years ago in a courthouse in Lowmarsh while a boy stood watching through the rail. The mark of the Vellwater Consolidated Debt Office: a circle bisected by a wavy line, the river and the ring, the symbol that meant *this human being is collateral*. Below it, in the clerk's cramped hand, the registration number, and beside the number a word that the Debt Office added for debts inherited rather than incurred:

*SURCHARGE.*

They charged you extra for inheriting. His father had died owing eleven crowns of surcharge on a debt of forty. Mirabel had spent ten years making sure no office ever again held a paper with his name on the collateral line, and here was a widow with the same burn mark on her papers, sitting in the same kind of tavern, making the same kind of offer to the same kind of man his father had been — the kind who says yes because saying yes feels like swimming toward shore even when it's the open sea.

The stove ticked. The net-menders argued. The boy swept his endless circles.

"How much does he owe?" Mirabel heard himself ask. It came out rougher than he intended.

"Ninety-two crowns, with the surcharge." She said it the way people say ages and anniversaries, numbers worn smooth by repetition. "At the current assessment it'll be a hundred and four by spring. It grows, Captain. Did yours grow? They always grow. That's the design."

He should have said no again. Every instinct honed across a decade of narrow escapes said *no*, said *the Survey closed those waters for reasons, said Halloway's gunboats patrol the middle reaches, said widows with brass compasses that point wrong are the beginning of every story that ends badly.* He had survived this long precisely by refusing such offers, by keeping his hull shallow and his name small and his curiosity drowned like a cat in a sack.

But she had said *did yours grow*, and she had laid the branded paper on the table like a mirror, and Mirabel Nystrom — who had built a life on the principle that the safest thing to feel was nothing — discovered that the nothing had a seam in it, and the seam had split.

"What makes you think he's alive?" he asked.

It was the wrong question. It was the question of a man who had already decided, and they both knew it. Sela Voss did not smile — her face seemed to have misplaced the machinery for it — but something in her posture eased, a cable paying out half a fathom.

"Because the compass still moves," she said. She turned the brass instrument toward him on the table. "Watch."

The needle hung quivering, straining upstream like a hound at a leash, trembling off true with a fine, ceaseless vibration — not swinging, not settling, but *shivering*, as if it heard something under the world and could not decide whether to fear it or follow it.

"They say the headwaters are forbidden because of the ice," she said softly. "Aldric used to say that was the official reason. He said the real reason is that the maps up there are lies, and lies need guarding." She met his eyes. "I'm not asking you to be brave, Captain. I'm asking you to be greedy. Double your rate, paid half in advance, and the rest when we find him or find where he stopped. That's the offer. It doesn't improve, and it doesn't repeat."

Mirabel looked at the compass shivering toward the glacier country. He looked at the burned circle on her contract, the river and the ring. He thought of his father's grave on the bluff above Lowmarsh, unmarked except by a debt number chiseled cheap and already weathering illegible, and of the ten years he had spent being no one to everyone, and of how well it had worked, and how much it had cost, and how the bill for safety, like every other bill on the Vellwater, only grew.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the landing, and somewhere upstream the river ran dark and high with melt, carrying the year's calendar toward the gorge whether anyone was ready or not.

"Half in advance," Mirabel said. "Paid to the harbormaster, not to me — I don't touch coin in towns where my face is known. We load today and run the middle reaches tomorrow at first light, ahead of the rise. You cook, you pull rope, and you follow my orders on the water without question, because the questions kill people up there faster than the ice does." He stood, and stuck out a hand that had not offered itself to anyone in longer than he cared to count. "And Mrs. Voss — if we find him dead, the contract ends there. I don't haul bodies for wages. The river keeps its own."

Sela Voss took his hand. Her grip was stronger than her hands looked, and colder, as if she'd been holding the compass too long.

"Then let's hope," she said, "that whatever's up there holding his needle — it's still holding *him*."

* * *

He loaded through the afternoon and told himself the heaviness in his chest was just the cargo riding wrong.

By dusk the Kestrel sat deep and trim at her mooring, and Sela Voss had claimed the forward bunk with the economy of a woman who had lived in worse. Mirabel stood alone in the wheelhouse running his pre-departure checks by lamplight — lines, pump, lamp-oil, the shallow-draft plates he'd need for the gravel bars above Corren's Ford — and it was only when he reached the last item on the list, the one he'd written himself years ago in ink gone brown, that his hand stopped.

*Item nine: nothing aboard that the Survey wants.*

Below in the cabin, faint through the deck boards, he could hear the small sound of the brass compass being taken from its oilcloth and set gently down — and beneath the river's night-noise, beneath the slap of water and the creak of mooring line, he would have sworn he could hear it trembling. Like a second pulse in the hull. Like something upstream, under all that ice, calling roll.

Mirabel blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening.

Then he went down to make ready, because first light comes early, and the melt waits for no one.
