## 3. What the Compass Points To

The Kestrel rode low at her moorings off Tarnwick Point, riding the slow brown breath of the river, and Mirabel Nystrom sat alone in her wheelhouse with a dead man's compass in his hands and no honest reason for the feeling in his chest.

He had told himself he wouldn't look at it again until morning. He had told himself a great many things since the widow Voss had walked out of the Saltcellar with her widow's black dragging the sawdust, and none of them had held. He had told himself he'd sleep. He had told himself the compass was brass and glass and a needle, nothing more, and that a needle could be bent, could be lied to, could be dropped on a stair and knocked a hair off true so that it pointed at nothing but its own damage. He had told himself that whatever had happened to Aldric Voss and his six surveyors was government business, and that government business was a current he had spent ten years learning to swim around.

But he had taken the compass anyway. He had taken it the way a man takes a splinter into his thumb — without deciding to, and then unable to stop touching it.

Now the wheelhouse lamp burned low and orange, and the river talked to itself below the hull in that half-voice it used at night, the one that was never quite a whisper and never quite a warning, and Mirabel set the compass on the chart table and watched it.

It was a handsome thing. Old brass gone the color of dark honey, the case dented at one shoulder as if it had been dropped once and forgiven. The lid bore an engraving worn nearly smooth — he tilted it to the lamplight and caught the ghost of it, a mountain over a river, and beneath, letters he could only half read: *V. ET —* something. The glass over the dial had a fine scratch across it, like a hair caught under ice. And the needle —

The needle was wrong.

Not broken. Not sluggish. *Wrong* in a way that made the back of his neck prickle, because it wasn't trembling randomly the way a damaged needle does, shivering toward whatever had knocked it loose. It trembled *with intent*. It swung a slow arc, settled, swung again, like a hound testing the wind. And every time it settled, it settled in the same place — and the same place was not north.

Mirabel knew north the way he knew his own pulse. Ten years on the Vellwater would do that to a man; the river ran south by southwest out of the mountains and a pilot who couldn't hold north in his bones was a pilot who ended up wrapped around a wing dam with his cargo on the bottom. He turned the compass ninety degrees. The needle swung, wavered, and came back to its bearing — upstream. Northeast by a hair, up the long axis of the valley, toward the country no charts bothered to draw in detail because no pilot was supposed to go there.

He turned it again. Upstream. He carried it out onto the deck, cold air slapping his face, the river a black muscle sliding past, and held it level over the rail. Upstream. He walked it aft, walked it forward, stood at the stern where the wake unspooled behind them like a silver thread being paid out. Upstream. Always upstream, always that same hair-off-northeast line, as if the needle were a fish on a line he couldn't see, being drawn by something at the far end of it.

"Damn you," he said softly, to the compass, or to Aldric Voss, or to himself.

He went back inside and dug out his own compass — a cheap tin thing, river-pilot's grade, bought off a chandler in Lowmarsh and trusted with his life a hundred times. He set the two instruments side by side on the chart table. His own needle lay steady, faithful, pointing north the way it always had, north toward the port towns and the debt offices and the magistrates, north toward everything he had ever run from. The brass compass ignored it completely. The two needles might as well have belonged to different worlds.

There was a word for what that meant and he didn't like the word. Iron in the hull would do it — but the Kestrel was timber and copper, and he'd felt no pull, no deviation, at the wheel for a decade. Lodestone in the hills could bend a needle near a shore — but they were anchored mid-channel, and the needle wasn't bending, it was *choosing*. He had seen a needle choose only once before, and the memory of it came up out of him like cold water from a deep well, and with it came the voice of the man who had shown it to him.

* * *

Old Tam Vetch had run the middle river back when Mirabel was all elbows and appetite, a boy with his father's debt already stapled to his name and nothing to his own but quick hands. Tam had a face like a dried riverbed and a way of talking to the water as if it were a temperamental horse, and he was the one who taught Mirabel to read a sandbar by the color of the foam, to smell rain two bends away, to feel a snag under the hull before the hull felt it. He was also the one who taught Mirabel the first rule of the Vellwater, the rule that came before all other rules, the rule every pilot learned with his mother's milk and kept like a scar:

*The river ends where the ice begins. You don't go up past the Gorge. Nobody goes up past the Gorge.*

Not *can't*. *Don't*. And when a boy of sixteen had asked why — because Mirabel had always been a boy who asked why, and it had cost him — Tam had taken the wheel from him, eased the boat into the slack water below the Narrows, and told it plainly, the way he told everything, as if reciting cargo manifests.

"Because the Survey says so, and the Survey's word is law above the Gorge and nothing below it, and a smart man learns which laws have teeth. Because the water up there isn't water, it's *meltwater*, and it doesn't behave, it doesn't read, it rises and falls with the sun on the glacier like a woman's temper, and no chart made by man will save you in a channel that redraws itself every afternoon. And because—" and here Tam had paused, and looked upstream at the far white gleam of the high country, and for the first and only time in Mirabel's memory the old man had looked *uncertain*, "—because the river doesn't start up there. Something else does."

Mirabel had laughed. He could still hear himself laughing, that particular teenage contempt, and he could still feel the flat of Tam's hand crack against the back of his head, not hard, but with meaning.

"You think I'm superstitious," Tam had said. "Fine. Here's no superstition for you. Year before you were born, a Survey party went up to chart the headwaters. Eight men, a good boat, the best instruments money bought. The boat came back. It came back drifting, empty, riding high, three weeks later, sixty miles downriver, hull sound, cargo lashed and dry. The instruments were gone. Every chart, every logbook, every notebook — gone. The men were never found and never heard from, and the Survey paid off the families and burned the search reports, and I know that last part because I carried the man who burned them, and he drank half a bottle of my whiskey and told me the reports said the party's compasses had all gone mad at the same hour on the same day, all of them pointing the same wrong way, and then the entries stopped mid-sentence."

Tam had looked at him then with those riverbed eyes. "A compass doesn't go mad, boy. A compass is a needle and a lie we all agree on. But there's iron under the world in places, and there are things under the ice older than the Survey and older than the lie, and if a needle ever points you somewhere north isn't — you put it in your pocket, you keep your eyes on the channel, and you thank whatever god you don't believe in that it wasn't pointing at *you*."

That had been twenty years ago. Tam Vetch had been dead eleven of them, drowned in the very middle reach he'd ruled, taken by a spring flood that had come up out of a clear sky, and Mirabel had inherited his boat, his debts, and his rules, and had kept all three the way a man keeps a dead man's coat — wearing it without quite believing it still fits.

He sat in the wheelhouse now with Tam's rule in one ear and the brass compass humming its slow intent under the lamp, and he felt the two of them pulling him in opposite directions like a mooring line come taut.

* * *

He did what a careful man does. He tested it until testing became something else.

Through the small hours he worked the compass like a problem at the wheel. He turned off the lamp to see if the needle cared about light — it didn't. He carried it to the bow, to the stern, down into the hold among the crated freight, up to the roof of the wheelhouse under the cold spill of the stars. Upstream. Upstream. Upstream, with that hair of northeast in it, that same unerring line, and the closer he brought it to the bow — to the point of the boat, to the direction of the high country — the steadier it grew, until at the very stem of the Kestrel, held out over the black water, the needle stopped trembling altogether and lay quiet as a dead man's finger, pointing up the throat of the valley like a sentence waiting for its ending.

It was pointing at the glacier fields. He understood that with a certainty that had nothing to do with evidence. Not at the Gorge, not at the Narrows, not at any of the named and charted fears along the way — past all of them, past the end of the maps, at the white massif that sat on the horizon of every clear day like a debt unpaid, the headwaters, the forbidden country, the place where the Vellwater was born out of the ice and where, if Tam Vetch could be believed — and Tam Vetch could be believed, that had been the whole trouble with him — where something older than the Survey slept with its instruments in its teeth.

And the needle pointed at it the way a needle points at nothing else on earth. The way a needle points at home.

Mirabel stood at the stem for a long time, the cold working through his coat, and let himself think the thought he'd been refusing since the Saltcellar. Because the compass wasn't lost. That was the thing. A lost instrument wanders. This one *knew*. Somebody had made it to point here — or it had been made to point somewhere else and the here had gotten into it — and either way, a man had carried it up that river and it had brought him to whatever it pointed at, and the man had not come back down. Six men had not come back down. And a widow with burned papers was offering double rate for a dead man's route, and Mirabel Nystrom, who had survived a decade by never once following a needle that pointed anywhere but safe, was standing on the stem of his own boat at three in the morning, feeling the pull of it in his chest like the first lean of a current under the keel.

He thought about the debt mark on Sela Voss's contract. He thought about the one on his own, the inherited one, the Nystrom debt that had outlived his father the way a stain outlives the spilled wine, the number that had followed him down every mile of this river for ten years, growing interest like silt. He thought about how the Survey's men had come aboard once, years ago, young and bored and carrying the authority of the valley in their satchels, and how he had smiled and shown his papers and been invisible, and how being invisible had felt like winning and had slowly, over the years, come to feel like drowning.

The needle pointed upstream. The needle had never once pointed at anything for him. Everything he'd ever followed, he'd followed out of fear.

"Curiosity," he said aloud, to the river, and the river said nothing back, which was its way of agreeing. "That's how it gets you. That's how it got eight men and a boat."

But even as he said it he was already doing the arithmetic he couldn't stop himself from doing — the calving season, the meltwater, the window that opened above the Gorge for a few weeks each year and slammed shut like a trap; the distance to Corren's Ford, where a man could hear things in the market that never made it into official print; the fact that Captain Dree Halloway's name had come up twice in a week in two different river towns, which meant the Survey was already asking its own questions, which meant the current was already moving whether he swam in it or not.

He went back inside. He wrapped the brass compass in a square of oilcloth, the way Tam had taught him to wrap anything that mattered against the damp, and stowed it in the chart drawer under the tide tables, under his own hand-drawn charts of the middle reach, charts that stopped, every one of them, at a wavy line and a notation in his own younger handwriting: *Gorge Narrows — beyond this, nothing known, nothing owed, nothing wanted.*

He looked at that notation for a while. Then he took out his pencil and, beneath it, in small letters, added: *ask at Corren's Ford.*

It was the most reckless thing he had written in ten years, and it was only four words, and his hand was steady, and that frightened him more than the needle had.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin. The Kestrel took the current south, downriver toward Corren's Ford and the market noise and whatever was waiting for him there, and Mirabel stood at the wheel with the morning fog burning off the water in long rags and the compass a small hard weight against his chest where he'd stowed it inside his coat — not in the drawer after all, he'd found, when the moment came to leave it; some things a man tells himself he's putting away and then carries anyway.

The river ran wide and easy here, and he read it without thinking, the way he read everything — the foam-lines marking the channel, the leaning willows marking the current's edge, a raft of broken reeds riding the seam that said *shoal water, give it room*. His hands did the work. His mind was sixty miles upstream, in a white country he had never seen, where a needle pointed at a secret and eight men had walked out of their own camp into the snow.

By midmorning the smoke of Corren's Ford stood up over the trees, gray against gray, and the first market boats began to thicken the channel, and Mirabel brought the Kestrel in toward the landing with the unhurried competence of a man who has done this a thousand times and expects to do it a thousand more.

He was fifty yards off the dock when he saw the Survey launch tied at the customs berth, black hull, government gray trim, flying the quadrant colors, and a woman in a Survey officer's coat standing at the head of the gangway with her hands clasped behind her, watching the arriving boats come in one by one.

Watching them the way a needle watches north.

Mirabel eased the wheel over, unhurried, a man adjusting for current, and took the Kestrel in past the customs berth toward the far side of the landing — and did not look away from the channel, and did not look at the woman, and felt her attention pass over him like a shadow over the water, and heard, in the back of his skull where Tam Vetch's voice had lived for twenty years, a new and colder sentence being written:

*Ask at Corren's Ford. But ask carefully. Something there is already asking about you.*
