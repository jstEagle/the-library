## 4. The Survey's Shadow

Corren's Ford smelled of tar, tallow, and the particular rot of a market town that had grown too fast for its own drainage. Mirabel came in on the morning tide with a hold full of salted pike and a face he had practiced into blankness. That was the trick of it — not hiding, but forgetting. A man who looked like he was remembering something was a man worth watching. A man who looked like nothing at all could pass through the world like water through a net.

He tied off at the lower wharf, where the pilings leaned like tired old men and nobody asked questions about papers because half the boats there ran on none. The Kestrel settled against the fenders with a groan he'd learned to read as contentment. Ten years he'd kept her afloat, and ten years she'd kept him ahead of everything chasing him: creditors, inspectors, one memorable season of a revenue cutter captained by a man with a memory like a ledger book.

The compass rode inside his coat now, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. He'd told himself it was practical — the thing was brass and old and might fetch something if it came to that — but he knew the shape of a lie when he carried one. It wasn't the brass he was keeping warm. It was the question. All night at anchor, lying in his bunk listening to the river work at the hull, he had felt the thing through the layers of cloth, patient as a held breath, pointing upstream toward the white shoulders of the mountains where no boat was supposed to go.

*Later*, he told himself. *First the pike, first the coin, first the ordinary business of staying invisible.*

The fish sold well. Corren's Ford always ate well in the weeks before the melt, stocking larders against the high-water months when the middle river turned to brown fury and nothing moved but driftwood and drowned things. Mirabel worked his stall in the covered market with the ease of long habit, weighing, haggling, making change, and all the while doing what he actually did in every town on the Vellwater, which was listen.

A riverman's ears were worth more than his hands. By midmorning he had collected the usual harvest: grain prices up, the ferry at Two Sisters laid up with a cracked shaft, a customs clerk caught taking bribes and replaced by another customs clerk who would do the same within the year. And underneath the ordinary current, something else. A thread of unease running through the talk like cold water under warm.

It surfaced first at the pie stall next to his, where a woman named Marta Osk traded gossip the way other people traded grain.

"They've closed the upper valley," she said to a customer, not quietly enough. "Roads, that is. Survey posted notices at every gate from here to Graylock. No passage above the falls without writ."

"Since when?"

"Since the team went missing, I'd say." Marta lowered her voice, which only meant everyone leaned in. "Six men. Government men, mind, with proper papers and proper pay. Went up before the freeze to chart the headwaters, and come spring — nothing. Not a body, not a boot. The Survey sent its own people after them and those came back inside a fortnight, gray as ash, wouldn't say a word except *the route is closed*. Now they're paying good silver for any talk of strangers seen heading upriver."

Mirabel weighed out a customer's pike with steady hands and let the words wash over him without letting anything show in his face. But the cold thread had found him now. He felt it move along his spine.

*A survey team. Sela Voss's husband. Six men gone into the headwaters, and the government's answer was to close the valley and buy silence.*

That afternoon, in the back room of the Ferryman's Rest over a plate of eel stew he barely tasted, he heard the name.

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest was the kind of place where information changed hands faster than coin, and Mirabel had spent a decade learning its geography: which table put you in earshot of the dockhands, which corner caught the customs men's talk, which stool by the stove belonged to the pilot who ran the gorge runs and knew everything worth knowing between the falls and the delta. His name was Tam Wicke, and he was drinking alone, which meant he was willing to be bought with a bottle.

Mirabel set the bottle down and sat.

"Captain Nystrom." Tam didn't look surprised. Tam never looked surprised; it was the whole foundation of his trade. "Thought you were dead. Or downstream."

"Both, some days."

"Rumor says you took a passenger at Graylock. Woman. Widow, they say." Tam poured two fingers and studied the color of it. "Rumor also says she paid double rate and asked strange questions."

"Rumor says a lot of things."

"Rumor does." Tam drank. "But rumor doesn't get asked direct by Captain Halloway. Rumor doesn't have its name written on a list."

The word *list* went into Mirabel like a fishhook and sat there. He kept his hands easy on the table. "Whose list?"

"The Survey's." Tam said it lightly, the way a man names a storm. "There's an enforcer in town. Dree Halloway. Came up from the capital on the mail packet three days ago, wearing Survey gray and asking after boats. Specifically boats that might carry passengers upstream past the falls. She's got a paper with names on it, and she crosses them off or circles them, and I'm told — this from a man who saw it over her shoulder at the harbormaster's — that one of the names circled twice is Nystrom."

Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. Inside, Mirabel discovered that a man could hear his own heartbeat if the room went quiet enough.

"Halloway," he said. "What kind of captain is she?"

"The kind they send when they don't want answers. They say she burned a village's boats on the Senn once — all of them, in their slips, so the families couldn't fish the season. They say she smiles." Tam turned the bottle toward him, an offer or a warning. "You're going up there, aren't you. Past the falls. After whatever's left of that survey team."

"I'm going where the money points."

Tam laughed, short and without much humor. "Then point your money somewhere else, Captain. Whatever those six men found up in the ice, the Survey would rather it stayed found by nobody. And Halloway doesn't chase. She waits. She'll stand at the narrows with her gunboat and her gray coats and let the river do her killing for her." He finished his drink. "I've run the gorge eleven times. I've seen what the Survey does to boats it doesn't like. You want my advice? Sell the woman's trinket, take her widow's silver, and be nowhere when the melt comes."

Mirabel stood, left the bottle, and put a coin on the wood. "Thanks for the drink, Tam."

"That's not what you paid for."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But it's what I'm leaving you."

* * *

He walked the town after dark, because a man being watched should know by whom, and Corren's Ford at night gave up its secrets the way a bank gives up silt — slowly, in eddies, to anyone patient enough to stand still in the current.

The Survey had taken rooms at the Harbormaster's House, the stone building on the rise with the flagpole, and its windows burned yellow against the dark. Two gray-coated men stood at the door. A third walked the wharf below with a lantern, unhurried, reading boat names off transoms the way a priest reads sins. Mirabel watched him from the shadow of a net shed, close enough to see the lantern light catch on a clipboard of oiled paper, close enough to see the man stop at the gap in the moorings where the Kestrel had been tied that morning before Mirabel had moved her, on instinct older than thought, to a slough a quarter mile downstream where willows swallowed her outline whole.

Ten years of instinct, and it had never earned its keep better than tonight.

The walker moved on. Mirabel counted to a hundred, then slipped along the fence line toward the market square, where the day's notices were nailed to the board beside the well. He'd read them that morning without interest: grain tariffs, a lost dog, the closure of the upper roads. Now he read them again, slowly, and found what he'd missed.

The closure notice was new ink over old. Beneath the official proclamation, someone had pasted a second sheet, smaller, plainer: a list of persons whose movements were *of interest to the Survey*. Eleven names. Most were rivermen known for running cargo past inspection points. At the bottom, in different ink — added later, he judged, the letters pressed hard enough to bite the paper — was *M. Nystrom, master, Kestrel*.

Not crossed off. Circled. Twice.

The chill that went through him then was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the specific cold of a man who has built his whole life on not being seen, discovering that he has been seen all along — that while he practiced his blankness and moved through towns like smoke, somebody in a gray coat had been writing his name down, patiently, the way a river writes its course into stone. He thought of all the years he'd mistaken invisibility for safety, and understood suddenly that they were different things entirely. Invisibility was a debt too. It compounded. And somewhere upstream, the Survey held the note.

He stood in the square a long time, long enough that the watchman made two passes and decided the shadow by the notice board was part of the architecture. Above the rooftops, far north, the mountains stood against the stars, white and silent, wearing their glacier like a crown. Somewhere under that ice was a vault, a dead man's proof, a truth that had cost six lives already and was now costing Mirabel his anonymity — the last possession he had valued.

Behind him, boots on cobbles. Measured. Unhurried. The walk of a person who has never once needed to hurry.

He turned.

She was small, which surprised him; he had imagined Halloway large, the way you imagine a storm. Gray Survey coat, immaculate. Dark hair cut short and practical. A face that was, as Tam had promised, smiling — a pleasant, reasonable smile, the kind that made the smile itself the threat. Her eyes did not join in. They were pale and patient and fixed on him with the mild satisfaction of a clerk finding a figure add up.

"Captain Nystrom," she said. Her voice was low and even, a voice for giving orders that sounded like suggestions. "I hoped we'd meet. You're a difficult man to find, and yet here you are, standing directly beneath your own name. Almost as though you wanted to be seen."

"Just reading the tariffs."

"Mm." She glanced at the board, at the circled name, and back. "The Survey pays well for cooperation, Captain. Boats, information, silence — all of it has a price, and I've found rivermen are honest about prices if nothing else. Six of our people are missing in the upper valley. Their families grieve. The government requires certainty. If you hear anything — anything at all — about where they went, or why, or what they were looking for—" she produced a card, heavy stock, embossed with the Survey's compass rose, and held it out, "—you'll bring it to me. Won't you."

It was not a question. It was a hook, dressed politely, waiting to be swallowed.

Mirabel took the card. His hand did not shake; a decade of dodging debts had trained even his pulse into obedience. "If I hear anything," he said, "I'll weigh it."

Something flickered behind the pale eyes — amusement, maybe, or appraisal. "Weigh it carefully," Captain Dree Halloway said. "The melt comes early this year. Rivers change. Men who stand in the wrong channel when the water rises..." She smiled her pleasant smile, wider by a degree. "...tend to end up downstream of their own intentions. Good night, Captain."

She walked away toward the yellow windows of the Harbormaster's House, and Mirabel stood in the empty square with the card in his hand and the cold of the mountains on his neck, understanding at last that the hunt had already begun — that it had perhaps begun years ago, in some office in the capital, when a clerk first wrote his name on a list of men who knew too much about the wrong stretches of river.

He went back to the Kestrel in the dark, slipping down the willow-choked slough where she lay hidden, and woke Sela Voss with a hand on the cabin door and four words pitched low:

"We leave before dawn."
