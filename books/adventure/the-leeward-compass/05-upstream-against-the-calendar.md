## 5. Upstream Against the Calendar

The Vellwater forgives nothing going up.

Going down, a man could be lazy and live — let the current do the thinking, keep the hull off the stones, and the river would carry him like a favor. But upstream was a negotiation renewed every hour, every bend, every bar of gravel that had moved in the night. The Kestrel fought for every mile, her engine laboring at half-throttle against water that wanted her back downstream, and Mirabel stood at the wheel with his hands light on the spokes, reading the surface the way other men read faces.

He had taken Sela Voss aboard at Graylock Landing two days past, and she had not yet learned to stay out of the way.

"You're fighting it," she said from behind him, on the morning of the third day, as they worked the long reach below Tarn's Elbow. The river ran brown-green there with snowmelt, swollen already though the true melt hadn't begun, and the current came at them in long muscular pulses, like something breathing under a blanket.

"I'm not fighting it," Mirabel said. "I'm arguing with it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Fighting costs you the boat." He eased the wheel a spoke to starboard, feeling through the deck plates the tremor of the propeller biting shallower water. "Arguing just costs you time. And time I've got. It's everything else I've been short of."

She didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to. In three days he had learned that Sela Voss laughed rarely and only sideways, out of the corner of her grief, as if joy were a debt she couldn't afford either. She stood at the rail now with Aldric's compass in her hand — she carried it everywhere, he'd noticed, the way a woman carries a letter she can't read — and its needle swung in its little brass house, trembling off true, straining upstream toward the white shoulders of mountains still fifty miles distant.

"It's worse today," she said. "The needle. It pulls harder when we go north."

"Then we'll go south," Mirabel said, "and disappoint it."

But he watched it too. He'd been watching it since the second night, when he'd taken it from its oilcloth on the chart table and held it under the lamp and turned slowly in place, and the needle had ignored him entirely — ignored magnetic north, ignored iron, ignored everything a compass was built to honor — and pointed instead, steady as scripture, upriver. Toward the glacier fields. Toward the headwaters no licensed boat had entered in thirty years, because the Survey had closed them with a stroke of red ink and kept them closed with gunboats.

His old mentor, Anselm Creel, had told him once, drunk on plum brandy in the wheelhouse of a boat long since rotted to ribs: *There are two kinds of water on the Vellwater, boy. The kind that wants your cargo, and the kind that wants you. Everything above Corren's Ford is the second kind.* Mirabel had been nineteen and immortal then. He was thirty-four now, and merely stubborn, which was nearly the same thing but slower.

* * *

The middle reaches made liars of charts.

Mirabel worked from memory and from the river itself — the color of the water over a shoal, the shape of the standing waves, the smell of silt versus stone. Each morning the rapids were harder than the day before. The melt had started in earnest now, high on the icefields where the sun reached even in the cold months, and every dawn brought more water down, and every extra foot of water rearranged the rocks beneath it.

"The map says this channel runs left of the island," Sela said, standing beside him at the wheel as they approached the White Stair, a quarter-mile of broken water where the river dropped through a staircase of granite shelves.

"The map is four years old," Mirabel said. "The river is four days old. Rivers get reborn every spring; maps just get older." He throttled down, studied the water. The left channel, which should have run clean, showed a boil of confused water midstream — a rock newly awash or newly grown. "Right side. Watch the line."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Nothing yet. When I say *fenders*, throw the fenders over the starboard side before we hit the shelf wall. When I say *pole*, take the pole off the roof rack and stand ready at the bow. And whatever happens—" he looked at her, and she looked back, gray-eyed and unflinching, "—don't touch the wheel. Not even if it looks wrong. Especially if it looks wrong. It always looks wrong."

The Kestrel went up the White Stair the way a cat goes up a fence — quick, committed, with no dignity whatsoever. The engine screamed in the short bursts between shelves; the hull ground once, horribly, along submerged granite; spray came over the bow in cold sheets. Sela threw the fenders when told and took the pole when told and did not touch the wheel, and when they broke out above the last shelf into the long green quiet of the upper pool, she sat down hard on the deck boards and discovered her hands were shaking.

"Tea," Mirabel said. "Second drawer down from the stove. There's honey somewhere if the ants haven't found it."

"You knew that rock was there," she said. "Under the water. You'd never run this stretch this early in the season."

"I didn't know it. I read it." He killed the engine and let the boat drift in the pool, and the silence after the engine was so complete it rang. "A boil like that means water hitting stone and turning. A smooth tongue of current means depth. You learn to see the river telling on itself." He paused. "My father taught me that. Or tried to."

"Your father was a captain too?"

"My father was an indentured man who happened to own a boat." Mirabel said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy so you don't have to keep carrying it in your arms. "Which is a different thing entirely."

* * *

They moored that night in a willow cut below the old ferry ruins, and over supper — barley and smoked fish and tea sweetened with the last of the honey — Sela asked, carefully, the way you ask about a scar: what happened to him.

Mirabel turned his cup in his hands. He had not told this story in ten years, and he told it now mostly to the fire, in the low voice men use for things that happened to someone they used to be.

"There used to be a Nystrom claim," he said. "Up past the middle reaches. Forty acres of bottomland, river frontage, a stand of timber. My grandfather cleared it. It was ours — not paper-ours, real-ours, the kind of ours where you know every stone by name. Then the Survey came through with their assessment rolls, and my father signed."

"He signed away his own land?"

"He signed a debt instrument dressed up as a land title. That's how they did it, how they still do it. They call it a *reclamation levy* — the Survey says the valley's improvements cost money, so every landholder owes a share, and if you can't pay the share, you sign the consolidation, and the consolidation converts your freehold into leasehold, and the leasehold into indenture, and the indenture into..." He gestured vaguely at the dark river. "...this. A life spent working off a debt that grows faster than you can work. The arithmetic is designed to fail. That's not a flaw in the system. That *is* the system."

Sela had gone very still. "And your mother?"

"She saw it coming. She wanted to contest the assessment, take it to the magistrate at Corren's Ford. My father said the paperwork would drown us faster than the river ever could. So she took me and went to her sister's, downstream, and my father stayed to work the debt." Mirabel fed a stick to the fire. "Three years later the spring flood came early. There was a timber run he shouldn't have made — rafting logs down the White Stair in water that was already too high, trying to earn the last hundred crowns of a debt that had somehow become two thousand. His raft hit the new rock. The one the flood put there that year, the way floods do." He shrugged. "They never found all of him. The river keeps what it likes."

Sela said nothing for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere across the water a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"So you learned the river," she said finally, "that killed him."

"I learned the river that *didn't* kill me." Mirabel looked up. "That's the whole difference between us, him and me. He trusted paper. Paper lied. The river never lies — it tells you exactly what it's going to do, if you speak it. Every rock I read right is a word I learned from watching him read wrong." He heard his own voice harden and didn't soften it. "I used to think I ran cargo to stay invisible. Now I think I run it to practice. One day the Survey comes for everyone on this river, and I intend to be the best-read man on the water when they do."

"That's not a living," Sela said quietly. "That's a grudge with a rudder."

Mirabel almost smiled. "Yes," he said. "It is."

* * *

The days stacked up like cordwood. Up and up, lockless, towline-less, the Kestrel clawing her way through water that rose around them like a tide running the wrong direction. Mirabel taught Sela the boat because he had no choice — a crew of one couldn't run the upper rapids alone, not with the melt coming on — and she learned the way she did everything, silently, completely, without complaint. By the fifth day she could throw a line with a boatman's snap. By the seventh she could read a shoal's color well enough to warn him of a gravel shift. She slept badly — he heard her turning in the forward bunk most nights, and some nights heard her speaking softly to the compass, or to Aldric through it, he couldn't tell which — but she worked like a woman paying something down, hour by hour, and Mirabel recognized the currency.

On the ninth day they locked through the Narrows approach — not the Gorge proper, that was still ahead, but the long grinding race called the Ladder, where the river dropped forty feet in a mile and the standing waves stood tall as a man. They ran it at first light, when the water was lowest, and even so the Kestrel shipped a ton of green water over the stern and Mirabel spent an hour afterward pumping the bilge while Sela bailed with a bucket, both of them soaked to the skin and laughing — actually laughing, both of them, once, briefly, at the sheer absurd insolence of being alive — before catching themselves and returning to their separate silences.

It was that evening, moored in a side slough thick with the smell of alder and warming mud, that Sela said, unprompted, watching the sunset turn the water to hammered copper:

"Aldric hated boats."

Mirabel looked up from the engine log.

"Ten years married and he never once set foot on one willingly. He got seasick crossing the ferry at Graylock, a five-minute crossing." Her mouth did the sideways thing that was almost a smile. "And he went upriver anyway. With a survey team, into the headwaters, in a government launch. Because they told him to." She turned the compass over in her hands. "He was obedient, Mirabel. That was his whole tragedy. He believed the Survey was what it said it was. He believed the maps were honest. He went up there to measure the truth for them, and I don't think it ever once occurred to him that they might not want it."

"And you think they didn't."

"I think nobody sends an enforcer named Halloway looking for a lost *typewriter*." She met his eyes. "I think my husband found something up there, and I think the something found him back, and I think the compass is the only piece of him that ever tried to come home."

Mirabel looked at the compass. In the last copper light its needle trembled against its glass, straining upstream, patient as hunger, and he felt again that pull in his own chest, answering it — curiosity wearing caution's coat, the way it always did.

"We make the Gorge Narrows in two days," he said. "After that, no charts, no channels, no traffic. Just the mountain water and whatever the Survey doesn't want found." He closed the engine log. "Get some sleep, Mrs. Voss. Tomorrow we argue with the river again."

* * *

But sleep didn't come for him either. He sat in the wheelhouse long after the fire died, listening to the river breathe against the hull, and thought about his father's signature drying on that consolidation paper twenty-five years ago — a tired man's small neat letters, signing away forty acres and every acre of every generation after, for a debt that had been manufactured the way this floodwater was manufactured: inevitably, impersonally, from a great height.

*They count on us drowning quietly,* he thought. *In water or in ink, they don't much care which.*

Well. The melt was rising. The calendar was burning down. And somewhere ahead, past the gorge, past the forbidden line, a brass needle knew the way to the thing that would unmake every signature like his father's.

Mirabel Nystrom stood his midnight watch and let the grief do what it had always done, what he had trained it to do like a draft animal: pull.

Two days later, rounding the last bluff before the Gorge, he brought the Kestrel about into the narrows' shadow — and stopped so suddenly the wake slapped the stern.

Below the cliffs, where the charts promised only ruin and stilted shacks, a village clung to the drowned shoreline. And on the water below it, black and patient against the evening current, rode a gunboat flying inspection colors.

Someone had come upstream ahead of them.
