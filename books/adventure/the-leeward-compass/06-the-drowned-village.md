## 6. The Drowned Village

I had never seen water hold a grudge before.

That was the thought that came to me as the *Kestrel* rounded the bend below Harrow's Reach and the reservoir opened out ahead of us — a wide, flat sheet of grey-green water so still it looked less like a river than like something holding its breath. Mirabel had gone quiet at the wheel. He had been quiet for an hour, in fact, ever since the banks began to change character: the willows giving way to drowned stumps, pale and boneless-looking, rising from the shallows like the fingers of buried men.

"Company water," he said finally, when I asked what it was. "From the dam works upstream. They flooded the valley floor nine years back to feed their mills." He said it the way a man reads a signpost — flat, factual, already finished with it. But his hands on the wheel were not finished with anything. They were white-knuckled, and I had learned enough of him by then to know that when Mirabel Nystrom went silent, that was where the feeling lived.

The village announced itself before we saw it. A bell, first — a cracked, arrhythmic tolling somewhere off our port bow, half-swallowed by fog. Then the smell: woodsmoke and river-mud and something older underneath, something like rot sweetened by moss. And then, through the thinning mist, the roofs.

They rose out of the water at strange angles, these roofs — some whole, some caved in, all of them black with nine years of standing flood. The lower stories of the houses had gone under entirely; you could see doorways beneath the surface, dark rectangles where current moved in slow, ghostly pulses, stirring curtains of weed. But the villagers had adapted the way water adapts, finding every low place. Walkways of lashed timber ran between the upper floors. Stairs climbed from second-story windows down into boats. Laundry hung between chimneys that should have been the highest point of any house and were now merely the middle. Children sat on ridgelines that had once been eaves, dangling lines into water that had been their grandmothers' gardens.

"They live here," I said. It came out stupidly, but I couldn't help it. "On top of it."

"On top of what's left," Mirabel said. "Survey calls it Reservoir Settlement Four. Calls them tenants." He spat over the rail, an old habit done with real feeling. "They were landowners once. Freeholders, some of them, going back past the collapse. Then the dam went in and the water came up to their windowsills, and the Survey offered them a choice. Relocate to company barracks downstream, or stay and sign indenture papers for 'improvement costs.' Rent on their own rooftops, essentially. Most stayed. People don't leave their dead."

He brought us alongside one of the walkways with a boatman's delicacy I was only beginning to appreciate — no engine roar, just the soft kiss of hull against pilings — and made fast. "We'll take on water and bread," he said. "It's the last honest market before the gorge. Keep your hood up and your questions quiet."

I did neither, in the end. But I didn't know that yet.

* * *

The market was the roof of a granary, or had been. Now planks stretched across its sagging spine, and stalls stood where haylofts used to be, and the whole structure groaned softly with the movement of the water beneath it, a sound like a great animal breathing in its sleep. Everything sold there was wet or had been: fish, reed-craft, moss for packing wounds, candles rendered from something I decided not to ask about. The people moved carefully, always, even the children — a whole community that had learned to walk as if the ground might not be there.

An old woman sold me bread. Her stall was barely a plank and a tarpaulin, her loaves small and dense and good, and she counted my coins twice because she could not trust her eyes anymore, running each one under her thumb like a bead on a string. She had a face like driftwood — grey, grain-split, polished smooth in places by weather — and hands that shook until they touched the dough, and then didn't.

"You're not from the company boats," she said. Not a question. Her eyes had gone from me to Mirabel, haggling two stalls down, and then to the satchel at my side, and stopped there.

"No," I said. "We're going upstream."

Her thumb stopped moving. For a moment the whole breathing market seemed to go quiet around us, though of course it didn't — the noise went on, the barter and the gulls and the lap of water under the floorboards. It was only me who had stopped hearing it.

"Upstream," she repeated. "Past the narrows."

"To the headwaters," I said, and heard myself say it, and understood that I had decided something in the saying, though I'd told myself for three days I hadn't decided anything at all.

The old woman set down the coin she was holding. Very deliberately, the way you put down something fragile. "Show me what you carry," she said. "In the satchel. The thing that pulls."

"I don't know what you—"

"The compass, girl. Aldric Voss's compass. You carry it like a coal in your pocket — everyone can see the shape of you bending around it. Show me."

My hand was on the satchel buckle before my mind caught up. That is the thing I keep returning to, in the nights since: how quickly I obeyed her, how it felt less like a stranger's command than like a debt being collected. I drew out the brass compass and laid it on the plank between us, and it lay there trembling, the needle swinging in its slow, stubborn arc away from north, away from every sensible direction, toward the ice.

The old woman looked at it for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, and steadier, as if the shaking in her had been waiting all these years for a reason to stop.

"My husband helped build the survey markers," she said. "Before the dam. Before any of this. He was paid in scrip and thanked like a dog. He walked the valley with instruments, and one day he came home and wouldn't eat, and when I asked him why he said: *Marta, they're measuring for something that isn't ours to give.*" She turned the compass over with one finger, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Then the water came. And he went upriver with four other men to find whatever it was the Survey wanted so badly they'd drown a valley for it. None of them came back. The company sent a letter saying they'd been lost in a surveying accident, and enclosed a bill for the cost of the search."

She laughed then — one dry syllable, no more, a stone dropping in a well.

"Aldric Voss," she said. "You're his wife. I can see it now. You have his chin. His stubbornness sits on you like a coat."

I couldn't speak. I nodded.

"He came through here, you know. Before he went up. Bought bread from me, same as you." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "And he showed me this — showed me how it wouldn't point north. I asked him what it pointed to, and he said, *the key they couldn't break.*"

"The key," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "What key? What does it open?"

"That's not mine to tell you, and he wouldn't tell me either. He only said—" and here she leaned across the plank, close enough that I could smell the bread and the river on her, close enough to see that her eyes were not clouded at all but very clear, clear the way deep water is clear, "—he said the Survey has been writing debts for sixty years that nobody ever owed. Every family in this valley. Every freehold they drowned, every barrack they filled, every child born into a contract signed by a grandfather who was tricked or drunk or simply illiterate. It's all paper, girl. Paper and seals. And paper burns, and seals crack, if someone finds the proof of the forging." She closed my fingers around the compass herself, folding them shut over the warm brass. "He said the vault holds the true rolls. The original grants. The names of the men who invented the debts. He said it was put where only something that points wrong could find it."

The market breathed around us. Under the floorboards, the drowned village shifted its weight in the dark.

"Why are you telling me this?" I managed. "You don't know me."

"I know your husband walked through my market and bought bread and told me the truth, and I have spent nine years wondering if I dreamed him." She pushed the loaves across the plank, all of them, refusing the coins I tried to give. "Take the bread. Take the truth. And take this—" she pressed something else into my other hand, small and hard: a whistle carved from antler, "—if the enforcer's boat comes, blow it twice. The children watch the water. We see everything on this river, and we owe the Survey nothing but our rent, which we pay in silence."

* * *

Mirabel was waiting at the rail, coiling a line with the excessive care of a man pretending not to have been listening. He wasn't pretending well. He never did.

"She talked to you about the debts," he said. Not looking at me.

"She talked about a lot of things."

"Nystrom." He said my name — my married name, the name on nothing, the name I'd given him at Graylock Landing because it was all I had left of Aldric. He tied off the line and finally faced me, and I saw that behind his usual guardedness there was something raw and moving, like a fish under ice. "Whatever she told you. Whatever you think that compass opens. I need you to hear what I'm about to say, because I've watched hopeful people on this river my whole life, and hope is the most expensive cargo there is. It sinks boats."

"Does it?"

"It sank mine." He said it lightly, which is how I knew it was heavy. "My father had a claim too. Up the middle reaches, forty acres of bottomland, granted fair before the Survey got hungry. He fought them for eleven years. Petitions, lawyers, hearings in towns where the judges owned stock in the company. And every year the debt grew — fees, interest, penalties for contesting — until the claim itself was worth less than what they said he owed against it. That's the arithmetic they use. It doesn't matter what you own. It matters what they say you owe." He looked out at the drowned rooftops, the laundry lines, the children fishing from the eaves of their own homes. "He walked into the river in the end. Left a note saying the water was the only part of the valley they hadn't figured out how to bill him for."

The current moved beneath us, patient and enormous.

"So you understand why I'm asking," he said quietly. "Not whether the vault exists. Whether opening it helps anyone, or just gets more people billed for the search."

I looked down at the compass in my hand. It trembled against my palm, insistent, alive, pointing its wrong-pointing way toward the ice, toward Aldric, toward whatever he had found and hidden and died — no. Toward whatever he had found and hidden. I would not finish that sentence yet. I refused to finish it yet.

"Martla's husband went upriver with four men," I said. "Aldric went up with six. Everyone who goes looking comes back changed or doesn't come back at all. And meanwhile—" I gestured at the village, the stilted ruins, the whole breathing ruin of it, "—meanwhile the children are born into contracts their grandfathers never signed. That's what inheritance means here, Mirabel. It means receiving a bill for a life you never lived."

He flinched. Only slightly — a man like Mirabel flinches in millimeters — but I saw it, and I knew I'd found the truth of him, the thing under the irony and the river-lore and the careful forgetting. He had inherited a debt too. Of course he had. Men don't get that fluent in the Survey's arithmetic without doing sums of their own.

"And if the rolls are forged," I went on, softer now, "then it isn't inheritance at all. It's theft, dressed up as legacy, passed hand to hand for sixty years. And somebody wrote down the truth and hid it in the ice, and gave us a needle that points at it."

The old woman's voice came back to me then, unbidden: *the key they couldn't break.*

Mirabel was silent a long moment. Then he cast off the line, and took the wheel, and swung the *Kestrel*'s bow upstream, into the current, toward the narrows and the gorge and everything beyond.

"Blow that whistle twice if you see a gunboat," was all he said.

But he was heading upstream, and we both knew what that meant, and above us the first grey peaks of the high country had come out of the fog like watchers who had been waiting a very long time to be seen.

Behind us, fainter and fainter, the cracked bell of the drowned village tolled on — keeping, I thought, some kind of count.
