## 7. Halloway Catches the Current

The gunboat came upriver on the third morning past the drowned village, and Mirabel knew it before he saw it — knew it the way a man knows a storm by the smell of the air, by the particular hush that falls over water when something large and wrong is moving through it.

He was at the wheel with coffee going cold in his fist, running the Kestrel through the long slow bend below Heron Shallows, when the birds told him. The herons had lifted off the eastern bank all at once, a ragged white scatter against the grey sky, and they did not come down again. Herons came down. Herons always came down; patience was their whole religion. Something had frightened them off their water.

Then he heard it: the low diesel throb, deeper-voiced than any cargo boat, riding the current's echo up the valley walls.

Mirabel set down the cup without drinking and climbed onto the roof of the wheelhouse, one hand on the rail, and looked back the way they'd come.

She was maybe two miles downstream, sitting low and mean in the water, black hull, grey superstructure, a stub smokestack breathing a thin dark line into the morning. A Survey gunboat — he'd seen them twice in ten years and both times from very far away, the way you see a wolf across a frozen lake. She flew inspection colors at her masthead: the blue-and-white pennant of the River Authority, which meant she could stop anything afloat, board anything, seize anything, ask any question she liked, and the asking would not be gentle.

"Captain?" Sela Voss stood in the companionway hatch with a coil of rope over one shoulder, hair tied back, hands already working at some task because idleness made her worse, not better. She followed his gaze downstream and went very still. "Is that—"

"That's Halloway." He didn't know how he was sure. He was sure. Ten years of reading the river had taught him to read boats too, and this one moved like its captain: no wasted motion, no drift, coming straight up the channel's spine as though the river were a road built for her alone. "Get below. Take the compass off the bulkhead and put it in the flour tin under the galley floor. Don't wind the chronometer. Don't light the stove."

"You think she'll stop us."

"I think she'll stop everything." He was already turning the wheel, easing the Kestrel toward the western bank where the current ran slack along a drowned willow stand. "And I intend to be somewhere else when she does."

* * *

There was a slough three miles above Heron Shallows that appeared on no chart Mirabel had ever bought or stolen. The Survey charts showed it as marsh, which was true in August and a lie the rest of the year. It opened behind a curtain of deadfall — two cottonwoods that had leaned together over the entrance since before his father's time, their trunks furred with moss, their crowns long since fallen into the water — and behind them the river split around a nameless island and braided itself into a dozen threads of slack water, each one narrower than the last, until they lost themselves entirely in a reedbed the size of a town.

His father had shown him the entrance when Mirabel was nine years old, poling their leaky skiff through the gap while young Mirabel held his breath, certain the trees would scrape the skin off both sides of the boat. *Any fool can hide in deep water,* his father had said, laughing. *It takes craft to hide in shallow.* His father had been full of sayings like that, right up until the day the river took him, and most of them were worth exactly what Mirabel had paid for them. But this one he had kept. This one had kept him alive.

He brought the Kestrel in on the flood side of the tide, engine at bare idle, using the current itself to swing the stern through the gap between the cottonwoods. There was perhaps a hand's width of clearance on either side. He heard the willow brush whisper along the hull like a warning and then fall silent behind them, and then the Kestrel was through, sliding into the green half-light of the slough, and he killed the engine and let her drift to a stop among the reeds with barely a ripple.

From the outside, he knew exactly what the place looked like: nothing. Reed and deadfall and brown water, indistinguishable from ten miles of identical nothing on either bank. He had once watched a Survey patrol boat pass within thirty yards of the entrance without so much as slowing. The trick was not to be invisible. The trick was to be uninteresting.

Sela emerged from below with the flour tin hugged to her chest and her face pale. "You've hidden here before."

"I grew up here." He took the tin from her and stowed it in the bilge locker beneath the anchor chain, then threw a canvas tarp over the deck gear — not to conceal the boat, which was impossible, but to make her read wrong at a glance. Cargo boats were common. Cargo boats under tarp, anchored in reeds, were boring. Boring was armor. "Now we do the hard part."

"Which is?"

"Nothing." He said it flatly, and saw her flinch. "We sit. We don't cook, we don't smoke, we don't talk above a whisper, we don't let anything shine or clank or move. Gunboats carry glasses, and glass finds motion before it finds shape. If she passes us by, we wait an hour after she's gone and go on. If she doesn't—" He looked at the reeds, at the narrow channels threading away northward through them, deep enough for a loaded skiff if you knew which thread was which, and he did. "If she doesn't pass us by, there are ways out of here on foot. We lose the Kestrel and keep our skins."

Sela looked at him for a long moment. "You'd give up your boat?"

"I'd give up my boat every hour of every day if it meant keeping my neck," he said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so. He busied himself checking the anchor line instead. "Sit down, Mrs. Voss. And take off anything that catches light."

* * *

They heard the gunboat before they saw her through the reeds — the diesel throb swelling, then falling away, then falling away further, and further, until it stopped altogether, and the silence that replaced it was worse than the sound.

Mirabel lay prone on the foredeck with his cheek nearly touching the planks, looking out through a seam in the reed screen he'd widened with two fingers and no more. Sela crouched beside him, breathing carefully, the way people breathe when they are teaching themselves not to panic. Through the gap he could see a slice of the main channel and, beyond it, the near shore: a fishing camp, three huts on stilts, nets drying on poles, a couple of punts pulled up on the mud. He knew the family who worked it distantly — the Ostrander brothers and their mother, quiet people, licensed people, the kind who paid their fees and kept their heads down and asked the river for nothing but fish.

The gunboat slid into view and anchored mid-channel with a competence that hurt to watch. She was smaller than she'd seemed from a distance — a river patrol cutter, thirty yards, twin screws, a bow-mounted winch and a boarding ramp folded flat against her flank. Six crew visible on deck, in Survey grey. And at the rail, forward, a woman in a long coat the color of wet slate, hands clasped behind her back, watching the fishing camp with the unhurried attention of someone deciding what to have for dinner.

Even at this distance, even through reeds, Mirabel felt the weight of her stillness. Captain Dree Halloway did not scan the banks. She did not search. Searching was for people who didn't know. She simply looked, the way a magistrate looks, and let the world arrange itself for inspection.

A launch went down from the gunboat's davits. Four men in it, armed, one carrying the Authority's ledger-board in a waterproof case. They crossed to the fishing camp in under a minute, and the Ostranders came out of their huts and stood in a row on the mud like children called before a schoolmaster, and Mirabel's stomach tightened around the cold coffee he'd never drunk.

He couldn't hear the words. The water carried sound strangely here, swallowing speech and giving back only tone. But he didn't need words. He'd sat through enough inspections in his decade of dodging to know the choreography by heart: questions asked slowly, answers given quickly, papers demanded, papers produced, the ledger-board tilted to catch the light. The elder Ostrander — Tam, broad and grey-bearded, a man who had once sold Mirabel eels without asking his name, which was the highest courtesy the river offered — gestured toward the upstream reaches, palms open, shaking his head. *No, sirs, nothing passed. No, sirs, we've seen no survey team.* The gestures of a man telling the truth and praying truth is enough.

It wasn't. It never was, with the Survey. One of the greycoats stepped forward and took Tam Ostrander by the collar, and walked him down the mud to the waterline, and Mirabel understood what was about to happen about four seconds before it happened, and there was nothing — nothing — he could do about it except lie flat in the reeds and watch.

They dunked him. Three times. Held him under just long enough each time that when they hauled him up he came up thrashing, coughing, his beard streaming, and each time the man with the ledger-board bent close and asked again, patiently, the way you'd question a lock that wouldn't open. On the fourth dunking they held him longer. When they finally dragged him onto the mud he didn't get up right away. He stayed on his hands and knees, dripping, and his brother started forward and a rifle butt caught him in the shoulder and put him down, not hard, not brutally — casually, the way you'd move a piece of furniture — and that casualness was the worst thing Mirabel had seen in ten years on this river, and he had seen men drown in their own nets.

Beside him, Sela had gone rigid. Her hand had found his sleeve without either of them deciding it should, and her grip was hard enough to bruise. "Do something," she whispered. It wasn't a request. It wasn't even addressed to him, really. It was addressed to the universe.

"If I do something," Mirabel breathed, "there are six more of them forty yards away, and then nobody ever opens Aldric's vault, and every debt in this valley stands forever. Including mine. Including yours." He made himself keep watching. He owed the Ostranders that much — to watch, and remember, and hate cleanly. "What I can do is make sure this cost something later. That's the trade. That's the whole ugly trade, Mrs. Voss. You swallow it now and spit it out later, or you choke on it now and it's done."

She didn't answer. But her grip on his sleeve eased by degrees, and she kept watching too, and he respected her for it, because most people looked away.

After a long interval — twenty minutes, perhaps, stretched to the length of a season — the launch went back to the gunboat. The ledger-board closed. Tam Ostrander was left on the mud with his brother kneeling over him, and the gunboat weighed anchor, and her diesels woke with that deep patient throb, and she turned her bow upstream and came on.

Came on. Toward the slough. Toward the deadfall curtain and the reeds and the Kestrel lying under her tarp like a sleeping animal.

Mirabel felt fear arrive in his body the way cold water arrives in a boot — sudden, total, climbing. The gunboat drew level with the drowned willows. Through the reed seam he could see her rail, her rail-mounted lamp, the slate-grey coat at the forward rail. Halloway was looking at the bank now. Looking at the reeds. Not searching — she never searched — but looking, letting the world arrange itself, and for one lurching moment Mirabel was certain the arrangement had failed, that her eyes had found the tarp, the masthead, the lie of the water—

The gunboat passed. Thirty yards off. Close enough that he could see the pennant snap and fill, close enough to count the rivets in her hull plates, and then her wake rolled into the slough mouth and lifted the Kestrel gently, gently, and set her down again, and the black hull went on upstream without pausing, without slowing, trailing her thin dark smoke, growing smaller against the northern distance where the valley narrowed and the glacier country began.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The herons came back down to the eastern bank one at a time, cautious, resuming their religion.

"She didn't stop," Sela said finally. Her voice sounded scraped. "Why didn't she stop?"

"Because stopping boats isn't her work." Mirabel sat up slowly. His hands were unsteady and he watched them until they weren't. "Stopping boats is clerk's work. She left that to the greycoats and the ledger. She's going where the compass goes. She knows already — or she guesses well enough — and she's not wasting her morning on fishermen." He looked upstream, at the empty channel, at the space where the gunboat had been. "She beat one old man half to death this morning for nothing. For practice. To remind the river who owns it. And she'll do it again tomorrow, and the day after, all the way to the ice, unless—"

He stopped. The fury had come up in him quietly, without heat, the way frost comes — not rage but a kind of crystallizing, a hard clear cold settling over everything he looked at. All his life he had been afraid of the Survey the way small animals are afraid, the way you're afraid of weather: duck, run, hide, survive, forget. Lying in the reeds with the taste of another man's drowning in his mouth, Mirabel found that the fear had changed its chemistry. It was still fear. But underneath it now, laid down in layers like sediment, was something with weight and edges. Something he could carry. Something he could spend.

"Unless what?" Sela asked.

"Unless somebody gets to that vault first," Mirabel said, "and opens it so wide they can't ever shut it again." He got to his feet, joints stiff from the cold deck, and looked down at her. "Get the flour tin. We're going to sit here the full hour like I promised, and then we're going to run this river harder than she can follow, because I know water she doesn't. And Mrs. Voss — when we reach the Gorge Narrows, there's a woman there named Osket Brann who controls the portage, and she'll want things from us that I don't want to give. Whatever happens up there, whatever she offers or takes — remember this morning. Remember what it costs to be polite to these people."

Sela Voss looked at him for a moment, and then she nodded once, slow, and went below to fetch the compass.

Mirabel stayed on the foredeck, watching the empty channel, listening to the gunboat's engines fade into the northern distance. Somewhere ahead of them, Captain Dree Halloway was steaming toward the source of the Vellwater with the authority of the government behind her and, he suspected, very little else. Behind them lay a beaten fisherman and a burned village and a decade of his own careful, cowardly invisibility.

Ahead lay the ice.

He found, to his own quiet astonishment, that he was no longer afraid of it.
