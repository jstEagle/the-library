## 8. The Smuggler's Toll

The Gorge Narrows announced itself long before it appeared. First the river changed its voice — the broad, patient murmur of the middle Vellwater tightening into a hiss, then a growl, as though the water had remembered some old grievance and meant to settle it against stone. Then the banks began to climb, pine giving way to bare granite shoulders, and the sky narrowed to a ribbon of gray between walls that seemed to lean in and listen.

Mirabel brought the *Kestrel* in close under the eastern bank, where the current ran slack in the shadow of the cliffs, and let her drift. Ahead, the river vanished into a cleft no wider than a wagon road, choked with spray and thunder. There was no running that. No pilot alive had ever run the Narrows, not with cargo, not with a prayer. The water went through in a single white scream, and what it carried came out the far side in splinters.

Which meant the portage. And the portage meant Osket Brann.

"You've gone quiet," Sela said. She stood at the rail with the compass cradled in both hands, the way she'd held it for three days now — not like an instrument but like a bird she was afraid would startle. "Is it the rapids?"

"It's what lives above them." Mirabel killed the engine and let the silence of the gorge press in around them, broken only by the distant roar. "There's a trail along the north wall. Two miles of switchbacks over solid rock, and every plank, every pulley, every mule on it belongs to one woman. Nothing passes the Narrows without her say-so. Not cargo. Not people." He paused. "Not especially people."

"Smugglers," Sela said.

"Entrepreneurs," Mirabel corrected, "with knives." He'd traded with Brann's people twice before, years back — whiskey downstream, quinine up, the usual quiet commerce of the middle river — but he'd never dealt with the queen herself. The stories were enough. Osket Brann had taken the portage from three brothers who'd run it before her, and the brothers, the story went, had been given a choice: work for her or swim. They'd swum. In chains, at gunpoint, but they'd swum, and the river had kept them.

He looked up at the north wall. Somewhere above the tree line, half-hidden in haze, he could make out the glint of the great hoist cables Brann had strung across the gorge — spider silk thick as a man's wrist, carrying crates of contraband and, occasionally, contraband of another kind entirely. People fleeing Survey debt collectors used the portage too. Brann charged them triple and asked no questions, which in the upper valley passed for mercy.

"We'll tie up at the lower landing," he said. "I'll go up alone. You stay with the boat."

Sela turned to look at him, and there was that steadiness in her face again, the one that kept catching him off guard. Widow's steadiness. Debt-holder's steadiness. He was beginning to understand it wasn't calm at all — it was just fear that had been made to stand still and hold something.

"Aldric wrote about this place," she said. "In his letters. He called it 'the tollgate.' He said the woman who ran it had eyes like river ice — pretty from a distance, fatal underneath."

"He wasn't wrong."

"Then I'm coming with you."

"Sela—"

"She's expecting a boat captain," Sela said. "She isn't expecting a widow holding a compass that doesn't point north. Whatever you're going to bargain with, I'm part of it now. I've been part of it since Graylock Landing." Her chin lifted a fraction. "And I don't wait on boats. I waited two years for Aldric to come home. I'm done waiting."

Mirabel studied her a moment, then shrugged, because there was nothing else honest to do. "Stay behind me anyway. And if I say run, you run."

"Where?" she said, gesturing at the walls of the gorge. "Run *where*, Captain?"

He had no answer for that, so he took the wheel and brought the *Kestrel* in to the lower landing — a shelf of timber and pilings cantilevered out from the rock, sagging under the weight of twenty years of smuggled freight. Two men sat on a crate at the head of the ramp, whittling, and they didn't stop whittling when the boat touched, which told Mirabel everything about how welcome he was expected to be. One of them had a crossbow across his knees, unstrung but present, the way a dog lies down but keeps its ears up.

"Boat's the *Kestrel*," the older one said, not looking up from his knife. "Out of Corren's Ford way. That right?"

"That's right."

"Brann's expecting you."

"I haven't sent word ahead."

The whittler smiled without lifting his eyes. "Don't need to. Word travels faster upstream than boats do. There's been questions asked at the landings below. Man named Nystrom. Boat named *Kestrel*. Traveling with a woman." He finally looked up, and his gaze slid past Mirabel to Sela and lingered there with professional appraisal. "Survey's asking. Enforcer woman. Halloway."

The name landed in Mirabel's chest like a dropped anchor. Three days since Corren's Ford, three days of hard running through rising water, and he'd almost let himself believe they'd outpaced her.

"What did you tell her people?" he asked.

"Told 'em we hadn't seen you." The whittler stood, brushing shavings from his trousers. "That's true so far. Whether it stays true depends on the toll." He jerked his chin toward the switchback trail. "Up you go. Both of you. Brann sees everyone herself these days. Nervous season."

* * *

The portage camp clung to the cliffside like a swallow's nest — platforms and shanties bolted to the granite, connected by stairs and catwalks, all of it dominated by the great hoist house where Brann's crews winched freight up the wall and lowered it past the falls on the far side. The noise was tremendous: chain-rattle, mule-bray, the constant complaint of timber under load. Everything smelled of pine pitch, mule sweat, and woodsmoke.

Osket Brann received them on the top platform, in a room built half of rock and half of ship salvage — a cabin whose back wall was the cliff itself, its front windows salvaged from some drowned steamer, still bearing faded gilt lettering along their tops: *LADY VELLWATER — SALOON DECK*. She sat behind a desk that had once been a hatch cover, and she was smaller than Mirabel had imagined, a compact woman in a man's oilskin coat, gray-shot hair bound back with copper wire, hands folded like a judge's.

Her eyes, when they found him, were exactly as Aldric had written. River ice.

"Mirabel Nystrom." She said it slowly, tasting it. "They told me you were clever. Clever men usually come to me before the Survey's enforcer is three days behind them. You're four days late and lying about it with your whole face."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No." She unfolded her hands. "You wouldn't." She nodded at Sela. "And the widow Voss. Whose husband walked into the headwaters with a government pencil in his hand and hasn't walked out. Sit down, both of you. This won't take long, because I already know what you want, and I already know what it costs, and the only question left is whether either of you has the stomach for the price."

Mirabel sat. Sela did not; she remained standing, the compass in her coat pocket, and Mirabel saw Brann notice, and mark it.

"The passage," Mirabel said. "Over the portage, past the falls, down to the upper landing. Quietly. No names in your ledger."

"Everything here goes in my ledger. The question is under whose name." Brann leaned back, and the chair — a captain's chair, brass fittings green with age — creaked. "Here's my position, Nystrom. There's a gunboat on the middle river flying inspection colors, and its captain has offered me more money than your passage is worth simply for the courtesy of a message when you pass through. Not even to hold you. Just a message. A lantern hung on the lower mast, and her crew does the rest." She tilted her head. "Do you know what a message is worth these days? Enough to feed my people through the calving season. Enough to buy off the toll-takers on the far side. Enough that turning it down is a kind of madness."

"And yet you haven't sent it."

"Not yet." For the first time, something moved behind the ice — not warmth, exactly, but interest. "Because I knew you before you were worth anything, Nystrom. Ten years ago, running whiskey past the Ford with your father's debts on your back. You were careful even then. Careful men are rare, and rarer every year." She spread her hands. "So here is my offer, and I'll only make it once. I'll take you over the portage tonight. I'll hide the *Kestrel* in the boathouse under the falls — she'll be safe from Halloway, and from the flood season too, if it comes to that. Food, guides, silence in my ledger. All of it free."

Mirabel felt the trap before he saw it, the way he felt sandbars — a wrongness in the water ahead. "Nothing's free on the Vellwater."

"This isn't. It costs you the compass."

The words settled into the cabin like cold air into a hull. Beside him, he heard Sela go very still.

"The compass," Mirabel repeated.

"The brass one the widow carries. The one that doesn't point north." Brann's eyes moved to Sela, then back. "You think I don't know what you're carrying? Half the river knows by now. A surveyor's instrument that ignores the pole and pulls toward the glacier — do you understand what that's worth? Not to Halloway. To *collectors*. To the kind of people who buy things governments have lost and sell them back at ruinous rates. I can name three buyers who'd pay enough for that compass to retire every soul in this camp for life." She leaned forward. "Give it to me, and you walk over my portage tonight, hidden, fed, unnamed. Keep it, and you can swim the falls for all of me. Those are the prices. Choose."

Mirabel's hand had drifted, without his permission, toward his own coat pocket — toward the compass, which Sela had handed to him that morning and which now rode against his ribs like a second heart. He made himself stop. Made himself breathe.

Every instinct he owned said *yes*. Say yes, hand it over, live. The compass was a curiosity, a dead man's toy; the route it pointed to was forbidden, flooded, likely fatal. What was one brass instrument against a boat, a guide, a clean ledger, a night's start on Halloway?

But he thought of Aldric Voss walking into the whiteout with his team, following that trembling needle toward something worth dying beside. He thought of the drowned village they'd passed, chimneys standing in brown water, and the old woman's words — *the key they couldn't break.* He thought of his father, signing away a claim with a shaking hand, and of the debt mark burned into Sela's contract papers, and of ten years of his own life spent being invisible, unseen, unowned, owing.

The compass was the first thing on this river that had ever pointed anywhere but away.

"No," Mirabel said.

Brann raised an eyebrow. "No?"

"It's not mine to sell. And even if it were—" He stood, slow, keeping his hands loose and visible. "Even if it were, I wouldn't. You want to send your lantern, send it. But you should know something first, Osket Brann. Halloway doesn't pay for messages. She pays until she doesn't need the messenger anymore. Ask anyone who's worked for the Survey longer than a season. Your price buys you a season. Maybe two."

The ice in her eyes didn't crack, but something moved beneath it — a shadow, a fish, a memory. "Get out of my cabin," she said quietly.

"Gladly. Leave the window open on your way—"

"*Both* of you," Brann said, and now her voice had an edge that stopped even Mirabel. "Before I remember why I stopped being a forgiving woman."

* * *

They were halfway down the catwalk when Sela stopped.

Mirabel felt it more than heard it — her footsteps ceasing behind him, the small vacuum of her absence. He turned. She stood in the spray-damp lamplight with her hand closed around something at her throat, and her face was the face of a woman stepping off a ledge.

"Sela. What are you doing?"

"Go back," she said. "Wait by the boat. Don't watch."

"Don't watch *what*? Sela—"

But she was already climbing back up the stairs, and there was a set to her shoulders that he recognized from mirrors, from his father, from every desperate soul who'd ever signed something they couldn't unsign. He followed her anyway, because he was a coward in exactly one direction and it was always toward other people's disasters.

He reached the top platform in time to see her push open Brann's door without knocking.

"—told you his answer," Brann was saying as the door swung wide. "Now get—"

"My answer," Sela said. She crossed the room and laid something on the hatch-cover desk, and the lamplight caught it and threw it back gold.

A ring. Plain band, worn thin on one side from years against a finger, with a single small stone that had been reset at least once — Mirabel could see the old solder scars. A poor woman's wedding ring. A loved one's.

"That's passage," Sela said. "For two. Tonight. Under no name at all."

Brann looked at the ring for a long moment. Then she looked at Sela, and whatever lived under the ice in her eyes surfaced briefly, and it was old, and it was tired, and it had paid prices of its own.

"Widow," she said, not unkindly. "Do you know what this is worth? Nothing. Less than nothing. Gold's common as gravel up here. I could buy fifty rings like it with a week's toll."

"I know," Sela said. "It isn't a payment. It's a pledge. You deal in pledges — I've watched your people. Every contract on this portage is sealed with something that can't be replaced, not something that can be counted. So seal ours with this. When we come back down — if we come back down — I'll redeem it. Whatever it costs me, I'll come back for it. Until then, you hold the only thing in the world I can't bear to lose, and I hold the only thing in the world you apparently can't buy." She drew a breath. "That's better security than gold. You know it is."

The cabin was silent except for the groan of the hoist cables outside, the endless labor of the cliff. Mirabel stood in the doorway and felt something opening in his chest that he didn't have a name for — shame, mostly, cold and specific. Shame that she had done this. Shame that she had needed to. Shame that his pride — his fine, stubborn, worthless pride, the last furniture of a man who owned nothing — had priced itself higher than her ring, and she had paid the difference without telling him.

Brann picked up the ring. She weighed it in her palm, and read the solder scars, and read something else besides — the story inside the metal, the two years of waiting, the man who hadn't come home.

Then she closed her fist around it and looked at Mirabel.

"Your captain has pride," she said to Sela. "Pride is expensive. You just paid for his." She rose, and took an iron key from a nail behind the desk, and tossed it to him. He caught it against his chest. "Boathouse under the east falls. Berth six. Your *Kestrel* will be safer there than she's ever been on open water — and if you're not back before the calving floods, she'll belong to me, so consider that your deadline, Nystrom." She turned to Sela, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Passage for two, tonight, after dark. My brother Toma runs the upper descent. Tell him Osket says: *no lantern.*"

"Thank you," Sela whispered.

"Don't thank me. Redeem it." Brann sat back down, the ring vanishing into her pocket, and the ice came up over her face again like water closing. "And Nystrom — one thing more, freely given, since you gave me truth about Halloway." She fixed him with those river-ice eyes. "The enforcer sent a rider through yesterday. She knows you're coming this way. She's not sending a message ahead anymore." A pause. "She's coming herself. You have until tomorrow's dark, maybe less. Whatever you're walking toward up there — walk faster."

* * *

They loaded the *Kestrel*'s essential gear into Brann's canvas portage packs as the light died — food, rope, the survey journal they'd found at the abandoned camp, dry clothes, and the compass, which Sela took back from him without a word and stowed against her breastbone, where it belonged. Below the falls, in berth six, the *Kestrel* rocked gently in the boathouse gloom, her paint scarred, her rails worn smooth under ten years of Mirabel's hands.

He stood a long time looking at her.

"She'll be here," Sela said from the doorway.

"Boats are promises," Mirabel said. "That's what my father used to say. Every hull is a promise you make to the water." He touched the doorframe once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "I've broken most of mine. Not this one."

They climbed the switchback trail in the dark, guided by Toma — a huge, silent man with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light — past the groaning hoists and the mules asleep on their feet, up and over the shoulder of the gorge while the falls roared below them like something impatient. At the crest, Toma paused and looked back the way they'd come, down the black ribbon of the middle river.

Far downstream — miles, but visible, unmistakable — a light was moving against the current. White, steady, riding high in the water. Inspection colors.

Halloway.

Toma spat over the edge. "Your enemy travels fast," he said.

Mirabel looked at the light, then at Sela, then up, where beyond the gorge's rim the first peaks of the glacier country stood pale against the stars, cold and enormous and waiting.

"Faster than us," he said. "On water." He shouldered his pack. "So we stop racing her on water. From here, Captain Halloway walks."

Above them, faint on the night wind, so faint he might have imagined it, the glacier made a sound — a deep, tectonic groan, like a door being tested in its frame.
