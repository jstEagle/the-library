## 9. Night on the Shingle

The moraine announced itself before we saw it — a black spine of broken stone lying across the valley floor like something the glacier had coughed up and abandoned. All afternoon we had poled and dragged the Kestrel through water gone the color of milk, thick with rock flour ground off the ice above, until even Mirabel, who read rivers the way priests read scripture, began to mutter about channels that shifted under his hands.

Now he beached her on a shingle bar of grey slate, flat as a table and wide enough for a fire that would not spread, and stood with his boots in the shallows looking upstream at what remained of the world.

"That's it," he said. "That's as far as she goes."

I had heard him talk to the boat before — small apologies, mostly, when the hull scraped stone — but this was different. He said it the way a man tells a horse they've come to the end of the trail together.

"Can she be defended here?" I asked. "If Halloway comes?"

He looked at me then, and I saw him decide not to answer honestly. "She can be hidden," he said. "Nothing on this river is defensible. That's the first thing you learn. The second thing is that hiding works better than defending anyway." He ran his hand along the gunwale the way another man might touch a sleeping child's head. "She'll wait for us. Boats are patient. It's people who aren't."

We made camp while the light went long and copper across the ice fields. Mirabel built the fire low, behind a windbreak of stacked slate, and cooked river trout wrapped in clay he'd dug from the bank, and I realized somewhere between the first bite and the last that I had not eaten a proper meal since Aldric disappeared. Grief does that. It eats your appetite before it eats anything else.

The cold came down off the glacier in earnest once the sun dropped behind the western ridge. It wasn't like river cold, which has movement in it, breath in it. This was old cold, stored cold, cold that had been waiting inside the ice for longer than there had been anyone to feel it. We sat close to the fire without discussing it, shoulders almost touching, and watched the glacier face five miles upstream catch the last light and hold it, glowing faintly blue against the darkening sky like a lamp left burning in an empty house.

"You've been quiet all day," Mirabel said. "Quieter than usual, I mean. And you keep touching your pocket."

I hadn't known I was doing it. My hand went to my coat and found the compass there, its brass case warm from my body heat, and I understood that he was right — I had been checking on it every few minutes like a woman checking on a sleeping child, or a prisoner counting the bars of a cell.

"I need to tell you something," I said.

He waited. He was good at waiting; I'd learned that much about him in three weeks on the river. Most men fill silence with their own noise. Mirabel treated silence like a mooring — something you tied yourself to and held still against the current.

"Aldric didn't just disappear," I said. "And he wasn't just surveying."

* * *

I told him all of it, there on the shingle, with the fire burning down to coals and the glacier humming its one low note across the miles of frozen air between us and it.

I told him how Aldric had come home in the spring two years ago after his first season with the Survey, and how he'd been different — not frightened exactly, but *compressed*, the way a man gets when he's holding something too large for the room he's standing in. How he'd started locking the door of his study. How he'd taken to walking at night, and coming back with mud on his boots from places there was no honest reason to walk.

How one night in autumn I'd woken to find him gone from our bed and followed him down to his study, where he sat surrounded by papers by candlelight, copying figures from Survey ledgers into a notebook of his own, and when I asked him what he was doing he'd looked up at me with an expression I have never been able to forget — relief and terror in equal measure, the look of a man who has been carrying a weight alone and has just remembered he isn't alone after all.

"He showed me the ledgers," I said. "The original ones, from the founding of the indenture system. Not the copies the valley offices keep — the originals, from the Survey archive. He had authority to access them because of his position. And Mirabel—" My voice caught, and I made myself go on. "The debts are invented. All of them. Every family in this valley that owes the Survey, owes it for land the Survey never bought and never owned. The numbers were written into the rolls sixty years ago out of nothing. There was no purchase. There was no agreement. There were no payments ever received, because there was nothing to pay for. A clerk created the debt entries, and the clerks after him collected interest on them, and the interest became liens, and the liens became generations of families bound to ground that was theirs before the Survey ever existed."

Mirabel had gone very still. Not the stillness of surprise — I don't think any part of what I said surprised him, not really. It was the stillness of a man watching a map of his own life redraw itself.

"My father," he said quietly.

"Your father's name is in the rolls. So is mine. So is everyone's." I pulled the compass from my pocket and set it on the slate between us, where the firelight moved across its brass face. "Aldric spent that whole winter verifying it. Cross-checking. He found the original clerk's own notes — marginalia, corrections, a private ledger where the man tracked what he'd done like a sinner keeping count of sins. And then he found something else. A vault."

"The drowned village woman called it that," Mirabel said. "The key they couldn't break."

"She knew more than she let on. Aldric met with them too — the stilt-people, the ones the reservoir took. Some of the oldest families in the valley ended up there, the ones who refused hardest. They kept things. Papers passed down in oilskin, fragments of the truth, stories their grandmothers told them. Aldric gathered everything. And then he hid it."

"Where?"

"At the source. There's a place behind the calving face — Aldric found it during his first survey season, a cavern in the blue ice, dry, stable, older than any government that ever claimed this valley. There are carvings in the walls, Mirabel. Marks from before the Survey, before the provinces, before any of it. People have been coming to that ice for a thousand years to mark that they existed. Aldric decided it was the one place in the world the Survey would never think to look, because the Survey believes nothing exists except what it has measured." I turned the compass over in my hands. Its needle trembled, straining upstream toward the glow of the ice. "This is his locator. He built it himself — reworked the mechanism so it ignores north entirely. It points to the vault. He tuned it to the cache itself. As long as the vault stands, the compass knows the way."

"And the team?" Mirabel asked. "The men who went up with him?"

"They helped him carry the cache in. And then—" I stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, the glacier groaned, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep. "The journal you found at their camp. You read it. The compass sang near the ice wall, and they walked into the whiteout together. I've thought about that for months, Mirabel. I've dreamed it. And I believe Aldric sent them away. I believe he told them to walk out and forget, and they refused, or they couldn't — and then the storm came, and the mountain did what the mountain does. Or—" I couldn't say the rest. I picked the compass up and held it against my chest.

"Or he stayed," Mirabel said softly. "To guard it."

I nodded, and could not speak for a while.

"Why didn't he bring it down?" Mirabel asked finally. "Why hide it at all? Why not take the proof to Corren's Ford, to the magistrates, to—"

"To whom?" I said. "The magistrates are appointed by the provincial governor, who is appointed by the Survey directorate. The courts enforce the liens. The churches bless the contracts. There is nowhere in this valley the Survey's word isn't already written on the walls." I looked at him. "Aldric understood that bringing the proof down meant losing it. One fire. One 'lost' satchel. One clerk's mistake and sixty years of theft stays buried forever. No — it had to stay above the law's reach until it could be brought down all at once, publicly, irrevocably. Read aloud. In front of everyone. Names spoken in daylight where they can't be unspoken."

"And he went up alone to arrange it," Mirabel said, "and never came down."

"He wrote me a letter before he left. I have it memorized. He said: *If I don't return, the compass will find someone. It always points at what matters.* I thought it was a husband's poetry. I thought he was being dramatic because he knew I'd worry." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I didn't know he was leaving me instructions."

Mirabel reached over and fed a split of slate-dry driftwood to the coals. For a long time he didn't say anything, and I let him not say anything, because I could see the thing working in him — I could see the boy from the flashback he'd finally given me pieces of, the one whose father signed away their claim and drowned owing it, meeting the news that the signature had been a fraud from the very beginning. That his father hadn't failed. That his father had been robbed, systematically, by arithmetic.

"The Nystrom entry," he said at last. His voice was flat in a way I'd learned meant the opposite. "In the rolls. What does it say?"

"Eighty acres, upper middle reach. Debt assessed at founding, no transaction record. Interest compounded annually for fifty-one years." I recited it because I had copied the rolls myself, all of them, the winter Aldric worked. "Your father inherited a number that was never real, Mirabel. And paid on it his whole life."

He nodded slowly. He put another piece of wood on the fire, though it didn't need it, and I understood that his hands needed the work more than the fire needed the fuel.

"All those years," he said. "Running. Dodging collectors. Sleeping in the Kestrel with one eye open, ten years of my life spent being invisible so nobody could collect on a debt that was *invented*." He laughed too, and his laugh was also wrong. "I've been hiding from a ghost. A clerk's pen stroke."

"Yes."

"And if the vault opens — if the rolls are voided —"

"Then the land belongs to the families who work it. All of it. Legally, permanently, past any appeal. Aldric built the documentation so airtight that even the Survey's own courts can't refuse it. That was his genius. He didn't write a manifesto. He wrote *paperwork*. Bulletproof, boring, undeniable paperwork. He used to say the revolution would arrive in triplicate."

That startled a real laugh out of him, brief and rough, and then he looked at the compass in my hands, and then upstream at the ice, and I watched the last of the running man drain out of his face. What replaced it was something older and harder and quieter.

"Halloway," he said. "She wants it destroyed. The vault, the documents, the witnesses."

"She'll want us dead before the calving seals the route. If the flood closes the canyon with the proof still inside, it's buried for another year — maybe forever, if the Survey uses the year to make us all disappear quietly. She doesn't have to catch us. She just has to slow us down."

"Then we don't get slowed down." He said it simply, without drama, the way he steered through rapids — reading the water, choosing the line, committing. "We go up tomorrow at first light. Light loads. Leave the Kestrel."

I heard what it cost him to say that — leave the boat, his last possession, his home for ten years — and I put my hand over his where it rested on the slate, and he didn't pull away.

"There's something else," I said. "Something I should have told you before I hired you, and didn't, because I was afraid you'd refuse. The letter. Aldric's letter. There was a line I left out." I made myself meet his eyes. "*If the compass finds a man named Nystrom, trust him with everything.* He knew your name, Mirabel. He'd traced the rolls far enough to know whose son you were. He chose you before I ever found you at Graylock Landing. I was the messenger. The compass was the message."

Mirabel was silent for a long moment. Then he huffed — not quite a laugh, not quite a breath knocked out of him.

"Ten years dodging the Survey," he said, "and I was drafted by a dead surveyor." He shook his head. But something in his shoulders had settled, some argument he'd been having with himself since Graylock Landing finally concluded. "All right, Sela Voss. Tomorrow we climb. And when we come back down—" he looked at the fire, at the dark, at the blue lamp of the distant ice — "we come back down with paper strong enough to hang them all."

* * *

I slept badly, which was normal now, but differently than usual. Usually I dreamed of Aldric drowning, or walking into white, or sitting at his desk with his back to me. That night I dreamed instead of the market cross at Corren's Ford, and a voice reading names aloud, and each name landing on the cobbles like a coin, and the crowd growing larger with every name until the whole valley was there, listening.

I woke once, deep in the dead hours, to a sound I couldn't place — a vast, slow exhalation from upstream, the glacier shifting in its sleep, settling its weight. Beside the dead fire, Mirabel sat up, awake too, the compass cradled in both hands, watching its needle strain toward the mountains in the starlight.

"It's louder tonight," he murmured. "The pull. Like it knows we're close."

"Is that possible? A machine knowing something?"

He considered it with the seriousness he gave to rivers. "Everything up here knows something," he said. "The ice remembers more than we do. Maybe the brass is just honest about it."

He handed the compass back to me, and I held it against my chest, and felt it tremble — faint, insistent, faithful — like a heartbeat under the brass, like a man on the far side of the whiteout, still keeping his appointment.

We broke camp in the grey half-light, cold biting through wool, breath hanging in flags. Mirabel walked the Kestrel's line twice, checked her mooring, whispered something to her bow that I pretended not to hear. Then he shouldered his pack, and I shouldered mine, and we turned our backs on the river that had carried us and started up the slate scree toward the ice-carved canyon, toward the source, toward whatever Aldric had left waiting in the blue dark behind the calving face.

Behind us, far downstream, I thought I heard it — a sound too low for ears, more felt than heard, a vibration in the stone under our boots. An engine, perhaps. A gunboat pushing hard against the current, closing the miles we'd gained.

Mirabel heard it too. He didn't turn around. He only lengthened his stride, and said, without looking back:

"Faster, then."
