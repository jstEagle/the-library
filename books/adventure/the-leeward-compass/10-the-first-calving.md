## 10. The First Calving

The sound came at dawn, before the light did.

Mirabel was standing in the shallows of the shingle bar, ankle-deep in water so cold it burned, when the mountain spoke. It was not thunder — he knew thunder the way a riverman knows his own pulse, and this was not that. Thunder rolls and fades. This *cracked*. A single report, vast and clean, like the sky splitting along a seam no one had known was there, followed by a long groaning exhalation that rolled down the canyon and set the gravel trembling under his boots.

He turned toward the glacier's face, three miles up-valley and visible between the canyon walls as a wall of blue-white light, and watched a piece of it fall.

It was impossible to judge the scale of what he saw. That was the first thing he understood, standing there with the water pulling at his ankles: the eye had no reference for it. A chunk of ice the size of a chapel — or a cathedral; distance made liars of them both — sheared from the face and dropped, and the sound of its landing arrived seconds later, a boom that he felt in his sternum rather than heard, and then the white plume went up, snow-smoke rising slow as a thought, and the river below the face flinched.

The whole river. He could see it from here — the Vellwater's youngest water, gray-green with rock flour, running out from beneath the ice — and the entire surface of it jumped, a single ripple racing downstream toward him like something fleeing.

Behind him, Sela came out of the tent with her hair half-braided and her hand already on the knife at her belt. She looked at the mountain, then at him, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the sound hadn't already said better.

"That's the calving starting," Mirabel said. His voice came out flat. He was surprised by how flat. Inside him there was a noise like that crack, some old structure giving way, but none of it reached his mouth. "That's the window opening."

"And closing."

"And closing. Both at once. Same door." He waded back to the bank, wrung out his trouser legs with the automatic economy of a man who had dried clothes over a hundred campfires, and looked up at the glacier for a long moment. The face stood serene again, as if nothing had happened, as if it hadn't just shrugged off a hundred thousand tons of itself into the valley. That was the thing about mountains, his mentor Ovid used to say. They never apologize. They never even notice.

* * *

They spent the morning making ready, and every hour the glacier spoke again.

Not always the great cracks. Most of the sounds were smaller, stranger — a ticking, a knocking, deep inside the ice, like knuckles on a door; a hiss of meltwater finding new channels; once, a long metallic shriek that Sela said sounded like a ship tearing loose from its moorings, and Mirabel, who had heard a ship do exactly that in an ice storm off Graylock years ago, could not argue. The glacier was not quiet. It was conversing with itself, negotiating its own weight, deciding what to keep and what to let go.

The Kestrel sat beached on the shingle above the high-water line, her hull propped on driftwood rollers, her deck swept, her lines coiled. Mirabel had run her aground here two days earlier at the last navigable water, wedged her bow into the gravel with the care of a man putting a child to bed, and told himself — the way you tell yourself things at night, in the dark, in the voice that lies so it can let you sleep — that she'd be here when he came back down.

Now he stood beside her with a canvas pack in one hand and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since dawn.

The route window was open. That was the good news, if you wanted to call it news. The melt was up, the ice was moving, the fissures behind the calving face would be breathing — exhaling cold air through whatever passages the summer had carved, which meant they might be passable where winter had sealed them shut. Aldric Voss's journal notes, copied in Sela's careful hand, suggested as much. But windows on glaciers were not like windows on houses. They did not stay open out of politeness. Every hour the sun climbed, more of the face let go, more meltwater charged down the canyon, and the low route along the river — the only route their strength allowed — grew less a path and more a gamble.

"We can't take her any farther," Sela said. She had come to stand beside him at the boat without his noticing, which unnerved him; a decade alone on the water had tuned him to footsteps on decks, and hers he still missed. "Even if we tried to warp her up the shallows—"

"I know." He ran his palm along the gunwale. The wood was sun-warm, the paint scarred, the brass fittings green at the edges. Ten years of his life lived in that curve of timber. "I know."

"You could wait here. Take her back down after the calving season, meet us at Corren's Ford in the autumn—"

"No." It came out faster than he intended, and harder. He heard how it sounded and made himself soften it. "If we come down and she isn't here, I'll want to know it was because the river took her, not because I left her. There's a difference. I can't explain it, but there is."

Sela studied him. She had a way of looking at a man as though reading a chart — checking his soundings, noting where the water shoaled — and he felt it now, patient and unembarrassed. "She's your last possession," she said quietly. "Isn't she?"

The question landed somewhere below his ribs. He looked at the Kestrel and thought about everything she was. Not a boat. A *record*. Every plank a year. The patched section amidships where he'd run the Narrows too fast in flood and torn out a strake, and limped forty miles downstream bailing with a bucket, laughing the whole way because the alternative was screaming. The wheelhouse where he'd slept through a decade of nights, listening to rain on the roof, listening to nothing, listening to the particular silence of a man who has arranged his life so that no one depends on him and no one is owed anything — including himself. The hidden drawer under the bunk where he kept the papers that proved who he wasn't. The name painted on the stern, *Kestrel*, in letters he'd repainted every spring, because a man should keep at least one promise perfectly even if it's only to a boat.

His father had owned land once. One field, terraced above the middle river, apples and barley, gone into the Survey's ledgers when Mirabel was nine years old, signed away by a man whose hands shook and whose arithmetic was worse than his desperation. And when the land went, something in Tomas Nystrom went with it, and ten years later the river finished the job, and Mirabel had learned the lesson the way children learn best: thoroughly, and forever. Own nothing. Owe nothing. Be a shadow on the water, quick and light and impossible to seize. The Kestrel had been the single exception — the one thing he'd permitted himself to hold — and he had told himself for ten years that even she was only borrowed, only a tool, only a means, that he could walk away from her tomorrow if the current demanded it.

Standing beside her now, with the glacier knocking its knuckles up the valley, he discovered the truth, which was that he had been lying beautifully and for a very long time.

"She's not a possession," he said finally. "Possessions are things you own. She's—" He stopped. Started again. "She's the only conversation I've had for ten years that never lied to me."

Sela put her hand on the gunwale next to his. She didn't touch him. She just rested her hand there, near his, the way you'd rest a lamp in a window.

"Then leave her well," she said. "Not abandoned. Well."

* * *

They stripped her in the early afternoon, working in the easy rhythm they'd found over the weeks upstream — Mirabel knowing what mattered, Sela learning faster than anyone he'd ever crewed with, both of them speaking mostly in gestures and short phrases, the language of shared labor, which is older and truer than most languages.

Out came the anchor and chain. Out came the spare line, the sail canvas, the tools worth carrying, the iron stove plate, the lantern. Out came the food locker's contents, repacked into two canvas packs and a seal-skin roll. Out came, after a moment's hesitation, the small tin box from the hidden drawer under the bunk — the papers proving who Mirabel Nystrom was not — and he weighed it in his hand for a long time, feeling its small dense uselessness, and then, in a gesture that surprised him more than the glacier's cracking had, he dropped it into the fire they'd built to burn the trash.

Sela watched the tin go into the flames and said nothing, which was its own kind of comment.

"Dead weight," he said.

"Mm," she said.

"It is. It's dead weight. Everything in there is about a man who doesn't exist anymore."

"Mm," she said again, and handed him the water skins, and let him have the last word, which he suspected was her way of taking it.

By mid-afternoon the Kestrel stood empty. They had lashed her cover down, doubled her mooring lines to living trees well above the flood-scour line, driven stakes, piled stone anchors — everything a riverman does for a boat he intends to find again. It was theater, maybe. Both of them knew it might be theater. The calving flood, when the big one came, would not consult their moorings. But Mirabel worked each knot with full attention anyway, because the work was the goodbye, and the goodbye deserved to be done properly.

Last of all he went aboard, alone, and stood at the wheel.

The wheelhouse smelled of himself — tar, sweat, the ghost of ten thousand cook-fires, the faint sweet rot of a bilge that never quite dried. He put his hands on the spokes, worn smooth and pale where his palms had polished them, and stood there while the glacier ticked and knocked up the valley, and let himself feel all of it, every year of it, the whole drowned decade, because he had learned on the water that grief avoided is grief compounded, and grief faced is just weather. It passes. You bail, you patch, you keep your heading.

"Thank you," he said aloud, to the boat, like a madman, like a riverman, which are often the same thing. "You carried me. Now sit tight."

He stepped off over the bow, onto the shingle, and did not look back. He was proud of that later — that he didn't look back — and ashamed of the pride, and he supposed that mixture was simply what it cost to be a man leaving the only home he'd had.

* * *

The canyon above the last water was a country made of the glacier's old decisions.

They climbed through it as the light went long and gold, following a track that was barely a track — a shelf of gravel and lichen-cracked stone above the river, sometimes vanishing entirely into scree, sometimes forcing them down almost to the waterline where the melt ran fast and milky and loud. The walls rose sheer on either side, granite striped with the marks of ancient ice, grooved and polished like the inside of a kiln, and everywhere the evidence of water's patience: potholes drilled thirty feet above the present river, boulders the size of cottages sitting in the channel as if placed by a careless god, hanging valleys bleeding thin silver waterfalls into the main gorge.

Mirabel had never walked this far upstream. No one he knew had, not honestly — the maps went blank above the Gorge Narrows, marked with the Survey's dry notation *unsurveyed — glacial hazard*, which was official language for *we don't know and we don't want to spend the men finding out*. He had expected desolation. What he found instead, as the miles passed, was a kind of terrible beauty that made him feel briefly, absurdly, like a trespasser at a service.

The light did things in that canyon he had no names for. It came off the ice fields above in long slanting sheets and turned the granite rose-gold; it filled the melt-pools with a blue so pure it looked manufactured; it caught the dust of some distant collapse still drifting in the air and hung it there, glowing, like the canyon was remembering a fire. Sela stopped twice just to stand in it. Mirabel found himself stopping too, and hating that he stopped, and stopping anyway.

They made camp at dusk on a gravel fan where a side creek came down, in the lee of a boulder split cleanly in half by frost. Small fire, screened. Cold rations heated in a tin. Sela checked the compass — Aldric's compass, the brass one, riding now in a padded case against her chest — and reported its behavior in the clipped tone she'd adopted for it, the tone of a woman discussing a family member's illness.

"Trembling. Stronger than yesterday. Still bearing up-canyon, straight at the face."

"Still singing?" He'd taken to using the dead surveyor's word for it, from the journal — *the compass sings near the ice wall* — because no other word fit. The needle didn't point the way needles point. It strained. It quivered against its pivot like a live thing smelling home.

"Still singing." She closed the case. For a while neither of them spoke. The side creek talked to itself over the stones, and far up the darkening valley the glacier knocked, patient, enormous, keeping its own time.

"Mirabel." Her voice came differently now. Lower. "Back at the boat. When you burned the papers. What was in them? Truly?"

He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Debt assignments. Transfer records. My inherited obligation, registered in Corren's Ford, assigned to my father, originated—" he waved a hand at the darkness up-valley, at the whole invisible machinery of it, "—originated wherever those things originate. Twenty-two years of interest accrued on a field of apples that hasn't existed since I was a boy. Papers saying I owed my life to a ledger."

"And burning them changed nothing legally."

"Not a thing. The ledger in Corren's Ford is the true copy. Mine was just... the shadow of it. The part I carried." He smiled without much humor. "But shadows get heavy, Sela. Ten years of carrying a shadow. You'd be amazed."

She was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had that careful quality he'd learned to recognize — the sound of a woman approaching her own confession sideways. "Aldric carried papers too. In his head, mostly. Numbers. He'd lie awake doing arithmetic and I'd ask him what he was calculating and he'd say, 'Whether the valley adds up.' I thought it was a joke." She turned her hands toward the fire. "I don't think it was a joke anymore."

"No," Mirabel said. "I don't suppose it was."

Up the canyon, the glacier cracked again — smaller this time, a pistol-shot echo rolling down the gorge — and both of them looked up instinctively, the way you look toward a door when someone knocks.

"The window," Sela said.

"Closing." He calculated by feel, the way he read sandbars: sun's angle, air temperature, the sound of the meltwater, which had risen audibly since noon, a fuller, hungrier voice in the dark. "Two days. Maybe three if we're lucky and the cold comes back at night. After that the low route floods and the face lets go in earnest, and nobody walks this canyon until next summer."

"Then we walk fast."

"We walk fast," he agreed, and lay back on his bedroll, and looked up at the strip of stars the canyon allowed.

He expected to grieve the Kestrel all night, and he braced for it the way you brace for rough water. But somewhere past midnight, waking briefly to check the fire, he noticed the strange lightness in his chest and took inventory, puzzled, before he understood: he was carrying nothing. No boat to protect. No papers to hide. No false name to maintain, no port official to dodge, no debt-shadow dragging behind him through the dark. For the first time in his adult life, Mirabel Nystrom owned nothing at all, and the feeling was not emptiness.

It was ballast coming free. It was the boat lifting off the sandbar when the flood finally rises — that sudden, terrifying, glorious lift, when the thing that held you down becomes the thing that carries you.

He lay back down and slept without dreaming, and the glacier sang to itself all night, and in the morning they broke camp in the gray light and climbed toward the source.

By noon of the second day they could see the ice wall through the canyon's last bend — a mile of blue-white light standing against the sky — and the compass case against Sela's chest was trembling hard enough now that they could hear it, a faint dry rattle like a moth against glass.

And below them, at the canyon's mouth, far behind, Mirabel heard — or thought he heard, or perhaps only felt, in the soles of his boots, in the marrow of him — a sound that did not belong to the glacier at all.

Engines. Small ones. Coming up the valley, slow and steady, the way a man rows who is in no hurry because he knows exactly where you're going.
