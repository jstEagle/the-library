## 11. Tracks That End Nowhere

The moraine gave up its secrets grudgingly.

We had climbed since first light through a canyon the glacier had chewed out of living rock, walls of gray granite streaked with the rust-bleed of old iron seams, and by midday the river — what remained of it this far up — had shrunk to a braided trickle running milky over stones ground round as knuckles. The air tasted of cold stone and something faintly sweet, like the inside of an icehouse. Sela walked ahead when the footing allowed and behind when it didn't, and we had stopped speaking much, because up here breath was currency and words spent it fast.

It was Sela who saw the tent pole first.

"There." She pointed with her chin, hands buried in her sleeves against the wind that came down off the ice field in long, steady exhalations, like the mountain breathing in its sleep.

I followed her arm to a shelf of gravel above the stream's edge, half-screened by a fallen slab of rock furred with lichen the color of old brass. A pole jutted from the scree at an angle no careful camper would have chosen. Beyond it, another. Canvas, weathered to the pale gray of fish skin, sagged between them.

I stood very still for a moment and let the place come to me the way I let a river come to me — not looking straight at anything, letting the whole of it press against my attention at once. It was a habit from years of reading sandbars: the eye catches the obvious, but the water tells you the truth in its margins.

The margins here were wrong.

"Nobody's been looted," Sela said. She had already started up the slope, boots crunching on shale. "If they'd been robbed, there'd be nothing left. If they'd starved, they'd have eaten their boots. If they'd—"

"Slow down," I said, though she didn't, and I went after her because leaving her alone among the dead's belongings was worse than whatever waited up there.

* * *

There were four tents. Three stood as they'd been pitched, guy-lines still taut, pegs seated deep in the gravel. The fourth had collapsed inward, its ridgepole snapped, and snowmelt had pooled beneath it and frozen into a lens of dirty ice that held the canvas like amber holds a fly.

Between the tents lay the camp itself, arranged with the tidy geometry of government men: a cook fire ringed with stones, a crate of survey instruments under oilcloth, a stack of ration tins with two opened, a folding table bearing maps pinned flat with stones against a wind that hadn't blown hard enough yet to move them.

And meals. That was the thing that put the cold in me deeper than the altitude did. Tin plates sat where men had set them down. One held a heel of bread gone to stone, a smear of rendered fat still glossy on its surface after however many months. Another held beans, dried into a dark crust, a spoon standing upright in them as if the eater had stepped away mid-bite and meant to return.

"No bodies," Sela said quietly. She was turning in a slow circle, and I watched her do what I was doing — counting the ways a camp could end and finding none of them fit. "No blood. No signs of a struggle. No graves."

"Government men don't dig graves for each other anyway," I said. "They pack them downriver for the paperwork." But even as I said it I knew it wasn't true either. There would have been *something*. A dropped coat. A scattered instrument. Men don't leave a table mid-meal unless the mountain tells them to.

I crouched at the fire ring. The ashes beneath were compacted by meltwater and refreezing, seasons of it, but the arrangement of stones told me the fire had been built competently, banked properly the last time it was lit. These were not panicked men. These were men who expected tomorrow.

Sela lifted the oilcloth from the instrument crate. Beneath it, nested in straw, lay a theodolite with its brass fittings untarnished, a box of glass plates, a leather case of drafting tools, and — my stomach tightened — a second compass. Ordinary, government-issue, its needle swinging lazily and settling wherever it pleased, drunk or broken or simply honest about how little it cared for north up here.

"Aldric's was better than this," she murmured. "He had it made special. He never said why."

"He knew why," I said. "He just didn't say."

She looked at me then, and I saw her deciding whether to be angry that I'd spoken of her husband with such certainty. She decided against it. Grief makes room for strange alliances.

* * *

The journal was in the third tent, wrapped in a spare shirt inside a waterproof satchel, which told me its owner had valued it above his rations. The shirt smelled of camphor and cold. I carried it out to the light and sat on a crate while Sela crouched beside me, close enough that I could feel her breath fog past my shoulder.

The name inked inside the cover was *T. Ellery Voss*, assistant surveyor, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *for whoever comes looking — read all of it before you judge us.*

"His brother," Sela whispered. "Tam. He was twenty-three."

I opened it and began at the back, because journals written by dying or disappearing men always turn honest at the end, and I wanted the truth before the bureaucracy.

The early entries were exactly what you'd expect: elevations, barometric readings, complaints about the food, a sketch of a marmot done with real affection. Tam Voss had been a good hand and a bored one. Then, six weeks in, the handwriting changed. Not deteriorated — *tightened*, letters pressed harder into the paper, as if the man writing them needed the page to hold still.

*Anomaly confirmed again today. Aldric is beside himself. The needle won't hold north within two miles of the ice wall. It doesn't wander — it PULLS. We ran the traverse three times and got three identical bearings, all pointing into the glacier, all pointing at the same spot. There is nothing on any map at that bearing. There is nothing ON the map at all. The upper valley is blank paper. Ask yourself who blanks a map.*

I turned pages. The entries came faster now, crowded together, some written sideways in margins.

*Aldric showed me what he's really been sent to find. Not anomalies. CLAIMS. Old ones. Pre-collapse. The vault isn't Survey property — it predates the Survey. And the debt rolls we've been carrying copies of... God. I've been helping carry chains for our own enslavement.*

Sela's hand closed on my wrist. Her fingers were freezing and shaking and did neither of those things gently. "Keep reading," she said, so I kept reading.

*Halloway's name is on the supply manifests. Not Dree Halloway the enforcer — someone with the same surname, thirty years ago, listed as "resettlement officer." Resettlement. That's what they called it when they flooded the villages.*

I felt Sela go rigid beside me, and I understood why. Halloway. The name had been following us up the river like a shadow follows a boat, and now it reached ahead of us too, written in a dead man's hand before any of us had ever heard it spoken across the water.

But it was the last entry that I have never managed to leave behind. It was dated the day before the date the team was marked missing, and it filled the final page and spilled onto the back cover, the pen digging nearly through the cardboard:

*The compass sang last night.*

*I don't know how else to write it. We were camped at the ice wall — closer than we should have gone, Aldric insisted, he said we were almost there — and around midnight the compass began to hum. Not rattle. HUM. A note, low and steady, like a wet finger on glass rim. Perrin laughed at first. Then nobody laughed. You could feel it in your teeth. In your chest. It wasn't frightening exactly. It was — inviting. Like hearing your own name called in your mother's voice from a house you haven't seen since childhood.*

*Aldric says the vault was built by people who understood the iron in this mountain, who tuned it somehow, and the compass is the other half of the tuning fork. He says it's been trying to sing to us since Graylock Landing and now we're close enough to hear.*

*I want to believe him. I want to follow that sound more than I have ever wanted anything, and that is precisely what frightens me. Because I don't think the wanting is mine.*

*Perrin stood up an hour ago and started walking toward the wall without his coat. Aldric had to shake him awake from it. Perrin says he doesn't remember standing. Says he dreamed the ice was open and warm inside, full of light, and everyone he's ever lost was waiting.*

*We are going in together at dawn. All of us. I know how that sounds. I am writing this so that somebody, somewhere, knows we chose it — or believes we did. If you find this camp and no bodies, do not think we were taken. Think that we were CALLED, and ask yourself what kind of mountain calls men by name, and whether the Survey has known about it all along, and whether that is why the maps are blank.*

*The compass is singing right now. It has not stopped since midnight. It is the most beautiful sound I have ever heard and I am afraid of it and I love it and tomorrow we walk into the whiteout together, toward it, and I find that I cannot imagine wanting anything else.*

*Tell my brother's wife I'm sorry. Tell her the land was worth it. Tell her—*

The entry ended there, mid-sentence, the pen stroke trailing off the edge of the page in a long thinning line, as if the hand writing it had been pulled away.

I closed the journal. The silence of the camp rushed back in — that particular high-altitude silence, thin and total, broken only by the trickle of the stream and the occasional deep groan of the glacier somewhere above us, shifting its weight like a sleeper turning over.

Sela was crying without any noise at all, tears freezing on her cheeks before they could fall, and I let her cry, because there was nothing in me equal to comforting anyone just then. My hands wouldn't stop wanting to check my pockets. I finally gave in and drew out the brass compass.

It lay in my palm, trembling. Not swinging — trembling, a fine constant vibration, like a struck bell too quiet to hear.

Like something humming just below the threshold of sound.

* * *

We searched the rest of the camp because searching was better than thinking. We found no weapons fired, no flare casings, no torn clothing. We found eight bedrolls, seven of them slept-in and one of them — Aldric's, by the monogram — folded and unused for weeks before the end, as if he'd taken to sleeping at the ice wall itself. We found a tin of pencils sharpened down to stubs and a child's drawing tucked in one man's kit, a stick-figure family under a crooked sun, and I put it back exactly where it lay because some things are not mine to move.

It was late afternoon when Sela found the tracks.

She called my name — actually used my name, first time in days — and I came around the collapsed tent to where she stood at the camp's upstream edge, looking down at a stretch of fine gravel and old snowpack preserved in the shadow of the boulder field.

Footprints. Dozens of them. Boot prints, government-issue hobnails, all of them walking the same direction. Toward the glacier. None coming back.

They marched in loose formation at first, then tightened, converging, until near the edge of the snowfield they ran in single file, eight sets of prints overlapping, stepping in one another's marks like men following a leader — or a sound. And at the margin where the gravel gave way to bare ice, the prints grew strange. Shorter strides. Toes dug in. The prints of men leaning forward into a wind that the sheltered hollow couldn't explain.

Then the ice took them. The trail ran out onto the glacier's lower tongue and faded as the surface snow of intervening months had healed over it, and the last visible print was half-filled with refrozen meltwater that caught the afternoon light and burned blue.

Beyond it, the ice wall rose.

I had seen it distantly for two days, a white rampart closing the head of the canyon, but from the camp's edge it showed its true scale. Two hundred feet of blue-green ice, cliff-sheer, seamed and ridged like the muscle of some buried animal, streaming meltwater in a hundred silver threads. The sun was dropping behind the western ridge and the wall caught the last of it and held it, glowing from within, lit like a lantern behind frosted glass.

And the whole face of it was groaning. Not calving — not yet — but breathing, settling, uttering long descending notes that I felt in my boot soles and my back teeth. A chorus of low tones, layered, rising and falling.

Sela gripped my arm. "Mirabel. Listen."

"I hear it."

"It sounds like—" She stopped. Started again. "When I was a girl, my grandmother had a glass harmonicon. You wet your finger and run it round the rim and it sings. It sounds like that. Like dozens of them."

I didn't answer, because answering would have meant saying what I was thinking, and what I was thinking was this: Tam Voss had written that the compass sang, and that the singing was beautiful, and that he could not imagine wanting anything else. Eight grown men, provisioned for months, armed, disciplined, experienced in cold country, had finished their supper and walked out of a safe camp into a whiteout, together, smiling if their footprints told the truth, toward a wall of ice that killed everything it touched.

And the brass compass in my pocket was vibrating against my ribs like a heart that wasn't mine.

"We camp here tonight," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Not at the wall. Here. We light a big fire, and we eat hot food, and we tie ourselves together when we sleep, and in the morning we look at that wall in full daylight before we take one step toward it."

Sela nodded. But she was still looking at the ice, and her face in the dying light wore an expression I recognized from the drowned village, from the widow at Graylock Landing, from every mirror I'd avoided for ten years.

Longing.

That night I did not sleep well. I lay with my back to the fire and the compass wrapped in two shirts and stuffed in the bottom of my pack, ten feet away, and twice I woke to find myself sitting up, facing the glacier, with no memory of having moved. The second time, Sela was already awake, watching me, and she didn't ask, and I was grateful, because I had no answer that wasn't the journal's last line repeated back in my own voice.

Near dawn, the wind rose out of the west and brought cloud off the ice field in a slow gray flood, and by the time the sun should have cleared the ridge there was no sun, only a thickening white murk pressing down the canyon, erasing the wall, erasing the ridgelines, erasing the world beyond arm's reach.

A whiteout, building. The same weather, perhaps, that had swallowed eight men whole.

I stood at the edge of camp with the journal in my coat and the compass humming against my chest like a second pulse, and I looked into all that blankness and understood, finally, what had frightened Tam Voss more than the mountain's call.

It wasn't that the ice wanted them.

It was that some part of each of them had wanted it back — and the mountain had merely agreed.

Behind me, Sela broke the compass's silence at last. "It's louder," she said. "Mirabel — it's getting louder."

And through the gathering white, faint and unmistakable, so did I begin to hear it: a note rising off the glacier, low and sweet and patient, calling us by a name I couldn't quite make out — yet.
