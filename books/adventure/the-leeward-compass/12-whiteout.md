## 12. Whiteout

*Mirabel*

The mountain told them an hour before the sky did.

Mirabel felt it first in the soles of his boots — a tremor too fine to be a footstep, a shiver running up through the moraine like a whisper passed along a chain of hands. Then the light changed. The pale afternoon sun, which had been lying warm as brass on the glacier's shoulder, went suddenly the color of pewter, and the wind that had been at their backs all morning swung around without warning and began to push against them like a hand flat on the chest.

"Storm," he said.

Sela looked up from the compass cradled in her mittened palms. "How can you know that? There's not a cloud."

"There will be." He nodded eastward, where the ice fields ran up into a haze that wasn't haze at all but snow being torn off the heights and flung sideways before it had even finished falling. "Up here the weather doesn't arrive. It's *made*. The glacier breathes out, and what comes out is winter." He had learned this from his father, who had learned it from a fur trader who had gone up the Vellwater's head once and come back with half a face and a lifetime of stories nobody paid for. He'd thought those stories were drunk-talk. He was learning, day by day on this climb, how much of his father had been true.

They were two days above the abandoned survey camp now, following the canyon the meltwater had carved through the ice — a corridor walled in blue-green glass on either side, so smooth in places that their reflections traveled beside them like drowned companions. The compass had led them unerringly: its needle no longer trembling but straining, leaning toward the head of the canyon the way a dog leans at the end of a rope. Aldric Voss's brass instrument wanted to go home.

But the weather wanted them to stop.

"We should find shelter," Sela said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren't. He had watched her grow quieter over the past two days, watched the frost take small tolls he could name — the white fleck at the corner of her jaw she kept touching, the way her left glove had begun to hang loose because her fingers inside it had drawn up into claws she couldn't fully open. She hid it well. She hid everything well. But Mirabel had spent ten years reading water, and people were only water that walked.

"Not yet," he said. "Shelter in the open is a grave with better manners. We want walls. Real ones."

"And if there are none?"

He didn't answer that. Ahead, the canyon bent westward, and beyond the bend he could see the terrain opening — the ice spreading out into a field of broken plates and pressure ridges, a sea frozen mid-storm, jagged and blue and full of holes. A crevasse field. Every instinct he owned said *around*, but around meant climbing the moraine's shoulder in rising wind with darkness coming, and the compass strained straight across.

"The needle says through," Sela said.

"The needle doesn't have to put its foot down."

"No," she agreed. "We do."

He looked at her then — really looked — and saw something he recognized from his own mirror on hard mornings: the particular set of a person who has decided that fear is a luxury billed to someone else. She was thinking of Aldric. Somewhere under all that ice, or ahead of them, or already found and waiting, and every hour they hesitated was an hour the calving season ate.

"All right," Mirabel said. "Through. But we rope together, and you do exactly what I say, when I say it, even if it sounds mad. Especially then."

She tied the rope herself, quick and competent, a bowline he hadn't taught her. There was a great deal about Sela Voss he hadn't taught her.

* * *

*Sela*

The white came all at once, the way night comes to a candle.

One moment Sela could see thirty yards of broken ice ahead, Mirabel a dark sure figure testing each step with his pole; the next, the world contracted to a circle six feet wide, then four, then nothing but the rope in her hand and the sound of wind that was less a sound than a condition, a constant tearing shriek as though the air itself were being unpicked stitch by stitch.

She had been cold before. She had been born cold, more or less — Graylock Landing in a lean winter, a house that leaked wind like a sieve leaks grain. But this was different. This cold did not strike and pass. It *waited*. It stood beside her patiently, an enormous patient animal, and breathed on the back of her neck, and waited for her to tire.

"Marks!" Mirabel's voice reached her shredded by the wind, barely words. She had insisted he call her Marks — her childhood name, the one Aldric used, the one nobody else living knew — because out here, in the white, names mattered, and she had wanted to give him one that was hers alone. "Marks! Left! Two steps!"

She obeyed. Under her boot the snow gave way and she heard — felt — a hollow boom roll away beneath her, vast and distant, the sound of a space with no bottom accepting a small offering of powder snow. A crevasse, hidden under the bridge of fresh snowfall. If she had been one step right instead of left—

"Again!" And again. And again. He read the field the way he read the river, she realized — not seeing it but *hearing* it, feeling the density of the snow through the pole, listening for the drum-sound of hollow places, steering them along seams invisible to any eye. Twice he stopped her dead with a raised fist and made her stand motionless for a full minute while he probed some treachery ahead. The cold poured into her stillness. She counted her heartbeats to keep from counting her fears.

By what she guessed was late afternoon — the light never changed enough to tell — the rope went taut and hauled her forward faster than walking, and she stumbled into the lee of a pressure ridge where two slabs of ice had leaned together like conspirators, leaving a slot of still air between them. Mirabel was already chipping at the base of the ridge with the pick end of his pole.

"It'll do for tonight," he shouted. "Barely."

It did not do for tonight. She knew that within the hour. They hollowed out what shelter the ridge offered, backs to the ice, knees up, sharing one blanket because two blankets layered separately wasted heat, and the temperature fell through the floor of the world. Sela's feet stopped hurting, which frightened her more than pain ever had — she remembered Aldric telling her, laughing, terrible, about the surveyor's rule: *when the cold stops talking, start screaming.* She flexed her toes inside her boots until they woke and complained, and made herself keep doing it, hour after hour, a metronome against the dark.

Somewhere in the black hours she began to shake and could not stop. Not the ordinary shiver of cold but a deep rhythmic clattering of her whole frame, teeth first, then spine, then everything, as if her body had become a bell rung by the wind. Mirabel pulled her in against his chest, wrapped both arms around her, and began, absurdly, to talk.

"—so there's this lock keeper at Tarn Mill, oldest man on the middle river, and he swears the Kestrel's haunted—"

"I don't need a story."

"Everybody needs a story. It's the only fuel that burns wet." His voice was rough and low, close against her ear, a human sound in a world that had forgotten humans. "This lock keeper, he says every spring a woman in a green coat walks the towpath at dawn asking after a boat that sank forty years back. And I says to him, sir, I've piloted past Tarn Mill a hundred dawns and never seen her. And he says, course you haven't, boy. She only boards boats that are lost."

Sela laughed — a cracked, gasping thing, but a laugh. "And are we lost?"

"Not while I'm counting your toes." His hand pressed her boot. "Wiggle."

She wiggled. He talked. The story got worse — the ghost woman, it turned out, had opinions about cargo manifests — and somewhere in the middle of it the shaking slowed, and somewhere near the end of it she slept, wedged against him in a slot of ice while the storm screamed overhead like a world being scoured.

* * *

*Mirabel*

The second day was worse, because the second day he understood they might not walk out of it.

The storm did not break with the false dawn. It thickened. Visibility dropped from nothing to less than nothing — a white so total it produced shapes, phantoms, ridges and figures and doorways that dissolved when approached. Twice Mirabel rose to scout and sat back down within twenty steps, having nearly walked off the edge of the pressure ridge into a void the color of milk. The compass was useless in the whiteout's chaos — worse than useless, because its needle still strained faithfully toward the source, and the source was precisely where the storm was being manufactured. Following it now would mean walking into the mouth of the weather itself.

So he navigated the only way left: by memory. By the shape of the land as it had been before the white erased it. He made Sela describe their route aloud with him, step by step, backward — the bend in the canyon, the long slab of wind-carved ice like a ship's hull, the crevasse with the shattered snow-bridge, the twin boulders of gray stone riding the ice like rafts. Memory was a rope too. He paid it out hand over hand.

By midday Sela had stopped speaking except when spoken to, and her answers came slower each time, arriving like objects lowered down a deep well. When he checked her fingertips through the glove liners, the color was wrong — waxy, pale as the ice itself, and the left hand answered his touch a half-second late.

"Frostbite's starting," he said quietly. "Real frostbite. We move or we lose fingers."

"If we move we get lost."

"Then we get lost moving." He got her up. He got the rope on. And he did the thing he had sworn since the gorge he would not do unless death itself held the other option: he took out the compass and let it choose.

The needle leaned hard into the storm, unwavering, patient, certain. Toward the glacier's heart. Toward whatever had called Aldric Voss and eleven men and a government's worth of instruments up into the white and swallowed them whole. Mirabel looked at that straining brass needle and felt the hair rise on his neck, because it did not behave like metal anymore. Metal pointed. This *wanted*.

"Where's it taking us?" Sela asked. Her voice was thin.

"Shelter, if there's any mercy in it. The storm's blowing off the high fields — anything solid should be on our side of the wind." He clipped the compass inside his coat, against his chest, where its faint pull felt almost like a pulse. "Stay behind me. Step where I step. Count your steps — if we lose count, we're ghosts."

Two hundred and forty steps. He counted every one, shouting numbers into the wind like a man calling a prayer against a flood. At ninety the ground tilted. At a hundred and sixty they crossed a snow-bridge that boomed beneath them and held. At two hundred and thirty-nine his leading boot struck not snow but stone — bare, dry, ancient stone — and then the wind, which had been a wall at their faces, was suddenly at their backs, and the roaring dropped away as though a door had closed, and Mirabel Nystrom stood blinking in a dim blue light, holding a stranger's widow upright, alive, inside the mountain.

An ice cave. A real one — not a hollowed drift but a cavern, high-vaulted, its ceiling a smooth sweep of glacier ice glowing with the buried daylight, its floor old rock dusted with blown snow. The wind still howled somewhere far above and behind, muffled now, a beast shut outside a strong room. The silence inside was so complete that Sela's ragged breathing sounded like surf.

Mirabel got her sitting against the wall, got her boots off, got her feet into his hands. The toes were bad but not ruined — dusky, painful, *there*. He worked them until color came back, working in silence, and she watched him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

"You counted," she said finally. "All two hundred and forty."

"Habit. Rivers keep score too."

Then she turned her head, and went very still, and he followed her gaze to the wall.

* * *

*Sela*

The marks covered the ice in a band at eye height, running along the cavern wall like a frieze — hundreds of them, cut deep into the glacier's face and preserved in it, sharp-edged, untouched by time because time itself was frozen in the walls.

Not letters. Not any letters she knew. Spirals and branching lines, chevrons stacked like fish scales, circles with rays, long processions of small repeated signs that might have been words or might have been waves. Some were worn shallow, older than others; some were crisp as yesterday's work. Whoever had carved them had come back, season after season, generation after generation perhaps, adding to a record no living alphabet could read.

"Aldric drew things like this," she whispered. "In his notebooks. He thought they were river-marks — old channel markers, from before the Survey, before the companies. He said the valley had a script older than any charter." Her throat tightened. "I used to tease him. I said he was looking for treasure maps."

"Maybe he was." Mirabel had risen and stood before the carvings, his bare fingers hovering just above the ice, not quite touching. The blue light moved on his face. "Look at the spacing. See how they cluster?" He traced the air above a dense patch of spirals. "That's not decoration. That's a warning, or a claim, or a prayer. Somebody stood exactly here, in a storm exactly like ours, and needed the wall to remember something."

"Older than any government," Sela said. The phrase arrived in her mind whole, and she said it aloud because it was true and because saying true things had become, in the last days, a kind of lifeline. "Older than the debt rolls. Older than the dams. People were *here*, Mirabel. Before anyone decided who owned this valley, people were here, and they marked it, and the ice kept their mark when everything else washed away."

She looked down at her own hands — the damaged left one cradled in its wool wrapping — and thought of the papers in her trunk at Graylock Landing, the contract with the burn-marked seal, the debt inherited from a grandfather she'd never met for land she'd never seen. Three generations of families paying for a signature none of them had made. And here, inches from her face, was proof that ownership had once meant something else entirely: not paper, not ledgers, but this — a hand, a tool, a wall, a message passed forward freely to whoever survived long enough to read it.

Her eyes were stinging and she blamed the cold, and Mirabel had the grace not to look at her.

"They walked into the whiteout," she said instead, steadying her voice. "Aldric's team. The journal said the compass sang near the ice wall and they walked in together. Do you think—" She stopped. "Do you think they found this place?"

Mirabel was quiet a long moment. The glacier ticked and settled around them, ancient sounds, housekeeping sounds, a building older than nations keeping its accounts.

"I think," he said slowly, "that a cave like this doesn't stay secret. I think men lost in a whiteout find it the same way we did — by falling into the one pocket of quiet on the whole mountain. And I think a surveyor clever enough to hide a vault and forge a locator compass would look at these marks and understand exactly what kind of place he'd been led to." He turned to her, and the blue light made his scarred, weathered face look almost young. "Your husband didn't wander up here, Marks. He was *called*. Same as us."

Outside, the storm went on screaming, but beneath it — so faint Sela might have imagined it, so faint she would spend the rest of her life half-believing she had imagined it — the brass compass, inside Mirabel's coat, gave a single soft knock against his ribs. Like a knocker on a door. Like something below them, under miles of ice, turning over in its sleep and beginning, at last, to wake.

They slept that night in the oldest room in the valley, shoulder to shoulder against the carved wall, and neither of them dreamed of debts.

At first light the wind had died. Mirabel crawled to the cave mouth, looked out at a world scoured clean and glittering and brutally clear, and came back wearing an expression Sela had learned to dread.

"Storm's done," he said. "And the compass has stopped pointing at the glacier." He held it up. The needle strained downward — steeply, impossibly, toward the floor of the earth itself. "It's pointing *under* us. We're standing on top of it."
