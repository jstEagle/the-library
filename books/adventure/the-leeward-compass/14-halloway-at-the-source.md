## 14. Halloway at the Source

The ice sang all night.

I don't know how else to describe it. Not the groaning of a hull, not the creak of a house in wind — something older and more deliberate, as if the glacier were turning over in its sleep and muttering names. Mirabel slept badly beside our small fire in the lee of the moraine, one hand curled around the brass compass even in his dreams, and I lay awake listening to that singing and thinking about Aldric, who had heard it too, at the end, and walked toward it.

We had found his camp two days before — the tents still pitched, the kettle still on its stone, the journal with its last sane entry. *It sings near the wall. The others have stopped speaking. They say it is calling them home.* And then nothing but blank pages, and the whiteout that had swallowed eight men whole.

So when I woke on the third morning to the sound of boots on scree, my first thought — God forgive me — was that Aldric had come back. That the mountain had relented. That I would turn and see him standing against the blue light of the ice, gaunt and frost-burned and alive, saying *Sela, you shouldn't have come, but I'm glad you did.*

It was not Aldric.

* * *

There were three of them coming up the canyon's throat, and even at half a mile I knew the walk of the one in front. Captain Dree Halloway did not walk like a woman crossing ice. She walked like a woman inspecting a property she already owned. Her two men came behind her at parade distance, rope coiled over one shoulder, rifles slung muzzle-down the way soldiers carry them when they expect obedience rather than resistance.

Mirabel saw them a moment after I did. He had been banking the fire, and he went very still, the way he goes still on the river when the water ahead changes its mind. Then he said, quietly, "Sela. The satchel."

I had slept with it against my chest for two nights, strapped under my coat, Aldric's documents pressed to my ribs like a second heart. My hand went to it now without thinking. "They tracked us from the gorge," I said. "How?"

"Brann sold us." His voice was flat, but I heard what lived under the flatness — the shame from the portage, still festering. "Or Halloway paid someone who talked. Doesn't matter now." He stood, brushing frost from his knees, and looked down the canyon at the three approaching figures, and I watched him do the arithmetic he always did: current, load, draft, depth. Except this time the river was made of ice and the cargo was a dead man's truth.

"We could run," I said. "Up the fissure. We're close — the compass was pulling hard last night—"

"And she'd follow us into the vault, and then there'd be no one left to carry anything out." He turned to me. His eyes in the glacier light were the color of water over slate. "No. We meet her here, on open ground, where I can see her hands. You keep the satchel. If anything happens to me, you go up that fissure and you don't come back down, do you understand me? Not for me. Not for anyone."

"Mirabel—"

"Promise me."

I promised him. It cost me nothing to promise and would cost everything to keep, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so.

* * *

Halloway stopped twenty paces out — close enough to talk without shouting, far enough that her men could bring their rifles up if they needed to. She was not what I had imagined. I had built her, over the weeks of hearing her name, as something large and blunt, a hammer of a woman. Instead she was slight, gray-eyed, weathered the way river people are weathered, with a scar running through one eyebrow like a stitch in cloth. She could have been any captain's widow on any landing between Graylock and the Ford. She could have been me, ten years harder.

"Captain Nystrom." Her breath smoked. "And Mrs. Voss. You've made good time. Brann said you would."

"Brann talks too much," Mirabel said.

"Brann sells exactly as much as keeps her alive, which is a discipline you'd do well to study." Halloway let her gaze travel past us, up the canyon, to where the calving face rose three hundred feet into the morning, blue-white and groaning. "You're closer than I expected. I'll give you that. Most men would have turned back at the Narrows. Most sensible men would have turned back at the drowned village."

"You've been there," I said, before I could stop myself. "To the village."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, gated, gone. "I've been everywhere on this river, Mrs. Voss. It's my river."

"It's nobody's river," Mirabel said. "That's rather the point of rivers."

For a moment I thought she might smile. She didn't. She unslung a leather case from her shoulder instead, opened it with a gloved hand, and drew out papers — heavy paper, cream-colored, the kind the Survey used for contracts worth breaking families over. She held them out flat on her palm so we could both see the seal.

"A captain's commission," she said. "Vellwater Trade Authority, permanent charter, your own vessel — a real one, not that patched-up skiff you abandoned below the moraine. Crew of six, guaranteed cargo priority, exemption from every toll between here and the delta. And beneath that—" she tapped the second page "—full and final forgiveness of all debts registered against the name Nystrom. All of them. Your father's, your inherited portion, the interest, the penalties. Struck from the rolls. You'd be the first free man in your family in three generations, Captain, and you'd own a boat again by spring."

The wind came down off the glacier and pushed at us, cold as a hand. I watched Mirabel's face while she spoke, and it was the strangest thing — because I had seen that face refuse things before. He refused Sela's first offer twice. He refused Brann's compass bargain without blinking. But this refusal didn't come. What came instead was hunger, naked and terrible, the hunger of a man who has been invisible for ten years being offered a name.

"How long have you carried those papers?" he asked.

"Since Corren's Ford."

"You printed them before you knew whether I'd found anything."

"I printed them because I knew you'd find something." She folded the commission closed. "You strike me as a thorough man, Captain. Thorough men find things. That's why I came myself, rather than sending these two and a shovel."

"And the documents?" I said. "Aldric's work? The ledgers?"

"The ledgers are Survey property, taken illegally from a sealed site. Hand them over, and the glacier takes the rest — meaning no witnesses, meaning no testimony, meaning none of this ever happened." She said it plainly, without cruelty, the way a surgeon says *we'll need to take the arm*. "You go home rich and free and safe. The valley stays exactly as it is. Which, Mrs. Voss, is how the valley has stayed for sixty years, and how it would have stayed if your husband had stayed home where he belonged."

My hands were shaking inside my mittens. Not from cold. "He belonged here," I said. "He belonged wherever the truth was buried, because somebody had to dig it up."

"Did it free him?" Halloway asked, and her voice changed — dropped half a tone, lost its official edge. "Your husband. Did the truth warm him? Because I stood in that camp you found. I read that journal. Eight men walked into a whiteout chasing a song, Mrs. Voss, and the ice kept them. Truth doesn't float. It doesn't feed anyone. It just sits in a satchel getting heavier until it drags you under." She looked at Mirabel. "You of all people should understand weight. You've spent ten years throwing ballast overboard to stay afloat. Here's your chance to finally travel light."

* * *

She turned away from us then, deliberately, giving us room — a negotiator's trick, or perhaps something more honest than a trick. Her men held position. Mirabel and I stood in the wind between the fire's ashes and the ice, and I watched him wrestle.

I want to write down exactly what I saw, because I think it matters, because I think it was the truest thing I witnessed on the entire journey, truer even than the vault.

His jaw worked. His thumb found the compass in his pocket and pressed against it through the wool, the way a man presses an old wound to remind himself it's real. And his eyes went downstream — I could see it, the direction of his looking, though his head never turned. Down the canyon, down the gorge, past the drowned village with its stilted ruins, past Graylock Landing, all the way to a life where no one checked papers at the dock, where his father's name was ash on a ledger instead of a chain, where the Kestrel — or some better boat, some new boat — rode high and empty of debt.

Temptation wears a familiar face. That's what I understood, standing there. It doesn't come to you as a stranger offering gold. It comes wearing your own future, the one you'd have chosen if the world had been kinder. It comes sounding like your father's voice saying *at last, son, at last.*

"Halloway," Mirabel said at length. "One question."

She turned back. "Captain."

"The drowned village. Tarnholt." He said the name slowly, watching her. "Before the dam, before the reservoir. You know it."

"I know the file."

"I think you know the smell of it. Woodsmoke and willow and the mudflats at low water. I think you know the bell on the meetinghouse, cracked in '41, that rang flat on every note except the fifth." He took a step toward her, and her men shifted, and she lifted two fingers and they stilled. "Because I've seen the rosters, Halloway. I've spent a decade memorizing every list the Survey keeps, because my name's been on enough of them. And there's a Halloway on the Tarnholt rolls. Dree Halloway, age nine, dependent of Edda Halloway, indentured, parcel fourteen, flooded acreage, compensation: nil."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the glacier to speak into it — a deep internal crack, somewhere beneath us, like a knuckle cracking in the earth's hand.

When Halloway finally answered, her voice was quiet and level and worse than shouting. "Parcel fourteen was my grandmother's orchard. The trees were still bearing when the water came up. I watched the tops drown from the ridge. My mother held my hand so tight she broke two of my fingers, and she never once looked away, and neither did I, and the next spring the Survey offered me a scholarship because educated orphans make loyal employees." She smiled then, and it was the coldest thing I have ever seen a human face do. "So yes, Captain. I know the smell of it. I know it every morning when I put on the uniform. And I know this too: power is the only shelter that ever held. Not truth. Not memory. Not justice. The Survey drowned my home, and the Survey feeds me, clothes me, and keeps me warm, and every family in that valley would trade their grief for my berth tomorrow if you asked them honestly. Don't preach to me about the drowned. I'm the most successful thing the flood ever produced."

"That's not success," I said. "That's just drowning slower, and dragging others under to stay up."

Her gray eyes came to me, and for one unguarded instant there was something in them — nine years old, on a ridge, watching orchard tops go under — and then it was walled away, brick by brick, and the enforcer stood there again.

"The offer stands until sundown," she said. "After that, my men and I proceed to the source, and whatever we find there, we find alone. Think about traveling light, Captain. You've earned the right to try it." She began to turn away, then paused. "Mrs. Voss. For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — your husband died guarding something that will change nothing. I've read enough history to know how this ends. The paper burns, or the paper is filed, or the paper is read aloud once and forgotten by winter. The river doesn't care. I learned that at nine years old, standing on a ridge. The river doesn't care about any of us."

Then they were walking back down the canyon, three dark figures on the white ice, and the wind took their boot-prints and filled them with spindrift, and it was as if they had never been.

* * *

Mirabel didn't speak for a long time. He rebuilt the fire with hands that weren't quite steady, and fed it splinters of driftwood the old floods had left wedged in the moraine, and watched the flame take. I sat across from him with the satchel against my chest and waited, because I had learned on this journey that Mirabel Nystrom's silences were not empty; they were full, the way a lockgate is full, holding back a great pressure that will move everything when it moves.

"She grew up there," I said finally. "In the village. She's one of ours, Mirabel. One of the valley's children."

"I know."

"And she chose them anyway."

"She chose survival." He fed another splinter to the flames. "There's a difference, and it's the width of a hair, and it's the widest difference in the world." He looked up at me. "Sela. How much of what she said was true?"

"About the paper burning? About nothing changing?"

"About traveling light."

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty. "Some of it," I said. "Enough of it to frighten me. Enough that if I were alone — if it were only my freedom on offer — I don't know what I'd have done." I pulled the satchel strap tighter. "But it isn't only mine. That's the whole point of what Aldric found. It never was only mine. That's what she's never understood, because the Survey taught her that everything is only ever yours — your berth, your warmth, your shelter. She thinks we're negotiating over property. We're not. We're carrying other people."

He nodded slowly. Above us, the calving face caught the midmorning sun and threw it back in blades, and the singing started again, low and rising, and I saw him cock his head to it the way he cocks his head to a changing current.

"She gave us until sundown," he said. "Which means she expects us to run, or to deal, or to die. She doesn't expect us to climb." He stood, shouldering his pack, and the compass came out of his pocket into his palm, trembling, straining upward toward the fissure like a dog at the end of a rope. "The great calving is coming, Sela. You can feel it in the ground. When this face goes, it goes all at once, and everything below the moraine goes with it — including three Survey men standing on it taking notes."

"What are you saying?"

He was already moving toward the ice, and the light on his face was not the light of a man choosing escape. "I'm saying we stop hiding from her, and start using the only weapon we've got that she can't buy." He glanced back at me, and for the first time since the gorge, since Brann, since the whole long climb, he almost smiled. "The mountain. Get the satchel tight, get your crampons on, and stay exactly behind me. We're going to give Captain Halloway precisely what she asked for."

"Which is?"

"The source." He stepped onto the blue ice. "All of it."
