## 15. The Price of Invisible Men

The cavern breathed cold.

Mirabel felt it against his teeth, that deep glacial exhalation that came up through the blue floor like the slow pulse of something sleeping. Behind him, the fissure mouth framed a slab of grey sky; ahead, the ice walls curved inward until the light gave out, and in that failing light sat the things Aldric Voss had died beside — the oilskin satchel, the ledger rolls wrapped in waxed canvas, the recording cylinder with its brass fittings gone dull as an old coin.

And between Mirabel and all of it stood Captain Dree Halloway.

She had come into the cavern the way water comes into a hull — quietly, and then all at once. Two men behind her, one holding a lantern high enough to throw their shadows huge across the carved marks on the far wall, those spirals older than any government, older than any debt. Sela was pressed against the ice at Mirabel's left shoulder, her breath coming fast and shallow, and he could feel her trembling through the two feet of air between them, or maybe that trembling was his own.

"You've had time to think," Halloway said. Her voice didn't echo here; the ice ate it. "I watched you climb for three days, Captain Nystrom. I know what you're worth. I know what you owe." She let that word sit in the cold. "I'm offering to make it nothing."

"Nothing," Mirabel repeated.

"A captain's commission. Survey river service, if you want it — or private charter, your own papers, your own name clean in every port from Graylock to the sea. The debt roll burns tonight. You walk out of these mountains owning yourself." She tilted her head, and the lantern light moved across her face like water over stone. "All I ask is the satchel. The cylinder. The ledgers. Leave them where they lie, and let the glacier keep its secrets. It's kept worse."

* * *

He made himself look at her. Not at the pistol resting easy in her gloved hand, not at the two men flanking her — the big one with the lantern, the narrow one whose eyes never stopped moving between Mirabel and Sela and the satchel like a man counting money. He looked at Halloway's face, because there was something there he'd been trying not to see since Corren's Ford, since the gunboat, since the reeds where he'd crouched and watched her question a stilt-village fisherman until questioning became something else entirely.

She was younger than her rank. That was the thing. Thirty, maybe thirty-two, with lines around her mouth that came from pressing lips together rather than from laughing, and eyes the flat grey-green of reservoir water.

"You grew up downriver," Mirabel said.

Halloway's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter drawn one inch, no more. "What makes you say that?"

"The way you stand on a deck. Feet wide, knees soft. Nobody learns that in a Survey academy; they learn it poling skiffs before they're tall enough to see over the cargo." He nodded past her, back toward the fissure, toward everything below. "You learned it in the drowned country. Merrow's Reach, or one of the stilt towns. Am I close?"

The narrow man shifted his weight. The lantern flame shivered. For a long moment the only sound was the glacier working somewhere above them, a groan so deep Mirabel felt it in his sternum more than heard it, the sound of a mountain deciding.

"My mother sold fish on the Merrow embankment," Halloway said at last, and her voice was perfectly level, which was how Mirabel knew it cost her. "Before the dam. After, she sold fish to the company store, on credit, at company prices, until the credit ate everything she had left. I was eleven when we moved into the stilt houses. I was fourteen when I understood that the Survey hadn't drowned our village by accident. They'd drowned it because the valley floor was worth more underwater than under us." She looked at her own gloved hand, flexing it once. "So yes, Captain. I know exactly what those papers would say. I could have written half of them myself."

"Then why—"

"Because power is the only shelter there is." She said it simply, the way you'd state the depth of a channel or the weight of a cargo limit. No heat in it. No apology. "I watched my mother be honest for forty years and it bought her a stilt house and a cough from the damp. I watched good people drown twice — once when the water came, once when the ledgers came. And then I got smart, or cruel, whichever word you prefer, and I joined the thing that did it, and now my mother lives in a dry house in Corren's Ford with a stove that works and a doctor who comes when she coughs." She met his eyes. "The Survey erased my home, Captain. It also feeds her. I chose the hand that holds the bread over the hand that writes the elegy. Every person in this valley makes that choice eventually. Most just make it smaller."

* * *

Something cracked in Mirabel. He felt it happen — not loudly, not dramatically, but with the quiet structural dread of ice shifting under a hull. Because he understood her. That was the horror of it. Ten years on the Vellwater he had built his whole life on the same arithmetic: stay small, stay invisible, stay alive. He had told himself invisibility was freedom, but standing here in the cold heart of the mountain, listening to a woman say aloud the creed he'd only ever whispered to himself, he heard what it really was. Invisibility wasn't freedom. It was shelter — the cheapest kind, the kind that cost you everything you might have been.

His father had chosen differently. His father had signed nothing quietly, had argued, had petitioned, had walked sixty miles to file objections nobody read — and had drowned owing a debt that was, if Aldric Voss was right, invented in some Survey office by a man who never saw the river. Two roads. Both of them ended in the water.

Except maybe a third road. Except maybe this satchel, this brass compass trembling in Sela's pocket toward a truth buried in ice.

"Why tell me all this?" Mirabel asked. "You don't need me sympathetic. You need me compliant."

"I need you to understand that I'm not offering you a trick." Halloway took one step closer, boots crunching on ancient ice-dust, and lowered her voice as though the two men weren't there — or as though they were furniture. "I've read your file, Nystrom. Ten years of dodging pilots' fees and harbor dues and one very patient magistrate in Lowmarsh. You've spent a decade being nobody. I'm offering to make you somebody with a signature. There are men who'd kill for less than what I'm holding out right now."

"And the villagers," Mirabel said. "The fisherman in the reeds. The ones who talk to boats like mine. What do they get?"

Halloway was quiet for a moment. "They get the valley they already live in," she said. "Which is more than the glacier will give them."

Above them, the mountain groaned again — longer this time, a rising note like a bow dragged across the lowest string of some enormous instrument. Dust sifted from the fissure ceiling. The narrow man glanced upward, and Mirabel saw it: the flicker of fear that even Halloway's people carried, the animal knowledge that they were standing inside the throat of something vast and indifferent.

The calving season. Three days, maybe four, before the face above the source let go and sealed this canyon under a hundred tons of new ice until next spring. Maybe sooner. The glacier had been talking all morning, and glaciers, his old mentor Tam used to say, only raise their voices when they mean it.

* * *

"All right," Mirabel said.

Sela's breath caught beside him. He felt her turn, felt the weight of her stare, and he kept his eyes on Halloway because looking at Sela now would undo whatever he was building.

"All right," he said again, and made his shoulders drop, made his hands hang loose, put ten years of rivercraft into the performance of a man giving up. "You want the satchel. Fine. But I'll carry it out myself — it's wrapped against damp, and if you unwrap it wrong up here the wax cracks and the ink blooms inside a day. I've hauled paper cargo through wet seasons. You haven't." He shrugged, a small defeated shrug, the shrug of a man who has run out of river. "Let me carry it down to the boat, hand it over on open water, and you can burn your debt roll tonight like you promised. That's the deal. That's the only deal."

Halloway studied him. He watched her study him — watched the flat reservoir-water eyes move across his face looking for the flaw in the hull, the seam, the sandbar under the brown surface. He thought about the Kestrel, lost now to the moraine, and grieved her a second time in passing, because she had been the last thing he owned and he was learning, hour by hour, what a strange and hollow word *owned* had always been for him.

"The satchel doesn't leave this cavern," the narrow man said.

"It leaves," Halloway said, without taking her eyes off Mirabel, "when I say it does." A pause. Then, to Mirabel: "Why should I trust a smuggler's promise?"

"Because you read my file," Mirabel said. "Ten years dodging debts, and I never once burned a contract I signed. Ask anyone from Graylock to the delta. I'm a liar, a runner, and a coward, Captain, but I honor a bargain, because a man on this river who doesn't honor bargains doesn't eat." He held out his hand, palm open, empty. "That's the bargain. My carrying it down. Your burning the roll."

Another groan overhead, longer, and this time a crack followed it — sharp, percussive, somewhere up beyond the fissure mouth, like a mast parting in a storm. Ice-dust drifted down through the lantern light, glittering, unhurried, beautiful in the way of things that don't care about you.

Halloway looked at his hand. Looked at his face. And Mirabel saw her decide — saw the exact moment the arithmetic in her came out favorable, because she wanted the papers intact more than she wanted them witnessed, because a woman who had climbed from a stilt town to a Survey captaincy knew better than anyone that you sealed a deal at the moment of maximum leverage, and leverage was a perishable cargo.

"Done," she said, and took his hand. Her grip was cold even through the glove. "But you walk in front, Captain. And if you deviate one length from the route I choose—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. The narrow man patted the pistol at his hip with the tenderness of a man touching a favorite child.

* * *

They gathered at the fissure mouth while Halloway's men checked lamps and rope. Sela drifted to Mirabel's side under the cover of adjusting her pack straps, and her voice came low, barely a stir of air:

"What are you doing?"

"Buying us an hour," he murmured, eyes forward. "Maybe two. The face above the source is going to let go — you heard it, same as me. When it does, this canyon becomes a riverbed. Halloway knows it too; that's why she took the deal. She wants to be off the ice before it moves, and she needs me walking point because she thinks I'll lead her straight and true to save my own skin." He paused. "She's not wrong about what I am, Sela. She's only wrong about today."

"And after today?"

"After today," Mirabel said, "the glacier decides. All I have to do is be slow in the right places. Take the long line through the crevasse field. Lose the lamp. Be the invisible man one last time — except this time everybody sees me doing it." He felt something loosen in his chest, something that had been clenched for ten years, and recognized it with quiet astonishment as hope, or its first cousin. "She grew up drowning, Sela. I understand her. I understand her all the way down. But understanding isn't forgiveness, and the valley gets its papers whether she likes it or not."

Overhead, the glacier spoke again — and this time the whole cavern answered, a shudder running through the blue walls, and from far above came a sound none of them mistook: a deep, grinding exhalation, the sound of an enormous weight testing the edge of its patience.

Halloway looked up. For one unguarded instant, Mirabel saw fear move across her face like a shadow of birds — and beneath the fear, faint and quickly buried, something almost like homesickness.

"Move," she said. "We descend now, all of us, before the mountain changes its mind."

Mirabel lifted the satchel, slung it across his chest, and stepped out of the cavern into the falling light — leading them, as promised, down toward the ice, and toward the hour when the price of invisible men would finally come due.
