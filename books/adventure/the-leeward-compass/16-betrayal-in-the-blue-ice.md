## 16. Betrayal in the Blue Ice

The cavern breathed.

That was the first thing Sela had understood about it, standing in the blue half-light with Aldric's body beside her and the satchel open at her feet: the glacier was not silent. It inhaled through the fissure behind them, a long cold draw of air, and exhaled again, and somewhere deep in the ice the sound of water moving under pressure made a note like a struck wire. She had thought she would weep when she found him. She had imagined it for months — the moment of finding, the falling to her knees, the grief arriving like weather. Instead she knelt on ancient ice beside her husband's frozen face and felt almost nothing, because Halloway's boots were ringing on the stone outside, and grief would have to wait its turn.

Aldric looked peaceful. That was the cruelty of it. The cold had kept him the way he'd been on his last morning — beard frosted, eyes closed, one hand resting on the document case as though he'd only stepped away to warm himself and meant to come back. He had stayed. All those months, while she lit lamps in an empty house and told herself he'd run, he had sat down here in the dark beside the proof that would free them all, guarding it with his body because his body was the last lock the Survey couldn't pick.

"You knew," Sela said softly. "You knew they'd come for it. So you became the thing they couldn't move."

Mirabel stood at the fissure mouth, listening. He had gone very still, the way he did on the river when the current changed its mind. "They're close," he said. "Two sets of boots. Maybe three." He glanced back at her, and in the strange light his face was all planes and shadow. "Can you carry the satchel?"

"I've carried worse."

"Then we go now, before—"

Light bloomed at the entrance. Not lantern-light — the hard white stab of a survey lamp, the kind that stripped every shadow from a room and left nothing to hide behind. Sela flinched. Mirabel didn't. He moved between her and the light with the unhurried ease of a man stepping around furniture, and by the time Captain Dree Halloway's silhouette filled the fissure mouth, he was leaning against the cavern wall with his arms crossed, as if he'd been waiting for her all along.

"Captain Nystrom." Halloway's voice echoed off the ice, flat and carrying. She came forward slowly, lamp raised, two men fanning out behind her — Verrick, broad and gray-faced, and the younger one, Tull, whose hand had not left his pistol since the gorge. Her breath smoked. Her eyes went past Mirabel, past Sela, and settled on the open cache, on the ledgers and the recording cylinder and the neat stacks of Aldric's handwriting, and something moved across her face too fast to name. "Well. You found it."

"We found *him*," Sela said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "Your Survey left him here to freeze."

"The Survey leaves nothing," Halloway said. "The Survey *keeps*. There's a difference, widow, and it's the whole architecture of the world." She lowered the lamp until its circle took in the documents, and Sela watched her read the top ledger upside down, lips moving slightly, and watched the color leave her face by degrees. Whatever Aldric had written there, Halloway recognized it. Had perhaps signed some version of it herself.

Mirabel straightened. "You offered me a commission," he said. "Down in the canyon, you offered me my debts burned and a boat with the Survey's colors on it. I'm told your superiors are generous."

Halloway's gaze snapped up. For a heartbeat hope crossed her features — ugly, hungry hope, the kind Sela had seen on creditors' faces — and then it curdled into suspicion. "You're bargaining."

"I'm a riverman. Bargaining is most of what I do." He shrugged. "But I don't deal with subordinates. No offense intended. I want it from whoever signs the rolls. So you'll send your men back down to carry word, and the widow and I will wait here with the documents, and when your principals come—"

"No."

It was not Halloway who said it. It was Tull. The young enforcer had drifted left while the others spoke, circling toward the cache, and now he stood with the lamp-light full on his face, and Sela saw with a lurch that whatever had been holding him together had come apart somewhere on the climb. His eyes were wet. His pistol was level and shaking.

"Tull," Halloway said quietly.

"They changed the order." He didn't look at her. He was looking at the documents, at Sela, at Mirabel, at all of them, the way a man looks at a debt he can't pay. "This morning. Before the ice field. Word came up the line — no witnesses at all. Not the widow. Not the captain. Not—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, Sela could hear him hating it, "—not anyone who's seen the vault. That includes us, Captain. You know it does. You've known since Corren's Ford."

The silence that followed had texture. Sela felt it press against her eardrums like deep water. Beside her, Mirabel had gone from loose to coiled without appearing to move at all, the way a river goes from glass to flood.

"Verrick," Halloway said.

The big man shifted his weight. He was looking at Tull's pistol, and then at Halloway, and Sela understood, with the slow horror of watching a sandbar give way beneath a moored boat, that Verrick was doing arithmetic. Three witnesses. Two guns. One order from above that everyone in the cavern had just learned about, and one captain whose protection had suddenly stopped being worth anything.

"Put it down, Tull," Verrick said. Not unkindly. Which was worse.

"They'll do to us what they did to Aldric's team," Tull said. "Walked into the whiteout, the report says. Walked in together and never walked out. You think that was the mountain?" His laugh was small and terrible. "I read the file, Verrick. I *filed* the file."

And that was when everything happened at once.

Tull fired — not at Mirabel, not at Sela, but upward, into the vaulted ice above the cache, whether to make them all flinch or because his nerve broke in the pulling, Sela never learned. The crack of the shot in the enclosed blue dark was enormous, a physical blow, and the glacier answered it. The wire-note under the floor rose half a tone. Somewhere overhead, a seam let go with a sound like a giant knuckle cracking, and a fine snow of ice-dust sifted down through the lamplight.

"*Out*," Mirabel roared, and the word was not a suggestion; it was the voice he used on the Kestrel when the river turned against them, and Sela's body obeyed it before her mind did. She dropped to her knees, swept the ledgers into the satchel, grabbed the cylinder, felt Aldric's frozen shoulder brush her arm — *forgive me, forgive me, I'm taking it now, love, I'm taking it where you meant it to go* — and slung the strap across her chest as the ceiling shed another groaning sheet of rime.

Chaos wore many shapes after that, and she remembered all of them wrong afterward, in different orders, the way dreams rearrange themselves. Verrick lunging for Tull, the two of them going down in a tangle. Halloway shouting orders that no one obeyed. A second shot, wild, punching a star of white into the wall near Sela's head, close enough that she felt the spatter of ice chips on her cheek. And Mirabel — Mirabel moving through it all like a man in his own house, calm in the way that had frightened her once on the river and reassured her ever since, hauling her toward the fissure mouth by the sleeve.

"The route," he gasped. "The ice bridge over the crevasse — the one we came across — it's been groaning since yesterday. Load it right and it goes."

"What load?"

He looked at her, and there was no apology in his face at all. "Everything we can drop on it. Go!"

They burst out of the fissure into the canyon's raw daylight, and the world was white and shrieking wind and the glacier groaning like a ship in a storm. Behind them, boot-steps — Verrick first, then Halloway, then Tull stumbling after with his pistol still out, all of them fleeing the settling dust of the vault as if the mountain itself had joined one side or the other and none of them knew which.

The ice bridge spanned the crevasse forty paces ahead: a shelf of old blue ice thrown across the gap where the glacier had cracked and healed and cracked again, thick as a wagon road, groaning under its own weight. On the far side, the descent path wound down toward the moraine and, days below that, the valley. It was the only way out. Mirabel had said so himself, twice, on the climb up: *one bridge, one road, and the mountain holds both.*

Now he skidded to a stop ten paces short of it, and Sela nearly ran past him before his hand closed on her arm.

"The satchel," he said. "Give it here."

She clutched it. Instinct, pure animal instinct — the documents were Aldric, were the valley, were three generations of names read aloud at last — and for one black moment she thought he had turned, that this was the betrayal, that the commission had been real—

"Trust me," Mirabel said, "the way you trusted me on the river."

She gave it to him.

What he did then took perhaps twenty seconds and she understood perhaps half of it. He wrenched loose the iron piton they'd hammered into the ice on the ascent, drove it instead into the bridge's upstream lip at the point where the blue ice met older, whiter ice — a seam, she saw, a scar, the place where the glacier had healed badly. From his pack he took the coil of fuse-cord they'd carried for signaling, and the little clay pots of blasting oil Osket Brann had pressed on him at the gorge in lieu of a toll refund, and he packed them against the seam with hands that did not shake, and all the while the others came on behind them, closer, closer, Halloway's voice cutting the wind: "*Nystrom! You walk away from this and I swear on my mother's grave—*"

"Your mother's village is under a reservoir," Mirabel said, without turning around. He struck the fuse against the flare-stone, and it caught with a hiss like an angry goose. "I think she'd understand."

Then he shoved Sela toward the bridge. "Cross. Fast. Don't look at the cord, don't look back, just cross."

She crossed. Forty paces of groaning, singing, trembling ice with a burning fuse behind her and her dead husband's life's work banging against her ribs, and she did not run so much as fly, boots skidding, arms out, the crevasse gaping below her so deep that the light simply gave up partway down and became a color with no name. On the far lip she spun.

Mirabel was still on the near side. Of course he was. He waited until the fuse spat its last inch, and then he did not run for the bridge at all — he threw himself flat behind a ridge of pressure-ice as the charge went off.

The sound was smaller than she expected. A cough, a crack. Then the glacier finished the argument. The seam let go along its whole length with a boom that rolled up the canyon and came back off the walls three times, and the ice bridge — the only road, the one road — folded into the crevasse like a knee giving way, tons upon tons of blue ice sliding down into the dark, and the fissure mouth beyond it, the vault entrance, the whole approach, collapsed in a slow thundering cascade that sealed the mountain's secret behind a wall of fresh rubble.

Screaming started. Not Sela. Tull — Tull had been caught on the bridge when it went, or near enough; he'd leapt and missed, and now he hung from the shattered downstream lip, both hands on the edge, boots kicking over the abyss, howling for help in a voice that bounced off the canyon walls.

Verrick reached the broken edge and dropped to his belly and grabbed for him, and Sela saw the big man strain, teeth bared, boots scrabbling for purchase on the rotten rim — and then the rim itself calved away under him, a slab the size of a door, and Verrick slid backward with it, still holding Tull's wrist, and the two of them went down into the crevasse together. The sound they made lasted longer than she wanted it to. Then the glacier swallowed even the echo.

Which left Halloway.

She stood alone on the near side, on the shrinking island of ice between the collapsed vault approach and the ruined bridge, and she did not scream, and she did not beg. She looked across the widening gap at Sela, at the satchel strap across Sela's chest, and her face in that moment was so nakedly, humanly furious that Sela felt something twist in her chest that she refused to call pity.

"The documents," Halloway said. Her voice carried clean across the gap. "Throw them over. Now, and I'll find another way down, and I'll lose them in the paperwork for a year, and maybe everyone lives. That's the offer. It won't come again."

"There is no other way down," Sela said. "You taught me that yourself, at the gorge. One bridge, one road."

Something flickered in Halloway's eyes — the recognition of her own words coming home, the way debts come home. She took one step nearer the edge, testing the rotten rim, weighing it, and Sela saw her decide. Saw her choose, the way Mirabel had chosen at the source, the way everyone in this story chose at the end between the thing they were and the thing they might be.

Halloway jumped for the far lip.

She was strong. She was desperate. She nearly made it — her fingers actually hooked the ice, and Sela lunged forward on instinct, absurdly, uselessly, one hand outstretched — but the lip was the same rotten rim that had killed Verrick, and it went to powder under her grip. She slid. She caught herself once more, lower, on a spur of blue ice, boots swinging over the depth, and for a long suspended moment she hung there with the wind tearing at her coat and the crevasse breathing its cold breath up around her, and she looked up at Sela Voss with an expression that was not hatred.

It was arithmetic. The same arithmetic Verrick had done. The same arithmetic the whole valley had lived inside for three generations: what a person weighs against what a person costs.

"The satchel," Halloway said, very quietly. "Please."

And Sela — kneeling at the edge, Aldric's documents heavy against her heart, her husband's killer hanging from her fingertips' reach — discovered that she could not answer. Not yes. Not no. The word simply wasn't there.

The spur of ice let go.

Halloway fell the way heavy things fall, without grace and without delay, and the crevasse took her the way the reservoir had taken the village, the way the debt rolls had taken the valley: completely, administratively, without malice. The sound rose up out of the dark once and did not come again.

Then there was only the wind, and the groan of the settling glacier, and Sela on her knees at the edge with her hand still stretched out over nothing.

* * *

Mirabel came across the wreckage of the bridge route carefully, testing each step, and reached her, and crouched beside her, and said nothing for a long time. Below them the crevasse breathed. Above them the sky had begun to bruise toward evening, and far up the canyon, deeper than sight, the glacier made a new sound — a low, gathering, tectonic mutter, like a held breath, like a wave deciding.

"It's coming," Mirabel said quietly. "The great calving. Hours, maybe less. We need to be high on the south wall when it goes."

Sela nodded. She could not make her hand come back from the edge. Mirabel took it gently and folded it closed, and pressed it against the satchel, against the documents, against Aldric.

"She asked," Sela said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "At the end. She said please."

"I know."

"And I couldn't say anything. Any word at all. I just — knelt there."

Mirabel looked out over the crevasse, at the place where the captain had gone, and his face was unreadable and then, briefly, entirely readable, and Sela understood that he was not unmoved — that victory had landed on him the same way it had landed on her, heavy and cold and strangely like loss.

"That's not a failure," he said at last. "That's just what winning costs, up here. Downriver they'll tell it differently. They'll say we beat her." He stood, and pulled her up, and shouldered his pack. "Come on. The mountain isn't finished talking, and I'd rather not be in its mouth when it does."

Behind them, deep in the ice, the held breath went on gathering. Ahead, the descent path fell away toward the moraine, toward the middle river, toward the valley — and the first far-off rumble of the great calving rolled down the canyon like a promise, or a warning, and they began to run.
