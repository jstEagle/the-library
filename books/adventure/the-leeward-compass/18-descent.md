## 18. Descent

The valley had changed its mind about everything.

Mirabel felt it first in the water — or rather, in the absence of it where water should have been. The middle reaches of the Vellwater, which he had read for ten years like a man reading his own palm, were unrecognizable. Channels he could have navigated blindfolded now ran dry, their beds exposed as pale scars of silt and stone. And where there had been dry ground before, water moved — new brown rivers wandering across old fields, finding channels that had been buried for a generation, following paths no chart had ever drawn because no living person remembered them.

They walked because there was nothing left to sail. The Kestrel was somewhere behind them under forty feet of flood-churned meltwater, and the great calving had taken not just the glacier's face but the whole upper canyon, collapsing the route into a chaos of ice blocks and scree that would defeat any climber for a decade at least. Whatever came next would come on foot.

So they walked down, through a valley being reborn in real time, and Mirabel carried the satchel against his chest like a second heartbeat.

He had slung it crosswise over one shoulder at first, the way he'd carried cargo manifests all his life. But by the second hour of descent he had shifted it around to rest flat against his sternum, both straps crossed behind his back, so that he could feel it with every breath — the stiff corners of the document tubes, the weight of Aldric Voss's ledgers, the recording cylinder wrapped in oilcloth riding against his ribs. It was not heavy. It weighed less than a coil of anchor line. But he felt it constantly, the way you feel your own pulse when you've been frightened enough, and he found he did not want to stop feeling it. If he stopped feeling it, he might believe it wasn't real. That the vault, the frozen man, the names in the debt rolls — that all of it had been some oxygen-starved dream of the whiteout, and he was still lying in an ice cave waiting to die.

Sela walked ahead of him, favoring her right foot where the frostbite had nipped her toes. She refused to say how bad it was. She had wrapped them in strips of her own petticoat back in the ice cave and had not complained since, which told Mirabel more than complaint would have. She moved like a woman counting steps, putting her grief down one foot at a time so it couldn't catch up to her. They had left Aldric beneath the ice with the vault opened above him, and neither of them had said a proper word about it yet. There hadn't been time. There still wasn't. The mountain had made sure of that.

Below them, the valley floor spread out in the long afternoon light, and it was wrong in ways that made Mirabel's riverman's eye ache.

* * *

They reached the drowned village at dusk.

Mirabel had passed it twice from the deck of the Kestrel — once upstream-bound, once down — and each time he had kept his eyes on the channel markers and let the stilted ruins slide past at the edge of his vision like something shameful he'd agreed not to look at. From the water, the village had been a skeleton: roof beams standing in the reservoir shallows, window frames empty as eye sockets, the tops of a church spire and a granary breaking the surface like drowned men who had given up struggling. The Survey's dam works had backed the water up over Netherhollow thirty years ago, and the company had paid the villagers in indenture contracts instead of coin, and the valley had learned not to speak of it.

Now the reservoir was draining through the breached dam downstream, and Netherhollow was emerging.

It did not look like a resurrection. It looked like a corpse rising. The water had fallen twenty feet in two days, and what it revealed was black with thirty years of drowned silt — mud to the second-story windowsills, mud in the streets, mud furring every beam. The smell reached them a quarter mile off: rot and river-bottom, the sweet-rotten breath of a place that had been held underwater and was now gasping.

But there were lights in it. That was what stopped Mirabel mid-stride on the levee road. Lanterns moving through the upper stories of the stilted houses — the ones built after the flooding, the shacks the survivors had raised on pilings above their own rooftops, generation stacked on generation like sediment. People were coming out onto the walkways between the houses, leaning on rails, pointing down at the waterline dropping past their ancestors' doors.

An old woman saw them first. She was standing at the end of the main walkway, wrapped in a shawl despite the season, and she watched them approach along the levee with the stillness of a heron. When they drew close enough, she spoke without greeting.

"You came down off the ice."

Not a question. Mirabel stopped a respectful distance away. Behind her, more figures gathered on the walkways — children peering between adults' legs, men with lanterns raised, faces he half-recognized from market days at Corren's Ford, faces that had always looked at him with the flat caution people reserved for boatmen who didn't talk about their business.

"We did," Mirabel said.

"The mountain made a noise this morning," the woman said. "Like the world cracking open. My grandmother heard that noise once, when I was a girl, the day they closed the dam gates. She said when the mountain speaks, the valley changes hands." Her eyes went to the satchel against his chest. She looked at it a long moment. "You're carrying paper."

"I'm carrying proof," Mirabel said. He heard his own voice come out rougher than he intended. Two days of storm and ice and walking had scraped it raw. "Proof of what was done here. To this village. To every family in this valley."

The old woman studied him. Then Sela stepped forward, and the lantern light caught her face, and the old woman's breath caught audibly.

"Voss," she said. "You have his look. You have his look exactly." She put a hand over her mouth. "We heard the survey team went up and never came down. We heard the enforcers asking questions in the spring. We thought—" She stopped. Started again. "Is he—"

"He stayed at the source," Sela said quietly. "He got the proof out. All of it. Every name. Every forged signature. It's going to Corren's Ford, to the market cross, and it will be read aloud until everyone in the valley has heard it."

The silence that followed was the kind Mirabel had only heard twice before in his life — once at his father's funeral, and once the moment before a river took a boat he loved. A silence full of things too large to say.

Then the old woman turned and called out in a voice cracked but carrying, a word in the old valley dialect that Mirabel didn't know, and the walkways erupted. Doors banged open. People came running along the planks, and someone began ringing the church bell — the drowned church bell, hoisted up onto a piling frame years ago, rung only for funerals. Except tonight it rang fast and bright, the way bells ring for weddings and landings and the end of wars.

They were pulled up onto the walkways. Someone pressed a bowl of fish stew into Mirabel's hands. Someone else knelt to look at Sela's foot and clucked over it and brought clean linen and a jar of rendered goose fat. Children touched the satchel's straps with reverent fingers until their mothers pulled them back. An old man — Mirabel recognized him dimly, a stilt-fisherman who sold eels at Graylock Landing — gripped his forearm with both hands and shook it and said nothing at all, just shook it, his eyes wet, his mouth working around words that wouldn't come.

And through all of it, the villagers talked about the flood. Not with fear. With a terrible, satisfied certainty.

"The water's finding the old channels," a young man said, spreading his hands toward the darkening valley where new rivers gleamed in the last light. "My grandfather's orchard is under three feet of moving water right now — clean water, snowmelt, running where the stream ran before they drowned it. You can see the fence lines. The apple trees are standing up out of the mud like they were waiting."

"It's judgment," an older voice said from the shadows, and nobody argued. "The Survey built a wall and said the water belonged to them. The mountain broke the wall and gave it back. That's all the law there ever needed to be."

Mirabel ate his stew and said little. He was watching the faces. He had spent ten years making himself invisible among people like these — keeping his eyes down, keeping his name out of conversations, learning the exact degree of anonymity that kept the debt collectors and the port officials looking past him. He knew what fear looked like on a valley face. This was not it. This was something he had never seen in the Vellwater country in his whole life. It took him a while to name it, sitting there with the stew warming his hands and the satchel pressing its heartbeat against his chest.

It was hope without apology. Hope that expected to be believed.

* * *

They slept that night in a dry room for the first time in nine days — a loft above the eel-fisherman's stilt house, on pallets of woven reed, with the sound of the falling water lapping against the pilings below like a tide going out on a wound. Mirabel lay awake most of it anyway. His body ached in places he hadn't known could ache; his feet had gone soft inside his boots during the ice crossing and now blistered with every step; and every time he closed his eyes he saw Aldric Voss sitting upright in the blue ice, frost on his beard, his hands folded over the cache he had guarded with the last of his warmth.

A man who had stayed. Who had sent his compass downstream with a stranger rather than abandon the documents, and then sat down beside them in the cold and waited for a rescue he must have known, in the end, was not coming — and had guarded the proof anyway, because guarding it was the only part of the work left to him.

Mirabel understood that with a clarity that felt like a knife turning. He had spent a decade running. Running debts, running officials, running the memory of his father signing away their claim with a shaking hand and then walking into the Vellwater with his coat pockets full of stones. He had told himself the running was survival. Lying in the loft, listening to the reservoir drain off the bones of Netherhollow, he admitted what it actually was: it was the same thing Aldric had done, inverted. Aldric had stayed with the truth because leaving it meant it died. Mirabel had run from the truth because staying near it meant he died. Same knowledge, opposite directions.

Tomorrow they would reach the breached dam. After that, Corren's Ford. After that, the market cross, and the reading, and whatever came of it — Halloway's superiors, if any survived her; the Survey's lawyers; the valley's fury. The satchel would stop being his heartbeat and become everyone's. He found he was already grieving that a little, the way you grieve giving up a secret you've carried so long it grew into you.

He slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of clear water running in channels no one had dug.

* * *

The dam announced itself by sound before they saw it.

A steady, colossal rushing, like a waterfall the width of a town, and beneath it a lower groan of masonry under strain. They crested the last levee ridge in the gray of morning and Mirabel stopped walking and simply stood, because his eyes required a moment to accept what they reported.

The Hollis Dam — the Survey's pride, the wall that had drowned Netherhollow, the structure every schoolchild in the valley was taught to call a gift — had a hole in it the size of a chapel. The calving flood had come down the canyon carrying house-sized blocks of ice, and one of them, or several, or the whole roaring mass of them, had struck the dam's central span and torn it open from crest to foundation. Water poured through the breach in a brown cataract, and the reservoir behind it was visibly lower than the maps said it should be, its shrinking margins exposing wide beaches of naked mud where thirty years of drowned fences, foundations, and tree stumps stood up into the air like the ribs of something long swallowed.

And below the dam, the freed water was doing exactly what the young man in Netherhollow had described. It was not spreading wild. It was *looking*. Mirabel watched a secondary current peel away from the main flood, wander across a hayfield, hesitate at a low hedge row — and then find it, an ancient drainage ditch buried under silt, and pour itself into that old channel with the eagerness of a dog coming home. Further down, another stream was reclaiming a millrace. Another was filling a well shaft and overflowing it, running bright and clean across a dooryard where a woman stood on her porch with her hand over her mouth.

The river was remembering. The valley was remembering. Everything the Survey had buried under still water was being handed back.

"They'll call it a disaster," Sela said. Her voice was hoarse. She was staring at the breach with an expression Mirabel couldn't fully read — triumph and desolation braided together, because the water that was freeing her neighbors' land was the same water that had carried away the Kestrel, and the same flood that had sealed Aldric in his tomb of blue ice. "In the capital. In the Survey reports. They'll call it a catastrophe. Loss of critical infrastructure."

"And down here?" Mirabel asked.

She watched a boy wade into the shallows of a resurrected stream, shrieking with joy, hauling out a fish that had no business existing in water that had been mud yesterday.

"Down here they'll call it Tuesday," she said, and laughed — a cracked, exhausted, disbelieving laugh, the first Mirabel had heard from her since the ice, and it did something to his chest that he decided not to examine.

They picked their way along the dam's flank. Work crews were already out — Survey men in their gray uniforms, absurdly official against the ruin, driving stakes and stringing warning lines and arguing over instruments. None of them stopped the two ragged figures walking past. One young clerk looked up, took in the satchel against Mirabel's chest, took in their frost-nipped faces, and went very pale and looked back down at his paperwork with tremendous concentration. Word traveled faster than water, apparently. Or fear did.

By midday they were below the dam entirely, on the old towpath road that followed the Vellwater's original course — the true course, the pre-dam course, which the river was patiently re-excavating beneath their feet. Mirabel walked it like a stranger in his own house. For ten years this stretch had been slack water, deep and featureless, a barge highway as predictable as a corridor. Now it was becoming a river again — riffles forming over gravel bars that hadn't seen current in thirty years, cutbanks slumping, the water braiding and choosing, alive in a way that slack water never was. His professional eye itched to read it, and he realized with a strange pang that he wanted to pilot a boat down this new river someday. Wanted it the way he had never allowed himself to want anything, because wanting was a debt you could default on.

Behind them, Netherhollow's bell was still ringing. Ahead, past the far bend, Corren's Ford lay a day's walk downstream — the market town, the portage, the place where he had lied smoothly to strangers about his business and discovered his name on a list. Somewhere ahead of them, word of Captain Dree Halloway's fate was traveling too, or would soon. The fissure had taken her reaching for the satchel. Her surviving man was entombed behind collapsed ice. But organizations like the Survey did not die with their captains. They sent inquiries. They sent worse.

None of which mattered tonight. Tonight there was a barn offered to them by a farmer whose grandfather's name was in the fabricated rolls, and bread still warm from a shared oven, and Sela asleep almost before she finished eating, her bandaged foot propped on a stool, her face in sleep finally unclenched.

Mirabel sat up late by the banked fire with the satchel in his lap. He unwrapped the recording cylinder from its oilcloth and turned it in the firelight — brass and wax, Aldric Voss's careful engraving on the rim: *For the valley, freely given.* He did not play it. That was for the market cross, for every ear in the valley at once, for the names read aloud until they lost their power to shame. He simply held it, the way you hold a sleeping child, and felt the strangeness of being trusted with something.

His father had owned nothing at the end but stones in his pockets. Mirabel had owned a boat, and the mountain had taken it, and he had discovered on the ice that losing it felt less like theft than like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. What he carried now was heavier and lighter at once. Paper. Names. A dead man's voice waiting inside a brass shell.

Tomorrow, Corren's Ford. Tomorrow, the reading.

He wrapped the cylinder away, cinched the satchel, and laid it against his chest as he lay down, and let its small stubborn weight keep time with his heart until he slept.
