## 19. Reading the Proof Aloud

The road into Corren's Ford smelled of mud and woodsmoke and, beneath it all, of the river — that green mineral breath that clings to everything within a mile of moving water. Sela walked it on feet she no longer trusted, because for eleven days her feet had known only ice and scree, and now the ground was soft and gave too easily, as if it might swallow her out of sheer habit.

Mirabel carried the satchel. He had carried it since the glacier, slung against his chest under his coat like a child, one hand pressed flat over it whenever they passed anyone on the road. She had watched him do it a dozen times without seeming to decide to. It was not protectiveness exactly, or not only. It was the way a man holds the one thing in the world that is his.

Her hands were still wrapped where the frostbite had bitten them, strips of blanket boiled in snowmelt, and so Mirabel had done everything — built the fires, broken the ice on the streams, laced her boots when her fingers failed at the knots. He had never once spoken of what they'd left behind them: Halloway going down into the blue dark with her hand outstretched, the canyon walls folding shut behind them like a book closing, the flood's white roar chasing them up the switchbacks until their ears rang with it for two days after. He talked instead about small things. The quality of the light on the water. Whether gulls meant fish below. How far a man could walk in a day if he stopped counting.

She understood. Some things had to be set down gently or not at all, like hot iron.

But Aldric was in the satchel too. Not his body — she had kissed his cold forehead in that cavern and left him to the mountain he had chosen over her, and she would carry that choice the rest of her life — but his handwriting, his careful surveyor's capitals, his voice caught in the wax of the recording cylinder. Every mile toward Corren's Ford, she felt him growing heavier in that bag, felt him pressing closer to being real again, to being *known*, which was the only resurrection the world offered any of them.

*They'll read it,* she kept telling herself. *They have to read it.*

And then, less often, in the hour before dawn when the fire burned low: *What if they don't? What if they hear it and shrug and go back to their debts, because a debt is a wall and people are afraid to live outside walls?*

She had been afraid of walls her whole married life. She was done with them either way.

* * *

Corren's Ford announced itself by sound before sight: the market bell, cracked and flat, tolling the third hour; the bawl of cattle in the pens; the river sliding past the pilings with its new voice. That last one stopped Sela in the road. The Vellwater had always run brown through the Ford, heavy with the dam's held-back silt, sluggish as something drugged. Now it ran quick and clear, and you could see the stones on the bottom, and children were already crouched along the banks pointing at fish.

The flood had come through here too. Not the killing crest — the gorge and the middle reaches had taken that — but enough water to lift the wharves off their moorings and leave them stacked like spilled matches, to fill the lower market street ankle-deep in fine gray silt that was already drying to powder in the sun. People moved through the wreckage with a strange unhurried energy, salvaging, rebuilding, arguing. Nobody looked defeated. Defeat required believing the thing that beat you would last forever.

At the market cross — a squat stone pillar older than the Survey, older than the town's name, its carvings worn to suggestions — a crowd had gathered. Two hundred people, maybe three hundred, standing in the drying mud, and above them on the cross's stepped base stood a thin man in a clerk's coat with a paper in his hand, reading aloud in a voice pitched to carry.

Sela caught fragments as they pushed through the edge of the crowd.

"...by order of the magistrates sitting in emergency session... all rolls and instruments held by the Land Survey Office of the Vellwater Valley are declared suspect..."

"Who is that?" she asked.

"Proctor Ellery," said an old woman beside her, not turning. "Kept the tithe records thirty years. Honest as winter." A pause. "Was honest before this week, anyway. We'll see."

On the cross, Proctor Ellery turned a page. "—and further, the following instruments are read publicly, in full, per the ancient right of the market cross, that no man may say he did not hear—"

He stopped. Sela saw him look down at the page in his hands, and she saw his shoulders change, the way a man's shoulders change when the weight he has been carrying turns out to be heavier than he thought. When he spoke again his voice had lost its official smoothness.

"These are the debt rolls of the Vellwater Survey," he said. "Compiled over nineteen years. I am going to read the names."

* * *

Later, Sela could never fully reconstruct those hours. Memory broke them into pieces, bright and separate as river glass.

She remembered the first name — Harl Ondek, of the lower terraces, forty acres, debt assessed at three hundred crowns — and the silence that followed it, the whole crowd holding its breath as though the name itself might be dangerous.

She remembered the fourth name, and the fifth, and the tenth, and the murmur beginning to move through the crowd like wind through wheat, because everyone was listening for their own name and finding their neighbors' instead, and each found name was a door opening somewhere.

She remembered Mirabel beside her, absolutely still, his hand flat over the satchel even now, even here, even with the documents already in the proctor's hands — copies, the originals safe in the magistrate's strongroom — as though letting go of it might undo everything.

She remembered the moment the proctor reached the fabricated entries. The cylinder had been played twice already that morning, in the magistrate's hall, before witnesses — Aldric's voice, thin and crackling through the wax, naming the clerks who had invented the assessments, the accounts where the invented money went, the instruction from the Survey's directors that the valley's ancestral claims be "converted to encumbrance" so that the land could pass quietly into company hands within a generation. *Converted to encumbrance.* Aldric had repeated that phrase in his recording with a surveyor's precision, the way he repeated anything that disgusted him, flatly, without heat, letting the words hang themselves.

When the proctor read the fabrication ledger aloud — entry after entry showing debts created from nothing, assigned to families whose grandfathers had held freehold — the crowd made a sound Sela had never heard before. Not a roar. Something lower than a roar, deeper, a sound like the glacier had made in its last hours, a groan of something enormous shifting at last.

"Nystrom," the proctor read. "Mirabel Nystrom. Debt inherited, original instrument dated forty-one years past. Assessment fraudulent per Schedule C. Voided."

Beside her, Mirabel made no sound at all. But Sela looked at him and saw a man struck by something that was not quite pain and not quite relief, standing very straight, jaw tight, eyes fixed on the middle distance as though reading something written there. Forty-one years. His father had been dead thirty-five of them, drowned in the river with the debt still on his back, and there was nothing anyone could give him now but a word read off a stone step in front of strangers.

It was, Sela thought, both nothing and everything. It was the whole world and it came too late, and it was still the whole world.

The names went on. Ondek. Voss — her own name, and she heard it as if from across the river, heard the widow's portion listed and voided, heard Aldric's name read among the dead surveyors with honor, and had to press her wrapped hands against her mouth. Brann, of the Gorge — someone in the crowd laughed shortly at that, and someone else said, "Osket Brann holds land?" and a third voice said, "Osket Brann holds everything, always has," and the laughter spread and broke some of the terrible tension, which was perhaps mercy.

Three hundred and eleven names. It took four hours. The proctor's voice wore hoarse and a young woman took over for a stretch, then a boy of maybe sixteen who stumbled on the legal phrasing but read every name clean. Nobody left. The crowd grew as the day went on — people coming in from the stilted ruins downstream, from the terraces, from the flooded lowlands where the reservoir had dropped twenty feet in a week and was still dropping, uncovering fences and foundations and orchard trees that had stood drowned for a generation, their black branches suddenly leafing out in the sun as if they had only been waiting.

That was the image Sela knew she would keep: the orchards. Dead-drowned trees standing in the returning shallows, and on some of them, impossibly, buds.

* * *

By late afternoon the magistrates came out onto the cross steps themselves — three of them, old men and one hard-faced woman, looking shaken in the way of people who have spent their lives enforcing rules and just learned the rules were weapons. The senior magistrate read the order in a dry voice: all debts arising from Survey instruments were void ab initio, as if they had never existed; all lands held under such encumbrance reverted to the families of original claim; the Survey office was closed and its records sealed pending inquiry.

"The families of this valley," the magistrate finished, and here his voice did shake, slightly, "have held their own land these three days past. As of this hour, the law agrees with them."

Silence. Then the old woman next to Sela — the one who had called the proctor honest-as-winter — began, very quietly, to weep. Not sobbing. Just tears running down a face that stayed perfectly composed, as though they were simply weather, as though she had been waiting sixty years for rain.

Around her the crowd was doing a hundred things at once: shouting, praying, embracing, standing stunned, laughing at nothing, asking each other over and over, *did you hear, did you hear your name, did you hear* — and through it all ran that thread of disbelief, the same thread Sela felt pulled taut through her own chest. *It worked. It can't have worked. It worked.*

Because that was the cruelest trick of a debt, she understood now, standing in the mud with her bandaged hands and her dead husband's name ringing in her ears. Not the money. The money was almost incidental. The trick was that it taught you to expect nothing — that it got inside your wanting and shrank it, year over year, until a free valley seemed as fantastic as a city in the clouds, until freedom itself became a rumor you'd learned not to repeat in front of the children.

Aldric had believed anyway. That was the whole of him, really. Everyone else in the survey team had seen the ledgers and looked away, and Aldric had copied them out in his precise capitals and hidden them in the ice and died guarding them, because he was constitutionally incapable of unseeing a fact.

She missed him so violently in that moment, standing in the celebrating crowd, that the market tilted.

Then Mirabel's arm was under hers, steadying her, saying nothing, and she leaned on it, and the tilt passed.

* * *

Evening came down gold over the river. Fires were lit along the banks — celebration fires, cooking fires, the same flames serving both — and someone brought out a fiddle, and the music was ragged and joyful and slightly drunk.

Sela found Mirabel apart from it, sitting on a bollard at the ruined wharf's end, the satchel finally off his chest and open on his knees. Inside lay a single sheet of paper: the voided instrument bearing his name, which the proctor had pressed into his hands personally, with both hands, like a man returning a stolen thing.

"You could sell that story a hundred times over," Sela said, sitting beside him. "In the cities. They'd pay for the telling alone."

"I know." He turned the paper over, studying the back of it as though it might have more writing there. "I've been trying to feel different since noon. I keep waiting for it to arrive — the feeling. Being free." He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't come like a feeling. It comes like weather. You just notice, after a while, that the pressure's gone off."

"What will you do?"

He was quiet long enough that the fiddle music filled the space between them, and the river made its clear new sound underneath.

"There's talk of the trade reopening," he said at last. "Middle river's passable by spring, they think. Someone will have to run it. Someone who knows the sandbars." He glanced at her, and for the first time since the glacier she saw something in his face that was not grief or wariness or the habit of invisibility. It was almost shy. "There's a hull half-buried in silt down the bank there. Cargo boat, sound frame, flooded to the gunwales. I looked at it an hour ago like a thief."

"And?"

"And I haven't decided whether I'm allowed to want it." He said it plainly, without self-pity, and that plainness was worse than self-pity would have been. "Ten years I wanted nothing I couldn't carry. Wanting gets people found."

"Aldric used to say—" Sela stopped. Started again. "Aldric used to say that a map isn't the territory, but a bad map will kill you faster than no map at all. He said most people are walking around with maps of the world drawn by whoever hurt them first." Her throat tightened. "I think yours needs redrawing, Captain Nystrom."

Something moved through his face at the name — his true name, spoken aloud in public today for the first time in a decade, attached to nothing owed. He looked down at the voided paper and, very carefully, folded it and put it away in the satchel, in the place where the documents had lain against his heart.

"There's a grave upstream of here," he said. "My father's. On the bluff above the old ferry crossing, if the flood didn't take it." He stood, and the evening light caught the gray in his hair, and he looked out at the river running clear and fast and free toward the sea. "Tomorrow I'm going to go show him a piece of paper."

* * *

Sela stayed at the fire long after he'd gone to whatever borrowed corner he slept in. Around her the valley celebrated its own existence, and she let the noise wash over her and thought about disbelief — how it lingered even now, even here, even with the proctor's reading still echoing off the stone. Three hundred and eleven names. Somewhere in the crowd tonight, three hundred and eleven households were lying awake doing arithmetic they'd never dared do before, counting acres that were theirs, and not one of them quite believed it yet. Belief would take a season. Belief would take harvests.

She took out the compass.

She had carried it since Graylock Landing, and it had not left her pocket since the ice. By firelight the brass case glowed dull gold, dented, scarred, the glass clouded where a crevasse had kissed it. She opened the lid and watched the needle swing — and stop — and hold, steady as a plumb line, on true north.

All the way home it had pointed north. Faithful, ordinary, finished. And yet tonight, cupped in her palm under a foreign sky, she could have sworn she felt it tremble — once, faintly, like a sleeper stirring — toward the mountains, toward the white silence where Aldric lay with his work completed around him.

She closed the lid. Held it a moment longer. Then, because tomorrow there would be lawyers and magistrates and the long slow work of a valley learning to own itself, and because some things should not wait for convenient days, she stood and went to find Mirabel Nystrom, to tell him what she had decided the compass was for.

Behind her the fiddle played on, and the river ran clear in the dark, carrying the first free water in a generation down to the sea.
