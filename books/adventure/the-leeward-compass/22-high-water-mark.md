## 22. High Water Mark

The morning fog came off the Vellwater the way it always did in late spring — slow, unhurried, like something that had nowhere else to be and knew it. Mirabel Nystrom stood at the wheel of the *Aldric's Compass* and watched it lift off the water in long gray ribbons, dissolving against a sky already going pale gold at the eastern edge. A year ago he would have been running this stretch alone, engine muffled, lights doused, watching the banks for lanterns that meant trouble. Now the wheelhouse windows were open to the wind, the running lamps burned honest and bright, and his name was painted on the stern in letters a port official could read from fifty yards without squinting.

He still wasn't entirely used to it.

"Captain?" The voice came up through the deck hatch, young and eager and slightly breathless, the way it had been every morning for three weeks. "Sandbar off the starboard bow — I think. Maybe."

Mirabel smiled and didn't answer right away. That was part of the lesson too. He let the boy sweat for the length of ten heartbeats, then leaned out the window and looked.

Tam Oskarsson was sixteen, all elbows and ambition, standing at the rail with one hand shading his eyes and the other gripping the shroud like the river might try to take it from him. He'd come aboard at Corren's Ford with a canvas bag, a borrowed coat two sizes too big, and a hunger Mirabel recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. The boy's father worked the fish weirs below Graylock Landing; his grandfather had held river rights before the Survey rolled over them like a cartwheel. Tam had never seen those papers. He only knew the family had once owned something, because his mother said so on the bad nights, quietly, the way people say things they've stopped believing.

"Look again," Mirabel called down. "What color is the water over it?"

Tam squinted. "Green?"

"Green where?"

"Just — green. All of it."

"Come up here, then. You'll never learn it from the rail."

The boy took the ladder two rungs at a time and crowded into the wheelhouse, dripping river mist, smelling of tar and wet wool. Mirabel shifted left to give him room at the glass and pointed with his chin.

"There. See how it goes dark past the willow snag? That's not depth. That's sand holding water the way a sponge does. And there—" he traced the line where the current split around an invisible shoulder, "—the river tells you what it's hiding, if you watch how it moves instead of what it looks like. A bar reads as smooth water lying about itself. Riffles read as rough water telling the truth. Learn which is which and you'll die old."

"Smooth lies, rough truth," Tam repeated, mouthing it like a prayer he intended to keep.

"That's the whole catechism." Mirabel eased the throttle back a notch. "My father taught me the same words, more or less, though he'd have dressed them up fancier. He liked the sound of his own teaching."

It was the first time in a year he'd said it aloud without the old weight behind it. Tam glanced at him — quick, curious, polite enough not to ask — and Mirabel found he didn't mind the glance. There was a whole country now between himself and the man who had signed away their claim and gone into the brown water owing it. Some mornings the country was wider than others. This morning it felt crossable.

Below them, the middle river unrolled — wide, braided, glittering where the sun broke through the last of the fog. Passenger traffic had come back to these reaches in the autumn after the flood, first as curiosity-seekers wanting to see the breached dam, then as traders, then as families moving upstream to land that was suddenly, astonishingly, theirs. The *Aldric's Compass* ran the route twice a week now: Corren's Ford to Graylock Landing and back, forty souls aboard at capacity, a proper schedule posted at the dock, fares collected openly, receipts written out in Mirabel's own hand. He kept the receipts in a locked box under the wheelhouse bench. Sometimes, alone at night, he opened the box just to look at them — small paper proofs that a man could move through the world leaving a record of himself and nothing bad coming of it.

"Captain," Tam said again, quieter now. "Is it true you used to run this river the other way? Hiding?"

Mirabel considered the question. A year ago he'd have lied, smoothly, out of habit. But habits were like sandbars; the river rearranged them whether you consented or not.

"It's true," he said. "Ten years of it. I could tell you which farmhouses kept dogs and which ones kept grudges. I knew every slough on this water well enough to disappear into it. Best-hidden man on the Vellwater." He turned the wheel a spoke, easing wide of the dark water past the willow snag, and watched Tam mark the correction. "You know what I learned, being invisible that long?"

"No, sir."

"That nobody sees you, nobody feeds you either. Invisibility keeps you alive and nothing else. It's a shelter the size of a coffin." He heard the words come out of him and recognized, faintly, another voice underneath them — Sela's, or maybe his own from a year ago, talking to a woman in a blue uniform on the ice. Power is the only shelter, Dree Halloway had said, and she had believed it with her whole scarred heart, and it had killed her in the end as surely as the fissure had. She'd been wrong, but she hadn't been stupid. That was the hard part to carry. He carried it anyway.

Tam was watching him with the particular patience of the young, who know when a story isn't finished even when the teller has stopped.

"But you're seen now," the boy said. "And you're still here."

"I'm still here," Mirabel agreed. "That's the whole trick of it, Tam. Get seen, and survive it. Everything else is just reading sandbars."

* * *

They made Graylock Landing by midday, riding a rising meltwater current that smelled of snowfields far upstream. The dock had been rebuilt twice since the flood — once by the Survey's labor gangs before the order came down to abandon the works, once by the townspeople themselves, who had salvaged the Survey's own timber and driven the pilings with the Survey's own hammers, and had enjoyed it, by all accounts, enormously. The new pilings were painted river-gray, but someone had carved a high-water line into the third pylon from the end and marked it with a date. Children touched it when they passed, the way pilgrims touch a saint's foot.

Sela was waiting at the head of the ramp.

She came down to the boat most weeks now, ostensibly to check on the cargo manifest, actually because the river had become hers the way it had once been his — a thing to be read, argued with, trusted. She wore her hair back and her husband's old survey coat, brass buttons gone dull with honest wear, and she carried a satchel over one shoulder that Mirabel knew contained copies of everything: the ledgers, the debt rolls, the recording cylinder's transcription, Aldric's final journal. She was teaching herself the law of conveyances from a book she'd bought in Corren's Ford, working through it the way she'd once worked through ice fields — stubbornly, thoroughly, refusing to be turned back by anything.

"Forty-one passengers booked for the return run," she said, stepping aboard and handing him the manifest. "Two of them are going up to see the gorge. A schoolteacher and her sister. They want to know if the walk above the Narrows is safe."

"The walk's safe enough," Mirabel said. "The company's what you'd want to check."

Sela smiled. It was their oldest joke now, worn smooth as river stone. She leaned on the rail beside him while Tam supervised the loading below, shouting instructions with an authority he absolutely had not earned and mostly pulling it off anyway.

"You visited the grave," she said. Not a question. She always knew. He'd stopped wondering how.

"Yesterday. Before we cast off." He looked upstream, toward the bend that hid the landing road. "Took him the voided paper. Read him the number on it — the debt, I mean — and then told him the number was worth nothing now. Less than nothing. Worth remembering, is all." He paused. "I told him about the boy, too. About teaching him the bars. Figured that was the inheritance he'd have wanted to hear about, if he'd ever gotten the chance to want anything."

Sela put her hand briefly on his forearm — a touch light as fog, gone as fast. "He heard it," she said, in the tone she used for things she had decided were true and would brook no argument about. "Both of them did. The dead listen better than the living. They've got nothing else to do."

Down at the ramp, the schoolteacher and her sister came aboard, chattering, pointing at the carved high-water mark. One of them asked Tam what it meant, and Mirabel heard the boy explain it — hesitantly at first, then with growing confidence, the story spilling out of him in the shape he'd absorbed from the adults around him: the dam, the drowned village, the vault, the flood, the names read aloud at the market cross. He got some details wrong. He got the shape of it right. That was how history started, Mirabel thought. Somebody's half-right telling, repeated until the corrections stopped mattering.

He rang the bell, and the *Aldric's Compass* backed clear of the dock and turned her bow upstream.

* * *

The middle river had changed its mind about itself.

Where the reservoir had drowned the valley, the freed water had spent a year finding its old channels, cutting through silt, uncovering fence posts and orchard rows and the foundations of houses that had stood before anyone alive could remember. Stilted ruins still rose from the shallows in places — the village the old woman had shown them, the one that had recognized the compass — but now the stilts stood in running water instead of stagnant pool, and families had rebuilt among them, lashing fresh platforms to the old bones, growing beans in planters along the rails. Smoke rose from cookfires in the evenings. Children dove off the lower platforms into the current and screamed with pleasure. The place had stopped being a wound and started being a neighborhood.

Mirabel slowed as they passed, as he always did, and the passengers lined the rails, and he told the story again — the true version, the careful one, the one with names in it. He named the old woman who had called the compass *the key they couldn't break*. He named the man Halloway's men had beaten at the landing, who had lived, who now ran the ferry across the narrows and laughed too loudly at everything. He named Aldric Voss, surveyor, who had walked into the whiteout carrying proof and stayed there guarding it. He did not name Dree Halloway. Some debts you void publicly; others you settle privately, in silence, at the place where a woman fell reaching for a satchel she thought would buy her back her childhood. He paid that one every time he passed the gorge, and he expected to go on paying it for the rest of his life, and he had decided that was fair.

The gorge itself had softened. Without the dam holding back the silt, the Narrows ran faster but cleaner, the water scouring itself year by year, and Brann's people — Osket had retired to a chair by the fire and a position as something between magistrate and queen-emerita — had strung proper guide ropes along the portage path. Passengers could walk it now. Some did, for the novelty. Most stayed aboard and let the pilot thread the current, holding their breath at the right moments because everyone else did.

It was past the Narrows, in the long quiet reach below the moraine, that Tam asked the question.

They had the wheelhouse to themselves; Sela was forward talking with the schoolteacher, and the afternoon light lay on the water like hammered tin. Ahead, the canyon walls drew together, and beyond them, visible now on clear days from the open water, the glacier field stood white against the sky — smaller than it had been, the great calving face having retreated a full mile up-valley, but still vast, still patient, still keeping what it kept.

"Captain," Tam said. He'd been working up to it for an hour; Mirabel had watched him circle the words like a heron circling a fish. "What's up there? Past the gorge. Really."

Mirabel let the boat find her line through the current before he answered. Then he nodded at the white wall far ahead, gleaming in the sun.

"Something worth the walk," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind there is." He glanced at the boy, and something in his chest went loose and warm and slightly frightening, the way it did whenever he caught himself caring about the future — a sensation he was getting more practice at, but had not yet mastered. "There's a cavern behind where the ice used to stand. There's marks on stone older than any government, older than any debt. There's a man buried in the blue ice who did the bravest thing I ever saw done, and there's a compass needle that finally found its rest. And there's land up there, Tam — real land, surveyed clean, held by families who can point at a paper and say *mine*. You can't get any of it from a boat. That's the thing nobody tells you about owning something. Sooner or later, you have to get out and walk."

Tam looked at the mountains for a long time. The wind moved his hair. When he spoke again his voice had changed — steadied, the way a boy's voice steadies when he catches his first glimpse of the man inside himself.

"Someday I'll walk it," he said. "You'll see."

"I don't doubt it." Mirabel put the boy's hand on the wheel, over his own, and felt the tremor of the rudder come up through the wood like a pulse. "But first you'll learn the bars. Smooth lies, rough truth. Say it."

"Smooth lies, rough truth."

"Again."

"Smooth lies, rough truth!" — louder this time, laughing, and the schoolteacher looked up from the rail at the sound, and Sela too, and Mirabel let the boy hold the wheel alone for a full minute, the *Aldric's Compass* running steady and true up the bright water, while the glacier stood watch ahead and the river, clear as conscience, carried them home.
