## 1. Salt on the Wind

The thief had a three-hour head start and no water worth mentioning, which was why Beatrix Whitlock let him run.

She'd watched him go at dawn from the porch of the waystation at Dry Fork — a lean man in a dust-colored coat, leading off her client's mule with a sack of stolen silverware rattling against its flank like a bad idea. He'd looked back once, seen her standing there in her ranger's coat with her arms crossed, and made the mistake every fool on the flats made: he'd assumed distance meant safety.

Beatrix finished her coffee. She paid for it. She checked the mule's tracks where they came off the hardpan and read them the way other people read letters — shod near hind, favoring the left fore, a stone bruise not yet abscessed. Then she filled her canteens, shouldered her pack, and set out across the white.

The salt flats did not forgive. That was their one virtue, as far as Beatrix was concerned. Out here a man's mistakes followed him in plain sight, printed into the crust behind him like a confession he couldn't stop signing. The sun came up and turned the whole world to hammered tin, glare bouncing off the ground until a person couldn't tell horizon from sky, and the heat climbed into the skull and started making promises. *Rest a while. Sit down in that beautiful shade.* There was no shade. There never was. Men died on the flats sitting down in shade that wasn't there.

She walked at the pace she could hold until dark, and then she held it past dark, because the moon was up and the crust glowed faintly under it, enough to keep the track. Nine miles out she found where the mule had balked at a stretch of thin crust and the thief had whipped it — two raw crescents in the print pattern, spatter of salt kicked sideways. Twelve miles out, a camp: cold fire built from nothing but a handful of greasewood, spent fast, and the marks where a man had lain down too close to it and gotten up again in the night, stiff and sorry. He was drinking his water faster than sense allowed. They always did, the first day, when the fear was still fresh and the flats still felt like a place you could outrun.

Beatrix sat on her pack roll, ate a strip of dried goat meat, and looked north to where the Leeward Range stood up along the edge of the world, white-capped even in summer, holding the light after everything else had given it back. She looked at it the way you look at a scar. Not thinking about it. Just acknowledging it was there, the way you acknowledge weather.

Nine years this autumn.

She chewed, swallowed, and put it away, and slept four hours with the compass in her pocket instead of her pack, as always, though she'd have denied it if anyone asked.

* * *

Dawn found the track turning east, toward the Serevane Escarpment, and Beatrix understood what kind of man she was following. A smart thief would have cut south for the rail spur at Corren's Rest, thirty miles of open walking but water tanks at the end of it. This one was angling for the old brine wells — thinking shade, thinking the ruined pump house there, thinking like a man who'd heard stories about the desert instead of living in it. The wells were forty miles from anything. If he reached them, he'd be drinking brine by tomorrow night and dead by the day after, and she'd have to carry that arithmetic home along with the silverware.

So she stopped being patient.

She ran the track through the morning, boots punching the crust, sweat cutting channels through the salt dust on her face until she tasted the flats themselves. By midday the prints were staggering, long skidding intervals between short desperate ones, and once — just once — the round impression of a man on his knees. At the base of the escarpment's first red buttress, where the salt gave way to gravel and the first stunted tamarisk threw a shadow the size of a dinner table, she found the mule tied with a trembling knot and the sack of silverware abandoned beside it, one fork spilled out on the stones like something dropped in flight.

He hadn't even made it to the well.

She found him twenty minutes later, wedged into a slot in the rock face where the shadow pooled deepest, curled around an empty canteen with his hat over his face. When her shadow crossed him he made a sound — not a word, more a small animal noise — and when she pulled the hat away his eyes were the color of egg yolks, sunk deep, lips cracked black.

"Ranger," he rasped. It might have been a greeting or a plea.

"That's me." She knelt, got an arm under him — he weighed less than her pack — and dragged him out into the light where she could work. Water first, a mouthful, wait, another. His hands shook so badly she had to hold the canteen. "Name."

"Hobbs." Cough. "Josiah Hobbs."

"Well, Josiah Hobbs. You stole a mule, a sack of plated silver, and about six days of your own life, all in one morning. Two of those I can give back." She tipped water into him again. "The third one's yours to keep."

He laughed, or tried to, and it turned into coughing, and when it passed he lay against the rock looking at her with the flat wonder of the nearly dead. "You tracked me," he said. "Across the flats. In this."

"It's what I do."

"For a sack of forks."

"For a debt," Beatrix said. "The forks are how the debt gets paid. It's not the same thing, but out here it's close enough."

Hobbs closed his eyes. She thought he might sleep, and let him have a minute of it while she saw to the mule — good animal, forgiving, already nosing at her jacket for salt. She rubbed its bruised foreleg, checked the hoof, gave it water from her second canteen. When she came back, Hobbs was watching her with an expression she couldn't place at first. Then she placed it. It was the look people got when they were working up to say something they shouldn't.

"You're Whitlock," he said.

"Who told you that?"

"The man at Dry Fork. The one who hired you. He said—" Hobbs stopped, wet his cracked lips. "He said you were the one who came back off the Leeward. The only one."

The words landed somewhere below her ribs, the way they always did, precise as a dropped stone down a well. Beatrix kept her face still. On the flats you learned to keep your face still; the glare punished any expression equally.

"I came back from a lot of places," she said. "Drink your water."

But Hobbs wasn't done. Dying men and fools rarely were, and he was some portion of both. "My brother worked the supply trains," he said. "Out of Corren's Rest, years back. Before the glacier route closed. He used to talk about the expeditions — the big one, nine years ago. Said there was a woman on it carried a compass nobody else could make sense of. Brass case, worn smooth on one side, needle that didn't point north." His yellow eyes fixed on hers, and Beatrix realized her hand had gone, without her permission, to her pocket. To the smooth worn brass resting against her thigh like a second heartbeat. "Said it pointed leeward. Off the ice. Toward something out in the white that wasn't on any map."

The wind moved along the escarpment, hissing through the tamarisk, carrying grit that ticked against the rock like slow applause.

"My sister's name was Wren," Beatrix said quietly. It was not information she gave out. It came out anyway, the way blood comes out of a split lip.

"Wren Whitlock." Hobbs nodded slowly, as if confirming a ledger entry. "That was the name. My brother said when they brought back what was left, the compass came home before she did. Like it knew the way and she—" He stopped himself, too late, and had the grace to look ashamed of it.

*Like it knew the way and she didn't.*

Beatrix stood up. She took the empty canteen, filled it from her own, and set it inside his reach. Then she untied the silverware sack from the mule and looked at it for a long moment — cheap plate, a hotel's monogram, the whole weight of somebody's grievance — and looked at Hobbs, who was watching her do it with the dawning, disbelieving hope of a man watching weather turn.

"You'll die out here if I leave you," she said. "And I'm not in the business of killing men, even stupid ones. So here's how it goes. You walk with me to Corren's Rest. Three days, easy pace, we've got water now that you're not spending it like a drunkard. You give the silverware back, you take whatever the magistrate gives you, and you don't ever steal from anyone feeding a debt again. You understand me?"

"Yes," Hobbs said. "Ma'am. Yes."

"And you don't talk about my sister again. Not to me. Not to anyone."

"No, ma'am."

They started down off the escarpment in the long gold light of late afternoon, the mule plodding, Hobbs leaning on Beatrix's shoulder for the first mile until his legs remembered their trade. The flats opened ahead of them, vast and featureless and shining, going violet at the edges as the sun went down, and Beatrix felt the familiar thing settle over her — the thing that wasn't peace and wasn't purpose, just the flat fact of forward motion. Another thief walked home. Another ledger line closed. Somewhere a hotel would get its forks back and a magistrate would get his afternoon's entertainment and the world would turn exactly as it had turned yesterday, and none of it — not one grain of all this salt — added up to anything at all.

She'd been telling herself for nine years that it would. That if she just kept paying debts, kept bringing things home, kept walking people out of places that wanted them dead, the arithmetic would eventually resolve. That the sum would come due and mean something.

It didn't. It just kept being arithmetic.

* * *

They made Corren's Rest on the third evening, coming down off the last rise as the town's lights pricked on one by one, oil lamps and cook fires strung along the single street like beads on a wire. The rail spur ended here. Beyond it there was nothing but scrubland climbing for two hundred miles toward the green foothills and then the white, and most nights the wind came down off that direction smelling faintly of cold, a smell Beatrix felt in her teeth before she ever smelled it properly.

Hobbs gave up the silverware without drama. The hotel keeper, a wide woman named Prue who'd been cheated twice before by better men, looked at the recovered forks, looked at Hobbs's blistered face, and pronounced judgment with a shrug: he'd wash dishes for a month and consider himself lucky. The magistrate didn't even come out of his office. In Corren's Rest, justice was mostly a matter of arithmetic too, and the sums had balanced.

Beatrix collected her fee from the factor who'd posted the bounty — forty dollars, minus the twenty he tried to shave off for the mule's strained leg, argued back up to thirty-five — and ate a bowl of stew at Prue's counter while Hobbs washed dishes in the back with the humility of the recently resurrected.

"You look tired, ranger," Prue said, refilling her coffee unasked.

"I'm always tired."

"No." Prue set the pot down and studied her with the frankness of women who fed towns. "Tired is tired. You've got the other thing. The looking-past thing." She nodded toward the window, north, where the last light was dying on the distant smudge of the ranges. "How many years is it now?"

Beatrix put money on the counter rather than answer, and Prue took it, and let it go, the way wise people did.

Her room above the assay office was narrow and clean and faced north, which she'd chosen years ago for reasons she no longer examined. She lit the lamp, hung her coat, and sat on the bed with the ledger she kept in the bottom of her pack — an actual book, cloth-bound, columns ruled in faded ink. Debts owed to her. Debts she owed. Favors spent and favors banked. Thirty-five dollars entered under the factor's name. A line struck through Hobbs, marked *paid in kind*. Page after page of it, nine years of it, the whole architecture of a life built out of other people's obligations.

At the top of the current page, in ink older than everything beneath it, a single entry she had never struck through and never would:

*Wren — everything.*

The compass came out of her pocket. It always did, at night, though she told herself it was habit, maintenance, nothing more. Brass case worn mirror-smooth on the left side where a thumb had worried it for years — Wren's thumb, before. Beatrix's thumb, after. The lid opened on a hinge she'd oiled a thousand times. Inside, the card and the glass and the needle, and the needle doing what it always did: ignoring north entirely, swinging slow and certain toward the northwest, toward the high white mass of the Leeward Glacier fifty leagues distant, pointing leeward, off the ice, toward something that wasn't on any map.

Every instrument maker in three territories had looked at it. Magnetized by the ore in the case, one said. Broken, said another, and offered to fix it, and Beatrix had taken it back out of his hands before he touched it. Only Wren had known what it was, and Wren had died on the ninth of Thaw-month, nine years ago, along with eleven others, on an expedition whose official report ran four pages and explained nothing — instruments lost, route sealed by calving, survivors: one.

One survivor. Cassian Voss, who had led them out and then led no one anywhere ever again, who had vanished from the territory for six years and come back gray and silent and rich enough to be mysterious about it.

Beatrix closed the compass. Her thumb found the worn spot on the case, the smooth brass hollow, and rested there.

Tomorrow she'd check the notice board, maybe take a contract south, something with water in it. Something far from the smell of cold on the wind. She was reaching for the lamp when she heard footsteps on the stairs — the assay clerk's tread was heavier, these were light, quick, and they stopped at her door, and a folded paper slid underneath onto the floorboards.

She was up with her knife out before the footsteps reached the third stair, but the hallway was empty. No voice. No door closing. Just the building ticking around her and the paper lying pale in the lamplight.

She picked it up with two fingers, the way she handled things she didn't trust.

No letter. No signature. A single sheet, folded once, and inside it — pressed flat, drawn in fine sure lines she recognized before she understood why — a map. The Leeward Glacier, the approach corridor, the crevasse fields rendered in careful hatching, the calving face marked with dates. Someone who knew the route. Someone who'd walked it.

Someone who drew crevasse fields the same way Wren had drawn crevasse fields.

In the corner, in a hand she did not know, a single date. The anniversary. Nine years to the day since the mountain took her sister.

And beneath the date, three words:

*The debt stands.*
