## 2. The Debt Ledger

The office above the livery smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of horse — a smell that had soaked into the walls so long ago it had become part of the building, like the water stains on the ceiling or the grooves worn into the floorboards by thirty years of boots. Beatrix Whitlock sat at the desk with the ledger open before her and the window cracked to let in the evening air, which came off the salt flats carrying the last heat of the day and the smell of brine and creosote bush.

She had owned this room for six years. She had never quite made it hers. The desk was someone else's design, heavy oak with brass corners that had gone green at the edges; the chair had been built for a man broader through the shoulders; even the ledger itself, with its ruled columns and its stiff leather spine, seemed to have opinions about how a life should be organized that hers refused to follow.

But she kept the ledger anyway. That was the discipline her father had left her, along with the name and the scarred hands: *a ranger who doesn't know what she's owed is a ranger who can be bought.* He'd meant it as a warning against other people. He had not anticipated that the person most likely to sell her out would be herself.

Beatrix dipped her pen and began.

*Owed to me.* The column ran down the page in her tight, slanted hand. Fourteen dollars from Hask Grier for the two days she'd spent tracking his runaway mule team across the flats — he'd paid six and promised the rest "when the well money comes in," which was the same thing he'd said in spring. Thirty dollars from the town council for the survey of the north fence line, held up because Alderman Pruett wanted the report amended to say the fence was in better shape than it was. Eleven dollars from the widow Sarn for the night Beatrix had sat up with her boy when the fever came, which the widow insisted on treating as charity and Beatrix insisted on treating as debt, because charity was a thing you gave away and a debt was a thing that tied two people together, and Beatrix Whitlock had very few ties left and guarded the ones she had.

Then the second column. *Owed by me.*

Doc Halloran, forty dollars, for the winter she'd come back from the flats with frostbite in three fingers and no money to speak of. The liveryman below, two months' rent, deferred on the understanding that she'd take it out in work — she'd re-shingled his south roofline in April and they both pretended that covered more than it did. And then the favors, which didn't go in columns but lived in her head anyway: the sheriff owed her for looking the other way on the Grier mule matter; the telegraph operator owed her for the night she'd carried his drunk brother home off the boardwalk; half the town owed her small kindnesses that she had never once called in, because calling them in would mean admitting she needed them.

At the bottom of the page, in the space where a careful accountant might write *balance*, Beatrix wrote nothing. There was no balance. There was only the slow arithmetic of a life that stayed exactly even — never ahead, never behind, never going anywhere.

She set the pen down and rubbed her eyes.

Through the window, Corren's Rest was settling into its evening. Lamplighters working the boardwalk. The saloon's piano starting up, badly, the same three songs it always played. A freight wagon creaking past on the far road, headed nowhere she'd ever want to go. It was a good town, Corren's Rest. Dry, honest, too stubborn to die even when the railroad passed it by eight miles to the south. People here left their doors unlocked and their debts recorded. It was the kind of place a person could spend a whole life in without anyone noticing she'd stopped moving.

Nine years ago tonight — no. Not tonight. In eleven days. Beatrix knew the date the way she knew the date of her own birth, without consulting anything, the number just *there*, carved somewhere below thought. Nine years since the Leeward Glacier took Wren. Nine years since the telegram came to this same desk, back when the desk belonged to the man she'd bought it from, and a stranger's voice read her the words: *Whitlock, W. Lost with party of six, icefall section, recovery not possible.*

*Recovery not possible.* As if Wren were a dropped tool. As if the mountain had simply misplaced her and couldn't be bothered to look.

Eleven days. Every year the anniversary arrived the same way the calving season did — announced well in advance, inevitable, and still somehow a surprise when it finally broke over her. For a week beforehand she'd find herself short-tempered, restless, taking tracking jobs she didn't need just to be outdoors, sleeping badly, waking at odd hours with her hand already closed around the compass on the cord at her throat before she was fully awake enough to know why.

The compass. She drew it out now, out of habit, out of the small compulsion that governed her the way prayer governed other people. Brass case worn smooth at the edges, the lid hinge loose, the needle cage clouded with age. On the inside of the lid, scratched in a girl's careful letters: *W.W. — for finding your way back.*

Wren had been the one who was supposed to stay found. That was the joke of it, the joke nobody laughed at anymore: Beatrix was the tracker, the ranger, the one who could follow a broken blade of grass across four miles of hardpan. And Wren — Wren who got lost going to market, who once spent an afternoon circling the same hilltop because every direction looked the same to her — Wren was the one who'd walked onto a glacier and never walked off.

Except she hadn't gotten lost. That was the thing Beatrix had turned over nine thousand times in nine years. The Leeward expedition had been mapped, provisioned, led by men with names that had been in newspapers. They hadn't wandered. Something had happened to them — something the official report called *crevasse fall, party separated in whiteout* in language so bland it felt like a lie told politely. And Wren, who couldn't navigate her way out of a church social, had been carrying Beatrix's gift to her: the compass that was supposed to bring her home.

It had come back instead. Mailed from a supply depot at the glacier's foot, eight months after the loss, wrapped in oilcloth, no note. Just the compass, and the scratch of her sister's handwriting inside the lid, and the needle pointing — always, faithfully, uselessly — leeward. Toward the ice. Toward whatever was out there that had taken her.

Beatrix closed the lid. The click of it was very loud in the quiet room.

"Evening, Miss Whitlock." The voice came up the outside stair, followed by boots on the boards, and then the liveryman's gray head appeared around the doorframe. Cobb. Seventy if he was a day, hands like braided rope. "Telegraph fella said this came for you on the afternoon wire. Didn't want to leave it in the box, on account of it being registered."

He held out an envelope the way a man holds out something that might bite.

Registered mail was for banks and lawyers and death notices. Beatrix took it. "Thanks, Cobb."

"You all right? You look like you seen a ghost."

"I'm fine."

He lingered a moment longer than politeness required, then went back down the stairs, and she heard him pause at the bottom, considering whether to say something else, deciding against it. The stair door swung shut.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, expensive. No return address. Her name written across the front in a hand she didn't recognize — precise, upright, the letters formed with a draftsman's care, each one the same height as its neighbor. Not Voss. She knew Cassian Voss's hand the way she knew a scar; seven years ago he'd written her a letter that had ended her career in the territorial survey, and she could still reproduce every word of it from memory. This was not that hand. This was not any hand she knew.

She weighed it in her palm. Heavy. More than paper inside.

She opened it with her belt knife, carefully, because whoever wrote like that would notice a torn envelope.

Inside: a single sheet of cream paper, folded once. And between the folds, a map.

Not a printed map. A drawn one. Ink on drafting linen, the linework fine and confident, hachures shading the contours of a valley she knew better than she knew her own face — the approach corridor to the Leeward Glacier, the moraine fields, the serac bands marked in the cartographer's shorthand of the trade. Someone had drawn this who had stood on that ground. Someone who knew the ice.

And there were additions. In red ink, laid over the black base drawing: a route. Not the standard approach — the southern route, the safe one, the one every guidebook ran — but a line threading up through the eastern icefall, the broken country, the maze of crevasses and collapsing seracs that no sane expedition entered after midsummer. The red line climbed the icefall, crossed the high shoulder, and terminated at a point on the upper glacier marked with a small, deliberate X.

Beside the X, in the same draftsman's hand, a single notation: *Cache.*

Below the map, on the sheet of paper, there was no signature. No greeting. No explanation. Only a date, written in the same upright script:

*The 14th.*

Eleven days from now. The anniversary.

Beatrix sat very still. The lamp hissed. Downstairs, Cobb's horses shifted in their stalls, and the piano in the saloon ground through its repertoire, and none of it touched her. She was reading the map again, and again, the way she'd read the telegram nine years ago — hunting the words for what wasn't written in them.

A cache. On the Leeward Glacier. At the head of the eastern icefall, in country so broken that the survey maps marked it with the old warning glyph — the crossed pickets — meaning *no route, do not attempt*. And a date: the 14th. The day the ice on the Leeward began to move. The day the calving season opened and sealed every approach until autumn. Whoever sent this was telling her: *come now, or not at all.*

And they were telling her on the anniversary. Which meant they knew the date. Which meant they knew about Wren.

Which meant they knew her.

Beatrix set the map flat on the desk and smoothed it with both hands, the way she'd smooth a pelt, reading it the way other people read faces. The base drawing was old — the linen had yellowed, and the contour style was twenty years out of date, the kind of work done before the territorial survey standardized its methods. Survey vintage. Someone had drawn this on the Leeward itself, or copied it from drawings made there.

On the Leeward. Twenty years ago or more.

Her eyes went to the lower margin, where draftsmen signed their work, where the survey stamped its seal. The corner had been cut away. Cut cleanly, with a blade, removing whatever name or mark had been there — and taking with it a strip of the border decoration, so the cut couldn't be disguised as damage. Someone had wanted the authorship destroyed and had wanted her to know it had been destroyed deliberately.

A message inside a message. *I know this ground. I won't tell you how.*

Beatrix leaned back. Outside, full dark had come down, and the lamplighter's flames made small orange islands along the boardwalk. She became aware that her heart was going hard and steady, the way it did before a shot or a storm, and that some part of her — the part that had been awake for nine years without knowing it — was standing at attention.

Because here was the truth under the dread, and she examined it honestly, the way she examined everything: she was not afraid of the map. She was afraid of how much she wanted it to mean something. Eleven days before the anniversary, a route drawn to a cache on the glacier that killed her sister, delivered by an unknown hand — if she were a sensible woman she'd feed it to the stove and pour herself a drink and let the 14th pass the way the last eight had passed, alone, awake, listening to the wind come off the flats.

Instead she caught herself doing what she always did. Reading the ground. The red route through the icefall wasn't guesswork — it threaded the serac bands with the confidence of someone who'd been through them, more than once, recently. The X sat on the upper glacier at a spot that matched nothing in the published surveys, which meant the sender knew something the maps didn't. And the date — the date was either cruelty or urgency, and Beatrix had spent enough years around liars to know that cruelty wasted effort, while urgency spent it freely.

Someone wanted her on that ice on the 14th. Someone who knew the glacier, knew the calendar, and knew exactly which wound to press.

There were perhaps five people alive who fit that description, and she could name them. One of them was dead. One of them was herself. And one of them —

One of them was Cassian Voss, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, the way thoughts about Voss always did. Seven years since the survey scandal. Seven years since the letter that had named her as the source of falsified field data — her data, her instruments, her name dragged through the hearing while Voss sat composed at the witness table with his neat beard and his neat testimony and watched her career dismantled piece by piece. She'd thrown a drink in his face at the hotel bar afterward, and he had not flinched, and he had not explained, and she had hated him with a cleanliness and simplicity that had become, over the years, one of the few fixed points of her inner life.

Voss knew the Leeward. Voss had been on the first relief expedition that reached the icefall after Wren's party vanished — she'd learned that much later, pieced together from old rosters, and had never asked him why he'd never mentioned it. Voss wrote letters in a hand nothing like the one on this envelope. But Voss also had the means to hire someone whose hand was nothing like his own.

Or it was none of them. Or it was a crank. Or it was a trap — bait laid for a ranger with a reputation, luring her onto broken ice in calving season to die in country where no one would ever look.

Beatrix looked at the stove. Then at the map.

Then she did neither. She folded the map along its original creases, put it back in its envelope, and locked it in the drawer of the desk where the ledger lived — the drawer with the false bottom, the one that held everything she couldn't file and couldn't burn. The telegram copy from nine years ago. The newspaper clippings. The inventory of Wren's recovered effects, all four lines of it. And now this.

She banked the lamp low and sat a while longer in the dark, the compass warm against her sternum, listening to the wind come up off the salt flats and thinking about the 14th.

Eleven days. Enough time to make inquiries. Enough time to learn who'd sent a registered letter through the Corren's Rest wire office — registered mail left a trail, and trails were the one thing in this world Beatrix Whitlock had never once failed to follow.

Enough time, also, for whoever it was to learn that she'd received it. If they were watching.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the piano downstairs finally stopped, and the town's sounds thinned to wind and harness-chain, and Beatrix sat with her hand closed around the compass, the needle inside pointing its patient, faithful way toward leeward — toward the ice, toward the X on the map locked three feet from her elbow, toward whatever had been waiting out there for nine years with her sister's name on it.

The 14th, she thought. Somebody knows something.

She'd be damned if she'd let the glacier keep it.
