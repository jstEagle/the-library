## 3. An Old Rival's Terms

The letter had no signature, but the map had a smell, and the smell was the Leeward.

Beatrix knew it the way a dog knows a storm coming — something in the paper itself, the cheap waxed stock that survey crews used because it didn't rot when the melt came through. She'd spent nine years trying to forget that smell. Nine years, and the first thing she'd done this morning, sitting in the back room of the Corren's Rest assay office with her ledger open and her tea going cold, was press the map flat against the desk and breathe it in like a woman leaning over a well.

The date on the map was circled in red. Twenty-two days out. Calving season opened thirty-one days after that, if the mountain kept its usual temper, and the mountain rarely did anyone favors.

She'd sat with it for two days. She'd told herself she was deciding.

She was not deciding. She was waiting to see how much of her pride she could afford to lose.

* * *

The assay office was the only building in Corren's Rest with a lock worth picking, which was why the town's one honest banker, a widow named Halloran, let Beatrix keep her accounts there in exchange for walking her home after dark. The back room smelled of ink and silver dust and, faintly, of the desert that came through the door every time someone opened it — the fine white powder that got into everything in this town, that got into your teeth, your ledger, your grief.

Beatrix ran her thumb down the column of figures she'd written the night before. It was a short column. It was always a short column now.

Owed to Halloran: forty-two crowns, interest accruing at a rate she'd negotiated down twice and couldn't negotiate down again. Owed to the farrier for the mule's shoes: six. Owed to the widow Prentiss for the winter's coal, carried over from last winter because Beatrix had spent the coal money replacing the compass's glass face when a rock chip cracked it on the Saltline survey — a repair she'd have sworn to anyone was necessary and knew, privately, was a form of prayer.

Favors spent: all of them. That was the column that hurt. Old Serrano at the trading post, who'd fronted her rope and pitons on a promise two seasons back. The doctor who'd stitched her arm on the Kestrel job and taken payment in a story she'd never told anyone else. You could only spend a friend's patience so many times before the account closed, and Beatrix had been living for three years on the interest of favors already spent, the way a man lives on the fat he laid down in a better season.

Nine days ago she'd chased a boy across the salt flats for a stolen saddlebag and let him go because he'd mentioned a compass like hers. She'd told herself it meant nothing. She'd told herself a lot of things meant nothing lately. That was what a small life did to you — it taught you to call the hunger by other names. Discipline. Solitude. Keeping faith. She was forty-one years old and the best tracker in three territories and she was owed forty-two crowns to a widow who'd never once pressed her for it, which was somehow worse than being pressed.

And in nine days — in eight, now — it would be the anniversary. Nine years since Wren went up the Leeward Glacier with fourteen other people and came back as a name on a stone marker at the trailhead, a marker Beatrix had visited exactly once and never again, because the marker said *lost to the mountain* and Beatrix had never believed the mountain was the whole of it.

She closed the ledger. The map lay beside it, red circle staring up at her like an eye that wouldn't blink.

"Whoever sent that," Halloran said from the doorway, "knows you better than I'd like."

"Everyone who knows me knows I'm for sale cheap this year."

"Everyone who knows you knows that's a lie." The old woman came in and set a fresh cup of tea on the desk, unasked, the way she'd been doing for six years. "You're not cheap, Beatrix. You're just broke. There's a difference, and the man who sent that map had better know it."

* * *

Cassian Voss came to Corren's Rest on the evening mail coach, which was the least dramatic entrance available to a man of his reputation, and Beatrix suspected he'd chosen it deliberately.

She watched him climb down into the street's amber lamplight and felt something in her chest go tight and strange. He was gray. That was the first thing. Not silver at the temples the way distinguished men went gray — gray all through, the color leached out of him like a photograph left in the sun. The Cassian Voss who had stood up at the Guild tribunal eleven years ago and dismantled her survey work with a smile had been broad and ruddy and certain, a man built like a door. This one was narrower. The certainty was still there, but it sat differently on him, the way a coat sits on a man who's lost weight. His hands, when he took off his gloves, were the hands of someone who'd been cold for a long time and would be cold again.

He found her at the Saltline, because of course he did. She'd gone out to the edge of town to think, standing where the hardpan gave way to the first white shimmer, and she heard his boots on the crust behind her with a full minute's warning and let him come anyway.

"Whitlock."

"Voss." She didn't turn. "You've got nerve, mailing me a map with no name on it."

"You'd have burned a letter with my name on it."

"You'd have deserved it."

He came around to stand beside her, close enough to talk, far enough not to be a threat, and she gave him that much. The sun was going down behind them and the salt was turning rose-gold, the whole flat lit up like the inside of a shell, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because the salt at dusk did that to people. Even enemies.

"You look well," he said.

"Liar."

"You look like a woman who's been eating," he amended. "That's more than I expected. I heard the Kestrel survey went badly."

"The Kestrel survey went fine. The Kestrel *payment* went badly." She turned to look at him then, and let him have the full weight of her attention, which she knew he understood was a currency too. "Say what you came to say. The coach back leaves at dawn and I'd like to be rid of you before it does."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive the journey to his mouth. "I need a guide. Leeward Glacier, up the west corridor to the high shelf, and back. Out and home inside the calving window. I have a crew — four, plus me. What I don't have is anyone who knows the route, and the route is the difference between an expedition and a burial party."

"No."

"You haven't asked what it pays."

"I haven't asked what you're retrieving, either, and I notice you haven't said." She crossed her arms. "Instruments, your letter said. Scientific instruments left on the shelf by a survey that never happened, because no survey has touched the Leeward in nine years, because the route's been sealed since — " She stopped. He watched her stop. "Since Wren's expedition. Which you know. Which everyone knows. So either you're retrieving something that isn't instruments, or you're retrieving instruments that someone left up there who had no business being there, and either way, Voss, you're lying to me in the first five minutes, which is a record even for you."

The wind came off the flats and moved the salt shimmer around them like breath on glass. Voss looked out at it for a long time.

"I'll pay two hundred crowns," he said. "Half on departure, half on return to Corren's Rest. Plus equipment, plus your debts settled if anything goes wrong — all of them, whatever's outstanding, whatever you owe and to whom. I know about the ledger, Beatrix. I know about the coal and the rope and the doctor."

The number landed in her chest like a dropped stone. Two hundred. It was more than she'd cleared in the last three years together. It was the coal and the rope and the doctor and Halloran's forty-two crowns and a winter after that with the stove lit and the ledger closed and no one in this town owed anything to her or her to anyone, and she hated him for knowing it, and hated herself more for the way her body had answered before her pride could vote.

"That's not an answer to my question," she managed.

"No. It isn't."

"What are you retrieving, Voss?"

"Equipment." He said it flat, and she heard the seam in it, the little catch where the word had been rehearsed. "Survey equipment. It was left up there by people who shouldn't have been up there, and it needs to come down before the melt season opens the crevasse fields and it's gone into the ice for good."

"Whose equipment?"

"Beatrix."

"Whose. Equipment."

He turned to face her fully then, and this close, in the last of the light, she saw what the gray had done to his eyes. They'd gone pale. Not blind-pale — winter-pale, the pale of a man who'd spent years looking at snow and had some of it stay in him. There was something underneath the pale that she'd never seen in him before, and it took her a moment to name it, because it was so foreign to the man who'd ruined her.

It was fear.

"You want the truth of it," he said quietly, "and I understand why. You've earned the truth of it more than anyone alive. But I'm asking you to take this contract on the terms I can give, and the terms are these: I pay you to guide my crew up the west corridor and back down before the calving. What we do at the top is not your concern, and I will not lie to you about anything that touches your safety or your sister's name. That's the whole of what I can offer, and it's more than I've offered any living soul in nine years."

"Your crew knows?"

"My crew knows what they need to know to do their work."

"And if I say no?"

He looked out at the salt. The rose-gold had gone to violet, and the first stars were out over the hardpan, hard and white and far away.

"Then I hire someone worse," he said. "And people die, and whatever I'm trying to finish stays unfinished, and I'm too old and too tired to pretend that outcome doesn't matter to me. So I came to the best guide in three territories and I put two hundred crowns on the table and I asked her to trust me one more time, knowing what I did to her the last time she did."

* * *

They walked back to town without speaking. That was its own kind of conversation, the two of them on the darkening road, boots on the crust, the town's lamps growing ahead of them one by one. Beatrix used the time the way she used all silence — to work.

Eleven years ago, Cassian Voss had stood in front of the Guild tribunal and read from her own field notes in a voice like warm honey, and by the time he finished, the notes that proved the Kestrel pass was sound read like a forgery, and Beatrix Whitlock read like either a fool or a fraud, and the Guild had chosen fool, because fool could be forgiven and fraud had to be struck from the rolls. She'd never learned why he did it. She'd had theories for a decade — ambition, a rival's bribe, simple cruelty — and she'd retired every one of them in turn, because none of them fit the man she'd known before that day, and she'd never been able to make the after-Voss and the before-Voss into one person no matter how she turned them.

The man walking beside her now fit neither theory. The man beside her was afraid of something on a glacier, and had come to the one person with the best reason on earth to refuse him, and had offered her enough money to buy back her life, and had refused — *refused* — to lie to her about her sister.

That was the part that kept catching in her like a fishhook. He'd said *your sister's name* like a man handling something with an edge. He'd said *nine years* without her prompting, without hesitation, as if the number lived in him the same place it lived in her.

"Voss," she said, at the edge of the lamplight, where the town began. "Were you on the Leeward? Before? Ever?"

He stopped walking. For three full seconds he stood in the dark between lamps, and she watched him decide something, and she watched him decide against it.

"Ask me again when we're off the ice," he said. "If you still want to know."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat at her kitchen table with the unsigned letter and the map and the ledger, three documents of her life arranged in a row, and she did the arithmetic that pride hates.

Pride said: *He ruined you. He stood in a warm room and ended your career with your own words and never said why, and now he wants your legs and your ice-sense and your name on his liability waiver, and you should spit in his tea and go back to chasing saddle thieves across the salt.*

Need said: *Forty-two crowns. Six more to the farrier. Coal. Rope you can't replace. A winter coming, and the winter after that, and a body that's forty-one and won't be forty-one forever, and no other offer on any table anywhere, and there won't be one.*

And underneath both, quiet, in the voice she tried never to listen to: *He said her name. He said nine years. He knows something about the glacier that you've spent nine years not knowing, and the only door to it is through him.*

She thought about Wren. Not the memorial version — the real one, the girl who'd chewed her thumbnail when she read, who'd named her gear after weather, who'd stood in this same kitchen at nineteen and said, *Bea, when I get back, I'm going to buy you out of this town and we'll see the coast.* The compass had come back in the personal effects, needle bent two degrees off true, and Beatrix had carried it on every survey since, and it had never once pointed anywhere she wanted to go.

The candle burned down. The salt wind worried at the shutter. And somewhere in the small hours, without ever quite deciding, Beatrix Whitlock found that she had decided — the way you find you've been walking a long time before you notice the road.

She wrote the terms in her ledger in her careful surveyor's hand, because if it went in the ledger it was real, and she was a woman who trusted paper more than people:

*Voss expedition. Leeward, west corridor. Two hundred crowns. Terms: my route, my say-so on the ice, no lies touching the crew's safety. What he's retrieving — unknown. What he knows about Wren — unknown.*

*Accepted.*

She looked at the word a long time.

* * *

She found him at dawn at the coach yard, already waiting, a single travel case at his feet, as if he'd known — as if he'd known the way he seemed to know everything about her debts and her ledger and her life, which was a thought she set aside to examine later, on the ice, with a rope on.

"Terms," she said, without greeting. "My route, my word is law above the snowline, and the moment I judge the corridor dying, we turn around — calving window be damned, contract be damned, and you still pay in full. And one more."

"Name it."

"When this is done — whatever you're retrieving, whatever it is — you tell me what happened up there nine years ago. All of it. Not for the Guild, not for a tribunal. For me. That's not a clause, Voss. That's the price, and the two hundred is the *discount*."

The coach horn sounded down the valley, a long low note off the hardpan. Voss looked at her for a moment, and the fear was still in him, but there was something else now, something that looked almost like relief, and that frightened her more than the fear had.

"Agreed," he said. "We leave in three days. There's a woman in Salt Landing — a cartographer, Odile Marchetti. She'll have the crew list. And Beatrix — " he picked up his case, and paused, and did not quite look at her — "pack for cold that doesn't quit. The Leeward's not like the Kestrel. The Leeward keeps things."

He walked toward the coach. She stood in the yard with the compass in her pocket, needle bent two degrees off true, pointing — as it always did, as it had for nine years — not north, but leeward. Toward the ice.

Toward whatever her sister had left up there, and whatever Cassian Voss was afraid of, and whatever she had just, for two hundred crowns and a promise, agreed to walk into.

She went home to pack.
