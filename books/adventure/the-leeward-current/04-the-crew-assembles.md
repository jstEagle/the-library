## 4. The Crew Assembles

The letter from Cassian Voss arrived on a Tuesday, which Odile Marchand would later consider the last honest day of her life.

It was a single sheet of good paper, heavy stock, the kind that cost more per page than she made in an hour of drafting survey maps for the Corren's Rest land office. The handwriting was precise and slightly old-fashioned, the letters leaning like men against a wall. It contained three things: a sum of money that made her sit down without meaning to, a date six weeks out, and a request — no, an instruction — to assemble a crew of four and say nothing about it to anyone until they were already on the ice.

*Say nothing.* Voss had underlined it twice.

Odile read the letter four times at her kitchen table while her tea went cold, and by the fourth reading she had stopped looking at the money and started looking at what wasn't there. No destination named. No purpose stated beyond *scientific retrieval*. No signature block beyond his name, as if his name were explanation enough. And beneath all of it, threaded through every careful sentence like wire through cable, the unmistakable tension of a man writing around something.

She had worked with Cassian Voss once before, nine years ago, in the season when everyone still spoke of the Leeward Glacier expedition as a triumph rather than a tragedy. She had been twenty-six and hungry, drawing maps from other people's coordinates because no one would take her into the field. Voss had been thirty-five and famous, and he had looked at her draft of the approach corridor and said, *You draw ice like you've stood on it.* She had carried that sentence for years the way some people carry a coin from a dead parent.

Then the first expedition came home short four souls, and Voss sealed his office door, and the maps stopped circulating, and within two years the whole route had passed into rumor. Odile had gone back to drafting parcels and irrigation ditches for men who thought north was whatever direction their fences faced.

Now here was his handwriting again, offering her more money than three years of fence lines, and asking her to lie to the people she hired.

She should have burned it. She told herself that later, many times, and each time the telling rang a little less true. Because the truth was simpler and uglier: she needed the work, and she wanted the field, and nine years of wanting does things to a person's judgment that no amount of honesty can repair.

She folded the letter, put it in the tin where she kept her mother's sewing needles, and began making lists.

* * *

The glaciologist found her before she found him, which she took as a bad sign. Men with nothing left to lose tend to move fast.

Dr. Elias Merrick was living above a chandlery on the salt end of Corren's Rest, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, surrounded by the wreckage of a career Odile remembered mostly from scandal sheets: the falsified core samples, the retracted papers, the hearing where he'd stood silent while his own institution dismantled him. The stories disagreed about whether he'd faked the data or been framed by a rival, but they agreed on one thing — he hadn't fought the charges. He'd simply stopped speaking in his own defense, which struck Odile as either the behavior of a guilty man or a proud one, and after ten minutes in his company she still couldn't tell which.

He was fifty-ish and thin, with the particular pallor of a man who had spent decades indoors lit by south-facing windows, and his hands were the hands of someone who had once handled instruments worth more than the building they sat in. He kept them folded now, carefully, as if they might be confiscated.

"Voss wrote to me," he said, before she'd finished sitting down. "I assume he wrote to you."

"He did."

"Did he tell you what we're retrieving?"

"No."

Merrick smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a man confirming a suspicion he'd hoped was wrong. "Neither did mine. He offered me a salary I can't refuse and a confidentiality clause I can't afford to break. You know what that combination means, Miss Marchand?"

"It means we're being paid well enough not to ask questions."

"It means," Merrick said, "that somewhere between here and the glacier, we're going to wish we'd asked them." He unfolded his hands and laid one flat on the table between them, palm up, an odd gesture of surrender or invitation. "I'll come. I want you to understand that. Whatever's buried up there, I want to see it more than I want my name back, and my name is the only thing I've wanted for eleven years. But I want you to go in with your eyes open. Voss isn't funding science. Science doesn't require silence."

Odile studied him. In her trade she'd learned to read terrain the way other people read faces — the lie of a slope, the treachery hidden under fresh snow — and she tried to apply the same discipline now. What she saw was a man who had already decided, and was performing reluctance the way actors perform grief: thoroughly, and a little too long.

"You'll have your instruments," she said. "And your silence. Same terms as mine."

"Then we understand each other." He rose, and shook her hand with a grip colder than she expected. "One more thing. When we're on the ice, watch how he looks at the glacier. Not at us. At it. A man's face tells you everything his contract won't."

She thought about that all the way home, walking through streets going amber with evening, and decided Merrick was either the most honest man on the expedition or the most dangerous, and that the two categories were not mutually exclusive.

* * *

Ferro she found by reputation and then by following the sound.

Every town has one mechanic whose name travels by word of mouth among people who depend on machines staying alive — the haulers, the well-drillers, the mail sledge drivers. Ferro's name traveled farther than most, and always the same way: *doesn't talk, doesn't miss, doesn't overcharge.* Three words of description, and in Odile's experience those were the only three that mattered.

She found the workshop behind the freight yard, a corrugated shed breathing heat and solvent, and inside it a woman of perhaps forty bent over the exposed heart of a crawler engine, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred with burn lines like contour markings. She didn't look up when Odile entered. She didn't look up when Odile said hello. She finished seating a piston ring, wiped her hands with a rag that was more oil than cloth, and then turned and gave Odile a look of frank, unhurried assessment, the way a mason looks at a wall he's been asked to judge.

"Odile Marchand," Odile said. "Cartographer. I'm hiring for a glacier expedition. Six weeks, maybe eight. Cold work, hard pulling, and I'm told our transport will need keeping alive."

Ferro's eyes went to a slate hanging by the door, chalked with half a dozen names and rates, then back to Odile. She picked up the chalk, wrote *WHOSE ENGINE*, and set the chalk down.

"A Voss crawler. His own machine, I think. Older model."

Ferro wrote: *HEARD IT RUN?*

"Not yet. He says it's sound."

Something moved through the mechanic's face — not quite a smile, more the ghost of professional contempt — and she wrote: *VOSS SAYS MANY THINGS. BRING IT. I'LL SAY.*

It was the longest conversation Odile had ever conducted in chalk, and it was more direct than most she'd had in words. Terms were settled in five minutes: double standard rate, half up front, and one condition Ferro added herself, tapping the slate twice so the chalk clicked — *NO ONE TOUCHES MY TOOLS.*

"Agreed," Odile said. Then, because she couldn't help herself: "Can I ask why you don't talk?"

Ferro looked at her for a long moment, and Odile braced for offense, but what came instead was patience. The mechanic picked up the chalk one more time and wrote, in small neat letters: *TALKING IS HOW PEOPLE LIE. I GOT TIRED OF HEARING MYSELF DO IT.*

She hung the slate back on its hook and returned to her engine, and Odile let herself out into the cold, thinking that of the four of them, the mute one might be the only person telling the truth about anything.

* * *

Tam Averill was nineteen, and finding him was not the problem. The problem was that he was the fifth boy to answer the posting, and the first four had all been wrong in ways Odile could measure: too heavy, too soft, too clever, too frightened. Tam was wrong in a way she couldn't measure, which bothered her more.

He showed up at her door at dawn with a pack already packed, which meant he'd slept in it, which meant he'd probably slept in it for weeks, ready to leave at any hour for anywhere. He was all elbows and appetite, with sun-cracked lips and the particular brightness in the eyes of someone who had never yet been hurt badly enough to learn caution.

"Ma'am. Miss Marchand. I saw the posting at the yard office — porter, glacier route, experience preferred but not required, and I know I don't have the experience part but I've got—" he dug into his coat and produced a folded paper with the reverence of a man producing a deed, "—references."

The references were from a freight handler, a stable master, and a woman who ran the boarding house where he ate. Odile read them. They were warm, brief, and slightly desperate in the way references written for boys usually are — full of *willing*, *strong for his size*, *eats anything*, and one memorable line from the boarding house keeper: *has never once stolen so much as a roll, which in this town is a character reference.*

"Why a glacier?" Odile asked. "There's mining work closer. Paid work. Warm work."

Tam's face did something complicated — pride wrestling with honesty, and honesty winning by a nose. "Everyone says the Leeward route's impossible now. Sealed since the disaster. And I keep thinking — somebody drew those first maps. Somebody walked it before it was impossible. And if it ever opens again, even once, I want to be standing there when it does." He caught himself, flushed, and added quickly, "That sounds stupid. My uncle says wanting things is how you get hurt."

"Your uncle is right," Odile said. "But he's also wrong, or neither of us would be having this conversation." She looked at the boy — really looked, the way she'd look at an untested bridge, checking for the flaw that wouldn't show until load. "It's eight weeks minimum. The pay is good. The conditions will be worse than anything you've imagined, and I say that knowing you haven't imagined much yet. If I take you, you do exactly what I say, exactly when I say it, and you don't improvise. Improvisation kills porters."

"Yes, ma'am."

"If I say drop the load, you drop the load. Even if it's food. Even if it's me."

A pause. "Even if it's you?"

"*Especially* if it's me. Dead weight pulls you into crevasses, Mr. Averill, and I intend to die of old age in a house with a good stove." She held out the references. "Report to the freight yard Thursday, dawn. Bring the pack. Leave the optimism — it weighs more than you think."

He grinned, and the grin was so young and so uncomplicated that Odile felt something in her chest tighten protectively, and she hated it, because protection was a debt and she was already carrying too many.

* * *

They assembled for the first time a week later, in the back room of the freight yard office, around a table scarred by decades of cargo manifests. Voss had sent word that he would join them at departure and not before, which Odile found telling — a man who trusts his crew meets them early; a man who manages them arrives late.

So it fell to her, and she stood at the head of the table with Wren's-route maps — no. With *survey* maps, the old public ones, the ones drawn before the sealing, and laid them out under lamplight while the others watched.

Merrick sat closest, fingers hovering over the contour lines without quite touching, the way believers hover over relics. Ferro stood, arms crossed, near the door, equidistant from exit and engine. Tam perched on the edge of a crate as though the crate might revoke his invitation.

"The route runs northwest from the last settlement water," Odile said, tracing it. "Three days' crawl to the foothills, then the river crossings — which will be interesting, given the season — then the valley approach. After that, the corridor." She tapped the blank space on the map where the corridor should have been. "Which none of these maps cover, because the surveys stop here. Everything past this line is nine years out of date, and the glacier will have changed every meter of it."

"Changed how?" Tam asked.

"Ice moves," Merrick said quietly. "People think of glaciers as frozen. They're rivers. Slow ones, but rivers. A crevasse field mapped in spring may be a bridge by autumn and a grave by the next spring. Every map of a glacier is a map of a place that no longer exists."

"Cheerful," said Ferro — no. Ferro wrote nothing; she simply raised an eyebrow, and somehow the eyebrow contained the entire sentiment.

"We'll navigate by what's true, not what's drawn," Odile continued. "Sounding poles, flags, rope discipline on anything white. Ferro keeps the crawler alive through the foothills; past that we're man-hauling, so everything we carry, we chose to carry. Questions?"

Merrick had questions — technical, sharp, the first time Odile had seen him look fully alive, and she noted that his questions were all about the ice itself, its structure and depth and temperature, and none about the cargo they were supposedly retrieving. A scientist who doesn't ask what he's collecting is a scientist collecting something else.

Tam had one question, asked with the care of a boy handling glass: "The people who didn't come back. The first time. Do we — I mean, is there any chance we'd come across..."

"No," Odile said, faster than she meant to.

The room went quiet. Ferro looked at her. Merrick looked at her. Tam looked at the table.

"The glacier doesn't give things back," Odile said, more evenly. "That's the first rule of this route. We go in for instruments, we come out with instruments, and we don't go looking for anything else. Understood?"

Nods all around. Too easy, she thought. Too smooth. She had delivered that speech like a woman defending ground she'd already lost, and she suspected at least one person at the table had heard it that way.

* * *

Afterward, she stayed behind to roll the maps, and found Ferro hadn't left either. The mechanic stood at the window watching the yard lights, and when Odile joined her, she took out her slate and wrote, without preamble:

*YOU KNEW THE ONES WHO DIED.*

It wasn't a question. Odile considered lying, and heard Ferro's chalk-line logic in her own head — *talking is how people lie* — and answered honestly.

"Everyone in this business knew someone on that expedition. It was nine years ago. Four dead, route sealed, and nobody who was on it will speak about it. That's not suspicion. That's just weather."

Ferro erased the line slowly. Then wrote: *VOSS WAS ON IT.*

Odile went still. "Where did you hear that?"

*EVERYONE KNOWS. EVERYONE FORGETS THEY KNOW.* The mechanic shrugged, a big rolling motion. *MAN SEALS HIS OWN ROUTE, THERE'S A REASON. MAN PAYS DOUBLE FOR SILENCE, THERE'S A BIGGER ONE.*

"You took the job anyway."

Ferro met her eyes, and wrote the last line of the night, in letters larger than the rest, chalk pressing hard enough to squeak:

*MONEY'S HONEST EVEN WHEN THE MAN ISN'T. I FIX WHAT BREAKS. THAT'S ALL ANY OF US CAN DO OUT THERE.*

She hung up the slate, nodded once — a soldier's nod, a contract closing — and walked out into the dark toward her workshop, leaving Odile alone with the maps and the lamp and the blank space where the corridor should be.

Odile stood a long time looking at that blankness. Somewhere under all that white lay four graves and whatever secret had bought them, and tomorrow she would begin teaching a boy with optimistic eyes how to cross a river that wanted him dead, using charts drawn by a woman she'd never met and a route sealed by a man who paid for silence.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the blank space on the map seemed almost to breathe.

Six weeks, she thought. Six weeks until first light, and the last honest day of her life was already behind her.
