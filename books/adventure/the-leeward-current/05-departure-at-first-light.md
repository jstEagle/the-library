## 5. Departure at First Light

They left Corren's Rest in the blue hour before dawn, when the lamps along the freight yard were still burning and the town smelled of cold ash and engine oil. Beatrix rode point on a dun mare named Sorrel who had carried her through six years of patrols without ever once spooking at gunfire — the only creature in her life she trusted completely.

Behind her came the expedition: two supply wagons drawn by mules, the patched-together crawler Ferro had spent three nights rebuilding, and eleven souls counting herself. Voss rode at the rear like a man guarding his own shadow. Odile had the second wagon with her map case strapped to her chest like an infant. Merrick the glaciologist kept to himself near the instruments, checking crates that didn't need checking. Tam, the porter, was seventeen and grinned at everything, including the dark.

The desert opened ahead of them, flat and silvered with frost, running north toward the scrublands and beyond that the green line, and beyond *that* — nine days out if the weather held — the Leeward Glacier.

Beatrix let the crew find its rhythm for the first hour. Then she began her work.

She'd agreed to guide them, not to trust them, and those were different contracts. Voss's story was simple enough: scientific instruments left at a high station on the glacier's shoulder during a survey four years ago, needing retrieval before calving season closed the route for another year. It was plausible. It was also wrong in three places she could see from horseback, and she intended to find all three before they crossed the green line, because after that there would be no turning back cheaply.

* * *

The first crack showed at the morning halt, when she helped unload the instrument crates.

They were heavy. Too heavy. She hefted one marked BAROMETRIC APPARATUS and felt it shift inside — dense, packed tight, no rattle of glass or brass. Barometers were delicate things. You padded them like eggs. This crate had been packed like ammunition.

"Ferro," she said. The mechanic looked up from the crawler's engine housing, wrench in hand. He couldn't speak — nobody had told her why, and nobody would — but he read a face well. She turned the crate so he could see it, raised an eyebrow.

He shrugged. Packed it himself, apparently. He tapped the side twice and mimed something with his hands — layers, wrapping, careful work.

"Barometers," Beatrix said flatly.

Ferro held her look for a long moment, then went back to his engine. He hadn't lied, exactly. He just hadn't confirmed anything either. She filed it away.

* * *

The second crack came at midday, when the route bent east around a dry wash and Voss rode up beside her for the first time since dawn.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm working."

"Working at what?"

"Your story." She let the silence sit. Sorrel picked her way down into the wash, hooves crunching salt crust. "Four years ago, you say. A survey party. Whose survey?"

"University charter. Out of Halvard."

"Halvard hasn't funded glacier work in six years. Their endowment collapsed when the shipping lines pulled out of the coast." She'd done her reading the night before the contract signing, sitting in her room above the assay office with a stack of old trade gazettes. "Who paid for your survey, Voss?"

"A private sponsor."

"There it is." She glanced sideways at him. He was grayer than she remembered — the man who'd ruined her reputation twelve years ago over a disputed route claim had been black-haired and quick-mouthed, all blade and charm. This one had weathered. The charm had gone to rust. "A private sponsor pays to leave barometers on a mountain and then pays again to fetch them back? Before calving season? That's not science. That's a deadline."

Voss watched the horizon the way men do when they don't want to be seen watching anything else. "You took the contract," he said. "The terms were clear. Guide us there and back. What we do on the ice is our business."

"What you do on the ice becomes my business the moment it kills someone I'm responsible for."

"It won't."

"That's what everyone says the week before it does."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a scar through his upper lip she didn't remember from before, white against the windburn. "You haven't changed," he said.

"You have. That worries me more."

* * *

The third crack she saved for the afternoon, and it was the one that mattered.

At the rest stop below a ridge of red rock, she walked the wagon line while the crew ate. Odile had her charts spread on a fold-top table, weighting the corners with stones, and Beatrix drifted past the way a person drifts past — casual, uninterested, eyes doing all the work.

Odile's route map was good work. Better than good. It showed the Leeward approach corridor in fine detail, crevasse fields marked, elevation lines dense as woodgrain. And along the margin, in a different hand entirely — older, cramped, slanted hard to the right — were notes.

Beatrix stopped walking.

She knew that handwriting. She'd known it across kitchen tables and postcards and the last letter Wren had ever sent, which Beatrix still kept folded in the drawer beneath her socks, re-read once a year whether she wanted to or not.

"Where did you get these?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

Odile looked up. The cartographer was maybe fifty, sharp-faced, gray streaking her cropped hair, with the permanent squint of a woman who'd spent thirty years reading horizons. She considered Beatrix for a moment — weighing, Beatrix thought, how much truth this stranger had already earned.

"Recovered notes," Odile said. "From the first Leeward expedition."

"The one that lost people."

"The one that lost everyone but one man." Odile smoothed the chart's edge with two fingers. "Voss brought me fragments. Journals, sketch maps, a bearing log. Enough to reconstruct the safe corridor. He said the originals belonged to him."

Belonged to him. Beatrix's jaw ached and she realized she was clenching it. Nine years ago Wren Whitlock had walked onto that glacier with six others and come home as a name on a memorial plaque in Corren's Rest, and now her handwriting was circulating in another woman's map case like loose change.

"They're good notes," Odile went on, watching her. "Whoever wrote them knew ice. Knew it better than I do."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "She did."

She walked away before Odile could ask the obvious question, and spent the next mile hating Cassian Voss with a clean, cold efficiency that felt almost restful. He'd had Wren's papers all this time. All these years. He'd sat on them the way a man sits on a debt, and now he was spending them — spending *her sister* — as ballast for whatever lie he'd built this expedition on.

By evening she had her three cracks: crates packed wrong, a sponsor with no name, and her dead sister's handwriting anchoring the route. Any one of them might be innocent. Together they were a shape, and the shape was a hole where the truth should be.

But the story still *held*. Barely. Every answer Voss gave, however thin, checked out against the ground itself. The distances fit. The water sources fit. The window before calving season fit — barely, which was perhaps why he'd been so precise about the date in the contract. A liar building a fake story usually gets the details wrong in ways the land exposes. This story had been fitted to the land like a key cut from the lock's own wax.

Which meant Voss had been planning this for years.

That was the thought she carried into camp.

* * *

Camp went up fast and clean — the crew had been chosen for competence if nothing else. Ferro staked the wagons and rigged the cook fire behind a windbreak of crates. Merrick ate alone, as he had at every meal so far, hunched over a notebook he angled away from the table. Odile corrected her charts by lamplight. Tam — the boy — seemed to be everywhere at once, hauling water, feeding mules, asking questions of anyone too tired to refuse.

Beatrix took first watch because she never slept well the first night out anyway, and because she wanted the camp's rhythm mapped in her head before she trusted any part of it.

Tam found her an hour after full dark.

He came up the rise to her position carrying two cups of coffee, which was either politeness or strategy. Seventeen, long-limbed, sunburnt at the nose, with the restless energy of a dog kept too long indoors. He handed her a cup without asking and settled onto the rock beside her like they'd arranged it.

"You're the ranger," he said.

"I'm the guide."

"Same thing, isn't it? Out here." He blew on his coffee. "Back home they say rangers can track a lizard across glass."

"Back home they say a lot of things."

He laughed, easy, unbothered by her flatness in the way only the very young can be. Then, after a silence that lasted exactly as long as his nerve did:

"Can I ask you something?"

"You already did."

"The compass." He nodded at her belt, where it hung in its worn leather case — a small brass thing, older style, the lid dented, the needle housing cracked and mended with wire. "I saw it when you were saddling up. That's a Meridian surveyor's compass, isn't it? The old kind, before the sealed bezels. My uncle had one. Said you could adjust the declination screw by hand, but you had to know the year it was calibrated or it'd lie to you." He paused. "Yours is dented pretty bad."

Beatrix drank her coffee and said nothing.

"Sorry," Tam said quickly. "I didn't mean — I just think instruments are interesting. Where a person's instruments have been, I mean. That dent—"

"It fell off a cliff," Beatrix said.

It wasn't even a lie, technically. Things fell off cliffs in many ways.

"Oh." Tam chewed on that. "Was it — I mean, whose was it? Before you?"

The wind moved through the scrub below the rise, hissing dry through the branches. Somewhere back at camp, a mule stamped. Beatrix looked north, where the stars were coming out hard and white over the black line of the world, the direction they'd ride tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, until the desert gave out and the ice began.

"My sister's," she said.

And then, because Tam's face had gone soft with the particular hunger of the young for other people's grief, and because she had no intention of feeding it: "She was careless with her gear. I'm not. Get some sleep, porter. You're on second watch."

Tam took the dismissal the way he seemed to take everything — cheerfully, and without leaving. He lingered a moment, cup in both hands.

"She died out here somewhere, didn't she," he said. Not a question. The crew talked; of course they talked; a crew was nothing but talk held together by rope. "On the glacier. That's why Voss hired you. Nobody guides the Leeward anymore except somebody with a reason."

Beatrix set down her cup with great care, the way she'd been taught to set down things she wanted to throw.

"Telling stories around a fire is one thing," she said. "Telling them on watch is another. Go to bed, Tam."

This time he went. She heard him pick his way down the scree, heard the low murmur of the camp, heard Ferro bank the fire coals. She sat with the compass in her lap, thumb moving over the dent in the brass — a shallow crescent, the shape of a rock edge, the kind of mark a fall makes.

Wren hadn't been careless with her gear. Wren had been the most careful person Beatrix had ever known, careful the way surgeons are careful, checking everything twice and then a third time for the pleasure of it. If her compass had a dent in it, the mountain had put it there, and the mountain had also put her sister under itself, and every night for nine years Beatrix had wound the little mechanism and watched the needle swing and settle, always settling leeward, pointing the way Wren had gone.

She should have told the boy the truth. Or nothing at all. Instead she'd given him a pebble where a boulder belonged, and he'd pocketed it, and someday — on the ice, probably, when things went wrong, because things were going to go wrong, she could feel it the way animals feel weather — he'd hand that pebble back to her at the worst possible moment.

Below the rise, a lantern flickered in Voss's tent. Still awake. She watched the light move — pacing, or writing, or standing over something spread on a table.

Beatrix finished her coffee, cold, and made herself a promise with the practical clarity of a woman signing a contract: she would take these people to the glacier and bring them back alive, because that was the job and jobs were the only thing holding her together. But somewhere between here and the ice, Cassian Voss was going to tell her what was actually in those crates, and why her sister's handwriting was in his cartographer's map case, and what a private sponsor wanted from a dead ship's worth of secrets.

And if he wouldn't tell her —

Well. She had nine days to make him.

The lantern in his tent went out. The desert breathed. On her lap, the compass needle trembled once and settled, faithful and stubborn, pointing leeward into the dark.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and the land had changed overnight the way it always did — frost burned off by eight, the salt flats giving way to scrub, the first green smudge visible on the northern horizon like a rumor neither confirmed nor denied.

Beatrix rode point and did not look back at Corren's Rest, because there was nothing there that needed looking at, and everything ahead that did. Behind her, the wagons creaked, the crawler coughed and caught, and eleven souls moved north together into the last easy miles any of them would get.

At noon, Odile drew rein beside her and unrolled a fresh chart against her saddle horn.

"We'll hit the river crossing tomorrow," the cartographer said. "Spring melt's running high. Ferro thinks the crawler can ford it." A pause, and a sidelong glance, shrewd and unhurried. "He's wrong about that, by the way. I've seen this crossing in melt season. We lose something there tomorrow — the only question is what."

Beatrix looked at the green line growing on the horizon, and at the mountains beyond it, white and patient, waiting the way creditors wait.

"Then we'd best decide," she said, "what we can afford to lose."

Odile rolled up the chart, and the column moved on.
