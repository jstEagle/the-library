## 6. The Threshold of Green

Odile Marchetti had spent eleven years drawing maps of places that did not want to be mapped, and she had learned to read a landscape the way other people read faces. The desert around Corren's Rest had been all hard lines and open hostility — dunes like clenched fists, salt flats that glared back at the sun. But on the third morning out from town, she stood at the front rail of the lead wagon and watched the land begin to soften.

It started with color. A thread of green in the washes where water ran after storms, then scrub — grey-green sage and bitterbrush thickening along the old river terraces, then stunted piñon clinging to the north slopes of low mesas. The air changed too. It carried damp now, and the smell of growing things, and beneath it something colder, mineral, ancient — the breath of ice still a hundred miles distant.

"First time I've seen trees in two years," Tam said beside her, hanging half out of the wagon bed like a boy at a fair. He was nineteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, and he narrated everything he saw as though the world might otherwise go unrecorded. "Look at them! Actual trees!"

"They're smaller than you think they are," Odile said.

"Everything's smaller than you think it is until you get there." He grinned at her. "That's what my mother used to say."

"And was she a cartographer?"

"She was a laundress who never left the valley. But she was right about it anyway."

Odile almost smiled. She turned instead to her field board, strapped across her forearm, and began sketching the transition line — desert margin, scrub belt, first terrace — because a map drawn later was always a map drawn worse. Behind her, the second wagon groaned over a rut, and Ferro's engine answered with a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something unpleasant.

That engine was the reason she'd hired him, or one of them. Ferro could not speak — no one knew why, and he had never found a way to tell them, and eventually everyone stopped asking — but he communicated through his hands with a fluency most men never achieved with their whole bodies. He had taken the expedition's auxiliary engine, a cantankerous twenty-horsepower unit Voss had bought surplus off a mining concern, and rebuilt it in three days in a Corren's Rest stable yard. When he finished he'd wiped it down, stepped back, and tapped the flywheel twice with one knuckle. That was his whole report. *It will run.*

So far, it had run.

"You're frowning at your map," Tam said.

"I'm not frowning. I'm thinking."

"They look the same from here."

"Then look somewhere else."

He laughed and didn't look somewhere else, and Odile let it stand, because there was something about the boy's cheerfulness that was easier to be near than she wanted to admit. She had assembled this crew herself — picked each name off a list of debts and disgraces, the way Voss had once picked hers. Merrick the glaciologist, whose career had ended in a plagiarism scandal he still insisted was a misunderstanding. Ferro, who worked for cash and asked nothing. Tam, who had never been anywhere at all and wanted it so badly it embarrassed everyone. And Beatrix Whitlock, who had signed Voss's contract with an expression like a woman signing her own arrest warrant.

Odile had worked with rivals before. She had never worked with two people who hated each other quite so precisely as Whitlock and Voss. Their animosity wasn't loud. It was structural, load-bearing, woven into every exchange — Voss giving orders in that flat, reasonable voice of his, and Whitlock obeying them exactly, completely, and with a courtesy so cold it could have preserved meat. She called him *Mr. Voss* the way a judge says a sentence aloud.

And yet. On the second night, when the wind came up hard off the flats and threatened the tent lines, Odile had watched the two of them stake down the mess canvas without a word exchanged, moving around each other with the economy of people who had once done this together, many times, long ago. Whatever had broken between them, the machinery of their competence still meshed. It unsettled her more than open war would have.

She finished her sketch, closed the board, and looked north, where the mesas gave way at last to foothills — and above the foothills, faint as a watermark, white.

The Leeward Glacier lived up there, behind three days of bad ground and one river that Odile already distrusted. She had charted the approach from Wren Whitlock's recovered notes — pages copied in a careful hand by some archivist years ago, annotated in margins that were sometimes engineering and sometimes something closer to prayer. *Cross at the narrows below the split boulder if the water is under the knee. If higher, wait. Waiting costs less than crossing.* Wren Whitlock had been dead nine years, and her handwriting was still keeping people alive. Odile thought about that more often than was comfortable.

Behind her, the engine coughed.

* * *

It happened at the river, which surprised no one except perhaps the river.

They reached the crossing at midday of the fifth day out — the narrows below the split boulder, just as the notes described, a channel thirty feet wide running fast and brown-white with snowmelt, loud enough that they had to shout over it. The water was high. Not catastrophic, not yet, but higher than knee-depth on anyone, and Odile stood on the bank doing the arithmetic Wren's marginalia demanded: *waiting costs less than crossing.*

"We camp," she said. "We cross tomorrow at first light. Meltwater drops overnight. It'll be six inches lower by dawn."

Voss nodded, already turning to give the order, and that was when Ferro, who had been standing at the second wagon with his head tilted toward the engine like a physician listening to a heart, held up one hand.

Everyone stopped.

Ferro crouched at the engine housing, laid his palm flat against it, and waited. Then he made a short, sharp gesture — a fist opening, fingers flung wide — and pointed downstream.

"What?" Tam said. "What's he—"

"He says it's going," Odile translated quietly. "Or something in it is."

Ferro pulled the inspection plate. What came out of the housing was a thin rope of steam and a smell like burnt sugar, and beneath it, visible through the opened casing, a hairline fracture in the block that wept coolant in a bright thread, one drop at a time, into the dust. He had patched it in Corren's Rest — Odile had watched him do it, a weld so fine it looked like calligraphy — but five days of corrugated track and thermal cycling had found the flaw's deeper cousin. The crack ran where cracks should not run. There was no second patch. There was no second anything; the spare parts inventory consisted of gaskets, belts, and hope.

Voss arrived at the wagon in four long strides. "Can you fix it?"

Ferro looked at the crack for a long moment. Then he shook his head once, without drama, the way a man confirms a fact rather than surrenders to one.

"How far can it carry us?"

Ferro held up his hands: *unknown.* Then he pointed at the engine, then at the sky, then made a shrinking gesture. *Less than you want.*

"The supplies," Merrick said. He had come up from the rear, and his voice had the particular tightness it got whenever the instruments were mentioned. "The core samples are in that wagon. If the wagon goes into the river—"

"The wagon isn't going into the river," Odile said. "Because we're not driving it across tonight. We're camping, and tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow the crack will be worse," Merrick said. "Thermal cycling. Every freeze-thaw propagates it. If we're going to cross, we cross now, while the engine can still turn the wheels."

"If we cross now," Odile said, "we cross in water that's over the axles, with a dying engine, hauling eight hundred pounds of gear. Your instruments float, Dr. Merrick. People don't."

"Then we lighten the wagon. We carry what matters across on foot and ferry the rest."

"With what? You want to swim the meltwater?"

"I want to *solve the problem,*" Merrick snapped, "which is more than the maps are doing."

The word *maps* landed exactly where he intended it. Odile felt it in her jaw. Before she could answer, Beatrix Whitlock spoke from ten feet away, where she had been standing silently at the water's edge for the entire argument, watching the current with the fixed attention other people gave to fires.

"The water's dropping already," she said.

Everyone turned. She crouched, touched two fingers to a stone at the margin, and drew a line in the wet silt above it. "Mark it. Ten minutes, mark it again." She straightened. "Meltwater doesn't argue. It just falls. Question is how fast, and whether the engine lasts longer than the drop."

Voss looked at her. Something passed across his face — not warmth, nothing so simple, but recognition, the grim comfort of a man hearing a voice he'd braced against for years and finding it steady. "How long?"

"Two hours, maybe three, gets us under axle height. Maybe not under hub." She glanced at Ferro. "Can it turn wheels in eighteen inches of water?"

Ferro considered. Then he held up one finger — *one problem* — and mimed, slowly and precisely, the motion of water entering the intake, and the engine stopping mid-stream, mid-current, loaded.

Silence, except the river.

"So we don't risk the engine in the current," Beatrix said. "We winch it."

Tam blinked. "Winch it? With what?"

"With the wagon itself." She was already walking the bank as she talked, reading the ground the way Odile read contour lines. "Anchor the wagon on this side, upstream of the ford, set the drum brake. Run the winch cable across, tie off on the far side — that boulder, it's solid, it's been sitting there since the last ice age. Ferry the cargo across first, by hand, on the cable. Then bring the wagons over light, one at a time, engine at idle, cable taking the load. Current pushes, cable holds. Engine only has to beat the friction, not the river." She looked up at Voss. "You have two hundred feet of cable on the lead wagon."

"I have two hundred and forty," Voss said.

"Then we're rich."

There was a moment — Odile would remember it afterward, would find herself returning to it during harder hours on the ice — when nobody moved, and the plan hung in the air between them, complete, obvious, and entirely unclaimed by any single authority. It hadn't come from Voss. It hadn't come from her, the cartographer, the nominal organizer of this enterprise. It had come from the ranger, standing in the shallows with wet bootlaces, and it was better than anything anyone else had said, and everyone present knew it.

Merrick recovered first, because pride heals fastest in the wounded. "The cable anchor on the far side. Someone has to swim it across."

"No," Beatrix said. "Someone walks it across. Upstream end, the gravel bar — see it? Water's shin-deep there, maybe thigh in the middle. Cold enough to stop your heart if you stay in too long, so you move." She was pulling off her jacket as she said it, folding it with the same unhurried precision she applied to everything. "I'll take the first run."

"You don't have to—" Tam started.

"That's the point of me," she said, and waded in.

* * *

The crossing took the rest of the day and cost them nothing but skin.

Odile directed it from the bank with her board on her arm, sketching as she went — not the river, which refused to hold still, but the system: anchor points, cable angles, the sequence of loads. It was, she realized somewhere in the second hour, the first thing this crew had built together rather than merely occupied. Ferro anchored the wagon and tended the drum brake with his whole body, leaning into the work, reading tension through the cable the way he read engines through his palms. Voss ferried loads beside Beatrix, and the two of them fell into a rhythm across the water — she carrying, he anchoring, a rotation of shouts and hand signals that needed no negotiation because it had been negotiated years ago by people they no longer were. Tam hauled cargo and asked questions and learned faster than he dropped things. Even Merrick worked, grimly, competently, carrying his instrument cases himself across the gravel bar with a tenderness he extended to nothing else on earth.

The water took Beatrix three times. Each crossing she came up the far bank streaming, lips gone grey-blue, and each time she walked directly back to the upstream end without pausing, because stopping was how the cold got its vote. On the third crossing she stumbled in the deepest channel and the current took her sideways a full step before the cable caught her hand, and Odile heard Tam make a sound like he'd been struck. Beatrix regained her footing, finished the crossing, and stood on the far bank wringing out her sleeve, and if her hands shook, she kept them busy enough that shaking was only a rumor.

By late afternoon the cargo was across. By golden hour the empty wagons followed, engine idling, cable singing its low note, Ferro feathering the throttle while the river shoved and the winch held. The fractured engine got them both vehicles over the narrows and died forty minutes later on the far bank with a sound like a sigh of relief, steam rising gently from the cracked block into the cold evening air.

Ferro lifted the hood, looked at it for a while, and then closed it again with a gentleness that was nearly funerary. He patted it once.

"It got us across," Tam said. "That counts for something."

Ferro considered this. Then he held up his hands and signed something brief.

"What did he say?"

"He says," Odile translated, "that machines are honest. They die exactly when they die. No earlier, no later, and never out of spite." She looked at the dead engine, then at the white shoulders of the foothills going rose-colored in the last light. "He thinks people could learn from that."

Camp went up on the gravel terrace above the water, and for the first time since Corren's Rest the work of making it happened without being assigned. Tam gathered wood because wood existed here now, real firewood, sage and piñon deadfall. Merrick checked his instruments and, finding them dry, permitted himself to sit near the others. Ferro cooked — nobody had assigned this either; he had simply taken it over on the first night with a look that dared anyone to object — and produced a stew from stores that tasted better than it had any right to.

Beatrix sat apart, as she always did, wrapped in a blanket with her boots off, working her feet back to life. Her compass lay on the flat stone beside her, brass case catching the firelight. Tam drifted over to it the way he drifted toward everything interesting, and Odile saw him reach, and saw Beatrix's hand close over the instrument a half-second before his fingers arrived — not angry, not even sharp, just absolute, a door closing.

"Sorry," Tam said. "I only wondered—"

"It belonged to my sister," Beatrix said.

"Oh." Tam sat with that. "Is she—"

"Gone." Beatrix put the compass inside her coat. "Nine years ago. On the glacier we're walking into."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the darkening valley, the river kept talking to itself.

"That's why you took the job," Tam said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't clever, and it was exactly the kind of thing a nineteen-year-old says because no one has yet taught him which doors stay shut.

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment, and Odile braced for the door to close again. Instead the ranger said, quietly, "Eat your stew, Tam."

He ate his stew.

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls, Odile stayed up with her charts, correcting the day's positions by lamplight. Three days to the glacier's toe, if the ground allowed. Four if it didn't. She heard footsteps and looked up to find Voss standing at the edge of the lamplight, a shape of grey wool and shadow, looking north at the mountains he had sworn, nine years ago, never to look at again.

"She's good," he said. It was not clear whether he meant Beatrix or someone else entirely.

"Yes," Odile said.

"You knew the route from the sister's notes. Before I ever showed you mine."

"Wren Whitlock's annotations are better than any survey done since. Whoever archived them understood what they had." Odile set down her pencil. "Why did you really hire me, Mr. Voss? You don't need a cartographer. You need a guide, and you have one. You need muscle, and you have that too. So why am I here?"

Voss was quiet long enough that the fire settled twice. When he finally spoke, his voice was level and very tired.

"Because the notes you copied aren't the whole of Wren Whitlock's records," he said. "And because when we find what's up there, someone on this crew will need to draw a map of it. And I need to know, before that happens, which of us that map serves."

He walked away toward his tent, leaving Odile alone with her lamp, her charts, and the white silence of the mountains waiting at the edge of the page.
