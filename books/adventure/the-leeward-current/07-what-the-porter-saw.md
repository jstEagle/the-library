## 7. What the Porter Saw

Tam had never stood a watch in his life before this expedition, and it showed.

Ferro had tried to teach him the routine on the first night out from Corren's Rest — check the sledge lashings, walk the perimeter of the camp, keep the stove fed but not roaring, and above all, listen. *The desert talks at night,* Ferro had written on his slate, tapping the words twice with one blunt finger before wiping them away. *Learn its voice and you'll hear when something's wrong.*

Tam was learning. Slowly. The desert's voice turned out to be a hundred small sounds he'd spent nineteen years ignoring: the tick of cooling metal in the supply sledges, the whisper of sand moving against canvas, the dry rattle of a lizard crossing gravel somewhere beyond the firelight. Every hour or so the wind changed key, and every time it did, his heart jumped and he told himself, firmly, that it was only wind.

It was the third night of the march, camped in the lee of a ridge of rust-colored stone that Odile called the Sentinel on her map. Behind them lay two days of hard travel; ahead, according to Beatrix Whitlock, three more days of scrubland before the first true cold began. Above him the sky was an ocean of stars so dense it seemed less like darkness than like a different kind of light.

He was on the middle watch, midnight until four. Voss had set the rotation himself: Ferro first, Tam second, Beatrix last, with Voss taking the dawn shift alone, as he did every night, refusing all offers to share it.

*The man doesn't sleep like other people,* Tam thought, feeding a twist of scrub-brush into the stove. He watched the flames take it and thought about how strange it was to be trusted with something. Back in Corren's Rest, nobody had ever trusted him with anything heavier than a delivery crate. Now he had a rifle slung across his back — unloaded, Beatrix had insisted, until he could prove he wouldn't point it at anything he loved — and a watch to keep, and four people sleeping behind him whose lives were, in some small way, in his hands.

It should have frightened him more than it did. Mostly it made him stand straighter.

The hours moved. He walked the perimeter twice, checked the lashings, found one strap on the fuel sledge working loose and cinched it down with the satisfaction of a man performing surgery. Around two o'clock the wind died completely, and the silence that followed was so total he could hear his own pulse in his ears.

That was when he heard the voices.

Not voices, exactly. One voice. Low, pitched under the wind even though there was no wind, coming from the far side of the ridge where the sledges were parked and the ground fell away into a shallow draw.

Voss.

Tam froze, one hand on the cold rail of the nearest sledge. It wasn't unusual for Voss to be awake — the man seemed to run on some fuel none of them had access to — but he wasn't supposed to be over there. The dawn shift was his ritual, not the dead hours of the night. And he wasn't supposed to be talking. In six days of travel, Tam had heard Cassian Voss speak perhaps forty sentences total, all of them clipped, all of them functional: *Load the water. Check the bearing. We move at first light.* The man treated words like coins he couldn't afford to spend.

But now he was talking, low and continuous, and there was something in the cadence that made the hair rise on Tam's arms. It didn't sound like a conversation. There was no one else out there. It sounded like — Tam searched for the word — like a man reading aloud. Or praying. Or making a report.

Curiosity was a sin Beatrix had already scolded him for once — *on the ice, curiosity kills faster than thirst* — but Tam was nineteen, and the night was vast, and the voice kept going, and his feet made the decision before his head could veto it.

He moved along the base of the ridge, boots finding the soft sand where his steps would be silent, and climbed the low shoulder of stone on his hands and knees. From there he could look down into the draw without silhouetting himself against the stars.

Cassian Voss stood beside a small fire — a real fire, not a stove-fire, which broke half a dozen rules of desert discipline Beatrix had drilled into them all — and he was feeding papers into it. One at a time. Deliberately. He'd spread a leather folio open on a flat rock beside him, and as each page caught and curled, he watched it burn with an attention that looked almost tender, the way a man watches a wound close.

And he was speaking. Tam edged closer, straining, and the words came through the still air in fragments:

"...said we'd never come back. I meant it. You knew I meant it..."

A page went into the flame. The fire flared orange, and for a moment it lit Voss's face from below, and Tam saw something he had no framework for: the expedition leader, the gray ghost of a man who had hired them all and paid them double the going rate without blinking, looked *afraid*. Not of the dark. Not of the route ahead. Afraid the way a man is afraid of a debt.

"...they'll ask why. They always ask why. Let them ask..."

Another page. Ash lifted on a breath of air and drifted up past Tam's hiding place, settling on the stone like gray snow.

Then Voss said a name, and the world tilted sideways.

"*Wren.*"

Just the name. Softly. The way you say the name of someone you buried. "*Wren, if you can hear me — I'm keeping my end. Whatever they paid, whatever they promised, it ends here. I won't let them dig her up twice.*"

Tam stopped breathing.

Wren. He knew that name the way everyone who'd traveled with Beatrix Whitlock for more than a day knew it — not because she spoke of her sister, but because she carried her. The brass compass on a cord around her neck; the way her thumb found it whenever the conversation turned to family, or home, or anything with roots. Odile had told him the story once, quietly, over the cartography table while Beatrix was out checking the route: a sister lost on the Leeward Glacier nine years ago, on an expedition that had gone into the white and mostly hadn't come out. A sister whose compass Beatrix wore like a second heartbeat.

And Cassian Voss was standing alone in a desert draw at two in the morning, burning papers and saying her name like a confession.

Tam pressed himself flat against the stone. His mind was sprinting through possibilities and finding none of them good. Maybe Voss had known Wren. Maybe everyone remotely connected to the glacier country had known Wren; she'd been a legend of the ice routes, Odile said, the kind of climber whose name got spoken in the same breath as the passes she'd opened. That could be innocent. Grief was innocent. Men burned letters of the dead all the time —

But Voss had said *I'm keeping my end.* Keeping your end implied a bargain. Bargains implied terms. Terms implied —

Another page caught the light as Voss turned it toward the fire, and by chance the flame illuminated it fully for half a second, and Tam saw, printed neat and formal across the top, a letterhead he recognized. Everyone in Corren's Rest recognized it. The Halvard Trust. The old money, the museum money, the money that funded expeditions the way other men bought horses.

The same letterhead, Tam realized with a slow cold drop in his stomach, that appeared on the contract Voss had signed with each of them. He'd seen it when Odile walked him through his terms — *Halvard Trust Expedition 14, per diem plus completion bonus, sign here.* The Trust was paying for this trip. And Cassian Voss was burning the Trust's correspondence in the dark, alone, saying the name of a dead woman over the ashes.

One more fragment came through, barely louder than breath:

"...she trusted me too. That's the part you don't understand. That's the part that *costs*."

Then silence. Voss straightened, swept the last of the ash with the side of his boot, kicked sand over the fire, and stood for a long moment looking north — toward the glacier country, toward the Leeward, toward whatever it was he'd actually hired them to retrieve. Then he folded the empty folio closed, tucked it inside his coat, and walked back toward camp with the measured tread of a man who had done nothing stranger than check the weather.

Tam stayed flat on the rock until his elbows ached and the stars wheeled a full hand-span across the sky.

* * *

He didn't sleep after his watch ended. He sat by the cold stove with his blanket around his shoulders and rehearsed what he was going to say, and discarded it, and rehearsed again. By the time the eastern sky went from black to iron to a thin bruised rose, he had settled on the truth, delivered plainly, because he couldn't see any way to make the truth sound small.

Beatrix rose with the light, as she always did, and was halfway through coiling her bedroll when Tam crossed the camp to her and said, quietly, "Ranger Whitlock. Can I talk to you? Away from the others."

She looked at him — really looked, the way she did, like she was reading a map of his face — and something in his expression must have answered her question before he could, because she set down the bedroll and said, "Walk," and led him out past the picket line to the flat below the ridge. Toward the draw. She didn't know yet why he wanted distance from the camp, and the irony of arriving at the exact scene of the crime wasn't lost on him.

They stopped where the ash was. Tam watched her notice it — a dark smudge of burnt paper in the sand, half-scattered by the night wind — and watched her go very still.

"Last night," Tam said. "On my watch. I heard someone talking out here. I came up over the ridge." He swallowed. "It was Voss. He was burning papers. Trust letterhead — I saw it in the firelight. And he was..." He hesitated, and then, because there was no kind way to say it, said it. "He was talking to her. To Wren. He said her name. He said — *I'm keeping my end. I won't let them dig her up twice.*"

For a long moment Beatrix Whitlock did not move at all.

Tam had seen her angry — the first day, when he'd nearly dropped a crate of instruments, she'd dressed him down with a fluency that left his ears ringing for an hour. He'd seen her tired, seen her amused, seen her patient with Odile's endless re-measurements and Ferro's silent, gestured instructions. But he had never seen her do this: nothing. Absolute stillness, the stillness of an animal that has just scented something lethal and is deciding whether to freeze or flee. Her thumb had found the compass at her throat without her seeming to know it. Her knuckles around the little brass case had gone white.

"Say it again," she said. Her voice was level. Too level. "Exactly what he said."

"*I'm keeping my end,*" Tam repeated. "And — *I won't let them dig her up twice.* And before that, he said, um—" He closed his eyes, replaying it. "*She trusted me too. That's the part that costs.*"

The compass clicked. Tam realized Beatrix had flipped it open without looking at it, the needle trembling between her fingers, and she was staring at it as if it might tell her whether he was lying.

"He was on the expedition," she said. Not a question. The words came out flat and final, like stones laid on a grave. "Nine years ago. On the Leeward. He was *there*."

"I don't—"

"The manifest was sealed after the inquiry. Families got names, dates, causes. I got a date." Her thumb moved over the compass lid, back and forth, a small compulsive motion. "Cassian Voss's name was not on the list of the dead. I checked. God help me, I checked every name against every crew roster I could buy or steal, because I needed to know if anyone else had come off that ice alive, and his name was nowhere. Which meant either he wasn't there—" She stopped. Breathed. "Or he came off the ice and buried himself so deep that even the inquiry couldn't find him. Nine years. He built a whole reputation in the southern trade routes. A clean one. And the whole time—"

She turned and looked north, toward the horizon where the scrubland was already giving way, mile by invisible mile, to the high cold country. Toward the glacier. Toward the place where her sister had died and where, in five days' time, Cassian Voss intended to lead them.

"He hired me," she said softly. "He hired *me*, Tam. Out of everyone on the frontier, he sought out the sister of the woman he—" The sentence failed her. She rebuilt it. "He knew who I was. He knew exactly what he was asking, and he let me believe it was instruments. Scientific caches. *Retrieval.*"

"Maybe it is instruments," Tam offered, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Maybe the papers were — old business. Settling accounts. People burn things for a hundred reasons—"

"People don't burn Trust correspondence at two in the morning and pray over the ashes," Beatrix said. "People don't say *keeping my end* unless somebody gave them terms." She closed the compass. The click of the lid sounded, in the enormous quiet of the dawn, like a lock turning. "He made a promise over my sister's grave, and then he broke nine years of silence to come back to the ice, and he brought witnesses. He brought *me*. Either he's honoring that promise in the only way left to him—"

"Or," Tam said, because the other branch of the logic was sitting in his chest like swallowed ice, "he's breaking it. And he needed us so that when it breaks, it isn't on his hands alone."

Beatrix looked at him then, and Tam saw something that frightened him more than the burning papers had: respect. Grim, unwilling, the look of a woman reassessing the value of a tool she'd underestimated.

"You saw all that from a rock in the dark?"

"I saw a man afraid," Tam said. "The rest is just — arithmetic."

For a moment the ghost of something crossed her face that might, in another life, have been a smile. Then it was gone, and she was all business, turning back toward the camp where Voss was already awake, already standing, already watching them from across the embers of the cook fire with eyes that missed nothing and gave nothing back.

"Here's what happens now," Beatrix said, low and fast. "You say nothing. Not to Odile, not to Ferro, not to anyone. If Voss learns what you saw before I understand *why* he did it, he has options — bad ones. He can turn us around, strand us, buy us, bury us. Right now his advantage is that he thinks his secret held. I intend to keep that advantage until I've decided what to do with it."

"And what are you going to do?" Tam asked. "Turn back?"

Something moved behind her eyes — grief and fury and nine years of a debt she'd carried across a desert and up onto the ice, all of it suddenly, horribly, in question.

"No," Beatrix Whitlock said, watching Cassian Voss across the dying fire. "I'm going to get him to the glacier. Because whatever he's been keeping for nine years — whatever my sister died to protect or died to hide — it's time somebody asked him about it in a place where he can't walk away."

Across the camp, Voss lifted the coffee pot in their direction. An invitation. A courtesy. His face was calm as carved stone.

Tam's stomach dropped through the sand.

"He's coming over," Tam whispered.

"I know," said Beatrix, and put on her best ranger's face, and turned to meet him.
