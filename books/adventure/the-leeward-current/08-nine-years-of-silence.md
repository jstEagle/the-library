## 8. Nine Years of Silence

Beatrix didn't sleep.

She sat in the lee of the supply sledge with her back against the canvas and the compass open in her palm, watching the needle hold its stubborn line toward the leeward dark while the fire across camp burned down to a red eye. Tam's words kept circling her like gulls over a kill: *He was burning papers. He said her name. He said Wren.*

The cold came up through the ground and into her spine and she let it, because the cold was honest. It asked nothing of her except that she endure it. Everything else in this expedition had begun to lie.

Across the dead fire, Voss's tent stood black against the last of the stars. She could see the shape of him inside it if she let herself imagine it — the gray at his temples, the careful hands, the voice that had learned to sound reasonable about everything. Nine years ago that voice had stood up in a room full of sponsors and surveyors and said, *Whitlock's route readings were falsified,* and the room had believed him because he was Cassian Voss and she was nobody from nowhere with a dead sister and no proof. Her name had never fully recovered. Neither had his, though he'd worn the damage differently — like a coat he'd decided looked good on him.

She closed the compass. The click of it was very small and very loud.

Dawn came the way it did at altitude: not gradually but all at once, as if someone had struck a match behind the eastern ridgeline. The peaks went from charcoal to rose to a white so clean it hurt. Beatrix was already standing when the light reached Voss's tent, and when he crawled out into the blue morning, pulling on his gloves, he found her waiting between him and the cook stove.

"You're up early," he said.

"We need to talk."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has been waiting for a bill to arrive. "Coffee first."

"After."

He studied her for a long moment, then nodded once and walked past her toward the ridge above camp, and she followed, because he'd chosen the ground and she wanted to see whether he knew why. He did. He climbed to a shelf of bare rock overlooking the valley where they'd made their last camp before the ice proper, out of earshot of the others, with the whole white corridor of the Leeward spread below them like something freshly unwrapped. A place where shouting wouldn't carry. A place where, nine years ago, another conversation might have ended badly.

"You've been sitting on this since the boy told you," Voss said. He didn't turn around. "I wondered how long you'd wait."

"Tam told me what he saw. Papers burning. My sister's name." She kept her voice level the way she kept her footing on scree — deliberately, testing each word before she put weight on it. "What I want to know is how long you've known my sister was on your precious first expedition. Before you hired me. Before you wrote that letter. Before any of it."

Voss turned then. The dawn light caught the gray in his beard and the old scar at his hairline she'd never asked about. "Since always," he said. "Wren Whitlock was the best ice reader I ever worked with. Better than me. That's not sentiment. That's a fact I've carried for nine years like a stone in my boot."

The words landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, hot. "You stood in front of thirty people and destroyed me, and you don't get to say her name like you loved her."

"I never said I loved her." His voice didn't rise. That was the thing about Voss that had always made her want to hit him — nothing ever moved him, or nothing showed. "I said I worked with her. And I did. For six weeks on the Leeward, the best six weeks of my career, until—"

"Until what?"

"Until the night she died."

* * *

The wind came up the valley in a long slow breath, moving the surface snow off the glacier below in ghost-streamers that caught the light and vanished. Beatrix became aware that her hands had curled into fists inside her mittens and made herself uncurl them, one finger at a time.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "And understand that I will know if you lie to me. I've spent nine years learning to read liars on the trail. They all have tells. Yours is that you get more precise."

Something flickered across his face — pain, maybe, or its close cousin, respect. "You have changed," he said quietly. "The girl I knew would have already swung at me."

"The girl you knew trusted you. Start talking."

Voss walked to the edge of the rock shelf and stood looking down at the glacier as if he could see nine years backward along it. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into a register she hadn't heard from him before — lower, stripped of its polish, almost rough.

"Eleven years ago I mounted an expedition to the Leeward headwall. Scientific cover — ice cores, accumulation data, the usual. But the real objective was a rumor. A trapper's account from sixty years prior, a journal fragment out of Corren's Rest, stories about a wreck seen high on the ice after a storm season. A ship, Beatrix. Sailing timbers. Inland, above the tree line, embedded in the glacier at two thousand meters."

"A ship." She said it flatly, giving it back to him. "That's your story. That's what you burned papers over."

"That's what we found." He turned to face her fully now, and whatever was in his eyes made her take half a step back before she stopped herself. "You can decide in a moment whether I'm lying. You'll know soon enough anyway, once we're on the ice. But hear the rest first, because the rest is the part that matters. We found it. Wren found it, actually — she read the pressure ridges better than any of us, and she led us through the icefall to a blue-ice embayment on the north flank where the glacier had exhumed a hull. Oak ribs. Iron fastenings. Scorch marks on every plank, from the inside out. A vessel that could not possibly be there, that had been there longer than the town your mother was born in."

Beatrix heard her own breathing. The valley was very quiet around them, the kind of quiet the high country keeps before weather.

"What was in it?"

"Cargo. Sealed crates, waxed canvas, marked in a script none of our people recognized. And documents in the captain's cabin — logs, letters, a manifest. Enough paper to fill two sledge bags." He paused. "And enough of it survived the burn that some pieces are still legible. That's what I was burning two nights ago. Copies. Transcriptions I made eleven years ago and have kept moving from hiding place to hiding place ever since, because there are people who would pay anything for them, and I mean anything — enough to buy Corren's Rest three times over — and what those papers describe should never be sold to anyone at any price."

"Then tell me what they describe."

"No."

The refusal came out fast and final, a door closing, and the anger she'd been holding on a short leash all night finally slipped it. She crossed the distance between them in three strides and shoved him, both hands flat against his chest, hard enough that he stumbled back a step on the frozen rock.

"Don't you dare," she said. "Don't you stand there and hand me half a story. My sister died on that ice. I have spent nine years not knowing how — not knowing whether she froze, whether she fell, whether she died alone, whether she suffered, whether she called for anyone. I built my whole life on top of that hole like a house built over a mine shaft. And you knew. You knew everything, and you let me go on not knowing, and then you had the gall to hire me, to bring me back here, and burn her name in a campfire where a nineteen-year-old porter could see it. So you will tell me how Wren died, Cassian. That part is not yours to withhold. That part belongs to me."

The wind moved between them. Voss didn't defend himself, didn't steady himself against her push; he just stood there and took it, and she realized with a strange distant clarity that he had been waiting to be hit by exactly this, maybe for nine years, maybe since the day he'd written her name on that contract.

"She died securing the cargo," he said. "That's the truth of it, as much of it as I can give you. The burn went wrong."

* * *

He told it standing up, facing the glacier, and Beatrix listened with her arms wrapped around herself and the compass a small hard square against her sternum through her coat, and the sun climbed another degree and neither of them noticed.

"There were eight of us who went up to the wreck. Wren, me, Halvard, the Okonkwo brothers, Priya Shen, two porters whose names I'll say if you ask but can't say otherwise, and the sponsor's man — a fellow named Aldous Crane, who'd funded the whole venture and come along to inventory what we found. It was Crane who understood first what the cargo was worth. Not scientifically. Financially. There are collectors — I won't describe them, you'll learn enough soon — who trade in exactly the kind of thing those crates held, and Crane had been in contact with one of them before we ever left. The expedition was never science. It was retrieval. We were the delivery crew and none of us knew it except him."

"And Wren figured it out."

"Wren figured it out before anyone." His jaw tightened. "She read those ship's documents by lamplight for two days. Whatever she found in them scared her in a way I'd never seen. Wren wasn't scareable — you know that better than anyone. She used to walk crevasse bridges without testing them, laughing. But she came out of that cabin gray-faced, and she found me, and she said: *this cannot leave the mountain.* And I laughed at her. I want you to know that. Whatever else you carry away from this conversation, carry that. She came to me with the truth and I laughed, because I was twenty-nine years old and famous and I thought the world was a place where important things got published and everyone got safer from knowing more. She argued with me for a day and a half. Then she stopped arguing and started acting."

"Crane," Beatrix said. "She was going to stop Crane."

"Crane had sent a signal party down the mountain — a runner with a message to a relay station, arranging pickup. Wren intercepted the runner. And then she went back up to the wreck alone, at night, in falling temperature, and she started the burn herself. The cargo, the documents, all of it. She was halfway done when Crane and two of the men he'd bought caught her at it."

Beatrix's breath had gone shallow. Below them the glacier lay white and innocent, and she hated it with a feeling so total it was nearly calm.

"There was a fight," Voss said. "On the ice above the embayment. I arrived in the middle of it — I'd gone after her when I understood what she'd done, and I've thanked God every day since that I was late rather than absent. Crane's men had a rope on her. The ice under them—" He stopped. For the first time his composure cracked, visibly, a seam opening down the middle of the man. "The ice under them failed. All of it. A crevasse bridge, loaded wrong, stressed by the heat of the burn. It went, and it took Crane and one of his men and—" His voice did something she had never heard it do. It broke. "It took Wren. I got a hand to her. One hand. On the lip. And she looked up at me, and Beatrix, she wasn't afraid. She said, *let go, it's done, most of it's burned.* And the bridge went the rest of the way, and I couldn't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Beatrix stood in the rose-and-white morning with her sister's death arriving in her body all at once after nine years of arriving in pieces, and it was worse and better than every version she had invented in the dark, because Wren had chosen it. Wren had known the cost and paid it on purpose, alone, at night, to keep something terrible from reaching the world, and her last act on earth had been to tell the man holding her hand to let go.

Beatrix sat down on the rock. Her legs simply declined to hold her anymore, and Voss didn't reach for her, which was — she would remember this later — the only decent thing he could have done.

"The rest of us swore silence," he said, when the wind had passed. "Those of us who weren't bought. We reported the expedition a failure. I took the blame for the falsified route readings — I gave the sponsors a scandal instead of a mystery, because a scandal ends questions and a mystery breeds them. Your name was the price of burying theirs. I've regretted it every single day, and I'd do it again tomorrow, because the alternative was people like Crane's buyer digging into what happened up here, and that cannot happen. Then I sealed the route. Every map I touched, every report, every marker — the Leeward corridor reads as impassable, unstable, worthless. Calving season comes early and leaves late. No one funds a route that eats expeditions." He looked down at her, and his eyes were dry and terribly tired. "I swore the glacier would keep her, Beatrix. That was the oath. Nobody goes back. Nothing comes out. The mountain holds its dead and its secrets together, and the world stays safe from both."

"Then why am I here?" Her voice came out scraped raw. "Why did you write to me? Why break your own oath?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he reached inside his coat and drew out a scrap of paper — burnt at one edge, folded soft with handling — and held it out to her without letting go of it, so that she had to take hold of the other side, and for a moment they stood connected by a single charred fragment like two people on either side of a crevasse.

"Because three months ago," Voss said, "someone else broke it first. Someone sent money to a man on my own crew. Someone is reopening the route, and I don't know who, or how far their reach goes, or what they already know. I need the best ice reader alive on this glacier, and God forgive me, that's you — Wren taught you both, even if only one of you got the lessons straight from her." His grip tightened on the paper, then released it. "I brought you here to help me make sure the oath holds. That's the truth. All of it that I can give you today."

Below them, far down the valley, a sound rolled out of the glacier — deep, tectonic, a groan like a door opening in the bones of the world — and both of them turned to look. High on the headwall, a serac the size of a chapel leaned, shivered, and settled, shedding a plume of powder snow that hung in the still air like smoke.

Calving season was coming early this year. Beatrix felt it in the sound, in her teeth, in the compass against her chest with its needle swinging slowly, slowly off true.

"Get the crew moving," she said, standing. "We're burning daylight, and I want to see this ship of yours before the mountain changes its mind about showing it."

Voss searched her face for something — forgiveness, maybe, or a verdict — and finding neither, nodded and started down toward camp. Beatrix stayed on the rock a moment longer, alone with the groaning ice and the ghost of her sister's last words. *Let go. It's done.*

"Not yet," she said to the glacier, and went down after him.
