## 9. The White Corridor

Odile had drawn maps for eleven years, and she had never once hated a map until she drew this one.

She sat in the lee of the supply sledge with her drafting board braced against her knees, the wind trying to peel the paper off its clips, and she looked at what she had made: a corridor of white bounded by two walls of blue-black ice, narrowing ahead into a throat of seracs where the Leeward Glacier bent around the shoulder of Mount Cael. Every line on it was an argument between what she knew and what she guessed. The solid strokes were hers — surveyed by sextant, paced out by boot and wheel. The dotted lines were not hers at all.

They came from Wren Whitlock's notes.

Voss had given them to her on the third night out of Corren's Rest, without ceremony, folded inside an oilskin packet that smelled of old smoke. *Cartographic references from the first expedition,* he'd said. *Use whatever's useful.* He had not said where they'd come from or how they'd survived, and Odile, who had learned long ago that Cassian Voss answered exactly as many questions as he chose to, had not asked. She'd simply taken them back to her tent and unrolled them under the lamp, and there they were: field sketches in a quick, confident hand, elevations penciled along the margins, crevasse fields marked with small, patient crosses, and here and there a note in the same hand that had nothing to do with ice at all.

*The blue is older than it looks. Trust the blue.*

*Wind sings in the second moraine before a storm. Listen.*

*If we don't come back this way, the east wall route is a lie. Don't take the east wall.*

Odile had sat with those pages for a long time that night, feeling like a burglar in a dead woman's house. Then she'd sharpened her pencil and gone to work, because the dead woman's hand was better than anything Odile could have produced blind, and on the Leeward Glacier, better was the only standard that mattered.

Now she folded the day's working copy into her coat and stood. Ahead of them, the valley opened its mouth.

* * *

They had crossed the terminal moraine at dawn, when the light came up gray-gold and the glacier below them lay still as poured metal. Beatrix had gone first, as she went first everywhere, reading the ground the way Odile read paper. Tam came behind her with the tow-line over his shoulder, too solemn about it, gripping it like a rope thrown to a drowning man. Ferro walked beside the sledge with one hand resting on the frame, the way another man might rest a hand on a horse's neck. Merrick brought up the rear with his instruments clinking in their cases, and Voss walked alone, off to the flank, the way he always did, as if he were keeping company with something none of them could see.

By midmorning they were down on the ice itself, and the world changed.

Odile had seen glaciers before — survey work in the northern ranges, a season on the Verre coast — but nothing had prepared her for the scale of this. The Leeward was not a river of ice; it was a country of it. The valley walls rose sheer on either side, a thousand feet of striated blue, and the glacier's surface rolled away ahead of them in frozen swells and hollows, dusted with wind-blown grit, veined with meltwater channels that even in the cold sang faintly somewhere beneath their feet. The air had a taste to it, mineral and ancient, like water from a well too deep to be trusted.

"God," Tam said quietly. "It's *alive*."

"It's not alive," Merrick said. "It's a mass in motion. Alive implies—"

"It groans," Tam said. "Listen to it. It groans."

And it did. Under everything, beneath the crunch of their boots and the creak of the sledge runners, the glacier spoke — long, tectonic sighs that seemed to come from miles away and inches below at once, the sound of a thing shifting its weight in its sleep. Odile had heard it described in a dozen expedition accounts, always in the past tense, always by men who had come home. None of them had captured it. It sounded less like geology than like patience.

"Rope spacing," Beatrix called back, and the banter died. "Twenty feet. Nobody steps anywhere I haven't stepped. Odile — your marks?"

Odile checked the wands she'd planted at the last rest stop, little bamboo flags snapping in the wind, then consulted her board. "Corridor holds straight for three kilometers. Then—" She tapped the dotted line. "Then we're in Wren's territory."

Beatrix didn't turn around, but Odile saw her shoulders set. "Then we're in yours," she said. "Wren's just lending you the pen."

* * *

The first crevasse found them an hour later, and it found them the way they all find you: silently, from below, disguised as nothing at all.

Beatrix's probe pole went through what looked like solid snow-bridge with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and she froze, weight already rocked back, one arm out for balance. "Bridge," she said. "Marked?" Odile shook her head — it wasn't on Wren's map either, which meant it was new, which meant the glacier had moved since the first expedition, which meant every dotted line on her board was a suggestion rather than a promise. They spent forty minutes cutting steps around it and planting a wand, and no one spoke during any of it except to call distances.

After that, the crosses on Wren's map began to make sense. There were dozens of them, clustered like insect eggs along both margins of the corridor, and where Wren had marked them nine years ago, the ice had often obliged by opening exactly there — as if the dead woman's pencil had been a kind of prophecy. But there were new ones too, bridges that had thinned, old crossings that had yawned wide, and twice Odile's route deviated from Wren's entirely, threading between hazards that hadn't existed when the first expedition came through.

At the second deviation, Voss drifted over to look at her board. He studied it for a long moment, gloved finger hovering above the dotted lines without touching them.

"She was good," he said. It wasn't clear whether he meant it for Odile or for himself.

"She was better than good." Odile kept her voice flat. "These marginal notes — the ones about listening for storms, trusting the blue ice. That's not survey technique. That's intimacy. She knew this glacier like a person."

"She did." Voss's jaw worked once. "Better than any of us."

He walked away before Odile could decide whether that was grief or guilt, and decided, as she usually did with Voss, that it was both, and that the ratio was none of her business yet.

* * *

The whiteout came on the third day, and it came like a lid closing.

There was no warning worth the name — a thickening of the light at noon, a softening of the shadows, and then, within the space of an hour, the world simply ceased to have edges. Sky and ice became the same white. The valley walls dissolved. Odile stood at the front of the rope team and could not see Beatrix ten feet ahead of her except as a smudge, and then not even as that — only the tow-line rising out of the murk, and beyond it nothing, a whiteness so total it pressed on the eyes like cotton packed against them.

This was the hour Odile's maps earned their keep.

She couldn't see the terrain, so she became the terrain's memory. Distance by the metronome count she kept in her head — she'd calibrated her pace against the wheel on the flats and now trusted it more than her eyes. Bearing by compass, checked against the corridor's axis, corrected for the drift she knew the katabatic wind would try to push on them. Elevation by the aneroid, read by feel because her fingers were too cold for fine work. And underneath all of it, holding the whole fragile structure together, Wren's notes: *the corridor bends left at the twin seracs; if you lose the seracs, trust the bearing, not the slope — the slope lies in fog.*

"Left bend in four hundred meters," Odile called forward, voice flattened by the white so that it seemed to travel nowhere and everywhere. "Count it with me."

They counted. Four hundred meters of nothing, each step identical, each step a small act of faith in arithmetic. At three-eighty Beatrix's probe found the rise of the bend, and at four hundred the tow-line swung gently left, and Odile felt the whole team follow her numbers around a corner none of them could see, and it was the finest navigation of her life and absolutely invisible, performed inside a ping-pong ball, witnessed by no one.

Tam started laughing somewhere in the middle of it — a thin, slightly unhinged sound — and Ferro reached back and squeezed his shoulder once, and after that nobody talked, and the counting carried them through.

They camped that night in the lee of a serac the size of a chapel, and the wind sang in the ice around them, high and eerie, a note that rose and fell. Odile lay in her bag listening to it and remembered Wren's marginal scrawl: *Wind sings in the second moraine before a storm. Listen.* This wasn't the moraine. But the singing was the same singing. She crawled out into the dark, checked the barometer twice, and then went and woke Beatrix.

"How bad?" Beatrix asked. No preamble. She'd been asleep for perhaps twenty minutes.

"Maybe nothing. Maybe a day of delay." Odile hugged her arms inside her coat. "But Whitlock wrote that the wind sings before weather, and it's singing, and I'd rather be wrong awake than right surprised."

Beatrix looked at her for a moment in the lamplight. Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth exactly, but the recognition of competence, which among people like them was worth more. "Wake the others," she said. "Double the anchors."

The storm arrived four hours later, and it was not a day of delay.

* * *

For thirty-one hours they did not move.

The katabatic wind came down off the icefields above them like something poured, dense and screaming, and the temperature fell so fast that the tent fabric stiffened and the guy-lines rang like guitar strings. They lay in a two-tent pile with the sledge lashed hard against the serac and took turns holding the poles, because the alternative was watching the poles snap. Somewhere in the second night a section of the serac calved off with a crack like the sky breaking, and the blast of displaced air flattened the windward tent flat against them all, and Ferro held the frame with both hands and his whole body while the canvas thundered, and afterward he looked around at their faces in the lamplight and shrugged, as if to say: *this is fine.*

Nobody slept much. Nobody starved, either — barely. They melted snow on two stoves shielded by their bodies, ate cold ration paste, drank water that tasted of iron and glacier flour. Merrick recited data to himself in a low voice, latitudes and pressures and dates, a rosary of numbers. Tam, gray-faced and shivering, asked Odile how she could possibly know where they were when they couldn't see six feet.

"I don't know where we are," Odile told him. "I know where we *were*, and I know we haven't moved. On a glacier, that's most of knowing where you are."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is humility."

On the second morning the wind dropped as abruptly as it had risen, and they dug themselves out into a world remade. A foot of fresh snow lay over everything, smooth and blue-shadowed, erasing their tracks, erasing their wands — half the markers gone, snapped off or buried. The corridor ahead had changed shape overnight; a slumped section of the south wall had shed debris across a quarter mile of the route, and the crevasse field beyond it, the one Wren's map showed as a single tidy cluster of crosses, had widened and merged into something that looked, from the ridge where they stood assessing it, like a broken window.

Merrick said what everyone was thinking. "We can't cross that."

"We can," Beatrix said.

"On Wren's map it's fifty yards of cracks. That's two hundred yards of *abyss*."

"That's why we're not using Wren's map for this part." Beatrix turned to Odile. "Can you thread it?"

Odile studied the field for a long time. The sun was out, hard and clean, and the shadows in the crevasses were deep enough to read — that was the one mercy, the one argument for going now rather than waiting for cloud. She could see the bridges, or guess at them: the drifts of fresh snow lying smooth over gaps, the faint sag lines where a bridge was thin, the wind-scoured ribs where the ice stood proud and might be trusted. It was like reading a page written in a language she almost knew.

"There's a line," she said slowly. "Diagonal, north to south, along the wind-scoured spine. It'll be slow. Probe every step. If anyone rushes, anyone dies." She looked at the team, at Tam's frightened face and Ferro's steady one and Merrick's calculating one, and heard herself add: "And if I say stop, we stop, even if stopping means standing on a bridge the width of a coffin until dark. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Beatrix, and one by one the others said it too, and Voss, who had been silent at the edge of the group, said it last, and quietest.

* * *

The crossing took five hours and cost Odile ten years.

She went first, probing, placing each foot only where the pole had already sworn the ice would hold. Behind her the team moved in her exact footsteps, a human chain stitched across the broken window of the glacier. Twice the pole went through and they rerouted, backing up along the spine with their hearts in their throats. Once a bridge boomed — a deep, resonant *whump* that Odile felt through her entire skeleton — and they stood motionless on it for a full minute, five people balanced over a drop whose bottom the lamplight of imagination could not find, until the ice decided to keep them.

At the worst of it, halfway across, Tam's leg punched through to the thigh.

He didn't scream, which saved him; screaming makes people move, and moving kills. He went rigid and said, in a small, terribly polite voice, "Odile," and Odile was already turning, already talking him through it — *don't pull, spread your arms, put your weight on the ice, let us come to you* — while Beatrix anchored and Ferro crawled forward on his belly with the spare rope coiled over his shoulder like a bandolier. It took eleven minutes to get him out. When they finally hauled him onto the spine and he lay there gasping, snow packed down his collar, the hole beneath him breathed out a gust of air from somewhere very far below, cold and mineral and old, the glacier's own exhalation, and Tam looked down into the blue dark and then deliberately looked away.

"Keep moving," Beatrix said gently. "You can shake later."

He kept moving. They all kept moving. And near dusk they came up the far side onto intact ice, and turned, and looked back at what they had crossed, and no one said anything at all, because there was nothing to say that the silence didn't say better.

That night, camped in the shadow of the great bend where the glacier turned toward the mountain's heart, Odile redrew the day's route onto her master sheet. Her hands were steady enough now. She inked the spine line in her own hand, solid stroke, surveyed fact — and then, because it seemed owed, she copied Wren's original crosses beside it, dotted, preserved, a record of what the ice had been and what it had become.

"You keep her marks," Beatrix said, appearing at her shoulder with two cups of tea. She handed one over.

"They're still true," Odile said. "Mostly. Just... translated."

Beatrix looked at the map for a while, at the neat lattice of a dead woman's caution rendered in living ink. "She'd have liked you," she said at last, and walked away before Odile could answer, which Odile understood was the point.

* * *

Two days later, rounding the shoulder of the bend, they found the cairn.

Tam saw it first — a man-made shape in a world with no right angles, a stack of stones waist-high on a rise of old ice, wind-polished but unmistakably built by hands. He stopped so suddenly that Ferro nearly walked into him, and pointed, and the whole team went still.

Odile knew what it was before anyone said it. She had known since the third night out of Corren's Rest, since the oilskin packet, since the marginal notes in that quick confident hand. She had been navigating toward it for days without letting herself think so.

The stones were fitted with care, the way a careful person builds when they know the building may be the last thing they do. And wedged into the top course, facing outward, was a flat slab of slate with letters scratched into it, half erased by nine years of wind but still legible, still patient:

**W.W. — FOR WHOEVER COMES AFTER. TAKE WHAT YOU NEED. LEAVE THE REST.**

No one moved. The wind moved — stirred the frost-feathers on the stones, hummed in the ice below — and the glacier went on groaning its long patient groan, and Beatrix Whitlock stood looking at her sister's handwriting from ten feet away as though the ten feet were a hundred miles.

"Beatrix," Odile said quietly.

Beatrix took one step. Stopped. Took another.

And Odile, watching her reach the cairn and lay her bare hand — bare, in that cold, as if it mattered, as if it were a handshake — on the stone, understood that whatever Wren Whitlock had left buried in this ice, they had arrived at the place where the map ended and the truth began.
