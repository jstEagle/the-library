## 10. Wren's Cache

The valley narrowed on the third day of the White Corridor, and the glacier began to speak.

Beatrix had crossed enough ice in her life to know the difference between a mountain that was merely cold and one that was awake. This one was awake. All through the night watches she heard it — a deep, arrhythmic groaning beneath their boots, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep, and every so often a crack that split the silence with the clean violence of a rifle shot. The others had stopped commenting on it by the second morning. You couldn't fight a sound like that. You could only walk and let it talk.

They came around a shoulder of blue ice just past noon, Odile in front with her map board strapped to her chest like a breastplate, and Beatrix saw the cairn before anyone else did.

She knew it the way you know your own handwriting on an envelope across a room.

It stood on a shelf of wind-scoured ice at the base of the serac wall — a rough tower of flat stones stacked with a care that no accident of geology could explain, maybe four feet high, weathered gray and furred with frost on the leeward side. Nine years of storms had been at it. It still stood. Wren had built things to last; she'd built them even when she knew she wouldn't be there to see how long they lasted.

Beatrix stopped walking. Behind her, Tam nearly walked into her pack, and Ferro made a low sound of inquiry, but she didn't answer any of them. She stood with the wind pulling at her hood and looked at a pile of stones her dead sister had set down with her own two hands, and for a long moment the glacier's groaning seemed to come from inside her own chest.

"Whitlock." Voss's voice, from the rear of the line. Flat. Careful. "What is it?"

She didn't turn around. "A cairn."

"Marked on your maps?"

"No."

Odile came up beside her, breath steaming, eyes going from the stones to Beatrix's face and back again. The cartographer was quick — quicker than Beatrix liked sometimes. "You know whose it is," Odile said. Not a question.

"It's mine," Beatrix said. "Ours. My family's." She unslung her pack without looking at any of them. "Camp here. I need an hour."

Voss started forward. "Whitlock—"

"I said I need an hour, Voss." She heard her own voice come out with an edge she hadn't meant to show, and she didn't take it back. "You've gotten nine years of my patience. You can give me sixty minutes of privacy on top of it."

The silence behind her stretched long enough that she could hear the ice ticking as the sun warmed its surface. Then Voss said, quietly, "Take two if you need them," and turned the crew back down the slope to find shelter from the wind.

* * *

She approached the cairn the way you approach a grave, which is to say slowly, and with the strange awareness that the person you're visiting isn't actually there.

Up close she could see Wren's work in every stone. Her sister had been the patient one, the precise one — the Whitlock who could mend a torn tent seam so you'd never find it, who sharpened knives to an edge that would shave hair, who kept her gear in an order that bordered on ceremony. Each stone in the cairn was seated against its neighbors with that same deliberate care, wedged and balanced so the wind couldn't worry them loose. Frost had welded them further. Time itself had become part of the construction.

There was a gap near the top. A hand-sized hollow between two larger stones, plugged with what had once been a leather-wrapped bundle and was now a frozen mass of hide and ice, fused to the rock around it like something grown rather than placed.

Beatrix knelt. Her knees cracked in the cold. She set her bare hands against the leather — she'd pulled off her gloves without deciding to — and the cold bit into her fingers immediately, a bright white pain, and she let it. It felt correct. It felt like paying something.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out small in all that white space. "I'm late."

The wind said nothing. The glacier groaned somewhere below the shelf, deep and indifferent.

She worked at the bundle with her knife then, chipping carefully at the ice collar where leather met stone, taking ten minutes over what would have taken thirty seconds with a hammer, because whatever was inside had survived nine years up here and she would not be the thing that broke it now. The cold climbed her wrists. Her fingers went from pain to numbness and she kept working, breathing through her teeth, until the last shell of ice fell away and the bundle came free in her hands with a sound like a tooth leaving its socket.

It was heavier than she expected. She carried it twenty paces downwind, out of the worst of the blowing spindrift, and sat on her pack with her back to the wind, and unwrapped it.

Inside the leather were three things.

A tin of matches, rusted shut. A folding knife with a bone handle that Beatrix recognized instantly — their father's, the one Wren had taken instead of the compass when they'd divided his effects, because Wren had always said the compass should go to the sister who wandered and the knife to the sister who stayed. Wren had been the one who wandered. The joke had been on both of them.

And a journal. Bound in oilcloth, swollen with moisture, its pages fused into a single block of ice-stiffened paper. Half-frozen. Half-destroyed. But whole enough.

Beatrix held it in both hands and did not open it.

This was the moment she'd been walking toward for nine years, and she found that she was afraid of it in a way she hadn't been afraid of crevasses or whiteouts or Voss himself. Because everything she knew about her sister's death fit inside a story she'd told herself a thousand times: instruments, science, a storm, bad luck, a good woman in the wrong place. The story was bearable. The story had a shape. And this journal was the one object in the world that could break the shape open and spill out whatever had actually happened up here — whatever Wren had seen, and built this cairn to keep, and died beside.

She thought about calling Voss up the slope. He'd been here. He knew. Whatever was in these pages, he already knew it, and he had chosen nine years of silence over telling her, and she hated him for it, and she understood him for it, and both of those things at once made her want to put the journal down and walk away and never come back.

Instead she opened it to the first page, and the ice-crusted paper cracked along its spine with a sound like a small bone breaking, and she began to read.

* * *

The early pages were almost ordinary. That was the cruelest part.

Wren wrote the way she talked — quick, wry, always slightly ahead of herself. *Day 1. Left Corren's Rest with eleven souls and too much optimism. Cassian counts everything twice and still forgets his own gloves, which tells you something about men who lead expeditions.* Beatrix read that line three times. Cassian. Voss had been there from the first page, from the first morning, and he had never said, not once in nine years, not even standing in her kitchen offering her a contract with her sister's ghost between them.

*Day 4,* the journal went on. *The porter Jules sings in his sleep. Merrick — new man, glaciologist, hired late — asks too many questions about what we're carrying and not enough about where we're putting our feet. Note to self: watch Merrick.*

Beatrix's breath caught. She looked up the slope toward the camp, where Merrick's orange tent pegged the white like a wound. Nine years ago Wren had marked the same man, and Beatrix had let him onto this glacier anyway, because Voss had vouched for him, because the money was right, because she'd stopped trusting her own instincts somewhere in those nine empty years and hadn't noticed until now.

She read on. The days accumulated. Weather notes. Route notes. Small jokes. *Ferro's engine coughs like a smoker but won't die. Odile draws maps faster than we can walk them.* Some of these people had been on the first expedition too. Ferro. Odile. Names she'd known for years, faces she'd shared fires with, and not one of them had ever said *we were here with your sister.*

Or — she turned a fused page carefully, warming it against her stomach until the fibers loosened — maybe they had tried, and Voss had stopped them. There was a line, Day 9: *Cassian has asked us all to keep the route quiet until we're home. Says the buyer wants secrecy. Says it protects the science. Odile thinks he likes being the only one holding the map. Both things are true, probably.*

The buyer. The word surfaced in Wren's handwriting like a body rising in dark water, and Beatrix felt the first true chill of the afternoon, colder than the wind, colder than the ice under her. Someone had paid for the first expedition. Someone had wanted something brought back from the Leeward Glacier badly enough to fund eleven people crossing it. And Voss had spent nine years making sure no one came back this way at all.

*Day 12.* The handwriting changed here — smaller, tighter, pressed hard into the paper. *We found it today.*

Beatrix stopped. Above her the serac wall threw a long blue shadow, and the light was failing, and she knew she should get back to camp before dark, and she did not move.

*I don't have words yet so I'll give you facts,* Wren had written. *Two miles past the serac field, where the old charts say there's nothing, there is a ship. A sailing ship. Masts still standing, hull whole, frozen into the ice face like a fly in amber. Odile says by the build of her she's two hundred years old, maybe more. Two hundred years, Bee. The sea is four hundred miles from here. Somebody sailed a tall ship up a desert valley and into a glacier, or the glacier moved four hundred miles, or the world is not the shape we were taught. Cassian stood in front of it for an hour and didn't say a word, and I have never seen him not have words.*

Beatrix looked up at the glacier.

All that white. All that groaning, patient weight. Somewhere inside it — ahead of them, two days' travel, if Voss's pace held — a ship. Sails and masts and tarred rope, centuries from any water that could float it. She thought of the compass in her pocket, its needle swinging leeward whenever they walked this valley, pointing not north but *toward*, and her skin crawled with the slow, cold understanding that the compass had never been broken. It had been pointing at this the whole time.

She read on. The entries shortened. The handwriting grew cramped, the ink thinning — they were running low on everything, fuel first, then food. *Day 15. Cargo hold is sealed. Cassian won't let anyone open it. He and the buyer's contract say salvage rights, but Cassian acts like a man guarding a wound. Merrick keeps circling the hold. I've started sleeping with the knife.*

*Day 17. Something is wrong with the crew. Jules won't go near the ship anymore. He says it hums. Ferro checked — no machinery aboard, nothing that could hum. Jules still hears it.*

*Day 18. Cassian told us tonight what the buyer really wants. I'm writing this at midnight because I can't sleep and because someone should know, in case. In case what, I don't know yet. But the hold isn't cargo. The hold is why the ship burned.*

Burned. Beatrix frowned at the word. The ship in the ice — she'd seen nothing of it, but Voss's briefing had spoken of a wreck, a hull, salvage. Nothing about fire.

The next page was fused solid. She worked it loose with her thumbnail, patient, gentle, her heart going hard and strange, and got it half-open, and the entry was short:

*Day 19. We voted. Eleven of us. What's in the hold cannot leave this glacier — not because it's valuable, though God knows it is, but because of what it does to people. Cassian says burn it. Merrick says the buyer will pay more than we'd see in ten lifetimes. The vote is tomorrow. I'm voting with Cassian. Whatever else he is, he's right about this, and I think he's the only one brave enough to do it.*

Below that, one more line, added later in different ink, hurried, the letters slanting downhill as if written while walking:

*Bee — if you're reading this, then I didn't come home, and you came looking anyway, which means you're exactly who I always said you were. Don't trust the fifth man. Trust the compass. It points leeward for a reason. — W*

Beatrix sat very still.

The wind had risen. Spindrift came off the serac wall in long white banners, and the light was nearly gone, and somewhere below the shelf the glacier let out a long descending groan, and she realized her hands were shaking — not from the cold, or not only from the cold.

*Don't trust the fifth man.* Eleven had left Corren's Rest. She counted backward through the journal's names — Voss, Wren, Odile, Ferro, Jules, Merrick, and four porters whose names she hadn't reached yet. Five officers and crew. A fifth man among them. And Merrick, who Wren had flagged on Day 4, who circled the hold, who argued for the buyer, who was at this moment fifty yards below her in an orange tent, warming his hands over their shared stove.

Nine years ago her sister had died up here securing something — that was what Voss had finally admitted at dawn, that partial truth wrung out of him like blood from a stone. Securing it. Burning it. And the buyer who had funded the first expedition had waited nine years and found another way in, and this time he'd bought himself a glaciologist.

Beatrix closed the journal. Wrapped it in the oilcloth. Held it a moment longer against her chest, feeling through the layers the faint irregular lump of the pages, like a heartbeat slowed almost to stopping.

"I'm here, Wren," she said. "I finished the walk."

Then she stood, stiff-legged, and turned down the slope toward the campfires, and the compass rode heavy in her pocket, its needle straining leeward, toward the ship, toward the hold, toward whatever her sister had died to keep buried — and toward the fifth man, whoever he was, who was sleeping warm not forty feet from where Beatrix would lay her own bedroll tonight.
