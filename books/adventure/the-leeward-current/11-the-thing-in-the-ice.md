## 11. The Thing in the Ice

Tam had counted the days wrong.

He'd been keeping a tally scratched into the handle of his ice axe — one stroke for every morning since Corren's Rest — and by his count they should have been two days from the wreck. But Odile stood at the head of the column that fourth morning with her map held flat against her knee and a look on her face that Tam had learned to read the way sailors read weather: something was ahead of them, or behind them, or both.

"Odile says we're close," Beatrix said, falling into step beside him. She said it lightly, but Tam had noticed she never said anything lightly anymore. Not since the cache. Not since she'd spent half a night alone with Wren's journal and come back to camp with her eyes like banked coals.

"Close how close?"

"Voss says today." She nodded up-valley. "The blue line on your map."

Tam looked. The glacier's surface had changed overnight — or seemed to have. Where for days it had been gray wind-scoured ice stitched with moraine, now the whole corridor ahead ran a deep, luminous blue, as if someone had poured dye into the mountain's veins. The light came *up* out of it. It made his eyes ache and he couldn't stop looking.

"That's not normal, is it?"

"Nothing about this is normal," Beatrix said. "Keep your crampons sharp."

The crew moved out in their usual order — Voss first, then Beatrix, then Tam roped between Ferro and Merrick, Odile navigating from the rear where she could see the whole line against the ice. The rope hummed taut between them when the grade steepened. Nobody talked much. Even the wind seemed to have gone somewhere else to blow, and in its absence Tam could hear the glacier itself: a long, slow groaning underfoot, like the hull of some enormous ship working in heavy seas.

That thought arrived without permission and would not leave.

They climbed through the morning. Twice they crossed pressure ridges where the ice had buckled into walls taller than Tam, blue on the sunward face and rotten gray on the shadow side, and twice Beatrix went ahead alone to test the footing while the rest of them stood breathing steam and watching her boots find holds none of the rest of them could see. On the second ridge Ferro put a hand on Tam's shoulder — just briefly, just enough weight to mean something — and pointed down-valley the way they'd come, then up toward whatever waited ahead, and shrugged as if to say: *same thing, different direction.* Tam grinned. He was still learning to read Ferro's hands, but he was getting faster.

It was near midday when Voss stopped.

He didn't call a halt. He simply stopped walking, so that Beatrix nearly walked into him, and stood looking up the valley with his head tilted like a man listening to music only he could hear. Then he took off his goggles.

Tam had never seen him do that. Above eight thousand feet, on bare ice, with the glare coming off the blue like a furnace door left open — nobody took off their goggles. Snow-blindness up here wasn't a risk; it was a schedule.

But then Tam crested the last rise behind him and understood.

The valley opened. That was the first thing — the walls fell away on either side and the glacier spread out into a broad white basin ringed by peaks, and the light in the basin was wrong, was *blue*, was coming up through the ice from below as though the whole floor of the world were a window and something enormous were standing underneath it, holding a lantern.

And there, in the middle of the basin, tilted maybe fifteen degrees, its bow buried to the hawseholes and its stern rising out of the ice like a rib picked clean —

— was a ship.

* *

Tam forgot the cold. He forgot the rope, forgot the tally on his axe handle, forgot his own name for a moment. He just stood there with his mouth open inside his balaclava and his breath fogging his goggles and stared.

It was a sailing ship. Not a steamer, not one of the new diesel trawlers he'd seen in the harbor at Corren's Rest as a boy — a sailing ship, three-masted, with the stumps of its yards still crossed and shrouds hanging in frozen festoons from the starboard rail. The hull was dark wood gone silver-black with age, and it rose perhaps forty feet out of the glacier at the stern, weathered smooth as driftwood, its transom carved — he could see it even at this distance, could see the letters, worn but legible, painted once in gold that time had stripped to ghost:

**SALACIA**

"How," Tam heard himself say. His voice came out small in all that space. "How is that *here*."

Nobody answered right away. The rope between him and Ferro sagged as the big mechanic came up beside him, and Tam felt rather than saw Ferro go still — the particular stillness of a man who fixes things for a living confronting a thing that cannot be fixed because it cannot be understood.

"It's four hundred miles inland," Merrick said. His voice had a strange texture to it, thin and fast. "Minimum. There isn't water within two hundred miles of this basin that could float a vessel that size. There hasn't been in — " He stopped. Tam glanced back and saw the glaciologist working his jaw like a man chewing on arithmetic too large to swallow. "The ice here is old. Older than the valley. If the ship were carried in by glacial flow it would have been ground to pulp. It would be *shredded*. It's intact. It's intact and upright and — "

"And it's been sitting here waiting for us," Voss said.

His voice did what it always did — cut through everything flat and cold, no room in it for wonder. But Tam was watching him closely now, had been watching him closely ever since the night of the burning papers, and he saw what the others might have missed: Voss's hands, wrapped around his ice axe, were shaking. Not much. Just enough.

Beatrix said nothing at all. She was looking at the ship the way Tam imagined a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

They descended into the basin in a long careful traverse, the blue light strengthening around them until everything — their faces, their ropes, the breath between words — was stained that impossible color. The ice underfoot changed too. It grew smoother, clearer, and Tam kept catching glimpses of depth beneath his boots: bubbles frozen in chains, a darkness far down that might have been shadow or might have been more ice, older ice, ice stacked on ice like pages in a closed book.

And then, forty feet from the hull, Odile called the halt, and Tam saw what the ice held closer in.

A body.

No — not a body. A shape. A man-shaped darkness suspended ten feet below the surface, arms out, coat ballooned around it, drifting downward through the centuries at a speed so slow it hadn't yet finished falling. The face was turned away. The clothes were wrong — high-collared, buttoned to the throat, a style from paintings in schoolbooks.

"Don't," Voss said quietly, when Merrick started forward. "Don't look at him. Look at the ship. We're here for the ship."

"We're here for *instruments*," Tam said, before he could stop himself. "That's what the contract says. That's what you told us."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Tam felt Ferro's hand settle on his shoulder again, heavier this time. *Easy.*

"Yes," Voss said. "Instruments." And walked on toward the hull.

* *

Up close, the *Salacia* was bigger than it had any right to be. The stern loomed over them like a cliff face, and the timbers — oak, Merrick breathed, *English oak, look at the grain* — were scarred and patched and furred with frost. A rudder the size of a barn door hung exposed above the ice, its pintles rusted to orange lace. Frozen spray — or frozen something — sheeted the lower hull where it met the glacier, welding ship and mountain into a single object, as if the sea had reached four hundred miles inland to claim its own and then forgotten to let go.

Ferro found the way in. Of course Ferro did. He circled the stern twice, tapping the planking with a knuckle, listening, and then pointed up — to a gash in the transom where a section of hull had been torn away, whether by rock or ice or something aboard, Tam couldn't guess. Beyond the gash, darkness. Beyond the darkness, the ship's interior, open to them like a throat.

"Ropes," Beatrix said. "Two points. Nobody goes in alone. Nobody wanders."

They went in through the gash in pairs, boots crunching on glassy frost, headlamps cutting cones through air so cold it crackled. Tam went third, after Voss and Beatrix, with Ferro's bulk solid behind him, and that ordering saved his sanity, because the interior of the *Salacia* was a place his mind kept trying to reject.

The orlop deck was intact. Intact and *furnished* — hammocks still slung, a lantern still hanging from its hook, a wooden tankard sitting on a barrel head as if set down a moment ago. Frost had taken everything, coated everything, turned the whole world below decks into white velvet, but the shapes beneath the frost were unmistakably, horribly ordinary. A game of draughts, half-played, the pieces frozen mid-board. A jacket folded over a chair back. A Bible open on a desk in the great cabin, its pages fused into a single milky block, a spectacles case beside it.

"It's like they left yesterday," Tam whispered. His voice bounced strangely in the frozen dark. "It's like they're coming back."

"They didn't leave," Merrick said from somewhere aft. His lamp swung wildly; he was moving too fast, touching things, reading things. "Look at the dates — I need the dates — the chronometer, if the chronometer survived — " His voice cracked upward into something almost like laughter. "Do you understand what this is? Do you understand what this *means*? The trade routes, the cartography, the entire — this ship predates every chart of this coast by a century, it can't be here, it *cannot be here*, and yet — "

"Ferro," Beatrix said. "Get him moving forward or get him quiet."

They worked aft through the frozen ship, and Tam made himself do what Beatrix had told them: look, catalog, don't feel. Cargo hold next. The hold was where the ship's story lived, and the ship's story was written in emptiness — row upon row of cradles built to carry barrels, crates, casks, and nearly all of them empty, the wood pale and unstained where cargo should have pressed it dark. Whatever the *Salacia* had carried across an ocean no chart remembered, most of it was gone.

Except the stern-most compartment.

That door was shut. Shut and barred — from the outside, Tam realized, staring at the iron bar across it. Someone had locked that compartment and thrown away the key, or wanted very much for people to think they had.

Voss stood in front of the barred door for a long time. Long enough that Tam's toes started to burn inside his boots. Long enough that the others drifted close without meaning to, drawn the way people are drawn to a wound.

"You knew," Tam said. It came out of him before he could weigh it. "You knew it was here. You've known for nine years."

Voss didn't turn around. "I knew it was here," he agreed.

"And the instruments? The scientific station? The — everything you told us in Corren's Rest?"

"I told you what needed telling to get good people across bad ice." Now he turned, and his face in the lamplight was old and gray and tired in a way Tam had never seen on him. "I'm not going to apologize for that. I am going to ask you to trust me for another day. One day. Then you'll know everything, and you can hate me with the full picture in hand."

"Sir," Tam said, and was surprised to hear how steady his own voice was, "we already know everything. We know you burned papers. We know you knew my — " he caught himself, glanced at Beatrix, whose face gave nothing away, "— we know you knew people on the first expedition. We followed you onto a glacier on a lie. The picture's already pretty full."

For a moment Tam thought Voss was going to answer. Behind him, Ferro shifted his weight with a soft squeak of leather, and Beatrix's hand moved — not to her knife, exactly, but to the neighborhood of her knife.

What broke the moment was Merrick.

Merrick had stopped listening. Merrick had, in fact, stopped being part of the group at all — he had drifted along the bulkhead during the exchange, lamp raised, and now he made a sound. A small sound. A sound like a man finding a coin he'd lost years ago in the lining of an old coat.

"There's writing," he said. "Under the frost. On the door."

They crowded in. The compartment door was iron-banded oak, and beneath a skin of frost, scratched deep into the wood with something sharp — a knife, a nail, desperate fingers — ran lines of text. Old-fashioned lettering, laborious, the work of someone writing in terrible conditions with a dying light. Merrick brushed the frost away with his sleeve, gentle as a priest, and read aloud, his voice gathering speed:

"*We have done what can be done. The fire would not take it all. God forgive us, we were sent to fetch it and we have instead — *"

The sentence stopped. Not faded — stopped, mid-word, the last letter a gouge that had split the plank.

Below it, in a different hand, smaller and harder, someone had added a single line:

"*BURNED FROM WITHIN. DO NOT OPEN. DO NOT CARRY IT OUT.*"

* *

The silence in the hold was total. Tam could hear his own pulse in his ears, could hear the glacier groaning somewhere far above them through forty feet of ice, could hear — or thought he could hear, and would spend the rest of his life unsure — a faint settling creak from the ship's bones, like a sleeper turning over.

Then Beatrix lifted her lamp higher, and the light climbed the compartment door, and everyone saw it at the same moment.

The scorch marks.

They ran up from the seams of the door like black ivy — not the soft gray charring of a fire fed from outside, but hard-edged, *interior* burns, the wood blistered outward, blown outward, as if the fire had been born in the sealed dark of that room and clawed at the door trying to get out. The iron bar across it was warped. The hinges were fused. Whatever had burned in that compartment had burned hot enough to warp iron and had done it *inside a locked box*, and the men who scratched warnings into the door had done it afterward, in the knowledge of it, in the fear of it.

"Scorched from the inside," Odile said softly. She had come up the line silent as snowfall, and her voice in the frozen hold was the worst thing Tam had heard yet, because it wasn't afraid. It was *taking notes*. "The whole hull will show it, if we look. Fire doesn't start in a sealed compartment without help. And it doesn't burn that hot on ship's stores." She turned her lamp on Voss. "What was the cargo, Cassian?"

"The cargo," Voss said, "is why I swore off this route. Why I swore the rest of you onto it. And why, in about six hours, we are leaving."

"But Wren — " Tam started, and stopped, because Beatrix had gone rigid beside him.

She was staring at the scorch marks. At the smallest of them, low on the door, near the frost line. Tam followed her gaze and saw it: a mark in the char, deliberate, cut into the blistered wood with a blade. Small. A curve and a cross-stroke.

A compass rose. Drawn the way you'd draw it in a margin. Drawn the way someone draws it who has grown up watching a sister's needle swing leeward.

"She was here," Beatrix said. Her voice was very quiet and very level, which Tam had learned, in the weeks since Corren's Rest, was the most dangerous register she owned. "Nine years ago. She stood exactly where I'm standing, and she looked at this door, and she marked it." Her lamp hand did not shake. Everything else about her did. "Voss. What did she find in there?"

Above them, muffled by forty feet of ice, the glacier let out a long groan — and this time it didn't stop. This time the groan deepened, gathered, became a rumble that Tam felt in his teeth, in his chest, in the soles of his boots, and dust sifted down through the lamplight from the frozen beams overhead.

Voss looked up at the ceiling of the world.

"Ask her yourself," he said. "Because unless I'm wrong, she left the answer in the one place we haven't looked. And I think the mountain just told us our time to look is running out."
