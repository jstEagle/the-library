## 12. Voss's Confession

The wind had died sometime in the night, and the silence it left behind was worse.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the blue ice where the ship lay embedded like a splinter in a wound, and she listened to that silence. It was not empty. It was the silence of a held breath — the whole glacier waiting to see what they would do next. Behind her, the camp was quiet too: Tam asleep in his bag with his boots still laced, Ferro sitting awake by the stove as always, Odile bent over her charts by lamplight, and Merrick —

Merrick was in his tent, and Beatrix found herself counting the seconds between the sounds he made. He'd been careful since the wreck. Too careful. She hadn't told anyone yet what she suspected, because suspicion without proof was just another kind of weight, and everyone on this ice was already carrying more than their legs could hold.

Voss came out of the command tent at first light, or what passed for first light this far into the white corridor — a bruised gray glow along the eastern seracs, no sun, just the idea of one. He looked older than he had in Corren's Rest. The cold did that. It stripped men down to whatever was true underneath.

"Whitlock." His voice was rough from three days of storm-wind. "Walk with me."

She fell into step beside him without answering. They walked along the flank of the hull, where the ice had been cut back by the first expedition's tools — old cuts, nine years healed over with fresh freeze, but visible if you knew where to look. Voss knew where to look. He walked the line of them like a man visiting graves.

"You've been waiting for this," Beatrix said.

"Nine years."

"Don't flatter yourself. I've been waiting since Tam told me you said her name."

Voss stopped. His breath hung in the air between them, a small gray ghost that dissolved before either of them could look away from it.

"Wren," he said. Not a question. A surrender.

"Say it properly."

He turned to face her fully then, and Beatrix saw something she had never seen on Cassian Voss's face in all the years she'd known him — not when he'd outbid her for the Corren contract, not when he'd testified against her survey methods and gotten her license suspended for a season, not even now, standing in front of a ship that should not exist. She saw fear. Not of the ice. Of her.

"The first expedition wasn't scientific," he said. "You know that already. You've known since the cache."

"I know your story has holes in it big enough to drive a sledge through. I want the whole thing. Every piece. And Voss—" she stepped closer, close enough to see the frost in his eyebrows, "—if I catch you shaping it, editing it, making yourself smaller in it than you were, I will know. I spent nine years learning how people lie about the dead."

He nodded slowly. Then he looked past her, up the glacier, toward the dark seam in the ice where the hull rose out of the blue like the ribcage of something drowned.

"It started with a buyer," he said. "Same buyer, Whitlock. The one who funded us is the one who funded this. Old money, older name. He collects things that shouldn't exist. Instruments, manuscripts, artifacts — and once, a very long time ago, he tried to buy a story off a dying sailor about a ship that went into the ice two hundred years ago, impossibly far inland, carrying cargo that burned."

Beatrix felt the cold move through her coat as though it had been waiting for permission. "Cargo that burned."

"That's what the sailor said. That the crew set fire to their own hold rather than let it reach port." Voss's jaw worked. "The buyer thought it was delirium. A good story, worth owning, nothing more. But he kept the account. And forty years later, when satellite surveys picked up a mass anomaly under the Leeward Glacier — something long, something shaped like a keel — he remembered the story. And he decided to go looking."

"And you took his money."

"I took his money." No flinch. He gave her that much honesty, at least. "Eight of us. Wren was our glaciologist — the best field reader I ever worked with, better than Merrick on his best day, and Merrick knows it, which is half of why he hates being here. We found the ship in six weeks. We were supposed to catalog it, extract samples, mark the site for a proper recovery team the following season."

"But?"

Voss walked again, and she followed, and he led her to the place where the cut ice opened onto the exposed section of hull — the scorched planking, blackened from within, the burn pattern that had curdled Tam's wonder into something quieter. He put his gloved hand against the char, gently, the way you'd touch a coffin lid.

"But Wren got inside first."

* * *

The interior of the wreck was accessible through a gash in the starboard side where the ice had flexed and split the planking — a gap barely wide enough for a slim person with a small pack. Wren had been both. Voss didn't take Beatrix inside; he stood at the mouth of the gash and told the rest with his back half-turned, as if he couldn't say it while looking at her.

"She went in on day thirty-nine. Alone. She said she wanted clean readings and the rest of us breathed too much. That was Wren." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh in some other life. "She was in there four hours. When she came out, she wasn't the same. You know how she got when she'd decided something. That look. Like a door closing."

"I know the look," Beatrix said quietly.

"She showed us the hold." His voice dropped. "There were crates down there, sealed with pitch, marked in a language none of us read — and beneath the markings, burned into the wood itself, symbols that predated any language we did read. And the crates weren't empty. They hummed. Not sound — you felt it in your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes. Wren said the wood around them had grown strange. Rings gone wrong. Grain spiraling. Whatever was in those crates had been working its way outward for two centuries, slow as frost, patient as frost."

"And the scorching—"

"Came later. After we understood what we'd found." Voss finally turned. "The buyer's advance included a radio schedule. Weekly position reports, encrypted. Wren cracked the cipher on day forty-one — she was better at that than any of us knew, she'd learned it from your father, did you know that? — and she read the traffic between the buyer and his man in the capital. Recovery contracts. Museum negotiations. A private facility being built to house the collection. And one line, repeated three times across different messages, that she memorized and then burned the paper it was written on."

Beatrix's heart was a slow, heavy drum. "What line?"

"'The cargo must arrive intact. Personnel are expendable.'"

The wind chose that moment to return, a low moan running the length of the valley, and neither of them moved until it passed.

"There were eight of us," Voss said. "Wren told five. She didn't tell three. One of those three was our expedition doctor, Aldous Ren. He'd been the buyer's man from the start — hired on, paid extra, reporting through the cipher. When Wren announced she was going to burn the hold, Ren went for the radio. Ferro—" Voss caught himself, corrected. "Our mechanic. A woman named Sefa, Ferro's mother, actually. She broke Ren's wrist and wired the radio dead. And then eight people stood on the ice and voted."

"What was the vote?"

"Six to two. Burn it." He said it flatly, but his hands weren't flat; they were fists inside his mitts. "Ren and the porter, Ives, voted to preserve. Six of us carried fuel oil down into the hold over two days and soaked every crate, every timber around them, everything. Wren rigged the ignition herself. She wouldn't let anyone else do it. She said—" and here Voss's voice finally fractured, a hairline crack running through nine years of stone, "—she said whoever lit it should know exactly what they were burning. She stayed to make sure it caught. The fire drew the oxygen out of the gash faster than anyone calculated. The flashover came up through the hull like a breath. She was twenty meters inside."

Beatrix did not remember deciding to sit down. She was simply on the ice, knees folded, the compass a hard point against her sternum through her coat, and Voss was kneeling across from her, and neither of them spoke for a long time. Above them the seracs stood in their ancient patience. Somewhere deep in the glacier, ice settled with a sound like a door closing far away.

"She built the cairn before she went in," Beatrix said at last. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, which felt like a betrayal of its own. "I found it. I found her journal."

"I know. I led you there. Odile's maps come from my memory of the route — I let her believe she'd reconstructed it from fragments." He rubbed his face with his mitt. "That's the second thing I owe you truth for. There have been so many I've stopped counting."

"Why bring us here at all? If you swore it would stay buried. If you spent nine years making sure no one came back." She looked up at him. "Why reopen it?"

"Because someone else did." Voss's eyes went hard. "Eight months ago, a survey company filed an exploratory permit for the Leeward corridor. Shell company. I traced it in six weeks — same lawyers, same bank signatures, same buyer. He never stopped. He just waited for me to age, for the route records to decay, for the people who remembered to die off one by one. Sefa died two winters ago. Ives is in a sanatorium. Ren vanished in '19 and I used to hope someone had found him, and now I only hope no one ever does." He exhaled. "So I made a choice. If he was going to dig up the wound, I would get there first, find out what remained, and decide — one last time — whether it stays buried."

"And what remains?"

"Char," Voss said. "Ash fused into the ice. The hull timbers are saturated with it. Whatever was in those crates, Wren killed it — or unmade it, or sent it wherever such things go. But the buyer doesn't know that. He thinks the fire failed, or was exaggerated, or that enough survived to be worth a fortune. And so he did what he always does. He bought himself a way in."

Beatrix felt it land before he finished saying it — the sentence arriving in her body ahead of her mind, the way a crevasse bridge announces itself through the soles of your boots a half-second before it gives.

"Merrick," she said.

"Merrick." Voss nodded once. "His fellowship was revoked the same year the shell company was formed. His debts were bought by the same bank. He thinks he's a scientist recovering knowledge. He's a crow picking at a grave for a man who will melt him down for parts the moment he's served his purpose."

* * *

They walked back to camp separately, ten paces apart, because Beatrix needed the distance to think and Voss, to his credit, seemed to understand that.

The camp looked small and human against the immensity of the ice — four tents, the stove's thin ribbon of smoke, Odile's flag lines snapping in the returning wind. Tam was up, hauling the morning's water blocks, and he waved when he saw her, and Beatrix waved back, and the wave cost her more than anything had cost her in nine years. Because now she knew what was coming. Now she knew that somewhere in that little cluster of canvas, a man was reporting their position on a schedule, and that the buyer's reach extended all the way out here, into the white silence, into the one place on earth where Wren's sacrifice had actually held.

Odile met her at the chart table. The cartographer's eyes were sharp and sleepless. "You talked to him."

"Nine years' worth of talking."

"And?"

Beatrix looked at the map spread between them — the corridor behind them, the calving front ahead, the red-inked line through the icefall that Wren had drawn with hands that had hours left to live. She thought about telling Odile everything. She thought about the record, the truth, the legend of Wren Whitlock burned down to its honest timber. She thought about Tam, who had looked at that scorched hull with wonder, and what the truth would plant in him.

"Later," she said. "First we deal with the rat."

Odile's gaze flicked toward Merrick's tent, then back. "You knew."

"I suspected. Now I know. His debts, his funding, his permit history — it all runs through the same bank that owns the shell company that filed for this corridor." She kept her voice level, pitched under the wind. "He's been transmitting. Voss checked the wreckage of the first camp yesterday while we were walking. Found a stripped wire, a hidden antenna lead. Someone reactivated it during the storm."

Odile was quiet for a moment. "Then he knows we know is coming eventually. Which means we move before he does."

"No." Beatrix folded the map closed with deliberate slowness. "We let him think he's safe. We let him send one more report. And we write that report ourselves."

Across the camp, Merrick emerged from his tent, blinking into the gray light, a thermos in his hand, smiling his thin academic smile. "Morning," he called. "Weather window looks like it might open tomorrow. Should we discuss the extraction plan?"

"Yes," Beatrix said pleasantly. "Let's discuss exactly what we're taking out of here."

And beside her, she felt Odile go very still, and felt Voss watching from the tent flap, and understood that the heaviest thing any of them would carry off this glacier was not going to fit in a crate.

It was going to be the truth — and someone on this ice was already paid to steal it.
