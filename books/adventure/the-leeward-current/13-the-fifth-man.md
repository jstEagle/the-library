## 13. The Fifth Man

Odile Marchetti had spent thirty years reading landscapes, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other: the land never lied, but the people on it did.

She was crouched at the mouth of her tent at first light, breath fogging, a pencil behind her ear and Wren Whitlock's recovered notes spread across her knees, when she noticed the wrongness for the third time.

It was small. It was always small. That was what made it dangerous.

Merrick's tent was pitched forty paces downslope, angled away from the wind like everyone else's — but its guy line ran to a boulder that sat slightly apart from the others, and the snow around that boulder had been disturbed. Not trampled. Disturbed. A neat arc of scuffed crystals, as if someone had knelt there and turned.

She had seen it two nights ago and told herself it was nothing. She had seen it again last night, fresh, and told herself it was meltwater. There was no meltwater at nine thousand feet in shadow. She knew better than to lie to herself; it was the first thing they taught you in survey school, back before the survey schools stopped teaching anything worth learning.

So this morning she did not tell herself anything. She got up, stoked the stove, made coffee for six, and watched.

Merrick emerged from his tent at 06:40, exactly as he had every morning since the wreck. He was a precise man — precise in his instruments, his boots, his little ritual of tapping the frost from his parka with three strokes of each hand. He ate standing up. He checked the barometer twice. And then, while the others broke camp, he walked toward the latrine trench, which lay east.

The boulder lay west.

Odile marked the page she was on, closed the journal, and followed him.

* * *

He didn't go to the boulder. Of course he didn't. He went to the trench, did what men do at trenches, and came back whistling something tuneless through his teeth. But on the way back he passed within arm's reach of the rock, and his hand brushed it — casually, the way a man steadies himself on ice — and Odile saw the glint.

A wire. Hair-fine, copper-colored, running from the underside of the boulder's overhang down into a drift, and from the drift into Merrick's tent.

Her heart did not speed up. That surprised her. Thirty years of expeditions had taught her body that panic was a luxury billed later, with interest. Her heart simply noted the fact and kept working, like a good instrument.

She waited until the crew was hauling sledge harnesses and Voss was shouting about rope coils, and then she drifted past the boulder the way cartographers drift everywhere — eyes down, mind elsewhere, harmless. She knelt as if adjusting a crampon strap.

Under the overhang, wedged in a socket of old ice, sat a transmitter.

Not a radio. A beacon. Compact, military-ugly, its casing wrapped in tape against the cold, an antenna no thicker than the wire she'd spotted snaking away through the drift. Its power cell was the size of a fist and it hummed faintly, a sound you'd never hear unless you were lying in the snow with your ear to the ground, which is precisely where Odile now was.

There was a dial on its face. She couldn't read the markings without her glasses, but she could read the wear: the dial had been turned recently, often. Someone had been adjusting it every night, walking out here in the dark, kneeling in the cold to keep a promise made in some warm office very far from the ice.

Someone had been telling the world exactly where they were.

Odile stood, brushed the snow from her knee, and went to find Beatrix Whitlock.

* * *

She found her at the supply sledge, checking lashings with the economy of a woman who had done ten thousand of them. Voss stood nearby arguing with Tam about the weight distribution, and Ferro was under the engine housing doing whatever mute communion Ferro did with machines. Good. Witnesses, but not too many.

"Ranger," Odile said quietly. "Walk with me."

Beatrix looked up. Whatever she saw in Odile's face made her set down the lashing without finishing it. "How far?"

"Just past the tents."

They walked. The glacier groaned beneath them, that long tectonic complaint it made every morning as the sun warmed the upper ice, and Odile realized she would never hear that sound the same way again. For eleven days it had been the mountain talking. Now it sounded like a door left open.

"There's a beacon under the split boulder west of camp," she said. "Shortwave. Battery powered. It's been transmitting our position since at least the river crossing — I'd stake my maps on it. The dial gets adjusted nightly." She paused. "Merrick's tent is the only one wired to it."

Beatrix said nothing for four full strides. Odile counted them. Then: "You're sure it's not yours. Survey equipment."

"I know every piece of kit on this expedition because I packed half of it and weighed the other half. That unit isn't ours." Odile kept her voice level. "I've been turning it over since dawn, trying to find another explanation. There isn't one. A man doesn't kneel in the dark every night to adjust a machine he doesn't know is there."

"Merrick." Beatrix said the name the way you'd spit out grit. "The disgraced glaciologist. The one Voss vouched for."

"The one Voss vouched for," Odile agreed. "Which means either Voss is a fool or Voss is part of it, and Cassian Voss has been many things in his life but I've never once believed him a fool."

They stopped at the edge of camp. Ahead, the white corridor stretched north toward the calving fields, beautiful and indifferent, and somewhere behind them the ship sat in its blue tomb with its scorched hull and its cargo nobody should ever own. Odile thought about how many times she had walked past Merrick at meals. How he'd asked her once, politely, about their route through the icefall — just professional curiosity, he'd said. Just a man wanting to understand the map.

"He asked me about the red route," she said. "Two nights ago. By the fire. Casual as anything." Her jaw tightened. "I told him it was Wren's line. I told him it was the only way through if the corridor closed. I handed him everything he needed to sell us, and I liked him while I did it."

"You didn't know."

"I never know. That's the job. You read the land because the land can't be bribed." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and let it stand. "People are the only terrain I've never learned to trust."

Beatrix was watching Merrick across the camp. He was helping Tam coil a rope, patient, competent, showing the boy a knot with the air of a teacher. He looked like nothing at all. That was the worst part. Forty years of expedition horror stories and the traitor never once looked like a story. He looked like a colleague.

"How do you want to play it?" Odile asked. "We could take him now. Tie him, take the beacon, say nothing until we're off the ice."

"And if he has friends coming?" Beatrix's eyes stayed on the glaciologist. "If someone's been following that signal for days, we need to know before we decide what he's worth to them. No. We confront him. Here. Now. With everyone watching, so there's nowhere this goes sideways without witnesses."

"That's not the cautious play."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "But I've been cautious my whole life and it hasn't paid out yet."

* * *

They did it at midday, when the light was flat and everyone was in camp. Voss objected — Odile watched him object, watched the anger come off him in waves, and filed away the question of whether the anger was real — but Beatrix overrode him with five words: "Your vouching got us here."

Merrick stood by the cold stove with the beacon on the table between them, retrieved from its icy socket by Ferro, who had carried it back with the expressionless care of a man transporting a scorpion. The whole crew ringed the tent. Outside, the wind moved across the ice and the glacier ticked and settled, and inside, the silence had weight enough to bend the canvas walls.

"Do you know what this is?" Beatrix asked.

Merrick looked at the device for a long moment. Odile studied his hands. They were steady. Whatever else he was, he wasn't a man who startled easily, and she found she respected that even as it chilled her.

"It's a position beacon," he said. "Shortwave pulse, encoded grid reference, adjustable interval. Standard salvage-tracking hardware." He lifted his eyes. "I imagine you found it near my tent."

"We found your tent wired to it," Odile said. "Every night, adjustments. Every day, questions about our route. I want to hear you say it, Dr. Merrick. Who are you transmitting to?"

For the first time, something moved behind his face — not guilt, exactly. Weariness. The look of a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten which shoulder it lived on.

"A collector," he said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Tam, at the back, actually leaned forward.

"His name won't mean anything to you," Merrick went on. "He uses three or four. The one I was given is Aldous Ren. He's a buyer of antiquities. Rare materials. Things that shouldn't exist and fetch prices because they shouldn't." His gaze traveled around the circle, touching each of them. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending I was coerced. I was hired. Eight months ago. My debts were cleared, my reputation was to be restored, and my task was simple: accompany the expedition, confirm the site, and transmit coordinates at fixed intervals so that a recovery team could follow before the route sealed."

"The same buyer," Voss said. His voice came out low and rough, and Odile heard something in it she had never heard in twenty years of knowing the man's reputation — fear. "Say the name again."

"Aldous Ren."

Voss turned away. He pressed both fists against the table and hung his head over the beacon like a man looking into open water, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet enough that everyone had to lean in to catch it.

"Ren funded the first expedition. Nine years ago. His money put twelve people on the Leeward Glacier." He straightened slowly. "Wren Whitlock died keeping his cargo out of his hands. Six others died in the attempt. And now his money has been sitting in my crew for eleven days, drawing me a map to the exact spot I swore on their graves I would never lead anyone to."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the ice calved far to the south — a deep report, like artillery, rolling up the valley and dying among the peaks.

"So the sabotage," Beatrix said. Her voice was terrible in its calm. "The radio. Was that you?"

"Yes." Merrick didn't flinch. "After the storm began, Ren's instructions changed. No recovery team could move in weather like this. I was ordered to make sure none of you could call out either — no rescue, no early extraction, no chance anyone reaches the coast with word of what's here before his people arrive. We were meant to be isolated until the corridor opened." He paused. "I repaired the antenna joint myself after Ferro's fix. Badly. Enough to keep it dead."

Tam made a small sound. Odile glanced at him — the boy looked physically ill, and she remembered that it had been Tam and Ferro who'd crawled out into the storm to mend the radio in the first place, who'd frozen their fingers on a machine this man had already murdered.

"Why?" It was Beatrix again. "Why tell us any of this? Why not lie?"

"Because you'd have found out anyway." Merrick nodded toward Odile. "She reads terrain. I've watched her do it for eleven days. And because—" he exhaled, and for one moment the precision cracked and he was only a tired man on the wrong side of the world— "because I've seen the ship. I've stood inside it. I read enough of the journal pages Voss confiscated to understand what burned in that hold, and I have spent every night since lying in my tent doing arithmetic about what Ren pays versus what Ren does to people who deliver." He met Beatrix's eyes. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you the recovery team is already moving. They were staged at the coast before we left Corren's Rest. When this storm breaks, they'll have my final transmission, and they'll be four days behind us on a route that will take us six."

The silence afterward had a different texture than the silence before. Before, they had been afraid of a traitor among them. Now they understood the traitor was the least of it — that the enemy had never been in the tent at all. The enemy was on the glacier behind them, and ahead of them, and folded into the money that had built the expedition from its first day.

Voss laughed. One short, awful syllable, with no humor anywhere in it.

"Eleven days," he said. "I assembled this crew. I vetted every man. I chose Merrick because his disgrace made him cheap and hungry and loyal to whoever fed him, and I told myself that was safe, because I was the one feeding him." He shook his head slowly. "I built the fifth man into my own expedition. Ren didn't even have to try."

"Then we change the arithmetic," Beatrix said.

Everyone turned to her. She had picked up the beacon and was turning it over in her gloved hands, studying the dial, the antenna, the worn adjustment marks — reading it the way Odile read a contour line.

"They're four days behind and following a signal." She looked up at Merrick. "Can you change what it says?"

He blinked. "Transmit false coordinates? Yes. The encoding is simple. But they'll verify against terrain eventually, and when the signal stops matching—"

"Then it buys us time, not safety. Time is what I'm asking for." She set the beacon down and looked around the circle, and Odile saw the thing in her face that had gotten them this far — not hope, exactly. Something harder and more useful than hope. "The corridor is closing. Odile's red route may already be dead. We have a wounded liar, a broken radio, a storm on the mountain, and a recovery team at our backs. So here's what happens next. Merrick transmits our true position one more time — tonight, on schedule, so Ren's people don't suspect a switch. After that, he transmits whatever I tell him to transmit, and if he deviates by one pulse, Ferro takes the beacon and I take his coat, and he can explain the cold to Ren's team himself."

Merrick held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man signing something.

"And the rest of us," Odile said quietly, "start planning the icefall. Tonight. Because if his numbers are right, we don't have six days on Wren's route anymore."

Outside, the wind rose. Far up-valley, the glacier answered it with a long, grinding groan, and Odile felt the sound in her teeth and understood, with a cartographer's certainty, that the mountain had heard them too — and that the red route was going to cost them everything they had left.
