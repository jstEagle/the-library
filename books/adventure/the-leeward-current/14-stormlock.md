## 14. Stormlock

The wind came down off the ice cap like something with a grudge.

Tam heard it before he felt it — a sound like a freight train running on no rails at all, somewhere above them in the dark. He was on watch in the lee of the wreck's hull, wrapped in two blankets and a fur hood, when the temperature dropped so fast his breath turned to gravel in his throat. Then the first gust hit, and he understood why Voss had made them anchor every line twice.

"Stormlock!" Beatrix's voice carried through the roar, flat and certain. "Everyone inside — now!"

He didn't need telling twice. The katabatic wind poured off the high glacier in a cold river, dense as water, and it found every gap in a man's clothing the way water finds cracks in stone. Tam stumbled the last thirty yards to the camp with the wind shoving between his shoulder blades, and then Ferro's big hands caught his pack straps and hauled him through the flap of the main tent into sudden, blessed stillness.

Inside, the world had shrunk to canvas walls and lamplight. Odile was already pinning the tent seams, checking stakes by feel. Voss sat against the supply crates with his eyes closed, conserving himself the way old men in the mountains learn to. Merrick huddled near the stove, hands out, glasses fogged white. And Beatrix stood at the entrance with her back half-turned, watching the fabric bulge and snap as though she could read the storm's intentions in it.

"How long?" Tam asked.

She looked at him. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, and for a second he saw how tired she actually was — how tired they all were, four days out from the wreck, five from the cache, nine years deep in whatever this expedition really was.

"Katabatics can run six hours," she said. "They can run six days. We find out which."

* * *

They found out it was going to be neither quick nor kind.

By the second hour the wind had buried the sledges to their crossbars. By the fourth, the guy lines sang — an actual note, a high keening C that Ferro identified by tapping the tent pole and holding up fingers until Tam nodded. The temperature inside the tent fell despite the stove, because the stove ate fuel, and fuel was now a number with a floor under it that everyone could see.

Voss did the arithmetic aloud, because Voss believed fear managed best in daylight. "We have coal for the stove for maybe forty hours at full burn. Half rations stretch food to eight days. Water we can make from snow if we have fire." He looked around the circle of faces. "So we don't have fire unless we must. Cold is survivable. Hunger is survivable. Stupidity is not."

Nobody argued. Tam pulled his knees up inside his parka and tried to make himself small, small being cheap.

It was Odile who broke the silence, in her dry cartographer's way. "The wreck is forty meters away. Stone-and-ice construction, if you can call it that — the hull's solid enough to shelter behind, and there's that hollow under the stern where the wind won't reach. If this runs long, we might move camp."

"If this runs long," Voss said, "we'll discuss moving camp."

But it was already running long. That was the thing about storms that Tam learned over those first eighteen hours: they didn't pass, they *persisted*. They settled in like a debt collector. The note of the guy lines never changed. The snow didn't fall so much as travel sideways, a horizontal blinding river of it, and when Tam crawled out at shift change to relieve himself he lasted ninety seconds before the cold found the one seam at his wrist and he came back in gasping, his hand a claw he couldn't feel.

Beatrix worked the feeling back into his fingers without a word, chafing them between her own rough palms, and said only, "Breathe slower. Panic spends heat."

He breathed slower. He was learning, day by day on this ice, how much of survival was just bookkeeping — of heat, of breath, of fear. His father had been a porter on lowland surveys and had died of a fever, ordinary as rain. Nobody had ever taught Tam that the world contained places where simply existing was a ledger you could go bankrupt in.

He slept in shifts. He woke to the C-note of the lines and the hiss of the storm and Merrick's glasses catching lamplight across the tent, the glaciologist awake when everyone else wasn't, sitting very still, facing the corner where the radio sat in its case.

* * *

On the morning of the second day — if morning meant anything down here, where the light through the fabric was the same gray whether the sun was up or under the world — Tam took stock of the food crate, because that had quietly become his job. Not by appointment. On the first night he'd counted the biscuit tins without thinking, porter's habit, knowing what a sledge carried, and Beatrix had seen him do it and said, "You count. Good. Keep counting."

So he kept counting, and what he counted told a story nobody wanted to hear aloud.

"We're short," he said, at the morning council, voice cracking on the first word. He cleared his throat and started again. "Two days short, if the storm runs past tomorrow. Someone's been drawing extra rations."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Voss's eyes went around the circle slowly, weighing each of them. Odile's jaw tightened. Ferro looked at his own hands as if they might have eaten something without telling him.

Merrick pushed his glasses up. "Porters miscount," he said. "Or the tally at Corren's Rest was wrong. These things happen."

"These things don't happen," Beatrix said mildly, and the mildness was worse than shouting. "Not on my loads."

"It happens under stress," Merrick insisted. "Memory is unreliable—"

"So is honesty, sometimes." She let that sit exactly long enough. "From here, rations are issued by Tam, witnessed by Odile. Nobody touches the crate otherwise. Including me."

"Including you," Voss repeated, and something passed between the two old rivals that Tam couldn't read — some old contract renewed in two words.

That should have been the end of it. It wasn't, because that evening the radio died.

* * *

They'd tried raising Corren's Rest once a day since the wreck, more for schedule than hope — the storm ate radio waves the way it ate everything else. But on the second evening, when Ferro keyed the set for the scheduled call, the needle on the meter lay flat and dead, and the tubes behind the vent grille stayed cold.

Ferro opened the case with the unhurried precision of a surgeon who already suspected what he'd find. Tam crouched beside him, holding the lamp, and watched the mechanic's thick fingers trace the wiring — input, transformer, the little forest of resistors — and stop.

Ferro held up a component between finger and thumb. A resistor, or what had been one. Its ceramic body was intact but its leads had been bent, carefully, and the element inside was cracked clean through. Not storm damage. Storm damage doesn't bend leads.

Ferro looked at Tam. Tam looked at the component. Neither of them said anything, because the tent was small and voices carried, and because saying it would make it true.

Then Ferro did something Tam had never seen him do: he took out his slate — the mute mechanic spoke in chalk and gestures, always had — and wrote a single line, angling it so only Tam could see.

*Who?*

Tam shook his head. He genuinely didn't know. But his mind was already doing the porter's arithmetic again, the counting habit, and the numbers kept circling back to the same place: the missing rations, and Merrick awake at odd hours, and the way the glaciologist had positioned himself near the radio case last night, very still, facing the corner.

And underneath that, colder: *why would someone kill our voice to the outside world?*

He wrote back on the slate: *Tell Voss?*

Ferro considered. Then he erased it and wrote: *Fix first. Then decide.* And beneath that, after a pause: *Need parts.*

Tam looked at the dead radio, then at the tent wall, beyond which lay forty meters of scree-covered ice and a ship that had been dead for centuries, and felt the strangest thought arrive: *the wreck has parts.*

* * *

Getting to the wreck required Beatrix's permission, and getting Beatrix's permission required telling her something, and telling her something required deciding what. Tam spent an hour turning it over while the storm screamed and the stove ticked down toward its rationed death, and in the end he chose the truth, because he'd watched what happened on this ice to people who dealt in anything else.

He caught her on watch at the tent mouth, hunched against the cold, and laid it out in a porter's plain words: the resistor, the bent leads, Ferro's plan. She listened without interrupting, which was itself frightening, and when he finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"You're sure it's deliberate."

"The leads were bent with pliers. Ferro showed me the marks."

"And you think Merrick."

"I think someone did it. I think the rations say someone's lying. I think—" He stopped, because the next part was disloyalty, and disloyalty on this crew could kill people. "I think we shouldn't accuse anyone until the radio works. Because whoever it is will be watching how we react. And if we're wrong about who—"

"—we've cut our own throat in a storm." She finished it for him, and something in her expression shifted, some recalculation of what the young porter was worth. "You think like a ranger, Tam."

"My da hauled loads twenty years. You learn where things go wrong."

"Where things go wrong," she repeated softly, as if the phrase meant something larger to her. Then, briskly: "All right. Here's how it goes. You and Ferro go to the wreck at shift change, when the wind's in its trough — it breathes, even katabatics breathe. Fifteen minutes, no more. You're looking for copper wire, any brass fittings, valve glass. The ship's electrical system is a hundred years dead but the metal's still metal. Take the ice axe and rope up. Double rope."

"Ferro can't hear the storm coming."

"Ferro reads the storm better than you do. Watch his hands." She paused, and added, quieter: "And Tam — if you see anyone else out there, you come straight back. Don't engage. Don't signal. Come back."

* * *

The traverse to the wreck was the worst fifteen minutes of Tam's life, and he'd once fallen down a mine shaft.

The storm's breathing was real — a rhythm of perhaps ninety seconds of relative mercy followed by a hammering gust — and Ferro timed it with a patience that seemed superhuman, watching the snow-stream's angle, tapping Tam's shoulder twice to mean *go*, once hard to mean *down*. They moved roped together through a world of white noise, visibility measured in arm-lengths, the wreck appearing out of the murk all at once like a whale surfacing: the black sweep of the hull, the frozen shroud-draped masts, the great barnacled curve of the stern where the old timbers had split open into a hollow the wind couldn't reach.

Inside the hollow, the silence was absolute and somehow louder than the storm. Their lamp threw shadows up the curved ribs of the hull, and Tam, catching his breath, looked up at the planking overhead and saw, for the first time up close, the scorch marks — great black blooms of burn spreading outward from somewhere deep in the ship's belly, as if the vessel had been fired from within. He'd heard the others talk about it. Seeing it was different. Seeing it made the hair rise on his neck, because wood burns outward, and this burned inward, and no lantern explosion he could imagine explained it.

Ferro tapped his arm. *Work.*

They worked. The wreck gave generously, the way ruins do — copper wire green with age but sound under the patina, stripped from a shattered instrument panel in what had been the officer's quarters; brass screws; a small glass vial from a medicine chest that Ferro held up to the lamp and pronounced, with a nod, a possible substitute for a cracked tube envelope. Tam pried fittings loose with the ice axe and felt, absurdly, like a thief in a tomb, and kept apologizing under his breath to whoever had sailed this ship and however they had died.

Halfway through, Ferro froze. Hand up. Head cocked — reading the storm through the hull, through vibration, the way deaf men read music through a floor.

Tap tap. *Go. Now.*

They made the return traverse in the trough, ran the last ten yards through the gust's leading edge, and came through the tent flap into lamplight and warmth and the eyes of four people, one of whom Tam was now almost certain was a saboteur, and none of whom could know what they'd just carried home in their pockets.

* * *

The repair took most of the third night.

Ferro built a workspace in the corner of the tent behind a screen of crates, and Tam became his hands' narrator and assistant — fetching, holding, soldering under instruction. The glaciologist's dead resistor had to be replaced entirely; Ferro wound new resistance from a length of nichrome wire salvaged from the stove's spare element, coiling it with a patience that made Tam's eyes ache. The cracked tube got the medicine vial treatment, which failed twice and worked on the third try, and when it finally lit — a soft orange glow blooming inside the little glass envelope — Tam felt like he'd helped deliver something alive.

The whole time, Tam kept one ear on the tent behind them. Voices drifted over the crate-screen: Odile re-plotting their position from memory, arguing drift rates with herself; Voss silent; Beatrix silent; Merrick talking — Merrick talking quite a lot, actually, about ice cores and accumulation rates and how the storm was a textbook katabatic event, filling the silence the way nervous men do, and Tam filed that away too. Guilt talked. He'd learned that much from the thief on the salt flats, a lifetime ago it seemed now, a man who'd confessed his whole life story to avoid a beating.

Near midnight, Ferro touched Tam's sleeve and pointed at the meter. The needle stood up when he keyed the set. Weak — the improvised coil ran hot, and Ferro shook his head at any thought of transmit power — but alive. Reception first. They listened through the static for Corren's Rest's scheduled beacon, and at 0400 by Tam's exhausted count, faint as a heartbeat under a coat, it came through: the little automated pulse of the frontier station, faithful as sunrise.

Someone was still out there. The world hadn't ended. The storm had only locked them in a room with each other.

Ferro chalked one word on his slate and showed it to Tam, and Tam felt the weight of it settle onto his shoulders like a loaded sledge:

*Now tell?*

Tam looked at the glowing tube. He looked at the crate-screen, beyond which Merrick's voice was still running on about accumulation rates. He thought about Beatrix's face when she'd said *you think like a ranger,* and about Voss's flat arithmetic, and about Wren Whitlock, whom he'd never met, whose compass rode in Beatrix's pocket through all of this like a heart that wouldn't stop beating.

"Not yet," he whispered. "Tomorrow. At first light. Both of us, together, to Beatrix and Voss — nobody else. Because if we tell the whole tent, whoever it is gets warning, and if they sabotaged a radio they'll do worse to a person."

Ferro studied him for a long moment. Then the big man wiped the slate clean and drew, instead of words, a small sketch: a stick figure standing straighter than stick figures usually stand, and beside it, a single check mark.

Tam laughed — the first time he'd laughed in three days, a rusty, stupid, wonderful sound — and clapped Ferro's shoulder, and went to his bedroll, and slept the sleep of someone who had, without noticing the exact moment it happened, stopped being the kid who carried things and started being someone the others might carry *with*.

* * *

First light, on the fourth morning, was a lie the storm told. The gray through the canvas was identical to every other gray. But Tam was up, and Ferro was up, and they crossed the tent together to where Beatrix sat waking, and Tam had the words ready — *the radio was murdered, we fixed it, we know who* — right on his tongue.

He never said them. Because across the tent, Merrick was already dressed, already packed, already standing by the supply sledge with his parka hood up, and the look on his face was not the look of a man waiting out a storm.

It was the look of a man whose clock had run out.

"Merrick," Voss said quietly, from the shadows. "Where exactly do you think you're going?"

And outside, beneath the endless scream of the wind, the glacier gave a long, groaning crack — the first calving tremor, deep and vast, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
