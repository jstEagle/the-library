## 15. Merrick's Bargain

The storm broke on the fourth morning the way a held breath breaks — all at once, and leaving everything changed.

Beatrix stepped out of the wreck's shadow into a world scoured clean. The katabatic wind had spent itself against the icefall to the north, and now the light came down hard and blue off the glacier, so bright it made her eyes water behind her smoked goggles. The snow around the camp had drifted chest-high against the sledge runners. The ship — *the* ship — rose out of the white like the ribcage of something that had died standing up, its scorched timbers rimed in fresh frost, its masts snapped and leaning at angles that hurt to look at.

Three days pinned in the hull's lee with six people and dwindling food had a way of sanding a crew down to their true grain. She'd watched it happen. Tam had come through it better than any of them — the boy who'd arrived at Corren's Rest all elbows and eagerness now moved through the camp like he'd been doing this for years, checking lines, chipping ice from the radio housing Ferro had rebuilt twice. Voss had gone quiet in the way Beatrix remembered from nine years ago, the quiet of a man carrying something heavier than his pack. And Merrick —

Merrick had come through the storm polished.

That was the thing she kept turning over as she walked the perimeter that morning, boots crunching on wind-packed sastrugi. Everyone else looked worn. Odile had frost-nipped fingertips she wouldn't complain about. Ferro hadn't slept more than two hours at a stretch since the radio went dead. But Merrick stood by the science tent each morning freshly shaved, methodical, almost cheerful, as if the storm had been an inconvenience scheduled for his convenience rather than three days of listening to the glacier try to kill them.

She'd been a ranger long enough to know what that meant. Men who stayed clean in a storm were men who knew when it would end.

She was halfway back to camp when he fell into step beside her.

"Ranger Whitlock." He said it pleasantly, hands jammed in the pockets of his parka, breath streaming behind him. "A word?"

"We're not short of words out here, Doctor. We're short of food."

"Which is precisely why I wanted a word." He glanced back toward the camp — Voss was visible as a dark shape at the wreck's hull, Tam smaller beside him — and then forward again, and his voice dropped into a register she hadn't heard from him before. Not the lecture-hall smoothness. Something older. Something that had learned to be quiet in rooms where money talked. "Not here. Walk with me to the ridge."

"The ridge is exposed."

"The ridge is private." He smiled. "Ten minutes. Then I'll carry water until my hands fall off, if you like."

Every instinct she had said turn around. But she'd spent nine years learning what happened when people didn't hear the whole of a thing — Voss had taught her that lesson with a silence, and Wren had paid for it. So she turned east instead, toward the low ridge of blue ice that separated the wreck basin from the crevasse field, and let him walk beside her.

They climbed in silence. The ice underfoot was old and dense and rang faintly when they stepped, like walking on a bell. From the ridgeline they could see the whole white corridor stretched out behind them — the route they'd come by, the cairn Wren had built, the valley walls rising in pale terraces toward the col. And ahead, south, the icefall: a frozen avalanche of shattered seracs tumbled across the glacier's throat, blue and green and black in the deep light, beautiful the way a snarl of broken glass is beautiful.

"That's our way home," Beatrix said. "Or it was."

"It's closing." Merrick said it without drama, a fact among facts. "You've seen the pressure ridges forming along the eastern margin. The calving season's opening early. Voss knows it. Your cartographer knows it. Which means," and here he turned to face her fully, and Beatrix saw that the pleasantness had been set aside like a tool no longer needed, "we have perhaps four days of viable route, and every one of them matters. Which brings me to my offer."

"I figured you didn't drag me up here to look at the view."

"No." He reached inside his coat and drew out a flat oilskin packet, dry despite three days of storm, and held it between them without offering it. "I'm going to speak plainly, because you strike me as a woman who respects plainness over performance. The man who funded this expedition — the man whose money bought Merrick's instruments and Merrick's discretion — has authorized a final payment. To you alone. For a service only you can render."

"I don't render services for your buyer."

"You haven't heard the price."

"I've heard enough prices in my life to know they always cost more than they pay."

Something flickered across his face — respect, maybe, or its counterfeit. He opened the packet anyway, and even in the flat glacier light the paper inside caught her eye: bank drafts, real ones, drawn on a house in the capital with a name she recognized from the ledgers of men who owned railroads. She didn't count them. She didn't need to. The top figure alone would have cleared every debt in her book at Corren's Rest — the feed bill, the farrier, the two years of taxes on her mother's claim she'd been paying down in installments like penance. It would have bought the town's clinic a new roof and the school a stove that worked. It was, very nearly, the exact sum she'd once calculated it would take to buy back the reputation Cassian Voss had cost her, though she'd never told anyone she'd done that arithmetic.

"Safe passage," Merrick said. "That's all. When we move out, you guide us — but you guide us by the western margin, slow, through terrain where a certain sledge could be delayed. A day, perhaps two. Long enough for me to be well clear with a sample of the cargo before anyone decides to follow. You don't have to lie. You don't have to fight anyone. You just have to be slow, and look regretful about it afterward."

"And you walk away rich, and your buyer gets his prize."

"My buyer gets his curiosity satisfied. That's all it is to him — curiosity. He's an old man with more money than years, and he wants to hold a piece of the past in his hands before he dies. Is that so monstrous?" Merrick tucked the packet away again. "Voss would have you believe this is about danger. About some contagion of ideas, some terrible knowledge that must stay buried. But I've examined the cargo, Ranger. Resin-sealed jars. Cloth. Metalwork. It's *cargo*. Beautiful, strange, centuries-old cargo, and it has sat in the ice for five hundred years without poisoning a single soul. What killed the first expedition wasn't the ship. It was men deciding what other men were allowed to know."

Beatrix looked south at the icefall. The wind had begun again, faint, moving snow off the serac tops in long veils.

"Wren died securing that cargo," she said. "You know that. Voss told us."

"I know Voss *says* that."

"She burned it." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "She burned it rather than let it reach civilization. That's not a story Voss invented. I read her journal. In her own hand."

"Then I'm sorry," Merrick said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it. "I'm sorry she died for a decision that was never hers to make. A handful of frightened men on a glacier, playing God with history. And now another handful stands on the same ice, preparing to make the same decision, and call it loyalty." He paused. "Your sister's name isn't on any monument, Ranger. No one will ever know what she did or why. Doesn't that gall you? All that sacrifice, spent on people who will never know her name — while men like my buyer get to decide what the world remembers?"

The question went in under her ribs like a splinter of ice.

Because it was the question. It had been the question since the salt flats, since the letter with no signature, since the night Tam told her Voss had spoken Wren's name into a fire. She had built nine years of life on the foundation of Wren's silence — had become a ranger, had taken small contracts and small thieves and small debts, had let the compass needle point leeward every morning like a prayer she didn't believe in — all of it resting on the belief that what Wren did had been worth what it cost. And here was a man with a packet of drafts saying: *it wasn't worth anything. It bought nothing. It bought silence, and silence is what cowards call loyalty when they're too afraid to look at what they buried.*

"Say your number," she said quietly.

"Whatever number you say first."

"Say it."

He named it. It was higher than the top draft. It was, she realized with a cold clarity, almost exactly double what Voss was paying her — as if the buyer's agents had done the same arithmetic she had, and priced her loyalty at precisely the rate required to insult it.

She laughed. She couldn't help it; it came out of her like something escaping.

Merrick's composure cracked, just at the edges. "You find this funny?"

"I find it honest." She pulled her goggles up onto her forehead so he could see her eyes. "For one moment there — one — I let myself stand in it. What it would mean. Debts gone. The roof fixed. Never having to count again." She shook her head slowly. "And do you know what I found underneath? Not greed. Fear. Fear that if I took your money, I'd finally have to admit that everything I've been carrying for nine years was for sale all along, and I just hadn't met the right buyer." She held out her hand, palm open. "Give me the packet."

His eyebrows rose. Hope is a stupid animal; it lives in everyone.

She fed it to the wind. One page at a time, she stripped the drafts from the oilskin and let them go, and the wind took them skittering south across the blue ice, white slips dancing over the crevasse field like birds, like letters never delivered, until the last one lifted off her palm and the packet in Merrick's hand was empty.

"That was—" His voice actually shook. "That was a fortune."

"That was a question," Beatrix said. "And I've answered it." She turned to face him full-on, and let him see what nine years of desert had built in her, which was not hardness exactly, but something load-bearing. "Here's what happens now, Doctor. You tell your buyer the answer was no. You keep your vials in your kit and your mouth shut on the march. And if I find you've transmitted so much as a bearing off this glacier, I won't need Voss's permission or anyone's contract to deal with you, because out here I am the law, and the law is very simple: nobody gets left behind, and nobody gets sold. Both of those apply to you."

"And if the route closes with us still on it?"

"Then we die equal," she said, "which is more than your money would have bought you."

She left him on the ridge. She didn't look back, because looking back would have shown her his face, and she suspected that somewhere beneath the shock there was relief — the relief of a man who had been ordered to do a thing and had now been given a reason to fail at it. She filed that thought away. It might matter later.

* * *

Back in camp, the storm's departure had unstoppered activity. Odile had her maps spread across two supply crates, weighted with ice axes, and was arguing geometry with Voss in low, urgent tones. Ferro was repacking the sledge with the silent economy of a man who expressed himself entirely through knots and torque. Tam was hauling water from the melt pool, and when he saw Beatrix come down off the ridge alone, his face did something complicated — he'd seen her go with Merrick, and he'd spent the interval visibly composing scenarios.

"Well?" he asked, too casually, setting down the yoke.

"He offered me enough money to buy Corren's Rest twice over."

Tam's jaw dropped. Odile looked up from her maps. Even Ferro's hands went still.

"What did you say?" Tam breathed.

"I said the wind could have it." She took the yoke from him and hauled a share of the water herself, because her arms needed something honest to do. "Get your kit squared away. All of you. We break camp within the hour."

Voss detached himself from the maps and came to her, gray-eyed, reading her face the way he read weather. "What did he offer?"

"Passage. Delayed passage, westward, while he ran for the coast with a sample." She kept her voice level, pitched for him alone. "He thinks the cargo is harmless. He thinks Wren died for nothing. He thinks loyalty is a story frightened men tell."

"And what do you think?"

Beatrix looked south, at the icefall, where the morning light was climbing the seracs and the glacier's groans had changed pitch overnight — deeper, wetter, the sound of a thing beginning to let go of itself.

"I think," she said, "that we're out of time to think. Odile — how bad is the eastern margin?"

Odile didn't answer immediately. She came around the crates holding Wren's recovered notes, copied in her own careful hand, and laid them beside her maps, and Beatrix saw the red ink for the first time — a line drawn through the heart of the icefall, jagged, impossible-looking, annotated in Wren's cramped script: *Only way through after thaw. Trust the blue ice. Count the seracs.*

"Bad," Odile said. "The corridor we came in by will be calving within days — maybe hours, if the warm front holds. Our only way home is the one your sister marked." She met Beatrix's eyes. "It's never been crossed. Not once. Not by anyone living."

The wind came down the valley then, and the glacier answered it with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and somewhere high on the southern wall, with a crack that none of them needed translating, the calving season began.

Beatrix picked up the compass from her pocket. The needle swung, steadied, pointed leeward — toward the ship, toward Wren, toward the red line through the ice.

"Then we'll be the first," she said.
