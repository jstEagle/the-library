## 16. The Breaking of the Route

Odile had charted coastlines that moved, rivers that wandered, dunes that swallowed survey markers whole. She had learned early that no map was a promise — only a photograph of a world caught mid-breath. But she had never before watched her own work die in real time.

It began as sound.

She was in the survey tent at dawn, correcting the day's traverse notes by lamplight, when the glacier spoke. Not the groans and mutterings they'd lived with for weeks — those were the ice settling its accounts nightly, familiar as weather. This was different. A single deep concussion rolled up the valley floor and through the soles of her boots, followed by a long tearing hiss, like a giant drawing breath through gritted teeth.

Then silence. Then Tam's voice outside, high with something between awe and fear: "The serac field. Look at the serac field."

Odile pushed out of the tent into gray light. Across the valley, on the eastern flank where the Leeward Glacier bent around the shoulder of the mountain, an entire wall of ice — two hundred feet of it, blue-green and ancient — was leaning. Not falling. *Leaning*, the way a tired man leans against a doorframe, and everyone watching understood that doorframes do not hold forever.

By noon it was down. The report came back from Ferro, who had climbed the moraine ridge with his field glasses: the seracs had calved in a single catastrophic release, and the debris — house-sized blocks tumbled like dropped dice — now choked the lower return corridor, the route they'd climbed three weeks earlier. The corridor wasn't closed. It was worse than closed. It was *changing*, shedding its skin a piece at a time, and every piece that fell made the next fall more likely.

"The season's opened," Voss said that evening, standing over Odile's table while she spread the maps flat. His voice was level, but she'd learned to read him, and what she read was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Calving was supposed to start in eleven days. The meltwater's running three weeks ahead of schedule. Whatever's driving this — warm current off the coast, some shift in the leeward winds — it doesn't care about our schedule."

"Then we leave tomorrow," Beatrix said from the tent flap. She'd been quiet all day, compass in hand more often than not, thumb worrying the worn brass case. "First light. We move fast and light."

"We can't." Odile said it before she'd fully decided to say it, and every head turned toward her. She pulled the corridor map toward her and laid her pencil across the route home — the route she had charted inward, the route that had held. "This is why I've been re-surveying every evening instead of sleeping. Look." Her pencil traced the line. "Here, the lateral moraine we descended — it's gone. Slumped into the melt channel. Here, the ice bridge over the crevasse field at Kilometer Nine — Ferro heard it cracking yesterday; by tomorrow it'll be soup. And here—" the pencil stopped, pressed hard enough to dimple the paper, "—the whole lower valley is now a debris fan under active collapse. We wouldn't be walking a route. We'd be walking a lottery."

"So we wait for it to settle," Merrick said. He'd been uncharacteristically subdued since the confrontation — since Odile had laid his transmitter before the crew and watched him confess to a buyer's name and a price. They hadn't voted to leave him behind. That was somehow worse for everyone. "Glaciers exhaust themselves. Give it four, five days—"

"Four or five days is four or five thousand tons of ice finding new places to be," Odile said. "And every one of them is downhill of us."

The silence that followed had weight. Tam sat closest to the stove, and Odile saw him looking at the map the way a young man looks at a thing that has stopped being abstract — the way you look at a coffin, maybe, or a wedding contract. Somewhere in the last week the boy had stopped being cargo-handling labor and started being crew, and Odile found herself tracking his face now the way she tracked the glacier: for signs of movement.

"There is another way," she said.

Voss looked up sharply. "There isn't."

"There is, and you know there is, because your first expedition considered it and rejected it, and Wren Whitlock wrote about it in her journal, and I have been reading that journal every night since we left the cache." Odile kept her voice flat, professional, because the alternative was letting it shake. "The icefall. The upper route, along the western wall, through the broken zone below the col. It's marked in red ink across three pages of her notes. With annotations."

"It's marked in red because it's a graveyard," Voss said. "Two men of the original party went in to test it. One came back. He didn't come back *whole*."

"And the main corridor was open then, so they had a choice." Odile met his eyes. "We don't have a choice anymore, Cassian. Your sworn-off route is buried under a collapsing serac field. The red route is still standing — barely. Icefalls shed too, but they shed *predictably*. Seracs fail all at once. Icefalls fail a piece at a time, and pieces give warning." She tapped the journal, bound in oilcloth beside her lamp. "Wren spent six days mapping the safe lanes through it before she died. Six days of observation, recorded hour by hour. It's the most complete icefall survey I've ever seen, and I've seen the professional ones. She did it alone, in bad light, with frozen fingers." Odile paused, and hated herself a little for what she said next, and said it anyway, because the clock was running. "She died making sure someone could follow her home. I intend to let her succeed."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the glacier groaned — a shorter sound this time, a smaller piece letting go somewhere in the dark, but close enough that the tent fabric trembled.

Beatrix broke the silence. "Show me her annotations."

* * *

They worked through the night, Odile copying Wren's red-route notes onto fresh sheets while Beatrix read over her shoulder, translating the dead woman's shorthand aloud in a voice that stayed steady by visible effort. Wren had written fast, in pencil, in a hand that degraded as the pages went on — the observations getting terser, the times logged at odd hours, 0300, 0340, 0415, as though sleep had become a luxury rationed like food.

*"Lane B holds past the second serac if you keep to the true right. Blue ice under the snow bridge at the narrows — crampons, no exceptions. The tower at the third gate leans east and drops rock at midday sun. Cross before ten or after four."*

Hour by hour. Hazard by hazard. A woman building a road out of a ruin, knowing she might never walk it herself.

"She surveyed it twice," Odile murmured, near dawn. "Look — same lanes, second pass, dates nine days apart. She went back through to confirm nothing had shifted." She shook her head slowly. "I've worked with national surveys that wouldn't do that."

Beatrix said nothing. But Odile saw her lay her palm flat on the page, over the red line, the way you'd touch a shoulder.

At first light they struck camp. The wreck came with them in fragments: Voss carried the evidence crate himself, refusing help, the scorched samples sealed and lashed under oilcloth. Ferro broke down the sledge rigging and rebuilt it lighter, shedding everything that wasn't rope, steel, food, or fuel. Merrick walked where they could see him, which was everywhere, and said almost nothing, and Odile caught Tam watching him with an expression she couldn't file — not hatred, not forgiveness. Arithmetic, maybe. The boy counting what a man was worth now that his price was known.

They reached the mouth of the icefall by mid-morning, and Odile stopped at the boundary and simply looked, because a cartographer owes the terrain that much honesty.

It was less a glacier feature than a shipwreck of one. The icefall was where the Leeward poured over a buried ridge and shattered — a mile-wide cascade of ice frozen mid-fall, towers and chasms and leaning slabs, blue shadow and blinding white, stitched together by snow bridges that might be pavement or might be paper. Meltwater ran beneath it all, audible everywhere and visible nowhere, a river talking to itself under the floor of the world.

Somewhere in that maze was a line drawn in red ink by a dead woman nine years ago.

"Rope spacing, twelve meters," Odile said, and heard her own voice go crisp, professional, the voice she used when fear needed a job to do. "Single file, always. Nobody steps anywhere I haven't stepped or probed. We move on my count and we stop on my count, and nobody — *nobody* — passes me without orders." She looked at each of them in turn. "Wren's notes give us lanes. Lanes change. So we verify everything. Every bridge probed, every lane confirmed before we commit. It will be slow. Slow is how we live."

"And if a lane's gone?" Tam asked quietly.

"Then we find the next one. She gave us four alternates." Odile shouldered her pack. "She thought of everything except coming home herself. The least we can do is use all of it."

* * *

The first day in the icefall was the worst kind of good: good enough to build confidence, which Odile knew was the most dangerous supply they carried.

Lane A ran true for the first kilometer exactly as Wren had logged it — the twin seracs leaning apart like a doorway, the compressed-snow ramp between them firm underfoot. Odile probed every meter with the bamboo pole, called her findings, moved the crew through in twos. By late afternoon they'd made better distance than she'd dared hope, and camped on a shelf of old ice Wren's notes described as *"the balcony — solid, drains well, room for four tents if you're friendly."*

They were friendly.

It was during dinner, melting snow over the stove, that the mountain reminded them who held the lease. A crack like a rifle shot split the evening — somewhere above and to the west — and every head came up. Then a slow rumble, gathering, and a section of the icefall's upper reaches let go. Not near them. Not threatening them. Just the glacier, shedding, a hundred meters of ice becoming powder and rubble in a long white exhalation that drifted across the valley and dusted their tents like flour.

Tam stood watching the plume settle, spoon forgotten halfway to his mouth.

"That's the clock," Odile said quietly, beside him. "Every one of those is a day coming off our margin. The meltwater's undercutting the whole structure. In a week, this entire icefall will be a different animal than the one Wren mapped. In two, it won't be crossable at all."

"How many days do we have? Honestly."

Odile considered lying to him and rejected it — the boy had earned better. "Five. Maybe six. After that we're not choosing routes, we're choosing graves."

He nodded, solemn, and then — and this was the thing about Tam, the thing she was beginning to rely on — he asked the practical question. "Then we don't camp long tomorrow. We break early and push through the narrows before the midday softening. Right?"

"Right." She allowed herself half a smile. "You've been reading my notes."

"Somebody has to. You only sleep three hours."

* * *

The narrows nearly killed them on the third day.

Wren's notes described them as *"the throat — 200m of lane B between walls that lean together. Blue ice, exposed. Fast passage, no stopping, no loads catching. If wind, wait. If sun, hurry."*

The sun was already high when they reached the entrance, later than Odile wanted — they'd lost ninety minutes at dawn probing a snow bridge that Wren had marked solid and finding it honeycombed, rotten through, rerouting through Lane C at the cost of an hour and a climb. Now the walls of the throat sweated in the warmth, trickling, ticking, shedding pebbles of ancient ice that rang off the lower surfaces like small bells.

"Fast means fast," Odile said. "Twelve-meter spacing. When you reach the far side, keep moving to the marker cairn and clear the exit. Go."

She went first, poles and crampons biting blue glass, the walls rising sheer on either side close enough to touch both at once. The ice sang around her — that high stressed whine load-bearing glaciers make, felt in the teeth more than the ears. Halfway through, she heard it change pitch.

"MOVE!" she shouted, and didn't look back, just drove forward, arms and legs, crampons skating, and behind her the crew's boots hammered, and above and behind came the crack and rush of a slab the size of a wagon letting go off the eastern wall. It hit the throat floor thirty meters back, where Tam had been standing four seconds earlier, and shattered into shards that hailed past Odile's shoulders, stinging through her hood, as she hauled herself out the exit and spun to count heads.

Voss. Beatrix. Ferro. Merrick. Tam — Tam stumbling out of the smoke of ice-dust, bleeding from a cut above his ear where a shard had kissed him, eyes enormous, alive.

"Count!" Beatrix barked. "Again!"

They counted. Five. All five. Ferro signed something rapid and emphatic that needed no translation, and even Merrick stood with his hands on his knees, breathing like a man reprieved.

Odile's hands were shaking. She hid them in her armpits and turned to check the route behind, because shaking hands were a problem for tonight and the route was a problem for now. The slab had filled the throat's midpoint. Lane B was severed. Behind them, the way they'd come was already becoming a memory.

"Forward's the only direction," she announced, when she trusted her voice. "Same as always. But faster now. The mountain's voting."

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of a serac Wren had nicknamed *"the bishop"* — tall, tilted, improbably steadfast — Odile updated the master map by lamplight and found Beatrix sitting across from her, uninvited, holding two cups of tea. She handed one over.

"You charted us through a collapsing icefall on a dead woman's notes," Beatrix said. "Three days in. I wanted to say what nobody says on the rope."

"Don't."

"I'm going to anyway." Beatrix wrapped her hands around her cup. "Nine years I've carried her compass and hated this glacier for taking her. I thought the ice was just — indifferent. A machine that ate people." She looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious and something else underneath, something that might have been wonder. "But she's *in* these pages. Every lane, every annotation, every alternate. She's been guiding this route for nine years, waiting for us to need it. That's not indifference. That's—" Her voice caught. She drank her tea instead of finishing.

Odile let the silence sit, because some silences are the kindest thing you can offer. Then she said, quietly: "Tomorrow we cross the section she marked in red with three exclamation points. 'The gallery.' Her notes say it's the crux — a hanging glacier above the lane, and the crossing window is narrow. Dawn to seven-thirty, before the sun hits the upper wall." She unfolded the map between them, the red line crawling through the maze like a vein. "If we make the gallery by dawn, we're through the worst of it. Two days to the col after that, and the descent beyond is stable rock."

"And if we miss the window?"

"Then we shelter under the bishop and try the next dawn. Assuming the bishop is still a bishop and not rubble." Odile rolled the map. "The clock's audible in everything now. You've heard it."

Beatrix had. They all had — the constant percussion of the failing glacier, cracks and booms echoing down the valley at all hours, the icefall rearranging itself in the dark like a sleeper turning over. Sleep had become something done in the gaps between sounds.

Beatrix stood, drained her cup, and paused at the tent flap. "Odile. When we're through — when this is over — I want you to know what her route was worth. What she was worth."

"I know what she was worth," Odile said. "I've been walking on it for three days."

The flap fell shut. Odile sat alone with the lamp and the maps and the sound of the mountain dismantling itself, and she did what cartographers do with the end of the world: she measured it, and wrote it down, and planned the way through.

Dawn would come in four hours. The gallery waited above, hung with ten thousand tons of patient ice, and somewhere beyond it, home.

She blew out the lamp and listened to the clock tick in the dark.
