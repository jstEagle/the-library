## 17. Merrick Runs

Tam woke to the sound of a sledge runner scraping against ice, and for a moment, half-dreaming in the blue dark of the tent, he thought it was Ferro doing repairs. Ferro worked at all hours. That was just something Ferro did — hands always moving, always fixing, a man who spoke with wrenches instead of words.

Then he heard the footsteps. Quick ones. Wrong ones.

He was out of his sleeping bag before his mind finished the arithmetic. The tent flap came up hard and cold and the wind hit him like a wall of needles, and there — fifty yards out, already small against the vast grey sweep of the icefall — was Merrick. Merrick with a loaded sledge harness over his shoulders, Merrick moving fast, bent into the wind like a man running from a fire only he could see.

"Merrick!" Tam's voice tore away east before it even left his mouth. He cupped his hands. "MERRICK!"

The figure didn't stop. Didn't turn. Just kept going, smaller and smaller, a black stitch unraveling across the white.

Behind him, the camp stirred. Beatrix emerged first, rifle slung, hair rimed with frost, her eyes going from Tam to the horizon and back with a speed that made him step aside. Voss came out slower, and when Tam glanced at him, the old man's face had gone very still — the stillness of a man watching a door close that he'd been leaning against for nine years.

"He took food," Tam said. His breath came out in ragged clouds. "Three days' rations, maybe four. And the spare fuel tin."

"Did anyone see which way?" Beatrix was already moving toward the supply cache, checking with quick, brutal efficiency. "North line or south?"

"South," Tam said. "Toward the icefall."

Voss made a sound low in his throat. It wasn't quite a word. It might have been a curse in some language dead long enough to be safe.

"The icefall," Odile repeated from the flap of the map tent, her voice flat with disbelief. She had her charts under one arm, rolled tight, as if she'd slept holding them. "He's taking a *sledge* into the icefall. Alone. At night."

"Not night." Beatrix checked her watch against the pale smear on the horizon where the sun would eventually bother to rise. "Dawn in forty minutes. Light'll come slow."

"Then we go after him in the light," Voss said.

"No." Beatrix turned. For a moment Tam saw something in her face he couldn't name — not anger, not fear. Something older and wearier than either. "We don't chase a man into collapsing ground. We've lost enough days to the storm. We cross today, while the corridor holds. Merrick made his choice."

"But—" Tam started.

"*But what?*" Beatrix rounded on him, and then stopped herself. He watched her take a breath, watch her put the anger away somewhere, the way she seemed to be able to do — folding it up small and putting it in a pocket where it wouldn't weigh anything until she needed it. "He sold us, Tam. He sold all of us. Every one of us standing here is alive right now because of choices harder than this one. We cross today."

Tam looked south, to where Merrick had been. The wind had already begun erasing the tracks, filling them with spindrift, softening them into suggestions. By noon they'd be nothing at all.

He should have let it go. He knew that even then, some part of him calm and clear-eyed, watching from a distance: *let it go, Tam, the man made his bed, the man is a traitor, the man nearly got you all killed.* But another part of him — the part that had signed onto this expedition because he wanted, more than anything, to be someone who didn't stand still while things went wrong — that part looked at those vanishing tracks and felt something tighten in his chest like a fist closing around wire.

Merrick had taught him stars. Back in the first week, before everything curdled, Merrick had stood with him outside the mess tent and pointed out constellations with a gloved finger, patient, almost kind. *That one's the Mariner. Sailors used it to find their way home.* Tam hadn't thought about that in weeks. He thought about it now.

"I'll go," he heard himself say.

Every head turned.

"You will *not*," Beatrix said.

"Somebody has to tell him the corridor's closing. Somebody has to bring him back before—"

"Before what? Before the ice does our work for us?" Voss's voice was quiet, which somehow made it worse. "Boy, that man would sell your boots off your feet at minus forty."

"Maybe." Tam held the old man's gaze, surprised at himself, surprised at how steady his own voice came out. "Maybe. But I'm the lightest one here. I move fastest over thin bridges. And you need every strong back for the crossing." He swallowed. "I can catch him before the icefall proper. Bring him back. Or at least know."

The silence stretched. Wind keened through the guy-lines. Somewhere deep underfoot, the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, like a ship settling, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Beatrix studied him for a long moment. Then she looked at Voss. Something passed between them that Tam couldn't read — some old arithmetic, some shared ledger he wasn't party to.

"One hour," she said finally. "You follow the tracks to the edge of the icefall and no further. You catch him, you bring him back. You don't catch him, you turn around at the boundary marker and you come home. That's not a request, Tam. That's the whole of it. If you're not back by the time we break camp, I'm coming after you myself, and I won't be gentle about it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And take this." She pressed something into his hand: a flare pistol, compact and cold. "One cartridge. If the ground opens under you, you fire it straight up and you pray Ferro's listening. Understood?"

"Understood."

Ferro appeared at his elbow without Tam hearing him approach — the man moved like snowfall — and pressed a coil of rope into his arms, then tapped Tam's chest twice with two fingers. A gesture Tam had learned by now meant *be careful*, or possibly *don't die*, or possibly both.

Odile handed him a strip of her map, torn to size, showing the first half-mile of the icefall approach with the safe channel inked in green. "The green line," she said. "Only the green line. The moment you leave it, I can't help you, and neither can anyone else."

Tam nodded, shrugged into his pack, clipped the rope, and stepped out into the wind.

* * *

The tracks were harder to follow than he'd hoped and easier than he feared.

Spindrift kept trying to bury them, but Merrick had been moving fast and heavy-laden, and a laden man leaves a deep signature — the drag mark of the sledge behind each footfall, a stutter-line stitched down the glacier like a sentence written by someone in a hurry. Tam followed it at a jog, breath rasping, the world reduced to a rhythm: *step, read the track, step, look up, step.*

The light came up slow and strange, the sun never quite clearing the ridge to the east, so the whole valley sat in a kind of bruised twilight, shadows lying long and blue across the ice. The icefall rose ahead of him like a frozen waterfall the size of a cathedral — seracs the height of buildings, leaning against each other at angles that hurt to look at, blue-green ice compressed so dense it seemed lit from within. Even at a distance it made his skin crawl. It looked less like geography and more like architecture. Like something built. Like something that could fall.

He caught sight of Merrick at the twenty-minute mark.

The glaciologist was maybe four hundred yards ahead, stalled at the lip of the icefall's lower apron, and even from that distance Tam could read the hesitation in his posture — the way he stood at the boundary between the smooth glacier and the chaos beyond, sledge trailing behind him, head turning, mapping routes with his eyes the way he mapped everything. Tam dropped low and ran crouched, using a pressure ridge for cover, closing the distance in bursts.

"Merrick!" He was close enough now that his voice might actually carry. "Merrick, wait!"

This time the man heard him. He turned — and Tam saw his face properly for the first time since the confrontation in the wreck, since Odile had laid out the evidence of the transmissions, since everything. Merrick looked terrible. Hollowed. Frost had built up in the seams of his hood, and his eyes had the glassy brightness of a man who hadn't slept and wasn't going to.

"Go back, boy." His voice carried flat across the ice, stripped of everything. "Go back to them."

"You come back," Tam panted, closing to fifty yards, thirty. "The corridor's closing. Odile says we have maybe a day, maybe less. You can't cross alone. Nobody crosses alone."

"I'm not crossing." Merrick laughed — a short, awful sound with no humor in it anywhere. "I'm cutting south along the base. There's an outlet valley. Harder, but slower-changing. I've run the models." He patted the sledge, and Tam saw the sample case lashed to it, the brass fittings catching the grey light. "I have what I came for. I don't need the corridor."

"You'll die out there."

"Everyone dies out here." Merrick's eyes flicked past Tam, back the way he'd come, calculating distances. "The question is whether you die for something. I spent nine years being careful, boy. Nine years teaching classes and writing papers nobody read and telling myself the money didn't matter. And then a letter comes, and suddenly it matters again, and I hate that it matters, but there it is." He shouldered the harness. "Tell Voss the buyer sends his regards. Tell him the debt's older than any of us."

"Merrick—"

"Don't follow me." And he turned, and drove the sledge forward, over the boundary, into the icefall's outer margin — not the green line, not Odile's channel, but a ragged parallel path closer to the southern wall, where the ice leaned and muttered.

Tam stood there with the flare pistol heavy in his pocket and the order heavy in his ears — *the boundary marker, no further* — and watched the man shrink into the maze.

He lasted eleven seconds.

Then he was running.

* * *

He told himself it was tactical. He told himself that a lone man in the icefall was a liability to the whole expedition — that if Merrick died badly, in view of the route, it would shake the others; that bringing him back was the mission; that Beatrix would understand once it was done. All of it was true, probably. None of it was why he was running.

He was running because of the Mariner. Because of a gloved finger pointing at the sky three weeks and a lifetime ago. Because somewhere underneath everything — the betrayal, the sabotage, the broken radio, the stolen supplies — Merrick had still been the man who taught him stars, and Tam could not shake the feeling that if he let the man walk into the ice alone, some part of himself would stay out there too, forever, standing at a boundary marker obeying orders.

The southern margin was worse than he'd imagined. The seracs here were older, leaner, leaning like drunks propping each other up, and between them the crevasses opened in families — not single cracks but systems, braided webs of blue darkness that the surface snow bridged with treacherous, sagging lids. Tam moved the way Odile had drilled them: probe every bridge with the axe shaft, weight it slowly, keep the rope loose in case. Twice the snow gave with a sound like a sigh and he found himself staring down into depths that went from blue to black to nothing at all.

He lost the tracks twice and found them again. The third time, he didn't.

What he found instead was the sledge.

It stood wedged in a lane between two leaning seracs, tilted hard to port, one runner snapped clean off. Beyond it, the snow was churned and broken — a struggle of footprints, a long furrow where something heavy had slid — and then the furrow ended at a hole in the world.

The crevasse mouth gaped perhaps six feet wide, its lips crumbled inward, the snow around it collapsed in a star pattern that Tam's eye read instantly and his stomach refused to accept. Rope lay beside it — Merrick's climbing line, paid out in a tangled heap, its far end disappearing into the blue. Cut. Not frayed. *Cut*, the ends clean and bright, and Tam understood with a cold, slow clarity that in his last moments Merrick had tried to save himself with a knife and a rope, and the rope had run out, or the knife had slipped, or the ice had simply been faster.

"Merrick!" His voice bounced off the serac walls and came back to him doubled, tripled, a chorus of ghosts. "MERRICK!"

Nothing. Only the wind, and beneath the wind — faint, rhythmic, patient — the sound of the glacier working.

He rigged his own anchor, a buried axe deadman, and paid himself down over the lip with the flare pistol jammed in his belt and his heart going like a drum. The crevasse walls closed around him, blue light fading to dusk to dark, and he swung his lamp in widening arcs.

Merrick hung below him, fifteen feet down, snagged in a constriction where the crack narrowed. Upside down. One leg twisted beneath him at an angle legs don't go. The harness had held; the fall had killed him anyway — or the cold had finished what the fall started, because there was no blood to speak of, just a fine rime of frost already blooming across his beard, his lashes, the open lenses of his eyes.

And in his raised right hand, frozen rigid, fingers locked — the sample vials.

Both of them. Clutched to his chest like a man clutching a child, or a fortune, or the last argument of his life. The brass caps glinted in Tam's lamplight. The liquid inside had frozen solid, opaque, and one vial showed a long fracture down its length where the impact had cracked it, a thread of dark seepage frozen mid-drip against the glass.

Tam hung there a long moment, breathing, looking at the man who had betrayed them all and who had taught him the name of the Mariner and who had died alone in the dark reaching for a fortune he'd never live to spend.

He thought about cutting the vials free. His hands actually moved toward them — the evidence, the sample, the thing the buyer wanted, the thing worth more than every debt any of them owed. One slice of the knife and he could carry the answer up into the daylight.

Then he looked at Merrick's face again. At the frost flowers on the open eyes. At the grip that death itself hadn't loosened.

*Everyone dies out here,* Merrick had said. *The question is whether you die for something.*

Tam cut the rope above the body instead. He climbed out of the blue dark into the grey day, and he stood at the lip of the crevasse for a moment with his head bowed, and then he turned and began the long walk back to camp — empty-handed, empty-sledged, carrying nothing out of the icefall but the knowledge of where the fifth man had ended, and the shape of his frozen grip burned into Tam's memory like a brand.

Behind him, as he crossed the boundary marker, the glacier gave a long, grinding groan, and high on the southern wall, a serac the size of a house leaned, shivered, and let go — collapsing into the lane where Merrick's sledge still stood, burying it, sealing the route, closing the book.

By the time Tam reached camp, the crew was rigged and waiting, and Beatrix took one look at his face and one look at his empty hands and asked no questions at all.

But it was Voss who spoke, quietly, watching the southern wall where the dust of the collapse still hung in the air like breath.

"Now we cross," the old man said. "While it's still letting us."
