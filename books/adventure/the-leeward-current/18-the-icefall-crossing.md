## 18. The Icefall Crossing

The red route began where the corridor ended — at a wall of shattered ice the height of a cathedral, split into blocks that leaned against one another like drunks holding each other upright. Wren had drawn it in her journal in a hand gone shaky with cold, three words beneath the line: *fast or never.*

Dawn came up gray behind us, and the glacier was already talking.

I'd heard ice settle my whole life on the Leeward — the deep groans, the cracks like rifle shots. This was different. This was a language. Every few minutes the whole valley exhaled, a long tectonic sigh that rolled up through my boots and settled in my teeth, and between the sighs came the smaller voices: the tick and hiss of pressure fractures, the wet grinding of seracs shifting their weight, somewhere below us the gurgle of meltwater finding new rooms in a house that was coming down around its own foundations.

"Calving season's open," Odile said, standing at the base of the wall with Wren's journal open in her mittened hands. She'd slept maybe two hours. We all had, in shifts, in the lee of a collapsed serac while the glacier shrugged off its winter skin. "The corridor behind us is gone. I watched it go at first light. It's the icefall or it's nothing."

"Then it's the icefall," Voss said. He said it the way he said everything now — flat, spent, a man carrying weight he couldn't set down. Nine days at the wreck had emptied him. What was left was still enough, barely, to lead.

He went first. I went second, on the rope, and behind me Ferro with the sledge, then Tam, then Odile, then Merrick's empty harness trailing on a second rope nobody had the heart to cut loose. We'd buried what was left of him two days back under a cairn of ice blocks, Tam's hands doing most of the work, and nobody had said a word over it except Tam, who'd said, quietly, "He was just a man who wanted to go home." Which was true, and not enough, and all there was.

The first pitch was a staircase of blue blocks, and we climbed it with our ice axes and our breath tearing white behind us, and for twenty minutes the glacier only grumbled. I let myself believe the grumbling was the worst of it.

That was the last easy thought I had for eleven hours.

* * *

The icefall was a river that had decided to become a mountain and then changed its mind halfway. Blocks the size of houses stood on end. Towers leaned at angles that made the eye flinch. Between them ran crevasses wide enough to swallow the sledge whole, some bridged with snow that looked solid and was not, some open to a depth the light refused to follow. Wren's route threaded through it all — a line drawn on paper by a woman with frostbitten fingers nine years ago, in a glacier that had been moving ever since.

Odile navigated by the journal and by the shape of the land behind the land. "The saddle," she'd say, pointing at a notch between two towers. "Wren marked a saddle with a split summit. There." And it would be there, or near enough, the glacier having dragged its furniture a few yards east or dropped it a few feet lower, and we would cross.

We crossed the first crevasse on a snow bridge that Ferro tested with a fallen block of ice and a length of rope, then crossed himself — the first time I'd ever seen him make the gesture — before he pulled the sledge across at a dead run. The bridge held. The glacier groaned its opinion.

The second one we jumped.

The third had no bridge at all, and we spent forty minutes cutting steps into the far wall of a blue slot while the ice ticked around us like something counting down. Tam went across first on the rope, swinging into the far wall, axes biting, and hauled himself up and over, and when his face appeared at the lip of the crevasse above me it was gray and streaming sweat and grinning anyway.

"Made it look easy," he called down.

"Liar," I called back, and threw him the rope end, and climbed.

* * *

It was at midday, on a slope of broken ice between two leaning seracs, that the glacier decided to test us properly.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like the world's largest door unlocking, deep and mineral and wrong — and then the serac to our left, the one the size of a granary, leaned. Not fell. *Leaned*, slow as a felled tree deciding, and Voss's voice cracked across the ice: "MOVE—"

We ran. There was no technique to it, no craft. We ran uphill on a slope of ice with axes flailing for purchase, and the serac came down behind us with a sound I felt in my sternum rather than heard, a white detonation, and the blast of displaced air shoved me flat against the slope and filled the world with snow.

When it cleared, the route behind us was gone. Not damaged. *Gone.* A hundred tons of ice sat where we'd been standing ten seconds earlier, and the debris field ran down the slope like a frozen avalanche, and where Wren's line had continued east there was now a wall of fresh-fractured blue.

"Route's dead," Odile said, when she'd stopped coughing. She was on her feet, checking faces, checking ropes, checking the sledge. Ferro had thrown himself over the cargo — over the evidence, over Voss's tin box of photographs and Wren's journal in its oilskin — and he gave a thumbs-up from the ice, breathing hard through his nose.

"Then we make a new one," Voss said.

"No." Odile was already turning pages, mittens clumsy, and I watched her find what she was looking for and go still. "Wren marked an alternate. Here — a traverse under the west wall, through the serac field. She wrote—" Odile's voice did something complicated. "She wrote *'bad ice, worse than the corridor, but it holds if the cold holds.'*"

"Then we pray for cold," Tam said.

Nobody laughed. We prayed for cold.

* * *

The traverse under the west wall was everything Wren's shaky handwriting had promised. The ice there was old — black-blue, dense as glass, squeezed for centuries — and it was *moving*, slow as honey, and the seracs above it shed blocks the way a rotting tree sheds branches: without warning, without rhythm, without mercy. We crossed in single file, roped at ten feet, moving when the glacier was quiet and freezing in place when it spoke, because you cannot run on ice that is talking. You can only be still and hope it is not talking about you.

It was there, on that traverse, with the west wall groaning overhead like a ship in a storm, that I nearly lost the compass.

It happened in three seconds. We'd stopped for one of the glacier's long exhalations, everyone frozen mid-step, and I'd reached up to check the rope at my chest — habit, nine years of habit, my hand going to the compass where it hung on its cord against my sternum, Wren's compass, the one thing I had carried of hers through every mile of desert and ice since I was twenty-six years old.

The cord snapped.

I felt it go — a tiny, obscene *tick* against my collarbone — and the compass dropped, bounced once off my boot, and went skating down the traverse slope on its brass back, spinning, flashing in the flat light, sliding toward a crevasse that opened at the slope's foot like a blue mouth.

I don't remember deciding to go after it. I remember the world narrowing to a brass disc and a line of descent, my body already moving, axe dropped, gloves scrabbling on glassy ice, and somewhere behind me Voss shouting my name in a voice I'd never heard him use, and the compass spinning slower, slower, coming to rest at the very lip of the crevasse, and me lunging for it with my whole body over the void—

—and a hand closing on my harness.

Ferro.

He'd come off the rope — broken the first rule of the ice, the only rule — and thrown himself down the slope after me, and his fist was locked in the webbing at my back with a grip like a winch, and my boots were on air, and the compass was an inch from my fingertips, teetering at the crevasse lip.

"Let it go," Ferro said. It was the first time I'd heard his voice in nine days. It was rough and low and absolutely calm. "Let it go, Beatrix."

"I can't—"

"It's brass." His grip tightened, hauling, my stomach dragging across the ice lip. "It's brass and it's cold and it doesn't know your sister's name. *You* know her name. Let it go."

And I don't know what would have happened — whether I'd have let it go, whether I could have, whether the compass or I would have gone into the blue — because my fingers closed on it. On the brass rim, on the cold of it, on the needle inside still pointing leeward, faithful as a heartbeat. And Ferro hauled, and I hauled, and between us we got my body back up the slope and flat against the old ice, both of us breathing like bellows, the west wall groaning its long complaint overhead.

I lay there with the compass crushed against my chest and shook. Not from cold. I'd been cold for two weeks. This was the other thing, the thing under the cold, the thing I'd been carrying since I was twenty-six, and it had almost gone into a crevasse on a glacier nine hundred miles from anywhere because I could not let one more piece of my sister go.

Ferro sat beside me and said nothing. He was good at that. After a while he took the snapped cord from my hand, and from his pocket he produced a length of wire — of course he did; the man carried wire the way priests carried scripture — and he bent it, twisted it, and looped it through the compass ring with two fingers in his mittens, and tied it off, and set the compass back against my sternum.

"Better cord than before," he said. "Won't snap."

"Thank you," I said. My voice came out in pieces.

He shrugged, which from Ferro was a sermon, and stood, and offered me his hand, and we went back to the rope.

* * *

We crossed the worst of the icefall in the last hour of light, when the cold finally, mercifully, deepened — twenty below, thirty, the glacier's voice dropping from groans to ticks to a silence so complete I could hear my own blood. Cold was the price of stillness and we paid it gladly. We moved fast and quiet across a field of pressure ridges that reared around us like a frozen sea, following Wren's line through a gap she'd marked with a single word — *gate* — and the gate was there, two leaning towers with a corridor between them wide enough for the sledge and not much more, and we went through it single file with our heads back, watching the towers lean over us, and we came out the far side onto the long white shoulder of the lower glacier, and the icefall stood behind us against the darkening sky like a city that had burned down while we watched.

Tam sat down on the sledge and laughed. Not because anything was funny. Because he was twenty-two years old and alive, and his body had to do something with it.

Odile stood looking back at the icefall for a long time. Then she took out the journal and, beneath Wren's red line, in her own careful cartographer's hand, she wrote the date, and four words: *crossed. all accounts paid.*

"Her accounts or ours?" Voss asked quietly.

"Both," Odile said, and closed the book.

We made camp on the shoulder, in the lee of a ridge, and Ferro got the stove going, and the smell of melting snow and the thin chemical sweetness of the fuel was the finest perfume I have ever known. We ate. We didn't talk much. Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, a spoon still in his hand, and Odile eased him down into the snow without waking him.

Voss came and sat beside me at the edge of the camp, where I was looking back the way we'd come. The icefall was a black serration against the last violet of the sky. Somewhere inside it, a ship lay frozen with its cargo burned and its secret kept, and it would lie there long after all of us were dust, and the glacier would carry it down toward the sea at the speed of a man walking very slowly, and that was right. That was as it should be.

"You went after it," Voss said.

"Yes."

"The compass."

"Yes."

He was quiet a while. "I watched you dive for a piece of brass over a crevasse," he said, "and I thought: that's the difference between her and me. I let go of everything. She can't let go of anything." He turned the tin cup in his hands. "I used to think that was your weakness, Beatrix. Out here I've learned it's the only reason any of us are still breathing. You hold on."

I put my hand over the compass, against my sternum, feeling the wire Ferro had twisted, cold through my glove.

"Hold on to something else now," I said. "The hard part's behind us."

Voss shook his head, and there was something in his face I didn't like — not fear exactly, but the memory of it, old and fresh at once. "The icefall was the mountain's honest work," he said. "Ice does what ice does. But we're two days from the lower trail, and the lower trail comes down to the valley, and the valley comes down to the mining road." He looked at me, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "Merrick didn't come out here alone, and he didn't come out here to die. Somebody paid him, and somebody will be asking why he hasn't reported in. We're carrying the one thing in this world that man's employer wants, and we're carrying it down a road."

I looked back at the icefall, at the dark, at the buried ship keeping its silence under a mile of moving ice.

"Then we'd better get down the mountain," I said, "before they come up it."

Behind us, far off in the black, so faint it might have been the glacier settling, something that was not the glacier made a sound — a thin, mechanical drone, rising and falling, rising and falling — and Ferro lifted his head from the stove like a dog catching a scent, and said one word:

"Engine."
