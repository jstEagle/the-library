## 19. What We Carry Out

The wind on the far side of the icefall was almost gentle, the way a hand is gentle after it has finished breaking something.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the moraine with her back to the mountains and let the lowland air fill her lungs — dust and lichen and meltwater, the smell of a world that still turned. Behind her, the Leeward Glacier shed its skin in slow thunder, calving towers of blue ice into the proglacial lake with reports that rolled across the valley like artillery fired by a patient army. The route they had come by no longer existed. The route they had left by was closing behind them hour by hour. Somewhere in that grinding white chaos lay a ship three centuries dead, and a woman nine years dead, and everything either of them had ever meant to anyone.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice weather — distantly, as a fact about the world rather than the self. She had crossed the icefall with the compass in her fist so tightly that its brass case had printed itself into her palm, a red rectangle she could still feel now, like a brand cooling.

Ferro sat on a supply crate twenty feet away, methodically cleaning grit out of the primus stove with a wire brush, saying nothing, because Ferro never said anything, and somehow always said exactly what was needed. Tam was asleep. The boy had gone down mid-sentence an hour after they made camp, face-first into his bedroll, still wearing his harness, one boot off and one boot on, and nobody had had the heart to wake him. He had carried more than his weight for six days. He had carried Merrick's sledge out of the crevasse field, or at least he had carried the fact of it, which weighed more.

Voss stood apart, as Voss always did, at the point where the moraine dropped toward the lake. He was holding his coat closed against the wind and looking back at the glacier the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

Odile was spreading her maps on a groundsheet, weighting the corners with stones, and Beatrix knew — had known since the icefall, since Wren's red line had saved all their lives — that the quiet argument they had been having for two hundred miles was about to stop being quiet.

* * *

They took stock at dusk, because Odile insisted, because Odile believed that inventory was a form of prayer.

She laid it all out on the groundsheet in the flat gold light: the fuel remaining, the food remaining, the state of the radio — repaired by Tam and Ferro with solder and stubbornness and a length of antenna wire salvaged from Merrick's abandoned sledge — the state of their bodies, the state of the route home. Ten days to the railhead at Kessel if the weather held. Five if it didn't hold and they were lucky.

Then she laid out the other inventory. The one that wasn't supplies.

Voss's oilskin packet came first. He set it down without ceremony: photographs taken inside the wreck's hold before the storm sealed them in, negatives wrapped in foil, pages copied from the first expedition's logbook — the real logbook, not the sanitized one that existed in the university archive — and a signed statement from the surviving member of that crew whom Voss had tracked down eleven years ago and sworn to silence. The paper was soft at the folds from being read too many times.

Wren's journal came next, and Beatrix watched Odile's hands hesitate over it, the way hands hesitate over a relic. Half the pages were fused into a single block of ice that had refrozen around the book in the cairn; the rest were legible, Wren's slanted handwriting marching across them, growing more hurried toward the end. Beatrix had read every unfused page twice on the crossing. She could have recited the last one from memory. She chose not to.

And then there was the third item, which Odile produced from her own kit with the reluctance of someone drawing a weapon: her own survey notes. Six weeks of soundings, sketches, bearings, the ship's position fixed to within thirty meters, the structure of the hull, the scorch patterns inside it, the composition of the cargo crates as far as they'd been able to determine before Voss ordered everyone out. Meticulous. Damning. Exactly the kind of document Odile had built her whole disgraced career on being able to produce.

"That's everything," Odile said. "That's what we carry out."

"No." Voss said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "That's what we carry *back*. There's a difference."

Beatrix had been waiting for this since the icefall. She'd felt it building between them like pressure in a crevasse bridge — that particular silence that means two people are both thinking the same forbidden thought and waiting to see who says it first.

"Say what you mean, Cassian," she said.

He turned from the lake. In the low light his face looked older than it had in Corren's Rest, older than it had nine years ago, older than it had any right to be. "I mean we burn it. All of it. The photographs, the copies, Odile's notes. The journal—" His eyes flicked to Beatrix, and for once he didn't look away. "The journal is yours. That's not mine to burn. But the rest goes into the lake tonight, and we file a report that says the route collapsed early, we lost equipment, we found nothing. Route impassable. Nothing found."

"You've had that phrase ready for a long time," Odile said.

"I've had it ready for eleven years. It's a good phrase. It kept people safe for eleven years." Voss crouched by the packet but didn't touch it. "You weren't there, Odile. You saw the hull. You saw the scorching. You know what the first crew found when they opened those crates. Do you want to explain to me what happens when a collector with Merrick's buyer's money reads your survey and sends a hundred men instead of five? Because I'll tell you. They'll bring drills and dynamite and a ship's crane if they have to, and they will open that cargo, and whatever burned Wren Whitlock's lungs out through a sealed hold will get a second chance at the world. And it won't be in a glacier next time. It'll be in a warehouse. A laboratory. A private collection with a lock on the door and a man with keys who doesn't believe in curses."

"The record isn't a curse," Odile said. Her voice was level, but Beatrix heard the flint in it. "It's a record. That's the entire point. You're proposing we do to science what Merrick tried to do to us — lie for money, except your price is silence instead of gold, and honestly I can't tell which of you is cheaper."

"Odile." Beatrix said her name once, a warning.

"No." Odile stood. She was a small woman and she made herself smaller when she was angry, coiled, precise, like a spring taking load. "No, Beatrix, I'm going to say this, because I've been polite for six weeks and politeness has cost me a career already. Cassian — you hired me to make maps. Not to make myths. Everything I have left in this profession depends on producing work that's true. I went along with the cover story about instruments because I needed the money and because I assumed whatever you were hiding was embarrassing, not apocalyptic. Now I've seen it. I've *mapped* it. And you want me to walk into Kessel and file a report that says nothing found, knowing that somewhere under that ice is the most significant maritime discovery in the history of the continent, and knowing that my name will be attached to the report that buried it?"

"Yes," Voss said. "Because your name being attached to a discovery gets people killed. Ask her." He nodded at Beatrix. "Ask her what a discovery cost."

The wind moved through the camp. Tam slept on. Ferro's wire brush had gone still.

"My sister," Beatrix said, "is not an argument."

Both of them looked at her.

"She's not a precedent and she's not a lesson and she is not a rhetorical device you two get to deploy at each other." Beatrix heard her own voice come out flat and cold and was grateful for it, because underneath the flatness was something enormous trying to stand up. "But since you've both decided she's on the table — Wren died keeping that cargo from reaching the coast. She died doing exactly what Cassian wants to keep doing. And Odile, you want to publish her death. You want to put her name in a footnote under 'expedition casualties' and print the coordinates of the thing that killed her so that every ambitious fool with funding can go stand where she stood. That's not a record. That's an invitation."

"It's the truth," Odile said.

"The truth is a cargo too," Beatrix said. "You don't get to just dump it on the market and call yourself honest. You decide what carrying it does."

* * *

The argument lasted until full dark, and then past dark, around the stove, with Tam awake now and sitting at the edge of the light with his knees pulled up, listening the way young people listen to the conversations that will decide what kind of adult they become.

It cost things. Beatrix could feel it costing even as it happened — small tears in the fabric of the crew that would not mend cleanly, threads pulled loose that would rattle in the seams forever.

Odile called Voss a coward, and meant it, and Voss took it without flinching, which was somehow more damning than if he'd denied it. "I've been a coward for eleven years," he said. "It's the most useful thing I've ever been. Every person alive today who might have walked into that valley and died opening a crate — they're alive because I was too cowardly to let curiosity win. You want courage, Odile? Courage is cheap. Courage is what Merrick had, striding off across the icefall with a sledge full of sample vials. Look how that ended."

"Don't you dare use him—"

"He's the honest version of your argument! He believed the truth should belong to whoever could pay for it, and the glacier collected the fee!" Voss's voice cracked on the last word, and Beatrix saw, with a strange clarity, that the crack wasn't performance. Eleven years of silence had been load-bearing, and Odile was pulling at it, and the whole structure was coming down in front of them. "Do you think I don't know what I'm asking? I'm asking you to spend your career to save strangers. I spent mine. I spent—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I spent a friendship. Ask her what that costs. She knows better than anyone."

And that was the cruelest cut of the night, because it was true, and because it landed on Beatrix like a debt coming due.

Nine years ago, Cassian Voss had ruined her reputation — that was the story Corren's Rest told, the story she herself had half-believed. The truth, extracted at dawn on the glacier in chapter after chapter of grief, was worse and simpler: he had cut her out of the first expedition's aftermath deliberately, publicly, unforgivably, because a ranger asking questions was a nuisance, and a disgraced ranger asking questions was nothing at all. He had made her the fall guy for his silence. He had let her hate him for nine years because hatred kept her away from the glacier, and distance kept her alive, and he had never once explained because an explanation would have led her back.

Every year of her exile had been his design. Every small pursuit that hadn't added up to anything — the salt-flat chases, the petty thieves, the ledger of debts — had been the shape of the cage he'd built around her, and he'd built it out of her own anger, and it had worked, and she had lived.

"I should hate you," Beatrix said to him, in front of all of them. "I spent nine years hating you and it turns out you were paying my salary the whole time. That's what you carried out of the first expedition, Cassian. Not guilt. A debt. To her. And you paid it by making me carry the interest."

Voss looked at her across the stove, and for the first time since Corren's Rest, since the contract, since the letter with no signature — the letter had been his, she understood now, the map and the date, dread dressed as curiosity, pride wrestling with need, all of it arranged by a man who couldn't ask forgiveness and wouldn't ask help — for the first time, he looked old.

"Yes," he said. "That's what it cost. So understand what I'm telling you now, Odile, when I say silence is worth it. I know the exchange rate."

* * *

Tam spoke for the first time near midnight.

He'd been quiet so long that his voice startled the camp — still cracking at the edges, a boy's voice doing a man's work. "Can I say something?"

"Go ahead," Odile said, wearily. She'd run out of arguments an hour ago and was defending her position now mostly through refusal to sit down.

"When we found Merrick." Tam looked at his hands. "In the crevasse field. He was frozen holding the vials. Like — he wouldn't let go of them even to try to climb out. One hand in the ice and one hand around the case." He looked up. "I keep thinking about why. He wasn't stupid. He knew the icefall was going. He ran anyway, with the samples, because the buyer's money only counted if he brought proof. And the proof killed him. Not the cold. The — the needing to prove it."

Nobody spoke.

"So I don't know the answer," Tam said. "But it seems like everybody who touches that stuff ends up holding it when they die. And maybe that's the finding. Maybe that's what the report should say." He shrugged, embarrassed, and pulled his blanket up. "Sorry. Go back to arguing."

He rolled over and was asleep again within minutes, because he was nineteen and the body takes what it needs. But the camp was silent for a long time after, and Beatrix watched Odile stare into the stove flame, and knew the boy had landed a blow neither of the veterans had managed.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when Odile finally sat down across from Beatrix, alone at the stove's dying heat. Voss had withdrawn to his tent. Ferro had never left; he sat in the shadows like a piece of equipment, present and unbothered, and Beatrix suspected he had heard every word and formed an opinion and would die before sharing it.

"You think I'm wrong," Odile said.

"I think you're right," Beatrix said. "That's the problem."

Odile blinked.

"Everything you said is true. The record matters. Names matter. If we bury this, we bury it under a lie, and lies have a way of surfacing with worse timing than truths." Beatrix fed the last of the fuel block to the flame and watched it catch. "But here's what I know that you don't, Odile. I've read her journal. All of it. The parts that survived." She paused. The words were heavy and she lifted them anyway. "Wren knew. At the end, she knew exactly what she was doing, and she wrote it down, and she chose — she *chose* — that no one should ever know. Not for glory. Not for science. She chose it because she did the arithmetic you're refusing to do. She counted the lives against the knowledge and she paid with hers to make the number come out right."

Odile was very still. "What did she write?"

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. "When we're home," she said. "At Corren's Rest. When everyone's together and there's no glacier listening. I'll read you the last page. All of you. And then we decide — all of us, together, with her voice in the room. Not before."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got. You want the record. Fine. But the record starts with her, and she hasn't spoken yet." Beatrix stood, joints protesting, nine years and six weeks of ice settled into them. "One day to Kessel's railhead, if the weather holds. Sleep, Odile. Whatever we decide, we decide it warm."

Odile didn't move. As Beatrix turned away, she heard her say, quietly, to the stove or to herself or possibly to Wren:

"I hope your sister knows what she's asking me to lose."

Beatrix walked to the edge of the camp and stood facing north, toward the railhead, toward home, toward the decision waiting for them like a door.

Behind her, the glacier calved again — a long, rolling boom, like a verdict postponed.

And in her pocket, against her palm, the compass needle trembled and settled, pointing leeward, pointing back, pointing at everything they were carrying out and everything they were leaving behind — and Beatrix understood, with a certainty that felt like frost forming, that whatever Wren's last page said, one of the two people arguing over her grave was going to walk away from Corren's Rest with the friendship broken past mending.

She only prayed it wouldn't be the crew that broke.
