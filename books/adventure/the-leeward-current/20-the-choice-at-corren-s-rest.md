## 20. The Choice at Corren's Rest

The town smelled of woodsmoke and horse and the particular dust of home, which is to say it smelled of nothing at all, and Beatrix stood in the street outside the Salt Larder with her pack still on her shoulders and let the ordinariness of it wash over her like a tide.

Three weeks on the ice. Three weeks of white wind and blue shadow and the groan of a glacier coming apart beneath their boots. And now: a dog barking two streets over. A woman arguing cheerfully about the price of flour. A boy hauling water with both hands, spilling half of it, not caring.

Tam stumbled past her and sat down hard on the loading dock, laughing — actually laughing, high and disbelieving, the laugh of someone who had been certain he was going to die and had just now remembered he wasn't. Ferro stood beside him, saying nothing as always, but his hand came down once on Tam's shoulder and stayed there, and that was more speech than most men managed in a year.

Odile came out of the larder's doorway with her face set like a woman walking into weather. "Beatrix."

"What."

"There are men inside. Two of them. They've been asking after us since yesterday." Odile's voice was low, flat. "They have papers. Contracts. They knew our names before we gave them."

Beatrix felt the cold come back into her hands, the ice-country cold that had never really left since the calving route. She shouldered past Odile and through the door.

The Salt Larder's back room smelled of tallow and old beer. The two men sat at the corner table like they'd grown there — one gray and soft, one young and sharp-eyed, both wearing the kind of clothes that were expensive in a way designed not to look expensive. A leather folio lay open between them. Beside it, a strongbox.

The gray one rose. "Ranger Whitlock. We're glad you made it back. Truly. The mountains took a toll, we understand—"

"Who's *we*?"

"My employer prefers discretion. You'll forgive me if I don't—" He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced on many faces. "You've had a long journey. Perhaps we could speak when you've rested."

"We can speak now." She didn't sit. "Say what you came to say."

He glanced at the young one, then back, and folded his hands on the table like a man settling in for scripture. "My employer funded an expedition to the Leeward Glacier eleven years ago. He has reason to believe certain items of scientific interest remain uncollected. He is prepared to offer substantial compensation for their recovery — or, failing that, for accurate information regarding their condition. There is also," and here the smile thinned by a degree, "a matter of certain records. Journals. Notes. My employer would consider their return a gesture of good faith, and would compensate accordingly."

"And if I say no?"

The smile held. "Then my employer will be disappointed. And when he is disappointed, he tends to become... thorough. There are ways this goes easily, Miss Whitlock, and ways it does not. Your debts alone—"

"You know about my debts."

"We know everything worth knowing. That's rather the point of us."

Behind her, she heard the door open. Voss came in with the slow deliberate tread of a man who had already decided how this was going to go, and Merrick's absence hung around him like smoke — though these two wouldn't know about Merrick yet, wouldn't know about the crevasse field, wouldn't know anything except that the expedition had come back thin and quiet and carrying sledge-wraps nobody had asked to see opened.

Voss stopped beside her. "Tell your employer," he said, "the route is sealed. Permanently. Tell him I'll swear to it again, in whatever court or ledger he likes. Nothing was found. Nothing exists."

"Mr. Voss." The gray man's tone turned almost gentle. "We both know that isn't true. We've known it for eleven years. Why do you think the first contract specified *recovery*, not discovery?"

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — something old and tired and very much like fear — and Beatrix understood, suddenly, completely, why he had sworn off the glacier. Not because of what was buried in it. Because of who kept asking after it.

"The answer's no," she said.

"It doesn't have to be tonight." The gray man slid a card across the table, heavy stock, no name, only an address in the capital and a date three days hence. "But it has to be soon. Good evening, Miss Whitlock. Mr. Voss."

They gathered their folio and their smiles and went out into the dust, and Beatrix stood looking at the card on the table until Odile picked it up and read it and read it again.

"They'll come back," Odile said. "Men like that always come back."

"I know."

"So what do we do?"

Beatrix looked around the room. Voss by the door, gray and grim. Odile with the card in her fist. Tam in the doorway, Ferro behind him, both of them listening. Her crew. Her debt-ledger crew, every one of them owed something, every one of them paid in full now except for the one thing nobody could invoice.

"Tonight," she said. "Everyone. The back room of my place. There's something you need to hear."

* * *

She hadn't opened the journal since the far side of the icefall.

It lay on her table where she'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth, half-fused pages thawed and dried and warped into shapes like autumn leaves. Wren's handwriting ran across them in that quick slanted script Beatrix had spent nine years trying to forget and never managing — the same hand that had written *gone to the pass, back by dark* on the note Beatrix still kept in the compass case.

The crew crowded into the small room. Voss took the chair by the window and didn't touch the whiskey Odile poured him. Tam sat on the floor with his back against Ferro's knee. Odile stood, arms crossed, the card from the agents still in her pocket like a splinter.

Beatrix unwrapped the journal and turned to the last legible page.

"I'm going to read this," she said. "All of it. Once. Then we decide what happens to it. All of us. Nobody decides alone — not Voss, not me. That's the deal."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the town went on being ordinary. She looked down at her sister's hand and began.

---

*"Day 41. The ship is real. I keep writing that because I still don't believe it, and if I write it enough times maybe it will stop feeling like the ice is lying to us.*

*A sailing ship, masts down, hull whole, frozen forty fathoms deep in the Leeward and visible only because the meltwater carved a lens in the blue ice. The captain says centuries old. The charts say there has never been sea here. Both things are true and neither one makes sense and I have stopped sleeping.*

*The cargo hold is intact. That's what changed everything. Because the cargo is not cargo. It's powder — black grain, packed in wax-sealed casks, thousands of pounds of it, and the markings on the casks are in no language any of us knows, and the wood of the ship itself is scorched. From the inside. Whatever happened aboard her happened fast and hot and was not enough to burn her, only to mark her.*

*Merrick — not ours, the old one, the surveyor — says the buyer will pay a fortune for even one cask. Says it's chemistry beyond anything we have. Says the world will be better for knowing.*

*I watched the men look at those casks today and I saw what was in their faces and it wasn't wonder. It was hunger. The same hunger I've seen in mining camps and claim-jumpers and the eyes of men counting other men's cattle. This isn't knowledge. Knowledge doesn't make your hands shake like that.*

*Day 44. The captain and three others want to haul a cask out. Voss says no. They nearly came to blows at the wreck. I have never seen Cassian afraid before. He is afraid now, and it is not of the ice.*

*Day 45. We voted. It went wrong. Half the crew sides with the captain. So we did the thing we swore we wouldn't do, and I am writing this so that someone, someday, knows it was not done lightly.*

*We burned the cargo. In the hold, in the ice, with what fuel we had and the ship's own tarred rigging. It took two days. The heat cracked the lens above us and the light came down blue as a church window and I thought, this is the most terrible beautiful thing I will ever see, and I was right.*

*Two men died getting clear of the burn. The rest of us agreed to seal the route. To say nothing. To let the mountain keep her. Because whatever that powder is, it was carried inland by a ship that had no business existing, and the people who built it wanted it lost, and I think they had reasons we cannot imagine and should not test.*

*If you are reading this, you are probably my sister, because you are the only person stubborn enough to follow me here, and I am sorry, Bee. I am so sorry. But listen: some truths are doors, and not every door should be opened just because you found the handle. The men who want what's in this ice will not stop at one cask. They will strip the mountain. They will call it science and it will be greed wearing science's coat, and the ones who pay the price will be people whose names will never appear in any record at all.*

*So I choose silence. Not because the truth isn't true. Because the truth, loose in the world, would cost more than it's worth, and the cost would land on people who never got a vote.*

*Keep the compass. Point it leeward. Forget me kindly.*

*— W."*

---

The room was silent for a long time. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the street the dog was still barking, patient and pointless, and Beatrix realized her hands were shaking and set the journal down flat so no one would see.

Tam spoke first, quietly, from the floor. "She knew. Nine years ago, she knew exactly what those men would be."

"She knew," Voss said. His voice was gravel. "I told you all on the ice what happened. What I didn't tell you is that I argued her side of it wrong. I wanted the record. I wanted the glory of the find, the papers, the names in the journals. Wren talked me down in one night. One night, and I spent nine years pretending I'd sworn off the glacier out of principle, when really I just couldn't stand to look at the place where I'd been small." He looked up at Beatrix. "Your sister was the best of us. I've known that longer than you have."

Odile hadn't moved. Now she uncrossed her arms slowly, like someone setting down something heavy. "I argued for the record," she said. "On the far side of the icefall. I argued that the world deserves the truth." She looked at the journal. "And I still believe that. Generally. As a rule." A breath. "But this isn't a rule. This is a door. And I've seen what's standing on the other side of it, waiting with contracts." She tapped her pocket, where the card was. "Wren's math holds. Some truths cost more than they're worth, and the bill goes to the wrong people."

Ferro said nothing. He never said anything. But he reached out and closed the journal's cover gently, the way you close a coffin lid, and nodded once, and that was his vote.

Tam looked around at all of them. "What about Merrick?" he asked. "He died for a sample that shattered in his hand. Doesn't he get — I don't know. Doesn't it mean something that he believed it was worth it?"

"It means," Beatrix said, "that the price is real. That's what it means."

She stood, and took the journal, and crossed to the stove.

"Wait," Voss said.

"No. You waited nine years. I'm done waiting." She looked at each of them in turn. "This is the last chance. Anyone who wants the record, say it now, and I'll put it in a bank vault instead of a fire, and we'll fight the lawyers and the agents and whoever's behind them for ten years, and maybe we win. Say it now."

Nobody said it.

So she fed the journal to the stove, page by warped page, and they watched Wren's handwriting curl and blacken and go up the flue in a ribbon of sparks, out into the ordinary night air of Corren's Rest, over the rooftops, gone. Beatrix kept her eyes open the whole time. She owed her sister that much — a witness, not a mourner.

When it was done, the room felt lighter and emptier at once, the way a house feels after a funeral.

"The agents have until the third day," Odile said. "What do we tell them?"

"Nothing," Beatrix said. "We tell them nothing, we sign nothing, we sell nothing. And when they threaten us — and they will — we tell them the route is impassable and the man who funded the first expedition can send as many contracts as he likes to a mountain that eats them." She picked up the compass from the shelf, turned it over once in her hand. The needle swung and settled, steady, pointing leeward. "Wren bought our silence with her life. The least we can do is honor the purchase."

Voss rose. For a moment he looked like he might say something — an apology nine years overdue, a debt of his own finally named — but instead he simply took his hat off the peg and said, "I leave tomorrow. Before they come back. There's a coast steamer, and after that, places a man can be forgotten." He paused at the door. "Whitlock. If you ever need—"

"I won't," she said. "But thank you for offering anyway."

He almost smiled. Then he was gone, and the sound of his boots faded down the street toward wherever a man goes to start being forgotten.

Tam lingered. "Ranger Whitlock? The compass. On the ice, you nearly lost it, and you went — I've never seen anyone look like that. What is it, really?"

Beatrix weighed it in her palm. Brass worn smooth, glass scratched, needle faithful to a direction that meant nothing on any chart.

"It's not mine," she said. "It never was. And in a few days, I'm going to take it somewhere it belongs."

Tam nodded slowly, and something in his young face said he understood more than she'd given him credit for, and then he too went out into the night, and Ferro with him, and Odile last, pausing at the door.

"For what it's worth," Odile said, "I think she'd have liked how it ended. Your sister."

"She'd have hated the burning," Beatrix said. "But she'd have understood it. That was worse, and better."

Odile smiled faintly, and left, and Beatrix sat alone in the tick of the cooling stove with the compass in her hand and the ash smell in the air, and outside, at the far end of the street, a lantern came around the corner and stopped — two figures, standing, watching her window.

The agents weren't finished. Of course they weren't. Men like that never were.

But the morning was coming, and with it the road north to the glacier's edge, and a cairn of stones under which a sister slept beside a secret, and a compass that had one last journey left in it — not toward anything, this time, but toward letting go.
