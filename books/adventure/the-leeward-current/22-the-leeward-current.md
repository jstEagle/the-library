## 22. The Leeward Current

The salt flats had not changed, and that was the first mercy of the year.

Beatrix walked them at dawn, when the crust held the night's cold and gave back a sound like porcelain under her boots — a thin ringing with every step, as if the whole white plain were a bell struck softly and endlessly. The sun came up behind her and laid her shadow out ahead, long and blue-black across the salt, and she followed it because there was nothing else to follow and no reason to follow anything.

A year ago she had crossed this same ground with a rope in one hand and a thief's collar in the other, breathing hard, angry in the small worn way that had become her only weather. She had caught the man — Doss, his name was, a salt-gatherer gone desperate — and she had let him go because he'd looked at the compass on her belt and said, *My grandmother had one like that. Never pointed north a day in its life.* She had stood there in the heat with her fist full of his shirt and felt something shift in her chest like ice settling, and she had opened her hand.

She thought about him sometimes. Not often. But when she did, it was with something close to gratitude, which was a strange thing to feel toward a man whose face you'd pressed into the salt.

Today the flats were empty. No thief, no quarry, no contract folded into her coat. She carried water, a little food, and the compass, and the compass was not for sale or trade or proof of anything. It was going home.

The town fell away behind her by midmorning — Corren's Rest with its tin roofs and its windmill turning its slow half-turns, the assay office where Odile's report now lived in a drawer: *route impassable, nothing found.* Beatrix had read it once, standing over Odile's shoulder while the cartographer signed it, and Odile had looked up at her with those level gray eyes and said, "You're sure."

"I'm sure," Beatrix had said.

"You could still change your mind. The record matters. Records are how the dead get to speak."

"Wren spoke," Beatrix said. "I heard her." And Odile had nodded once, capped her pen, and filed the lie that was also, in its way, the truest report anyone had ever written about the Leeward Glacier.

Now the town was a smudge, then a line, then nothing, and the desert took over — the red earth, the greasewood, the long swales where the wind combed the sand into ridges like the fur of some enormous sleeping animal. Beatrix walked the way she had walked for twenty years, reading the ground without meaning to: coyote tracks stitched along a wash, the old survey cairns slumped into gray teeth, the place where the stage road used to run before the river changed its mind. Her eyes did the work whether she asked them to or not. That was the ranger in her, the tracker, the woman who had spent nine years hunting small things across large empty places because the large thing she'd lost could not be hunted down.

But the large thing had been found. That was the strange arithmetic of this year. She had gone up onto the ice to retrieve instruments and come down carrying a truth too heavy to set down anywhere but in silence, and somewhere between the icefall and Corren's Rest the weight had changed. It had stopped being a debt. It had become an inheritance.

* * *

She camped that first night at the edge of the scrubland, where the green began — the threshold country, willow and cottonwood and the first cold breath off the high glaciers moving down the valley like water finding its level. She built a small fire, drank tea, and took the compass out of her coat.

It sat in her palm the way it always had: brass worn to a soft gold at the edges, the glass scratched in one corner where Wren had dropped it on the assay office steps the winter before she left, the needle resting in its lazy arc, pointing leeward. Always leeward. Toward the glacier. Toward the ship frozen in the blue heart of the ice, hull scorched from the inside, cargo burned by six people who had understood what they were holding and what it would do if it ever reached a harbor, a market, a hungry collector's vault. Toward Wren, who had stayed.

For nine years Beatrix had believed the compass was broken. A memento, a wound, a piece of her sister that pointed nowhere because nowhere was where Wren had gone. She had carried it through every contract, every pursuit, every petty chase across the salt, and it had never once pointed north, and she had never once forgiven it.

Then Voss had told her what the first expedition found, and Merrick had offered her enough money to erase every debt she'd ever owed, and she had stood in the wreck's shadow with the compass open in her hand and watched the needle swing — not wildly, not uselessly, but steadily, patiently, leeward — and she had understood that it had never been broken. It had been pointing the whole time. It had known where its owner lay before Beatrix knew the ship existed. Whatever metal lived inside it, whatever current moved it, it had spent nine years faithful to the only direction that mattered.

Some instruments don't measure the world, she thought, turning it in the firelight. They measure the person holding them. Hers had measured the distance between her and her sister, and it had taken her a year and a glacier to walk it down to nothing.

She closed the lid. The click of it was very loud in the dark.

* * *

The second day she climbed.

The foothills took her up through the green and then out of it, the trees shrinking to krummholz, the krummholz to lichen, the lichen to bare stone and the first dirty skirts of the glacier tongue, gray with a year's dust, weeping meltwater into channels that braided and rejoined. The air thinned and sharpened. By afternoon she could see the valley proper, the white corridor where they had nearly died, and her body remembered it before her mind consented to — the specific ache in her calves from the crevasse fields, the tightness across her ribs where the sledge harness had bitten, the way her lungs had burned in the whiteout when Odile's voice came through the storm calling bearings off a dead woman's notes.

She saw the icefall from two miles out and stopped walking.

It had changed. Of course it had. A year of calving had chewed the route Wren had marked in red into something new — seracs toppled, the great leaning towers of ice rearranged like furniture in a giant's house, the gap where Ferro had pulled her back from the compass lying loose in the wind now welded shut with fresh blue ice. There was no route anymore. There was only the mountain, remaking itself, keeping its own counsel.

Good, she thought. Let it be hard. Let it be impassable. Let Odile's report stand true in every way that counted.

She made camp early, above the meltwater streams, and slept badly in the way of people returning to places where they have been afraid. Twice she woke to the sound of the glacier — a deep internal groan, miles long, the sound of a living thing shifting in its sleep — and both times she lay listening until it faded, and both times she found herself thinking not of fear but of Ferro, who had heard that sound every day for three days and never once changed expression, and Tam, who had gone out alone into the crevasse field against orders and come back carrying grief instead of glory, and Voss, who had paid everyone in full and vanished before the train pulled out of Corren's Rest, leaving nothing behind but a ledger with every line crossed out and one word written at the bottom in his cramped hand: *paid.*

She wondered where he was. She hoped it was somewhere warm. She suspected it wasn't. Men who bury things in ice tend to live near their burials.

* * *

On the third day she reached the cairn.

Wren's cairn. Built by her sister's hands nine years ago, at the edge of the glacier's tongue where the ice began in earnest — stones stacked with the particular care Wren had brought to everything, each one fitted, the whole thing standing waist-high and stubborn against a decade of wind. Beatrix had visited it twice since coming down off the ice. Once with the crew, the day after the crossing, when they had stood around it in the exhausted silence of survivors and Voss had taken off his hat. And once alone, in the autumn, when she had come up to say the things she hadn't managed to say in front of other people.

Today she knelt beside it and put her hands flat on the top stone. It was warm. Nine years of summers lived inside it.

"Wren," she said.

Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. She had rehearsed nothing. She had planned this trip the way she planned all her trips — water, rations, route — and had refused to plan the part that mattered, because the part that mattered couldn't be planned. You didn't script a conversation with the dead. You just showed up and let them talk first.

The wind talked. It came down the valley off the ice, cold and clean, moving through the stones of the cairn with a faint hollow note, and Beatrix sat back on her heels and listened to it the way she had learned to listen to everything on the glacier: without demanding meaning.

"I brought it," she said finally, and took the compass from her coat.

She held it in both hands for a while. The brass was cold from the morning's climb and warmed slowly against her palms, and the needle inside lay quiet, pointing leeward, patient as it had been for nine years, as it would have been for ninety.

"I spent a long time thinking this was yours," she said. "A piece of you I got to keep because the rest of you went where I couldn't follow. I carried it like a debt. Everywhere. Every job, every chase, every sorry stretch of salt — I figured if I just kept working, kept collecting, kept paying things down, someday the ledger would balance and you'd be even with me and I could stop." She laughed, short and without much humor. "Ledgers don't work like that. Odile tried to tell me. Voss definitely tried to tell me, in his way, which is to say badly and nine years too late."

The wind moved. Somewhere up-valley, the glacier calved — a single deep report, like a door closing in a very large house — and the sound rolled down the corridor of stone and washed over her and passed on.

"But it wasn't a debt," she said. "I know that now. It was a message. You were pointing me the whole time, weren't you? Not north. Not home. Leeward. Toward the thing you stayed to guard. You wanted me to find it — or maybe you just trusted that if I ever did, I'd understand why you did what you did. And I do." Her throat tightened. She let it. "I read your last page, Wren. I read it out loud to five people in a hotel room in Corren's Rest while men with contracts waited downstairs, and every one of us chose what you chose. Nobody knows your name except us. Nobody will. The ship stays buried, the cargo stays ash, and the world gets to keep its comfortable story about the Leeward expedition lost to weather. Weather. As if the mountain killed you." She shook her head slowly. "As if anything so ordinary could have."

She opened the compass.

The needle swung once, settled, and pointed — as it always pointed, as it would point until the mechanism rusted solid — leeward. Up the valley. Into the white. Toward the blue depth of the ice where a three-hundred-year-old ship lay embedded like a fly in amber, its scorched hull holding its terrible secret, and somewhere near it, unmarked, unrecoverable, the sister who had lit the match.

"There's a version of me that keeps this," Beatrix told her. "Carries it another thirty years, hands it to somebody's kid eventually, makes it a relic. There's a version that sells it — Merrick taught me there's a price for everything, if you're willing to stop being a person to collect it. And there's a version that leaves it right here, on your cairn, where the needle can spend the rest of its life doing the only thing it ever wanted to do."

She turned the compass over in her hands. Wren's initials were scratched inside the lid — *E.W.* — small and crooked, done with a knife point when she was eleven years old and certain the instrument would define her life. It had. Just not the way she'd guessed.

"Pointing at you," Beatrix said. "That's all it's ever done. So let it."

She set it on the flat crown of the cairn, wedged it gently between two stones so the wind couldn't take it, and opened the lid fully so the needle faced the sky and the valley beyond. From here, if you knew what you were looking at, it pointed straight up the corridor of the Leeward Glacier, into the calving country, toward the buried ship and the burned cargo and the woman who had died securing both.

From here, it pointed at Wren.

Beatrix stayed kneeling a long time. The sun climbed. The meltwater ran. Once, far up the icefall, a serac leaned and fell with a sound like thunder learning to be gentle, and she watched the powder snow bloom white against the blue and thought: *she's under all of that, and it's beautiful, and it's hers.*

"Debt's paid," she said quietly. "Not by me. By you. Nine years ago. I was just slow reading the ledger." She stood, knees cracking, and brushed the grit from her trousers. "I'm not hunting anything anymore, Wren. Took me long enough. Don't tell anyone — I've got a reputation."

The wind took her words and carried them up-valley, leeward, and she let it.

* * *

She was halfway down the moraine when she saw the figure coming up the trail.

Small, quick, burdened with a surveyor's pack that was clearly too big for it, moving with the eager slightly-overbalanced stride of someone who hadn't yet learned to let the mountain set the pace. Beatrix stopped and shaded her eyes and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was still clenched.

Tam.

The porter — not a porter anymore; Odile had signed him on as a junior surveyor within a month of the dissolution, and by all reports the boy had a gift for contours — crested the rise below her, saw her, and stopped dead. Even at fifty yards Beatrix could see the grin break across his face.

"Ranger Whitlock!" he called. "They said you'd come up this week. Odile bet me you'd already be at the cairn by the time I got here." He puffed up the last of the slope, flushed and breathing hard, and stuck out a hand as formal as a treaty. She shook it. His grip had gotten stronger. Everything about him had gotten steadier.

"What are you doing up here?" she asked.

"Resurvey." He tapped the pack. "Odile wants the icefall mapped again — it's moved more than she predicted. New route reconnaissance, she says, though she won't say for what." He glanced up the valley, and his voice dropped into the register they had all learned on the ice, the one reserved for the thing they didn't discuss. "You?"

Beatrix looked back the way she'd come. The cairn was out of sight below the shoulder of the moraine, and the compass with it, doing its one work in the wind.

"Paying a visit," she said. "Settling accounts."

"And?" Tam asked. He was young enough to ask plainly. "How does it feel? Being done?"

Beatrix considered the question honestly, the way she considered terrain. Below her the desert waited, wide and sun-struck and utterly indifferent, the salt flats a white gleam on the far horizon like a coin someone had left face-up. Above her the glacier went on groaning and shedding and keeping its secret, and somewhere in the middle distance between those two worlds there was a life she hadn't lived yet, with no ledger in it, no quarry, no debt — just a woman who knew how to read ground and keep her word, walking into country that owed her nothing and needed nothing from her.

"It feels like the flats at dawn," she said. "Like the whole thing's ringing, and I finally know why."

Tam frowned, working it over, and then — because he was Tam, and because the ice had taught him that some answers arrive sideways — he grinned and shouldered his pack. "Come down to camp," he said. "Ferro sent coffee. Actual coffee. Says the coast has ruined him for anything else and we all have to suffer for it."

"Ferro talks now?"

"With Ferro it counts as shouting." Tam started down the trail, then paused, looking back up the valley one more time — at the icefall, at the white corridor, at whatever lived in the blue depths there. "Ranger? Do you ever think about telling? Still? After everything?"

Beatrix followed his gaze. The mountain stood silent under the noon sun, vast and blue-shadowed and self-possessed, holding what it held.

"Every day," she said. "And every day it gets easier not to. That's how you know it's the right kind of secret — the kind that gets lighter the longer you carry it, instead of heavier." She clapped him on the shoulder and turned downhill, toward the smell of woodsmoke and coffee, toward the low green country, toward the rest of her life. "Come on. Lead the way. You know the route better than I do now."

Behind them, at the top of the moraine, the wind moved over Wren's cairn and hummed through the stones, and the compass needle held its bearing — steady, faithful, pointing leeward — toward the sister who stayed, and the ship that slept, and the current that ran beneath the ice, unseen, unfound, and free.
