## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The wreck had no name on any chart Petra Brightwater had ever seen, and she had seen most of them — the good ones, the bad ones, the ones drawn by drunk cartographers who'd clearly never stood on a deck in their lives. It sat forty feet down on the seaward shoulder of Gullwrack Shoal, a black hulk of oak and iron that the fishermen called the Widow and refused to discuss beyond that single syllable of grief. Even the navy charts showed nothing there. Just clean blue water and a depth notation, as if the sea itself had been paid to forget.

Petra hadn't been paid to forget. She'd been paid — barely — to remember, and to bring back whatever the Widow was holding.

She hung in the green half-light above the wreck now, checking her gauges out of habit rather than need. Air at two-thirds. Bottom time twelve minutes. The current was running lazy today, a long slow breath from the southwest that stirred the silt off the wreck's flanks and let her see more than the usual murk. Forty feet below her fins, the hull rose out of the sand like the ribcage of something enormous that had died with its mouth open. Weed hung from the gunwales in yellow-brown curtains. A school of barracuda held station off the bow, silver needles, watching her with the flat indifference of fish that had seen divers come and go for longer than she'd been alive.

Her light swept the deck. Nothing new. The hatches had been stripped years ago — by whom, she'd never been able to learn, though the contract that paid her three hundred a week to dive here twice a month didn't invite questions. The work was simple: survey the hull, photograph anything that shifted, and if she found anything that *wasn't* there on the last dive, she was to leave it exactly where it lay, surface, and report. The money came through a man named Ferris, who worked out of a rented office above the chandlery and had the soft hands and softer voice of someone who had never done an honest day's labor in salt water. Ferris never asked how her mother was. Ferris asked about depth readings and silt conditions and whether the navy patrol boat had come by, and Petra, who needed the three hundred more than she needed conversation, answered what she was asked and kept her curiosity in the same sealed compartment where she kept her debts.

Except curiosity, like water, always found the crack.

She dropped toward the wreck, finning slow, letting the current carry her along the port side where the planking had rotted away to show the dark ribs beneath. The light went ahead of her, a yellow coin of brightness rolling over wood gone soft as old cheese. She knew this wreck now the way she knew the Kestrel's engine room — every seam, every collapse, every place where the structure had decided, decades ago, to give up and lie down. The stern cabin's roof had caved in the winter before last. The mainmast had rolled six feet toward the bow sometime in the spring, groaning through the water in a way she'd felt in her chest more than heard. The Widow was dying all over again, slowly, and Petra was one of the few people alive who had watched it happen.

Which was why, when her light found the gap in the sand near the forefoot, she stopped.

She knew this sand. She had memorized this sand. There was a drift pattern here, a long shallow dune that built against the hull's leeward side and scoured away after every blow, and she could have drawn it from memory with her eyes closed. The dune was wrong today. It was cut — cleanly, deliberately, by hands and tools, not current — in a trench maybe two feet wide running from the hull's shadow out toward open bottom. Someone had dug here. Recently. The spoil was still heaped in a ridge that the current hadn't yet had time to soften, and the edges of the cut were sharp enough that she could see the pale sand beneath the gray surface layer.

Petra hung there in the water, breathing slow, and made herself think it through the way her grandfather — no. The way an experienced salvor would think it through. Divers came to the Widow. She'd seen evidence of them before: a dropped wrench gone to rust-blossom, a cut line, a scoured patch where someone had anchored carelessly. But they came, they looked, and they left. Nobody dug. Nobody dug because there was nothing here to dig for — the wreck had been picked over before Petra was born, stripped down to the bones, left as a shell for the fish and the silt. That was the whole reason the contract existed. Survey work on a dead hulk. Make-work. A retainer to keep someone's eyes on a wreck nobody wanted and nobody would explain.

So who had dug a trench into her dune?

She should have done exactly what the contract said. Photograph it, leave it, surface, report. Ferris's voice was in her ear, dry as paper: *You observe, Miss Brightwater. You do not disturb.* And she would have — she was good at this job precisely because she was disciplined, because she could hang in cold green water with her lungs burning and do the boring, careful, correct thing — except that as she swung her light along the trench, something at its far end caught the beam and threw it back.

Not wood. Not stone. Metal, and not rusted. Bright.

She looked at her gauges again. Fifteen minutes. The trench was eight feet long. She could be there and back in three.

She told herself it was procedure. She told herself a lot of things, down there in the quiet, and most of them were lies, and she knew it, and she went anyway.

The trench ended at a cavity the diggers had opened in the sand where the hull's forefoot met the bottom — a pocket between two ribs, sheltered, the kind of place where things fell and were never found. Her light found the object inside before her hand did: a strongbox, iron-banded, maybe eighteen inches long, furred with a century of marine growth except where the diggers had scrubbed it clean. They'd been interrupted. The growth was stripped from the top and one side, and the rest of the box still wore the sea like a shroud. Their tools were gone. Their marks in the sand were sharp. They had been pulled off this work — or frightened off it — within the last few days.

The box had a lock, and the lock was intact, and set into the iron of the lid, where the growth had been scraped away, was a crest.

Petra had grown up around crests the way harbor kids grow up around rope: shipping houses, salvage firms, the little stamped insignias on bale seals and brass fittings that told you who had owned what and where it had come from. She knew the great marks of the coast — the Corvane fleet's three masts inside a ring, the harbor authority's lighthouse, the navy's anchor-and-crown. She could identify a dozen dead companies by their dies alone.

She had never seen this one.

It was simple, which was what made it strange. Most crests shouted. This one didn't. A wave, stylized, curling left. Above it, a single star. Below, a line of lettering too worn to read, though she traced it with her glove and thought it might be two words, the first starting with what felt like a *B*. Around the whole of it ran a border of small repeated marks — not decoration, she realized after a long moment of study. Characters. A script of some kind, tiny and precise, marching around the crest like a fence.

She photographed it. She photographed the box from four angles, the trench, the spoil heap, the cavity in the hull. Her hands were steady. Her breathing wasn't, and she noticed that, and corrected it, because panic down here was a luxury that killed people, and she had no intention of dying over an iron box in eight feet of trench on a wreck nobody would admit existed.

The box was heavy. That was the thing she'd remember afterward, in the long bad hours to come — not the crest, not the mystery, but the sheer stubborn weight of it as she got her hands under the iron edges and lifted, the silt billowing up around her in a soft gray explosion, the barracuda scattering from the bow like a handful of thrown knives. She should have left it. Every instinct trained into her by ten years of salvage said *leave it, log it, report it*. But the box came up out of its cavity with a sucking whisper, and beneath it, pressed into the sand, was an impression — a second crest, identical, stamped into something that had rotted away to nothing, and above the impression, faint as breath on glass, a smear of what might have been wax, red as a wound.

Someone had sealed this box twice. Someone had wanted what was inside kept *very* still.

Petra got a lift bag on it. She'd brought one, as she always did, because the Widow liked to hand you things at the worst possible moments — planking that shifted, a hatch cover that finally let go — and a diver without a lift bag was a diver who swam home carrying other people's history on her back. She clipped the line, fed air, and the box rose off the bottom like something waking from a long sleep, and she rose with it, watching the green light above brighten toward silver, toward the surface, toward the world where iron boxes and their crests would turn back into problems instead of wonders.

She broke the surface a hundred yards from the Kestrel, her dive flag up, the strongbox riding the lift bag beside her, and the first thing she saw was a gray navy launch standing off the shoal at anchor, its crew watching her through glasses.

Her stomach did something complicated. She waved, cheerful, a working diver with a bag of — she glanced down — she'd have to think of something. The launch watched her for another full minute, then turned away without signaling, without hailing, without anything at all, and that was somehow worse than if they'd boarded her. They knew. They'd been watching the Widow, and they'd watched her pull the box, and they'd decided she was someone else's problem.

Someone else's problem was, in Petra's experience, almost always Ferris.

* * *

Ferris was waiting on the Kestrel's deck when she got the box aboard, which was the second wrong thing, because Ferris had never once in eight months set foot on her boat. He stood at the rail with his hands folded behind him like a man at a funeral, his good coat buttoned to the throat despite the heat, and his eyes went to the lift bag and the shape inside it with an expression she could only describe as hunger wearing a mask of boredom.

"You're early," he said.

"Tide was kind." She stepped past him, dripping, and began unclipping the bag with hands she kept very casual. "Found a change in the sand pattern by the forefoot. Photographed it. Logged it." She tapped the camera housing. "You'll want the images."

"I'll want the box."

She'd known it was coming — had known it from the moment she'd seen his coat on her deck — but hearing it said plainly still landed like a hook behind her ribs. She turned around slowly. "The contract says I observe and report. It doesn't say anything about surrendering finds, because the contract assumes there aren't any. There haven't been any. In eight months."

"And now there are." He smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind that reached the eyes without warming them. "The terms are what they are, Miss Brightwater. Everything of interest on that wreck belongs to the client. You know this."

"I know the contract says *report*." She kept her voice level. Down below, in the cold and the green, she'd been steady; up here, in the sun, with the box dripping seawater onto her deck and her mortgage, her fuel bill, and her mother's medical debt all stacked invisibly behind her like ballast, she found steadiness harder. "It doesn't say *hand over*. And I'd like to know what it is I'm handing over, and to whom, because I've been diving that wreck twice a month since last autumn and nobody's ever told me what it is, why the navy charts show open water where there's a ninety-foot hulk, or why the patrol boats watch me like I'm carrying plague."

Ferris regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes, she could see the arithmetic running — the cost of telling her against the cost of not telling her, the value of a diver who asked questions against the trouble of finding another one. She was cheap. That was her whole leverage, and both of them knew it, and the knowledge sat between them like a third person.

"You're a good diver," he said finally. "Careful. Discreet, until today. I'd hate for that to change."

"Then answer one question."

"The box, Miss Brightwater."

She looked at him. She thought about the trench, the sharp edges, the interrupted diggers, the navy launch that had watched her surface and turned away. She thought about the crest — the wave curling left, the star, the tiny marching script — and the way it had felt under her glove, like a signature on a letter addressed to someone who wasn't her, or maybe was. And she thought about three hundred a week, and what three hundred a week bought, which was not enough, and never had been.

She unclipped the lift bag and let the strongbox thump onto her deck between them, seawater sheeting off its banded sides, the scraped-clean crest facing up at the sky.

Ferris looked down at it, and for just a moment — half a second, no more — the mask slipped, and what she saw underneath wasn't hunger anymore. It was fear. Real fear, cold and immediate, the fear of a man looking at something he'd been told was buried and finding it not buried at all.

Then it was gone, smoothed over like sand over a trench, and he had the box in his arms and was stepping to the rail where his tender waited, and he was Ferris again, soft-voiced and soft-handed and entirely unreadable.

"Your contract's concluded," he said, not looking at her. "Payment's been transferred. Don't dive the Widow again, Miss Brightwater. Not for money, not for curiosity, not for anything. Some wrecks are named that way for a reason."

He dropped into the tender. The little engine coughed, caught, and carried him away across the harbor's brown-green water toward the chandlery, the box riding in the bilge between his polished shoes like a sleeping animal.

Petra stood on her deck in the sun, alone with the slap of water against her hull and the fading stink of the launch's exhaust, and realized her hands were shaking.

She looked at the camera housing in her other hand. Four angles of the crest. The trench. The wax smear, red as a wound, pressed into the sand of a wreck nobody would name.

She hadn't downloaded the images yet.

She went below, closed the cabin door, and did.
