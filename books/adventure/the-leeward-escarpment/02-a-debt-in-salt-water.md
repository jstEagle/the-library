## 2. A Debt in Salt Water

The notice came with the morning tide, folded inside oilcloth and weighted with a brass nut, the way all official things arrived at Gullhaven harbor — delivered by hand, because no one trusted the post, and stamped twice in red wax, because no one trusted anything less.

Petra Brightwater read it standing on the Kestrel's aft deck, bare feet cold on salt-slick planking, and let the paper hang from her fingers like something dead.

*Six days.* The Harrow & Lune Consolidated had given her six days to settle four hundred and eleven crowns of principal, plus interest accrued since the spring, plus — this was the part that made her laugh, a short ugly sound that startled a gull off the rail — *recovery fees for administrative costs incurred*. They were charging her for the letters threatening her. She was paying them to tell her they were taking her boat.

She looked up from the paper and there was the Kestrel, all thirty-one feet of her, riding her mooring with the patient, slightly aggrieved air of an old working vessel waiting for someone to make a decision. Teak gone gray at the rails. A diesel engine that started on the third crank if you asked nicely and never on the first if you didn't. A hull Petra had re-caulked herself two winters ago, lying on her back in the slings at Merrow's yard while sleet came sideways under the shed door. Her grandfather's boat — though she tried not to think of it that way anymore, because thinking of him that way was like pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurt, and it always did.

Six days. Then Harrow & Lune would send their men with a writ and a winch, and the Kestrel would go up on their transport barge, and Petra Brightwater would stand on the quay with empty hands and watch the only thing she owned in the world steam away toward some buyer who hadn't bled on every plank of her.

Four hundred and eleven crowns.

She'd made ninety-two last month. And last month had been a good one, before the navy contract dried up without explanation — before the wreck nobody named went quiet, before her handler Corliss stopped answering his wire with anything but *stand down, salvage suspended, further instructions pending*. Suspended meant unpaid. Pending meant indefinite. And Harrow & Lune did not deal in pendings.

Petra folded the notice along its creases until it was small enough to disappear into her jacket pocket, where the other notices lived, a paper tumor against her ribs. Then she went below, opened the chart drawer, and began doing arithmetic.

* * *

Arithmetic was its own kind of diving. You went down into it and held your breath and hoped what you found down there wouldn't drown you.

The numbers were bad. She laid them out anyway, coin by coin on the chart table: eleven crowns and change in the tin bolted under the berth. Sixty owed to her by Toma Pell, if Toma ever surfaced from his bender long enough to remember he owed it, which history suggested he would not. The fuel tank stood half full, which was worth maybe eight crowns of diesel at the co-op price. Her dive gear was sound — the compressor needed a valve job she couldn't afford, but the tanks held air and the regulator breathed clean, and gear that worked was the one currency Gullhaven respected.

Against that: four hundred eleven crowns, six days, one boat.

There were exactly three ways a woman with a boat and no money made money in Gullhaven, and Petra had spent the morning cataloguing them like a diver surveying a wreck for anything worth cutting free.

Fishing was out. She wasn't rigged for it, and the herring boats were already out in force, and even if she'd had nets, a week of herring wouldn't cover a tenth of the debt.

Salvage was out — or rather, salvage was *in*, everywhere except anywhere it paid. The good wrecks were locked behind contracts she no longer held, and the navy had been quietly posting exclusion zones all summer around sites nobody would explain. She'd asked Corliss once, straight out, over bad coffee in the harbor office: *What's down there that needs three gunboats?* He'd looked at her with those flat gray eyes and said, *Brightwater, the smartest thing you can do is not be curious,* and that had frightened her more than any answer, because Corliss was not a man who frightened easily and she'd seen his hand shake on the cup.

Which left the third way. The third way was jobs that came to you at night, paid in cash, and never appeared on any manifest. Running a passenger who wanted no record. Carrying a crate whose weight didn't match its paperwork. Towing someone somewhere no honest person needed to go after dark.

She hated the third way. It was the way her mother had gone under — not the debt itself but the night work, the men who paid cash and remembered your face, the slow erosion of being someone who could be bought after dark. Petra had sworn at fifteen she'd keep her work in daylight or starve.

That had been nine years ago. Starvation, she now understood, took longer than pride.

* * *

The man found her at the Lantern Hook, which was not a coincidence and she knew it. Everyone knew everything at the Hook; it was the harbor's second ledger, the unofficial one, where debts and reputations were marked in gin instead of ink.

He was old. That was the first thing — older than the room's average by twenty years, sitting alone at the corner table with his back to the wall and his face turned toward the door, a posture so practiced it looked like furniture. Weathered brown as old rope, with a beard going to iron-gray and hands on the table that were scarred in ways Petra recognized: rope burns across both palms, an old hook wound between thumb and forefinger, knuckles swollen with decades of salt water. A workingman's hands. A workingman who had worked very hard and very long at something.

He didn't drink. That was the second thing. The glass in front of him had sat untouched so long the condensation had dried and the bartender had clearly given up pretending the man might order another. He just sat there, watching the door, watching her, with eyes the color of shallow water over sand — pale, patient, unblinking.

When she sat down across from him — because he'd tipped his chin at her in a way that was somehow both request and assumption, and because her curiosity had gotten there ahead of her caution — he studied her for a long moment before speaking.

"You're Brightwater."

"Everyone here is somebody," Petra said. "It's a small town."

"Kestrel. Thirty-one foot, converted trawler, re-engined." His voice was low and rough, like gravel poured slow. "You dive, too. Deep. I asked."

"Who's asking?"

"A man who needs a tow." He slid something across the table with one finger. Not money — a fold of paper, and when she opened it her breath caught despite herself: fifty crowns, real notes, worn soft as cloth. Half again what she'd earn in a fortnight of honest diving.

"Where?"

"Past the reef line. North-northeast, past the Serrat shoals." He named coordinates then, flat numbers recited like scripture, and Petra felt the hair rise on her arms, because she knew those waters. Every diver knew those waters. That was open ocean beyond the shelf edge, deep water, water with nothing in it worth towing anyone to unless—

"No," she said, and pushed the notes back. "I know what's out there. Nothing. There's nothing out there but depth."

"There's something out there," the old man said. "For me there is." And something moved through his face then, briefly — grief, or its fossil record — before the weathered calm closed back over it like water over a dropped stone. "I'm not asking you to understand. I'm asking you to take my money and run your engine. You don't have to come past the rail. You don't even have to look."

"That's the problem," Petra said. "People who don't want you looking are usually the reason."

Something flickered in the pale eyes — not offense. Recognition. As if she'd said a password.

"I'll pay double when we arrive," he said. "Hundred total. Cash. Tonight, before moonrise. And I need you quiet about it. No manifest, no harbor master, no chatter on the wire. Can you do that?"

A hundred crowns. A quarter of her debt, gone in one night. Her mind ran the arithmetic again and got a different answer this time, the way numbers do when survival leans on the scale.

Her mother's ghost said *walk away*. The paper tumor in her pocket said *you have five days now*.

"One condition," Petra heard herself say. "If whatever you're doing out there gets you killed, I'm not diving after you. I tow, I wait, I bring back whoever comes back aboard. That's the whole contract."

The old man smiled then, and it changed his whole face — cracked it open like driftwood splitting, showing something younger underneath, something almost bright. "Fair terms," he said. "You drive a hard bargain, Miss Brightwater."

"It's why I still own my boat."

"For now," he said, and pocketed nothing, leaving the fifty on the table between them like a bet already placed. "Moonset tonight. North side of the breakwater, no lights until we're clear of the point." He rose, unhurried, and she saw that beneath the heavy coat he moved like a man much lighter than he looked — balanced, careful, a body that had learned economy the hard way. At the door he paused. "Silas Vane," he said, as if finishing a form. "You can call me Silas."

Then he was gone into the fog coming off the water, and Petra sat alone with fifty crowns and the growing certainty that she had just agreed to something that would not stay simple.

* * *

She told herself, running north in the dark, that it was just money. That was the story she steered by: a strange old man, a strange errand, a hundred crowns, five days left on the clock. Simple transactions between strangers. The kind of thing that happened in harbors everywhere, every night, and amounted to nothing.

The Kestrel ran well. She always did once you forgave her the third crank — the diesel settling into its workaday thrum, the bow lifting to the long swell rolling in from open water, spray coming over the rails in cold silver sheets that tasted of distance. Petra stood at the wheel with her collar up and the compass light glowing green on her wrist — the old brass compass, her grandfather's, the one piece of inheritance she'd kept, its needle steady as a heartbeat on north-northeast.

Behind her, Silas Vane sat on the deckhouse roof with his back against the stack, where the engine warmth reached him, and did not speak. He'd come aboard at the breakwater carrying a canvas pack that rode his shoulders like it weighed nothing and probably weighed forty pounds, and he'd said only, "Good boat," in a tone that carried more than the words, and then he'd been silent for two hours.

Gullhaven's lights sank astern. The Serrat shoals slid by to port, their white water visible even in the dark, a long seam of broken foam like a wound in the black sea. And then the bottom fell away — Petra felt it before the fathometer confirmed it, the swell changing character as the water deepened, longer, slower, the sea suddenly remembering it was ocean — and they were past the reef line, in water two hundred fathoms down, with stars overhead and nothing else in the world.

Vane stood up.

"We're here," Petra called over the engine. "This is where you paid me to bring you. So what—"

But he was already moving to the rail, shrugging out of the heavy coat, and in the dim glow of the binnacle she saw what was underneath: not a fisherman's jersey but a prospector's harness, straps and rings, and clipped to it a dive rig far better than hers — a rebreather, military pattern, scrubber canisters gleaming dully in the dark. No tanks. No bubbles. A machine for going deep and telling no one.

"Two hundred fathoms," Petra said. Her voice came out flat and hard, anger covering the other thing rising in her throat. "Nobody dives two hundred fathoms. Not on that rig, not alone, not at night. What is out there, Vane?"

He paused at the rail. The wind pulled at his iron-gray beard. For a moment he seemed to weigh the question, weighing her too — and she understood, with a diver's instinct for pressure changes, that whatever he decided in the next ten seconds would decide other things, later things, things she couldn't yet see.

"Thirty years I've been coming to this water," he said. "Tonight I finish it. Or it finishes me." He put one boot on the rail, and the pale eyes found hers, and there was that brightness again, banked like a coal. "You said you wait for whoever comes back aboard, Brightwater."

"I said—"

"So wait," Silas Vane said, and stepped off the Kestrel into two hundred fathoms of black water, without a splash, like a man returning to a house he'd owned all his life.

Petra stood frozen at the wheel, engine idling, the wake dying away behind her in slow silver rings. Somewhere below, deeper than any diver should live, an old man was descending into the dark with her hundred crowns in his pocket and thirty years of something on his back.

She checked her watch. She told herself she'd give him one hour.

The needle of her grandfather's compass trembled once against its glass — north-northeast, steady, steady — and then, as if the sea itself had leaned close to look at her, it settled, and waited, and the hour began.
