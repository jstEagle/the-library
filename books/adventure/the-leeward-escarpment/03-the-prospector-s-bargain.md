## 3. The Prospector's Bargain

The shot came from the water.

Petra heard it as a flat crack rolling across the harbor's black surface, and for one stupid half-second she thought it was the Kestrel's old winch cable snapping — a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat. Then the stranger folded against her wheelhouse door like a coat falling off a hook, and she understood.

"Get down," he said, which was absurd, because he was the one bleeding.

She dropped anyway, instinct older than thought, her knees finding the deck plates by memory while her hand found the knife strapped under the rail. Above her, out past the breakwater lights, an outboard engine opened up and then cut to silence, as if whoever ran it had decided noise was no longer necessary. That was worse. That meant they were coming in quiet.

The stranger was propped against the wheelhouse with his legs splayed and his left hand pressed high on his chest, between collarbone and shoulder, and blood came through his fingers in slow pulses that caught the harbor light and made it red. He was sixty at least, maybe seventy, weathered down to leather and wire, with a prospector's beard gone iron-gray and eyes that were still working even while the rest of him shut down. His right hand was clamped around a canvas satchel like a man holding onto a rail in heavy seas.

"They followed me from the point," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I paid your girl at the fuel dock double to say you weren't sailing tonight."

"She lied badly, then."

"She lied fine." A wet cough took him, and when it passed he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it meant he'd already done this arithmetic. "They just know boats. There's only one boat going out past the reef line tonight, and it's yours."

Petra stayed low and looked at him properly for the first time since the fuel dock, three hours ago, when he'd pressed a roll of bills into her palm and said one word: *north*. She'd taken the money because she always took the money now; debt had made her a creature of simple reflexes. She had not looked closely at his face. She looked at it now and felt something shift in her chest, some tumbler turning over, though she couldn't have said what it matched.

"You're shot," she said. "I need to get you to a doctor."

"There's no doctor past the reef line." He laughed, and it cost him; she saw the pain move through him like weather. "There's nothing past the reef line but water and me dying on your deck, Miss Brightwater. So let's not waste the tide on things we can't change."

Her name. He hadn't given it at the dock. She hadn't given it. The harbor wasn't big enough for secrets, true, but hearing it come out of a bleeding stranger's mouth landed differently than gossip did — it landed like something delivered, addressed, *meant* for her.

"How do you know my name?"

"I've known it longer than you've been alive." He said it simply, without theater, which somehow made it more frightening than any dramatics could have. Then his eyes went past her shoulder, toward the dark water beyond the breakwater, and all the softness went out of them. "Engine. Now. They'll board us if we're still tied up when they round the mole."

Petra's mind did what it always did under pressure: it got cold and fast and narrow, a channel of clear water between two reefs. Options. Stay, and answer questions from men who shot old men in harbors. Run for the navy pier, half a mile south, and hand this whole mess to men in uniform — except she'd spent last week diving a wreck the navy wouldn't let anyone discuss, and she had learned exactly how much comfort their protection was worth. Or run north, out past the reef line, into open dark water, with a dying man and a roll of someone else's money and a boat she owed eleven months of payments on.

The Kestrel's engine turned over on the second crank, grumbled, caught. She slipped the lines herself rather than send her hands above the rail, and brought the old trawler out of her berth stern-first, quiet as breath, no running lights. Behind them the harbor sat black and innocent. Somewhere in that blackness an outboard idled, patient as a shark.

Only when the breakwater fell astern and the swells of open water began to lift the hull did she allow herself to crouch beside him again.

He had gotten the satchel open. Inside, in the spill of her own deck lantern, she saw oilcloth, a tin of matches, a water skin, and a package wrapped in waxed linen the size of a chart tube, sealed at both ends with drips of dark red wax stamped with a signet ring. And beneath all of it, a folded square of oilskin that crinkled like paper.

"Don't open it here," he said quickly, before her fingers were even halfway there. "Not till I'm done talking. Talking first. Promise me that much, Brightwater. An old man wants to be heard before he's read."

"That's a strange way to ask for help."

"I'm not asking for help." He shifted, and the movement pulled a grunt out of him, and his hand came away from the wound darker than before. "Help's for people who are going to recover. I'm asking for delivery."

* * *

For an hour she ran north-northeast, past the last of the fishing fleet's anchor lights, past the reef line where the sea changed its voice from a mutter to a long low roar, and out into the deep swell where the Kestrel rolled like a tired animal and the stars came down close enough to steer by. He talked the whole time, in pieces, the way drowning men talk — bursts and silences, sense and drift — and she steered and listened and did not once believe all of it, and did not once disbelieve any of it either.

His name was Silas Vane. He'd been a prospector, a beachcomber, a wrecker of the honest kind, thirty years walking the leeward coast with a magnetometer and a stubborn streak. Thirty years looking for one particular ship.

"The Escarpment," he said, when he could talk. "You know the drop-off?"

"Everyone knows the drop-off. Nobody dives it."

"Nobody dives it because nobody's been paid enough to dive it." Another cough, longer this time. When it finished he lay back against the wheelhouse and stared up at the stars, and his voice went somewhere far away, to a place thirty years deep. "There's a colonial trader down there. Pre-annexation. She went off the charts official — navy wrote her down as lost with all hands in deep water, forty miles east. But she wasn't east. She was running dark along the escarpment, avoiding the customs cutters, and she hit the shelf in a storm and went down the throat. Everything she carried is still in her hold. And what she carried wasn't gold."

"What was it?"

"Paper." He almost laughed. "Thirty years I chased gold like every other fool on this coast, and the whole time the prize was paper. Deeds. Letters. A confession signed and witnessed and sealed in lead so it would keep. Proof of who really owned the shipping fortune everyone on this coast bows to. Proof of how it was taken." His eyes came back from wherever they'd been and fixed on her, bright and terrible. "Paper burns, Miss Brightwater. Gold doesn't. That's why they want it. Not to sell it. To burn it themselves, safe, where no one can ever read it again."

"And the men who shot you work for—"

"For the man whose grandfather's name is on that confession." Vane closed his eyes. Opened them. "Corvane."

The name went through the wheelhouse like a change in the wind. Everyone on the coast knew Corvane — the salvage armada, the dredge barges, the fleet that found wrecks before anyone else even knew they were lost, the magnate whose company logo was stamped on half the mooring buoys in the harbor. Petra had spent her whole career working in the shadow of that fleet, taking the small jobs Corvane's tenders didn't bother with, hating them in the vague way poor people hate institutions too large to argue with.

Now the hate sharpened to a point.

"Why you?" she asked. "Why does a prospector have the map to a wreck the Corvane fleet can't find?"

Vane was quiet for a long moment. The Kestrel rose and fell. Out in the dark, miles astern, she thought she heard an engine, or thought she didn't; at night, on water, hearing was mostly imagination.

"Because it was my sister's," he said finally. "The fortune. Before it was anyone's, it was hers. Her husband held the original partnership deed — half the enterprise, earned, documented, witnessed. And the man who became Corvane's founding partner took it from her. Took the deed, took the ships, took her name off everything, and when she fought it, he—" Vane stopped. His jaw worked. "He took more than paper, in the end. My sister died poor and unnamed, and her grandson grew up on stories instead of inheritance, and that grandson was me, and I have spent thirty years being called a madman for looking for what was stolen in plain sight."

The wind shifted. Petra adjusted the wheel without thinking, three degrees, keeping the swell on the quarter, and the whole time part of her mind stood apart and watched this scene from outside — a broke diver, a dying stranger, a legend of sunken paper — and asked itself how she had come to be here, in this story, on this night. Debts did this. Debt put you at fuel docks at midnight taking strange money from strange men. Debt had narrowed her life down to a channel, and now the channel had led somewhere no chart showed.

"You said the map shows the wreck site," she said. "That's why they want the satchel."

"They want what's in the archive room. The map just gets them there faster." He fumbled the oilskin packet free of the satchel, and even that small effort left him gray-lipped and gasping. She moved to steady him and he waved her off with a hand that shook. "No. Let me. A man should hand over his own life's work."

He held the packet out to her across the deck, and the deck light caught the wax seals, and Petra did not take it.

"No," she said.

Vane blinked. Blood loss had slowed him, but the surprise still got through.

"No?" 

"I'm a salvage diver, Mr. Vane. I know what happens to people who carry other people's treasure. Tonight proves it — you're the proof. If I take that packet, I'm worth shooting too, and I've got creditors who'd do it for less than Corvane would pay them." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used with buyers who tried to lowball her, and hated it a little. "Take the packet back. I'll run you anywhere you want. South, west, out to the deep water anchorage, whatever you need. But I'm not carrying your war."

"It isn't my war anymore." He tried to sit up straighter and failed, and the failure seemed to cost him something worse than blood. "Look at me, girl. I've got twenty minutes in me, maybe thirty, and the nearest hospital is six hours behind us and full of Corvane's donations. This packet has one destination and one carrier left, and the tide doesn't care which of us it drowns." He pushed the packet toward her again, laying it on the deck planking between them, and drew his hand back slowly, deliberately, the way a man lays down a weapon. "I'm not giving you a burden. I'm giving you a choice. You can leave it on the deck and I'll die next to it, and tomorrow it goes to the bottom of the strait with both of us, and the Corvanes of this world keep everything they ever stole, forever, and no one will ever even know there was anything to forgive ourselves for missing. Or you can take it."

"And if I take it?"

"Then you carry it to the Leeward Escarpment, and you find her, and you open what needs opening. And you tell no one. No harbormaster, no navy man, no friend, no lover, no priest. No one. Because the moment one living soul knows, the fleet knows, and the fleet doesn't leave witnesses." His voice dropped, roughened, became almost gentle. "It's a terrible gift, Miss Brightwater. I know that. I've got nothing else."

* * *

She left him and went forward to check the bilge, which needed no checking, because she needed her hands busy and her face away from his. The bow deck was slick with spray. She gripped the rail and watched the black water slide past below and let the cold come into her, the good cold, the diver's cold, the kind that made decisions possible.

Every instinct she owned said throw it overboard. Every instinct, and underneath the instincts, arithmetic: she owed eleven months on the Kestrel, four thousand to Marlowe's yard, an uncounted sum to a wreck the navy forbade her to discuss, and six days — five now — before the creditors took her boat and her livelihood and, effectively, her life. A dead prospector's map to a legendary wreck was not a rescue from any of that. It was a deeper hole. It was the hole at the bottom of the hole.

But she kept seeing the strongbox from the forbidden wreck, the crest stamped in its lead seal, sitting in her handler's evidence locker where she'd never see it again. She kept hearing the navy officer's voice: *that site does not exist, diver.* Powerful men buried things on this coast. She had known it professionally for years, the way you know a current — abstractly, by its effects. Tonight she'd met the current face to face, and it shot old men in harbors.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, that tumbler turning in her chest. *I've known your name longer than you've been alive.* She turned back aft.

Vane had slid down the wheelhouse wall. She knelt beside him and found his pulse at the throat, fast and thin as minnow line, and knew the arithmetic had changed again. It wasn't twenty minutes anymore.

"Vane." She tapped his cheek. "Silas. Look at me."

His eyes found her. They were clearing, strangely, the way dying men's eyes sometimes cleared, all the fog burned off and only the essential left.

"Yes," he whispered.

"I haven't said yes yet."

"You're going to." Almost a smile. "You've got a promise-shaped face."

"That's the stupidest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I work the waterfront."

"Good. Stupid keeps you alive out here. Clever divers drown." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he gripped her wrist with shocking strength, his fingers finding the pulse point as if taking a reading. "Listen. The compass."

"What compass?"

"Yours. The brass one. Your grandmother's pattern — you keep it on a lanyard, you never let anyone touch it, I saw it at the fuel dock." Her hand went involuntarily to her jacket pocket, where the old brass compass rode everywhere she went, the one relic of a family that had otherwise dissolved into scattered graves and silence. He nodded at the motion, satisfied. "It's a key, girl. Not a figure of speech. The bezel comes apart. The ring inside is marked. When you find the archive room — and you'll know it, it's the only room on that ship with a door — the ring marks match the lock. That's why I chose you. That's why it had to be you."

"Chose me? You picked me off a fuel dock three hours ago because I was cheap."

"I picked you thirty years ago," Silas Vane said, "because of who you are. The rest was just waiting for you to be born and grow up stubborn." His grip loosened. His voice was going somewhere she couldn't follow. "The packet. Take the packet, Petra. Finish what I started. Tell no one. Promise me. A dying man's promise is the only contract on this coast that can't be bought out."

The engine droned. The swell lifted the Kestrel and set her down. Far behind them, faint as a conscience, an outboard engine coughed and caught and settled into pursuit rhythm, patient, unhurried, certain.

Petra Brightwater looked at the wax-sealed packet lying on the deck between them, and at the old man dying beside it, and understood with perfect clarity that whichever way she reached, her life would divide at this moment forever after — before the packet, after the packet — the way divers' lives divided at their first blackout, their first bent friend, their first body. There was no version of tonight where she stayed the same woman.

She picked up the packet. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"I promise," she said. "The Escarpment. No one else. I finish what you started."

Something went out of Silas Vane then — not the life, not yet, but the weight, thirty years of it, set down at last. He exhaled a long breath that smelled of salt and pipe smoke and blood, and his hand found hers and squeezed once, and his lips moved.

She leaned close.

"—her name was Maren," he breathed. "My sister. Say it out there, when you find her. Say her name. They took everything but the name."

"I'll say it."

"Good girl." His eyes drifted to the stars, and stayed there, and though his chest rose twice more, shallow as tide pools, he never looked back down.

Petra sat on the deck of the Kestrel in the middle of the black sea with a dead man's head growing heavy in her lap and a wax-sealed map under her arm and a pursuer's engine threading closer through the dark, and she did the only thing left to do, the thing she'd been built for without knowing it until this moment.

She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, once, efficiently. She laid Silas Vane out straight along the lee rail and covered him with the canvas tarp, because a man deserved that much dignity and she might not be able to give him more. She went to the wheel, brought the Kestrel's head around northeast, and opened the throttle all the way, and the old trawler leaned into the swell like a horse that had finally been asked to run.

Behind them the outboard pursued. Ahead of them, past the reef line, past the patrol lanes, past six days of creditors and two hundred miles of open water, the Leeward Escarpment waited under the dark with its secret in its throat.

Petra put the packet inside her jacket, against her heart, where the compass already lived, and steered into the night, and did not look back.
