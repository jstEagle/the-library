## 4. What the Map Shows

Petra did not sleep. She told herself it was the tide tables, the maintenance schedule, the hundred small tyrannies of a boat that was one bad season from being taken from her — but the truth was the map, rolled in its oilcloth, sitting in the chart drawer like a loaded thing. She could feel it through the wood. She had felt it all night, the way you feel a tooth that wants to ache.

Dawn came gray and salt-stained over the harbor, and the Kestrel rocked on her mooring with the patience of a creature that did not know it was dying. Six days. The creditors had given her six days, and she had spent one of them already, hauling a bleeding old man out past the reef line and listening to him die on her own bunk with her hand pressed over his wound and his blood soaking through to her knee. *Carry it to the Escarpment. Tell no one.* She had promised. She had not yet decided what the promise was worth, or what it would cost, and the not-deciding sat in her chest like a swallowed hook.

She made coffee because her hands needed a task that wasn't shaking. The galley stove caught on the third try. Outside, the harbor was waking — gulls arguing over the fish offal, the ferry horn clearing its throat, the first light catching the masts of the Corvane Salvage Fleet where they lay at their private berth across the water, black-hulled and silent as a held breath. She looked at them a moment too long. Then she took the map out of the drawer and carried it to the chart table and unrolled it flat, weighting the corners with the coffee tin, a brass weight, and her father's old dividers, which she kept because they were good tools and not for any other reason she was willing to name.

* * *

The map was not a chart. She had known that from the first glance, in the dark, with a dying man's blood cooling between them, but knowing it in daylight was different. Daylight made it worse, or better — made it real.

It was drawn on sailcloth, heavy and salt-stiff, and it was drawn by hand, in iron-gall ink gone brown as old rust. The cartography was beautiful and wrong: coastlines that bent where real coastlines didn't, soundings written in a cipher of small hooked characters, a compass rose whose points were not north and south but letters. *B. R. I. G. H. T.* — no. Not quite. She leaned closer. The rose's eight points carried eight characters, and four of them were letters she almost recognized, and four were symbols: a key, a wave, a star with seven points, and a small closed circle like an eye.

Vane had told her almost nothing. There hadn't been time. There had been time only for the wax seal — cracked now, she had broken it with her thumbnail an hour after he died, alone on a black sea, because she was not a woman who could sit all night next to a sealed mystery — and for the words *the Escarpment* and *tell no one* and, last of all, when his voice had gone down to a thread, a word she had taken for delirium: *Maren.* He had said it the way you say a name you have said ten thousand times, and then he had said nothing again, ever.

She had not told anyone he was dead. She had towed his body to the leeward of Gannet Rock and buried him at sea at first light, weights in the canvas, the words she half-remembered from her grandmother's funeral said wrong and too fast. It had felt like the only honest thing she'd done in a year.

Now she spread her own charts beside the map — the working charts, the ones with her pencil annotations, her soundings, her whole professional life in graphite — and began the slow, unglamorous work of translation.

The coastlines bent, she realized, because the map was not drawn to bearing. It was drawn to *depth*. Every line that looked like a coast was a contour of the seafloor, and the soundings were not soundings at all but a running text, a message threaded through the geography like a wire through coral. She had spent eleven years reading water. She knew what the sea looked like when you drew it honestly and what it looked like when someone was hiding something inside it, and this map was hiding something with the discipline of a woman who had learned to hide things from men who searched her house.

*A woman.* Petra stopped, coffee cooling in her hand. She didn't know why she thought that. The hand was fine and sure, the letters small and upright, the pressure even — and something in her gut, in the place where she kept the knowledge she couldn't justify, said *a woman drew this, and she drew it to be found by someone who loved her.*

* * *

By midmorning the cipher began to give.

It gave the way locks give — not all at once but in stages, each turn revealing only that there was another lock. The hooked soundings were numbers in an old merchant code, the kind used by shipping houses before the Consolidation, when every fleet kept its own arithmetic so its rivals couldn't read its ledgers. Petra knew the code the way she knew the taste of cheap coffee: her grandmother had taught it to her as a game, when Petra was small, drawing hooked numerals in the condensation on the galley window of a house that had been sold out from under them before Petra was tall enough to see out of it unassisted.

*Grandmother.* The word surfaced in her mind unbidden, trailing cold. She pushed it down. It was grief doing what grief did — reaching for the dead, making connections that weren't there. Silas Vane was a stranger. A prospector. A man who had run from something until men with guns caught up with him on her deck. Whatever he had carried, it was his history, not hers. She was a salvage diver with six days to keep her boat. She was not a woman in a story. Stories were for people who could afford them.

She worked anyway. She told herself it was because the map might be worth something — because a cipher this elaborate guarded something worth selling, and selling was the only language her creditors spoke. That was the story she told herself, and she almost believed it, the way you almost believe a chart that's off by two degrees.

The numbers resolved into bearings and distances. The bearings resolved into a track — a route, running from the old colonial harbor at Cape Meridian, south and east along the deep channel, out past the shoals where the navy's charts went blank and stayed blank, to a final set of coordinates sitting alone in the lower right of the cloth like a signature. She checked them against her own charts with the dividers, twice, because her hands had begun to be unsteady and she wanted to blame the dividers.

The coordinates sat on the Leeward Escarpment. Not near it. On it — at the lip of the drop-off where the shelf sheered away into black water, at a depth no colonial salvage operation could ever have reached, at a depth only a madman or a modern rig would attempt.

And beside the coordinates, in that fine upright hand, someone had written two things.

The first was a name. Not Vane's. Not any name she expected. The letters were small and formal, drawn with the care people give to words that matter:

*Brightwater.*

Petra sat back. The Kestrel rocked. Somewhere across the harbor a winch started up, a small mechanical complaint, and the gulls went on arguing, and the world continued to be the ordinary world, and none of it made any sense, because her name was on a dead stranger's treasure map.

It could be coincidence. She made herself say it aloud, in the empty cabin, to hear how it sounded. "Coincidence." It sounded thin, the way a hull sounds when it's taking water. Brightwater wasn't a rare name — there had been Brightwater Point on the old charts before the navy renamed it, and a Brightwater Street in the lower town that nobody could explain anymore, and her grandmother had always said, in the tone she used for things too painful to say plainly, that the family had *come from the water and been returned to it.* Petra had thought it was just something old women said.

The second thing beside the coordinates was a crest.

She had seen it before. That was the thing that made her hands go cold on the warm coffee tin. She had seen it eleven days ago, forty fathoms down, in the flooded wreck the navy forbade anyone to name, on a sealed strongbox she had carried up through the dark with her heart hammering against the regulator — a crest of a seven-pointed star over a wave, struck in brass, and her handler had taken it from her on the dock without a receipt and told her she'd never been down there, and she had believed him, because she'd had no choice, because believing him was cheaper than the alternative.

The crest on the map was drawn in ink, but it was the same. Seven-pointed star. Wave beneath. And around the star, she saw now, circling it the way the compass rose's letters circled their center, ran a ring of small characters — the same cipher, the same hooked numerals, and at the ring's top, where a motto would go, a single word she could read without any code at all, because it was not ciphered, because it was meant to be read by anyone who ever saw it:

*Maren.*

The name Vane had died saying.

* * *

She did not remember getting up. She was at the stern locker with the hatch open, and her hands were pulling out the canvas roll that held her tools, and from beneath the tools she drew the compass.

Her compass. The only thing she owned that had come down to her instead of being sold. Brass-cased, heavy as a heart, with a hinged lid worn smooth by a century of thumbs. Her grandmother had put it in her hand when Petra was nine and said, *This is yours to carry, and one day you'll know why,* and then had died before the day came, and Petra had carried it through eleven years of diving because it was the one object in her life that had never been collateral.

She set it on the chart table, next to the map, in the strip of sun that came through the porthole.

The lid's face bore a crest. Seven-pointed star over a wave. She had looked at it ten thousand times and never once seen it, the way you don't see your own face, and around it ran a ring of small characters — and when she turned the bezel, which she had always taken for a compass bezel, which had never pointed anywhere useful, the characters in the outer ring slid against characters in an inner ring she had never known was there, and the whole instrument resolved into something that was not a compass at all.

It was a key. It was a cipher ring, the kind used to lock and open coded correspondence, and it matched the ring around the crest on the map the way a lock matches its key.

Petra stood in her galley with the sun moving across the deck and her whole life rearranging itself around her with a sound she could almost hear — the sound of a hull shifting on its mooring when the tide turns, everything the same and nothing the same. The strongbox on the navy's forbidden wreck. The name on the map. The compass. Her grandmother's hooked numerals, taught as a game. *Brightwater, come from the water and returned to it.* Vane dying with a woman's name in his mouth. And underneath it all, like a hull-sound through the soles of your feet, the question she had been refusing to ask since the old man bled out on her bunk: *why me? Of all the boats in the harbor, why did he come to mine?*

He hadn't been fleeing blind. She saw it now, or thought she saw it — the way he'd looked at the Kestrel's transom when they'd towed him aboard, the way his eyes had gone to the name painted there. *Kestrel.* Not Brightwater. But he had asked, in the last lucid minutes, before the promise, before the map — she had half-forgotten it, buried it — he had asked who her people were, and she had said Brightwater, and something had come into his face that she had taken for pain and had not been only pain.

She sat down slowly on the chart bench, because her legs had informed her they were no longer negotiating.

The personal had come into the work. That was the thing she had built eleven years of discipline to prevent — you dove the wreck, you salvaged the wreck, you sold the salvage, and you never, ever dove into your own history, because your own history was where the water got deep and cold and there was no line to follow back up. And now the map on her table had her name on it in a dead man's cloth, and the compass in her hand was a key, and the navy was sitting on a wreck with her family's crest stamped on its strongboxes, and somewhere out there was an old woman's voice saying *one day you'll know why.*

She looked across the harbor at the Corvane fleet. Black hulls. Silent cranes. The private berth with its chain and its guards, the fleet that owned half the shipping in the region and the other half's debts, the fleet whose salvage tenders had been provisioning for two days with a thoroughness she had noticed and not wondered about, because wondering was expensive and she couldn't afford it.

She wondered about it now. A fleet that size didn't provision for nothing. A fleet that size didn't steam anywhere without a reason, and the only coordinates on this map — coordinates written in a cipher keyed to her own name — sat at the lip of the Leeward Escarpment.

Petra closed the compass lid. It made a small, definitive click, like a decision.

Six days to keep the Kestrel. One dead man to answer for. A promise she had made with blood on her hands, to a stranger who had turned out not to be a stranger at all — and a fleet across the water, black and patient, that might already know everything she knew.

She began, with steady hands, to plot a course.
