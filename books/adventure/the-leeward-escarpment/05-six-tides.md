## 5. Six Tides

The Saltglass Harbor woke the way it always did: badly, and with complaints. Gulls arguing over fish guts on the breakwater, deckhands coughing tar into the gray light, the tide slapping pilings like a creditor at a door. Petra Brightwater stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* with a tin cup of chicory going cold in her hands and watched three supply barges nose into the deep-water berths at the far end of the quay.

She noticed them because they were wrong.

Saltglass saw maybe two heavy-lift barges a month, and never before eight in the morning, and never moored bow-and-stern in the navy-adjacent berths that cost more per day than her boat cost per year. These were riding high, empty, waiting to be filled. Crates were already coming down the gangway of the customs shed — crates stenciled with a mark she'd been staring at for two days now, burned into the back of her eyelids since the moment her handler had pried the strongbox out of her hands and told her, in that soft bureaucratic voice, to forget she'd ever seen it.

A crest. A bird with its wings folded, standing on water.

Petra drank the chicory anyway. It tasted like burnt rope. Everything did lately.

* * *

By midmorning she was ashore, doing what she always did when the numbers in her ledger turned ugly and the sea offered nothing: she listened. Saltglass ran on gossip the way engines ran on diesel — dirty, inefficient, and absolutely essential. The harbor had a hundred ears and one mouth, and if you bought enough coffee and said little enough, the mouth would open on its own.

She started at Maren's chandlery, ostensibly to price line she couldn't afford. Maren herself was behind the counter, a woman built like a capstan, wrapping shackles in oiled paper.

"Three barges," Petra said, casual as weather. "Somebody's planning a long stay."

"Somebody's planning a war." Maren didn't look up. "Corvane."

The name landed in Petra's chest like a dropped winch block. She kept her face still. She'd grown up on that name the way harbor kids grew up on tide tables — Aldous Corvane, the fleet magnate, the man whose salvage armada had swallowed half the coast's wrecks and half the coast's salvage divers along with them. Every diver in Saltglass had a Corvane story. Most of them ended with someone else owning the wreck.

"Corvane doesn't provision here," Petra said. "He owns yards down south. Better prices."

"He does when he wants discretion." Maren tied off the paper with a snap. "My cousin loads for the customs shed. Says the manifests are sealed. Sealed, on a public berth. You know what a sealed manifest means?"

"It means somebody paid."

"It means somebody paid *a lot*." Maren finally looked up, and there was the particular gleam of a woman who loved being the one who knew things. "Diving bells. Two of them. Saturation gear — the kind you don't rent unless you're going deep and staying there. Compressor plants. Enough cable to moor a cathedral." She leaned across the counter, voice dropping. "And fuel for six vessels. Not three. Six. They're staging out past the reef line, at the old naval anchorage. My cousin says the crews are already aboard. Been aboard two days."

Two days. Petra's hands went quiet around the counter's edge. Two days ago she'd been hauling up a forbidden wreck under contract, finding a strongbox stamped with a folded-winged bird, and handing it to a handler who smelled of government soap and fear.

"And Corvane himself?" she asked, keeping it light, keeping it idle. "He doesn't strike me as a man who leaves his armada to other people's judgment."

Maren snorted. "He's here. Hasn't left his flagship — the *Meridian*, the black-hulled one at the outer buoy. But he's here. Came in on the night tide, no announcement, which for Corvane is like the sun rising without bothering to be yellow." She shook her head. "Whatever's down there, he wants it bad enough to come himself. And Aldous Corvane doesn't leave his desk for anything less than a fortune or a grave."

*Or both,* Petra thought, and felt the word *grave* settle somewhere cold behind her ribs.

* * *

She spent the afternoon confirming what Maren had sold her, because gossip was currency and currency could be counterfeit. The harbormaster's office gave her nothing but a clerk's shrug — the berths were leased through an intermediary, all proper, all boring, move along. The taverns gave her more. By the third one she had the shape of it: six vessels, provisioned for a month, dive crews signed under bonuses that made men go silent and superstitious when they talked about them. A tender captain who'd had too much rum said the coordinates were locked in the flagship's safe, but that he'd seen the chart for one second, one second only, when the mate unrolled it wrong-side-up on the mess table.

"Leeward side," the man had mumbled into his glass. "Deep water. Past where honest charts stop pretending."

Past where honest charts stop pretending. Petra had stood in exactly that water eleven days ago, breathing compressed air that cost her more than food, working a wreck the contract papers wouldn't name. A wreck nobody names. A wreck the navy quietly forbids anyone to discuss.

And now the biggest salvage fleet on the coast was provisioning for the same stretch of leeward deep, its owner sleeping aboard his own ship rather than miss a single morning of it.

She walked back down the quay in the flat gold light of late afternoon, past the barges, past the crates with their folded-winged birds, and stopped at the rail opposite the outer buoy where the *Meridian* sat like a statement of intent. Black hull. White superstructure. Antennas bristling off her like the quills of something that had decided long ago never to be touched again. Through a porthole, high up, lamplight moved behind a silhouette — a man at a desk, working while the harbor ate dinner.

Petra looked at him the way you look at a reef from the surface: knowing the shape of it, knowing the danger is mostly below the waterline, knowing you'll have to go down eventually.

Then she went home to do arithmetic.

* * *

The *Kestrel*'s cabin was four feet of headroom and a lifetime of her own handwriting. Charts layered over charts, tide tables pinned above the bunk, the ledger open on the desk with its columns of red marching downward like water filling a hold. She sat with the lamp turned low and pulled Vane's map from the oilskin pouch beneath her mattress — not that she owned a mattress worth hiding anything under, but habits of secrecy were cheap and this one had kept her alive so far.

The map again. The wax seal broken, the parchment soft as cloth from thirty years of handling. The Leeward Escarpment drawn in a prospector's careful hand, depth soundings noted in a code she was still peeling apart, and beside the marked position — that crest. The folded-winged bird standing on water. The same crest on the strongbox. The same crest, she now understood, on every crate going aboard the Corvane barges.

She spread the tide tables beside it and began to work.

The Escarpment lay better than two days' sail south-southeast, past the straits, past the reef fields, into water where the seabed fell away like a staircase kicked over by God. The *Kestrel* could make it in fifty hours if the weather held and sixty if it didn't. The Corvane fleet — heavier, slower, dragging bells and compressor plants and the institutional caution of men who answered to shareholders — would need longer to stage, longer to transit, longer to rig. But they had six vessels and she had one, and once they arrived they would saturate that water with divers until the escarpment itself got tired of holding secrets.

So it came down to tides. It always came down to tides.

She counted them the way her grandfather — no. The way Silas Vane had taught her, though she hadn't known yet, two nights ago, bleeding out on her deck, that he was her grandfather; she'd only known he was dying and desperate and that his hand had been steady when he pressed the map into hers. Slack low at Saltglass tonight, 21:40. Then the flood, then the ebb, each tide twelve hours and twenty-five minutes, each one a unit of time older than money and indifferent to it.

Six tides. If she cast off on tomorrow's dawn ebb, she would stand over the Escarpment with roughly six tides in hand before the first Corvane diver broke water there. Six tides to find a colonial wreck that had defeated everyone who'd ever hunted it. Six tides to finish what a dying man had asked of her with his last breath.

*Carry it to the Escarpment. Tell no one.*

She hadn't promised to beat a fleet there. He hadn't known about the fleet. Or — and the thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a splinter — maybe he had. Thirty years, the journals would later tell her, though she didn't know that yet either; thirty years was long enough to learn how the powerful moved when their buried sins began to surface. Maybe Vane had timed everything — his approach to her, his death, his map — knowing that the moment he moved, Corvane would move too, and that the only person fast enough, hungry enough, and angry enough to outrun an armada was a woman with nothing left to lose.

Her eyes went to the ledger. The red columns. The creditors who'd given her until week's end before they took the *Kestrel* out from under her, and with her the last thing she owned that floated, breathed, or answered when she spoke to it.

Six days. Six tides. The arithmetic of desperation had a certain elegance, she'd give it that. Her debts and her deadline were the same number. Whoever arranged her life had a flair for symmetry.

Petra closed the ledger. She didn't burn it, though she thought about it. Instead she took a pencil and wrote across the top of the page, in letters small and hard as barnacles: *Escarpment. Six tides.* Underneath it, after a moment, she wrote: *Tell no one.*

Except.

Except she couldn't do this alone, and the honesty of that admission sat in her chest like swallowed ballast. Diving the drop-off solo was suicide dressed as courage. Running the straits dark past whatever watchers Corvane had already seeded along the coast required a navigator better than she was, and Petra knew precisely what she was: a fine diver, a competent skipper, and a cartographer only in the sense that she could read a chart without turning it upside down. She needed someone who could forge a heading, spoof a signal, think in the enemy's grammar. She needed —

Juno Reyes.

The name surfaced with the reluctance of something long drowned. Disgraced naval cartographer. Court-martialed — the harbor versions disagreed on the details, but agreed on the shape: she'd falsified or refused to falsify something, crossed Corvane somehow, and been fed to the disciplinary machinery whole. Now she drank at the Anchorline and repaired charts for fishing boats at rates that suggested she hated the work and herself in equal measure. Petra had bought a corrected survey from her once. It had been flawless, and delivered with a sneer, and Petra had respected both equally.

Tell no one, Vane had said. But telling Reyes wasn't telling — Reyes was a tool, a means, a pair of hands attached to a brilliant, bitter mind. Petra could keep the map, keep the compass, keep the truth about Vane and the crest and everything else behind her teeth, and hand Reyes nothing but a heading and a wage she couldn't pay.

That was the plan. That was the clean version of the plan.

She looked at the map again, at the crest, at the name she still couldn't say aloud even alone in her own cabin — the name inked beside the Escarpment in Vane's hand, the family name she'd carried all her life without knowing it was also a key. *Brightwater.* Somewhere out on that black water, a man who might be her father was provisioning six ships to bury it deeper.

Petra blew out the lamp. Through the port she could see the *Meridian*'s lights at the outer buoy, patient as a spider, and beyond them the dark line of the sea that held everything — the wreck, the proof, the fortune, the grave, the answer.

Six tides. Let the fleet provision. Let the magnate count his cable. The tide didn't care whose blood was in the water; it only cared who moved first.

Tomorrow's ebb, she thought. Dawn. And she began, in the dark, to load the *Kestrel* by touch.
