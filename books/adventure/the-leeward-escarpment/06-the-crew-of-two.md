## 6. The Crew of Two

Petra found Juno Reyes where the harbor found everyone it had finished with: the Saltcellar, a tavern at the end of the fish-packing pier, where the floorboards were stained dark with brine and older sins and the lamplight came through windows furred with salt so thick it turned everything the color of old bone.

She'd asked three people before someone would say the name. The first spat. The second laughed. The third — a crab-woman with hands like knotted rope — had jerked her chin toward the Saltcellar and said, "You want Reyes, you'll find her losing money at cards and winning anyway, because nobody'll sit across from her long enough to take it all back."

That was nearly true. When Petra pushed through the door, Juno Reyes sat alone at a corner table with a spread of cards before her, playing patience against herself with the grim concentration of a woman laying out a court-martial. She was smaller than Petra expected, compact and weathered, with gray shot through her black hair at the temples and a naval tattoo half-hidden under a rolled sleeve — an anchor wrapped in a surveyor's chain, the ink faded to blue-gray shadow. A leather tube, the kind that carried rolled charts, leaned against her chair like a dog too loyal to leave.

"Reyes?" Petra said.

The woman didn't look up. "If you're from the Admiralty, I've already answered your questions. If you're from Corvane, tell him he can buy my silence for what he paid for it the first time — nothing." She turned a card over with a precise, contemptuous flick. "And if you're neither, you're standing in my light."

"I'm neither." Petra sat down uninvited, across the table, into the light. "I need a cartographer."

That got her eyes. They were dark and quick and unhospital, the eyes of someone who had learned to count exits in every room she entered. "You need a *chartmaker*. There are six of them on this waterfront who'll draw you a pretty picture of the harbor for two coppers." Her gaze went down to Petra's hands — rope-scarred, salt-cracked, the hands of a working diver — then up again, reassessing. "You're not harbor trade. You're going somewhere."

"I'm going to the Leeward Escarpment."

The card Juno was holding stopped moving. For a long moment the only sound in the Saltcellar was the tick of the stove and a drunk singing softly to himself three tables away, some ballad about drowned sailors that everyone in this town knew by heart and no one believed.

Then Juno began gathering her cards together, unhurried, squaring the deck against the table's edge. "Funny thing," she said. "I drew those waters for the Navy for eleven years. And in eleven years, there was one region on every chart I ever made that got redlined before publication. Not marked hazardous. Not restricted. Just — removed. Blank space with a border around it, like a hole cut out of the world." She slid the deck into its case. "Guess what was inside the hole."

"The Escarpment."

"The Escarpment." Juno smiled without any warmth in it at all. "So either you're insane, or you know something worth killing for. In my experience those categories overlap more than people like to admit." She stood, took up her chart tube, and looked Petra over once, thoroughly, the way she might sound an unknown bottom. "Walk with me. I don't make agreements in rooms with this many ears."

* * *

They walked the seawall as the tide turned, the water below them sliding backward off the barnacled stones with a long hiss, uncovering the green-black weed and the rusted iron of old mooring rings. Gulls argued over something dead on the flats. The evening smelled of tar and fish guts and the particular cold sweetness of rotting kelp, and Petra found it comforting, which said something about her life she preferred not to examine.

"Terms first," Juno said. "I don't sign articles. I don't share cabins. I get a full copy of any chart we make or recover, and when this is done — whatever 'done' means — I walk away with my name cleared or I walk away with enough money to stop caring whether it is."

"That's four terms."

"I'm efficient." She stopped walking and faced Petra directly. "Now yours. What's actually down there? Because I watched your face when you said Escarpment, diver, and it wasn't greed. It was—" she searched for it, tapping two fingers against the chart tube, "—recognition. Like you'd seen a ghost wearing a familiar coat."

Petra looked out at the water. The Kestrel rode at her mooring in the roadstead, small and stubborn against the last light, and Petra thought about Silas Vane dying on her deck with blood between the boards, about the wax seal she'd broken with shaking hands, about the name Brightwater inked beside a crest she'd pulled out of a wreck the Navy wouldn't discuss.

"You heard of the salvage contract on the Meridian shoals? Month back?"

"Everyone did. Navy-adjacent. Quiet."

"I brought up a strongbox off that wreck. Sealed. Stamped with a crest — a leaping fish over crossed anchors, ringed in laurel. My handler confiscated it before I'd even gotten it dry and told me to forget the dive happened." She turned to face Juno. "Two days later a dying man gave me a map with that same crest on it, and my own family name written next to it, and told me to carry it to the Escarpment and tell no one. So either I'm chasing a fortune, or I'm chasing the reason my whole life has felt like a locked room. I can't tell which yet. Maybe they're the same thing."

Juno had gone very still. "Show me the crest."

"I don't have anything to show. Confiscated, remember?"

"Draw it."

Petra crouched, wet her finger in a puddle left by the ebbing tide, and drew on the flat stone of the seawall: the fish, the crossed anchors, the laurel ring. Her hand remembered the shape better than her mind did; it came out clean and exact.

Juno stared at it for so long the water began to dry and thin the lines. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — flatter, harder, the voice of a woman reading coordinates off a chart she'd hoped never to consult again.

"Corvane House mark," she said. "Not the merchant flag they fly on their ships — the *house* mark. The private one. It appears on exactly one document class in the entire colonial registry, and I only know that because I was the one who catalogued them, before—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Before Aldous Corvane stood up in front of the Board of Inquiry and read out a list of errors attributed to my charts. Errors I never made. Charts I never drew. Someone altered my work, diver, and forty-one sailors died running a reef line I had surveyed clear. And Corvane's fleet just *happened* to hold the sole salvage contract on everything that went down."

The gulls screamed. Out on the flats, the tide kept retreating, patient as arithmetic.

"So no," Juno said quietly, "I don't trust you. You walked out of a forbidden wreck with a Corvane house seal and you're still breathing, which means either you're luckier than anyone alive or somebody let you keep it. But I'll come aboard your boat, and I'll sail to the Escarpment, and I'll put my hands on whatever papers are down there — because if that crest is on what you think it's on, then the truth of what Aldous Corvane built his fleet on has been sitting on the bottom of the sea for sixty years, waiting for someone stupid enough and angry enough to go get it." She extended her hand. "That's not trust. That's convergent interest. Don't confuse the two."

Petra took the hand. It was dry and strong and closed around hers like a shackle closing — firm, deliberate, and not unkind.

"Convergent interest," Petra agreed. "Provisioning tomorrow. We cast off at dawn, ahead of the third tide."

"Dawn," Juno repeated, and something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "I haven't been to sea since they took my commission. Eleven years, and they took it in nine minutes." She looked down at the drying lines of Petra's drawing, almost gone now, the crest dissolving back into the stone. "Let's go see what else they've been erasing."

* * *

Juno arrived at the Kestrel an hour before midnight, while the harbor lamps burned low and oily in the still air, carrying two sea bags, her chart tube, and a wooden case that clinked like bottles. She came up the side without being told how, found the companionway in the dark, and stepped into the little cabin where Petra was hunched over Vane's map by lamplight, trying to force the last of the prospector's cipher marks to mean something.

"Your draft is two feet too deep for the inner channel at low water," Juno announced, by way of greeting. "We go out on the making tide or not at all. Also your compass is mounted five degrees off true, which explains why every heading you've ever sailed has been slightly wrong, and also—" she set the wooden case on the table, right on top of the map, deliberately, "—you have no charts beyond the reef line, and I do."

Petra looked up slowly. "Are you always like this?"

"Yes. It's why they gave me a commission and why they regretted it." Juno opened the case: inside, nested in wool, were instruments — parallel rules, dividers, a sounding lead with a brass chain, and a sextant whose arc gleamed with use rather than polish. "Everything I own that matters fits in one box. The Navy kept the rest. Consider it a dowry."

Petra moved the case aside and smoothed the map flat. "Then look at this. Tell me what you see."

Juno bent over Vane's handiwork — the coast rendered in a draftsman's shorthand that was fifty years out of date, depth figures in an old numbering, the Escarpment itself marked with a symbol Petra hadn't been able to parse: a circle quartered by a cross, with a single letter at each quadrant.

For a long time Juno said nothing. Her fingers hovered above the paper without touching it, tracing the air above the lines the way a blind woman reads.

"This isn't a treasure map," she said at last. "It's a *survey*. Partially. These depth figures match a hydrographic style that was discontinued before I was born — I've seen it in the archive vaults, on charts stamped with the same house crest you drew in the sand tonight. Whoever made this had access to the original colonial surveys. The ones that supposedly burned." Her finger stopped above the quartered circle. "And this — this is a bearing rose, but not for navigation. Four bearings, four quadrants. It's a lock, diver. A geometric key. Whatever's at the Escarpment isn't just hidden, it's *sealed*, and this tells you how to open it."

"It needs a second piece," Petra said quietly. "Something that completes the pattern."

"How do you know?"

"Because Silas Vane spent thirty years looking and never dove alone." Petra met Juno's eyes across the lamplit table. "He needed help he couldn't trust. Neither do I. So here's my term, added to yours: we don't lie to each other about what we find. Not to protect each other, not to protect ourselves. The moment one of us starts hiding things, this falls apart, and we both drown. Agreed?"

Juno straightened. Outside, somewhere across the harbor, a ship's bell rang the first hour past midnight, and the sound came flat across the water like a word spoken in another room.

"You realize that's the most dangerous provision on any article of war ever signed," Juno said. "Total honesty between two strangers with everything to lose."

"I realize."

"Good." Juno stuck out her hand again, and this time the grip was different — less shackle, more anchor-set. "Agreed. Now show me your stores, because if you're planning to feed me hardtack and hope for six days, I'm getting off this boat right now."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, with a wind out of the northeast that smelled of open water and weather to come. They cast off on the making tide, as Juno had decreed — Petra at the wheel, Juno forward handling lines with a competence that surprised neither of them once they saw it, the muscle-memory of eleven years of navy discipline living in her shoulders and hands regardless of what the Admiralty had taken from her on paper.

The Kestrel slipped out past the breakwater as the harbor woke behind them, past the packet boats and the fish-fleets and, at the far end of the anchorage, the dark hulls of a salvage tender flying the Corvane house colors, coaling for sea.

Petra saw them at the same moment Juno did. Neither spoke. But Petra put the wheel over a point, opening the distance, and Juno went below without being asked and came back up with the sextant and a face like a closed door.

By mid-morning the harbor was gone, the land had thinned to a smudge, and the open sea ran gray-green and endless under a sky that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Juno worked at the rail with chart and pencil, plotting their track in her precise draftsman's hand, and Petra steered and watched the horizon and felt the strange, uncomfortable sensation of not being alone on her own boat for the first time in six years.

"You're brooding," Juno observed, without looking up.

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding *and* navigating. I can multitask too." She penciled a fix, blew on the ink. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the Saltcellar. You have a question. Sailors always do. Out with it, or it'll fester below decks and come up as mutiny by Thursday."

Petra held the wheel steady and let the question out, finally, the way you pull a splinter — fast, before you can lose your nerve.

"When they broke you. The inquiry. Did you do it?"

Juno's pencil stopped. For a moment the only sounds were the wake chuckling along the hull and the wind in the rigging, and Petra thought she'd overstepped, that the fragile treaty of the night before had already cracked.

"No," Juno said. "I didn't do it. I loved the service more than I've ever loved a person, and I was good at it, and they took it in nine minutes with a signature." She resumed plotting, her voice level, her eyes on the chart. "Which is why I can tell you the thing you're actually afraid to ask yourself, diver. You're wondering if your dying prospector sent you to the Escarpment because of who you are — blood, family, inheritance, all of it. And you're afraid the answer is yes, because if it's yes, then everything you've built yourself into — the debt, the boat, the diving, the self-made woman who owes nothing to nobody — is just a story you told to survive being somebody's daughter."

The wheel trembled very slightly under Petra's hands. She steadied it.

"And if it's no?" Petra said.

"Then you're here by choice, and choice is worth more than blood." Juno looked up, and for the first time there was something almost gentle in the sharp dark eyes. "Either way, the answer's the same: we sail east, we dive the drop-off, and we find out what's true. The sea doesn't care which story you prefer. It just shows you what's there."

Petra was still turning that over — the fairness of it, the cruelty of it, the strange comfort of it — when Juno went suddenly rigid at the rail, her pencil frozen mid-fix.

"What?" Petra said.

Juno didn't answer immediately. She raised the sextant, swept the eastern horizon once, twice, and lowered it with the deliberation of a woman confirming bad news she'd already guessed.

"Sail," she said. "Fine on the starboard bow, hull-down, standing the same course we are. Too big for a coaster. Too fast for a trawler." She snapped the instrument case shut. "And diver — unless I've forgotten how to read top-hamper after eleven years, that's a Corvane tender. Somebody knew we were provisioning."

Behind them, far to the west, the harbor was a memory. Ahead, the smudge of the tender hung on the horizon like a promise, or a threat, and the wind was freshening out of the northeast, and the first of the six tides was already running out beneath the keel.
