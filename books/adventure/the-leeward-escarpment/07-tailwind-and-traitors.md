## 7. Tailwind and Traitors

Juno Reyes had spent eleven years of her life making charts, and the first thing she had learned — before ink, before instruments, before the navy taught her that a coastline was a kind of argument the land made against the sea — was that you could tell a lot about a helmsman by how they held a heading. The sloppy ones wandered. The frightened ones overcorrected, sawing at the wheel like they were trying to shake something off their hands. The good ones held their course the way a person holds a grudge: quietly, constantly, without ever seeming to think about it.

Petra Brightwater held her heading like a grudge.

Juno stood in the Kestrel's cramped wheelhouse with a mug of coffee she'd already let go cold, watching the woman at the wheel thread the morning light. They were four hours out of the cove where they'd cast off, running south-southeast toward the straits, and Petra hadn't touched the wheel so much as *agreed* with it, small corrections of two and three degrees, her body swaying with the swell as if she and the boat shared some private metronome. She hadn't spoken in an hour. Juno had counted.

She was beginning to understand what kind of person she'd signed on with. It was not entirely reassuring. It was, however, exactly the kind of person she needed.

"You're staring," Petra said, without turning her head.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring *and* navigating."

"Multitasking." Juno set the cold coffee down on the chart table and unrolled the straits chart, weighting its corners with the brass fittings she kept for the purpose. "Come look at this when you get a chance. Or don't, and I'll describe it to you in words of one syllable."

That earned her a glance — quick, assessing, gone again. Petra crossed to the table in three steps, one hand never leaving the overhead rail, and looked down at the chart. Up close she smelled of salt and diesel and something faintly metallic, like coins held too long in the hand. Her eyes went straight to the red pencil line Juno had drawn.

"That's our track?"

"That's the track we *want* them to think is our track." Juno tapped the line's end, where it bent eastward through the Gullet, the wide, honest, well-marked passage between the outer shoals. "It's the sensible route. Deep water, clear margins, a navigation buoy halfway through that some government actually maintains. Any salvage master worth his brass would take it."

"But we're not taking it."

"We are absolutely not taking it." Juno drew a second line, this one in soft graphite, barely visible unless you tilted the chart to catch the light. It peeled away from the first just north of the straits and dove south into a lattice of reef symbols so dense they nearly merged into solid green. "We're taking this."

Petra studied it the way she'd probably study a wreck from the surface — reading the shape of it, judging what lay beneath. "That's the Serrated Teeth."

"It's called that because it eats boats."

"I know what it's called. I've dived three of those wrecks." Petra's finger traced the graphite line without touching the paper. "The *Marisol* went down there in '09. The *Cormorant Queen*. There's a trawler on the eastern spur that still has her nets out, forty years later, like she's fishing for ghosts."

"And every one of those wrecks is why nobody runs the Teeth," Juno said. "Which is why nobody will be looking for us there. Corvane's tender pilots are good, but they're harbor-good. They follow buoys and depth sounders and the habits of other men. Put them in a reef field where the sounder lies and the buoys don't exist, and they'll do what all careful men do."

"Slow down."

"They'll slow down," Juno agreed. "And then they'll stop. And then they'll wait for daylight, or wait for orders, or wait for someone braver to go first. And we will be through."

Petra looked up at her. In the flat morning light her eyes were the color of deep water over sand — pale, unreadable, with something restless moving underneath. "You've run the Teeth before."

"No."

Something flickered across Petra's face. Not quite disappointment. Recalculation.

"I've *charted* them," Juno said. "Three seasons, before—" She stopped. The sentence had a cliff edge in it and she wasn't ready to go over it, not yet, maybe not ever. "Before I stopped working for people who deserved good charts. I surveyed that reef field with a lead line and a sextant and a naval theodolite, and I know where the channels are better than the men who named them. The channels move. The rocks don't. Trust the rocks."

"The rocks don't lie," Petra said slowly, "but they also don't forgive."

"No," Juno said. "They don't. That's rather the point."

* * *

The tender appeared an hour later, exactly where Juno's stomach had been telling her it would.

She saw it first as a smudge on the southern horizon, a gray suggestion against the gray-blue haze where sea gave up trying to be sky. By the time it resolved into hull and superstructure she had already done the math twice: twelve knots, closing angle of thirty degrees off their quarter, no flag showing but the silhouette unmistakable. Corvane fleet lines. Corvane fleet white, scuffed to workboat gray. A tender off one of the big salvage ships, the kind they used to run lines and divers between moored vessels — fast enough to be annoying, tough enough to ignore weather, crewed by men whose job description included making other boats' lives difficult.

"Tender, south by southwest," she said. "Twelve knots. He's been paralleling us since the horizon, which means he picked us up before I did, which means he's got glass better than mine, which means he's been counting our crew since dawn."

Petra didn't answer immediately. She came to the wheelhouse window and stood beside Juno, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and watched the smudge grow. Juno watched her instead of the boat. There was a particular stillness that came over some people when danger showed itself — a settling, like sediment finding the bottom — and Petra Brightwater had it in full measure. No fear, exactly. Something more like recognition. An old acquaintance arriving at the door.

"How long has he known about us?" Petra asked.

"If he's Corvane's, he knew before we cast off. Harbor gossip travels faster than boats." Juno shrugged. "Half the waterfront thinks we're running contraband. The other half thinks we're running something worse."

"Which is?"

"A map."

Petra's jaw tightened, the only tell. "Can we outrun him?"

"In a race? Maybe. The Kestrel's got clean lines under that rust." Juno nodded at the tender. "But we're not racing. If we run, we confirm everything. We become a story he tells tonight over dinner: two women in a leaky ketch, running scared, headed somewhere worth chasing. Right now we're just another coaster bound for the straits. We stay boring until the last possible minute, and then we stop being visible at all."

"And the map says the last possible minute is—"

"There." Juno pointed to a low green shoulder of land rising off the bow, the northern gate of the straits. Behind it, further south, the water darkened where the Gullet opened its honest mouth. "Past the headland, the fairway splits. He follows the marked channel east. We go west, into the Teeth, and we go dark."

"Dark."

"No lights. No radio. Engine at minimum revolutions. We let the land hide us while he commits to the channel, and after that—" Juno smiled, and was aware even as she did it that the smile had more teeth than warmth in it. "After that, he can stare at empty ocean and wonder."

Petra was quiet for a moment. The wind moved a strand of hair across her face and she didn't brush it away.

"You've done this before," she said. "Run from someone."

Juno kept her eyes on the tender. "I told you. I worked for the navy."

"That's not the same as running."

"No," Juno said. "It isn't." She gathered the chart off the table, folded it along its worn creases with the care of long habit. "But I learned the same lesson either way. The people chasing you always believe you want the same things they want — money, safety, the marked channel, the sensible route. They build their whole pursuit on the assumption that you're rational the way they're rational." She met Petra's eyes. "So be irrational. Be so irrational it costs you. That's the price of disappearing."

Petra looked at her for a long moment, and Juno had the distinct sensation of being measured — weighed against some internal standard, some ledger of debts and trust the woman kept behind those pale eyes. Then Petra nodded once and turned back to the wheel.

"West into the Teeth," she said. "Show me the first mark."

* * *

They passed the headland at 1400, and the tender, faithful to its nature, held for the marked channel.

Juno watched it through the glasses as the green shoulder of the headland slid between them — watched the tender's bridge, watched the glint of glass on glass as someone aboard watched them right back. For one suspended moment the two vessels regarded each other across the narrowing water, hunter and hunted both pretending to be something else. Then the land closed like a door, and Juno lowered the glasses, and said, "Now."

What followed was four hours of the most demanding seamanship Juno had ever witnessed from the deck of a boat this size, and she had once served under a captain who threaded a destroyer through an ice field by feel.

Petra ran the Kestrel dark and slow through a labyrinth that had killed everything that ever tested it. She went by eye and by Juno's voice, the engine ticking over at bare steerage way, the boat ghosting between coral heads that showed themselves only as subtle wrongnesses in the water — a paleness beneath the blue, a ripple standing where no current should stand, the sudden absence of birds where a reef broke near the surface. Juno stood at the bow rail with the chart memorized and the lead line in her hand, calling depths and bearings, and Petra translated each call into rudder and throttle without hesitation, without question, without once asking whether Juno was sure.

That was the part that undid her, a little. Juno had spent years in institutions where every instruction was second-guessed, every judgment audited, every mistake catalogued and eventually weaponized. Petra took her word like it was ballast — dropped it straight down and built on it.

"Starboard ten," Juno called. "Hard starboard after the next swell — there's a coral spine under the surface, dead ahead, it won't show till we're on it."

The swell lifted them; the water ahead went suddenly, horribly clear, and a ridge of coral rose into view like the backbone of something drowned. Petra put the wheel over and the Kestrel leaned, slid, cleared the spine by what Juno would later swear was less than a fathom, and settled back into the channel on the far side as though nothing had happened.

"Depth," Petra called back, calm as arithmetic.

"Four fathoms. Five. Holding five." Juno swung the lead and hauled it dripping. "Channel bends left around the next patch. Then it opens up for half a mile, and after that—" she checked the sun, checked her watch, checked the shadow angles against the chart in her head, "—after that it gets ugly."

"Define ugly."

"Serpent's Neck. Forty meters wide, coral walls on both sides, and the tide turns through it in ninety minutes. If we're not through before the turn, the current sets us sideways onto the teeth and no engine ever built gets us off."

"What's the tide doing now?"

"Flooding. We have maybe two hours of fair stream left." Juno coiled the lead line. "It's enough. Barely."

"Then we're fine," Petra said, and Juno believed her, and was surprised by how much that belief cost her, and paid it anyway.

* * *

The Serpent's Neck took everything its name promised.

They entered it with the light going long and amber, the coral walls rising sheer on either side, close enough that Juno could have touched them with a boat hook — close enough to see the fire coral glowing orange in the shadows, the small darting fish that owned these waters and regarded the intruding keel with proprietary disdain. The channel did not bend so much as writhe, S upon S upon S, and the current ran through it like breath through a flute, shoving the stern, grabbing the bow, insisting on its own opinion of where the boat should be.

Petra fought it the whole way, and it was beautiful, and Juno hated herself a little for thinking so.

There was no room for error and no time for fear, and Petra had apparently decided some time ago — perhaps years ago, perhaps in some other wreck, some other debt — that fear was a luxury billed separately. She worked the wheel in small fierce increments, feathering the throttle, letting the current carry them where it wanted and stealing the helm back at the last possible instant, over and over, a negotiation conducted at eight knots in a stone throat. Sweat stood on her temples. Her forearms stood out like cable. Once — only once — the current won a full second of argument and the Kestrel's port side swung toward a wall of coral fanged with dead shell, and Juno heard herself shout a warning, and Petra was already correcting, already ahead of the disaster, as if she'd felt it coming through the wheel before Juno saw it with her eyes.

Then the walls fell away. The channel spat them out into open lagoon water, wide and calm and turning gold with the dying day, and the tension went out of the air so abruptly that Juno's ears seemed to ring with its absence.

Petra eased the throttle up. The Kestrel lifted her head and ran.

Neither of them spoke for a full minute. The engine hummed. The wake unspooled behind them, straight and clean across the empty lagoon, and somewhere far to the east, beyond miles of reef and one very confused tender, the marked channel lay empty of anything but honest water.

"He lost us," Juno said finally. It wasn't gloating. It was confirmation, the navigator's habit of fixing a position even when the position was a feeling.

"He lost us," Petra agreed. Her hands stayed on the wheel. Her shoulders, though — Juno noticed — had come down from around her ears for the first time since the headland.

Juno went aft and put water on for real coffee, and found, rummaging in the galley locker, that Petra stocked it the way a person stocks a lifeboat: everything essential, nothing extra, tins labeled in a handwriting that was brisk and slanted and utterly unsentimental. She made two cups anyway. Some rituals survived even on a lifeboat.

She carried them forward and handed one through the wheelhouse door without ceremony. Petra took it, wrapped both hands around it, and drank. For a while they stood together in the wheelhouse, watching the sun go down over the Serrated Teeth, turning the whole drowned graveyard behind them to copper and rose.

"My grandfather fished these reefs," Petra said at last. It came out of nowhere, or seemed to. "He used to say the Teeth were the only honest thing on the coast. Everything else would take your money and smile about it. The Teeth just take you."

"Was he a good fisherman?"

"He was a careful one." Petra turned the cup in her hands. "Careful like you. Reading water nobody else read." She glanced sideways, and in the low light her expression had shifted into something Juno hadn't seen on her before — not trust, exactly, not yet, but the ground trust gets poured on. "Where'd you learn to call a channel like that? Really."

Juno looked out at the darkening water. The cliff edge in the sentence was still there. But the day had cost something from both of them, and it seemed suddenly stingy to hoard what remained.

"I surveyed these reefs for the Hydrographic Office," she said. "Three seasons. And then I found out what the Office was really charting — not the reefs. The salvage rights. Every hazard I mapped, I was drawing a fence around somebody's claim. Corvane's claims." The words tasted like old ash; she'd swallowed them a long time ago and they still burned on the way back up. "When I objected, officially, in writing, I discovered that my signature on those surveys was worth more than my objection. So I left. Unofficially. In a manner of speaking."

"Disgraced," Petra said. Not cruelly. Just precisely.

"Disgraced," Juno agreed. "Cartographer without a chart table. You know what they say about us."

"I don't."

"Nothing. Nobody says anything. That's the disgrace." She laughed, briefly, without much humor in it. "You didn't ask me any of this when you signed me on."

"I didn't need to. I watched your hands." Petra nodded at Juno's fingers, wrapped around the warm cup. "People lie with their mouths. Hands don't know how."

The last light went off the water. Somewhere behind them, in the dark, the Serrated Teeth kept their silence, and somewhere beyond them a tender pilot was composing his report to Aldous Corvane, explaining how two women in a rust-streaked ketch had vanished off the face of the sea. Let him explain it. Let him chew on it all night. Every hour they bought was an hour of diving they'd own come morning.

Juno drained her coffee and set the cup down, and was reaching for the chart to fix their night anchorage when Petra spoke again, quietly, in the tone of someone setting a small object on a table between them.

"The crest on that strongbox. The one my handler took." Her eyes stayed on the darkening horizon. "You recognized it. Back at the dock, before I even asked. You went white."

Juno's hands went still on the chart case.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when we're anchored and there's no reef within fifty miles of us, I'll tell you what I know about that crest. All of it. Including the part about why Aldous Corvane will burn the ocean down before he lets anyone see what's inside that box."

Petra nodded slowly. "Why not now?"

"Because tonight," Juno said, "we've earned one hour of believing we got away clean. Tomorrow the truth starts costing again."

Out in the black water, unseen, unheard, the tide turned — and began, patient as everything the sea did, to run toward the Escarpment.
