## 8. The Navy Won't Discuss It

The gunboat came out of the haze at midmorning on the third day, gray as a knife blade and moving with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once needed to chase anything.

Petra saw it first — a smudge against the pale wall of sea fog that had been trailing them since dawn, a smudge that resolved into hull, superstructure, and finally the long black barrel of a deck gun that made her stomach drop through the soles of her boots. She'd been running dark for two nights, threading the reef maze off the Serrat shoals on Juno's hand-drawn charts, sleeping in forty-minute shifts, and for one vertiginous second she couldn't decide whether the sight of official navy paint filled her with relief or dread.

"Patrol cutter," Juno said from the helm, not looking up. She said it the way other people said *storm*. "Vireo-class. That's Harrow Station's complement. They don't come this far east unless they're sent."

"They sent them," Petra said.

"They were always going to send them." Juno's hands stayed easy on the wheel, but Petra had learned to read her now, three days of close quarters and shared watches, and she could see the tension banked down in her like fire in a coal scuttle. "Question is whether they've been sent to stop us or to make sure nobody else does."

The Kestrel was too slow to run and too small to fight. Petra stood at the rail and watched the cutter close the distance, white wake unspooling behind it like thread off a spool, and made herself breathe slowly while she ran the arithmetic she couldn't avoid: if the navy knew where they were, then someone had told them, and there were only two people alive who knew their heading. One of them was standing next to her.

"I didn't," Juno said quietly, without being asked.

"I know."

"You know because?"

"Because you want Corvane worse than I do, and selling us out doesn't get you that." Petra kept her eyes on the cutter. "Also because you've had three days to do it and haven't."

Juno huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Cold logic. You'd have done well in the service."

"The service would have taken my boat for back taxes within a year."

The hail came across the water in a voice flattened by loudhailer into something sexless and official: *Motor vessel Kestrel, heave to and prepare to be boarded. This is a lawful inspection under maritime authority.* The words had the cadence of a script, worn smooth by repetition, and that smoothness frightened Petra more than the deck gun did. Scripts meant procedure. Procedure meant someone above the boarding officer had decided how this would go before it happened.

She cut the engine. The Kestrel rolled in her own wake, gulls screaming somewhere overhead, and the gray hull slid alongside with a fendered bump that shuddered through the deck plates.

* * *

They came aboard four strong, and they were not the usual harbor-patrol assortment of bored conscripts and sunburned petty officers. The officer in charge was a lieutenant commander with a face like a closed ledger — mid-forties, weathered, with the particular stillness of a man who has been told exactly what he may and may not say and has made his peace with it. His name, offered flatly when Petra asked, was Aldren. He didn't offer a first name, and no one corrected the omission.

Two of his sailors went below without being invited. The third posted himself at the companionway. The fourth — young, freckled, unable to meet anyone's eyes — began going through the chart table on deck, and Petra watched him open the tube holding Juno's hand-drawn surveys with hands that trembled slightly.

"We're a salvage vessel in transit," Petra said. "Licensed, insured, current inspection tags. You can check—"

"We've checked." Lieutenant Commander Aldren stood at the rail, looking east toward the horizon where the Leeward Escarpment lay somewhere beneath fifty fathoms of blue, though nothing showed, of course. Nothing ever showed. "That's rather the point, Ms. Brightwater."

Her name in his mouth landed like a dropped tool. She hadn't given it. It was on her registration, yes, but registration checks took time and radio traffic, and this man had come aboard already knowing, which meant the knowledge had preceded them, which meant—

"You know my name."

"We know a great many things." Aldren turned from the rail. Behind him, the young sailor had laid Juno's charts out on the chart table and was photographing each one with a small camera, working methodically left to right. "You'll forgive the inconvenience. Routine security survey of the eastern approaches."

"There's nothing east but the Escarpment and empty water."

"Yes," Aldren said. "There is."

The word hung there, and Petra understood suddenly that she was not talking to a man but to a policy. Everything he said would be shaped around a single fact the way water shapes itself around a stone, flowing past, never touching, never acknowledging. She had dived wrecks before that people didn't want discussed — chapter one of her professional life, the forbidden contract, the strongbox with the crest, the handler's cold warning — and she recognized the texture of it instantly. Silence with weight behind it. Silence that cost money to maintain.

Juno came up from below with two of the sailors flanking her, her jaw set, and gave Petra a small shake of the head: *they took the logbook too.* Then she planted herself by the chart table and watched the young sailor photograph her work with an expression that could have stripped paint.

"Those are my originals," she said. "Survey work. Cartographic. There's nothing on them but depth soundings and reef lines."

"There's a heading on them," Aldren said mildly. "Repeated headings. Converging headings." He picked up the topmost sheet — the one with the strait transit plotted in Juno's precise naval hand — and studied it as if seeing it for the first time, which they both knew he wasn't. "Ms. Reyes, isn't it."

Juno went very still. "Lieutenant Commander."

"You'll remember me, then. Or at least my signature. I countersigned the board findings." He set the chart down with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the table's batten. "I'm sorry about how that ended for you. For what it's worth, I voted the other way."

"For what it's worth," Juno said, "you signed it anyway."

Something moved behind Aldren's eyes — brief, quickly shuttered, but real. Regret, maybe, or just the fatigue of a man who had carried a compromise too long. "Yes," he said. "I did."

* * *

They confiscated the charts. All of them — Juno's surveys, the coastal almanac, even the tide tables, which was so absurd that Juno actually laughed, a short bitter bark, until she saw that the young sailor was genuinely embarrassed and stopped. They took the Kestrel's logbook. They took the photographs of the charts. One of the sailors emerged from below with the wax-sealed map tube from Vane — Petra kept it wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare anchor locker, and for one endless ten-count she was certain they'd find it — but the sailor passed over the locker after a glance from Aldren, and Petra understood that Aldren had seen it and chosen not to see it, and she could not decide what that meant.

Then Aldren asked her to step to the rail, alone, and the sailors withdrew to the cutter, and for a moment the two officers stood facing each other over four feet of salt-stained deck with the whole gray ocean listening.

"I'm going to tell you some things," Aldren said, "and then I'm going to deny having told you any of them, and you're going to be wise enough not to repeat them to anyone who matters. Are we clear?"

"Perfectly."

"The eastern approaches are closed to salvage operations. Not restricted — closed. There is no notice to mariners, no published order, and there will not be. Any vessel found conducting dive operations east of the Serrat line will be detained, its equipment impounded, and its owner assessed penalties sufficient to—" he paused, and his gaze flicked, deliberately, to the Kestrel's tired rigging, her patched sails, the rust blooming along her rail — "sufficient to matter."

"You can't close open ocean."

"No," Aldren agreed. "But I can close your ocean. Yours specifically. Ms. Brightwater, listen to me, because I am spending credibility I don't have." He lowered his voice, and the loudhailer-flatness fell away, and underneath it was just a tired man in a uniform that fit him like a debt. "Whatever you think is out there, whatever you were promised, whoever sold you this heading — turn around. Take your crew, take your boat, go west, and dive something legal. Because the people who want that ground quiet are not the navy. The navy is just the fence they rent."

"And if I don't turn around?"

Aldren looked at her for a long moment. The fog was burning off behind him; sunlight was coming up on the water, hard and clean, and it caught the brass buttons of his coat and made them shine like little coins.

"Then you'll find out why the last vessel that ignored this warning is now part of the Escarpment," he said. "And I will have no choice but to write a report saying you were lost to weather."

He stepped back. The interview was over. As the sailors reboarded the cutter with their armful of paper, Aldren paused at the rail and said, almost as an afterthought, in a voice pitched only for her:

"One thing, since you'll learn it anyway. Your wreck — the one you think you're racing everyone to. Check your colonial registry dates, Ms. Brightwater. Check when salvage law was written. Then ask yourself why a ship that predates every claim ever filed on these waters needs guarding at all."

* * *

The cutter pulled away and stood off a quarter mile, and there it remained, visible, patient, a gray exclamation point on the horizon, watching them decide.

Petra stood at the wheel with her hands on the spokes and did not move for a long time. Juno came and leaned beside her, arms crossed, and neither of them spoke while the gulls worked the wake and the sun climbed and the cutter sat there like a held breath.

Finally Juno said, "They photographed everything. We're navigating home off memory and prayer."

"We're not going home."

"Petra—"

"He told us to leave. Officially. On the record, he warned us off, confiscated our charts, did everything by the book." Petra turned the words over, testing them. "And then he told me to check the registry dates. Why would he do that? Why would a man spend twenty minutes burying us and then hand us a shovel?"

Juno was quiet. When she answered, her voice had changed — the bitterness burned off it, leaving something sharper underneath. "Because he's been carrying this order for years and it's been eating him. Because he voted the other way once and signed anyway, and signing broke something, and this is the only way he knows how to not sign twice." She shook her head slowly. "Or because it's a trap. Both are possible. Both are possible, Petra."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've seen what happens to people who find meaning in the cracks of official behavior. It's how the service eats cartographers."

Petra laughed despite herself — a thin sound, gone fast. Then she turned east, toward the invisible escarpment, and let herself think it through properly, the way she thought through a dive plan, piece by piece, no wishful weighting.

Salvage law in these waters dated to the Colonial Maritime Act — she'd memorized its provisions when she got her license, mostly to learn how to evade them. Claims ran from the date of enactment forward. Anything sunk before that date belonged, in theory, to the crown's successor estates, except where treaty said otherwise, and the treaties were old and vague and nobody enforced them because nobody cared about pre-Act wrecks. Nobody had ever cared. The deep colonial era ships lay where they fell, unmolested, unclaimed, forgotten.

So why guard one?

Not for treasure — treasure could be claimed, taxed, litigated openly. Not for ordnance. Not for bodies; the sea kept those regardless. You guarded a wreck, quietly, expensively, for decades, for exactly one reason: because of what was *in* it. Something that could not be allowed to surface. Something whose discovery would cost specific people specific things.

And the strongbox — the first one, the one from the forbidden wreck, the crest stamped in lead that her handler had snatched away like a live coal. The same crest Vane's map bore. The same crest inked beside the name Brightwater on a chart drawn by a dying man's hand. If the navy had been sitting on that ground since before Petra was born, then the thing buried there hadn't become dangerous recently. It had *always* been dangerous. It had been dangerous when it sank. Which meant the theft it proved — the fortune, the name, the inheritance — hadn't been hidden after the fact. It had been hidden *from the beginning*, by the men who committed it, using the full weight of whatever flag they sailed under.

"It's not a salvage claim," she said aloud. The understanding settled into her like cold water into a boot. "It never was. Corvane's fleet isn't racing us to a wreck they found. They're racing us to a wreck they've always known about. Their family has known about it since before there was a Corvane fleet. Since the sinking."

Juno stared at her. "You can't know that."

"The navy does. Aldren does. Someone has been paying to keep that ground silent for longer than I've been alive, and navies don't spend money on sentiment." Petra's hands tightened on the spokes. "Vane spent thirty years circling it. Thirty years, and every approach he tried, somebody was already there ahead of him — not diving, just *watching*, keeping it closed. He wasn't fighting the sea, Juno. He was fighting a lock that gets re-locked every generation."

"And the key," Juno said slowly, and her eyes went to Petra's chest, to the compass hanging there under her shirt, "is currently wearing your neck."

The cutter's engine note changed. It swung in a wide arc, deliberate, showing them its stern — *your move* — and then stood away westward toward Harrow Station, growing smaller, growing grayer, until it was a smudge again and then a memory and then nothing at all.

Petra brought the Kestrel's head around to the east. Without charts, without tide tables, with a borrowed almanac page Juno reconstructed from memory and a lifetime of habit, they would navigate the way the old colonial masters had: by lead line, by sextant, by nerve.

"Six tides," she said. "Maybe five now. They'll have heard the patrol boarded us. They'll think we've been slowed."

"Or they'll think we've been scared off, and they'll ease up," Juno said. "Either way, we sail tonight, on this tide, while the water's high enough to clear the Serrat bar."

Petra looked east one more time. Somewhere under all that innocent blue lay a hold, a locked archive room, a dead man's thirty years, and a name — her name — written in ink before she was born, waiting in the dark for someone to come and finish what had been started.

The wind backed a point. She trimmed the sails and steered into it.
