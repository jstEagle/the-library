## 9. Storm Glass

The squall came out of the northeast like something with a grudge, and Petra Brightwater met it the way she met most things — with her jaw set and both hands on the wheel.

It had been building all morning, a bruise-colored line on the horizon that Juno had been watching through the sextant's scope without saying anything, which was how Juno said most things. By noon the wind had swung around twice, and by one o'clock the sea had gone from slate to pewter to a churning gray-green that threw spray in sheets across the Kestrel's bow. The barometer on the bulkhead was falling so fast it looked like it wanted to follow the needle straight through the glass.

"That's not weather," Juno shouted over the wind, braced against the cabin doorframe, hair whipping loose from its knot. "That's a message."

"Then we read it." Petra hauled the wheel over two points, feeling the rudder bite, feeling the whole boat groan beneath her like a living thing being asked too much. "There's supposed to be a cove on the leeward side of this headland. Vane marked it."

"You're trusting a dead man's map to save our lives."

"I'm trusting a dead man who spent thirty years on this water." She didn't look at Juno when she said it. She didn't need to. "He knew these reefs better than any chart office ever will."

The Kestrel took a wave broadside and shuddered, and Petra felt it in her teeth, in the old scar along her ribs where a winch cable had caught her three years ago, in every debt she owed and every day she'd borrowed against them. Six tides. They were down to four now, maybe three and a half if you counted honestly, and the storm was eating their margin like rust ate steel.

The headland rose out of the murk ahead of them, black rock furred with salt-stunted pines, and somewhere behind it — if Silas Vane's inked lines meant anything — was shelter.

"Stand off the tiller," she called. "I'll take us in on soundings alone."

"You can't see your hand in front of—"

"I don't need to see it. I need to hear it." And she did what her mother had taught her before her mother had left, what every salvage diver learns eventually: she listened to the water. The way it broke against stone told you the shape of the stone. The way it hissed over sand told you where the bottom shelved gentle. The silence between waves told you where the deep water ran, and the deep water was the door.

Juno went forward with the lead line, calling depths in a voice gone flat and professional, and Petra steered into the noise of the storm as if it were a language only she spoke. Twice the keel kissed bottom and her heart stopped; twice the water deepened again and let them go. Rain came sideways then, hard enough to sting, and the world contracted to the circle of the wheel, the sound of the lead, the black shape of the headland sliding past on their port side.

And then, all at once, the wind fell away.

Not gradually — it simply stopped, cut off like a door closing behind them, and the Kestrel glided into water so still it seemed to belong to another day entirely. A cove opened around them, small and round as a cupped palm, ringed with rock walls hung in dripping green. A stream fell from somewhere above in a silver thread. The rain still hammered the rim of the caldera-like cliffs, but down here there was only the creak of the hull, the tick of cooling engine metal, and breath.

Petra dropped anchor in eight fathoms and stood at the wheel for a long moment, hands shaking now that it was over, the way they always did.

"Well," Juno said from the foredeck, soaked through, holding the lead line like a scepter. "Your dead man knew what he was doing."

"He usually did," Petra said quietly. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

They found the camp an hour later, when the squall had blown itself out to a steady drizzle and the light came back low and gold through the breaking clouds.

It was tucked into a fold of the shoreline where the stream met the sea, half-hidden behind a curtain of ferns — and Petra would have missed it entirely if not for the fact that she was looking for it. Vane's map had shown the cove with a small symbol she hadn't understood until now: a triangle, which on his charts, she was learning, never meant danger. It meant home.

A lean-to of driftwood and salvaged canvas sagged against the rock face. A fire pit, long cold, ringed with stones worn smooth. Two crates stenciled with shipping marks from companies that hadn't existed in forty years. A cooking grate made from a section of railing — ship's railing, Petra realized, touching it, from some vessel old enough that its brass had gone green as sea glass.

"Nobody's been here in years," Juno said, circling the site with a cartographer's eye, reading the ground the way she read coastlines. "Look at the moss growth on the canvas. Five years, minimum. Maybe ten."

But someone had lived here. Someone had lived here for a long time, off and on, and Petra felt that truth settle over her like the drizzle itself as she knelt and lifted the rotting canvas flap over the lean-to's interior.

Inside, sealed in oilcloth and stacked with a precision that made her throat tighten, were journals.

Dozens of them. Ledgers, actually — old salvage-company logbooks with the company names struck through and Vane's own purpose written across the covers in a hand that started strong and grew shakier over the years: *Escarpment Survey.* Volume after volume after volume, thirty years of them, each spine dated in that same deteriorating script.

"Oh, Silas," she whispered, not knowing she'd begun to use his first name until the word was already out of her mouth.

She carried them out into the drizzle-light one armload at a time and laid them on a flat rock near the cold fire pit, and Juno crouched beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. There are discoveries that feel like theft and discoveries that feel like inheritance, and Petra understood, turning the first journal open with fingers she tried to keep steady, that this was the second kind.

*Day 1,* the earliest entry began. *The sister is dead eleven months today. I have sold the boat and bought a diving suit I do not know how to use. I will learn.*

Petra read it aloud, because some things needed air in them to be real, and her voice cracked on *sister*, and Juno looked up sharply but said nothing.

*Day 4. Everyone tells me to stop. The courts tell me to stop. Corvane's lawyers tell me to stop, very politely, in letters that cost more than my house. My sister drowned with the truth and they think the truth drowned with her. But I held her compass when they pulled her from the water. I know what she kept. I know why.*

Petra's hand went to her pocket. To the compass.

It sat there the way it always sat, warm from her body, heavier than brass had any right to be. She had carried it since she was nine years old, since the day her grandfather — no. Since the day Silas Vane had pressed it into her palm at a harbor rail and told her, *You keep this safe, little fish. One day it'll tell you where you come from.* She had thought it was a sentimental thing, an old man's trinket. She had carried it through every dive of her life anyway, because some objects refuse to be put down.

Now she drew it out and held it beside the journal, and the handwriting on the page seemed to reach up toward it.

*Day 19. Found the first chart fragment in the customs house records. The colonial registry burned in '61, but the ledgers survived in a private collection. The collection belongs to a man whose name I will not write here yet. Soon.*

*Day 40. Dived the outer shelf today. Nothing. My hands have not stopped shaking since the funeral. Perhaps the shaking is the searching. Perhaps they are the same thing.*

*Year 3. Year 9. Year 14.* The entries thinned and thickened like tide lines. Years of nothing. Years of fragments — a bill of lading here, a witness's sworn statement copied out in full there, a name repeated so many times it became a drumbeat: *Corvane. Corvane. Corvane.* And threaded through all of it, patient as water finding its level, the story of a woman named Maren Brightwater, who had married a rising shipping man and brought him her family's routes, her family's contracts, her family's name — and who had died in "an accident" off the Escarpment before she could file the papers that would have proven half of everything was hers.

Half of everything. Petra did the arithmetic without wanting to. The fleet. The yards. The salvage armada currently hunting the same wreck she was hunting. Half of it had been built on a dead woman's stolen inheritance, and the man who built it had a partner whose name appeared in the early journals almost as often as Corvane's own — a younger man, a clerk, a friend of the family, who had signed the false papers and vanished into respectability.

Her grandfather's handwriting again, from year twenty-two: *The partner is the key. Corvane is the wall, but the partner is the door. Find the door.*

Petra closed the journal carefully, as if it might wake.

"Petra." Juno's voice was strange. "You should look at this."

She was standing at the far end of the rock shelf, near the lean-to, holding something small and rectangular that she'd found wedged behind the cold stove — a tin box, rusted at the corners, lid half-open. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper that crumbled at Juno's touch, was a photograph.

It was a harbor photograph, the kind taken by the itinerant photographers who used to work the piers for pennies. A woman stood at a ship's rail, laughing at whoever held the camera, dark hair coming loose in the wind, one hand raised to hold her hat. Young. Vividly, unguardedly alive in the way photographs almost never manage.

And around her neck, on a cord, hanging against her collarbone where anyone could see it if they knew to look —

Petra's compass.

Not one like it. Not similar. The same. Same case, same crown, same worn crescent of bright metal where a thumb had rubbed it ten thousand times. The same compass that was at this moment lying in Petra's open palm, catching the last gold light of the broken storm.

The woman was Maren Brightwater. Petra knew it the way you know your own reflection turned inside out — the set of the jaw, the arch of the brow, features she had seen every morning of her childhood in a mirror and in the faded portrait that had hung, briefly, in a house they'd lost. Her grandmother. Dead before Petra was born. Dead in the water off the Escarpment, according to a journal written by a man who had spent thirty years trying to prove her death was murder.

"She wore it," Petra said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "All this time I thought he gave it to me because — I don't know. Because I was the only one who came to see him. Because somebody should have it." She turned the compass over. On the back, the cipher ring she'd never been able to open gleamed dully under its patina. "He gave me her compass. He gave me *her*. He just didn't tell me I'd have to dive thirty years down to find out."

Juno said nothing for a while. The drizzle had stopped. Somewhere above the cove, the wind was moving on without them, and the stream filled the silence with its small indifferent silver sound.

"My grandmother wore this," Petra said again, softer. "And her brother watched the water for thirty years trying to say her name out loud. And I've spent my whole life diving other people's wrecks for other people's money, and I never once asked why my own family had nothing. Why we had a name and no fortune attached to it. Why my mother left. Why—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Why the water always felt like it owed me something."

"It did," Juno said. "It does. It's just taken this long to pay up."

Petra laughed — a short, wet sound with no humor in it, or all the humor in the world, she couldn't tell which. She looked down at the photograph again, at her grandmother laughing at the camera with the sun on her face and the future around her neck, and she thought: *you were going to file the papers. You were going to take back what was yours. And someone decided the sea was a good place for that to end.*

The compass lay in her palm, and for the first time it didn't feel like a keepsake. It felt like a key that had been waiting thirty years for the right hand — or like a debt, passed hand to hand down through the drowning years, and now it was hers to carry, hers to spend, hers to finish.

"We should get back to the boat," Juno said finally. "Weather window won't hold past dark, and we've got two tides' lead if we leave now. Three if we're lucky."

Petra nodded. But she didn't move yet. She wrapped the photograph in fresh oilcloth from the Kestrel's stores and tucked it inside her jacket, over her heart, where the compass rode on its cord. Then she gathered the journals — all thirty years of them, every waterlogged hope and furious entry — and carried them aboard like ballast, like cargo, like bones.

They cast off as the light failed, the cove closing behind them like a secret kept, and Petra stood at the wheel with the storm-washed stars coming out overhead and her grandmother's compass swinging gently at her throat, pointing — as it always pointed — not north, but somewhere else entirely.

It wasn't until they were clear of the headland that Juno, bent over the chart table with the fleet's known position plotted in red pencil, went very still.

"Petra," she said. "Come look at this."

Petra came. And on the chart, where Juno had drawn the Corvane fleet's last reported heading and extended it forward through the night's hours, the red line ran straight and true — not toward the Escarpment's outer shoals, where any sane salvage master would begin.

Toward the hidden cove. Toward the camp. Toward the place where Silas Vane had kept thirty years of proof in a rotting lean-to — proof that someone in the Corvane organization had evidently known about all along.

"They're not following us," Juno said quietly. "They're following *him*. They always were."
