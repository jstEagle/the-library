## 10. Blood and Brass

Juno Reyes had spent eleven years reading other people's handwriting.

Naval cartography was a discipline of margins — the little annotations surveyors left in the corners of charts, the corrections inked over old errors, the initials of officers certifying what they had never seen with their own eyes. She had learned to read a hand the way a doctor read a pulse: for what it concealed as much as what it declared. So when Petra Brightwater finally surrendered Silas Vane's journals to the chart table in the Kestrel's cramped cabin, Juno did not open them like treasure. She opened them like evidence.

There were nine of them, bound in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, their spines furred with salt mold. The earliest was water-stained past saving; the ink had bloomed into gray flowers across half its pages. But the rest — eight volumes spanning three decades — were legible, and they were written in a hand so disciplined it made Juno's chest ache. Small, even letters. No wasted strokes. The writing of a man who had learned to record things in places where paper was scarce and light was scarcer.

Petra sat across from her, arms folded, watching the way a person watches someone handle something that might break. Outside, the cove held them in its cup of black stone. The squall had passed in the night, leaving the air scrubbed and cold and the water in the anchorage so clear that the Kestrel's anchor chain could be counted link by link down to the sand, thirty feet below, pale as a string of teeth.

"Start anywhere," Petra said. "Or start at the end. I don't care. Just tell me what he found."

"He didn't find anything," Juno said. "Not yet. That's the point of him." She untied the oilcloth on the second-oldest volume. "He looked for thirty years. Men who look that long aren't looking for gold. Gold gives up in a season or it doesn't give up at all."

The first page of the second journal was dated forty-one years back. *Day one,* it began. *They took the Meridian's papers out of the customs house this morning. All of them. Every manifest, every charter, every letter of registry belonging to the Brightwater Shipping Concern, removed by order of the Port Authority under seal. I asked the clerk whose order it was. He would not say. He only looked afraid.*

Juno read it twice. Then she looked up.

"Your name," she said. "It's not an accident, is it. Brightwater."

"It's my mother's name," Petra said carefully. "She never talked about where it came from. I figured it was nothing. A fishing family. Everyone here has a boat somewhere in the tree."

"Keep telling yourself that," Juno said, "until you can't."

* * *

She worked through the morning while Petra took the dinghy out to check the cove's seaward approach — a habit Juno was learning to respect rather than mock. The girl ran a tight boat. Sloppy divers died, and Petra Brightwater was not sloppy, whatever else she was. Whatever she was turning out to be.

The journals built their story the way coral builds a reef: imperceptibly, layer over layer, until the shape of it stood whole above the waterline and you wondered how you'd ever missed it growing.

Silas Vane had been nineteen years old when his sister married into the Brightwater name — no, Juno corrected herself, reading again. Not married. *Partnered.* That word appeared in the third journal, in an entry dated two years after the first, and it stopped Juno cold, because she knew what partnership meant in colonial shipping law. She had drawn up enough salvage claims to know. A partnership between a shipping concern and a merchant house meant shared registry, shared hulls, shared inheritance of everything the venture touched — including, if the partners so willed it, the wrecks.

*Marianne signed the articles today,* Vane wrote. *Father wept, which he denies. The Corvane house brings capital; we bring the routes. Aldous Corvane is younger than his reputation. He shook my hand and held it a moment too long, the way men do when they are measuring the bone.*

Juno set the journal down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Aldous Corvane. The name sat in her mouth like a coin she'd swallowed years ago and still felt turning. She had served under Corvane's flag once — not his, exactly, but the navy's, and the navy's charts went where Corvane's money pointed. She had watched the fleet's tenders strip a protected wreck off Sable Cay in broad daylight while the patrol sloop assigned to guard it developed engine trouble, and she had said so in an official report, and the report had come back to her with her own superior's face gone gray, and six months later she had been the one facing a tribunal for falsified soundings she had never taken. She knew how the machine worked. She had been a gear in it until it chewed her out and spat her onto the beach with a discharge that read *conduct unbecoming* and a future that read nothing at all.

So she read on. And the reef rose.

* * *

By midday the light through the cabin's single porthole had gone from white to amber, and Juno had filled fourteen pages of her own notebook with transcriptions, cross-references, dates. Petra came back dripping, saw her face, and said nothing — just wrung out her hair, lit the lamp against the gathering gloom, and sat down to wait. Juno was beginning to understand that this was Petra's whole method: wait, watch, then move faster than anyone believed possible.

"There's a pattern in the entries," Juno said. "Starting about year twelve of his search. Look." She turned the fourth journal toward Petra and put her finger under a line. *Checked the Escarpment bearings against the Meridian's last known position. Off by three miles. Checked again. Off by three miles. Someone has moved the wreck on the charts.*

"Moved it," Petra repeated flatly.

"On paper. Not in the water." Juno flipped forward, pages whispering. "Year thirteen. Year fifteen. Year twenty-two. He keeps finding the same discrepancy. The Meridian — that's the treasure ship, your wreck, our wreck — she went down on the leeward face of the Escarpment in '61, carrying specie and, apparently, documents. But every official chart from 1870 onward puts her three miles east. Into deep water. Unreachable water. Water where no diver of that era could ever have reached her."

"So nobody could prove anything she carried."

"Now you're doing my job for me." Juno leaned back, and the bench creaked. "Here's what the journals say happened, Petra, and I want you to hear all of it before you react, because I've read ahead and I know what's coming."

Petra's jaw tightened, but she nodded.

"Marianne Vane — your grandmother, if the blood runs where it looks like it runs — co-founded the Brightwater Shipping Concern with Aldous Corvane. Her routes, his capital. Standard colonial structure. But there's a clause in these articles Silas copied out by hand, because he couldn't get the originals — the originals vanished from the customs house the week after Marianne drowned."

"Drowned?"

"Boating accident. Squall came up out of a clear sky, according to the inquest. Silas didn't believe the inquest. He spent the next thirty years proving he was right." Juno turned to her transcriptions. "The clause is a survivorship provision. If either partner died without direct heirs, the entire concern — hulls, routes, contracts, and crucially the salvage rights to any vessel lost under the partnership's flag — reverted wholly to the surviving partner. If Marianne had a living heir, the estate split. Half to the heir, half to Corvane. Or the heir could buy him out. Either way, Corvane needed Marianne childless and dead."

"And she had a daughter."

"She had a daughter," Juno said quietly. "Evelyn. Your mother, if I'm right. And here's where it stops being history." She pulled the sixth journal close and opened it to a ribbon marker she'd tied there hours ago, when her hands had started shaking too badly to keep transcribing. "Year nineteen of the search. Read this one yourself. I can't do it justice."

Petra took the journal. Her eyes moved down the page, and Juno watched her go still the way water goes still before it freezes.

*Found her,* the entry began. *God forgive me, I found where they put Evelyn. Not dead. They couldn't make her dead, so they made her nothing. The orphanage at Gray Harbor has records showing a girl of the right age admitted under a different surname the month after the inquest. Paid for by a shipping agent who doesn't exist. I sat outside the gates for two days. She has Marianne's chin. She has Father's stubbornness, God help her. I could not approach. There were men watching the gate — I know watchmen when I see them, and these were paid to watch a child. I am writing this with my hands shaking and I will not apologize for it.*

Petra closed the journal with exaggerated gentleness, the way people handle things when the alternative is throwing them.

"My mother grew up in an orphanage," she said. Her voice was level. Too level. "Gray Harbor. She told me once it was charity luck that placed her with a fishing family at fourteen. Apprenticed out. She never said placed by whom." A muscle worked in her cheek. "She used to say we were lucky people. Lucky to be alive. Lucky to be poor. She said poor people's luck was the only kind nobody tried to steal."

"Corvane stole it," Juno said. "All of it. He killed your grandmother — or had her killed — erased your mother, buried the Meridian three miles deep on paper so no one could ever raise the proof she carried. Because the Meridian wasn't carrying gold, Petra. Or she was, but that was ballast next to the real cargo. Partnership deeds. Correspondence. Marianne kept duplicates of everything — Silas says so, year seven — and the last voyage of the Meridian was Marianne's own arrangement. She was moving her proof somewhere safe. Somewhere Corvane couldn't burn it."

"The Escarpment."

"The Escarpment. And then the navy sealed the site, and Corvane bought the silence, and the only man alive who knew both halves of the truth — where the ship lay and why it mattered — was a nineteen-year-old kid with a grudge and a head for numbers." Juno spread her transcription pages across the chart table like a hand of cards. "Thirty years, Petra. He searched for thirty years. He lost boats, partners, an eye, two fingers — there's an entry where he describes losing the fingers and it's the calmest thing in nine volumes. He never found the wreck. But he found the key to it."

"The compass." Petra's hand went to her pocket, an unconscious motion Juno had seen a dozen times since the night Silas Vane bled out on this deck. The brass compass lived there now, worn smooth, its cipher ring clicking faintly when Petra walked, like a small animal dreaming.

"Not a compass," Juno said. "Look at it properly. Take it out."

Petra hesitated — Juno saw the war behind her eyes, the diver's superstition about handing over the thing that keeps you oriented — and then she set it on the chart table between them, and the lamplight caught the brass and threw a small trembling coin of gold onto the low overhead.

Juno turned the instrument over. Beneath, where a maker's mark should have been, there was instead a ring of engraved characters rotating within a fixed outer ring: letters, numerals, and symbols she hadn't recognized until tonight. Now she did.

"This is a cipher ring. Naval issue, forty years ago — I trained on the predecessor model. You align the inner ring to a keyword and it converts plain text to cipher and back. The navy retired them because they were too simple. One keyword and the whole system falls apart." She looked up. "But it's not too simple if the keyword is a name only one family knows. And look at the engraving on the case."

Petra bent close. In the lamplight, fine as a hair, the crest was cut into the brass: a wave breaking over a keel, and beneath it, in letters so small they might have been decoration, *B & C*.

"Brightwater and Corvane," Petra whispered.

"The partnership mark. It was on the strongbox you pulled from the forbidden wreck, wasn't it? The one your handler confiscated." Juno let that land. "Same crest. Same concern. Your handler didn't confiscate salvage, Petra. He confiscated evidence, and he probably didn't even know whose evidence, only whose orders. This whole coast is Corvane's garden, and everything in it grows the way he prunes it."

Petra picked up the compass. Held it in both hands, the way you hold a wounded bird or a live shell. When she spoke, her voice had gone somewhere Juno hadn't heard yet — down below the pragmatism, below the debt-hardened competence, into a stratum of bedrock.

"He shot Silas," she said. "The men following him. Corvane's men. Thirty years, and the old man gets within arm's reach of the truth, and Corvane's dogs catch the scent." Her thumb moved over the cipher ring, and it clicked, one notch. "And dying, he gives it to a stranger with debts. Why? Out of everyone on this coast. Why me?"

"I think you already know," Juno said gently. "I think you've known since you saw your name on his map."

"Because of the blood." Petra laughed, short and bitter, and it was an ugly sound, and Juno didn't blame her for it. "Because I'm the last Brightwater. The heir he couldn't find. The half-owner he erased. I'm not diving for a payday anymore, am I. I'm diving for my own inheritance."

"No," Juno said.

Petra looked up sharply.

"You're not hearing me." Juno stood, and the cabin felt suddenly smaller, the lamplight tighter, the sea-sound against the hull louder. She gathered the journals, squared them, stacked them with the precision she used for everything that mattered. "You're not diving for an inheritance. Inheritances are given. What Corvane took from your grandmother wasn't given to him, and what Silas handed you wasn't given either — it was *entrusted*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. An inheritance you can lose. A trust you carry." She tapped the stack of journals. "This man gave thirty years. His sister gave her life. Your mother gave a childhood she never even knew was stolen. And you're sitting here talking about blood like blood is the point."

"Then what's the point, Juno?"

"The point," Juno said, "is that Aldous Corvane built the largest salvage fleet on this coast on top of a murdered woman and a forged chart, and every hull in that fleet, every contract, every captain's commission, rests on paperwork that a brass compass can unlock. We don't need to find gold. We need to find *paper*. The original articles. Marianne's correspondence. Whatever the Meridian was carrying that made a magnate spend forty years and a navy's worth of favors keeping three miles of ocean empty." She met Petra's eyes and held them. "And when we find it, we don't sell it. We don't trade it. We take it to every port authority, every admiralty clerk, every newspaper from here to the capital, and we nail it to the door."

Petra was quiet for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, a gull complained against the wind.

"You hate him," Petra said at last. "Corvane. This isn't just my fight for you."

"I was a gear in his machine," Juno said. "I got chewed. That's my business, and I'll settle it my way, in my own time." She began tying the journals back into their oilcloth, fingers steady now, steadier than they'd been all day. Purpose does that. "But right now, your business and mine run on the same heading. Six tides, you said. The fleet provisions for the same coordinates."

"Five now," Petra said. "Tide turned while you were reading."

"Then we sail on the next one." Juno cinched the last knot and looked up. "And Petra — the compass opens the archive, if Silas's notes are right. Which means from the moment we reach the Escarpment, you're not the diver anymore. You're the key. Corvane will know that too, if he knows what Silas knew. Whatever's waiting on that drop-off, it isn't just a wreck."

Petra slid the compass back into her pocket. Her hand stayed there a moment, curled around it, and when she lifted her chin the lamplight caught her eyes, and Juno thought: *there it is. The stubbornness. Marianne's chin, Silas wrote. Father's stubbornness, God help her.*

"Then we'd better get there first," Petra said.

Outside, the tide had begun to turn. The anchor chain swung slow and gleaming in the clear water, counting down.
