## 11. The First Tide Turns

The sea changed color before the land did.

Petra saw it from the wheel of the Kestrel an hour past dawn — a band of deep indigo unspooling along the northern horizon like ink poured into milk, where the shallow shelf they'd been crossing for two days gave way all at once to open water. The Leeward Escarpment. She had read about it in Juno's charts, heard it named in harbor taverns the way sailors name graveyards: respectfully, quietly, and never twice.

From this distance it was only a line. But she knew what that line meant. Somewhere beneath it the seafloor fell away four hundred feet in a single breath, a limestone cliff face drowned when the old world's waters rose, and somewhere along that drowned face — if Silas Vane's map told the truth, and every mile of the last five days argued that it did — a colonial treasure ship lay folded into the dark with her secret still sealed in her ribs.

"You're smiling," Juno said from the chart table, not looking up from her sextant work. "You only smile like that when you're about to do something expensive."

"I'm smiling because we made it." Petra eased off the throttle and let the hull settle into the long swell. "Two tides ahead of schedule. Vane's shortcut through the Sable Cut worked."

"Vane's shortcut nearly took our keel off on a coral head." Juno set down her pencil and finally looked at her, and as always there was that ledger-keeper's scrutiny in her face, the look of a woman who had spent eleven years in the naval survey office learning to measure everything and trust almost nothing. "And you're smiling anyway."

"I'm smiling because he got us here." Petra let that sit a moment. "That counts for something."

Juno didn't answer, but she turned back to her charts, and after a while she said, quietly, "It counts," and Petra understood that for Juno Reyes, that was practically a speech.

* * *

They came at the Escarpment the way Vane's map instructed: wide around the eastern shoulder, then in slow along the drop-off line, watching the depth sounder tick down — sixty fathoms, ninety, one hundred twenty — until the numbers went mad and the lead line found nothing at all. The water beneath them turned from green to blue to a blue so dark it wasn't a color anymore, just a suggestion of depth, a held breath.

Petra cut the engine and stood at the rail.

The Escarpment itself rose out of the sea a half-mile to leeward: a rampart of weathered coral limestone, white and gold in the morning light, streaked with the black tears of seabird colonies, hollowed at its base by caves that swallowed the swell and exhaled it again with a sound like slow breathing. Waves detonated against it in columns of spray. The wind came off it carrying salt and something older, mineral, the smell of stone that had been drowning for three hundred years.

"There," Juno said, pointing with her chin. A notch in the cliff line, barely visible, where the rock stepped down in terraces toward the water. "The map's anchor mark. That terrace would have been above the tide line when she sailed. They could have careened her right there — run her aground, unloaded whatever they were hiding, scuttled her off the edge."

"Or they never made it," Petra said. "Storm drove her onto the drop-off and she went down whole."

"Either way, she's down there." Juno folded her arms. "Question is whether we're the first ones down there too."

Petra swept the horizon with the binoculars. Empty water to the east, empty to the south. To the north, where the shipping lanes ran, a smudge of haze that could have been weather or could have been funnel smoke. She couldn't tell. That was the thing about the Corvane fleet — Aldous Corvane hadn't built the largest salvage operation in the hemisphere by being visible before he wanted to be.

"We dive now," she said. "While the water's ours."

* * *

She went down alone. It wasn't sentiment; it was arithmetic. The Kestrel needed someone aboard who could run her, and Juno's shoulder was still strapped from the reef incident in the straits, and besides — this part, the finding, felt like it belonged to her. To Vane. To whatever thread of blood or promise connected the old man dying in her cabin to the woman hanging on his rope over a four-hundred-foot wall of nothing.

The first fifty feet were bright and ordinary. Sunlight in shafts, a school of jacks turning like a silver wheel, the familiar pressure building in her ears as she cleared them, cleared them again, dropped into the blue.

Then the light began to fail, and the wall appeared.

It came up out of the dark the way a mountain comes up out of cloud — gradual, then suddenly, appallingly total. One moment she was suspended in featureless water; the next there was stone beside her, close enough to touch, falling away below her fins into a darkness her lamp couldn't reach. Gorgonian fans the size of cartwheels studded the face, orange and violet, filtering the current. A spotted eagle ray glided past at the edge of her light, unhurried, indifferent, wings beating like slow applause.

Petra hung there a moment and let the awe have her.

She had dived wrecks for nine years. Hulls in harbors, freighters on sandbars, a mail steamer broken across a reef like dropped crockery. She had thought herself past the point where the sea could make her feel small. She had been wrong. The Escarpment didn't feel like a place. It felt like a threshold — like diving along the edge of the world itself, with everything human and lit and survivable somewhere far above, and everything else down here, in the cold and the pressure and the dark.

*Move,* she told herself. *Grieve later. Find first.*

She followed the wall south, checking the map's bearings against her compass — against *the* compass, Vane's compass, riding in the pouch on her chest harness where it had ridden for six days. Every hundred yards she checked, and every hundred yards the bearing held true, and the certainty built in her chest like pressure of its own kind.

At the third terrace mark the wall changed character. Here some ancient collapse had tumbled a field of boulders down the slope, house-sized blocks furred with black coral, and the terrain went from clean vertical to chaos. Her lamp picked out overhangs, slots, tunnels where the current funneled through with a low moan. Any one of a dozen shadows could be a ship. Any one of a dozen slots could eat a diver and keep her.

She was working through the boulder field, running low on bottom time, when the current shifted — a cold pulse from the deep, the escarpment exhaling — and carried with it a smell she registered before she understood it. Iron. Tannin. Old wood.

She killed her lamp and drifted, listening with her skin.

There. Below and to the left, where the boulders gave way to a sloping shelf: a shape too regular for geology. Straight lines don't happen underwater. Not natural ones.

Petra finned down along the shelf, heart hammering against the mouthpiece, and the shape resolved out of the murk by degrees — a curve of encrusted timber, then another parallel to it, then the rib-bones of frames rising between them, each thicker than her body, sheathed in a century and a half of concretion and coral. A keel. Frames. The buried spine of a ship laid along the base of the wall like a body in a tomb.

She swam the length of it slowly. Two hundred feet if it was an inch. The timbers vanished into the talus slope on one end and into deeper dark on the other, and growing up through the wreck, angled forty degrees off true, was —

A mast.

It stood out from the rubble like a flagpole from a fallen fortress, snapped ten feet above its partners, furred white with coral, draped in dead men's rope long since become part of the reef. And wrapped around it, three turns, sagging between the turns with the weight of decades of marine growth, was cable.

Not rope. Cable. Wound wire, galvanized, the kind that goes on winches and salvage cranes — and stamped into a shackle plate near the masthead, where the growth had cracked away, letters she scraped clear with her knife:

**R.N.S. — PROPERTY OF THE ADMIRALTY — DO NOT LIFT**

Petra hung in the water beside the mast and felt the awe curdle.

Navy cable. On a wreck that predated the navy's own charts of these waters — Juno had sworn to that, had shown her the survey records, the gaps, the deliberate blank space where the Escarpment should have been sounded. Someone had been here. Someone official. Someone who had come down with government-grade lifting gear, wrapped the mast, and then — what? Stopped? Been stopped? Left the cable in place because leaving it was cheaper than explaining why it was there?

Or because whoever sent them wanted the wreck marked. Claimed. Held. A dog in a manger four hundred feet down, fenced with Admiralty wire, waiting for the day its real owner came to collect.

*The wreck nobody names.* Handler Reyes' voice from a year ago, from the forbidden contract: *You see nothing, you lift nothing, you log nothing.* And the strongbox with the crest she now knew belonged to her own family, confiscated before she could breathe on it.

It all fit. It had been fitting together for days, click by click, and now the last piece seated home with a sound she felt in her teeth: the navy wasn't guarding the Escarpment for the crown. It was guarding it for Corvane. Had been for years, maybe decades. The fleet magnate didn't need to race anyone to a wreck he already owned on paper — he just needed to make sure no one else ever reached it first.

Except Vane had. Except she had.

Her gauge needle was falling and she made herself stop thinking, made herself be a diver again instead of a daughter of a stolen name. She shot a datum line from the masthead, fixed the position against her compass and the wall's terraces, marked it on her slate in figures only she could read. Then she pulled the compass from its pouch, held it flat on her palm above the wreck, and watched the needle swing — once, twice — and settle, quivering, pointing not north but down and along the hull, toward the stern, toward whatever room in that drowned body had been built to hold a secret worth killing for.

The compass knew. Vane's compass, Brightwater brass, the inheritance key. It was pointing the way home.

Petra looked up from the wreck toward the surface, and the surface was wrong.

She saw it before she understood it: a flicker in the far blue, high up near the light, where the water should have been empty. A shadow crossing the shimmer. Then another. Long, low shapes, moving in formation, the way big ships move when they think no one is watching.

She kicked upward, dumping her slate into her thigh pocket, abandoning decorum for speed, and broke the surface thirty yards from the Kestrel with her regulator out and her heart going like a bilge pump.

Juno was already standing at the rail with the binoculars, and her face told Petra everything before Petra's own eyes confirmed it.

North-northeast, coming down the shipping lane with the morning sun behind them: smoke. Not one plume. Four. Five. Six, stacked against the horizon like organ pipes, and beneath them, resolving even at this distance, the gray geometry of hulls — tenders, a supply ship, and leading them a vessel long enough and white enough that Petra knew her name before Juno read it aloud.

"*Corvane Star*," Juno said. Her voice was flat, the voice she used when feeling would only slow her down. "Flagship. And she's not passing through. She's turning. Coming straight for the anchorage."

Petra hauled herself over the rail, tore off her mask, and stared north while the water ran off her in ropes. Six tides of head start, she had calculated in the harbor. She had used four getting here. She should have had two full tides to work the wreck in peace.

"They moved faster than the gossip said," she managed. "Or they were never provisioning at the harbor at all. They were already at sea."

"Doesn't matter which." Juno was already at the chart table, pencil flying, marking ranges and times with the mechanical precision Petra had learned to rely on. "At their speed they've got the anchorage by tomorrow's mid-morning tide. Maybe sooner if that flagship is making twelve knots, and she'll be making twelve knots, Corvane doesn't sail his flag slow." She looked up. "We have today. One working tide. Maybe six hours of usable bottom time before we either find what Vane died for or we're diving in front of an audience."

Petra looked down at her hands — at the slate, at the compass sitting in its pouch, needle still trembling with what it knew. Behind her, half a mile away and four hundred feet down, a ship three centuries dead lay wrapped in navy cable, holding her family's name in her flooded chest like a letter that had waited generations to be opened.

One tide. Six hours. And somewhere out there in the haze, a man she'd never met — a man whose blood might be in her veins — was steaming toward the same spot with a fleet and a claim and, if the navy cable meant anything, the patience of someone who had already waited thirty years.

"He can't know we're here," Petra said. "Not yet. We kill the generator, run silent, no lights tonight. I go back down within the hour and I don't come up until I've found the hold."

"And if the silt gets you? If the current pins you?" Juno's jaw was tight. "I can't follow you down, Petra. You know I can't."

"Then you keep my line and you pray," Petra said, and was surprised to find she was smiling again — the expensive kind, the kind Juno had named. "Because I promised a dying man I'd finish what he started. And I've got exactly one tide to keep it."

To the north, faint on the wind, came the first far-off thump of the fleet's engines, rolling across the water like a countdown.

Petra turned her back on it and started rigging her gear.
