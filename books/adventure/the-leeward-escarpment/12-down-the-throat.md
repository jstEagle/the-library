## 12. Down the Throat

The gunport was a mouth of black coral and rust, yawning out of the escarpment's throat at forty-one meters, and Petra hung in the blue water above it for a long moment, letting her breathing slow until the sound of it filled her hood.

Below her, the wreck lay on its side along the drop-off like a sleeping animal, its hull furred with a century of growth, its masts long since dissolved into the sediment slope. The navy cable she'd found yesterday still ran from the mast step up into the gloom — thick hawser line, military-grade, anchored to a deadman bolt drilled into the reef. Someone had come here before her. Someone had wanted this ship to stay exactly where it was.

She checked her gauges out of habit rather than need. Full tanks. Rebreather scrubber green. The compass hung at her chest inside its oilskin pouch, and through the fabric she could feel its weight against her sternum like a second heartbeat. Two tides. That was what she had. The Corvane fleet had appeared on the horizon that morning as a smudge of smoke and steel, close enough now that if she surfaced too high, too bright, their lookouts would read her bubble trail against the swell.

*Down the throat,* she told herself. *In, find the hold, out. Nothing else exists.*

She finned down through the last column of open water and the light changed around her — the hard silver of the surface giving way to the bruised violet of depth, then to the green-black murk where the escarpment swallowed everything. The temperature dropped so sharply it felt like passing through a wall. Her breath fogged the inside of her mask for half a second before the flow cleared it.

The gunport was bigger than it had looked from above. Three meters across, its iron lid torn away by whatever violence had put the ship here — storm, or something worse — the opening framed a darkness so complete it seemed to have texture. She swept her light across the lip and found the coral growing over the iron in smooth, fused ridges, old growth, decades of it. No one had widened this entrance recently. Whatever the navy cable was for, they hadn't gone in this way. They hadn't gone in at all, maybe. Maybe they'd only ever meant to keep others out.

*Good,* she thought, and did not examine why that thought felt like a door closing behind her rather than opening ahead.

She pulled herself through feet-first, one hand on the coral lip, easing her tank past the jagged edge where the iron had folded back like a peeled lid. Inside, the ship's interior opened around her beam in vaults and ribs — the overturned cathedral of a colonial merchantman, deck planking overhead where the deck should have been below, cargo space become ceiling, everything inverted and draped in silt the color of old bone.

Her light found shapes. Bales rotted to lace. A row of ceramic jars fused into the decking, their glazes still faintly glossy under the gray. The ribbed spine of the keel running away into darkness like the backbone of something enormous. And everywhere, everywhere, the silt — lying in soft dunes over every horizontal surface, knee-deep in the low passages, waiting.

Petra had worked wrecks for eleven years. She knew silt the way a sailor knows weather: intimately, superstitiously, with respect bordering on fear. Silt was patience given form. It waited for one careless fin stroke, one moment of lost orientation, and then it took the light and the way out and sometimes the diver too. She had pulled bodies out of silty holds before. She had promised herself, more than once, kneeling on the deck of the Kestrel with the sea flat as hammered tin, that she would never be one of them.

She moved like a monk. Hands and tips of fins only. Pull-and-glide along fixed points — a deck timber, a stanchion, a bollard crusted white — never swimming free in the open water of the hold, because open water in a wreck is an illusion; there is always something below you waiting to be stirred. She clipped her guideline to the bollard just inside the gunport, paid out line from her reel, and made the tie-off clean and taut. Juno had taught her that rig two years ago, back when Juno was still allowed to teach things, and Petra heard her voice now, dry and precise in the memory: *A guideline you trust is worth more than a partner you don't.*

She almost smiled behind her regulator. Almost.

The hold ran aft, narrowing as the hull drew down toward the stern. Her light reached perhaps eight meters before the water ate it. Beyond that, blackness with texture — the suggestion of bulkheads, the pale gleam of something that might have been china or might have been bone. Fish scattered ahead of her beam in nervous silver flickers. A moray watched from a split in the planking with its mouth working slowly, patiently, like an old man remembering grievances.

Vane's map, spread on the Kestrel's chart table under a lamp while Juno stood watch, had shown the archive room aft of the main hold, beneath what the old cartographer had labeled *the captain's country* — a chamber drawn small and precise, ringed in Vane's cramped hand with a notation Petra had read a dozen times since: *IRON DOOR. LOCK LIVES IN THE COMPASS. TELL HER THE RING TURNS COUNTER TO THE SUN.*

*Her.* He had written *her*, thirty years ago, not knowing which woman of his blood would come. Or knowing. Or hoping. Petra had turned that word over so many times it had worn smooth.

She followed the keel aft. The passage narrowed. Overhead, the inverted deck came down to meet the silt floor until she was moving through a slot barely wider than her shoulders, her tanks whispering against broken planking, her breath loud in her own ears — too loud, she thought, too much like panic, slow it down, four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm Juno had drilled into her on the crossing when Petra admitted, once, in the dark of the wheelhouse, that deep enclosed spaces made her hands shake.

*Your hands are allowed to shake,* Juno had said. *Just don't let them steer.*

Fifty meters in, by the reel's counter, the slot opened without warning into the aft hold, and Petra stopped so abruptly she drifted into the bulkhead.

The room was vast. Her light couldn't find its far wall. What it found instead was cargo — not rot, not lace, but cargo preserved by cold and depth and the accident of the ship's position: crates stacked in ordered ranks, tarred canvas still stretched over some of them, and between the stacks, standing upright in the silt like congregation, dozens of barrels, their iron hoops rusted to orange blooms but their staves holding. The ship had gone down with its heart still full. Whatever had sunk it, it hadn't been plundered first. No one had ever gotten this far and lived to carry anything out.

Or no one had been meant to.

The thought arrived uninvited and sat down beside her. She pushed it away and swam on, along the port-side stack, counting tie-offs, checking her line ran free behind her. At the reel's seventy-meter mark, her light caught it.

Iron.

A door set into the aft bulkhead, flush with the planking, so heavily corroded that it seemed grown from the ship rather than installed in it — and yet the edges were sharp. The frame was true. The hinges, though furred with rust, were massive, forged, deliberate. This was not a door that had been part of the original build. This had been added, later, by men who knew what they were carrying and feared it being taken.

And in its center, at the height of a standing man's chest, was the lock.

Petra hovered before it and let her light play across the face of the thing. Circular, wide as a dinner plate, its surface a ring of concentric bands etched with characters she didn't recognize — not letters, not numbers, but a script of interlocking curves, dense as circuitry, worn shallow by a century of salt. At the lock's center was a recessed socket, round, about the diameter of the base of her compass housing.

Her heart was suddenly very loud.

She unclipped the oilskin pouch with fingers that wanted to hurry and forced them not to. Slowly. Everything slowly. She unwrapped the compass in the sheltered pocket of her own body, cupping it against the current of her exhaust bubbles, and the brass caught her light and threw it back warm, gold against all this green decay.

The compass her grandfather — her *grandfather*, the word still fit badly, like a boot on the wrong foot — had pressed into her hands with his blood making the brass slippery. *Tell her the ring turns counter to the sun.*

There was a ring on the compass. She'd noticed it that first night aboard the Kestrel, turning it idly while Juno slept: a rotating bezel of brass etched with the same interlocking script as the lock, stiff with age, moving only under real force. She had assumed it was a bearing ring, some surveyor's tool, a prospector's habit. Now she held it up to the lock and saw the two objects were halves of each other, separated by decades and a theft and a family broken into pieces and scattered like wreckage.

She fitted the compass into the socket.

It seated with a click she felt through her palm rather than heard — a deep, mechanical rightness, the sound of a thing returning to the place it was made for. And then the lock woke. She felt it before she understood it: a tremor through the door, through the bulkhead, through the water itself, as though something enormous had shifted its weight in its sleep. From within the mechanism came a grinding, a sequence of heavy tumblers falling one after another, each impact thudding through the iron like a slow heartbeat.

Petra went cold all over. Not from the water.

*Counter to the sun.* She gripped the bezel ring and turned it counterclockwise. Stiff. Stiffer than anything should be after eighty years underwater. She put both hands on it, braced her elbows against the door, and leaned her whole body into the turn, and the ring gave — a grudging, rasping inch, then another, and with each increment another tumbler fell somewhere inside the mass of the door, and the grinding grew louder, and the vibration traveled up her arms into her teeth.

The lock was waking the ship. There was no other way to describe it. Somewhere overhead, sediment shivered loose from the inverted deck and drifted down through her light in slow gray veils. A barrel hoop shed its rust with a soft sigh. In the blackness beyond her beam, something large and unseen settled with a groan of tired wood.

*Stop,* said the part of her brain that had kept her alive for eleven years. *You're shaking the house down around your ears.*

*Keep going,* said the other part, the newer part, the one that had burned its maps and signed its name in blood-promise to a dying stranger. *You're two tides from nothing. Open the door.*

She turned the ring the rest of the way.

The final tumbler fell with a boom that rolled through the hold like distant thunder, and the door swung inward — six inches, a foot — and from the gap came a movement of water, cold and ancient, breathing out of the room beyond. And with the water came the silt.

Not the door's silt. The *ship's*. Eighty years of undisturbed sediment, held in delicate equilibrium throughout the flooded interior, and the pressure wave of the opening door moved through it like a hand dragged through a pond. Petra saw it coming — saw the gray wall rise off the deck thirty meters back down the hold, saw it gather and roll toward her with the unhurried certainty of a thing that has all the time in the world — and her body understood before her mind finished the sentence.

*Silt-out.*

She killed her light.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the dive, harder than the promise, harder than watching Silas Vane die on her deck with her name — his own name, his daughter's name — going unanswered on his lips. Every instinct screamed to run, to swim, to kick for the gunport and the open blue, and every one of those instincts was wrong, because kicking was what fed the cloud, and running blind in a wreck was how divers died with their tanks still half full. She killed her light, hugged the iron doorframe, tucked her fins against her body, and became, as much as a living woman could, a piece of the wreck.

The cloud took her.

There was no other word for it. The world ended. Blackness absolute — not the readable blackness of deep water, which always holds a rumor of direction, but the total, pressurized dark of a buried place. Her mask showed her nothing. Her light, even if she'd dared switch it on, would have shown her nothing but lit fog, a blindness with a glare on it. The silt pressed softly against her face, her neck, found the seam of her hood, and she breathed — four counts in, six counts out — and listened to her own heartbeat hammering and made herself do the only thing left to do.

Nothing. Perfectly. For as long as it took.

Minutes. She counted them off on her watch's lume dial, holding her wrist an inch from her mask, watching the luminous hands crawl. Her air was finite. Her time was finite. The fleet was on the horizon. All of it pressed on her, and she let it press, and she breathed, and she did not move, and somewhere in the fourth minute the terror crested — the animal certainty that she was already dead, already entombed, that no one would ever find her, that she would hang in this iron dark forever with her grandfather's compass in her hand and her promise unkept — and she rode the crest the way she rode surge on a wreck, loose-limbed, letting it pass through her.

*Your hands are allowed to shake. Just don't let them steer.*

They were shaking. She let them.

At minute nine, the water began to clear. It happened the way weather happens — imperceptibly, then all at once. First the lume of her watch gained a faint halo. Then her own outstretched hand resolved from the murk, a pale ghost. Then, at minute fourteen, enough light filtered from somewhere — a crack in the hull above, a lens of disturbed water catching the distant surface — that she could see the doorframe she clung to, and the gap beyond it, and the room.

She gave it five more minutes anyway. Discipline was the whole difference between the divers who came back and the ones who became stories.

Then she switched on her light, low beam, and looked through the open door.

The archive room was small — three meters square, lined floor to ceiling with shelving that had once been oak and was now black iron-wood, preserved by the same cold that had saved the cargo. On the shelves: ledgers, their spines swollen with water but intact, wrapped in waxed cloth that someone had sealed with care. A strongbox, smaller than the one the navy had taken from her at the forbidden wreck, its lid open, empty. And on a desk built into the far bulkhead, weighted down with a brass bar, a bundle of documents tied with ribbon gone the color of dried kelp, and atop the bundle, sealed in glass, a letter addressed in a firm old-fashioned hand:

*To the one who opens this door.*

Petra's breath caught so hard her rebreather coughed a warning.

She reached for the letter — her fingers actually closed on the glass — and stopped. Training again. The room was intact because the room was sealed, and the seal was broken now, and everything in here was balanced on the edge of decay. Take the documents. Protect them. Read them in the light of the living world.

She wrapped the bundle in the waxed cloth from the nearest shelf, then in the oilskin from her own kit, lashing it flat against her chest plate beneath the compass, tight against her body where no snag could take it. The ledgers she could not carry — not all of them — so she took the two smallest, the ones whose spines read, in faded gilt, *PARTNERSHIP* and *CORRESPONDENCE*, and left the rest, marking the shelf in her memory with the precision of a woman who intended to come back.

At the door she paused. Behind her, the archive room breathed its hundred-year air into the hold, beginning, from this moment, to die. She looked at the desk, at the empty strongbox, at the shelves she was abandoning, and she spoke into her mask recorder, her voice sounding strange and small in the iron silence:

"I'm coming back for the rest. I promise."

Then she turned down the guideline, into the settling gray, and began the long climb out of the throat.

She was twenty meters from the gunport, ascending the sloping corridor of the hold with the document bundle riding her chest like a second heart, when her light swept across the stern bulkhead and stopped on something she had missed on the way in — something the angle of her beam had never touched until now.

Letters. Brass letters, screwed into the wood beneath layers of film, and she wiped them clear with her glove, and read them, and read them again.

The ship's name. The stern carving, half-buried in silt, crowned with the crest she knew now better than her own signature — the crest from the strongbox, from the map, from the lock she had just opened with her grandfather's compass.

But it was not the name from Vane's map. It was not the *Leeward Star* or any name the journals had recorded. The brass spelled out a different ship entirely, a name that changed everything she understood about what she had just unlocked and why the navy had cabled the mast and why Aldous Corvane had crossed an ocean to stand over this drop-off.

Petra hung in the green dark, her breath loud, the fleet's smoke visible in her mind's eye already rising over the horizon, and copied the name onto her slate letter by letter, her gloves trembling.

Above her, faint as a memory, the first rumble of thunder rolled down through the water from a sky she could no longer see.
