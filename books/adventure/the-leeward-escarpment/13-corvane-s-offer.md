## 13. Corvane's Offer

They came for her at slack water, when the tide hung between breaths and the Escarpment lay quiet beneath a sea like poured pewter.

Petra saw the launch first — a black-hulled tender cutting a clean white seam across the anchorage, flying no colors but carrying the unmistakable geometry of money. It came straight for the *Kestrel* without hesitation, without inquiry, the way a thing comes when it has already decided what it wants. Juno was below with the charts spread out on the saloon table, and Petra stood at the rail with salt drying in her hair from the morning's dive, watching the tender grow, and felt the particular cold that divers know: not fear exactly, but the body's early warning that the bottom is about to change.

"Juno," she said, not raising her voice. "Company."

The tender swung alongside with practiced grace. Four crewmen in matching oilskins — matching, which told her everything; salvage crews wore whatever they owned — and among them, descending to the *Kestrel*'s deck as though it were his own quarterdeck, came a man she had only ever seen in harbor gossip and grainy newsprint.

Aldous Corvane did not look like a magnate. That was the first shock. He looked like a working captain gone soft at the edges — broad hands, weathered face, a beard trimmed close and going gray at the jaw. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a man who had spent decades looking into water and learned never to blink. He wore no ornament except a heavy brass chronometer on a chain across his waistcoat, and he moved carefully, like someone who had once been very strong and resented having to prove it now.

"Miss Brightwater." He said her name the way a man sets a card down on a table. "You've been busy."

Behind her, she heard Juno come up the companionway and stop. The silence from the galley hatch had a shape to it — Juno holding herself still, holding back whatever she wanted to say, because they had agreed on this much: Corvane's people would try something, and when they did, neither of them would spend anything they didn't have to.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Petra said. "You know my name."

"I make it my business to know names." He glanced around the *Kestrel* with an appraiser's eye — the patched sail cover, the dive gear coiled on deck, the rust blooming along the toe rail. "This is a working boat. Honest. I respect that. Most of the people who come out here chasing the Escarpment arrive on borrowed money and leave on a stretcher, if they leave at all."

"And you? Did you come to wish me luck?"

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of the hit, perhaps, or of the arm that threw it.

"I came to offer you a fortune," he said.

He said it plainly, without theater, and somehow that made it worse. She had expected bluster, threats, the whole liturgy of power. Instead he simply stood there on her rotten deck in his immaculate oilskins and named a number — a number so large it stopped being money and became weather, became a change in the climate of her life. Enough to clear every debt. Enough to buy a new boat, a real one, steel-hulled, with a compressor and a proper winch and a crew that got paid on time. Enough to never again feel the specific humiliation of counting coins at a fuel dock while some harbormaster looked through her like glass.

She kept her face still. Diving had taught her that: the face is the last thing to give you away, if you train it.

"And in exchange?"

"You weigh anchor. You sail home. You forget the coordinates."

"The wreck doesn't belong to you."

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "It belongs to the sea. But the claim papers belong to me, and the navy's silence belongs to me, and by Friday the survey buoys will belong to me, and this stretch of water will be so thick with my fleet that your little boat couldn't get a line down without three of my tenders watching. So you see, Miss Brightwater, the question isn't whether I take what's down there. The question is whether you profit from standing aside, or whether you do it for free, out of stubbornness, and go home poorer and wiser."

The wind shifted. Somewhere below them, far down the escarpment wall, the wreck waited with its navy cable and its sealed archive room, and Petra thought of the compass in its box beneath her bunk, the cipher ring turning inside its brass housing like a small patient heart.

"Why does it matter to you?" she asked. "A fortune like yours. What's down there can't add anything to it."

For the first time, something flickered behind those pale eyes — quick, quickly mastered, but she caught it. Not greed. Something closer to fear.

"That," Corvane said, "is above your depth."

* * *

He invited her aboard the flagship.

She went. Later she would turn over the wisdom of that decision a hundred times, but in the moment it seemed simple: she could learn nothing from the deck of the *Kestrel*, and everything — or enough — from inside the enemy's walls. Juno argued against it in a fierce whisper at the rail, her jaw tight. "It's a trap. It's always a trap. He doesn't invite people aboard to be polite."

"He doesn't need to trap me," Petra said. "If he wanted the *Kestrel* taken, we'd already be taken. He wants something else."

"What?"

"I don't know yet. That's why I'm going."

She left Juno with the compass hidden in the bilge locker under a false panel, with instructions written twice and burned once, and crossed the gap between the boats on a gangway that gleamed with fresh varnish. The flagship was called the *Meridian*, and she was beautiful the way fortresses are beautiful — all function dressed as elegance, a hundred feet of white hull and polished brass, cranes and decompression chambers and a dive platform aft that could launch six divers at once. Everything aboard spoke of resources deployed without limit. This was what a fleet looked like when one man's will ran through it like a keel.

Corvane received her in the main cabin, which had been stripped of luxury and fitted instead with charts — hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, covering every coast she recognized and many she didn't. A single lamp burned on the chart table. Two glasses and a bottle of dark rum sat beside it, and the sight of the rum did more to unsteady her than the number he'd named on her own deck, because rum was what her grandfather — what Silas Vane — had drunk, cheap and raw, in the last hours of his life on her saloon floor.

"You don't drink?" Corvane asked, when she didn't touch hers.

"Not on duty."

"Duty." He turned the word over. "You're diving a colonial wreck off a forbidden escarpment against a fleet with naval backing, alone, on a boat worth less than my tender's anchor chain. And you call it duty."

"It's what I promised someone."

"Vane," Corvane said.

The name landed like a weight belt dropped on deck. Petra kept her hands flat on the chart table and made herself breathe slowly, four counts in, four counts out, the way she did at forty meters when the silt started moving.

"You knew him."

"I knew him longer than anyone alive," Corvane said. "I knew him before he was Vane. Before he was a prospector haunting reef camps and trading maps for liquor. Sit down, Miss Brightwater. You've earned the truth, if nothing else. Truth is cheap for men like me. We keep barrels of it."

She sat. He poured himself a measure, drank half of it, and stood looking at the wall of charts as though reading a page no one else could see.

"Thirty years ago there were two families in this trade," he said. "Two names on every charter, every insurance bond, every shipping ledger from here to the capital. One name was Corvane. The other—" he paused, and she watched him choose, deliberately, the way a diver chooses which line to cut first— "was Brightwater."

The cabin seemed to tilt. She gripped the edge of the table.

"My father," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "was Elias Brightwater. He ran coastal freight out of Grayhaven. He died when I was nine. There was no fortune. There was a house with a leaking roof and a mother who took in mending."

"There was no fortune," Corvane agreed, "because there wasn't supposed to be one. Because the Brightwater share of everything — the charters, the warehouses, the escarpment route itself — was signed away, sold, buried, and forgotten, by a man who understood that paper is only as strong as the silence around it." He turned from the charts and looked at her fully, and for one unguarded instant she saw not a magnate but an old man, tired in a way that money could not reach. "Your grandfather was my partner. My brother in everything but blood. And then he broke, and ran, and spent thirty years trying to dig up what we'd buried together."

"Together," Petra repeated.

"You think I stole it?" For the first time heat came into his voice, and with it something that sounded horribly like sincerity. "You think one man robs another and builds an empire on it and sleeps soundly for thirty years? We built it *together*. And when the ledgers were questioned — when the admiralty auditors came asking about the escarpment concessions — it was your grandfather's signature on the transfer, his hand on the pen, his choice to walk away with a settlement and a new name and let me carry the whole weight of it forward. He sold his half and hated himself for it, and spent the rest of his life pretending he'd been robbed, because being robbed is easier to live with than having sold."

"That's not what his journals say."

Something sharpened in his face. "You've read his journals."

She said nothing. Silence, she had learned, was the only currency that appreciated in a room like this.

Corvane set down his glass. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a man stepping onto thin ice knowing he must cross anyway.

"Silas Vane found you two years ago. Did you know that? He wrote to me. One letter, after thirty years of silence. He said he'd found a girl — a diver, in debt up to her neck, working wrecks nobody would name. He said she had her grandmother's compass and her grandfather's stubbornness, and he asked me what I intended to do about it." Corvane's colorless eyes held hers. "I did nothing. I told myself the girl was no threat, that Vane was an old man chasing ghosts. And then he died on your boat, and you came out here anyway, and now I'm offering you a fortune to walk away, and you're sitting in my cabin asking questions I can't afford to answer."

"Then answer the one I asked on deck," Petra said. "Why does it matter? What's in that hold that a fleet and a fortune and the navy's silence can't protect you from?"

Corvane was silent for a long moment. The lamp hissed faintly. Through the stern windows she could see the *Kestrel* riding at anchor, small and shabby and hers, with Juno somewhere aboard watching the flagship through a spyglass and cursing her name.

"Because paper survives," Corvane said finally. "That's the curse of paper, Miss Brightwater. Men burn, drown, rot, forget. Paper waits. And there is paper in that hold — old paper, original paper — and if it surfaces, everything I built becomes a theft in the eyes of every court and every port from here to the capital. Not because it was a theft. Because it will be *called* one." He leaned forward, palms flat on the chart table, mirroring her posture without seeming to know it. "So. Last time. Name your price. Any price. A ship of your own. A fleet of your own. A life where no one ever takes your boat away again. Walk away, and in twenty years you'll be the one receiving desperate divers in a cabin like this one."

And Petra Brightwater, who had counted coins at fuel docks, who had watched creditors circle the *Kestrel* like gulls, who had held a dying stranger's hand and promised him she'd finish what he started — Petra looked at the fortune laid out on the table between them and felt how light it was. How weightless. A number, and numbers float.

"No," she said.

Corvane exhaled. It might have been disappointment. It might have been relief.

"I was afraid you'd say that," he said. "You're exactly like him."

Then he did the thing she had not prepared for, the thing no amount of discipline braced her against. He reached into his waistcoat and drew out a folded photograph, old and foxed with age, and laid it on the chart table between the two untouched glasses.

A young woman stood on the deck of a schooner, laughing, one hand raised against the sun. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a brass compass with a rotating cipher ring.

Petra's hand went to her collarbone before she could stop it — to the place where the compass rode against her skin every day, where it had ridden since Silas Vane pressed it into her palm and made her promise.

"Her name was Maren Brightwater," Corvane said quietly. "My partner's wife. The finest navigator I ever sailed with, and the only person Aldous Corvane was ever afraid of. She wore that compass on the day she drowned, and her son—" his voice caught, actually caught, a hairline fracture in the stone— "her son grew up to run coastal freight out of Grayhaven, and die poor, and leave behind a daughter who dives wrecks nobody will name."

The cabin tilted again, worse this time, and this time it did not right itself. Petra heard her own breathing, too fast, too shallow, the breathing of a diver ascending too quickly through water that had suddenly lost its pressure.

"Elias Brightwater," Corvane said, "was my son."

The lamp hissed. The charts stared down from their hundred walls — every coast, every route, every name — and Petra understood, with the vertigo of a woman whose depth gauge has just shattered, that she had been diving blind her entire life.

"You're lying," she whispered.

"I have lied my whole life," Corvane said. "Ask anyone. But not about this. Never about this." He straightened, and the magnate returned to his face like a mask sliding home, and when he spoke again his voice was level and terrible. "You are my granddaughter, Petra. The blood in your veins bought every plank of this fleet. So understand what you're refusing when you refuse me. Understand whose name you're really fighting."

Outside, the light was failing. Down the escarpment, two tides ahead of schedule, the wreck waited in the dark with its paper that survives — and somewhere between the flagship and the *Kestrel*, in the cold rising water between them, Petra would have to decide which name she owed.
