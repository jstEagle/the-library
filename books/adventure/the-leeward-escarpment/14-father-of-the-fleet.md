## 14. Father of the Fleet

The flagship was called the *Meridian's Pride*, and she wore her name like a threat.

Petra stood on her deck as dusk came down over the anchorage, watching the Corvane fleet settle around them like a pack of animals circling a kill. Six salvage vessels, floodlit and humming, their cranes folded against the coming night like sleeping birds. Beyond the hull, the Leeward Escarpment dropped away into black water where a dead ship waited with its throat full of truth — and here she stood, drinking wine she hadn't asked for, in the cabin of the man who wanted that throat sewn shut forever.

Aldous Corvane poured himself a measure of something amber and did not offer her a second glass. He was smaller than his reputation. That was the first thing Petra had thought when she'd been piped aboard under escort, and it still held true an hour later: a compact man, silver at the temples, with hands that were surprisingly delicate — a jeweler's hands, or a forger's. His eyes were the problem. They were pale as shallow water and they never stopped moving, cataloguing her the way she catalogued a wreck before committing to the dive.

"You dive well," he said. "My people tell me you went down the drop-off alone, no tender, no line. That's either courage or arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Petra said. "I counted what I had and decided it was enough."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he'd kept one. He crossed to the great stern windows, where the fleet's lights burned reflected on the swells, and stood with his back half-turned to her. A performance. She recognized it because she was performing too; two actors who each believed they were the only one on stage.

"Silas Vane," Corvane said, to the window rather than to her. "Do you know how long I looked for that man?"

*Careful.* She let three seconds pass. "Should I know him?"

"Petra." He said her name gently, tasting it. "You came out here on a dead prospector's map, to coordinates that appear in exactly one private archive in the world, wearing a compass older than your boat. You can drop the mask whenever you like. I only ask that we both admit we're wearing them."

The wine sat untouched in her hand. Behind the cabin's paneled walls she could hear the *Pride* breathing — the groan of tonnage at anchor, the far-off clank of the dive crews securing gear for morning. Somewhere below decks, Juno Reyes was locked in a brig with salt water for a view, and every minute Petra spent up here being civil was a minute stolen back from the man who'd put her there.

"Say what you called me here to say," Petra said.

He turned then, and the lamplight caught his face full-on, and for one unguarded instant she saw not a magnate but an old man who had been running from something for forty years and had finally run out of ocean.

"I'll tell you a story," he said. "It's the same story Silas would have told you, if he'd had the stomach to finish it. It just happens to be mine."

* * *

He told it standing, pacing the length of the cabin like a man walking a deck in a storm only he could feel.

"In 'forty-one I was nobody," he began. "A clerk in Port Calloway with ink-stained cuffs and a head full of ledgers. There was a shipping concern then — Brightwater & Vane. Two sisters' money and one brother-in-law's nerve. The sister was named Maren. Maren Brightwater." He watched Petra as he said it, hunting for the flinch. Petra gave him nothing but stillness, though inside her chest something turned over slowly, like a body rolling in deep water.

"Their ships ran the leeward trade when everyone else said it couldn't be done. Coral passages, uncharted shoals, monsoon seasons — Maren's captains sailed routes no chart house would insure. And do you know why?" He tapped his own temple. "Because Maren Brightwater had a memory like a sounding line. Every reef, every current, every safe anchorage between here and the Escarpment, held in her head. The compass was hers — a cipher instrument, her father built it — and with it she could read charts that meant nothing to anyone else. The whole fortune rested on that woman's mind and that brass ring."

"And you stole it," Petra said quietly.

"I *inherited* it," Corvane snapped, and for the first time the polish cracked clean through. "That's the story, girl, so hear all of it. Maren married. Badly. A charming man named Edmund Corvane — my father — who drank through her profits and forged against her accounts until the concern was rotten as a piling. When she found out, she threatened to dissolve the partnership, take the routes, the compass, everything, and leave him bankrupt. So my father did what weak men do." His jaw worked. "He went to the colonial admiralty with a list of invented debts and a bribe, and he had her declared incompetent. Insane. Stripped her of her own company in a courtroom in an afternoon. Her brother — Silas — raged about it for years, made threats, tried to buy back shares with money he didn't have. And when the old man died and the estate passed to me, Silas Vane took the one thing that wasn't legally ours to begin with. He stole the compass off my mother's mantel and vanished into the islands, and he has been squatting in the ruins of my family's name ever since, telling anyone who'd listen that *we* were the thieves."

The cabin was very quiet. Rain had begun somewhere above them; Petra could hear it ticking against the skylight glass, soft as fingers.

She made herself breathe. Made herself hold the rage behind glass, the way you hold breath on a deep dive — sealed, pressurized, deadly if released wrong. Because every word he'd said was a mirror turned backward, a theft dressed as inheritance, a murder dressed as misfortune. But some part of it might be usable. Liars always left you rope.

"So Vane took your compass," she said, "and thirty years later sends a stranger to find the proof of a partnership deed that — what? Exists?"

Corvane's eyes narrowed a fraction. "There are documents on that wreck. Papers Maren's captain stowed when the *Providence* went down — papers my father should have burned and didn't, because he was a coward even with his crimes. If Silas recovers them, he rewrites history. If I recover them—" He spread those delicate hands. "They burn, and a family's good name stays good."

"Your family's good name," Petra repeated. She looked down at her wine. "And the woman whose mind built this fleet. What happened to Maren?"

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind the pale eyes that was almost human. Almost grief.

"She died in the colonial asylum at Grey Harbour," he said. "Eighteen fifty-three. Pneumonia, the record says." Then the shutters came down again, smooth as a hatch closing. "These were hard decades, Miss Brightwater. Cruel things were done by cruel men, and I have spent my life building something clean on top of the wreckage. That is not a confession. It is a foundation."

*You just confessed,* she thought. *In your own voice, in your own cabin, and you don't even know it.*

* * *

She played it carefully after that. Neither yes nor no. She asked questions that sounded like a woman weighing an offer: What exactly was he proposing? What happened to the map? What guarantee existed that a deal with Aldous Corvane survived the signing?

He answered each one with the patience of a man buying land, and all the while Petra worked the room the way Juno had taught her to work a chart — noting the clock on the mantel, the marine chronometer beside it running four minutes fast, the schedule board half-visible through the door when a steward entered: *DIVE WINDOW 0600–1000 / TIDE SLACK / TEAMS ONE AND TWO*. Noting which way the companionway ran. Noting that her escort had been reduced to a single bored bosun's mate outside the door, because Corvane wanted this conversation unwitnessed.

And noting, above all, the time. Juno had been in the brig since sundown. Which meant that whatever Juno intended to do about it, she'd already started.

"—and I'm prepared to be generous," Corvane was saying. "The Kestrel, free and clear. Your debts — and I know about your debts, Petra, I make it my business to know things — settled tonight, before you sleep. Fifty thousand more besides. All I require is the map, the compass, and your silence. You sail away rich, and the past stays where the sea put it."

"And if I say no?"

He smiled then, fully, and it was worse than any threat. "Then you're a trespasser on a restricted wreck with no papers and a crew member already in my brig, and I'm a man with six ships and a very long memory. But I don't think you'll say no." He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell bay rum and engine oil beneath the cologne. "I think you're smarter than Silas was. He let blood decide him. Blood makes poor ballast."

"Does it," Petra said.

"It does." He studied her face with those cataloguing eyes, and then, softly, almost tenderly: "You have her chin. Maren's chin. Did you know that? I saw it the moment you came aboard. Fate has a sense of humor, putting his granddaughter on my doorstep."

*His granddaughter.* The words landed like a depth charge, silent and then shattering somewhere far below. He knew. Of course he knew — he'd known since the harbor, maybe longer, maybe since Silas Vane first surfaced in his ledgers thirty years ago. Every word of this evening had been spoken to family, in his mind. A family business meeting. An heir being offered her share.

Petra set down the untasted wine.

"I'll need until morning," she said. "To think."

Something cold passed through his expression — disappointment, or suspicion, or simply the impatience of a man accustomed to yes. "Until dawn," he allowed. "The bosun will show you to a berth. You'll forgive me if the door locks from the outside. Fleet regulations."

"Fleet regulations," Petra agreed pleasantly.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and gave him the last thing she'd carried aboard for exactly this purpose: doubt. "One question," she said. "The asylum at Grey Harbour. Were you there? When she died?"

Corvane went very still.

"No," he said finally. "I was at sea."

"Liar," Petra said softly, and walked out before he could decide whether to be angry.

* * *

The bosun's mate walked her aft along the dim passage, lantern swinging, keys jingling at his hip. Petra counted doors, counted turns, marked the hum of the dynamo room and the smell of the galley — building the ship in her head the way she built a wreck in the dark, stroke by stroke, until she could swim it blind.

Her assigned berth was small and clean and locked behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence. Through the single porthole she could see the Kestrel riding at her mooring beyond the fleet's lights, small and stubborn, and past her the black shoulder of the Escarpment.

Petra sat on the bunk in the dark and let the glass crack, just slightly — let the rage come up through it in a thin, controlled stream, hot enough to work with. Maren Brightwater had died in a room with barred windows while this fleet's founder counted her ships. Silas Vane had spent thirty years alone in the islands carrying a sister's name like a coal he refused to drop. And tomorrow at slack tide, Corvane's divers would go down the throat of the *Providence* to burn the last word she ever wrote.

Unless the schedule changed.

Petra lay back and listened. Above her, boots on the deck plates — the watch changing. Forward, a hatch clanging. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the brig: a woman's voice, low and unhurried, talking her way past a guard the way only Juno Reyes could talk her way past anything.

Petra closed her eyes and began, silently, to rehearse the face she would wear at breakfast.

Dawn was two hours out. The dive window opened at six.
