## 15. Sabotage

Juno Reyes had copied charts before. She had done it at the Naval Cartographic Institute, under lamps that hummed like wasps, copying coastlines so precise they were lies — every reef surveyed, every depth sounded, and still the official charts left the Leeward Escarpment blank as a held breath. She had learned then that a map was never a record of the sea. It was a record of what someone wanted you to believe about the sea.

So when she slid the fleet's dive schedule off the flagship's chart table and into the lamplight, her hands were steady. It was the rest of her that shook.

The *Corvane's Pride* was quiet at this hour, or as quiet as a ship of that size ever was — three hundred feet of salvaged empire, brass rails polished by men whose wages came from other people's wrecks. Juno had spent two days aboard as a guest, which on this ship meant a prisoner with better manners. Corvane had been almost courtly about it. *Stay aboard, Miss Reyes, as my guest, while we discuss terms with your captain.* The word *guest* had been repeated often enough to become a threat.

She worked fast, the way she'd been taught, the way she'd been caught doing once before — that was the disgrace, the court-martial that wasn't a court-martial because Corvane's money reached into the admiralty like a root through a foundation wall. She'd refused to falsify a survey. She'd said, at twenty-six, with the certainty of someone who had never yet lost anything that mattered, that the sea deserved better than convenient charts. The sea hadn't thanked her. The admiralty hadn't either. And now here she was, ten years older and ten years hungrier, tracing Corvane's own dive schedule onto tissue paper by the light of a lamp she'd angled away from the porthole.

Two tides. That was the number that mattered. Corvane's divers were scheduled to breach the hold on the second tide after next — which meant Petra had one tide, maybe less, if the weather held, and the weather was not holding. The barometer in the flagship's passage had been falling all day like a stone dropped down a well. Juno had watched it and thought of Petra down there alone on the Escarpment with a rebreather and a compass and a promise to a dead man, and had hated herself, briefly and completely, for being warm and fed and useless aboard the enemy's flagship.

The tissue came away clean. She folded it into a cylinder the width of her smallest finger, sealed it in wax from the chart table's own stamp — a small irony she allowed herself — and slid it into the oilskin pouch strapped beneath her coat.

That was when the lights came on.

Not the lamp. The passage lights, all of them at once, and the particular silence that follows, when a ship full of people stops pretending to sleep. Juno stood very still and did the arithmetic she should have done an hour ago. The guard at the chart room door had changed at midnight. The new guard had not been the one who'd nodded to her at dinner. The new guard had been the one who'd watched her hands.

"Miss Reyes." The voice came from behind her, unhurried, almost kind. Bosun's mate, by the insignia. Two more men in the doorway behind him, and behind them, filling the passage like a shadow given rank, the man himself.

Aldous Corvane wore a dressing gown the color of deep water and an expression of mild, genuine regret. It was the regret that frightened her. She had seen men angry and men cruel and men drunk on their own power, but she had never seen a man who was sorry — truly sorry, the way you're sorry about weather — about what he was about to do.

"You've been a guest on my ship," Corvane said, "and you've repaid hospitality with theft."

"It's not theft," Juno said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. "It's testimony."

He smiled at that, and the smile was worse than the regret. "Bosun, take the paper."

They took the pouch, the tissue, the wax seal with its borrowed stamp. They took it with a delicacy that told her Corvane had already decided what she was worth and what she'd cost, and that the two numbers had been reconciled in his favor long before she'd picked up the pencil.

"The brig is not what you're imagining," Corvane said, as they took her arms. "It's a cabin. With a lock. I find that most people behave better when they're not treated like animals." He paused at the doorway, and for one moment the mildness dropped, and she saw the thing underneath, the thing that had built a fleet and stolen a fortune and — she was sure of it now, sure the way she'd been sure about the falsified survey ten years ago — killed a prospector on a night tow. "Your captain is a remarkable woman, Miss Reyes. It would grieve me if she did something foolish. When you see her again, tell her that."

*When,* not *if.* Juno turned that over in the dark of the brig, on a bunk that smelled of tar and old rope, and could not decide whether it was a promise or a threat or simply the confidence of a man who had never once lost a thing he'd decided to keep.

* * *

They brought her water at dawn and no news, which was its own kind of answer.

Juno had been confined before — the court-martial inquiry, six weeks of rooms with locks — and she knew the architecture of captivity: how it shrank the world to sounds, how the mind, starved of fact, began to manufacture it. By midmorning she had reconstructed the flagship's routine from the footsteps in the passage. By noon she had catalogued every creak in the brig's door. By midafternoon she had begun, against her will and better judgment, to imagine what was happening on the Escarpment, and every version she imagined was worse than the last.

Petra diving alone. Petra diving alone into a hold that had killed better-equipped divers. Petra surfacing to find the *Kestrel* gone — because Juno knew, had known since the second day, what the work parties on the fantail were for. She'd watched them lower the cutting gear over the side with the casual competence of men doing a chore. She'd told herself it was maintenance. She'd told herself a great many things, these past two days, and every one of them had been a lie she'd needed in order to keep her hands steady at the chart table.

In the late afternoon, the ship's motion changed. Anyone who'd spent her life on water felt it the way you feel a floor tilt: a subtle wrongness in the swell, a new tension in the rigging's voice. The fleet was turning. Juno pressed her ear to the bulkhead and listened to the engines change their note, and did the arithmetic of headings she couldn't see, and concluded that the *Corvane's Pride* was coming about — not toward the Escarpment, but across it. A patrol pattern. A net.

They were going to sweep the wreck site. Not to dive it — not yet, not in this weather — but to make certain that nothing and no one left it.

And the *Kestrel*. God. The *Kestrel* was a thirty-year-old hull with a patched plating and an engine that ran on stubbornness and prayer, and Petra loved it the way you love a thing that has kept you alive when nothing else would. If they'd cut her loose — and they had, Juno was sure of it now, sure with the sick certainty of hindsight — then somewhere out in this building sea was a boat drifting without anchor, and somewhere in Juno's chest was a guilt that had no bottom.

She had come aboard to copy a chart. She had told herself it was a small betrayal for a large good, the mathematics of the lesser evil, and she had believed it right up until the moment the mathematics turned on her. That was the thing about betrayal, she thought, lying in the dark as the ship rolled: it never stays the size you make it. You steal a schedule and you lose a boat. You tell one lie and the sea takes the interest.

The guard brought her supper — actual supper, on a tray, with a cloth napkin, because Corvane kept his prisoners the way he kept everything, in good taste. She ate it because she'd need the strength, and hated herself for the practicality, and ate it anyway.

Through the porthole, high and small, she could see a slice of sky going the color of a bruise. The storm was coming. She could feel it in the ship's bones, in the way the barometer must be falling now like a man down a ladder. Somewhere south of here, on a ledge of black rock above a drowned ship, Petra Brightwater was watching the same sky.

*Hold on,* Juno thought, and then, because she was who she was, a cartographer, a woman who trusted lines on paper more than she trusted people: *hold on, and I will find you. I will draw you a way out if I have to draw it in my own blood on the back of my own hand.*

It was the kind of vow you make in a locked room, where vows cost nothing. She made it anyway.

* * *

The storm arrived in earnest two hours past sunset, and with it, chaos — and Juno had learned in ten hard years that chaos was the only door a prisoner ever got.

The fleet ran before it with every hand on deck. She heard them: the bosun's calls, the crash of gear, the scream of wind through the hawseholes, a man's voice raised in genuine fear as something heavy went over the side. The brig's lamp swung on its gimbal and threw shadows that lunged. And through the porthole — she'd climbed onto the bunk to reach it — she watched the flagship's deck lights strobe and fail and strobe again, and watched, in one of those strobes, a shape in the water alongside.

A swimmer. No — a diver, finning hard through the black water, away from the flagship's stern, toward the dark.

Juno's heart did something complicated and painful. Because she knew the stroke. She'd watched it from the *Kestrel's* rail for six days, that economical, furious crawl, the kick that wasted nothing. Petra. Petra in the water, off the flagship, in a storm, at night — which meant Petra had been aboard, which meant Petra had walked into the lion's mouth and was now swimming out of it, and the lion either didn't know yet or was letting her go.

Then the lights steadied, and the deck below the porthole filled with men, and Juno heard the bosun's mate — the kind voice from the passage — shouting orders about searchlights and grapples, and understood that the lion knew.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass and watched the searchlights rake the water like white knives. They'd find her. They'd find her and drag her back aboard and this time there would be no dressing gown, no regret, no guest. Juno's hands curled against the porthole's brass rim until the metal bit.

*Swim dark,* she thought, with the whole force of her mind, as if thought were a rope Petra could hold. *Swim dark and deep and don't come up where the light is. The light is where they are. The dark is yours.*

The searchlights swept. The swimmer's wake vanished under a wave. And then — Juno would replay it a hundred times in the days to come, hunting it for hope the way a miser hunts a coin — the nearest searchlight stuttered, guttered, and died. Not the storm. She saw the junction box, or rather she saw the two figures at the junction box, one of them swinging something heavy, and then both of them running for the rail and going over the side in a dive she recognized from a hundred harbors: the flat, practiced entry of people who had done this before, who had planned it, who were —

Mutiny? No. She squinted through the streaming glass. Not fleet crew. Smaller. Leaner. One of them turned as the wave took him, and the light caught his face for half a second, and it was a face she knew from a photograph in a dead man's journal — no. Not that. A face she knew from the harbor at Port Calloway, a face that had owed Petra Brightwater money and had, against every law of debt and nature, come to pay it back.

Friends. Petra had friends in the water. Or at least allies, which in Juno's experience was the same thing at sea.

The second searchlight died. Then the third. And the *Corvane's Pride*, blind on her blind side, rolled and groaned and lost her in the dark, and somewhere out there in the black and boiling water Petra Brightwater was swimming away from her father's empire with nothing but a rebreather and a compass and, if the sea was kind, her life.

Juno sat back down on the bunk in the swinging dark, and for the first time since the chart room she let herself feel it — all of it, the whole crushing weight of what her small theft had cost. The *Kestrel* adrift. The dinghy sunk — she'd seen the dinghy's wreckage on the fantail that morning and had known, had *known*, and had said nothing. Petra alone. Juno locked in a brass-and-teak coffin while the woman she'd sworn to help fought the storm with her body.

And yet. The searchlights were dark. The swimmer was free. The fleet was blind and the storm was worse and somewhere out there, against all arithmetic, Petra was still moving.

Juno lay back on the bunk and stared at the swinging lamp and began, methodically, to plan. Because that was the thing about her, the one thing the disgrace and the drink and the ten lost years had never touched: she did not stop. Give her a locked room and she'd find the room's dimensions. Give her darkness and she'd chart it. Corvane thought he'd confiscated everything she had, but he'd left her the one instrument no brig could hold — the map she kept in her head, every line of this ship, every bulkhead, every lock, every watch rotation she'd memorized in two days of playing the harmless guest.

She closed her eyes and drew it: the brig, the passage, the scuttle to the chain locker, the cable ports, the tender deck aft where the storm crews wouldn't be.

One tide. She'd give herself one tide.

Because somewhere out on the Leeward Escarpment, in the worst weather the sea had thrown at them yet, a woman who finished what she started was waiting — and Juno Reyes had finally, at long last, found something worth finishing too.
