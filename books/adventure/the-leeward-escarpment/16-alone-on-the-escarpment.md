## 16. Alone on the Escarpment

The storm took the skerry an hour after dark, and Petra took stock of what she had left in the world.

One rebreather, scrubber canisters for perhaps nine hours of bottom time if she was careful with her breathing rate. One dive knife. One waterproof bag containing a flare, a signal mirror, half a brick of compressed rations, and a compass that was not a compass. One tarp. One line of rope, forty feet, salt-stiff. And herself — which, floating on a shelf of black rock no bigger than a galley floor while the Corvane fleet's lights burned on the horizon like a city that had decided to hate her, felt like the thinnest inventory of all.

She had watched them take Juno. She had heard the splash of the Kestrel's dinghy going down cut loose and holed. Somewhere behind her, in the dark water between here and the flagship's floodlit decks, her boat rode at anchor under a guard who thought she was still aboard, asleep in the aft cabin.

They would check by morning. She had until morning before they came looking, and even then they'd look toward shore, toward the cove where Vane's camp rotted, not out here at the throat of the Escarpment where nothing lived but current and cold.

So: three dives. Maybe four if the scrubbers held. Three chances to open a lock designed to defeat everyone but a dead man's granddaughter.

Petra pulled the tarp over her head, wedged herself into the lee of a granite rib, and let the rain hammer the canvas while she worked the compass open under the weak shield of her body.

She'd studied it a hundred times since the cove, since Juno's fingers had traced the cipher ring in Vane's journals and gone still with recognition. But studying it aboard the Kestrel, with tea and lamplight and Juno muttering numbers, was different from studying it alone with a storm screaming across thirty miles of open water. Alone, the thing looked less like an instrument and more like a hand — her grandmother's hand, maybe, or Silas Vane's, reaching up out of thirty drowned years to close around hers.

The outer ring carried the cardinal points, worn nearly smooth. Inside that, a second ring of letters, twenty-six of them, fine as engraving on a watch case. Inside that, a third ring of numerals. The needle, when she'd finally pried the bezel free two nights ago, had turned out not to be a needle at all but a pointer with a weighted tail, balanced to rest wherever the rings beneath it were set.

A combination lock wearing the face of navigation. Whoever built it had understood that the one object a sailor never abandons is her compass — that you could strip a woman of everything else and she would still carry this, because carrying it was reflex older than thought.

*You'll know how,* Vane's journal had said, in the entry Juno had translated by lantern light while Petra pretended not to cry. *Not because I tell you. Because it's yours.*

She set the rings now by feel, blind under the tarp, counting detents with her thumbnail. The letters spelled BRIGHTWATER around the full circle — she'd confirmed that much from the journals, the family name laid into the bezel like a keel laid into a hull. The question was the inner alignment, the numerals, and the journals gave her only fragments: *her birthday*, Vane had written once, and another time, *the day the Marigold went down*, and a third, angrier entry: *he chose the date himself. He always did love signing his work.*

Three dates. Three rings. She had two of them — the birthday from the photograph's inscription, the sinking from the shipping manifests Juno had memorized before the navy confiscated their charts. The third she didn't have, and wouldn't, unless the first two were enough to make the mechanism forgive her.

Or unless she could read it off the lock itself, down there, in the dark, in the hold of a ship that had been waiting sixty years for someone to come home.

She slept in pieces, waking each time the sea changed its mind about which direction to throw itself, and each time checked the horizon out of habit. The fleet's lights stayed fixed. They weren't diving tonight. Whatever Aldous Corvane knew about weather, he respected it — or whatever he suspected about her, he preferred to let the storm do his hunting.

*Let him wait,* she thought, and was surprised by the steadiness of it. Not rage. Something colder and more durable, something that felt like ballast.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and the wind had backed two points north, which meant the lee of the skerry was dying by degrees. Petra stood at the edge of the rock with the rebreather on her chest and the compass in its pouch against her sternum and looked down at the water, which was not water so much as a standing argument between the sky and the seafloor.

The drop-off began eight feet below her boots. She could see the shelf lip, the sudden absence of bottom, the blue going to indigo going to nothing. Somewhere down that wall, past the thermocline, past the depth where light quit being a promise, lay the mast wrapped in navy cable, the gunport like a missing tooth, the silted throat of the hold, and the archive door with its brass escutcheon and its three concentric keyways.

First dive: reconnaissance. Confirm the route, stage a cache, mark the door. No heroics. Her instructor's voice, dead ten years now, said it in her memory the way he always had: *The diver who rushes the descent meets the bottom faster than she planned.*

She rolled off the rock into chaos.

The surface layer tried to keep her — surge shoving her back against the barnacled base of the skerry, kelp hauling at her fins like hands with opinions. She swam through it with short, brutal strokes until the churn released her and the wall took over, and then she was dropping along the Escarpment's face with the whole weight of the ocean pressing her ears and the whole history of the Brightwater name pressing somewhere deeper than her ears.

At forty meters the light turned to rumor. At fifty-five, her torch beam found the mast.

It looked worse than she remembered — or rather, it looked exactly as bad as she remembered and she had lied to herself about it. The navy cable ran hawser-thick along the spar, crusted white with decades of calcification, anchored into the reef with eyes bigger than her fist. Someone had wanted this wreck not just buried but *furnished* — a grave with a locked gate. She hung beside it a moment, breathing slow through the loop, watching her exhaled bubbles vanish into the scrubber instead of rising, and felt the particular loneliness of a rebreather: no silver string of bubbles marking her passage, no trace, no witness. Just her, and the dark, and the ship.

She followed her memory of the route to the gunport. The collapse hadn't shifted; the jagged aperture still gaped at the angle that had nearly killed her last time, still demanded she exhale into the loop and fold herself through sideways, ribs to rust, trusting the metal not to move. She marked it with a strobe clipped inside, its pulse swallowed almost instantly by the murk, and pushed through.

Inside, the hold waited with its silt like a held breath.

She moved by touch and torch along the guide line she'd left from the previous dive, counting frames, and reached the archive door with six minutes of planned bottom time remaining. Up close, in the full beam, she saw what she'd been too oxygen-starved and too terrified to see before: the escutcheon wasn't decorative. Three concentric grooves radiated from the keyway, and each groove was engraved — faintly, the way frost etches glass — with characters. Letters on the outer groove. Numerals on the middle. On the innermost, so small she had to press her mask to the brass to read them, a sequence of dates.

Four dates, actually. Four lines of tiny figures, three legible, one effaced by corrosion into a smear of green.

Her heart was doing something unprofessional. She made it stop.

Two of the legible dates matched what she already knew: the birthday, the sinking. The third was new — a date in early spring, sixty-one years ago. And the fourth, the corroded one, was the answer to a question she hadn't known how to ask.

She committed all four to memory the way divers commit everything: twice forward, once backward, then again. Then she backed out of the hold, through the gunport, up the wall, breaking the surface into rain that fell like small gray gravel.

* * *

The second dive was the arithmetic dive, and the arithmetic nearly killed her before the water got the chance.

Back under the tarp, shivering so hard her teeth chattered the numbers loose, she worked the problem. Two dates confirmed by the door. A third date, unknown meaning. A fourth, unreadable. The compass had three rings. The journals had said *three dates*. Which meant one of the four on the escutcheon was a lie — a decoy, a trap, a wrong setting that would seize the mechanism or worse, and she had no way to know which.

Unless.

She dragged the compass out and turned it under the gray light, and this time she looked not at the rings but at the case back, which she'd never had reason to open. It took her knife and ten minutes of swearing and a slip that opened a cut across her knuckle she didn't notice until the blood diluted pink in a puddle of rainwater. Inside the case back, scratched into the brass with something sharp and desperate — a nail, a fishhook, a dying man's final stubbornness — was a single line:

*He signed it the day he took her name.*

Petra sat with that while the storm walked circles around her skerry.

The day he took her name. Corvane — no, not Corvane yet, the partner, the man who became Corvane — had married into the Brightwater fortune or stolen it under a new signature, and the day he did it was the day the partnership deed was drawn. The deed was in the archive. The date of the deed was on the door. The third date, the spring date, was the signature date — and the fourth, the corroded one, was the decoy, because a man who loved signing his work would also love leaving a false signature where a thief might find it.

But which of the two remaining was false? Spring signature or corroded fourth?

She closed her eyes and thought about Vane. Thirty years searching. Thirty years of journals written in a hand that started furious and ended patient. A man who had time to booby-trap nothing and chose, instead, to leave instructions in brass scratchings — *he signed it the day he took her name* — because he trusted the person who found the compass to be smart enough and angry enough to understand.

The corroded date was the trap. Corrosion doesn't happen by accident on one line of four; someone had packed that groove with something acidic, something that would eat the true date and leave a plausible fake. Vane's warning told her which line to distrust without telling her why — because the why was on the door, and the door was sixty meters down.

Third dive. The real one.

She ate half the ration brick, forced water into herself, and slept two hours in fifteen-minute installments while the wind climbed from hard to criminal. By the time the light failed toward evening, the surface of the sea had gone from argument to riot, and every instinct she owned said *not tonight, not alone, not at that depth.*

Every instinct except the one that counted the fleet's lights on the horizon and noticed, for the first time, that there were fewer of them. Boats moving. Boats positioning. Come morning, Corvane's divers would be in the water, and whatever window she had was closing like a hatch.

She suited up in the dark.

* * *

Descent number three was the worst thing she had ever done on purpose.

The surface was a beating — she took a wave across the back of the head that rang her mask against her brow, lost the skerry from view in eleven seconds, and descended into a world where her torch beam bent and shredded in the surge. At thirty meters the swell died and the cold took its place, a cold that came through the wetsuit like intent. At fifty-five she found the strobe's pulse and followed it in.

The gunport tried to kill her properly this time. The surge, felt even at depth as a slow tidal breathing, sucked her against the rusted lip as she folded through, and she felt the steel bite through her suit sleeve and into the arm beneath — a long, cold line of pain she catalogued and dismissed, because panic was a luxury billed in air. She was through. She was in. The line on her arm could invoice her later.

Down the guide line. Counting frames. The hold breathing its silt around her, and this time she gave the silt the respect it had earned: finning high, hands only, a monk's discipline of movement, because a silt-out down here alone meant dying in a cloud of your own making with a door six feet away and no way to find it.

The archive door filled her beam.

She braced herself against the frame, pulled the compass from its pouch, and set the rings by feel and memory. Outer ring: BRIGHTWATER, the letters finding their detents one by one under her thumb, each click transmitted through the brass into her bones like a heartbeat learning a new rhythm. Middle ring: the birthday. Inner ring: the spring date — the signature date, the day he took her name.

She pressed the compass into the escutcheon.

It seated with a sound she felt more than heard, a deep mechanical *chunk* that traveled through the door and into the wreck and, she would have sworn, into the seabed itself. For one long second, nothing.

Then the door exhaled.

Sixty years of sealed air and brine pressure equalized through some valve she'd never have found, and the archive swung inward six inches — six inches of darkness that smelled, impossibly, faintly of cedar — and stopped, held by the wreck's own settled weight.

She had planned to go in, take the deed, take the letters, take everything, and be out in four minutes. That was the plan. That was the plan she abandoned the moment her beam crossed the interior and she saw the shelves, the oilcloth bundles, the writing desk bolted to the deck with a chair still tucked beneath it, and on the desk, centered with terrible care, a letter sealed with wax and addressed in a hand she recognized from thirty years of journals:

*For the girl who finishes things.*

Petra hung in the doorway of her inheritance and did not move.

Her air ticked down. The wreck breathed around her. Somewhere above, through sixty meters of murdered light, the storm was rearranging the surface of the world, and somewhere beyond that, the fleet was turning in its sleep, and none of it — not the debt, not the navy, not the man who shared her blood and called himself her father — none of it could reach her here.

She reached through the gap and took the letter.

And as her fingers closed on it, her torch swept the desk's edge and caught something she hadn't seen, something that turned the cold in her veins to ice water: fresh scratches in the wood. Recent. Days old, not decades. Tool marks.

Someone had been here before her. Recently. And whoever it was had left the letter behind — or wanted her to think they had.

The archive was not empty. And she was not alone in the dark.
