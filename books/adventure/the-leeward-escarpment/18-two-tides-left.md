## 18. Two Tides Left

The storm found her an hour after she surfaced.

Petra had been swimming for the skerry when the horizon went the color of a bruise — not the clean gray of ordinary weather but a deep violet-black that meant the sky intended to spend everything it had. She'd seen squalls like it twice in her life. Once off the Sable Teeth, where a fishing crew lost three men and their boat in eleven minutes. Once as a child, watching from her mother's window while the harbor fleet ran for shelter and one old ketch didn't make it, and the whole town stood on the seawall in silence until dawn.

She was still half a mile out when the first gust hit, flattening the swell into white steam. The skerry — a tooth of black basalt no bigger than the Kestrel's deck, crowned with a squat stone bothy some long-dead pilot had built against days exactly like this one — sat dead ahead, and Petra swam for it with the archive bag lashed to her chest and the sea trying to peel her away like a stamp from an envelope.

She made the rocks on the third attempt. The first wave threw her against the basalt and held her there, grinding, testing whether she would let go. She did not let go. The second wave lifted her high enough to hook her arm over a ledge, and she hauled herself out of the water the way she'd once hauled herself out of a flooded compartment forty meters down: by deciding, absolutely, that drowning was someone else's story.

The bothy's door had rotted off its hinges decades ago, but the walls were solid, dry-stacked stone, and the roof — slabs of slate pinned down with wire — had outlived every boat that had ever sheltered beneath it. Petra got inside, stripped her wet suit to the waist, wrung it out, and put it back on, because the wind coming through the gaps between stones had teeth and the temperature was falling fast. There was an old iron stove in the corner, rusted through at the seam, useless. A shelf held a clay jar gone to dust, a coil of tarred rope stiff as bone, and nothing else. Whoever the pilot had been, he'd left this place to the gulls a long time ago.

Outside, the world ended. Rain came sideways, then came so hard it stopped being rain and became the ocean attacking itself. Thunder rolled along the escarpment behind her like artillery. Through the doorway she could see white water detonating over the reef line where the *Kestrel* should have been anchored, and wasn't — couldn't be; Juno had taken her around to the lee anchorage before Petra went down the last time, and now the storm had closed the strait between them like a book snapping shut.

Juno was on the other side of that water. In Corvane's brig.

Petra sat with her back against the coldest wall, the archive bag between her knees, and made herself take inventory the way her grandfather — no. The way Silas Vane had taught her without ever meeting her. What do you have? One rebreather, two-thirds charged. One flare pistol, two flares. Half a brick of ration biscuit gone soft. A compass that was also a key. And a bag of paper that could buy her anything in the world except the thing she actually wanted.

What do you need? A tide window. A way across a quarter mile of boiling water to the lee anchorage, if the Kestrel was even still there. And then a plan for getting aboard a flagship crawling with Corvane's men and pulling Juno Reyes out of a locked compartment without getting either of them shot.

The arithmetic was bad. She went through it anyway, twice, because going through bad arithmetic calmly was most of what diving was, and most of what living was too, if you did it honestly.

* * *

It was full dark when she opened the letter.

Not Vane's letter — she hadn't known about that one yet. She'd found it folded into the back of the archive, wrapped in oilcloth with the deed and the letters from her grandmother, tucked behind a false panel in the strongbox's lid as though he'd wanted whoever opened it to earn the last word. She'd carried it up from the hold sealed in wax paper, and all through the swim and the climb and the first hour of the storm she hadn't touched it, because some part of her understood that once she read it, Silas Vane would finally be finished — finished the way the drowned are finished, with nothing left to say — and she wasn't ready to let him go.

But the storm wasn't going anywhere, and neither was she, and there is only so long a person can sit in the dark listening to the end of the world without reaching for whatever light they've been saving.

She struck the flare pistol's spare cartridge against the stove's iron lip — a trick Juno had shown her, wasting one of her two flares for light, and worth it — and wedged it upright in a crack in the floor. Red light filled the bothy like something breathing. Then she unfolded the letter.

The handwriting was cramped and old-fashioned, the hand of a man who'd learned penmanship in a schoolroom sixty years gone and never had reason to change it. It began without preamble, the way his journals had begun, the way dying men write when they know the reader will forgive them anything except wasted time.

*If you are reading this, then you got into the hold, which means you are either very good or very stubborn, and I am betting on both.*

*My name was Silas Vane. Your grandmother's name was Maren Brightwater, and she was my sister, and by the time you read this you will already know what Aldous Corvane and his partner took from her. I spent thirty years looking for this room. I want to tell you why.*

*Everyone will tell you it was for the money. The deed, the letters, the confession — enough to ruin him, enough to restore us. And yes. But that was only ever half of it. The other half is this: a man can lose everything he owns and still be rich, but a man who loses his name is poor in a way that has no bottom. They took our name off the ships. They took it off the ledgers. They took it off my sister's grave marker and put a stranger's crest there instead. For thirty years I have walked around under a borrowed name, and I have hated every day of it, and I promised myself that whoever finished this work would get to choose their own name at the end of it. Not inherit mine. Not inherit his. Choose.*

*I chose you, and I never met you, and here is how. Two years ago I paid a debt collector in your harbor to tell me about the divers who worked the forbidden wrecks. Most of them he described as men running from something. You he described differently. He said there was a woman who took contracts nobody else would touch, and paid her debts to the day, always to the day, even when paying them broke her. He said she once spent a season salvaging a wreck she knew was worthless because the widow who owned the claim needed the insurance papers recovered more than she needed money, and this woman did it at cost. He said — and I wrote this down word for word, girl, because it was the first hope I'd felt in twenty years — he said: "That one finishes what she promises."*

*That is why you. Not blood. Blood is an accident of birth and I have watched it rot in better families than ours. I chose you because you finish what you promise. Everything else — the compass, the map, the name — those were just tools waiting for the right hands.*

*So here is my last request, and you may keep it or refuse it, because a promise given freely is the only kind worth anything: when this is done, decide who you are. Not who Corvane says you are. Not who the ledgers say. Decide. And then go and be that person, and let the sea keep the rest.*

*Your grandfather,*
*Silas Vane*

The flare died slowly, guttering, and Petra sat in the returning dark with the letter pressed flat against her sternum, and discovered that she was crying — not the way she'd cried for him aboard the Kestrel, hot and helpless, but quietly, steadily, the way water finds its level. Thirty years. He had spent thirty years alone on rocks like this one, eating dust out of jars, and the thing he'd wanted at the end wasn't vengeance and wasn't gold. It was that she should get to say who she was.

And the first thing she knew, sitting there in the dark with the storm hammering the slate above her head, was that she was not Aldous Corvane's daughter. Whatever lived in her blood, whatever accident of birth had wired her to that man — it was weather. It passed over. What stayed was chosen.

She was Petra Brightwater. She finished what she promised.

She had promised Silas Vane she would carry the map to the Escarpment and tell no one. Done. She had promised herself, somewhere in the flooded dark of the hold with her air running thin, that she would not leave Juno Reyes in Corvane's brig to pay for Petra's war. Not done. Not nearly done.

She wiped her face on her sleeve, and began, methodically, to plan.

* * *

The storm blew itself hoarse sometime past midnight and settled into a long gray grumble, and near dawn the eastern sky cracked open just wide enough to show her the water.

The strait between the skerry and the lee anchorage was still ugly — white streaks where the wind funneled through, a heavy residual swell rolling in from open sea — but it was crossable. Barely. And the Kestrel was there. Petra stood at the base of the skerry with the cold water climbing her thighs and looked at the little boat riding her anchor a quarter mile out, masts bare, deck empty, hull low in the water in a way that made Petra's jaw tighten. Low, or listing?

She'd find out. She waded until she could swim, archive bag lashed tight, compass in its pouch against her ribs, and struck out across the strait with a stroke she kept deliberately slow, conserving heat, letting the swell carry her rather than fighting it. Twice a wave broke over her head and shoved her down and she rode it, loose, the way she rode silt-outs, trusting her body to know things her mind had stopped calculating.

Her hand touched the Kestrel's anchor chain and she almost laughed.

The dinghy was gone — scuttled, she assumed, another of Corvane's parting courtesies — but the Kestrel herself was intact, riding high again now that Petra's weight was aboard, decks swept clean by the storm, the little cabin dry. Juno had lashed everything down before taking her around the point, and Juno's knots were naval knots, tied to survive exactly this. Petra stood in the cockpit dripping salt water onto boards she'd half believed she'd never see again and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time in two days.

Then she went below, lit the alcohol burner, and started making plans with a pencil and the back of a chart.

Corvane's flagship, the *Meridian Crown*, lay at anchor off the escarpment's northern shoulder — she'd seen her lights through the storm's ragged edge, a constellation of them, arrogant even in weather that had driven every other vessel to shelter. Corvane would not sail in this. He would wait for the weather to finish, wait for his dive teams to recover, wait for word of the woman who'd vanished off his deck at night. He was a man who believed patience was a form of ownership. That was his weakness, and it was also hers, because a man who believes he owns the outcome stops guarding the door.

Two tides. That was what the almanac in her head told her, cross-checked against the storm's timing. Two tides before the strait settled enough for Corvane's tenders to run comfortable patterns, before his divers went back down to a wreck whose archive room stood open and empty, before anyone thought to ask hard questions about where Petra Brightwater had gone. Two tides to get close to the flagship, get below, get Juno out of the brig, and get clear.

Alone, in a stolen skiff she didn't have, against a crew of two hundred.

Unless.

She turned the chart over and stared at the blank back of it for a long moment, and then she began to draw — not a course but a shape, the shape of the escarpment's northern shoulder, the drop-off, the wreck, the cable. And beside it she drew the *Meridian Crown*'s silhouette as she'd memorized it from the night she'd slipped off her deck: the dive platform aft, the gantry cranes, the tender bays, and — crucially — the moon pool. Every salvage flagship Corvane built had one; Juno had described them with contempt, the lazy man's way to run dive operations, a flooded well through the hull so divers could enter and exit without surfacing on the open deck. Flooded. Open to the sea. Guarded, certainly, but guarded by men who had no reason to expect a diver to come up out of the ocean and into the belly of their ship in the middle of a post-storm morning.

A diver could do it. A diver with a rebreather, no bubbles, and nothing left to lose except a promise.

Petra set the pencil down and looked at what she'd drawn, and for the first time since Vane had bled out on her deck, the fear in her gut settled into something colder and more useful. Not calm, exactly. Direction.

She thought about what Vane had written. *Decide who you are.* All right. Here was the decision, then, made in a rocking cabin with the smell of alcohol fuel and old charts: she was not the woman who sold the proof and bought her freedom. She was not the daughter who inherited an empire of stolen ships. She was the woman who finished what she promised, and she had one promise left outstanding, and it had a name, and the name was Juno Reyes.

She killed the burner, checked the rebreather's charge, and went topside to read the water.

The second tide would turn at midday. By early afternoon the strait would lie down enough to run the Kestrel north under bare poles, hugging the escarpment's shadow where Corvane's lookouts wouldn't think to sweep. She'd bring her boat within a mile of the flagship — close enough to swim, far enough to be invisible — and she'd go into the water one last time, down through the cold green, up through the moon pool, into the throat of the beast.

And if it went wrong — she stood at the rail and let herself consider it honestly, because honest consideration was the price of every plan she'd ever survived — if it went wrong, then the sea would keep her alongside Vane and her grandmother and everyone else who had ever bet everything on a single breath. She found, examining the thought from all sides, that it didn't frighten her the way it once would have. She had already surfaced owning nothing but herself. Everything after that was diving.

The tide began to turn. Far to the north, the *Meridian Crown*'s lights still burned pale against the brightening sky, patient as a spider, certain as a man who has never once been robbed by his own daughter.

Petra went below and started unshipping the rebreather's spare canisters.

Two tides left. She intended to use both.
