## 19. Burning the Deed

The storm that had trapped her on the skerry broke at dusk, and Petra rode its dying swells toward the Corvane flagship like a splinter riding a wave.

She had spent the last of the daylight preparing, because preparation was the only prayer she trusted. The *Meridian Star* lay at anchor two miles off the Escarpment's leeward shoulder, her running lights burning fat and yellow through the rain-haze, her decks crowded with the silhouettes of men who thought the night belonged to them. Corvane had pulled his divers in when the weather turned; no fleet worked a drop-off in a rising sea, not even his. That meant the ship was heavy with idle crew and light on vigilance — drunk on their own numbers, certain that the woman they'd fished half-drowned off a rock was too broken to come back.

Petra had been broken before. She knew how to use the cracks.

The Kestrel was gone — scuttled in the straits, her hull feeding the crabs somewhere below the shipping lanes — but the fleet had left its garbage where storms pushed it. A tender, holed and half-swamped, had washed into the cove behind the skerry, and Petra had spent three hours with a bilge pump and a length of salvaged hose making it float again. It floated the way a drowning man floats: barely, briefly, and only if nothing asked too much of it.

She wore her dive skin under oilskins, the rebreather canister strapped across her back beneath the coat, and inside the coat, against her chest where her heartbeat could warm it, the oilskin packet. The deed. The letters. Corvane's confession, written in his own younger hand, admitting what he'd done to Marisol Vane — to Marisol Brightwater — thirty years ago on a night much like this one.

And Vane's letter to her. She had read it eleven times on the skerry, until the paper went soft as cloth. *I chose you not for your blood, girl. I chose you because you finish what you promise.* She carried those words the way other people carried knives.

The compass she carried too, though it wasn't needed anymore. Its work was done. It rode in her pocket like a small cold heart, ticking against her ribs with every swell.

* * *

She came at the ship from downwind and dark water, cutting the tender loose fifty yards out and swimming the rest, letting the storm's leftover chop cover the sound of her strokes. The *Meridian Star* had been built for prestige as much as salvage — a converted naval corvette with a gleaming superstructure and a stern ramp wide enough to launch three dive teams at once — and prestige meant lights, and lights meant shadow, and shadow meant a way up.

Petra found it at the anchor chain.

She came out of the water hand over hand, the chain cold and furred with weed, pausing at each link to let the roll of the ship carry her noise away. Above her, boots crossed the foredeck. Someone laughed. Somewhere aft, the engine room throbbed its low constant note, the furnace breathing heat through the deck plates like a sleeping animal. She memorized that sound. She would need it later.

Over the rail. Into the shadow between a winch housing and a stack of chain lockers. She crouched there, dripping, and let the ship teach her its rhythms: the watch changing at the top of the hour, two men walking the perimeter with lanterns and bad posture, the galley sending coffee forward around the half-hour. Corvane ran a tight fleet on paper. In practice, at anchor, in weather, his men were tired and bored and looking forward to shore leave, and bored men miss things.

The brig was below, aft, near the engine spaces — Juno had told her that much in the two minutes they'd had aboard the flagship before everything went wrong, gesturing with her chin while the guards marched her past. *Below, aft, by the engines. If you get clear, don't come back for me.* Petra had every intention of disobeying her.

She went down the aft ladder in the blind spot behind the funnel casing, moving when the swell moved, freezing when it didn't. The lower passage smelled of diesel and salt and old steel, and the heat rose through the deck plates until the air itself felt like a held breath. Two guards outside the brig door, exactly as predicted. One young, one older, the older one mid-story about a woman in Port Calloway, both of them leaning into the warmth like cats.

Petra had planned three approaches and discarded two of them on the swim over. What remained was ugly and simple.

She came around the corner at a stumbling run, oilskins open, gasping — a woman half-drowned, which was even true. "Please — please, the skerry, my boat — the patrol boat went down, there were men in the water—"

The young one stepped toward her first, which was the mistake she needed him to make. Her hand came up out of the coat with the wrench she'd taken from the tender's toolbox, and she caught him across the temple with a short, economical stroke that dropped him like rigging cut at the masthead. The older one got his mouth open — got half of her name out, actually, *you're the diver, you're Corvane's—* — and then her boot found the side of his knee and her elbow found his throat and the passage went quiet except for the engines and her own breathing.

She took his keys off his belt. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself, distantly, the way you notice weather.

The cell door opened on a smell of rust and despair, and Juno Reyes looked up from the bunk with a bruised face and eyes that went from flat to blazing in the space of one heartbeat.

"You absolute idiot," Juno whispered.

"Probably." Petra tossed her the spare jacket she'd brought. "Can you walk?"

"I can dance." Juno stood, swayed, steadied herself against the bulkhead. Her wrists were raw where the cuffs had been. She looked at Petra properly then, at the packet-shaped bulge under the oilskins, and something in her face shifted — recognition, dread, and underneath it a fierce, terrible hope. "You have it."

"I have it."

"Then we run. We get to the tender, we get to port, we take it to the admiralty, to the papers, to anyone—"

"No." Petra said it quietly, and the quiet stopped Juno colder than a shout would have. "He wins that way. Slowly. In courtrooms, over years, with lawyers and delays and a navy that's already been confiscating charts for him. He buys his way through trials the way he bought the harbor master." She looked down the passage, toward the throbbing heart of the ship. "There's a faster way."

Juno followed her gaze and understood, and Petra watched the understanding move through her like a current. "You're going to burn it."

"I'm going to burn it in front of him."

* * *

They took the guards' lantern and went aft, Juno walking with a limp she exaggerated whenever they passed an open doorway, playing the prisoner being transferred, while Petra played escort. It worked twice. The third time, a bosun's mate looked too long at Juno's face, and Petra solved it by shoving them both through a hatch marked ENGINEERING and locking it behind them with the guard's keys.

The engine room took them in like a throat.

Heat first, a wall of it, the air thick enough to chew. Then the noise — pistons hammering, steam hissing through joints, the great furnace doors glowing orange at the far end where stokers fed the maw with coal and, in wartime tradition kept alive out of thrift, anything else that would burn. The deck plates rang underfoot. Nobody saw them; everybody was busy being part of the machine.

Corvane would be on his bridge or in his cabin, but Petra didn't want his cabin. She wanted him here, where the fire already lived.

"Get the stokers out," she said. "Fire drill. Your call, cartographer — make it official."

Juno grinned through a split lip, straightened her ruined uniform collar into something resembling authority, and waded into the heat shouting about a fuel leak and a pressure fault and all hands clear of Number Two furnace, and by God she sounded like twenty years of naval discipline, and men who had never seen her face obeyed her anyway because obedience is a reflex, not a decision. Within four minutes the engine room floor was empty except for the two of them and the roaring furnace and the man who came down the ladder last, drawn by the shouting, furious at the disruption.

Aldous Corvane came down into his own engine room in a dressing gown over his clothes, silver-haired and upright, with the particular anger of a man interrupted in the act of winning.

"What is the meaning of—" He saw Petra, and stopped. And Petra watched, with a clarity she knew she'd keep for the rest of her life, as his anger rearranged itself into calculation, and the calculation into something almost gentle. Almost paternal. "Petra. You should be resting. After the skerry — you nearly died out there, girl. Come upstairs. We'll talk like family."

"We'll talk here," Petra said. "You like it hot, don't you? I've read your letters. You wrote about the tropics. You wrote that heat makes honest men of everyone, eventually."

His eyes flicked to the packet under her arm, and this time he couldn't hide it — the hunger, quick as a fish taking bait. "So you found it."

"I found everything." She walked backward, slowly, toward the furnace, keeping the deck plates between them. "The partnership deed. My grandmother's letters — Marisol Vane's letters, signed Brightwater after the wedding nobody in your ledgers admits happened. And your confession. Thirty years old, in your own hand. You wrote it to her, did you know that? You wrote it and never sent it. Guilt keeps strange archives."

"Those documents are worthless," Corvane said. His voice stayed level, but his hands had closed at his sides. "Paper. Thirty-year-old paper. No court in the region—"

"You keep saying courts." Petra pulled the packet free. The oilskin fell open. The deed was there, vellum gone amber, the twin crests pressed side by side in wax — Brightwater and Corvane, partners once, before one partner became a corpse in deep water and the other became an empire. "I'm not going to court. You taught me better than that, Father. You taught everyone in this fleet that possession is the whole of the law. So."

Behind him, unnoticed in the chaos he hadn't yet processed, Juno had drifted to the ladder and now stood at its foot, blocking it, arms folded, watching with the stillness of a woman memorizing every second.

"You want to know the joke?" Petra said. "Vane — Silas, my grandfather, the man you hunted across thirty years and finally shot on the deck of my boat — he wanted this published. He wanted your name dragged through every port from here to the Capes. I promised him I'd finish what he started, and for six tides I thought finishing it meant this." She held the deed up so the furnace light shone through the vellum, translucent, the ink floating in it like veins. "But I've been reading your letters, Aldous. All of them. And do you know what I learned?"

"Put it down," Corvane said, and for the first time his voice cracked along some hidden seam. "Petra. Put it down. Whatever you want — the debt, the Kestrel, a share, a name, my name, it's yours, it was always going to be yours—"

"That's the joke," Petra said. "It was always mine. Half of everything you own was always mine, by law, by blood, by every rule you've ever used to crush someone smaller. I could take it. I could stand on your quarterdeck tomorrow and be the heiress of the Corvane fleet, and everyone who ever sneered at me in a harbor bar would have to drink to my health." She smiled, and felt how little joy was in it, and how much else. "And I would spend the rest of my life knowing that everything I touched was paid for with my grandmother's life. With Silas's thirty lost years. With Juno's career. With every diver you've sent down into water you knew would kill them. Your whole empire is a corpse wearing a crown, and you've spent thirty years doing its makeup."

"Girl—"

"My name," Petra said, "is Petra Brightwater. Say it."

Something moved behind his eyes — rage, or grief, or the ghost of whatever he'd felt for Marisol's daughter before he'd helped bury her. "Brightwater," he said, and it came out like a wound reopening. "That name died with your mother."

"No," Petra said. "It's been waiting at the bottom of the Escarpment. Everything waits at the bottom of the sea. That's the one thing you and I ever truly shared, Father. We both believed the sea keeps what matters." She turned to the furnace. The stokers had left the great iron door ajar, and the fire inside roared orange and white, hungry, indifferent, the oldest honest thing on the ship. "The difference is, you wanted to take from it. I'm giving something back."

"Petra—" He lunged.

Juno was faster. She came off the ladder like a spring, caught his arm, twisted, and put him down onto the ringing deck plates with a knee between his shoulders and thirty years of disgraced-service fury in her grip. "Stay down, sir," she said pleasantly. "You're under citizen's arrest, pending the arrival of literally anyone with integrity."

Petra knelt beside the furnace door.

For one moment — one long, salt-slow moment — she held the deed above the fire and let herself feel the full weight of what it was. Freedom, folded in oilskin. Debt cancelled, name restored, the fortune that was hers by blood hers by right, the life she could have had instead of the one she'd drowned in. Every creditor in Port Calloway erased with one motion. Every winter of patched sails and thin soup avenged. All she had to do was keep it. All she had to do was be the kind of person the world had taught her to be.

*I chose you because you finish what you promise,* Silas had written. And what had he promised Marisol, all those years ago, kneeling by her grave or her empty grave or wherever the sea had taken her? Not vengeance. Petra was sure of it now. He'd promised remembrance. He'd promised that she would not be erased.

Burning the deed wouldn't erase Corvane. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, and Petra had done the sums alone on the skerry until dawn. Burn the proof, and his empire stood — richer than ever, untouchable, unprosecuted. But he would stand inside it knowing that his daughter had seen the whole rotten architecture and refused it. Refused the money, refused the name he offered, refused him. He would own everything and possess nothing, because the one inheritance that mattered had just walked into a furnace, and he would spend every remaining day of his life knowing that the only thing he could not buy was the one thing he'd wanted most: to be chosen.

Some punishments aren't sentences. They're mirrors.

"For Marisol," Petra said quietly. "For Silas. For the Kestrel." She fed the deed into the fire.

The vellum caught with a sound like a gasp, curled, bloomed gold at its edges, and was gone. The letters went next — her grandmother's careful script browning, lifting, dissolving into flame, thirty years of silenced voice suddenly, briefly, loudly silent. Last of all the confession, Corvane's own young handwriting writhing in the heat, and Petra held it until the fire licked her gloves, because she wanted him to see every word go.

He was screaming. She registered it distantly, Juno's weight pinning him, his voice cracking against the engine roar — *stop, stop, you don't understand what you're destroying, that's mine, that's MINE* — and Petra understood, with a clarity that felt almost like mercy, that he wasn't screaming about the evidence at all.

"No," she said, standing, turning to face him across the furnace's glow. "It was never yours. That was the whole point. You just finally heard it said out loud."

The fire settled behind her, chewing through the last flakes of ash, and the engine room seemed suddenly very large and very quiet, two people and a fallen king and the honest heat.

"Now," Petra said, and nodded to Juno. "Tell them."

Juno hauled Corvane up by his collar, turned him to face the ladder, and raised her voice into the ship's bones — every word of the confession she'd memorized in the brig, recited in a carrying parade-ground bellow that would reach the bridge, the mess, the crowded decks above: the murder, the theft, the forged succession, the bribed magistrates, the names, the dates, the sums. Every crewman within earshot of a speaking tube or an open hatch was about to hear exactly what kind of man owned their wages.

Corvane stopped struggling. He stared at Petra through the firelight, and his face did something complicated and breaking, and the last thing he said, as boots began thundering on the deck above — angry boots, mutinous boots, boots coming down — was:

"You're still my blood."

"Yeah," Petra said, turning for the ladder, Juno falling in beside her, the whole ship waking around them like a reef coming alive. "And the sea doesn't care whose blood you are. Neither do I."

Above them, the first alarm bell began to ring.
