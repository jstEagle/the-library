## 20. The Fall of Corvane

Juno Reyes had spent eleven years learning how to make a signal travel farther than it should.

It began as a discipline — naval cartography, radio theory, the mathematics of propagation across open water — and it ended, after the tribunal that stripped her commission and her name from the rolls, as a kind of exile's hobby: a bench of salvaged transmitters in the fo'c'sle of other people's boats, tuned and retuned until she could push a voice past the horizon on less power than it took to boil tea. The navy had called her work unauthorized. The harbormasters had called it dangerous. Corvane's lawyers had called it something worse, once, when she'd bounced a weather report off an old relay buoy to warn a fishing fleet away from his dredging grounds.

She had never forgotten how to do it. And now, standing in the Kestrel's cramped wheelhouse with salt drying white on her forearms and Petra Brightwater's blood still crusted under one fingernail where she'd gripped the ladder too hard coming aboard, Juno Reyes was going to do it one last time — bigger than she'd ever done it before.

The transmitter sat on the chart table like a small black animal. She'd built it herself three years ago out of a dead patrol set and a coil of copper stolen, technically, from a Corvane mooring buoy. It had a range of perhaps forty miles on a good night. Tonight would have to be very good indeed.

"You're sure about this," Petra said. She stood by the wheel, soaked to the waist still, hair plastered flat, looking less like a woman who had just burned a fortune than like one who had surfaced from a long dive and hadn't yet decided which world she belonged to. Behind them, astern, the Corvane flagship *Meridian Star* rode its anchor lights off the Escarpment, a city of brass and floodlamps floating on black water.

"I memorized every word," Juno said. "Twice through while they had me in the brig. Once more on the tender over here. I could recite it in my sleep." She flexed her fingers. They were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. "Corvane read the confession aloud to me himself, did you know that? In the brig. He thought he was being clever — thought if I heard it, I'd understand the futility of fighting him. Thirty years ago, Elias Vane's sister held half the shipping lanes of this coast in her name, and Aldous Corvane drowned her for it and signed the partnership deed with her blood still wet on his cuff. He told me all of it because he believed no one would ever believe me."

Petra said nothing. Her jaw was set in a way Juno had learned to read over six tides and one storm and one impossible dive: it meant she was holding something down deep, below the waterline, where it couldn't capsize anything.

"He was wrong about one thing," Juno went on, switching on the set. The dial glow lit her face amber. "He thinks power is what people believe quietly. But belief is just a signal, Petra. And signals can be jammed, or they can be repeated. He can't jam every frequency at once."

"The fleet will try."

"Let them." She keyed the mic. "Every captain in that armada has a hold full of cargo Corvane skimmed the insurance on. Every port authority from here to the capital has a ledger with his fingerprints somewhere in the margins. They've all known pieces of it for years. What nobody has ever had is the whole thing, spoken plainly, in one voice, at one time." She looked up. "I'm going to give it to them in one voice."

Petra crossed the wheelhouse and put her hand flat on the transmitter's casing, not quite touching Juno's shoulder — the closest thing to an embrace either of them had managed since the furnace room. "Then say it loud enough to reach my father," she said, and there was nothing soft in the way she said *father*, nothing pleading, nothing broken. Just a fact, laid down like ballast.

Juno keyed the transmit switch and began to speak.

* * *

She spoke for eleven minutes.

She gave the date, her name, her former rank, and the coordinates of the Leeward Escarpment, because credibility lived in specifics. Then she gave them Aldous Corvane's voice — reconstructed word for word from the brig, from the flagship's private cabin, from a decade of overheard boardroom conversations she'd carried out of disgrace like shrapnel carried out of a wound. The murder of Maren Vane-Brightwater in the winter passage, thirty-one years past. The forged deed of partnership, witnessed by two men later pensioned into silence. The confession Corvane had dictated to his own logbook during the lean years, drunk, certain no one would ever read it — the confession whose original pages Petra Brightwater had fed into the *Meridian Star*'s engine furnace not four hours ago, so that it could never be bought back, bargained over, or buried again.

"Proof burns," Juno said into the mouthpiece, and her voice carried the flat calm of a woman reading soundings. "But testimony doesn't. I am one witness among many, and I am telling you now: check your own ledgers. Check the pension rolls. Check who signed the salvage claim on the Escarpment wreck and under what statute — because that wreck predates every claim he's ever filed, and the navy has been confiscating charts to keep you from learning it. Ask yourselves why a man with thirty ships needs a dead prospector silenced. Ask yourselves why his own divers scuttled a dinghy and cut a boat loose rather than let two women reach a hold full of paper."

She paused. On the horizon, the *Meridian Star*'s lights burned steady, oblivious.

"Aldous Corvane," she said, "is a murderer, a forger, and a thief, and everything he owns is built on a drowned woman's name. This has been Juno Reyes, formerly cartographer, third survey flotilla. Receiving stations, please repeat."

She released the key. The wheelhouse went very quiet, filled only with the tick of cooling vacuum tubes and the slap of water against the hull.

"Now we wait," Petra said.

"No," Juno said, reaching for the frequency dial. "Now we do it again. Channel by channel. Harbor band, fleet working frequency, coastal watch, the fishing nets, the relay buoys. Eleven minutes, forty times over, if that's what it takes. Corvane's signal officers will be scrambling right now — they'll jam the main bands within the hour. So we go around them. I know frequencies those boys have never heard of, because I'm the one who mapped half of them before they took my commission." She glanced sideways, and for the first time all night she smiled, thin and sharp as a blade of glass. "You burn the paper. I burn the airwaves. Between us, there won't be a copy left he can control."

Petra took the wheel. "Then let's go around them."

* * *

The first reply came twenty-two minutes later, and it came from the last place Juno expected: the fishing fleet.

An old trawler out of Saltmere — callsign she recognized from a storm warning she'd bounced to them years ago, the skipper who owed her — broke into the coastal band with a voice like gravel in a bucket: *"Say again, unknown station. Say again all. We're copying you garbled but we're copying."*

So she said it again. All of it. And this time, when she finished, the band didn't fall silent. It erupted.

Voices overlapped — trawlermen asking for the coordinates to confirm against their grandfathers' charts, a harbor pilot demanding whether the confession named the winter passage wreck of the *Cormorant* (it did), someone reading out, slowly, incredulously, the pension numbers of the two witnesses, both of whom, a voice from Port Anselm confirmed a moment later, drew their money through a Corvane subsidiary to this day. The story was moving faster than any jammer could chase it, passed along the coast like fire along a fuse, each repeater adding its own corroboration, its own grudge, its own piece of the ledger.

"They're repeating it," Juno breathed. She sat back from the set, hands hovering, afraid to touch anything lest she break the spell. "Petra. They're *repeating it.*"

On the fleet working frequency, chaos was already blooming. She monitored it with one ear while she re-keyed the harbor band. Corvane's signal officer — a man named Hask, competent, loyal, tired — was ordering silence, ordering channels cleared, ordering the *Vigilant* and the *Iron Meridian* to shift bands. And the orders were being acknowledged in the old crisp fleet cadence, and then, one by one, the acknowledgments stopped.

At 0240 by the wheelhouse clock, the captain of the salvage barge *Deepwater Grace* — a woman named Okonkwo whom Juno had never met and would remember for the rest of her life — keyed the fleet frequency and said, in the flat tone of a report filed under protest:

"*Meridian Star*, Deepwater Grace. Requesting clarification of salvage claim authority, reference broadcast received 0215. Standing by."

A pause long enough to measure a heartbeat twice.

Then the *Iron Meridian* echoed it. Then the *Vigilant*. Then a tender, then another, then the derrick ship *Colossus*, her captain's voice cracking slightly on the acknowledgment, and Juno sat in the amber glow of her homemade radio and listened to the Corvane Salvage Fleet — thirty-one hulls, the largest private armada on the coast, the machine Aldous Corvane had spent thirty years building out of other people's wrecks — begin, quietly, formally, in perfect naval protocol, to refuse him.

"They're mutinying," Petra said softly. She hadn't left the wheel. She didn't need to; the Kestrel lay hove-to in the swell, and her hands rested on the spokes without gripping. "Not with guns. With paperwork."

"With procedure," Juno corrected. "It's worse for him. Guns he could fight." She watched the flagship through the salt-hazed glass. The *Meridian Star*'s lights had changed — boats moving between her and the lesser hulls, signal lamps flickering in urgent patterns Juno could half-read even at this distance. *Stand by. Hold position. Await orders.* Orders that were not coming, because the captains were standing by, and standing by, in the grammar of the sea, was the politest form of rebellion there was.

At 0315, the coastal watch station at Point Adair came up on the emergency band with a traffic advisory that made Juno laugh out loud — a short, disbelieving bark: two navy cutters and the patrol sloop *Resolute* underway from the anchorage, ETA to the Escarpment ninety minutes, "to investigate irregularities in commercial salvage operations."

"The navy," Petra said. "The same navy that boarded us."

"The same navy that's been confiscating charts for thirty years because somebody above them told them to," Juno said. "Somebody who isn't above them anymore. Watch — the moment the broadcast reached the wrong desk ashore, every officer who ever felt dirty about those orders got permission to feel clean. That's how institutions turn, Petra. Not all at once. All at the same time."

* * *

They came in with running lights blazing, which was itself a message; the navy never showed running lights unless it wanted to be seen.

Juno stood at the wheelhouse rail beside Petra and watched the cutters slice through the leftover swell, searchlights sweeping the anchored fleet, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched for eleven years. Not vindication exactly — vindication was a cold meal, and she'd learned not to appetite it. Something simpler. The feeling of a fix finally plotted, two lines crossing, the little x inked on the chart where the truth had been all along.

The *Resolute* put a boarding party on the *Meridian Star* at 0447. From two miles off, through Juno's battered binoculars, it looked almost gentle: a launch crossing the lit water, figures ascending the flagship's boarding ladder, a cluster of gold braid meeting them at the rail. Then the floodlamps on the *Meridian Star*'s aft deck came on all at once, and the party moved inward, toward the great cabin, and the binoculars became useless against glare.

But sound carried over water at night. It always had. Juno had built a living on knowing which frequencies traveled and which died, and grief, she had learned long ago, traveled on all of them.

They heard him before they saw him — Aldous Corvane's voice rolling across the anchorage, amplified by the flagship's own tannoy, which some panicking signalman had left hot. He was not arguing with the navy. He was not arguing with anyone. He was shouting across the water at a hove-to ketch with a patched sail and a burned-out engine room, as though the whole armada, the cutters, the law itself were merely weather between him and his daughter.

*"PETRA!"*

The name cracked across the swells like a mast going over.

*"You think burning it frees you? You think you're not mine? Every stubborn mile you sailed, every debt you dove to pay — that's MY blood in you! You found it because I AM IT! You can't sink me without sinking yourself —"*

The tannoy cut out mid-sentence. A cutter's searchlight pinned the aft deck, and through the binoculars Juno saw them take his arms — saw the great man of the fleet, the magnate whose tenders had shadowed them through the straits, whose divers had scuttled their dinghy, whose voice had offered Petra a fortune and then a name, go rigid, then slack, then walk — walked, not dragged, some last scrap of dignity clutched like a sinking man's spar — down his own boarding ladder into a navy launch.

The fleet watched in total silence. Thirty-one hulls, dark and still, riding their anchors like mourners.

And then, from the *Deepwater Grace*, a single blast of the whistle — one long note, the traditional salute rendered to a departing flag officer — held deliberately, grotesquely, until it curdled into mockery, and the whole anchorage broke: whistles, bells, pots banged on rails, the ragged cheering of sailors who had waited years for permission and were spending it all at once.

Beside her, Petra stood unmoving at the rail. Juno looked over. There were tears on her face, but her expression wasn't grief, exactly, and it wasn't relief. It was the look of a diver surfacing from a deep wreck, blowing the last of the bad air out of her lungs before she dared take a clean breath.

"He's still screaming it," Petra said quietly. "Even in the launch. 'My blood.' Like it's a chain he can reel me in with."

"It isn't," Juno said. "Blood's just a frequency, Petra. And you get to choose what you broadcast on it."

Petra turned and looked at her — really looked, the way she'd looked at the archive door before the compass cracked it — and something passed between them that needed no radio at all.

Then Petra straightened, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and nodded astern, toward the dark shape of the Escarpment rising black against the graying east.

"There's one more thing out there," she said. "One more promise to finish. And the tide turns in two hours."

Juno looked where she was looking — at the reef line, at the drop-off, at the place where a prospector's compass lay waiting in a flooded hold to be laid down at last — and reached for the ignition.

"Then let's not keep him waiting," she said, and the Kestrel came about, bow swinging toward the dawn.
