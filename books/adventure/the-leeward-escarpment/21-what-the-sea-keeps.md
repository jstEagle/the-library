## 21. What the Sea Keeps

The Kestrel came up on the third day, coughing silt and seawater, riding her lines like a drowned thing given back.

Petra stood on the salvage barge's deck and watched the cranes swing her free of the water, and she did not look away, even when the hull broke surface streaming green and the whole ugly business of it was laid bare — the gouged planking where Corvane's divers had cut her loose, the scuttle holes they'd punched below her waterline, the slow insult of a boat left to drown. Juno stood beside her with a clipboard she wasn't reading. Neither of them spoke until the Kestrel swung gently against the lifting slings, and Petra felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the night she'd swum away from the flagship in the dark.

"She'll dry out," Juno said at last. "Oak's oak. She was built stubborn."

"She was built by my grandfather," Petra said. "Stubborn is genetic."

It had taken eleven days for the world to finish falling over. Eleven days since Juno had keyed the fleet's general band from the bridge of the *Meridian Star* and read Aldous Corvane's own confession into every ear in the archipelago — the murder of Elias Brightwater's partner, the forged deed, thirty years of stolen shipping lanes dressed up as inheritance. Eleven days since the navy cutters had come around the leeward point with their guns run out, since Corvane had been marched down his own flagship's gangway in irons, screaming across the water at her: *You are still my blood. You hear me? Blood doesn't burn.*

She'd stood at the rail of the salvage barge and let him scream. She'd found, to her surprise, that it cost her almost nothing. Whatever he'd given her — half her name, half her face in the mirror some mornings, the particular way her jaw set when she was angry — he could keep the claim to it. The rest belonged to her.

The debts were another matter entirely, and here the sea had shown an unexpected mercy. With Corvane's arrest came the unraveling of everything his money had touched, and it turned out her creditors' paper traced back through three shell companies to a Corvane holding firm. The harbor magistrate voided the liens in an afternoon. Petra had walked out of his office owning the Kestrel free and clear, owing nothing to anyone, and had sat on her foredeck for an hour afterward feeling strangely weightless and strangely lost, like a diver who'd surfaced too fast and couldn't find the bottom anymore.

Now there was only one thing left to do, and it wasn't for money, and it wasn't for proof, and no one could take it or buy it.

* * *

They provisioned light. Water, coffee, rope, two sets of dive gear, and a small canvas bag that Petra kept in the wheelhouse where she could see it.

Juno noticed, of course. Juno noticed everything; it was what thirty years of charting coastlines and then having her career burned for noticing things had made of her. She didn't ask about the bag. She just altered their course by two points without being told, laying them down the long blue throat of the channel toward the Leeward Escarpment, and said, "Weather window holds four days. After that, a front comes through from the east."

"Four days is plenty," Petra said.

"For what, exactly?"

Petra looked at her. Juno Reyes was fifty-eight years old and had hands like knotted rope and eyes that had measured half the known ocean, and somewhere between the straits and the storm cove and the brig of the *Meridian Star*, she had stopped being a hired hand and become the closest thing to family Petra had left that she'd chosen herself.

"There's a grave marker needs setting," Petra said. "Down about forty fathoms."

Juno nodded slowly, once, and turned back to the wheel, and that was all the conversation the thing required.

* * *

The Escarpment looked different in fair weather. On the day they'd first sighted it, running ahead of the Corvane fleet, it had been a threat — a wall of drowned rock dropping away into blue-black nothing, the kind of feature that ate boats and kept them. Now, under a high pale sun with a long swell rolling in from the southeast, it was almost gentle. The water over the shelf went glass-clear, so clear Petra could see the coral heads ten fathoms down, scattered like coins someone had spilled and never bothered to gather.

They anchored over the drop-off in the late morning, and Petra went down alone.

Not because it was dangerous — though it was, the way every dive past the shelf edge was dangerous, the current along the wall running cold and hungry — but because this part was hers. Some things you don't share the doing of. She'd learned that from Silas Vane without ever meeting him alive: thirty years he'd spent searching, alone, keeping his promise to a dead sister, and if there was one lesson in that it was that grief is work you do yourself.

She descended along the wall with the sunlight narrowing above her, a coin of white shrinking to a hole, and the wreck rose up to meet her the way it always did — slowly, out of the gloom, the great colonial ship lying on her beam ends against the escarpment's shoulder, masts down, hull furred with a decade's growth of soft coral. The navy cable was still wrapped around the mainmast where someone, years ago, had tried to drag her up and failed, or been ordered to stop. It rusted now in orange blooms, a scar that would outlive everyone who remembered why it was there.

Petra hung in the water beside the hull and let herself feel it.

This was the place she'd nearly died. This was the place she'd found the archive room behind its brass lock, the compass's cipher ring turning in her numb fingers while the silt breathed around her like something waking. This was the place where her grandmother's letters had waited sixty years in the dark, where Vane's final letter to her had lain folded in oilcloth beside a dead man's confession. Everything that had broken her life open had happened right here, in this green silence, and now she'd come back to give something to it instead of taking.

She worked her way aft along the collapsed gunport she'd entered through, checking the line, checking her gauges, moving with the discipline that had kept her alive when terror hadn't managed to kill her. At the hold entrance she paused, and the memory of the silt-out rose in her — the world going to brown soup, her light swallowed, the long minutes of feeling her way out by touch and counting breaths. Her heart did its old stutter. Then it settled. That was the strange arithmetic of survival: the fear stayed, but it got smaller every time you came back.

Inside the hold, the archive room stood open, its door long since swung wide, the shelves empty. She'd taken everything worth taking and burned most of it in a furnace that still haunted her some nights — not the burning itself, which she'd do again in a heartbeat, but the look on Corvane's face as the deed curled and blackened. Not triumph. Something worse. Recognition. As if he'd finally understood what kind of person he'd fathered, and couldn't decide whether to be proud.

Petra shook it off. Down here, that man had no purchase. Down here there was only the wreck, and the water, and the work.

She swam into the great cabin, where the captain's quarters had been, and found the spot she'd chosen weeks ago in her head: beneath the stern gallery, where the light came down through a split in the planking in a single slanted column, visible even at this depth, a pale finger pointing at the deck. A place a sailor might choose, if a sailor got to choose. Near the light. Facing home.

She took the canvas bag from her harness and opened it.

Silas Vane's compass lay inside, wrapped in the same oilcloth he'd wrapped it in, the brass case gone dark with age, the cipher ring around its bezel still turning smooth as the day some machinist in a colonial port had cut it. The needle swung lazily, finding north, losing it, finding it again — faithful to a pole neither of them would ever see.

She'd thought hard about whether to bring it. It was the key to everything, the inheritance, the proof made metal. She could have sold it to a museum. She could have kept it, pressed it into Juno's hands, passed it down some hypothetical line of Brightwaters yet to come. Any of those would have been sensible.

But Vane had spent thirty years looking for this wreck, and he'd died on her deck within sight of the reef line, trading his last possession for a promise from a stranger who turned out to be his granddaughter. He'd never seen it. He'd carried the map of it against his chest through three decades of bad weather and worse luck, and the sea had kept him from it every single time.

So the sea could keep him now. That seemed only fair.

Petra wedged the compass into a seam between two deck planks beneath the light column, brass up, needle swinging, and packed the seam with wax she'd brought for exactly this purpose — a diver's trick, sealing fittings, borrowed for sealing memory. When she finished, the compass sat in the wreck's deck like it had grown there, like it had always been cargo, like some long-ago hand had stowed it in the one place aboard that caught the sun.

She put her glove against it briefly. The brass was cold, the way everything down here was cold, but her hand stayed a moment longer than the job required.

"Finished what you started," she said. Her voice came out as a blur of bubbles, shapeless, private. "Both halves of it."

Then she turned for the line and the light, and left him to his rest.

* * *

Juno was waiting at the anchor windlass when Petra broke surface, and she read the answer off Petra's face before Petra got a hand to the ladder.

"Done?"

"Done."

Juno nodded and didn't say anything else, which was its own kind of grace. They raised the anchor together, and the Kestrel — her Kestrel, patched and dried and re-caulked by two women who'd worked eleven days straight on a salvage barge deck — turned her bow toward open water, and the Escarpment slid astern into its long blue silence.

They ran easy, no hurry, the swell quartering comfortable behind them. Toward evening Juno cooked real food for the first time in weeks — rice and tinned fish and the lime pickles she hoarded like contraband — and they ate in the cockpit with their boots off, watching the sun go down red behind the islands they'd fled through and fought through and nearly died among.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Juno said, when the light had gone low enough that talking felt safe. "That photograph in Vane's camp. Your grandmother, wearing the compass on a chain. She looked like she knew exactly who she was."

"I've seen her face in the mirror," Petra said. "Some mornings."

"No." Juno shook her head. "You've got your own face. I've watched you use it for six weeks now. That's rarer than you think."

Petra turned her cup in her hands. Out past the breakwater of her own tiredness, she realized she felt — not happy, exactly. Lighter than happy. Like a hold pumped dry, riding high, ballast shifted and true.

"What happens tomorrow?" she asked. "For either of us?"

Juno considered the horizon. "Tomorrow I start drawing charts again. Real ones. There's a hydrographic office in Port Meridian that owes me an apology and a desk." She glanced sideways. "And there's a stretch of coast north of the Verge nobody's ever surveyed properly. Wrecks all through it, probably. Unrecorded ones."

"Unrecorded wrecks," Petra repeated.

"Ones nobody's forbidden anyone to discuss. Yet." Juno's mouth curved. "I'm told the best salvage diver in the archipelago works cheap these days. Owes no debts. Free and clear."

Petra laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising her, rising out of her like a bubble finding air after a long descent. She looked forward, north, where the dark water ran on past the edge of every chart she'd ever owned, toward coasts she'd never dived and names she'd never heard and whatever the sea had sunk out there, waiting, patient, keeping what it keeps until someone comes honest to fetch it.

"Set a course, then," she said. "I know a boat that was built stubborn."

Juno reached for the wheel, and the Kestrel leaned into the night, running clean.
