## 22. Leeward

The Kestrel came off the slipway at Port Meridian on a morning so clear the harbor looked like hammered glass, and Petra Brightwater stood on her own deck for the first time in six weeks and felt nothing like triumph.

She felt the deck under her boots — new planking amidships where the scuttling charge had blown through, scarred elsewhere, patched with timber Juno had haggled for at three different yards because no single shipwright would touch a boat with their history. She felt the wind coming off the water with that particular autumn sweetness, kelp and tar and cold iron. And she felt, beneath all of it, the strange weightlessness of owing nothing to anyone.

No debt. No contract. No map.

It should have been more than enough. Some mornings it was.

* * *

They'd raised her from the bottom of Meridian's inner basin in eleven days, which the yard owner called impossible and then billed them for anyway. The hull had taken water through the seam the Corvane divers had opened; the engine had flooded to the valve covers; the chart table had been a pulp of salt-ruined paper. But the keel was true, and the mast step hadn't cracked, and when the crane lifted her dripping into the gray morning light, Petra had watched the water pour out of her bilges and thought: *she finishes what she starts too.*

That phrase had followed her since the skerry. Vane's letter, read three times through the rain while the storm tried to peel the island down to bare rock, had given her back to herself in a way blood never could. He'd chosen her — not as a granddaughter, not as an heir, but as a diver who kept promises. Thirty years he'd searched. He'd needed someone who wouldn't stop at the first hard tide.

She'd burned the deed. She'd burned the letters. She'd stood in the furnace-light of Aldous Corvane's flagship and fed his empire's foundation into the fire page by page while he watched, and what she remembered most wasn't his face — it was the heat on her hands, and how clean it felt, and how she understood in that moment that she would carry the confession's words anyway, every one of them, because Juno carried them too, and some things once learned cannot be unlearned by burning paper.

Corvane was in custody now. The navy that had spent years refusing to discuss the Escarpment found its voice remarkably quickly once the fleet's own captains began testifying. There were hearings. There would be trials. Petra had been summoned twice and appeared once, gave four hours of testimony in a room that smelled of lamp oil and fear, and walked out without looking at the man in the dock.

He'd shouted her name across the courtroom. His name, technically. The one on the birth record she'd finally gone and read, alone, in the registry office, on an afternoon of flat gray drizzle.

*Petra Corvane.* It had looked like someone else's life.

She'd paid the fee for a copy and left it in the registry drawer. Let the paper keep it. Paper was good at keeping things; that was rather the whole trouble.

* * *

"You're brooding," Juno said, coming up the companionway with two mugs of coffee that smelled burnt in the way only Juno's coffee did. "I can hear it from below. It has a sound. Like a winch with sand in the gears."

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring at a chart you already know by heart." Juno set the mugs on the chart table, on top of the chart, which was a crime against cartography she committed daily and without remorse. "We've been underway four hours. There is nothing to navigate until the Serrat shoals, and I'll see those before you do, because unlike some people I actually look at the horizon."

Petra took the coffee. It was terrible. She drank it anyway. Six weeks of sharing a twelve-meter boat taught you which battles were worth fighting, and the coffee was not one of them.

Below them, past the Serrat Strait, the leeward coast unrolled — green going gold at the shoulders of the hills, the fishing villages smoking their winter catch, the whole shoreline soft and unhurried in a way the windward side never was. Somewhere behind that coastline lay the Escarpment, and the wreck, and the archive room they'd emptied of everything except one compass.

"Vane's camp," Juno said quietly, following her gaze. "You want to stop there."

It wasn't a question. Juno Reyes had spent thirty years reading charts and twenty years reading people, and she'd gotten frighteningly good at both before the navy took the second skill away from her.

"I want to bury him properly," Petra said. "There's a shelf of rock above the cove. Dry ground. You can see the water from it." She turned the mug in her hands. "He spent thirty years looking at that water from below. Seems right he should spend forever looking down at it from above."

Juno said nothing for a while. The Kestrel ran easy, her repaired hull humming that low note Petra had come to think of as the boat's voice.

"We'll need a shovel," Juno said at last. "And something to say. I'm bad at things to say."

"You're not."

"I'm excellent at coordinates and catastrophic at eulogies."

"Then give him coordinates," Petra said. "Give him the bearing from his camp to the wreck. He'd have liked that better than scripture anyway."

Juno smiled — the small, crooked smile that was still, six weeks in, the only kind she gave — and went forward to check the anchor rode, which did not need checking, which was how Juno prayed.

* * *

They buried Silas Vane two days later, on the shelf above the cove, in ground that took the shovel hard because it had never been broken.

Petra had expected grief to arrive like weather — a front you could see coming, brace against, sail through. Instead it arrived like water in a flooded hold: everywhere at once, patient, finding every seam she hadn't caulked. She knelt in the turned earth with her palms flat against it and thought about a night tow past the reef line, about a dying man's blood on her deck plates, about wax-sealed paper pressed into her hand with fingers that were already going cold.

*Carry it to the Escarpment. Tell no one.*

*Finish what I started.*

She had. God help her, she had — though what he'd started, in the end, had not been a treasure hunt at all. It had been a thirty-year act of love for a sister the world had erased, and Petra had simply been the last pair of hands it passed through.

Juno stood at the head of the grave with her cap in her hands and gave him, exactly as promised, coordinates. Bearing two-seven-four from the camp, distance one point three nautical miles, depth forty fathoms, position of the *Meridian Star*, may she rest, may her cargo rest, may the truth rest easier now than it ever did in life. Her voice didn't break until the last line, and then only slightly, and she pretended it was the wind.

Petra laid the compass on the wreck instead of in the grave — that had been decided weeks ago, on the last dive, when she'd wedged it into the binnacle mount of the old ship's wheel where the brass had worn smooth, where it sat even now behind forty fathoms of clear cold water, needle swinging slow and true toward nothing anyone needed anymore. A grave marker for a ship. A promise kept for a man. An inheritance, refused on the surface and honored in the deep.

They filled the grave. They stacked stones over it, the way prospectors mark a claim — except this claim was only a name, cut into the largest stone with a cold chisel from the Kestrel's toolkit:

**SILAS VANE**
*He finished.*

Petra read it twice. Then she picked up the shovel and walked back down to the boat without looking back, because some things you have to trust to hold their shape behind you.

* * *

They sailed leeward after that, because it seemed right, and because the leeward coast was riddled with wrecks nobody had names for.

That was the other thing six weeks had taught her: the sea keeps more than it gives back, but it gives back eventually, and usually to whoever is patient enough and poor enough and stubborn enough to keep diving. There was a collier on the Serrat banks, sunk in the last war, that the fishermen swore was full of brass fittings. There was a mail packet off Cape Ardent with a rumored strongroom. There were a hundred hulls lying in the green silence with their stories still sealed inside them, waiting for someone with a rebreather, a compass, and no particular interest in getting rich.

Word traveled, as word does. By the time they'd cleared the strait, three harbormasters had asked if she was the one from the Escarpment. Two offered contracts. One offered money outright just to hear the story, and Juno had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down on a bollard.

Petra told none of them anything. Not because the story was secret — half of it was public record now, splashed across every broadsheet from Meridian to the capital — but because the parts that mattered had never been the parts that could be told. How do you explain to a harbormaster what it feels like to burn your own inheritance? How do you describe the exact weight of a father's name, or the sound a promise makes when it's finally, fully kept?

You don't. You let the sea keep it. That was rather the whole point.

* * *

On the fourth day out, running down the coast with a fair wind and the first real warmth of the season on the water, they overhauled a small trading sloop becalmed in the lee of the Headlands, her crew of two waving wearily. Petra put the Kestrel alongside and threw them a towline without being asked, because that was what boats did for each other out here, and because someone had once towed a stranger past the reef line and it had turned out to be the most important thing she ever did.

The sloop's skipper was a young woman, sunburned, capable-looking, with rope-scarred hands and the hungry eyes of someone new to the water. She came aboard while the engines took up the strain, accepted Juno's terrible coffee with heroic politeness, and looked around the Kestrel's cabin with open curiosity — at the chart table, at the dive gear racked by the companionway, at the woman steering.

"You're Brightwater," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "The salvage diver. From the papers. The Corvane thing."

"I'm a diver," Petra said.

The young woman blinked. "But — the Escarpment. The fleet. They're saying you found proof that—"

"I'm a diver," Petra said again, and held out her hand. "Petra. This is Juno. We're headed for the Serrat banks — there's a collier down there nobody's properly surveyed. If you're interested in work, we could use a third pair of hands."

The young woman stared at the offered hand as though it might be a trick. Then she took it, and her grip was strong and salt-cracked and honest, and something in Petra's chest — something that had been clenched since a dying man bled out on these very decks — finally, quietly, let go.

"Maren," the girl said. "Maren Oduya. I don't know anything about salvage."

"Neither did I, once," Petra said. "The sea teaches fast."

* * *

They dropped the sloop at the Headlands anchorage at dusk, Maren Oduya promising — with the fierce solemnity of the young — to find them at Serrat within the month. Juno went below to cook something that would be, by any objective standard, an atrocity. Petra stayed at the wheel.

The coast slid by in the last light: leeward shore, sheltered water, the long green shadows of the hills falling out across the sea. Behind her, somewhere in the darkening east, the Escarpment slept with its secrets settled and its dead at peace. Ahead, the Serrat banks waited with a collier full of brass and a story nobody had heard yet.

No map. No debt. No name she hadn't chosen herself.

Juno's head appeared at the companionway. "Food in ten. Also, I re-rigged the stern light and I want credit for it."

"Noted for the log."

"And Petra?" Juno hesitated, half in, half out of the lamplight, and for a moment her face was younger and less guarded than Petra had ever seen it. "The collier. Whatever's down there. We're diving it as us, right? Not as anyone's daughters. Not as anyone's revenge. Just—" she gestured vaguely at the boat, the water, the whole improbable fact of them. "As us."

Petra looked at her helmsman's hands on the wheel — her hands, scarred and steady, holding a course she'd chosen with her eyes open — and found the answer had been sitting in her chest for weeks, plain as a bearing on a chart.

"Just us," she said. "Divers."

Juno grinned and disappeared below, and the Kestrel ran on into the leeward dark, her wake unspooling behind her like a thread being let go — and ahead, far out on the black water, a light blinked twice, three times, in a rhythm no chart had ever recorded, and Petra eased the wheel over to bring them closer, because that was what divers did.

They went toward the unknown. They always had.
