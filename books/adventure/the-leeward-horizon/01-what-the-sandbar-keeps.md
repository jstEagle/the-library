## 1. What the Sandbar Keeps

The river was low enough to show its teeth.

Yara Sandoval stood at the stern of the *Merry Adelie*, one boot on the rail, watching the brown water peel away from the sandbars like skin drawn back from bone. Three weeks without rain. The channels she'd run blind since she was fourteen had shrunk to threads, and every bar between here and the delta had grown a new spine of silt to catch careless keels. She could read the water the way other folk read scripture — the smooth tongue of current over a safe channel, the wrinkled boil where the bottom rose to meet you — and what the river was telling her this morning was simple: *be careful, or be aground.*

Freight made her careful anyway. Forty barrels of salt fish bound for Corvane's own processing docks at Grayharbor, which was its own particular irony, though she wouldn't learn that until later. Behind the *Adelie*, lashed bow-to-stern, wallowed her tow — a flat-bottomed barge riding so heavy it sat below its own watermark, groaning at every swell like an old man climbing stairs.

"Bar's moved," said Odji from the bow. He said it the way he said everything, flat as a dropped oar, and Yara had learned to trust him more than her own charts. Odji was her deckhand, sixty if he was a day, with a face like weathered teak and a superstition for every occasion. He'd thrown salt over the rail at every crossing since she'd known him. He never whistled aboard. He believed, with a quiet and unshakable conviction, that the river kept accounts.

"Which way?" Yara called.

"Left of where she was. Maybe forty yards." He squinted at the water, then spat over the side, an offering. "She's hungry this year, Captain. Low water makes her remember things."

"Low water makes *us* rich," Yara said. "Everything that sank in the wet season, we get to see now."

She brought the wheel over gently, easing the *Adelie* left of Odji's mark, the tow swinging wide behind her like a dog on a long leash. The engine room hatch was open below her, and up through it came the smell of coal oil and the steady, tuneless humming of Brannock Hale, her engineer, a big soft-spoken man who loved the *Adelie*'s engine the way other men loved their children and who could diagnose a misfire by its tone from three decks away.

The snag came an hour past noon, at the bend the old pilots called the Widow's Elbow, where the current doubled back on itself and the river had been quietly digging a hole for longer than anyone alive had been watching.

Yara felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the hull, deep and wrong, not the scrape of sand but the heavy, grudging drag of something that did not intend to move. The towline, trailing its barge behind, dipped taut and then *sang*, a low moaning note like a struck cable, and the *Adelie*'s stern slewed hard toward the bar.

"Odji!"

"See it!" He was already forward, gaffing at the line. "Captain, we're fouled — something's got the towline under the bar —"

"Brannock! Half astern, easy!"

The engine note dropped, reversed, and the *Adelie* trembled in the current, holding station while the towline groaned. Yara came down from the wheelhouse and knelt at the stern rail, looking over. The water at the Elbow was the color of strong tea, but the low season had thinned it, and she could see — just see — down where the line vanished: a pale shape in the murk, broad and angular, not rock. Rock did not have corners like that. Rock did not have *edges*.

"Odji, get the boat hook. Brannock, hold her steady."

It took them the better part of two hours, the sun sliding west and the tide — what little tide the low river allowed — falling all the while. They worked the line free with the gaff and a grapnel, and when the thing finally let go of the bottom, it came up slow and enormous, shedding mud like a sleeping animal rising, and swung against the *Adelie*'s hull with a boom that Yara felt in her back teeth.

A chest. A chest the size of a coffin, banded in iron gone to lace with rust, and cased in lead — actual lead, sheathed over wood, the metal dull and gray and scarred with a century of the river's slow teeth. Mud filled its seams. Barnacles had claimed one corner. And stamped into the lead on the lid, deep enough to have survived everything the river had done, was a seal.

Yara knew the seal. Everyone on the river knew the seal, the way everyone knew a dead man's face from the portrait in the courthouse. A leaping fish above a broken crown, ringed in nine stars.

The seal of Vessia.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. The river ran. Somewhere upstream a heron complained.

"Captain." Odji's voice had gone very quiet, and very flat, which was how Yara knew he was more frightened than he would ever admit. "That's drowned people's property. That's *dead nation* property. Vessia went under the water and under the ground both, and what the river keeps, the river keeps for a reason." He crossed himself with a wet hand. "We put it back. We put it back and we throw salt after it and we don't speak of it again."

"It's in my towline, Odji. The river gave it to us."

"The river *took* a barge off old Manette in '61 and gave back nothing. The river is not giving, Captain. The river is *handing you something to carry.*"

Brannock Hale climbed up from the engine room, wiped his hands on a rag, and considered the chest with the same unhurried attention he gave a seized valve. He knocked on the lead with one knuckle, listened, and knocked again.

"Lead-lined," he said. "That's money, Captain. Or papers somebody wanted to keep dry forever. Either way, lead like that, sealed like that — that's worth more than the *Adelie* and the tow together." He looked up at her, mild and certain. "We sell it. Grayharbor's got buyers. Corvane's agents pay cash for river salvage, no questions, and we could be out of the freight business by the dry season."

"Corvane's agents," Yara said, and left it there. Everyone on the river knew how Corvane's salvage men paid: cash first, and then the writs came, and then the men with writs came, and somehow by the end of it the salvager owed *Corvane* money. She'd watched it happen to the Voss brothers two seasons back. They ran freight for him now, on his boats, for his wages.

"Then not Corvane," Brannock said, unbothered. "Somebody else. It sells, Captain. Things like this always sell."

Odji stood at the rail with his back half-turned, as if the chest might notice him looking. "Things like this *sink*," he said. "That's how they got like this."

Yara knelt by the chest and put her palm flat on the lead. It was river-cold, colder than the water around it, as if it had kept the temperature of whatever deep place it had been keeping. The seal stared up at her. The leaping fish. The broken crown.

Vessia had gone under when she was a child — not all at once, the way a ship goes under, but the way a nation does: war, then famine, then the border treaties, then the purges, until one day there were no Vessians left to be Vessia, and the maps were quietly redrawn, and the seal became a story river mothers told to make children behave. *The river took them all. Don't go out past the Elbow after dark.*

She should put it back. She knew that the way she knew the channel. Some things the river gives back are not gifts; they are errands, and the river does not care whether you wanted the errand.

"Brannock, get the crowbar."

"Captain—" Odji started.

"She's my boat, Odji. And it fouled my line. That makes it mine to open and mine to answer for."

Brannock brought the crowbar, and the iron band gave with a shriek of rust, and the lid came up on a smell Yara had never smelled before and would never forget after: cold metal, old cedar, and something faintly sweet, like flowers pressed in a book for a hundred years.

Inside, packed in rotted wool: coins. Not many — a few hundred, gold, each stamped with the fish and the broken crown. Beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as leaf, a bundle of papers, and beneath the papers, a false bottom that Brannock found by the hollow sound of it, and pried up with the point of his knife.

The hidden compartment held one thing. A single document, heavy paper, sealed with wax that had gone the color of dried blood, and written in a fine, careful hand. Yara read it twice, there on the deck in the slanting afternoon light, with Odji refusing to look and Brannock reading over her shoulder in silence.

It was a naming. A line of succession, witnessed and sealed, in the old Vessian form. And at the bottom, the name of the last heir of Vessia — a name, a birth date, and nothing else. No death date. No record of drowning, no record of purge, no record of anything at all.

Just a name, and the words *heir and keeper of the treasury*, and a birth date that Yara did the arithmetic on twice because she could not believe it the first time.

The heir of Vessia, by that date, would be — if the child lived — eleven years old.

"Throw it back," Odji said softly, behind her. "Captain. *Throw it back.*"

Brannock said nothing, but he was looking at the gold with the careful, hungry stillness of a man doing sums.

Yara stood, the paper in her hand, and looked down the long brown reach of the river toward Grayharbor, where the masts of Corvane's salvage fleet stood against the evening like a forest of black spears. The river had handed her something to carry. She could feel the weight of it already, and she had not even lifted the chest.

* * *

They made two miles before dark and tied up at a mudbank cut called Halfway Rest, a shelf of willows and driftwood where half the river's pilots had slept off a bad channel at one time or another. Yara set the watch, fed the crew, and locked the chest in her cabin — not because she feared her crew, but because the chest felt like a thing that should not be left where a man could dream next to it.

She was up past midnight with the lamp turned low, reading the papers again. The naming. The treasury manifest — pages of it, in that same fine hand, listing not just the coins but plate, deeds, bonds, the *entirety* of what a drowned nation had managed to carry out of its own drowning. And letters. A dozen of them, bundled and tied with ribbon gone to thread, each addressed in the same hand to the same name — the name at the bottom of the naming — and each one beginning, in the old courtly style, *To the Keeper, wherever the water has hidden you.*

She read the first three. They were letters from a dying court to a hidden child. Instructions. Warnings. Love, of the formal, desperate kind that people write when they know they will not live to say it aloud. The last one ended: *If you read this, then you lived, and everything we were is not lost, only sleeping. Come home by water. We will keep it for you under the sand.*

Under the sand.

Yara set the letters down. Her cabin was very quiet. The lamp hissed. Out on the water, a night bird called once and got no answer.

And it was in that silence — the particular silence of a boat at rest, where every sound has a name and every absence of sound is a presence — that Yara heard the sound that had no name.

A small sound. From aft. From the direction of the coal bunk.

A cough. Small, and quickly smothered, the way a child smothers a cough when they have learned that being heard is the most dangerous thing in the world.

Yara sat very still, the naming-paper in one hand and the letters in her lap, and looked at the bulkhead as if she could see through it, down into the dark of the coal bunk where her cargo of fuel sat stacked, and where, for three days now, some small weight had been shifting in the night that she had told herself was the settle of coal.

The river, she thought, does not give gifts. The river hands you things to carry.

She rose, took the lantern down from its hook, and went to see what else the sandbar had given her.
