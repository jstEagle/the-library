## 2. The Stowaway's Tide

The chest sat in Yara's cabin all night like a guest who wouldn't leave.

She'd put it under her bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, and still she could feel it through the deck planks — a weight that had nothing to do with lead. Twice she got up to look at it. The second time, she lit the lamp and turned it over in the swaying light, running her thumb along the seal pressed into the wax: a leaping fish above three waves, worn but unmistakable. Vessia. Every child on the river knew the story — the kingdom that drowned itself rather than kneel, its palaces swallowed by the delta mud two generations back, its people scattered or dead. *Extinct*, the almanacs said, the way they said it of passenger pigeons and old languages.

Extinct things didn't stamp their names on lead.

The papers she'd found in the hidden compartment lay fanned across her chart table. Bills of lading in a flowing hand. A manifest of what the chest once held — coin, plate, deeds — most of it long since removed. And at the bottom, folded twice, a document that made her sit down slowly: a writ of succession, naming an heir. *To the last blood of Vessia, wheresoever the river keeps them.* Below the formal language, a name.

Yara read it four times. It was a girl's name. A child's name.

She was still staring at it when the knock came — Bram, her engineer, cap in his hands, eyes red-rimmed from a night in the engine room.

"Captain. There's smoke upstream. Three funnels' worth."

* * *

Corvane's fleet came down the river at midmorning, iron hulls shouldering the brown water aside, and the whole levee town of Marrow's Bend turned out to watch. Three hunters flying the salvage magnate's colors — a black trident on gray — and behind them a barge with a crane arm folded like a sleeping mantis. They anchored in the channel with the insolence of men who owned everything they could see, and by noon a launch was putting off toward the *Leeward Rose* with six armed men and a lawyer in a coat too fine for the river.

Yara met them at the gangway with her arms crossed and her face arranged into the flat courtesy that had kept her alive through twenty years of freight runs and worse.

"Captain Sandoval." The lawyer's smile showed a lot of teeth and no warmth. "Aldous Vetch, factor to Mr. Corvane. We're told you pulled something from the sandbar below the bend."

"Told by whom?"

"The river tells Mr. Corvane many things." Vetch produced a scroll sealed in black wax. "Imperial writ of salvage. All wrecks of Vessian origin revert to the Crown's grantee — that would be us. Anything you've recovered is to be surrendered. Now, and without fuss, and there'll be a finder's fee in it for you. Ten crowns." He said *ten crowns* the way a man tosses a bone to a dog.

Yara didn't look at the chest. She'd stowed it in the bilge space beneath the coal bunk before dawn, and she made herself keep her eyes on Vetch's face, easy and bored. "I snagged my towline on some old crates. Rotted through. Kindling and river muck."

"Mm." Vetch's gaze drifted past her shoulder, cataloguing the deck — old Tam at the rail, young Piet coiling line, the engine-room hatch standing open. "Mr. Corvane will be in these waters until the dry season. Six tides, I'm told, before the channels go shallow. If you remember anything worth remembering, Captain, you'll bring it to us. If we find you've kept something back—" He let the sentence trail off into the sound of the water, which was threat enough. One of the armed men smiled at Piet, slow and unpleasant, and touched his cudgel the way another man might touch a woman.

They went back down the gangway. Yara stood at the rail until the launch reached its ship, then let out a breath she felt in her ribs.

"Ten crowns," said Bram beside her. "For a chest that'd buy this whole town."

"For a chest that'll get us all hanged," Yara said. "Salvage law's theirs on paper. Only thing ours is what nobody can prove."

"That's why you gave it up so smooth."

"I didn't give anything up. I lied." She looked at him. "Which means every hour that chest sits aboard my boat, we're thieves in their eyes and fools in fact. First tide tomorrow, I mean to run it downriver to the assize court at Tallowmouth and wash my hands of it."

Bram studied her a long moment. "That's the safe course," he said, not quite asking.

"It's the only course."

But that night, going down to check the bilge where she'd hidden the chest, Yara heard breathing.

* * *

It came from the coal bunk — a small, quick sound, the breathing of someone trying very hard not to breathe at all. Yara stood in the dark hold with her lamp unlit and listened, and the pieces she'd been stepping around for three days began, horribly, to click together.

Three days ago, at the fueling dock above the bend, she'd caught a shape darting between stacked cordwood and had half-convinced herself she'd imagined it. Two nights ago, hardtack had gone missing from the galley — a handful, no more — and Tam had blamed rats. This morning she'd found a scrap of cloth snagged on the bunk ladder: coarse gray wool, hemmed with a fine stitch no beggar would own. And the coal, she realized now, had been settling oddly, as though something small shifted it in the night.

Yara struck the lamp.

In the corner of the coal bunk, wedged into the black like a bit of shadow that had learned to be solid, crouched a girl. Maybe eleven years old, maybe twelve — starved down to knuckle and elbow, hair cut raggedly as if with a knife, eyes enormous and unblinking in the lamplight. Not frightened, Yara noted with a strange lurch. *Watchful.* The girl held a coal shard in one small fist like a knife, and her whole body said: *I have decided how this ends, and you will not enjoy your part in it.*

"Well," Yara said quietly. "You've been eating my hardtack."

The girl said nothing. Her eyes flicked — once, fast — toward the chest.

And Yara felt the world tilt. She unfolded the paper she'd carried in her shirt pocket since morning, the writ of succession, and held it where the lamplight fell on the name written at the bottom. Then she looked at the girl. At the fine-stitched wool. At the way the child held her chin when afraid, which was exactly the way a queen might.

"No," Yara said, mostly to herself. "No, no, no. Of all the coal bunkers on all the rivers—"

"They burned the house." The girl's voice was rusty, a hinge unused. "The one with the seal on the gate. I ran. I ran a long time." Her fist tightened on the coal shard. "Are you giving me to them?"

Every sane answer was yes. Hand her over, take the fee, take ten more, be done with it. Corvane wanted an heir extinct; a living heir was a loose thread that could unravel his entire imperial writ, and he burned loose threads — the girl had watched him burn one already, if her hollow face meant anything. Give her up and the *Rose* sails clean, the crew stays paid, the dry season finds them fat and safe.

Yara looked at the child a long moment. Then she sat down on the coal, skirts and all, which she'd regret later, and held out the last piece of hardtack from her own pocket.

"Eat," she said. "Then you're going to tell me everything, starting with your name."

* * *

The letters were tucked in the false bottom of the chest, wrapped in waxed silk, and the girl — whose name, it emerged, was Iselle — read them aloud by lamplight while the crew pretended not to hover outside the cabin door. They were her mother's, written in the last year of the drowned court, and Iselle read them the way children recite prayers: without understanding, without needing to.

*I write this knowing no one may ever read it. The water comes up the stair tonight. Tomorrow we go to the high tower. My daughter sleeps against me and does not know she is the last of anything. Let whoever finds this know: we were not conquered. We chose. There is a difference, and it is the whole of our law.*

*If my Iselle lives — if the river is kinder than the empire — tell her this: a crown is not gold. Gold is only what a crown weighs. Ours was always meant to be set down.*

Iselle stopped reading. In the lamplight her face was very still, the stillness of deep water.

"She wrote that before I was born," the girl said. "Before the drowning even finished." She looked up at Yara, and there was nothing feral in her now — only a sharpness like a blade being drawn. "They think I'm treasure. Corvane thinks I'm treasure. You thought I was treasure."

"You're a stowaway who owes me for hardtack," Yara said. "That's all you are aboard my boat."

But even as she said it, she heard the fleet's anchor chains groaning in the dark channel, and she understood that the lie would only hold until dawn. Vetch knew. Somewhere in that gray iron belly of a flagship, a man with too many teeth had already done the arithmetic: a freight boat at the bend, a sandbar dredged, a chest gone quiet in the night. Six tides to dry season, and Corvane did not need proof to burn a boat — only suspicion, and a windless night.

Yara stood at her cabin window a long time, watching the hunters' riding lights swing at anchor like low, patient stars. Behind her, the child slept in her bunk, one hand curled around a letter, breathing the deep breath of someone who had finally stopped running — and Yara found she would burn before she let that breathing stop.

"Bram," she called softly through the door. "Wake the others. Tell them to vote."

"On what, Captain?"

"On whether we run the blockade at first tide." She turned back to the window, to the black water and the waiting fleet. "Tell them the truth. All of it. And tell them the tide won't wait."
