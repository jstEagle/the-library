## 3. Six Tides to Dry Season

The fog came off the delta at dusk, thick as wool, and Yara Sandoval steered the *Maren Lee* into it like a woman walking into a lie she'd told herself all her life: that no one was following.

They were following. She could hear them — the iron hunters' engines carried through the mist as a low, patient thrumming, the sound of something that did not need to hurry because it knew the river would eventually give them up. Corvane's boats drew too much water for the back-channels, but they didn't need channels. They needed only patience and coal, and Corvane had bought both by the mountain.

"Kestrel Bank," Brannock said at her shoulder, his voice pitched low. The old engineer had a way of naming sandbars the way other men named saints. "If we can cross her at half-flood we'll shave two hours off the fleet's line."

"And if we can't?"

"Then we're a rumor in the mud until morning, and morning is when their searchlights wake up."

Yara looked back once. The girl sat in the shadow of the wheelhouse with the chest between her knees, arms wrapped around it the way a drowning sailor hugs a spar. She hadn't spoken since the fog closed in. Her eyes were open in the dark, and Yara had learned enough of those eyes in three days to know they never truly shut.

"Half-flood," Yara said. "Take us over Kestrel."

The *Maren Lee* groaned across the bar with six inches under her keel, mud boiling up astern in a brown rope, and somewhere behind them the iron engines slowed, hesitated, turned aside. For one hour the river belonged to them alone.

* * *

They ran the second night on candle-oil and nerve. Yara worked the boat through the Narrows of Sallow Reed with the fog pressed so close against the windows it might have been the inside of her own skull, calling depths from memory — *seven feet, mark; five and a half, ease her left; pole depth, hold your breath* — while Tam Osha stood at the bow with a sounding line, his arm rising and falling like a metronome that kept them alive.

It was Tam who heard the boarding hooks first. A soft iron whisper against the rail, aft, where the fog was thickest.

"Off the wheel!" he shouted, and then the world came aboard.

There were six of them in oilskin masks, and they came over the stern quiet as herons. What followed lasted less than four minutes and took more than Yara will ever stop counting. Pell Wicker went down swinging a boathook at the first man over the rail, laughing the whole way down, because Pell laughed at everything including the thing that killed him. And Junie — quiet Junie, who mended nets and never said an unkind word about anyone — put herself between the masked men and the chest, between the chest and the girl, and did not move even when they made her regret it.

The crew drove them back over the rail. Two hunters slipped away into the fog trailing blood. But Junie's breathing had gone shallow and wrong, and Pell did not breathe at all.

They buried Pell at dawn in the reed shallows, weighted with chain, because the river had been his road home from every voyage and Yara would not deny him that last passage. Junie lay in the engine room with Brannock's coat over her, her hand gripping Tam's, fading by inches the fog outside could not touch.

"We should turn back," Tam said at the graveside. His voice cracked on it. "We should take her to a surgeon in Marsh Hollow and let Corvane have his treasure. It isn't worth—"

"It isn't about worth." Yara heard her own voice come out flat and hard and older than she felt. "Corvane burned a nation down to its last child and now he wants the ashes signed over to him by writ. If we turn back, every mile we've run means nothing, and Pell's river gives him up for nothing, and Junie—" She stopped. Started again. "Junie asked me this morning if we'd made the leeward shore yet. She thinks we're going to make it."

Nobody spoke. Fog beaded on every face like the river itself was listening.

"So we finish the run," Yara said. "For her. All the way."

Tam looked out at the gray water for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and went below to tell Junie the good news, and lied to her beautifully.

* * *

On the fourth tide Corvane stopped being patient.

They saw the firebrands arc out of the fog at Grayling Reach — burning pitch wrapped in tarred sacking, three of them in a high whistling spread — and Yara put the wheel hard over and took two and the third caught the aft deck and everything there went up like a festival. Brannock fought it with buckets and steam-jet and bare fury, and beat it down, but the *Maren Lee*'s spine had been kissed by fire and everyone aboard felt her shudder differently after that, the way a person walks differently after a fever.

"He knows the channels now," Brannock said grimly. "He's hired local pilots. We can't outrun iron forever, Captain."

"Then we don't outrun it." Yara spread the chart on the wheelhouse floor and weighted the corners with brass fittings, and the crew gathered around it like men around a fire. "Here. The Siltgate. Nobody crosses it at any tide but the last of the ebb, and nobody crosses it twice. On the far side is the Leeward Shore, and on the leeward shore is the wreck, and at dead-low tomorrow the wreck opens like a fist. One tide, gentlemen. One tide and we're done running."

"The Siltgate will strand us if we misread it," Tam said. "Strand us right in front of him."

"Then we won't misread it."

They crossed the Siltgate on the fifth ebb with the hull shrieking over packed silt, the *Maren Lee* surfing her own grave, and behind them the lead iron-hull grounded hard with a boom that rolled across the water like a struck bell. Cheering broke out on deck — real cheering, ragged and wild — and Yara let herself grin for exactly four seconds before the boat shuddered, slewed, and settled into the mud of the far flat with a finality that silenced every voice aboard.

She climbed down over the bow onto the glistening brown plain. The fog was thinning. Ahead, perhaps two miles across the flats, the sea itself waited beyond a low ridge of dune, and beyond the sea, faint as breath, the leeward hills.

Two miles. On foot. Carrying a chest that took two men to lift, across mud that would suck a boot off at every step, with one tide left in the world.

Brannock joined her at the bow, looking at the same distance. "Boat's done," he said quietly. "She'll float on the next flood and never sail again. Twenty-two years I kept her boilers breathing."

"I know."

"She was your father's."

"She was ours." Yara turned and faced them — Tam, hollow-eyed; the stokers Ash and Merrin with soot to the elbow; old Brannock; and the girl, standing at the rail with the fire-scarred deck beneath her feet and the chest's key already around her neck on a cord. "So here's the vote, since this stopped being my boat the moment you all chose to bleed for it. We can wait here for Corvane's longboats and beg his pardon. Or we carry what's ours two miles across that mud and open the wreck before the water comes back. I know how I'm voting."

"You haven't asked what we get," Tam said. There was a ghost of Pell's laugh in it.

"Land," said the girl.

Every head turned. She had not raised her voice; she hardly ever did. But it carried across the silent deck like the bell from the grounded hunter.

"When the chest is opened," she said, "there is land on the leeward shore. Enough for a house each. Enough for a dock, and fields, and a place where the fleet cannot follow. That is what is in the chest, besides gold. That is what Vessia kept." Her chin lifted, and for a moment Yara saw not a starving stowaway but something older standing in her, some drowned nation risen for one breath. "I am asking you to carry me to my inheritance. And I am telling you it is yours too. All of it. Every one of you."

The tide clock in Yara's head was ticking, but she let the silence stand anyway, because some silences deserve to stand.

Ash spat over the rail. "I've crewed for pay for eleven years," he said. "First time anybody's offered me ground to stand on that couldn't be taken back." He picked up one end of the carrying frame. "Vote's yes."

One by one, hands took hold of wood and rope. Last of all, Tam laid his palm flat on the rail above the engine room, where Junie slept too deep to vote, and said, "Yes. For both of us."

Yara took the front of the frame. The chest settled onto shoulders that had been strangers a week ago and were not strangers now and would never be strangers again.

"One tide," she said. "Let's spend it well."

They stepped off the bow onto the mud, six figures and a kingdom between them, and behind them, far out in the clearing fog, they could hear the iron fleet freeing its grounded lead ship — and coming on.
