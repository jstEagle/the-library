## 4. The Leeward Horizon

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Yara stood at the rail of what remained of her boat—the *Kestrel*, scorched to the waterline, listing in the shallows like a tired animal—and watched the river peel itself back from the bones of the world. Mile by mile the water withdrew, exposing mudflats the color of old pewter, ribbed and gleaming, and there, far out where no sane captain ever went, the dark shoulder of the wreck emerged from its grave of silt.

Six tides. They had spent five of them running, bleeding, burning. This was the last one, and the river had kept its promise: dry season would strand them here for a month, maybe more, marooned on a plain of mud that would bake hard as brick under a merciless sun. Corvane's iron hunters sat anchored at the channel mouth, patient as vultures. There was no going back. There was only forward, across the exposed flats, before the water returned.

"Deck's down to three inches of freeboard," Brannic said, limping up beside her. His arm was bound where the boarding hooks had found him; Tam's name was still raw in all their mouths. "She won't make the channel even if we could run it."

"We're not running the channel." Yara nodded at the wreck. "We're walking."

He looked at her, then at the mud, then at the girl standing at the bow with the wind pulling at her too-thin frame, and he grunted—the sound that, in Brannic's language, meant *aye*.

They went over the side with lines around their waists and planks for the worst of the mire. The mud took their boots to the ankle, then the shin, sucking and sighing with every step. The girl moved like she'd been born to it—light, quick, reading the flats the way Yara read channels, finding the firm ribbons between the soft places. Of course she did. Yara had stopped being surprised by anything about the girl two tides ago.

Her name, she had finally learned, was Ilse. Though Ilse said that wasn't really her name either—names had been taken from her the way everything else had been taken, one piece at a time, until all she carried was a seal-stamped secret in her chest and the papers in a drowned chest to prove who she was.

The hull of the wreck rose over them now, green with a century of river, its timbers furred with weed and barnacle. A carved crest still clung above the stern windows: a leaping fish beneath a seven-pointed star. Vessia. The nation that wasn't.

Yara set her shoulder against the hull and found the seam Brannic's survey had marked. "On three," she said. "Ilse—stay back."

"No," the girl said, and put her small hands beside Yara's on the wet wood.

Yara looked at her. The girl looked back, and there was nothing feral in her eyes now, nothing starved. There was only a kind of terrible patience, the patience of someone who had waited her whole life for one particular door to open.

"On three," Yara said again, and didn't argue.

The timbers gave with a groan that rolled across the flats like thunder. River water—what little remained trapped inside—gushed out over their boots, and then they were looking down into the hold of the drowned nation.

* * *

The gold was there, exactly as the papers promised. Bars and coins and plate, stacked in the dark like a hoard from a story, dull with silt but unmistakable. Enough to buy a fleet. Enough to buy a war. Enough, Corvane would say, to buy back a crown.

But Ilse went past all of it, wading through the shallowed hold to a strongbox at the stern, small and plain, unmarked by any seal. She knelt over it with the reverence of the devout, lifted the lid, and drew out a document wrapped in oilcloth and wax.

Yara watched her read it. Watched the girl's lips move. Watched her shoulders begin, very slowly, to shake—not with grief, Yara realized. With something closer to laughter, or its twin.

"What is it?"

Ilse held it out. Her hands were steady. "It's the charter. The Abdication of Vessia." She traced the signatures at the bottom, row upon row of them, hundreds, the whole parliament and every guild and every ship's captain of a nation signing itself out of existence. "I was taught the Vessians were conquered. That the empire burned them and scattered us and that someday, someone would rise and take it back." She looked up, and the light from the hatch fell across her face. "They weren't conquered. They *chose*. They dissolved themselves rather than let Corvane's empire claim a crown. There's nothing to reclaim. There never was. The throne ended on purpose." She laughed then, a small, disbelieving sound. "I'm the heir to a country that voted to stop existing. I'm the heir to *nothing*."

Yara thought of the chest on her deck, the superstitious dread in her deckhand's voice, the weight of a secret the river had given her. *The river doesn't make mistakes,* her grandmother used to say. *It only makes deliveries.*

"Then Corvane has no claim," Yara said slowly, understanding arriving like tide. "His writ rests on salvage of a sovereign nation's treasury. If Vessia abdicated—"

"If Vessia abdicated, the treasury isn't a nation's wealth. It's an inheritance with no state attached. Mine, by blood, to do with as I please." Ilse folded the charter against her heart. "And he can't have it."

* * *

Corvane came at slack water, when the flats were firmest, with thirty men in polished boots and the mud not even daring to touch them. He crossed the drowned wreck's shadow like a man entering a church he intended to foreclose on, and stopped before the girl with the smile of a merchant who believes every soul has a price.

"The last Vessian," he said. "Do you know how long I've looked for you? Your blood is the key to everything. With you, the treasury is conquest. Without you, it's merely theft—and I have writs that make theft legal." He extended a gloved hand. "Come aboard, Your Highness. We'll discuss terms. You'll find I'm generous to assets that cooperate."

Yara stepped into his path, and felt Brannic and the others flank her without being asked. Grief had done that—Tam's absence had done that—welded them into something no writ could pry apart.

"Careful, Captain," Corvane said pleasantly. "Obstruction of imperial salvage. I could have your crew pressed and your bones left to the mud."

"You could," Yara said. "But you'd want to hear what she has to say first. I believe it concerns your claim."

Corvane's eyes narrowed. He turned to the girl.

And Ilse—half-starved Ilse, coal-bunk Ilse, the girl who had crossed a river in a crate to escape his purge—climbed onto the wreck's broken rail so that all his men could see her, and held the charter high.

"I am Ilse of Vessia," she called out, and her voice carried across the flats, thin and clear as a bell. "Last of the blood royal, and I hold before me the Abdication Charter, signed by my nation's own hands. Vessia was not conquered. Vessia *renounced itself*—its crown, its throne, its sovereignty—rather than kneel to your empire. There is no kingdom to restore and none to claim. I renounce any title I hold, before these witnesses, forever." She let the silence sit for one long breath. "Your writ names the treasury of a sovereign Vessia. There is no sovereign Vessia. Your claim is void, Magnate. It was void the day your empire's greed made my ancestors choose the horizon over the crown."

Corvane's smile died by inches. "A child's paper. My lawyers—"

"Are yours," said a new voice.

They all turned. Out on the flats, where the mud lay firm and wide, the riverfolk were coming—dozens of them, then scores, captains and dredgemen and eel-fishers and salvage crews from every boat Yara had ever shared a channel with, armed with boathooks and old muskets and the particular immovable stubbornness of people whose river had been stolen from them. At their head walked Old Maren, the harbor-mistress, holding Yara's message in one raised fist.

Yara had sent it two nights ago, by a boy with a fast skiff and a good memory. She had not been certain it would be enough. She had sent it anyway.

"Your lawyers are yours, Corvane," Maren said. "This river is *ours*. And the riverfolk recognize the charter, and the girl's renunciation, and claim jurisdiction over these flats. Your fleet is anchored in our water without our leave. You can weigh anchor, or we can discuss it less politely."

Corvane looked at his thirty men. He looked at the three hundred on the mud. He looked, last of all, at the girl on the rail, who gazed back at him with the patience of someone who had already lost everything once and found it made her free.

"Captain Sandoval," he said at last, and the name was a promise of future trouble. "This isn't finished."

"It never is," Yara agreed. "But today it's finished enough."

* * *

They raised the treasury by lantern light as the tide came home, and Ilse stood in the hold of the drowned nation and did not take a single coin for herself.

She bought the crew land instead—a stretch of the leeward shore where the river met the sea, high ground with a deep anchorage, going cheap because everyone said the wind was wrong for it. She bought Brannic a forge and Tam's mother a house with a garden, and paid the widow's grief forward into ground where things could grow. She bought Yara a new keel, laid down by the best shipwrights on the river, and when Yara protested, the girl only said, "The river made a delivery. I'm just signing for it."

On the first evening, as the sun went down copper behind the new hull's ribs, Ilse came to stand beside Yara at the water's edge. The wind was wrong for the shore, everyone said. It pushed you out to sea instead of home.

Yara had never found that to be true.

"I've been thinking about names," the girl said. "Mine was taken. Ilse was the matron's name for me, at the last house. It was never mine." She looked out at the horizon, where the water went on forever, unclaimed by any crown. "I'd like one that was given. If it's not— if you don't mind."

Yara felt something in her chest come loose, some plank that had been sprung since the fire, since the mudflat, since Tam. "Sandoval," she said. "Riverfolk take the mother's name. And I've been calling you daughter in my head for two tides now, so we may as well make it legal."

The girl—*Ilse Sandoval*—turned her face away quickly, but not quickly enough, and Yara pretended not to see, the way you do with family.

Behind them the crew was singing, off-key, the old leeward song about the horizon that keeps what the river gives. The tide was coming in, patient and certain, filling the channels, lifting everything that floated.

The wind was wrong for the shore.

It carried them home anyway.
