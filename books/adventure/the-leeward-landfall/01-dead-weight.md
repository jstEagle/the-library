## 1. Dead Weight

The wreck had no name on any chart Rafael Thornbury could buy, borrow, or steal, and that was precisely why she loved it. Unnamed things belonged to no one. Unnamed things couldn't be taken from you, because to take a thing the takers first had to admit it existed, and the navy had spent seventy years perfecting the art of not admitting anything at all.

She rolled off the gunwale of the *Cormorant's Debt* at first light, when the water was still the color of tarnished pewter and the patrol boats were changing shifts. Forty minutes, give or take. That was her window. She'd learned it the way she'd learned everything worth knowing—by being wrong enough times that the corrections carved themselves into her.

The descent was cold in the way that made her teeth ache behind the regulator. Sixty feet down, the light went from pewter to bruise to nothing much at all, and then the wreck rose out of the gloom like a hill that had decided to become a ship instead. Corvettes weren't supposed to be beautiful, but this one had been down long enough that the sea had finished the work of softening it. Coral had colonized the gun mounts. A forest of black coral grew from the bridge windows like the wreck was weeping something slow and mineral. Fish moved through the open hatches with the bored confidence of tenants who'd been there longer than any landlord.

Rafael checked her gauge, checked her line, checked the surface for the shadow of a hull that shouldn't be there. Clear. She swam toward the aft section where the brass lived.

Brass was honest money. It didn't rust, didn't rot, didn't ask questions about provenance. The harbor dealer paid by weight and never looked too closely, because looking closely was bad for business on both ends. Valves, fittings, the maker's plates off machinery that officially had never been installed on a vessel that officially had never been sunk in waters that officially were closed to civilian traffic. All of it came up in her mesh bag like coins from a dead man's pocket, and all of it kept Marisol in blood-pressure medication and kept the *Cormorant's Debt*—a name Rafael had chosen as a joke that had stopped being funny—in diesel and rope and the particular expensive silence of a hull that hadn't been surveyed since before the war that mattered.

Her knife tapped the first fitting free. The sound carried strangely underwater, a bright metallic note that traveled farther than it should have, and she froze for three full seconds, listening through her bones. Nothing. Just the tick and sigh of the reef, the distant thunder of swell on the leeward cliffs.

She worked. This was the part of diving nobody wrote songs about: the small, patient violence of wrenching metal loose while your fingers went numb and your air crept down and your mind, unattended, began its slow inventory of everything you'd rather not think about. Her grandmother's hands, doing this same work in different water sixty years ago—or so the family story went, though the family story had holes in it wide enough to swim a corvette through. The name nobody said. The photograph with one face turned away, or maybe scratched out; Rafael had never been sure which, and Gran had never been asked twice.

Breathe. Work. Check your line. The rule of invisibility wasn't complicated. It was just total.

*Never log the site. Never sell to anyone who asks where it came from. Never dive it twice in one week. Never let your face be photographed above water or below. Be a rumor with a boat.* Marisol called it paranoia. Rafael called it the only inheritance her grandmother had left her that had appreciated in value.

The bag filled. Two hundred pounds of dead weight, which was what salvage always was—the past, hauled up by hand, sold cheap to people who wanted it melted into something new. She hooked the bag to the lift line, gave it two sharp pulls, and felt the *Cormorant's Debt* take the strain above. Somewhere overhead, Marisol would be cursing at the winch in Spanish and Portuguese simultaneously, which was how she cursed when she was happy.

Rafael hung in the water column for her safety stop, fifteen feet down, watching her own exhaust bubbles climb toward a sky she couldn't quite see. Below her the wreck settled back into its secrecy, patient as a grave, generous as one too. She thought—not for the first time—that she should feel guilty about picking it apart fitting by fitting. She didn't. The men who'd gone down with it had been erased so thoroughly that even their ship had lost its name. If she hadn't come for the brass, nobody would have come at all. At least she touched things gently.

Mostly.

Her safety stop ended. She kicked for the light.

* * *

Marisol had the brass sorted by the time Rafael got her fins off, which meant she'd been sorting since the first bag broke the surface, which meant her hands were aching and she'd done it anyway, because Marisol Vega did not believe in resting a body that still worked. Seventy-one years old, forearms like dock rope, a face weathered down to its essential geometry. She sat on an overturned crate in the stern, weighing fittings on a fish scale and muttering prices under her breath like a rosary.

"Valves are good," she said without looking up. "Plates are good. This—" she held up a corroded lump that might once have been a gauge housing, "—is garbage, and whoever cut it free wasted forty seconds of tank air."

"That was me."

"I know it was you. I'm telling you so you don't do it again." She dropped it in the scrap bucket with a clank of finality. "Two hundred twelve pounds clean. Dealer'll say one-ninety. We'll settle at two hundred even, because he cheats and I cheat and it comes out fair."

Rafael lowered herself onto the crate across from her. Her wetsuit steamed faintly in the early sun. The harbor lay flat and empty around them, a bowl of gray-gold water holding a dozen moored boats and not one other person awake. Gulls argued over something on the breakwater. The town behind the quay was still shuttered, still dreaming, unaware that two women had just robbed a ghost.

"You're breathing wrong," Marisol said.

"I'm breathing fine."

"You're breathing like a woman doing arithmetic." Marisol set down the scale and looked at her, and her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of old bottle glass, and they missed nothing, which was the problem with old people who'd spent their lives reading water. "Say the number."

Rafael said the number. It was worse than last month. The fuel debt alone, plus the compressor repair, plus the interest that accrued on both like barnacles. She said it flatly, the way you'd report a depth, and watched Marisol do the same arithmetic she'd been doing underwater, arriving at the same ugly sum.

"So," Marisol said. "We need the deep shelf. The *Santa Elena* section, past the drop-off."

"No."

"The *Santa Elena* pays triple."

"The *Santa Elena* is inside the exclusion line, and the exclusion line has eyes now. They've added a second patrol boat. I counted hulls yesterday while you were napping."

"I wasn't napping, I was conserving energy, which is what wisdom looks like from the outside." Marisol waved this off and picked the scale back up, weighing nothing, thinking hard. "Rafaelita. Listen to me. I have watched you thread a hookah hose through a collapsed cargo hold with a shark's fin's width of clearance. I have watched you hold your breath for four minutes because the compressor died and you were embarrassed to say so. You are the best diver on this coast, and the coast knows it, and the coast is poor, and that is the whole disease right there. Best diver, poorest harbor."

"Poets could put that on a plaque."

"Poets get paid." Marisol's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the thing Rafael had spent eleven years learning to read. "One run on the deep shelf. One. We clear the fuel debt, we fix the compressor properly instead of with prayer and wire, and we go back to robbing ghosts at our leisure."

Rafael looked out at the water. The tide was turning; she could see it in the way the mooring lines swung, all of them together, the whole harbor leaning imperceptibly toward the sea like a congregation toward the door. Out past the headland, somewhere beyond sight, the leeward cliffs stood over water that dropped away faster than any chart admitted. And beneath those cliffs—

Nothing. As far as she knew, nothing. But the charts around here lied the way official things lie: not with false information but with absence, with blank space, with the confident white nothing of places someone had decided didn't need mapping.

"One run," she said. "Next spring tide. Not before. And if I see a third hull out there, we abort, and you don't argue on the radio where they can triangulate us."

Marisol spat in her palm and held it out, an old deckhand's contract older than any paper. Rafael took it. The grip hurt, the way Marisol's grips always did, and Rafael understood it was a form of affection, possibly the only kind the woman had fully mastered.

"Two hundred even," Marisol said, nodding at the brass. "I'll take it to the dealer after mass. He's more honest before noon, God help him."

* * *

The dealer's back room smelled of diesel, coffee, and the particular green-metallic smell of money that had been underwater. Rafael waited outside, as always. Her face didn't go into rooms where transactions happened. She leaned against the wall in the alley shade and watched the harbor wake up around her: the ice truck, the priest walking fast with his collar tucked against the wind, two fishermen mending nets with the lazy economy of men whose real work was talking.

Father Ekwueme paused when he saw her, as he always did, and gave her the nod he gave everyone, and then the second nod, slower, that he only gave her. He'd tried for years to get her inside his church, and failing that, inside his confessional, and failing that, inside any conversation longer than a greeting. She liked him fine. That was exactly why she kept him at the length of a nod. People who liked you started asking questions, and questions were how invisible things became visible, and visible things got names put on them, and named things got taken.

She'd learned that lesson young. She'd learned it from a woman who'd had her name taken.

Marisol emerged from the back room counting bills with her lips moving, folded them into the oilskin pouch she wore against her ribs like a second heart, and jerked her chin toward the quay. They walked. Behind them the dealer's shutter rattled down halfway, and Rafael didn't look back to see whether he was watching. She already knew he was. Everyone watched everyone in a harbor this size; the trick was giving them nothing to watch with.

"Enough for the fuel," Marisol reported. "Not enough for the compressor. Enough for my pills, which were never in question, because I told the pharmacist I'd haunt him."

"You can't haunt anyone. You're too stubborn to die."

"It's true. Death has been trying to collect on me since 'sixty-eight and I keep disputing the invoice." She walked with her rolling gait, hands clasped behind her back, surveying her harbor like a dowager empress surveying grounds she technically owned. Then, quieter, without looking over: "You surfaced sad today."

"I surfaced tired."

"Tired is sad wearing work clothes." Marisol stopped at the foot of the quay where the *Cormorant's Debt* bobbed at her mooring, gray-painted, unlovely, seaworthy, theirs. "Eleven years, Rafaelita. Eleven years you dive alone, I run the boat, we sell the brass, we pay the debts, and every night you sleep aboard that tub like the harbor's going to steal something out of you if you come ashore. I have a bed. It has a roof over it. It has soup near it."

"I like the boat."

"You like the boat the way prisoners like their cell after ten years. Familiar isn't the same as chosen." Marisol untied the mooring line and coiled it with two efficient motions. "Go on. Take her out, check the bottom paint, whatever excuse you're constructing. Soup tomorrow. Real soup, at a table, with a spoon. That's not a request, it's a forecast."

Rafael took the helm. The engine caught on the second try, coughed, settled into its familiar uneven heartbeat. She idled out past the moorings into the open harbor, and Marisol stood on the quay getting smaller, one raised hand, then gone behind the ice house.

That was the moment it always hit. Not during the dive, where fear and focus kept the ledger balanced. Not during the work, where there was always another fitting, another pull on the line. It was here, in the flat middle of the day, with the throttle low and the harbor emptying of even the pretense of company, that the loneliness arrived and presented its bill. Rafael stood at the wheel of a boat named for a joke and looked back at a town where she knew every face and was known by none of them truly, and felt the specific weight of a life built entirely out of caution. It held. That was the thing about a life made of caution—it held, the way a sealed strongbox holds. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out.

She ran the *Cormorant's Debt* along the leeward shore, ostensibly checking the bottom paint, actually looking at the cliffs.

They rose sheer from the water for half a mile, black basalt banded with old lava flows, and at their base the sea worked ceaselessly at the rock with the patience of something that intended to win. Charts showed solid stone the whole way. But Rafael had dived this coast since she was nineteen, and she knew what charts didn't: that the basalt was hollow in places, that fresh water seeped from cracks in the cliff face after heavy rain inland, which meant rivers, which meant passages, which meant the white blankness on the map was hiding a geography somebody had paid to leave unmapped.

Today the water below the cliffs was behaving oddly. She noticed it without understanding it yet—a long smooth slick running along the base of the rock, the surface glassy where everywhere else it riffled, as if something underneath were pushing up. Or pulling down.

She killed the engine and drifted.

The tide was lower than it should have been. She'd been reading this harbor's moods for eleven years and the tide was wrong—drained, drawn out, the kelp line exposed dark and dripping well below where it ought to sit at this hour of this moon. An earthquake somewhere far off, maybe. The earth shrugging and the sea pouring toward the wound. The radio had said nothing, but the radio said nothing about most things that mattered.

And there, at the base of the cliffs, where the slick water lay thinnest over the rock—

Stone, where stone shouldn't be visible. A lip of it. An edge. Darker than the basalt around it, regular as a doorway, standing open to the drained sea like a mouth that had been underwater her whole life and had just, today, decided to breathe.

Rafael stood at the wheel for a long time. Every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her unnamed, unrecorded, alive—told her to start the engine and put a mile between herself and that opening before the tide came back and swallowed it and whatever was inside it, forever.

Her hand was already reaching for her mask.
