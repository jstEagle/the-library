## 3. Admission Charged

The rectory smelled of beeswax, salt, and forty years of pipe smoke that had soaked into the walls the way tide soaks into pilings. Father Ekwueme opened the door before Rafael finished knocking, as if he'd been standing behind it all morning, waiting for her to work up the nerve.

"You took two days," he said. "Longer than I expected."

"I didn't say I was coming."

"No. But you looked at the cave mouth from your skiff for a long time before you turned away, and people who look that long always come." He stepped back to let her in. "Coffee's terrible. Sit anyway."

She sat. The kitchen was small and crowded with the archaeology of a long life—chipped blue cups, a wall of framed photographs gone amber with age, a bookshelf sagging under ledgers and parish registers bound in cracked leather. On the table between them lay a tin box, dented at one corner, its lid already off.

Rafael kept her hands around the bad coffee and said nothing. She'd learned young that silence pulled more truth out of a room than questions did.

Ekwueme lowered himself into the chair opposite her with the careful economy of a man whose knees had opinions. He was old in the way driftwood is old—not fragile, exactly, but shaped entirely by what had been done to him. His collar was soft from washing. His eyes were not soft at all.

"Two nights ago," he said, "the sea went out past every mark on every chart, and when it came back it brought you something. You've been diving it since dawn yesterday. You haven't reported it. You haven't sold anything from it." He folded his hands. "I know this because I am an old man who watches his harbor, and because you are the only diver left here who still dives like someone hiding."

Her jaw tightened. "I dive like someone working."

"You dive like my grandmother prayed," he said. "Quietly, quickly, and expecting to be interrupted."

* * *

He let her finish the coffee before he touched the tin box. That, too, felt deliberate—a man who understood that some doors should be opened slowly, and that the person opening them should watch the other one's face while they did.

"Before I show you anything," he said, "I want to tell you about admission."

"The cave doesn't have a door fee, Father."

"It has the only kind that matters." He turned the tin box over in his hands, once, twice, like a man handling something consecrated. "In this parish we keep records the way other places keep grudges. Baptisms. Burials. Marriages. Land transfers. Fishing licenses going back to before the Concession had a name. I inherited the keeping of them from the father before me, who inherited it from the father before him, and somewhere in that chain of hands, the keeping stopped being just church business and became something else. A duty. A vigil." He looked up. "Do you know what the Meridian Concession was?"

"A salvage company. Old. Big. Gone."

"Gone," Ekwueme repeated, tasting the word. "That is what the paper trail says. The charter lapsed, the assets were dispersed, the name dissolved. Very tidy. But tidy things are made by hand, Miss Thornbury, and I have spent forty years reading the fingerprints on the tidiness." He set the tin box down between them. "Here is what I can tell you, and then you will decide whether you want to see what's inside.

"Every person who has ever entered that cave and come out again has been erased. Not killed—erased. Their names lifted out of the rolls. Their gravestones blanked or removed. Their fishing licenses voided as though they'd never held them. Their own children unable to find proof their parents existed. The first time it happened was 1912. The most recent time was 1968, and the man erased was a surveyor named Odili Bright, who went in on a government contract and came out to find his house reassigned to another family and his marriage certificate missing from the registry. He drank himself dead within the year, and when they buried him, the stone said only *plot 44*. No name. I was a young priest then. I asked why. I was told the record had been lost in a fire." His voice didn't rise at all. "There has never been a fire."

Rafael set her cup down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; everything behind her sternum was moving like water over a wreck.

"That's a story," she said. "A good one. Harbor stories are supposed to be good."

"Yes. And harbor stories are also how a place remembers what its paperwork is forbidden to." He opened the tin box. "You found strongboxes down there. Crested ones. So you already believe the cave was used by the Concession for something worth sealing. What you don't yet know is what happens to people who learn what that something is."

"And you're telling me anyway."

"I'm an old man," Ekwueme said simply. "Erasure takes time. They'd be erasing me until well after they'd need to."

It wasn't quite a joke. It wasn't quite anything else either. Rafael looked at the box—at the edge of a folded paper showing inside, yellowed, brittle—and heard Marisol's voice in her head saying *you don't bring trouble home, niña, you leave it where the water keeps it.* Two days ago she'd have agreed. Two days ago she hadn't seen the crest stamped into brass, hadn't felt the weight of sealed iron in her hands, hadn't lain awake listening to the tide and thinking: *someone built a vault under the leeward coast, and someone else decided no one would ever ask why.*

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The paper inside the tin was a crew list. Dated March 1912, letterhead of the Meridian Concession Company, Salvage & Recovery Division. Twenty-three names in a clerk's careful copperplate, each with a wage rate beside it and a column at the far right marked DISPOSITION.

Twenty-two of the dispositions read *returned to port.*

The twenty-third line was burned out.

Not crossed out—burned. A scorch mark the size of a thumbprint had eaten through the paper where the name should have been, taking the wage rate and the disposition with it, leaving a hole edged in brown, the fibers curled inward like something trying to close over a wound. The rest of the page was untouched. Whatever had burned that one line had burned nothing else, which meant it hadn't been an accident, and hadn't been careless, and hadn't been fire at all.

"Somebody took a coal or a heated wire to that name," Rafael said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "On purpose. After the fact."

"After several facts," Ekwueme said. "This copy survived because the parish required a duplicate of all manifests for burial insurance purposes—the Concession insured its crews through a diocesan fund, one of the few arrangements they couldn't alter without our notice. This is the diocesan duplicate. The company originals were destroyed, officially, in 1931. Unofficially—" he tapped the scorched line "—someone wanted one particular man unmade even in the copies that survived. They went through every ledger they could reach and burned the same line out of each one. We know because there are four surviving duplicates across three parishes, and all four carry the same burn, in the same place, made by the same hand."

"Who was he?"

Ekwueme was quiet a moment. When he answered, he didn't answer her.

"My predecessor's predecessor wrote a note in the margin of the register the week this manifest was filed. It says: *Concession vessel Meridian Star returned short one man. Company states he deserted at the upper river. No body. No wages owed. Family informed of nothing, as family cannot be located, though the family has lived on Chapel Street for sixty years.*" He looked up. "Chapel Street is four hundred meters from this rectory, Miss Thornbury. It has never been anywhere else."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes through that you feel in your teeth before you feel it in the hull. Rafael stared at the burned line. The scorch had a shape, almost—she could nearly read letters in the negative space, ghosts of ink pressed through from whatever had been beneath the page when the burning was done.

"Give me the name," she said. "Even burned, give me the—"

"I can't. The burn went through. But the wage rate survives in the margin of the register, and the register gives the next of kin listed at signing." Ekwueme slid a second paper from the tin—older, softer, the parish register itself, open to a page of 1912 entries. He turned it toward her and put one blunt finger on a line.

"*Signed aboard Meridian Star, salvage division,*" he read, "*wage twelve shillings six. Next of kin: sister, Adaeze Thornbury, Chapel Street.*"

The sound Rafael made wasn't a word. It came from somewhere below language, below thought—a single note of recognition struck on a bone she hadn't known she had.

Thornbury.

Her family had spelled it a dozen ways across the generations—Thornbury, Thornburry, once, memorably, Tornberry on a baptismal certificate nobody had ever corrected—but the name itself ran back through the harbor like a keel-line through water, and it ran through *Adaeze*, her great-great-aunt, the sister who had raised Rafael's grandmother after their mother died, the woman whose photograph still hung in Marisol's back hallway because Marisol's mother and Adaeze had been inseparable until the end.

And the burned man—the erased man, the one the Concession had sent upriver and written off as a deserter with no body and no wages and no family, while his family watched from Chapel Street—

"His sister's name," Rafael said. Her mouth was dry. "Adaeze. My grandmother's—my grandmother was named for her. My grandmother was *Amara Adaeze Thornbury*." She looked up. "Father. Who was the man? Who signed that manifest?"

Ekwueme closed the register gently, but his eyes stayed on her face, watching the ground shift under her the way he'd known it would.

"The manifest gives his name," he said. "But you already know it, don't you. You've known it since you were small enough to sit on a knee and hear about a great-uncle who went upriver on a salvage contract and never came home, and how the company told the family he'd run, and how your grandmother refused to believe it until the day she died, because there was nothing—no paper, no grave, no witness—to prove otherwise. Nothing left to grieve *with*." He laid his hand flat on the tin box. "They didn't just erase him. They erased the evidence of the erasure. That is what the Concession did, and that is what the cave holds, and that is why everyone who goes in comes out to find themselves becoming the same kind of hole in the paper."

Rafael stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand. Her chair scraped back and she was on her feet with her palms flat on the table, staring down at the burned line, and the fury that came up through her wasn't hot—it was cold and enormous and patient, the way deep water is patient, the way pressure is patient.

"He didn't desert," she said.

"No."

"They sent him into the mine. Or the flooded works, or wherever it is that river goes, and when he didn't come back they burned him out of existence rather than pay out a claim."

"That is what the pattern suggests," Ekwueme said. "Across forty years of reading. Yes."

"And the others. The ones erased in '31, in '54, in '68—"

"Went in after. Curious divers, hired surveyors, one journalist in 1954 who wrote two paragraphs about the cave before his newspaper's entire run for that week was pulped." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "The cave charges admission, Miss Thornbury. The price is your name. The Concession collects it."

Outside, the harbor light had gone flat and silver, the kind of light that makes every boat look like a silhouette of itself. Rafael became aware, distantly, that she was breathing too fast, and made herself slow it down the way she did at thirty meters, when panic was a luxury with a bill attached.

"Then I'm already paying," she said. "I've been in twice."

"I know."

"Then why am I still—"

"Because erasure requires machinery," Ekwueme said, "and the machinery requires someone to file the paperwork, and the paperwork requires a reason. A diver who finds a cave and tells no one is a rumor. A diver who files a salvage report is a line item." He rose, joints cracking, and carried both the register and the tin box to the shelf where they lived, slotting them back among the other books like returning bones to a crypt. "You have stayed invisible for years. I imagine you think that has protected you. And perhaps it has. But understand what you actually saw down there, and what it means now that you've seen it."

He turned back to face her, and for a moment the tired old priest fell away and she saw the vigil-keeper underneath, the man who had spent a lifetime guarding names against the dark.

"There is a whole drowned archive under that cliff," he said. "Payrolls. Manifests. Correspondence. Enough paper to prove what happened to every man and woman the Concession fed into that river—including yours. The Concession's heirs will have felt the earthquake too, Miss Thornbury. They keep their own vigil; theirs runs the other direction. When they come—and they will come—they will not come to recover treasure. They will come to make sure the cave closes with everything still inside it. Including, if necessary, you."

Rafael looked at the window, at the flat silver harbor, at her skiff riding empty at its mooring because she'd had no one to tell where she was going.

"I need to get the rest of those boxes up," she said quietly. "Before they do."

"Yes," Ekwueme said. "And you will need help to do it. Real help. Names, plural, on the same side of the paper." Something moved through his face then—grief, maybe, or its long-distant cousin, hope. "Which brings us to the hardest part, and the reason I asked you here instead of coming to you. Because the moment you lift another box, you stop being a rumor. And the moment you stop being a rumor, you will need people willing to stand next to you in the record."

He held out his hand—not for shaking, she realized, but for the coffee cup she'd forgotten she was still holding.

"Think about who you trust," Father Ekwueme said. "Then think about whether trusting them is still enough. Because the Concession never erased anyone alone. They always had help—from people who preferred not to look."
