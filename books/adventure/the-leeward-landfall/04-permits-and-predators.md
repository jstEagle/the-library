## 4. Permits and Predators

The ferry to Port Meridian had no name painted on its hull, which Adaeze Okonkwo found fitting. Everything in the concession's territory seemed to have been sanded down until it stopped meaning anything. The harbor office where she was to present her credentials had a brass plaque reading simply *MARITIME AFFAIRS*, though everyone on the dock called it the Company House, and had called it that for ninety years, through three changes of ownership and one war.

She carried her audit warrant in a leather folio against her ribs like a second passport. Forty-one pages of authorization, countersigned by the Ministry, granting her authority to inspect all permits held under the Meridian Concession Act prior to their scheduled expiry — permits covering salvage rights, dredging rights, river access, seabed mineral claims, and one annex, Annex K, so heavily redacted that entire paragraphs had been replaced with black rectangles, as if the document itself were drowning.

The ferry bumped the pier. A boy took her bag without being asked and set it down on the boards, palm out. She paid him and stood a moment, letting the harbor introduce itself: diesel and brine, gulls arguing over fish guts, the long green wall of the leeward cliffs closing off the northern horizon like a hand curled around something it didn't want seen.

*Everything here is held,* she thought. *Nothing is owned.*

Her predecessor's files had arrived by diplomatic pouch three weeks ago, eleven boxes of them, and she had read every page twice. Inspector Nwosu. Inspector Calloway before him. Inspector Brandt before him, going back to 1931. Eleven inspectors across nine decades, each assigned the same mandate — periodic review of concession compliance — and each producing, in the final week of their tenure, a closure memo of almost liturgical sameness. *No irregularities noted. Concession operations consistent with permit terms. Recommend renewal.* The language barely changed. Even the typewriter errors repeated: the same smudged *f* in *findings*, decade after decade, as if the memos were being traced rather than written.

Adaeze did not believe in ghosts. She believed in incentives. But she had spent the ferry crossing looking at those eleven identical documents laid side by side on her cabin table, and what she felt was not suspicion exactly. It was the particular cold that comes from recognizing a machine still warm.

* * *

The Company House smelled of floor wax and old paper kept too dry. A clerk named Pell — narrow man, spectacles, the apologetic posture of someone who has spent his life standing between doors — led her up two flights to an office that had clearly been prepared for her arrival: desk cleared, blinds angled, a carafe of water and a single glass arranged with hotel precision.

"Inspector Okonkwo. We're honored." The voice came from the doorway behind her, and she turned to find a woman in her sixties, silver hair pinned with architectural care, wearing a grey suit worth more than Adaeze's annual clothing allowance. "Vera Ashcombe. Custodian of Records, Meridian Concession Trust. I thought I'd welcome you personally — we don't get many visitors from the Ministry who stay longer than a tide."

"Custodian of Records," Adaeze said. "That's a title under the original charter?"

"It's a title under *every* charter," Ashcombe said pleasantly. "Records are the only thing this company has never let go of. Land comes and goes. Permits expire. But the archive—" she touched the doorframe lightly, the way one touches a shoulder, "—the archive is forever. You'll want to work with us, Inspector, not around us. It makes everything so much faster."

"I'll want to work accurately," Adaeze said. "Speed is your department."

Something moved behind Ashcombe's eyes — not anger; recalibration. "Of course. Pell will see to whatever you need. The full registry is at your disposal." A pause, measured to the half-second. "Within the terms of your warrant, naturally."

"Naturally," Adaeze said, and watched the woman go, and noted that Ashcombe had not once asked what she intended to find.

They already knew what she would find. That was the trouble. They had known for ninety years.

* * *

The registry occupied a long room on the ground floor, iron shelving, brass rails, ledgers bound in green buckram going back to the founding. Pell brought her the current permit file with the ceremony of a man serving communion, and left her alone with instructions to ring if anything was unclear.

Adaeze worked the way her mother had taught her to mend nets: patiently, in order, trusting the pattern to reveal itself. Permit 1-A, harbor dredging, renewed forty-one times. Permit 2-C, river navigation, renewed thirty-eight times, with a curious gap between 1954 and 1961 where the renewal stamps simply stopped and then resumed as if nothing had happened. Permit 4-F, seabed salvage, northern sector — renewed annually, unbroken, the fee always paid early, sometimes months early, as though the company feared even a day's lapse.

And then the annexes. Annex J: *restricted waters, navy coordination required.* Annex K: the blacked-out one. She held it to the window light, uselessly. Whatever was under those rectangles had been killed with ink, not hidden with it.

It was late afternoon when she cross-referenced the salvage declarations — the quarterly statements of what had been recovered from the wreck sites, weighed, valued, taxed — against the navy's own incident logs, copies of which the Ministry had included in her briefing pack because the wreck fell inside a designated naval exclusion zone and the two bureaucracies were supposed to talk to each other.

They did not talk to each other. That much was immediately clear. What was clear after four hours was stranger.

The concession declared modest recoveries. Brass fittings, copper ingots, the occasional porthole — a few hundred kilos a quarter, steady as rainfall. The navy's patrol logs, however, recorded something else: repeated night sightings of dive lights in the exclusion zone, a skiff running dark, hydrophone contacts logged and then crossed out in pencil by some watch officer who had apparently decided the paperwork wasn't worth the argument. And once, memorably, a boarding report from 2019 — a patrol boat had actually caught a diver on the wreck, name entered into the log, cargo seized — followed by a second document, filed two days later, ordering the log entry expunged and the cargo returned. No reason given. The expungement order bore a signature Adaeze didn't recognize and a stamp she did: the same crest that sat embossed in gold on the cover of every permit ledger in the room. The Meridian crest. A meridian line bisecting a wave.

The navy did not take orders from the concession. Officially. But someone had made a naval log entry disappear in an afternoon, and whoever that someone was, they had been doing it for a very long time.

She pulled the boarding report again. The diver's name was there in the original entry, typed: *THORNBURY, R.* — and beneath it, in the margin, in different ink, different hand, a single word added later: *void.*

Not the entry. The name. Someone hadn't just erased the event; they had erased the person, struck her from the record like a debt forgiven or a soul unregistered.

Adaeze sat back in the clerk's chair and looked at the word *void* for a long time.

She had grown up on stories about the concession, same as everyone in the coastal provinces. Her grandmother used to say the Meridian men could make you disappear faster than the tide could fill your footprints. It was a grandmother's saying, the kind of thing said over fish stew to make children eat quickly. Adaeze had filed it under folklore.

She looked at the margin note, and the folklore climbed out of the stew pot and stood dripping on the table in front of her.

* * *

She spent three more days in the registry, and the pattern only deepened. Salvage declarations that matched no wreck on the charts. Renewal fees paid on permits whose subject matter was redacted beyond recognition. And threaded through everything, recurring like a watermark, the same administrative signature — initials *M.V.*, elegant, unhurried, appearing on expungements, on fee receipts, on a 1977 letter to the Navy Board thanking them for their "continued discretion regarding sector activities." M.V. had been signing documents for fifty years. Either the initials belonged to a dynasty, or to a position, or to something that did not age.

On the fourth morning, Pell brought her coffee she hadn't asked for and lingered.

"The Custodian wondered," he said, eyes on the shelving, "whether you'd found everything satisfactory."

"Satisfactory isn't the word I'd use."

"No," Pell agreed softly. He glanced toward the door, then lowered his voice to something barely above the hum of the dehumidifier. "I've worked here thirty-one years, Inspector. I file what I'm given. But I'll tell you what I told no one else, because you'll be gone in a fortnight and I won't." He set a thin folder on the desk beside her elbow. "This came up from the harbor office last month. Routine. Except the harbor master was told to lose it, and he gave it to me instead, because the harbor master is my brother-in-law and he can't read."

"What is it?"

"Salvage declarations. Quarterly. Filed by an independent operator — not company. Small-time. Brass and copper off the old wreck." He tapped the folder once. "Except the tonnages don't match what the navy says is missing from the site, and the navy says nothing should be missing at all, because officially there's no wreck there. And except—" his voice dropped further, "—one of the declaration forms has a name on it that was supposedly voided years ago. Thornbury. R. Thornbury."

Adaeze looked at the folder. "You're giving me this why?"

Pell straightened, and for a moment the apologetic clerk was gone and something older stood in his place — tired, and careful, and brave in the way of small animals. "Because I file things, Inspector. It's what I do. And a thing that gets lost stops being a thing. I don't like losing things." He went to the door. "The Custodian will ask you to dinner before you leave. Accept. Refusing insults her, and insulted, she becomes thorough. Dinner is survivable. Thoroughness is not."

* * *

She read the salvage declarations that evening in her rented room above the chandlery, window open to the harbor sounds. Rafael Thornbury. The name surfaced in the records like a body that refuses to sink — logged, voided, logged again, persistent. A diver working alone on a wreck that officially did not exist, filing honest tonnage reports to a harbor office that was ordered to lose them, in a territory where honesty was apparently the most dangerous document one could produce.

Adaeze spread her materials across the bed: the boarding report with its voided name, the navy hydrophone logs, the quarterly declarations, the eleven identical closure memos spanning nine decades, and Annex K with its rectangles of dead ink. Separate papers. Separate decades. But laid edge to edge they formed a single continuous surface, the way separate stones form a causeway, and somewhere along that causeway, people had walked into the water and not come back onto any page ever again.

Her warrant authorized her to audit permits. It said nothing about divers, wrecks, or names. Strictly speaking, R. Thornbury was outside her jurisdiction — a private citizen conducting questionable salvage, nothing more. The clean path, the path every inspector before her had taken, was to note the discrepancy, recommend standard review, sign the memo, and go home.

She thought about Inspector Brandt, 1931, typing *no irregularities noted* while — what? While what? That was the question the black rectangles wouldn't answer. Something had been happening in this valley since before her grandmother was born, and eleven inspectors had looked directly at it and described it as nothing.

Adaeze got out a fresh sheet of ministry paper and, in her precise, upright hand, wrote a single line:

*Discrepancy flagged: independent salvager R. Thornbury — declarations inconsistent with naval records; identity previously subject to administrative voiding. Referred for follow-up.*

Then, below it, in smaller letters, a second line that appeared in none of her predecessors' memos:

*Follow-up to be conducted personally.*

She signed it, dated it, and placed it on top of the folder Pell had given her. Outside, the tide was going out, and far off along the leeward cliffs a light was moving — low, steady, patient — where no light had any official reason to be.

The machinery was still running. She could hear it now, everywhere, under everything: the soft, tireless sound of names being fed into it.

Adaeze turned off the lamp and began, methodically, to plan how to meet a woman who did not exist.
