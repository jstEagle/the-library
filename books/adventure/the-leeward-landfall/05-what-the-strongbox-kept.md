## 5. What the Strongbox Kept

The strongbox came up the cliff path in a fish crate, wrapped in Marisol's oldest tarp and carried on Rafael's back like a sleeping child that weighed as much as one. Forty kilos of brass-bound iron, salt-scabbed, its lock plate furred with coral the way old wrecks grow fur. She'd taken it from the cave at slack tide on the third day, after she'd counted eleven more boxes stacked in the dark like cordwood, after she'd swum out with her heart hammering and her lungs burning and one box's handle wrapped around her wrist because her hands had stopped working in the cold.

Now it sat on the boathouse floor under a hanging lamp, dripping into a ring of rust-water that looked, in the lamplight, almost like blood.

Marisol stood over it with her arms crossed, chewing the inside of her cheek. She was sixty-eight years old and had hands like braided rope, and she had not said a word since Rafael had come up the harbor stairs at dawn carrying the crate.

"You want to tell me," Marisol said finally, "why we're not selling this?"

"Because I don't know what it is yet."

"It's heavy. Heavy is money."

"Heavy is also a reason for someone to come looking." Rafael knelt beside the box and ran her thumb along the crest stamped into the lid. A meridian line bisecting a globe, flanked by two dolphins—the Meridian Concession mark, the same one embossed on the faded share certificates that hung framed in the harbor office like saints' icons, relics of the company that had built half the town and then, when the ore ran out, quietly let the other half fall into the sea. "You recognize this."

"Everyone recognizes this." Marisol spat, ceremonially, off the side of the dock behind her. "That doesn't mean I want it in my boathouse."

"It's your boathouse. I can take it to mine."

"Your boathouse has a hole in the roof and no lock." Marisol crouched down beside her, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and studied the crest. Her expression did something complicated—went hard, then soft, then somewhere between. "My father worked a Concession barge," she said, quieter. "Forty-one years ago he went out on the *Cormorant* and the *Cormorant* came back without him. They told my mother he was lost cargo."

"Lost cargo," Rafael repeated. The phrase sat wrong in her mouth, like a stone.

"That's what the settlement letter said. *Regret to inform the cargo aboard the Cormorant was lost in transit.* Cargo." Marisol touched the box lid once, briefly, the way you touch a grave marker. "She never believed it. Died not believing it. So." She straightened up. "Open it. But do it slow, and do it where nobody can see the light through the windows."

Rafael already had the lamp turned away from the glass.

* * *

The lock was a Concession pattern, a four-lever marine safe made in the shipyards at Port Anselm in the years before the war, and Rafael had opened three of them in her career—not by force, because force meant noise and bent metal and value destroyed, but by feel. You needed the right tools: two tension picks ground down from bicycle spokes, a stethoscope she'd bought from a defunct doctor's estate sale, and patience measured in hours rather than minutes.

She worked through the morning while the tide dropped outside and the harbor bottom emerged in its daily confession of lost anchors and snapped mooring lines. The levers inside the lock were stiff with a century of salt but fundamentally sound; the Concession had built things to last, because lasting things held their value and everything else could be written off. She thought about that phrasing as she fed the picks in and listened through the stethoscope to the small iron conversations inside. Lost cargo. Written off. The language of accounting applied to human beings, smooth and bureaucratic and final.

The third lever gave at noon. The fourth fought her until nearly two, and then surrendered all at once, and the bolt shot back with a sound like a knuckle cracking in church.

Marisol, who had spent the afternoon ostensibly mending nets ten feet away and actually watching every movement Rafael made, set down her needles. "Well?"

Rafael lifted the lid.

Inside, packed in wool that had gone gray and dense as felt, lay books. Not coin, not ingots, not the pearls or plate or salvage gold that forty kilos of strongbox usually promised. Books—dozens of them, ledger volumes bound in green buckram, their spines stamped in gilt that had survived the water better than anything organic should have, because the Concession had bought good materials and the cave had kept them dry. Dry. That was the thing she kept snagging on. The cave above the tide line, sealed, airless, cold—and inside it, paper preserved like flowers pressed in a Bible.

She lifted the top volume out and set it on the bench. The cover read, in faded gilt:

**MERIDIAN CONCESSION — OPERATIONS LEDGER — UPPER VALLEY WORKS — 1909–1913**

"Upper Valley Works," Marisol read over her shoulder. "There's nothing up the upper valley. There hasn't been anything up there since—" She stopped. Everyone stopped at the same place in that sentence. Since the flood. Since the dam went in and the company town of Meridian Landing went under thirty meters of reservoir water, and the valley gates were locked, and the navy charts went blank above the falls, and everyone agreed without ever saying so that some places were better left unasked about.

"There was something up there once," Rafael said. "A whole town."

"A company town. Company towns aren't real places. They're machines that happen to have streets." But Marisol had pulled a second volume onto the bench and was opening it with hands that weren't quite steady.

The pages were payroll.

Rafael knew ledgers the way most people know faces—she'd salvaged enough from enough wrecks, had sold enough of them to enough collectors, to read their grammar at a glance. Debits left, credits right, dates in the margins, initials of clerks at the foot of each page. This one followed the form exactly, except that the entries were wrong. Not fraudulent-looking—nothing so obvious. Just wrong in the way a room is wrong when furniture has been moved two inches.

*Wk 14, 1911. Transport, Upper Valley Works: 22 souls. Freight class: perishable.*

*Wk 15. Return transport: nil. Write-off: 22 souls, total loss, cause: transit mishap. Compensation disbursed per Schedule C.*

*Wk 16. Transport, Upper Valley Works: 31 souls.*

She turned pages. Week after week, year after year. Souls sent up. Return transport: nil. Write-off. Total loss. Transit mishap. The same clerk's initials—*J.M.*—at the foot of every entry, neat as stitching. And running alongside the soul entries, in the same hand, the ordinary business of a mining operation: tonnage of ore received at the lower works, quantities that made no geological sense for the played-out seams along the coast, quantities that only made sense if there was a rich vein somewhere inland, somewhere upriver, somewhere past the falls where the charts went blank.

"They were mining the upper valley," Rafael said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew something underneath it was screaming. "After the flood. After they drowned the town, they kept the mine going. Under the reservoir."

"And the crews?" Marisol asked, though Rafael could see she'd already done the arithmetic. Anyone could. Twenty-two souls up, zero back, write-off, repeat. Thirty-one souls up, zero back. Fourteen. Twenty-seven. "They didn't come back down."

"No."

"So what does 'transit mishap' mean?"

Rafael turned to the back of the volume, where the summary tables lived, where an accountant's conscience would either surface or drown. It drowned. The annual totals ran in clean columns: *Souls transported: 412. Souls returned: 0. Loss ratio: 100%. Note: consistent with operational parameters.*

Consistent with operational parameters.

Four hundred and twelve people. Sent upriver into a flooded mine beneath a drowned town, and written off as freight, and compensated at Schedule C rates—which she found two volumes later, and which worked out to less than the book value of a mule—and erased. Not killed in any single catastrophe anyone would have noticed. Erased the way you erase a pencil line: gradually, administratively, in entries no one outside the counting house would ever read.

The lamp hissed. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"My grandmother," Rafael said.

Marisol looked at her.

"She was born in Meridian Landing. In the company town. Before the flood." Rafael heard herself say it as though reporting someone else's salvage find—coordinates, depth, condition. "Her name was Ines Thornbury before she married, and she came down the coast road in 1913 with nothing, and she never talked about why, and when I was a girl and asked about the upper valley she hit me. Once. Only time she ever raised a hand to me. And then she cried, and I never asked again." She looked down at the ledger, at week 41 of 1913, at the last entry in J.M.'s tidy hand: *Transport suspended pending review. All prior write-offs confirmed final.* "She didn't run away from the town. She was written off of it. She was one of these numbers, and she got out, and she spent sixty years pretending she hadn't been."

The fury arrived first, and it was enormous, and it had nowhere to go. It filled the boathouse like floodwater. She wanted to put her fist through the hull of every boat in the harbor, wanted to swim back to the cave and burn the whole stack of green volumes, wanted—absurdly, uselessly—to find J.M., dead eighty years, and make him read his own columns aloud until the numbers turned back into names.

Then the grief came in behind it, quiet, and it was worse, because grief had a face. It had her grandmother's face, young, in a photograph Rafael had seen exactly once, in a locket that had gone into the ground with her. A face from a town at the bottom of a reservoir. A name on a page between *freight class: perishable* and *compensation disbursed per Schedule C.*

"Rafael." Marisol's hand closed over hers on the edge of the ledger. The old woman's grip was still strong enough to hurt. "Look at me. Whatever you're deciding right now, decide it slow."

"I'm not deciding anything."

"You're breathing like a woman deciding something."

Rafael made herself let go of the bench. Made herself stand, walk to the window, look out at the harbor doing its ordinary evening business—lanterns coming on, fish smell rising, the patrol boat making its lazy fifth circuit of the bay with its running lights doubled, the way it had for the past three days, ever since the earthquake swell. Ever since the sea opened its mouth under the cliffs and showed what it had been keeping.

They were watching the water. Which meant someone had told them to watch the water. Which meant the Concession—or what wore the Concession's name now—had felt the cave open the way a body feels a wound.

"Eleven more boxes," she said. "In the cave. Maybe more behind them. Payroll, correspondence, maybe the master rolls. Everything they wrote off, written down." She turned back. "Four hundred twelve souls in this one volume alone, Mari. There are families in this harbor whose grandfathers are in those columns, and they think their people were lost at sea. There are graves in the churchyard with the wrong dates on them because somebody's death had to be dated to a wreck instead of a mine. This isn't treasure. It's a list of everybody the Concession disappeared, in the company's own handwriting, signed."

"Which makes it worth more than gold," Marisol said slowly, "and worth less than nothing to anyone who'd buy it. And dangerous as a lit fuse to anyone who'd burn it." She looked at the strongbox, at the green spines, at the rust-water ring on her floorboards. "You're going to go back for the rest."

"The tide window's in two days."

"You're going to go back alone, like always."

Rafael started to say yes—it was reflex, the word lived in her mouth the way *lost cargo* lived in ledgers—and found it wouldn't come out. She looked at the ledger instead. At J.M.'s initials. At the machinery of erasure, still oiled, still turning, its modern hands now wearing navy uniforms and concession letterhead instead of buckram-bound books.

Alone, she could carry one box. One box was a secret, and secrets could be sunk, burned, buried, denied. Eleven boxes was evidence, and evidence needed witnesses, archivists, people who could read what she could only carry. It needed hands that weren't hers.

It needed a crew.

"Marisol," she said, "how well do you know Father Ekwueme?"

The old deckhand's eyebrows went up. "The legend-keeper? Well enough. He buries half this harbor and remembers the other half. Why?"

"Because he showed me a crew list from 1912 with a line burned out of it, and I need to know who else in this town keeps records the Concession doesn't know about." Rafael pulled the tarp back over the strongbox, over the green spines, over four hundred twelve names waiting in the dark to be read aloud. "And because tomorrow we're going to need more than two pairs of hands."

Outside, the patrol boat's lights swept the cliffs above the cave mouth, once, twice, and moved on—but slower than they had yesterday, and closer to shore.

Someone was coming for this. The only question left was who would be holding it when they arrived.
