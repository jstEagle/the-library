## 6. The Crew You Keep

The strongbox sat on the floor of my boathouse under a tarp that had once covered a generator, and I caught myself checking on it the way you check on a sleeping animal you don't trust. Every hour or so I'd lift a corner of the canvas, look at the Meridian crest stamped into the green-bronze lid, and pull it back down like I'd seen something I shouldn't have.

Three days. That's how long I'd managed to keep it secret, which by my standards was two days and twenty-two hours too long.

The problem was Marisol. She'd been my deckhand for six years, which meant she'd been reading me for six years, and she knew every tell I owned. When I came back from the cave mouth at low tide with silt still drying in my hair and said nothing about where I'd been, she didn't ask. She just started leaving things out for me—a fresh tank of coffee, a dry towel, her old dive knife sharpened without being asked—and waited the way weather waits.

On the third evening she cornered me at the workbench while I was pretending to re-lace a regulator hose I'd already laced twice.

"You've been diving something new," she said.

"I've been diving the usual."

"The usual doesn't leave you quiet." She leaned against the bench, arms folded, sixty-two years old and built like the pilings she'd spent her life around. "You come up from the wreck talking to yourself half the time. Complaining about the brass, the patrol boats, the price of diesel. This week you haven't said ten words that weren't about tide tables." She tapped the tarp with one sandaled foot. "And you've started covering things in my boathouse."

"Your boathouse."

"My name's on the lease because your name can't be on anything, Rafael. That was the arrangement." Her voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry—it was patient. "So either you show me what's under there, or I lift the tarp myself, and I'd rather you showed me."

I looked at her for a long moment. Six years of empty harbors, of surfacing to nobody, of splitting salvage money with the one person who knew what I actually was—and I'd still been planning to keep this from her. Some part of me had already written the speech: *it's safer if you don't know, it's better if you're not attached to it.* The same speech, I realized, that some clerk at the Meridian Concession must have written a hundred years ago about a crew list. *It's cleaner if they're not named.*

I pulled the tarp off.

Marisol crouched down and read the crest the way she read charts—slowly, thoroughly, running her thumb along the stamped meridian line through the M. Then she looked up at me.

"How many more of these are in the cave?"

"Eleven that I could see. Maybe more behind the collapse."

"And you were going to open them alone." It wasn't a question. She stood, joints cracking, and poured herself the last of the cold coffee. "Get the lantern. If we're doing this, we do it properly, and properly means witnesses."

* * *

Father Ekwueme kept his church the way he kept everything: barely, stubbornly, and against all reasonable expectation. The roof leaked over the vestry and not over the altar, which he considered a private joke between himself and God. He was seventy-eight, half-blind in one eye, and the only man on the leeward coast who remembered the Concession as anything other than a name on rusted buoys.

He listened to everything—the earthquake swell, the drained bay, the cave, the strongboxes, the burned line on the 1912 crew list that he himself had shown me, the name I hadn't yet been able to say out loud—and he did not interrupt once. When I finished, he sat with his hands flat on the table for so long I thought he might have fallen asleep with his eyes open.

Then he said, "You understand what you're holding."

"Ledgers."

"Ledgers," he agreed. "And ledgers are how the Concession killed people the second time. First they sent them into the water. Then they sent them out of the paper. A man who dies is dead once. A man whose name is struck from the rolls dies every time somebody looks for him and finds nothing." He turned his good eye on me. "Your grandmother has been dying that second death for a hundred and twelve years. You're the first person to bring back anything that could stop it."

"That's why I need—" My voice snagged. I started again. "I can't carry this alone. I don't know how to read what's in those boxes. Half of it's in company shorthand. And if the heirs find out the cave opened—and they will, Ekwueme, the tide that opened it was an act of God but the boxes are going to be an act of Rafael Thornbury—I need people who know things I don't. People who'll notice when I disappear."

"So this is recruitment."

"This is recruitment."

He almost smiled. Under the leak-stain and the dust motes, it transformed his whole face. "In 1911 my grandfather ferried Concession paymasters upriver in his ferry-boat and never asked them what was in the crates. He died ashamed of it. Not of the ferrying—of the not asking." He pushed back his chair. "I will ask. For him, and for her." He nodded toward the burned line I'd copied onto a scrap of paper, which lay on the table between us like a wound. "Who else?"

"A hydrologist named Tomas Reyes. Do you know him?"

The smile went away entirely. "Everyone knows Tomas Reyes. The question is whether anyone will speak to him."

"Why not?"

"Because eight years ago he published a gauge report saying the upper valley river flows were inconsistent with any natural reservoir, and the Concession had him stripped of his license, his post, and eventually his marriage. He was right, of course. That's never been a defense in this territory." Ekwueme gathered his coat. "He drinks at the Anchorline now. He'll be there tonight, and he'll be drunk, and he'll be brilliant, and you should go before the drinking overtakes the brilliance."

* * *

The Anchorline smelled of creosote and old rope, and Tomas Reyes sat at the end of the bar exactly where a ruined man sits: facing the wall, guarding his back, a glass of something cheap held in both hands like a small warm animal.

I sat down next to him without asking. He glanced at me sideways—one bloodshot pass from boots to face—and went back to his glass.

"If you're here about gauges," he said, "the harbor authority stopped paying me in 2017."

"I'm here about rivers."

That got his full attention. He turned on his stool, and up close I could see what ruin had left intact: quick hands, ink stains on the fingers even now, and eyes that moved like a man reading instruments. "Which river."

"The one that comes down from the upper valley and feeds the sea caves below the cliffs. The leeward coast. There's a cave that opened three days ago in an earthquake swell, and the water inside it behaves wrong. It surges on a cycle that doesn't match the tide. Twelve hours and eleven minutes, give or take, regular as breathing." I'd timed it myself, four times, before I understood I couldn't explain it alone. "I think it's not a cave. I think it's a drain. I think somewhere upstream there's a body of water filling and releasing, and whatever controls it isn't natural."

Reyes set down his glass very carefully, the way a man does when his hands have started to shake and he doesn't want them to know it.

"Twelve hours," he repeated. "You measured twelve hours."

"Four times."

"Nobody measures twelve hours." His voice had changed—sobered, though the glass was still half full. "Divers measure slack tides. Engineers measure discharge curves. Nobody stands in a flooded hole counting hours unless they think the number means something." He stared at me. "Who told you about the upper valley?"

"Nobody. That's why I'm telling you. I need someone who knows what's up there, because everyone official swears there's nothing up there, and nothing doesn't produce a twelve-hour pulse."

He laughed then, short and bitter, and knocked back the rest of his drink. "Nothing up there. Yes. I wrote forty pages on the nothing up there. Residual flow data that only makes sense if there's a controlled impoundment feeding the river. Seasonal drawdowns matching no rainfall record. Water temperature anomalies consistent with deep release from a reservoir that appears on no chart, no permit, no survey." He spread his ink-stained hands. "They took my license for it. Called it fabrication. My wife called it obsession, which was fairer, and left anyway."

"I'm not asking you to publish," I said. "I'm asking you to come and measure."

He was quiet for a long moment. Around us the bar noise went on—darts, arguments, the jukebox grinding through its four songs—but between us there was a silence with weight to it, the silence of a man being offered the exact thing he'd been destroyed for wanting.

"They'll come for whoever touches this," he said finally. "You understand that. The Concession's permits expire in a few months, and expiring permits make desperate families. They've had a hundred years of practice making problems into non-problems."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

He signaled the bartender for two coffees—the worst coffee on the coast, burnt and thick enough to stand a spoon in—and when it came he wrapped his hands around the cup and looked at me over the steam with something that had begun, cautiously, to resemble hope.

"Then I'll need my old field kit," he said. "And someone to drive, because I haven't held a license of any kind since 2017. And Rafael—" he'd learned my name from Marisol, who'd introduced us with the brisk efficiency of a woman managing a project she'd already decided was happening, "—when this goes wrong, and it will, remember that you asked."

"I'll remember."

"Good." He drank the terrible coffee and grimaced. "Twelve hours and eleven minutes. You know what that could be? A penstock cycling. A gated spillway on a timer. Which means there's machinery up there, working, maintained, a hundred years after the Concession supposedly abandoned the valley." His eyes had gone bright and feverish, the scholar rising through the ruin like a spire through floodwater. "Oh, I have missed being right."

* * *

We met in the boathouse two nights later, all four of us, because Marisol refused to plan anything in a place with a confessional and Reyes refused to plan anything in a place with windows, and the boathouse had neither. Bad coffee in a dented percolator. Charts spread over the workbench weighted with brass fittings. The strongbox open between us, its first ledger page flattened under glass from an old porthole.

It should have felt like a conspiracy. Instead it felt like something I didn't have a word for.

Ekwueme read the payroll entries aloud in his slow liturgy voice, translating the company shorthand line by line, and each translated name landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water. Marisol took notes in a spiral notebook, her handwriting as square and reliable as her welding. Reyes had commandeered the entire eastern wall for the river: gauges, dates, the pulse interval, arrows showing flow directions that officially did not exist. He'd drawn the upper valley from memory—every bend, every contour—and marked the likely impoundment site with a question mark he clearly intended to remove personally.

"Here." Reyes tapped a point where his penciled river crossed a contour line. "If the pulse is a penstock release, the intake has to be within thirty meters of this elevation or the math breaks. And the math—" he smiled thinly, "—does not break. It never breaks. That's why they hated me."

"And the mine town?" Ekwueme asked. "The one that isn't on any map?"

"Under it." Reyes drew a reservoir shape with two strokes. "You don't flood a valley by accident. You build a dam, you cite flood control, you relocate—or fail to relocate—the population, and a decade later nobody remembers there was ever a town. The water does the erasing for you. All the Concession ever had to do was the paperwork." He looked up. "The paperwork is in that box."

Marisol set down her pencil. "So we go up the river."

"We go up the river," I said.

"Past the checkpoints."

"Past the checkpoints."

"With Meridian-Vale's security boats on the water and the navy's patrol schedule overlapping ours at least twice." She said it evenly, cataloguing dangers the way she catalogued rust, and then she capped her pen. "Fine. But we do it in the *Alba*, not your skiff. My hull, my rules, and rule one is nobody dives alone. Not for brass, not for ledgers, not for anything. That's how people die out here—alone, with everyone ashore assuming they're fine."

I thought of all the years I'd surfaced to an empty harbor. Of how close that habit had come to killing me in the cave, with no one knowing where I was, no one counting my intervals, no one waiting.

"Rule one," I agreed.

Ekwueme closed the ledger gently, like closing a casket, or a book he intended to reopen. "There's a verse I've always disliked," he said. "'What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and lose his soul.' I used to think it was about greed. It isn't. It's about trade. The Concession gained the whole coast and lost every name in it, and called that a fair price." He looked around the boathouse—at Marisol's notebook, at Reyes's impossible river, at me. "We're going to make an unfair one. We're going to lose the world and keep the names."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the tide was turning; I could hear it in the pilings, that soft knocking of water taking back what it lends.

It was Reyes who broke it, quietly, staring at his own map. "There's one thing none of you have asked," he said. "The pulse interval. Twelve hours and eleven minutes. I told you it matches a penstock cycle, and that's true. But there's a second match, and it's been sitting in my old data for eight years waiting for someone to ask." He looked up, and the feverish brightness had gone out of his eyes, replaced by something colder. "Twelve hours and eleven minutes is also the interval between the navy's hydrographic survey flights over the upper valley. Same clock. To the minute."

The coffee pot ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the dark harbor, a boat engine started up—not the *Alba*, not any boat I knew—and ran at idle, unhurried, as if its only business was listening.

"Somebody's been synchronizing the water and the sky," Reyes said. "And I don't think it's the Concession."
