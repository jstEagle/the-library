## 7. Navy Business

The memo arrived at 0600, which told Adaeze Okonkwo everything she needed to know about its contents. Bad news traveled at the speed of bureaucracy; inconvenient news arrived before breakfast, when a person was too tired and too hungry to argue.

She read it standing at the window of her quarters in the naval station at Port Calloway, the paper thin enough that the harbor light bled through it. *Re: Audit File MC-114 (Meridian Concession Permit Renewal Cycle). Be advised that findings bearing on historical crew dispositions fall outside the scope of current audit authority. Recommend consolidation of working notes into summary format prior to quarterly review.*

Consolidation. She turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being shaved. In eleven years as an inspector—customs first, then the joint maritime authority, then this hybrid posting that answered to the navy when it suited the navy and to no one when it didn't—she had learned the taxonomy of soft language. *Consolidate* meant delete with paperwork. *Summary* meant the version that survived. *Recommend* meant an order wearing civilian clothes.

The memo was signed by Commander Voss, who she had met exactly once, at a welcome briefing where he'd spent forty minutes explaining the harbor's security zones and not one second asking what she intended to audit. He had a way of looking slightly past her left shoulder while speaking, as though the interesting part of any conversation were happening behind her.

Outside, the bay lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky still deciding whether to be gray or gold. Fishing boats were going out in ones and twos, their wakes stitching the water. Somewhere out there was the wreck—the navy's unnamed wreck, hull number redacted from every chart she'd been issued, the one local divers called by a dozen private names and officialdom called nothing at all. And somewhere out there, too, was Rafael Thornbury, salvage diver, holder of three permits, author of salvage reports that fit navy records the way a key fits a lock it wasn't cut for: almost, but not quite, and only if you didn't look too hard.

Okonkwo had looked too hard. It was, in fact, the entire job description.

She set the memo down on the desk, weighted it under her inkwell, and did not consolidate anything. Instead she opened the folder she'd begun keeping inside the folder—the one that lived in her personal satchel rather than the office cabinet, because cabinets had keys and keys had circulation lists—and reread what she'd assembled so far.

Three salvage reports from Thornbury, filed across four years. Brass fittings, copper sheeting, a ship's bell listed as *unidentified, degraded*. All recovered from grid squares that overlapped the navy's exclusion zone by margins small enough to be deniable and consistent enough to be deliberate. The woman knew exactly where the line was drawn and harvested right up against it, year after year, like a farmer plowing to the edge of someone else's field.

And the navy records—the ones Voss's office had released to her in a batch so heavily redacted it resembled modern art—listed no salvage recoveries at all from those grids. Not confiscated recoveries. Not disputed recoveries. *No activity.* As though the wreck produced brass the way a river produced fish, and everyone had agreed not to mention the fishing.

Institutions did not lie with documents, Okonkwo had learned. Institutions lied with absences. A page removed left a shape behind, the way a tooth leaves a socket. You learned to read the sockets.

She dressed, pinned her inspector's badge where it would catch the light, and went down to find out who else had received the memo.

* * *

Her clerk, a round-faced young man named Pell who filed things with the devotion other men gave to religion, confirmed it without being asked. "All section heads, ma'am. Commander's office sent them out together." He hesitated, then lowered his voice. "There was also a courier from the Concession House. Yesterday evening. Left a parcel for the Commander."

"A parcel."

"Wrapped in green paper, ma'am. The good kind, with the crest embossed." Pell said this with the faint awe of a man describing a visitation. "He carried it in himself. Didn't log it."

"Didn't log it," Okonkwo repeated.

"No, ma'am." Pell's ears had gone pink. He was twenty-three and honest, which made him either useless or invaluable; she hadn't decided yet. "I only saw because I was restocking the dispatch case. I shouldn't have—"

"You should always see, Pell. That's the job. Seeing is the whole of it." She took her cap off the hook. "If another parcel arrives, unlogged or otherwise, you'll note the time, the courier's description, and the size of the package. Nothing more. Understood?"

"Yes, ma'am." A pause. "Ma'am—are we in trouble? With the audit?"

"We are never in trouble, Pell. Trouble requires that someone admit something is happening." She settled the cap on her head. "We are merely being managed."

The Concession House stood on the bluff above the harbor, white-columned and self-satisfied, flying the Meridian crest—a trident crossed with an anchor, the empire's whole theology in two symbols: take from the sea, hold what you take. Okonkwo had walked past it twice since arriving, once officially and once not, and both times had noticed the same thing: the building had no back door that wasn't guarded, but it had a side gate, iron, unlocked, opening onto a lane that smelled of kelp and coal smoke. Institutions that feared auditors built walls. Institutions that owned auditors built gates.

She was not ready to walk through the front entrance and announce herself. Not yet. First she wanted to know what the green-paper parcel had contained, and there was exactly one place in Port Calloway where parcels became gossip: the chandlery on Rope Walk, where every skipper, diver, and deckhand in the harbor bought tar, twine, and rumor in equal measure.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of hemp oil and coffee burnt past redemption. Its proprietor, a leathery woman named Brannigan who had hands like mooring line, answered questions the way a chart answered questions: accurately, tersely, and only about what was already marked.

"Green paper, crest embossed, couriered by hand." Brannigan weighed a coil of rope for a waiting customer without looking at it. "That'd be Vale's man. Corrigan. Big fellow, walks like he's carrying a piano nobody can see."

"Delivered where?"

"Station. But he'd stopped here first." Brannigan set the rope down. "Bought a case of brandy. The Concession doesn't drink our stock—they've got theirs shipped in. So the brandy was for giving away." Her eyes came up, flat and shrewd. "You're the new inspector."

"I'm the new inspector."

"They send one every seven years." Brannigan began coiling the rope again, each loop precise. "Last one was a fellow named Ashworth. Nice enough. Wrote his report, went home, died comfortable in a house with a view." She tied off the coil. "The one before him drowned. Officially."

"And unofficially?"

Brannigan smiled without warmth. "Unofficially, everything here is official. That's the trick of it." She nodded toward the shelf. "Coffee's free to inspectors. It's punishment enough that we don't charge."

Okonkwo drank the coffee, which tasted like it had been brewed during the last administration, and asked her real question on the way out. "Rafael Thornbury. What do people say about her?"

"Nothing," Brannigan said. "That's what they say. Woman keeps herself like a closed ledger. Dives alone, files on time, pays cash, tips fair. Never drinks enough to talk." A beat. "Except Marisol might know more. Old deckhand, works the skiff with her sometimes. Bad hip, worse debts. You'll find her at the net sheds, most mornings."

Okonkwo wrote the name in her pocket notebook, in pencil, in shorthand only she could read. Then she walked down to the water.

* * *

She found Rafael Thornbury that afternoon, though not by arrangement—or rather, by an arrangement neither of them acknowledged. The salvage skiff came in at low tide, riding light, and Okonkwo happened to be walking the quay with her badge visible and her notebook open, which was as close to an ambush as civil procedure allowed.

Up close, Thornbury was younger than her file photographs and older than her years, in the way of people who worked alone underwater. Salt-bleached hair cut short and practical. Hands scarred at the knuckles. She moved off the boat with the economy of someone who had calculated the cost of every gesture and found most of them unaffordable.

"Inspector." No surprise in it. That interested Okonkwo. "Here for my filings? They're current through last month."

"I've read your filings."

"Then you're ahead of most inspectors." Thornbury began coiling a line, not dismissively—she kept working while talking, Okonkwo would learn, the way some people prayed. "Anything wrong with them?"

"On the contrary. They're meticulous. That's what concerns me." Okonkwo let the silence sit for a moment. "Meticulous is a choice, Ms. Thornbury. Sloppy is what people do when they have nothing to hide. Meticulous is what they do when hiding is the point."

Thornbury's hands didn't stop moving, but something behind her eyes recalibrated—a depth sounding taken quickly. "Is there a question in there, Inspector? Only the tide turns in forty minutes and I've got a client."

"There will be. Formally, eventually, through channels." Okonkwo held her gaze. "I'm telling you now, informally, so you understand the sequence. When the formal questions come, your answers will already be on record. Whatever you tell me today, you'll want to have told me again tomorrow, in writing, in the same words."

"That sounds like a warning."

"It's a courtesy. I extend very few." Okonkwo closed the notebook. "Your salvage grids overlap restricted waters. Your recoveries appear nowhere in naval logs. Either you are stealing from the Crown's seabed with extraordinary discipline, or you are salvaging a wreck the Crown has decided does not exist, and filing honestly about it anyway. I've spent three weeks trying to decide which. I haven't yet."

For the first time, Thornbury stopped moving. The harbor noise filled the space between them—gulls, halyards, a winch groaning somewhere down the quay.

"And if it's the second?" Thornbury said quietly.

"Then you're the most law-abiding criminal I've ever audited, and the law has a problem that isn't yours." Okonkwo watched her carefully. "I'm not your enemy, Ms. Thornbury. I'm also not—not yet, not today—anything else. I'm a woman reading a ledger full of missing pages, and you are the only person in this harbor who seems to know where they went."

Something flickered across the diver's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. Recognition, maybe. Or the reflex of a creature hearing its burrow mentioned aloud.

"I don't know what you mean," Thornbury said.

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "You wouldn't. Nobody ever does, the first time." She stepped back from the quay edge. "But you'll think about it. People always do. And when you decide you want to say something true to someone whose salary doesn't depend on your silence—" she took a card from her breast pocket and set it on the bollard, face down, "—you know where the office is. Ask for me by name. Not by title. Titles get intercepted."

She walked away without looking back, which cost her something, because every instinct she had trained into herself over eleven years screamed to watch the diver's face as she read the card. But watching was a form of pressure, and pressure made people lie, and Okonkwo had come to Port Calloway precisely because she was tired of institutions that preferred lies to pages.

Behind her, she heard the card picked up. Heard the skiff's engine turn over. Heard, beneath it all, the long slow breath of the tide beginning to go out.

* * *

That night she sat in her quarters with the memo, the redacted naval logs, and a bottle of the station's terrible sherry she'd bought purely to pour into the harbor plants, and she laid the pieces out on her desk like a surgeon laying out instruments.

A wreck the navy would not name. A concession whose permits expired in ninety days and whose heir had arrived in town with brandy and unlogged parcels. An inspector every seven years—one dead, one comfortable, and now one memo instructing her to consolidate. A diver who filed truthfully about a forbidden place, which was either madness or a message, and Okonkwo had been reading messages for eleven years and knew the difference.

And underneath all of it, the thing she had felt since her first morning here, walking the quay among fishermen who nodded to her and then turned away to finish their conversations: the sense of a town holding its breath around a name. Not fear exactly. Grief, maybe, worn so long it had hardened into habit.

She thought about Ashworth, writing his report in a house with a view. She thought about the inspector who had drowned, officially. She thought about the green paper, and what it cost to buy an auditor, and how cheaply it had apparently been done for decades.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper and, instead of consolidating, wrote at the top in her careful hand: *Questions the Concession does not want asked.* Beneath it, she listed them. There were nineteen. By midnight there were thirty-one, and the sherry remained untouched, and outside her window the tide had turned and was coming back in over whatever the sea kept at the bottom of the bay.

On the last line, after the thirty-first question, she wrote a thirty-second, smaller, almost to herself:

*Why does a woman who wants to be invisible file reports accurate enough to be found?*

She looked at that line for a long time. Then she folded the page into the inner folder, into the satchel, away from keys and circulation lists, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the harbor breathed. Somewhere out past the breakwater, past the unnamed wreck, past the cliffs where the charts simply stopped, the sea was doing what the sea always did in this place—covering, uncovering, keeping its own ledger in sand and salt.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would pull the personnel files of every inspector assigned to Port Calloway since 1900. All of them. Especially the one who had drowned.

Especially what he had been about to file.
