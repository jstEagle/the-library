## 9. Night Salvage

The moon didn't rise until nearly eleven, and when it did it came up thin and mean behind the cloud deck, a coin seen through dirty glass. Rafael counted that as luck. She'd planned the run for dark water and got it.

She stood at the bow of Marisol's skiff—the *Perla*, twenty-two feet of patched fiberglass and stubbornness—and watched the leeward cliffs slide past, black on blacker. Behind her, Tomas Reyes crouched over the gear bags with a diver's nervous hands, checking straps he'd already checked twice. Marisol ran the outboard at a low burble, engine hood wrapped in foam to kill the noise, her face lit faintly by the compass light.

"You're going to burn fuel we don't have if you keep circling," Marisol said without looking up.

"I'm not circling. I'm listening."

"For what? Patrols don't run this stretch after ten. You told me so yourself."

"And I told you the concession doesn't file float plans either." Rafael kept her voice level. That was the discipline of invisibility: never let the fear into your voice where other people could catch it. "We go in, we're down forty minutes, we're out. Two boxes, maybe three. No heroics."

Tomas looked up from the gear. In the three weeks since she'd pulled him out of his office above the defunct hydrology station—since Ekwueme had vouched for him over bad coffee and worse odds—he'd lost some of the academic softness around the middle but none of it in the eyes. He still looked like a man reading something alarming over your shoulder.

"Forty minutes isn't enough for three boxes," he said. "You told me the silt layer alone—"

"The silt layer is why we're doing this at night. Daylight brings every charter jockey on the coast past those cliffs, and one of them works for Meridian-Vale whether he admits it or not." She turned back to the water. "Two boxes. We'll come back for the third."

*We.* The word sat strangely in her mouth. For nine years she had dived alone, surfaced alone, walked the harbor alone with brass in her bags and no name on any manifest. Nine years of being nobody, which was safety, which was freedom, which was also just another word for the empty chair across from every meal. Now there were three people in the boat and one of them was sixty-one years old and running an engine she'd rebuilt herself, and Rafael found she could not look at any of them too long without feeling something she didn't have a name for yet.

"Buoy marker," Marisol said.

Rafael saw it—a scrap of pale cloth tied to a rock ledge below the tideline, invisible by day, ghostly now. Her own handiwork from two nights ago. Beyond it the cliff wall dropped sheer into water the color of ink, and somewhere under that ink was the cave mouth the earthquake tide had cracked open, and inside the cave were strongboxes stamped with a crest that should have died with the century.

She killed the discussion and started kitting up.

* * *

The descent was clean. Cold thermocline at twelve meters, then warmer, then the green-black blindness of the deep shelf, her torch beam cutting a tunnel through particulate drift. Tomas followed her fin-wash as they'd rehearsed, close enough to touch, and she heard his breathing go ragged and then steady as the cave swallowed them.

Inside, the world changed. The cavern opened like a throat, air pocket glowing faintly ahead where their bubbles gathered under the limestone ceiling, and beyond the air bell the flooded gallery ran back into the mountain—shelves of drowned archive, crates fused together by decades of brine, and the strongboxes, rowed along a stone bench like congregants waiting for a sermon that never came.

Rafael worked the first box free while Tomas held the light. The crest came up out of the muck under her glove: the meridian line, the crossed anchors, the motto in Latin she'd stopped translating because translating it made her want to break things. *Ex Profundis Veritas*—truth from the depths. A salvage company that had made its fortune pulling truth out of the water and drowning men to keep it there.

Her grandmother's name was in these boxes. Not in this one—she'd learned the filing logic from the ledgers already recovered, payroll batches sorted by season—but in one of them, on a page with a burned-out line, in a clerk's careful colonial hand. *Sent upriver. Written off as cargo.*

She got the box into the lift bag. Tomas took the second. They swam it out through the throat of the cave into open black water, and Rafael checked her gauge—nineteen minutes used, plenty of margin—and gave the ascent signal, and that was when she saw the light.

Not their light. It swept across the surface far above, a long white blade moving left to right, then stopping.

Then reversing.

She froze mid-water, torch cupped against her palm. Above them, muffled by fathoms, came the sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: an engine dropping off plane. Coming idle. Coming to a stop directly overhead.

Tomas's hand closed on her arm. Through his regulator she couldn't read his face, only his eyes, wide white rings in the torch spill.

Rafael made the decision the way she made all her decisions underwater: fast, cold, alone. She pointed up and toward the cliffs—away from the skiff, away from the light—and began to swim.

* * *

They came up in the shadow of the rocks, forty meters from where the *Perla* had been anchored, and Rafael broke the surface with her regulator still in her teeth and listened.

The security boat sat off the cave mouth, floodlight pinned on the water where their anchor line should have been. It was a concession vessel—she knew the silhouette, the hard chine, the radar mast with its civilian transponder lying about who it belonged to. Two figures moved on deck. One held a gaff hook and was dragging something aboard.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. Not bodies. The lift bags. Empty ones—they'd find nothing but nylon and their own disappointment. But they'd found the anchor. And an anchor meant a boat, and a boat meant—

The *Perla*'s engine caught.

Marisol. Rafael's blood went to ice water. Marisol was starting the engine, which meant Marisol was awake, which meant Marisol was about to do exactly what Rafael would have done in her place: run for the pickup point and get clear, leaving no trace, trusting the divers to handle themselves. Trusting them. Because Rafael had never once told her the whole truth of what they were pulling out of that cave, never told her how hot the trail had gone since the ledger pages, never told her that Cyrus Meridian-Vale's people had been asking questions at the fuel dock under the cover of a marine survey.

She'd kept her family safe by keeping them ignorant, and ignorance was about to kill them.

The security boat's floodlight swung off the water and snapped toward the sound.

"No," Rafael said, to no one, to God, to the arithmetic of distance. The *Perla* was a quarter mile out, idling slow to stay quiet, and the security boat could make thirty knots. There was no version of this where Marisol outran them. There was only the version where they never found her, or the version where—

The floodlight found the skiff.

What happened next happened in pieces, each one burned separately into Rafael's memory, the way bad things always are.

The security boat came around in a hard turn, bow wave standing up white. The *Perla* throttled up—Marisol, God love her, had the sense not to run straight but to cut for the headland shallows where the big hull couldn't follow. For ten seconds it almost worked. The gap held. The engine noise climbed. Then the concession boat's skipper did the thing Rafael would have done, the ruthless simple thing, and simply drove at the smaller vessel's flank to shove it into the cliff swell and swamp it.

The ram came at the last possible moment of visibility, and Rafael, swimming flat-out for the rocks with Tomas gasping beside her, saw it all lit white as a stage play: the big bow rising, the *Perla* trying to turn inside it, the impact.

It wasn't even loud at this distance. Just a crack, like a branch breaking, and then the floodlight swung away and the security boat backed off its victim and stood away north, unhurried, job done, transponder blinking its polite civilian lie.

"*Perla*, Perla, come in—" Rafael had the radio out of its dry bag before she'd fully registered climbing onto the rocks, thumb jammed on transmit, voice cracking on the second call. Static. Static. Then:

"—afloat. Mostly." Marisol's voice, thin and wet-sounding, with a laugh underneath it that fooled neither of them. "Hull's holed aft. I'm beaching her on the shingle before she finishes arguing with me."

"We're coming. Stay put. Don't—" Rafael stopped. Don't move. Don't bleed. Don't die. "Stay put."

* * *

Marisol had beached the skiff in a slot of gray shingle below the headland, and she was sitting in six inches of water holding her left forearm against her chest when Rafael reached her.

"It's nothing," Marisol said.

"Let me see it."

"It's nothing, niña. The rail caught me when we turned. I've had worse from a winch."

Rafael knelt in the surf and took the arm anyway, gently, the way you handle anything that might be broken, and Marisol hissed through her teeth and stopped pretending. The forearm was swelling wrong below the elbow, a ridge where no ridge lived, and there was a gash above it leaking into the seawater in slow threads. Not life-threatening. Not nothing. Sixty-one years old, on a rocky beach, at midnight, with a broken arm and a sinking boat, because Rafael Thornbury had wanted two more boxes.

Tomas waded up with the first aid kit and the spare tarp, and between the three of them they splinted the arm, bound the gash, and dragged the *Perla* high enough on the shingle that she wouldn't drown on her own. The hole in her stern was ugly—a puncture the size of a man's fist, fiberglass petals bent inward. Patchable, maybe, with resin and daylight and prayer. Maybe.

They got a fire going in the lee of the rocks, small and screened, because hypothermia would finish what the concession had started. Marisol sat wrapped in the tarp with her splinted arm propped on a crate, refusing, by silent imperial decree, to be the center of anything.

"So," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become an accusation. "Those boats. Concession colors?"

"You saw the crest?"

"I saw enough." She looked at Rafael across the fire, and her eyes in the flame-light were the same eyes that had run deck crews through two hurricanes and buried a husband and never once asked anyone's permission. "Three weeks I've been running your river runs and your midnight boxes, and you've told me it's salvage. Historical salvage. Papers and such." She paused. "Papers don't ram boats, Rafael."

Tomas said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing; she suspected it had been a professional skill long before it became a survival one.

Rafael fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Every instinct she owned, nine years of instinct, told her to give Marisol half the truth, the soothing half. But Marisol was sitting on a freezing beach with a broken arm and a dead engine because of the half-truths already given, and Rafael understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the lungs, that the secrecy hadn't been protecting anyone. It had just been keeping her alone. It had always just been keeping her alone.

"The Meridian Concession erased people," she said. "Whole crews. Men they sent into the flooded mine upriver and wrote off as lost cargo so the permits would survive. My grandmother was one of them. These boxes are the proof—the payrolls, the manifests, the signatures. And the heirs will burn the valley and everyone in it before they let that proof reach daylight. Including us. Including you." She looked up. "That's what you've been carrying in your boat, Marisol. I should have told you the first night. I'm sorry."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the sea worked patiently at the cliffs.

Marisol considered her for a long moment. Then she snorted—a wet, dismissive, entirely Marisol sound.

"Papers," she said. "All this, over papers."

"They're more than papers."

"I know they're more than papers, niña. I've known since the night you came back from that cave looking like you'd seen the Virgin and the devil shaking hands." She shifted, wincing, settling the arm. "I raised four children on salvage money and I know what a man looks like when he's hiding something worth killing for. I stayed anyway. I'm old, not stupid." A pause, and then, quieter, with the firelight moving on her face: "But you'll tell me everything now. All of it. The heir, the inspector, the navy, all of it. Because I don't dive blind twice."

"Everything," Rafael said. "I promise."

"Promises." Marisol closed her eyes. "Wake me when it's my watch."

* * *

Tomas didn't sleep either. He sat across the coals from Rafael with his knees drawn up, turning his phone over and over in his hands though there was no signal this far up the leeward coast and wouldn't be until dawn.

"The boxes," he said finally. "Did they get both?"

"Both. They're in the dry bags under the tarp." She nodded toward the pile. "They can have the boat. Not those."

He nodded slowly. Then: "When Marisol turned for the headland—I was watching the timestamps on my depth gauge. The security boat was on station over the cave within ninety seconds of our first lift bag breaking surface." His voice had gone quiet and precise, the scholar's voice, the one Rafael had learned meant he was building something. "Ninety seconds. They weren't patrolling. They were waiting. Which means someone told them the window. Which means either the fuel dock talk has gotten back to Vale faster than I thought—" he turned the phone over again "—or someone closer is talking."

Rafael looked at him. "Who's closer?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He fed the fire a piece of driftwood and watched the sparks climb. "There's something else. While you were cracking the second box, I surveyed the gallery wall. The crate stencils. Batch numbers, dates." He glanced up, and in the ember-light his face had the particular intensity Rafael had only seen once before—the night he'd spread his river gauges across Ekwueme's rectory table and shown them all that the water in the cave came from somewhere forbidden. "The batch numbers aren't random. They're sequential with the payroll dates in the ledgers we already have. Every crate in that cave corresponds to a crew written off as cargo. The cave isn't a dump site, Rafael. It's an index. Someone filed them."

"Filed them for what?"

"I don't know yet. But files imply a system, and systems imply a destination, and the destination of that river—" he tapped the dark ground beneath them, the ground that sloped invisibly up toward the upper valley, toward the reservoir, toward the drowned town whose name appeared on no current map "—is upstream. Everything is upstream. And I think if we cross-reference the gauges against the payroll dates properly—not roughly, properly—we can prove not just that men went up that river. We can prove when, how many, and where they ended up. A complete accounting." He looked at her across the dying fire. "An audit they can't erase, because it's built out of their own numbers."

Rafael sat with that. Out past the headland, the sky was beginning to consider graying, and the sea had gone quiet in the way it did before weather.

"First light," she said, "we patch the *Perla* enough to limp home. Then we start on the gauges."

"There's one more thing." Tomas hesitated, and she realized he'd been working up to it all night. "The inspector. Okonkwo. She flagged your salvage reports—Ekwueme heard it from the harbormaster's clerk. If she's auditing the concession's permits, and she finds your reports don't match navy records..." He let it hang.

"She'll come for me too."

"She might come for you *first*. You're easier to bury than a hundred-year-old company." He shrugged, apologetic, honest. "Or she might be something else. Inspectors who ask real questions don't tend to last long in this district. Either way—someone official is going to come to that harbor soon, and when they do, we need to know what we're showing them before they show us what they are."

Rafael looked east, where the clouds were thinning over the water, and thought about the floodlight sweeping the black sea, and the crack of the ram, and Marisol's arm swelling wrong in the firelight. Nine years of invisibility, and the first time she'd let people close, the machinery had found them in a single night.

Fine. Then invisibility was over. Not by choice anymore—by necessity. If they were going to be hunted, they would be hunted in the open, with names, with numbers, with an accounting no one could drown.

"Wake Marisol," she said. "We've got work."
