## 10. Charts of the Erased

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years being wrong in public, and he had developed a certain tenderness for the sensation. Being wrong meant you were still looking. It was certainty that had ruined him—certainty signed on a hydrological assessment, stamped by a ministry, used to justify a dam diversion that dried out three villages and ended his career in a single afternoon. He did not trust his own conclusions anymore. He tested them the way a man tests ice: weight first, then again, then a third time from a different angle.

So when Rafael Thornbury dropped the oilcloth bundle of ledger pages on his kitchen table and said, *Tell me what this is*, he did not answer for two days.

He answered on the third day, at four in the morning, with every window in the house lit and the floor around him buried in paper like the aftermath of a small white storm.

* * *

The ledgers themselves were beautiful in the way that instruments of cruelty are often beautiful—the hand tooled leather, the gilt-edged columns, the clerk's copperplate so disciplined it looked printed. The Meridian Concession had kept its books the way empires keep their books: immaculately. Every entry balanced. Every loss accounted for. That was the horror of it, Tomas realized early on. Nothing here was hidden. It was only arranged so that no one would ever ask the right question.

The pages Rafael had recovered from the strongbox were payroll abstracts, 1909 through 1914, quarterly summaries reconciled against cargo manifests. And running through them, quarter after quarter, was a line item that read:

**UPRIVER TRANSPORT — SPECIAL CARGO — 40 MEN — FREIGHT PAID**

Forty men. Freight paid. Not wages issued. Not rations drawn. Not return passage booked. Freight, as though they were crates of rivets or drums of blasting oil.

Tomas had seen that phrasing before. Everyone in the valley had, if they'd grown up on the stories—the old-timers who spoke of the mine town upriver the way you speak of a house where someone died, hushed and sideways. *They took men upriver.* But stories blur. Stories forgive. Ledgers do not.

He began with the dates.

The payroll abstracts gave him forty-one separate shipments across five years, each dated to the day. He wrote them all on butcher paper taped to his wall, forty-one entries in a column, and beside each one he transcribed the corresponding line from the river gauge records—which he happened to possess, because gauging stations had been his life's work and the upper valley station, Station Nine, had been his own responsibility once, before the ministry decided his readings were inconvenient.

That was the thing about being erased yourself, professionally speaking. You kept copies.

* * *

The correlation appeared on the second night, and it was so clean that he made himself disbelieve it for six hours.

Every shipment of "special cargo" had departed on a day when the river stood above 1.9 meters at the lower gauge. Not one exception. Forty-one for forty-one. And the departures clustered—he could see it now, standing back from the wall with cold coffee going stale in his hand—in the weeks after each quarterly wage reconciliation, when the concession's accounts came due and its labor costs needed to become something else. Something that didn't have to be paid again.

Men went upriver when the water was high enough to carry barges past the shallows, and they went upriver when the books needed closing.

Tomas sat down on the floor amid his papers and felt the particular vertigo of a pattern assembling itself—the same vertigo he'd chased through thirty years of hydrology, the moment when scattered points resolve into a curve and the curve resolves into meaning. Except this curve was made of people.

He checked the arithmetic anyway. Of course he checked the arithmetic. He ran the shipment dates against rainfall records against barge drafts against the seasonal flood cycle, and every cross-check held. Then he did the cruelest check of all: he pulled the casualty reports. The concession's official losses for those same five years recorded, in tidy summary, **CARGO LOST IN TRANSIT — RIVER ACTION — 1,640 SOULS EQUIVALENT**.

Souls equivalent. As though a man could be converted to tonnage at a fixed rate.

Sixteen hundred and forty. The payroll abstracts, added up, came to one thousand six hundred and forty men shipped upriver as freight.

The numbers matched exactly. To the man.

Tomas put his head in his hands and stayed that way for a long time, listening to the river he couldn't see from his house but could always hear at night, a sound like someone breathing in another room.

* * *

Marisol found him there in the morning. She let herself in the way she let herself in everywhere, without apology, carrying bread and a look that sharpened when she saw the walls.

"Dios mío," she said. "You've become one of those conspiracy people with the string."

"No string." His voice came out rusted. "Dates." He walked her down the column of entries, and to her credit—and Marisol's credit was considerable; she had run deck crews through weather that broke grown men—she followed every step. When he reached the reconciliation between the lost-cargo totals and the freight manifests, she stopped him with a raised hand.

"Say it plain," she said. "For me. No numbers."

"They called the men cargo," Tomas said. "They shipped them upriver to the mine town when the water was high, right after payday, so they wouldn't have to pay them again. And then they wrote them off as drowned. Officially. In reports the government accepted. Sixteen hundred and forty men, Marisol. Drowned on paper while they were alive in the tunnels."

Marisol was quiet for a while. She set the bread down on the table with exaggerated care, as though the table might be grieving too.

"Rafael's grandmother," she said finally. "The burned name. Ekwueme showed us."

"1912 crew list. Third quarter." Tomas touched the wall. "There's a shipment here. October 14th, 1912. Forty men, freight paid, high water. If her grandmother went up with that shipment—" He stopped. He had been avoiding the next sentence all night. "If she went up, she wasn't lost at sea. She was delivered."

* * *

He should have stopped there. A good scientist stops at what the data supports. But Tomas Reyes had been fired for stopping where the data supported and the ministry wanted otherwise, and somewhere in the wreckage of that career he had learned that the data does not care about your comfort, and neither, apparently, did he.

Because there was a second pattern in the pages, subtler, and once he saw it he could not unsee it.

The shipments didn't stop in 1914 when the payroll abstracts ended. They changed form. He found it in the maintenance requisitions Rafael had brought up in the second strongbox—requisitions for the upper valley road, for the reservoir gates, for "population management infrastructure" at a site whose name had been struck through with such violence the pen had torn the page. And the dates of those requisitions marched forward decade by decade, through the concession's whole history, right up to within living memory. Right up to the era when the navy began quietly forbidding anyone to name the wreck offshore. Right up to the era when inspectors filed identical closure memos and went home to comfortable pensions.

The erasure wasn't history. It was a practice. It had simply gotten better at hiding itself, the way water gets better at finding cracks.

Tomas spread the requisitions across the table and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, why the cave had been sealed, why the valley was forbidden, why a salvage diver who asked no questions had found an archive that should not exist sitting behind a rock face. The Meridian Concession had never stopped sending people upriver. It had only stopped calling them cargo. Now it called them nothing at all.

And he knew the river. God help him, he knew the river better than any living soul. He had surveyed every reach of it, modeled every flood pulse, and he knew exactly how much water moved through the upper valley, and where it went, and—this was the thought that lifted him out of his chair at three in the morning and sent him to the charts—

He knew where the mine town's drainage went.

Every drop of water that fell on the upper valley, every spring that fed the flooded workings, every seep through the abandoned galleries—it all came down one channel, through the limestone, into the coast. Into the cliffs. Into a cave mouth that an earthquake tide had cracked open like a letter long undelivered.

The river had been carrying the town's truth downhill for eighty years. All anyone had ever had to do was follow it.

* * *

By the fourth evening he had built the case the way he'd once built flood models: layered, redundant, defensible. Gauge correlations. Manifest reconciliations. Draft calculations proving the barges could only make the upper passage at high water, which matched the shipment dates to the day. A timeline overlaying payroll cycles, water levels, and requisition approvals. He drew it all onto a single chart—the kind of chart a ministry man could read in ninety seconds—and beneath it, in his smallest, most careful hand, he wrote the sentence he had been circling for days:

**THE PERSONS RECORDED AS CARGO LOST IN TRANSIT WERE ALIVE AT THE TIME OF SHIPMENT AND WERE CONVEYED TO THE UPPER VALLEY WORKINGS BY CONCESSION VESSEL. THE LOSSES WERE ACCOUNTING EVENTS, NOT ACCIDENTS.**

Accounting events. He stared at the phrase. It was the most damning thing he had ever written and it sounded like a footnote, and that was exactly what made it damning. The concession had hidden murder inside bookkeeping, and so the proof of murder lived in bookkeeping, and a bookkeeper's precision—not a gun, not a mob, not a hero's sword—was what would drag it into daylight.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking and found, to his surprise, that he wasn't afraid. For eleven years he had been afraid—of the ministry, of the concession's quiet letters, of ending up a cautionary tale told over drinks. But fear, he discovered now, required uncertainty, and he had none left. The ice had held under every test. Forty-one for forty-one. One thousand six hundred and forty for one thousand six hundred and forty.

Somewhere in the dark outside, the river breathed.

* * *

Rafael came at dusk, alone, hood up, moving through his gate like water finding a crack. She looked at the walls, at the chart, at the sentence at the bottom, and she read it twice. Then she read it aloud, slowly, the way you test whether a load-bearing beam will take your weight.

"Accounting events," she said.

"Not accidents." He handed her the manifest for October 1912. "Third quarter. High water. Forty men, freight paid." He watched her understand, watched it land in her the way it had landed in him—not as information but as inheritance. "If your grandmother's name is on the full crew list for that shipment, Rafael, then she stood on a barge. She was counted. Someone wrote *forty* in a column and closed the book on her while she was breathing."

Rafael's jaw worked. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way that cost something. "Then we go get the rest of the book."

"The master ledgers are upriver. At the source." He tapped the chart, the blue thread of the river running from the forbidden valley down through the limestone to the sea. "Everything drains from there. Everything points there. The water's been trying to tell us for eighty years—we just never learned to read it."

She looked at the chart a long moment. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere among them, he knew, patrol boats were idling, and a man named Meridian-Vale was doing arithmetic of his own.

"Tomas," Rafael said quietly, "you understand what this makes us."

"It makes us correct," he said. "I've waited eleven years to be correct about something that mattered."

She almost smiled. Then she folded the October manifest into her jacket, over her heart, like a letter from the dead, and said the words that turned his scholarship into a voyage:

"Pack your gauges. We leave with the next high water."
