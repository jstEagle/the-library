## 14. Upstream

We left before the harbor woke, which was the only way we'd ever left anything.

Marisol had the skiff loaded by lamplight—fuel drums lashed amidships, the waterproof document tubes strapped under the thwart where a customs man's boot wouldn't find them, and a coil of rope old enough that I'd half expected it to protest. She worked in silence, the way she did everything that mattered, her hands moving with the economy of sixty years of boats. When I came down the ramp she didn't look up.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm on time. You're early."

"Same thing when the tide's involved." She cinched the last knot and finally glanced at me, and in the gray light I saw she'd packed like a woman who intended to come back. That mattered more than she'd ever say out loud. "Tomas says the river gauge at Kiln Bend reads low. We'll scrape through the narrows if we're careful."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we'll be honest with ourselves about how much of this plan was ever going to work." She handed me the thermos of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. "Drink. You look like a woman about to do something stupid."

"I am."

"Then drink faster."

Ekwueme arrived next, carrying his satchel of papers against his chest like a breviary, and behind him came Tomas Reyes, who had never in his life been on time for anything except disaster. He was pale, overcaffeinated, and clutching a rolled chart the way other men clutched weapons.

"The checkpoints start at the old toll house," he said, unrolling it across Marisol's bait table without asking. "Two of them. The first is concession security wearing navy surplus—their paperwork won't say so, but their rifles will. The second is past the gorge, where the river narrows enough that they can run a chain across it."

"A chain," Marisol said flatly.

"Since the earthquake. They've been building it for weeks. Meridian-Vale doesn't want people going upstream. He wants people to *forget* there's an upstream."

I looked at the chart. The river ran up through country I'd spent my whole life pretending wasn't there—the leeward coast's blind spot, the valley every chart marked with a surveyor's polite fiction: *elevation data unavailable*. Forty years of maps agreeing not to look. Somewhere at the top of all that blank paper was a drowned town, a church spire, and a vault with my grandmother's name in it, written in ink by men who thought ink was the same as truth.

"We go through the gorge at night," I said.

"Through the chain?" Ekwueme asked gently. "Rafael, my friend, I have read your salvage reports. They are works of art. But even art has limits."

"Not through it. Under it." I tapped the narrows. "The chain will sit at the surface. It always does—they want boats stopped, not divers caught. Two of us go under with a line, rig it to the winch on the far bank, and we pull the skiff under and across."

Marisol stared at me. Then she laughed—one short bark, no humor in it, all respect. "You want to tow a boat underwater through a gorge at night."

"Yes."

"That's the stupidest thing you've ever proposed."

"It's the only thing that keeps us off their radio."

She looked at the chart a long moment, then at each of us in turn—Ekwueme with his papers, Tomas with his gauges, me with my coffee—and something settled in her face that I'd seen maybe three times in ten years. It was the look of a woman deciding a risk was worth becoming hers.

"Fine," she said. "But I drive. Nobody tows my boat but me."

* * *

The first checkpoint nearly ended us anyway, and it hadn't even been trying.

The old toll house sat on a bend where the river slowed and widened, and its floodlight found us a full kilometer before we reached it—a white cone sweeping the water like a lighthouse with a grudge. Marisol killed the engine and we drifted, oars out, playing at being fishermen in the dark, which would have been more convincing if fishermen carried four hundred pounds of archival documents in sealed tubes.

A boat came off the dock: aluminum hull, outboard muffled, two men in jackets cut from navy patterns. One held a spotlight; the other held a clipboard, which frightened me more. Rifles you can outrun. Paperwork follows you home.

"Fishing license," the clipboard said, in the bored voice of a man who asks because he must, not because he cares.

Marisol produced one from the tackle box without blinking. I have watched her lie to harbormasters, insurance adjusters, and once, memorably, a bishop, and she does it with the serene confidence of someone who believes the universe owes her a small number of successful crimes per decade.

"Where are you headed?"

"Up to the weir pools. Bass run at night this time of year."

The spotlight swung across our faces, lingered on the tarp amidships, moved on. The clipboard man wrote something. Then Tomas—God help us, Tomas—leaned forward and said, conversationally, "You know the flow rate below the gorge is down eleven percent from seasonal mean? If you're running patrols past the narrows, you should log that. It affects hull clearance."

There was a silence with weight to it.

The spotlight man looked at Tomas. Tomas looked back with the guileless expression of a hydrologist who has never once considered that facts can be incriminating. And the clipboard man—because the world is occasionally kind to fools—apparently decided that anyone who volunteered river statistics at a checkpoint could not possibly be smuggling anything worth having.

"Move along," he said. "Don't stop between here and the gorge. There's debris in the water."

"There is indeed," Tomas agreed warmly. "Mostly from the upper valley. Fascinating composition. Very old timber."

Marisol rowed us out of the light with her jaw set like a woman physically restraining herself from violence. When we were around the bend and the floodlight was a rumor behind us, she turned in the stern and looked at Tomas for a long moment.

"You beautiful idiot," she said. "You almost killed us."

"He seemed interested in the data."

"He was *armed*."

"So was the data, effectively—" Tomas began, and Ekwueme put a hand on both their shoulders like a priest separating dogs, and I laughed until my ribs hurt, quiet and helpless, hunched over the gunwale in the dark. It occurred to me somewhere in the middle of laughing that I couldn't remember the last time I'd laughed on a boat. Ten years of solo dives and empty harbors, and here I was, giggling like a girl while a defrocked archivist mediated a theological dispute between a deckhand and a scientist.

This, I thought. This is what I traded invisibility for. God, let it be worth it.

* * *

We lay up through the daylight hours in a side slough under deadfall, the skiff camouflaged with netting Marisol had rigged from actual fishing net, because she believed in hiding things in plain sight and also believed camouflage was a moral discipline. Ekwueme spread his documents on a tarp and we worked by shaded lantern, cross-checking payroll dates against Tomas's gauge records, building the timeline we'd need when we reached the town.

It was during those hours, with the river muttering past and the sun filtering green through the canopy, that Ekwueme told us about the spire.

"My grandmother was a girl when the Concession still ran trucks up that road," he said. He spoke the way he always did, evenly, as though reciting scripture he'd personally fact-checked. "She remembered the church bell. The company rang it for shift changes, and the whole valley could hear it. When the dam went in and the water rose, the bell kept ringing for a while—automated, on a clockwork timer, because nobody had told it the town was gone." He adjusted his glasses. "Fishermen downstream swore they heard it under the water. For years. The company put out a notice saying it was impossible. Acoustic phenomena. Gas pockets."

"Was it?" Tomas asked.

"Of course it was. Bells don't ring underwater." Ekwueme smiled faintly. "But my grandmother said something I've never forgotten. She said: *they can drown a town, but they cannot make the water forget what shape it was.*"

Nobody spoke for a while after that. Marisol checked her knots. Tomas made notes. I lay back against the gunwale and listened to the river, and tried to imagine the sound of a bell ringing beneath forty feet of water, calling shifts for a crew that had been written off as cargo.

That night we ran the gorge.

* * *

The chain was exactly where Tomas said it would be, strung bank to bank across the narrows, its links just breaking the surface in the moonless dark, a black seam stitched across black water. On the far bank a hut glowed with a single lamp, and I could see the silhouette of a watchman moving inside—close enough that we could hear his radio murmuring static and half-heard voices.

Marisol brought us in below the bend, engine off, drifting on the current she'd timed to the minute. We rigged the tow line in whispers: I took the bow line, Tomas took the stern, and we slipped over the side into water cold enough to feel like a decision being made.

Underwater, the gorge was a cathedral with the lights kicked out. The chain hung above us like a rosary, each link furred with weed, swaying slightly in the current. Below it the channel dropped away into a throat of rock, and the skiff's keel passed overhead—Marisol sculling her down, keeping her flat, keeping her quiet—with a line in my fist and my whole life's luck riding on a knot.

Halfway across, the current grabbed the stern line and tore it toward the rocks, and Tomas went with it. I felt the jolt through the bow line, saw his fins flash sideways in the murk, heard nothing—which was worse than hearing something. I let myself sink, planted my boots on a ledge, and hauled. The line came. He came with it, coughing into his regulator, one glove gone, and we surfaced together in the eddy below the chain with the skiff settling beside us like a tired animal.

In the hut, the lamp went out. A door opened. A flashlight beam swept the water above the chain, found nothing, and swung away.

Marisol pulled us over the gunwale by our collars, one at a time, like kittens. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Tomas's missing glove, then at me, then at the dark gorge ahead, and started the engine at the lowest idle I'd ever heard from her, a sound less like a motor than like breathing.

We ran the rest of the narrows with the current, lights dead, the walls rising on either side close enough to touch, and somewhere in that mile of black stone I understood what Ekwueme had been building all along with his bad coffee and his patient stories. Not a crew. A practice. You learn to trust the person on the line beside you the way you learn a prayer—not by deciding to, but by repeating it until it's the only thing holding you up.

* * *

Dawn found us in the wide water above the gorge, and the valley opened around us like a held breath finally let out.

The charts called it blank. The charts lied. Terraced slopes fell away to the riverbank, overgrown now but still legible—old orchard lines, a roadbed swallowed by alder, fence posts standing in the shallows like a congregation waiting for a service that never resumed. And ahead, where the reservoir began, the water stretched flat and pewter-colored to the foot of the dam, and breaking its surface near the center, black against the brightening sky:

The spire.

It leaned, weathered to bare timber, its bell gone from the belfry—or so it appeared from the water—but unmistakable, patient, absurd. A church steeple standing in the middle of a lake, forty years after the town under it had been officially declared uninhabited, unrecorded, and unmissed.

Ekwueme stood at the bow with his hat in his hands. Tomas was already shooting bearings, muttering depths. Marisol cut the engine and let us drift, and the silence that came down over the water was so complete I could hear my own pulse.

"That's her street," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound behaves differently when it matters. "Down there. Somewhere under us. She walked it every day for six years before they sent her down the mine."

"We'll find the vault," Marisol said quietly. It wasn't reassurance. It was a promise, delivered in the tone she used for knots and fuel levels—things she intended to make true.

And then Tomas, who had been scanning the shoreline with binoculars, went very still.

"Rafael," he said. "Smoke."

I took the glasses. Three kilometers back, on the roadbed we'd followed all night, a thin gray thread stood up against the trees. Campfire smoke. Deliberate. Visible. And below it, half-hidden in the alder, the sun caught something that flashed once and went dark—an aluminum hull, beached and waiting.

They hadn't stopped us at the chain because stopping us there would have made noise, paperwork, witnesses.

They'd let us through.

Meridian-Vale wanted us to lead him to the source. We hadn't escaped the gorge. We'd been *herded* through it, all the way to the one place in the world where he could seal the gates behind us and call it an accident of hydrology.

Marisol saw it a heartbeat after I did. Her hand found the throttle.

"How fast can you get us to the vault?" I asked.

"In this water, with this load?" She was already turning the bow toward the spire. "Pray faster than that."
