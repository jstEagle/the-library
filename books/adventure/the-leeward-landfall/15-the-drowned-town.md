## 15. The Drowned Town

The river stopped pretending to be a river an hour before dawn.

For three days it had carried us—first wide and brown and lazy through the lowland cane, then quickening into white water where Tomas's charts said the old company weirs still stood, then narrowing into a gorge so tight the sky became a green ribbon overhead. Marisol ran the skiff like she'd been born in it, reading the water the way other people read faces, and I sat in the bow with the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against my chest and watched the valley close around us like a fist.

Now the current simply quit. The water went glass-still and deep green, and the banks fell away on both sides into flooded forest—dead trees standing to their shoulders in the reservoir, their branches bare and silvered, reaching up like drowned men who had given up struggling. The silence was total. Not quiet—silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your eardrums.

"San Cristóbal Reservoir," Tomas said quietly from behind me. He hadn't slept. None of us had, not really, but he looked like a man who had been awake for years and only now arrived somewhere. "Built in 1911. Official purpose: hydroelectric generation for the coastal towns." He paused. "Unofficial purpose, if you read the concession's own engineering reports the way I've been reading them for twenty years: to put a town under water."

The church spire broke the surface two hundred meters ahead.

It was all that showed—a stone spire, maybe four meters of it, rising out of the flat green water with its cross long since snapped away, leaving a stub of masonry furred with pale algae. Around it, invisible beneath us, lay streets. A plaza. Houses with their roofs still on. A schoolhouse. A jail. And below everything, at the bottom of the deepest cut of the old river channel, the mine works themselves—the reason any of it had ever existed, and the reason none of it appeared on any map printed after 1913.

I had grown up on stories about this place. My grandmother used to sing a song about a town with a church bell that rang by itself, and I'd thought it was just a song, the way children think songs are just songs. She never said the name. Nobody said the name. That was the whole point of the Meridian Concession—you didn't say names, you didn't keep records, you didn't leave anything behind that could be subpoenaed. You erased, and you called it mercy, and you built a reservoir over the proof.

"Rafael." Marisol's hand on my shoulder. I realized I'd been staring at the spire without breathing. "We should move. Light comes up fast in the gorge."

She was right. We'd run the last rapids in the dark specifically so we'd arrive before Meridian-Vale's boats—and before whatever remained of the navy's patrol presence in the upper valley noticed us. Ekwueme had stayed downstream with the second skiff and most of our supplies, anchored in the dead trees where no aircraft would spot him, keeping watch on the river with a pair of binoculars older than I was and a calm that I suspected was load-bearing for all of us.

We tied off under an overhang where the waterline met raw rock, and I started kitting up, and my hands were shaking.

Not from cold. From something else. I'd dived wrecks for eleven years. I'd gone down into hulls full of silt and darkness and things that used to be people, and my hands had never shaken. But this wasn't a wreck. Wrecks are accidents. This was a decision somebody made—to flood a town, to drown a street where children had walked to school, because the alternative was letting the world read what the company had written down.

"Hey." Marisol crouched next to me, cinching my tank straps herself, the way she did when she thought I was too proud to ask. Her forearm was still taped where the security boat's rail had caught her, and she moved the arm carefully, but she was here anyway. She'd refused to stay downstream with Ekwueme. "Whatever's down there," she said, "you don't have to carry it alone. That's the whole point of a crew."

"I know," I said.

"You don't. But you will." She tapped my mask twice, the old deckhand's blessing, and pushed me gently toward the edge.

* * *

The water closed over me and the world changed temperature first, then color, then sound.

Fifteen meters down, the dead forest gave way to rooftops.

I had prepared myself for it—I'd told myself I was prepared—but nothing prepares you for a street. The reservoir bottom sloped gently, and where the old shoreline had been, the town simply began: a cobbled road running straight as a sermon between stone houses, their tile roofs mostly intact, their doors standing open. Silt had drifted in like snow over eleven decades, softening every edge, filling every room to the windowsills. A cart stood in the middle of the road, its wooden bed rotted to lace, its iron rims still round. A child's hoop lay against a doorstep.

Tomas descended beside me, his torch beam sweeping slow arcs. He'd insisted on coming down despite being the worst swimmer among us; he'd spent six months in a decompression chamber once, he said, and considered himself paid up on fear for one lifetime. He hovered above the road like a man afraid to step on the floor of a cathedral.

Beside a house with a blue door—still blue, impossibly, some mineral in the paint or the water—he found a nameplate. He brushed the silt away with two fingers, gentle as touching a sleeping face, and held his light to it.

*OCAMPO.*

He looked at me across the water. Neither of us spoke. There was nothing to say that the nameplate wasn't already saying. Someone had lived here. Someone had hung their name on their door because they believed they would keep living here, that their name meant something, that it would still be there tomorrow. Then men with survey instruments came, and the gates closed, and the river rose, and the name stayed on the door at the bottom of a lake while every record of Ocampo was burned or rewritten or filed under *lost cargo*.

My grandmother's song came back to me whole, there in the green dark, every verse. The bell that rang by itself. The road that went down to the water. I had thought it was a lullaby. It was a map. It was a memory passed hand to hand by women who weren't allowed to write anything down, sung to children who weren't told why, until the why wore off and only the melody survived.

I swam on.

* * *

The church was the anchor point. Every document we'd recovered from the cave strongboxes agreed on that much: the master ledger vault lay beneath the church, in what the concession's own correspondence called *the crypt annex*, a reinforced chamber built into the bedrock when the church was raised in 1898. The company had kept its true books there—payrolls, manifests, testimony transcripts, the complete machinery of erasure—because the safest place to hide a thing, Cyrus Meridian-Vale's grandfather had written in a letter we'd found sealed in strongbox nine, is inside the thing everyone is paid not to look at.

The church doors were open. Of course they were. They'd been open since 1912.

Inside, the water was darker, thick with suspended silt that turned our torch beams into solid columns. Pews stood in rows, furred grey. The altar was stripped. And behind it, exactly where the documents said, a stairway led down—stone steps descending past a rusted iron gate hanging half off its hinges, into the bedrock itself.

Marisol took point. She was better in confined spaces than any of us; she'd grown up diving the ballast tanks of freighters, she said, where the trick was to stop thinking of the space as small and start thinking of yourself as large. The stairway was narrow, black, and lined with niches—burial niches, most empty, some still holding the shapes of coffins that had never been moved when the flooding order came. The company hadn't even exhumed its own dead. Why would it? Dead men don't testify.

At the bottom, the gate opened onto the vault.

It was smaller than I'd imagined. A single chamber, maybe eight meters square, hewn from living rock, its walls lined floor to ceiling with steel shelving. And on the shelves—hundreds of them. Thousands. Ledgers. Boxes of files. Bundles of paper wrapped in waxed canvas and tarred string, stacked with the obsessive precision of men who knew exactly how damning every page was and intended to keep it anyway, because power keeps receipts even when it burns everyone else's.

Tomas made a sound through his regulator that might have been a laugh or a sob.

"They kept everything," he said, and his voice broke apart in the water into bubbles. "They kept *everything*. All those memos saying no records exist—all these are the records that don't exist."

I pulled the first bundle from the shelf nearest the door. The canvas wrapping crumbled at my touch, but the paper inside held—cold, stiff, faintly yellowed, but legible. The crest of the Meridian Concession stamped in faded violet ink. A date: March 1909. A heading in a clerk's careful hand:

*Register of Personnel Assigned to Upper Works — Disposition: Cargo Loss.*

Cargo loss. I turned pages with hands that had finally stopped shaking, because fury is steadier than fear. Page after page. Names. Dates of assignment to the mine. Dates of disposition. And in the margin of nearly every entry, in different hands across different years, the same notation, repeated like a litany:

*No recovery attempted. Family notified per policy. File closed.*

Per policy. My grandmother's brother had gone into the Upper Works in 1910, according to the family stories that weren't stories anymore. He was nineteen. The file would be here. His name would be here, in a clerk's violet ink, marked *lost cargo*, and somewhere in this same vault there would be a memo explaining that the mine face had collapsed and that recovery was deemed uneconomical, and somewhere else a letter confirming that the compensation paid to the family was calculated at the cargo rate rather than the crew rate, because crew required paperwork and cargo required only a receipt.

They had written it all down. That was the thing I hadn't understood until I stood in the water in the dark and touched the pages. They had written down every crime, every cover-up, every erased man, and locked it in a hole in the rock under a drowned church, because the one thing power cannot do is forget what it's owed. Erasure was never forgetting. It was filing.

"Rafael." Marisol's light found me. She pointed upward, toward the stairway, and made the signal we'd agreed on—flat palm, slow press. *Time.* We had maybe forty minutes of bottom time left before the ascent got dangerous, and the reservoir was deep enough and cold enough that every minute cost.

I looked at the shelves. Thousands of bundles. We had four waterproof cases and two dive bags.

"We take the payrolls," I said. "All years. And the disposition registers. And anything with testimony in it." I'd seen the word *testimony* on one index card already, in a drawer labeled 1912–1913, and my heart had done something complicated in my chest. "Tomas—you're the archivist. You triage. Marisol, packing. I'll pull."

We worked. There's a particular silence that falls over people doing urgent work in a sacred place, and we found it fast. Tomas moved along the shelves reading spines, pulling, passing. Marisol packed with a deckhand's economy, wedging each case full, sealing the gaskets, testing each latch twice. And I pulled bundles from the steel shelves of a vault at the bottom of a lake covering a town that officially never existed, and every bundle I lifted, I held for one second longer than efficiency required.

Because somewhere in these shelves was her.

I'd known it since the cave. Since Father Ekwueme laid the burned crew list on his table and I saw the gap in the line of names and felt the shape of it fit something I'd carried my whole life without knowing what it was. *Inés Thornbury,* the shape said. *Born 1889. Entered the Upper Works April 1911. Disposition—*

But the vault's index, nailed to the wall by the door, told me something the cave list couldn't. The 1912–1913 drawer. Testimony. Which meant someone had talked. Someone had come out of the mine and spoken, and the company had written down what they said before silencing them—which meant that somewhere in this room was not just a record of my grandmother's death, but possibly a record of her voice.

Her voice. Eleven decades at the bottom of the water, and her voice might be three meters to my left, in a folder, in violet ink.

"Rafael." Tomas again, urgent now, holding up a bundle. "1911 personnel register. Full year. It's here—it has to be here—"

"Not yet." My own voice sounded strange to me, flattened by water and resolve. "Testimony first. Then registers. If we lose everything else, we don't lose that."

He stared at me for a moment, and then he nodded, because he understood something I hadn't said out loud: that the registers proved the crime, but the testimony proved the person. And the person was the point. It had always been the point. Cyrus Meridian-Vale thought this vault was his inheritance. He thought the ledgers were leverage, insurance, the family's private account of its own sins, to be traded or destroyed as legacy required. He had never understood that the papers weren't his at all. They belonged to the named. To Ocampo behind his blue door. To the boy in the coffin niche. To Inés.

We filled the cases. Four of them, heavy as sin, strapped to our rigs. On the last pass, with our bottom time bleeding out and Marisol tapping her wrist, I took one more bundle—not for the archive, not for Okonkwo, not for the record. For me. A single folder from the 1912–1913 testimony drawer, thin, its index tab reading *THORNBURY, I. — Statement, re: Upper Works conditions — taken 14 June 1912.*

Fourteen days later, according to the register, the file was closed. Disposition: cargo loss.

I tucked it against my chest, inside my suit, where the oilcloth-wrapped ledgers usually rode, and I kicked for the stairs, and above me the church waited with its doors open, and beyond the church the drowned street ran straight through the drowned town toward the drowned world, and I swam up through all of it carrying my grandmother's voice like a lamp.

* * *

We surfaced in grey dawn light under the overhang, and the reservoir had gone from green to pewter, and somewhere far downstream—too far to see, not too far to hear—an engine was working hard against the current.

Marisol heard it first. She always did. She froze with her regulator halfway out of her mouth, head cocked, and then she was moving, hauling cases, no discussion. "Boat. Big. Coming upriver."

Tomas, gasping on the rocks, already calculating: "Nothing legal runs this stretch. The navigation permit ends at the weir. Whoever's burning fuel at five in the morning to come up here—"

"—is either us," Marisol finished, dragging the fourth case up the shelf, "or paid by the man whose grandfather built the vault we just robbed."

Ekwueme's voice crackled through the radio Marisol had rigged to the second skiff, low and unhurried as ever, which somehow made it worse: *"Two boats. Running dark. Half a kilometer below the narrows and closing. Children, I suggest you be elsewhere."*

I stood on the rock with the water streaming off me, the testimony folder pressed flat against my ribs under my suit, and looked back at the reservoir. The spire stood in the pewter light, patient as it had been for a hundred years. Somewhere beneath it, the vault door stood open for the first time since 1913, and the shelves stood half-empty, and the town lay silent around its emptied heart.

Let him come, I thought. Let him see what's missing.

Then I turned and ran for the skiff, because awe is for afterward, and sorrow is for afterward, and right now the river was the only road out—and the man who owned the valley owned every road but this one, and he had just figured that out too.
