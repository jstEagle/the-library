## 18. Rising Water

The first blast came up through the soles of Rafael's boots before it reached her ears—a deep, structural cough, like the valley itself clearing its throat.

She was forty feet down in the master vault when it hit, one hand still hooked in the strap of the ledger crate, and the water around her shivered into a fine gray haze as a century of silt lifted off the drowned streets below. Through her mask she saw the church spire lean. Not fall—lean, the way an old man leans to listen.

*Tomas's voice crackled through the rebreather comm, thin and distorted:* "Gates. That was the outer gate. They're not waiting for the permits."

"How long?" Rafael asked, and hated how calm she made herself sound, because calm was a currency and Marisol had taught her to spend it only where it bought something.

"Minutes. Maybe ten. The inner gates are older—they'll go easier." A pause, static chewing at it. "Rafael. The tunnels will flood from both ends. You have to be *in* the mine gallery before the second charge, or the pressure wave will take you apart against the rock."

Ekwueme's voice joined, steady as a psalm: "The gallery runs under the town. It connects to the cave mouth—the river's own throat. God dug you an exit, child. Use it."

Rafael looked down through the green murk at the vault door, half-open, the ledgers inside stacked like sleeping bodies. Hundreds of names. Her grandmother's testimony, pages she'd touched with her own gloved hands twenty minutes ago, ink still legible after ninety years because the company had sealed them in wax and iron and never dreamed anyone would come asking.

They'd kept the names safe by hiding them. Rafael was about to keep them safe by running.

"Strapping the crates," she said. "Marisol, tell me you're in position."

* * *

Marisol was not in position. Marisol was in a fight.

The skiff sat low in the reservoir's throat where the river narrowed between black basalt walls, and fifty yards downstream the demolition barge blocked the channel like a fist. Two concession security boats flanked it, floodlights sweeping the water, and Meridian-Vale's hired crew chief stood at the rail with a megaphone he hadn't needed yet, because his meaning was already written in the crated dynamite stacked on deck.

Marisol killed the skiff's engine and let the current carry her, silent, into the shadow of the cliff. Sixty-three years old, hands like knotted rope, and she steered with two fingers the way other women prayed—with total certainty and no expectation of being answered.

"Boat coming your way," Tomas said over the radio. "Small. Fast."

"I see it." She did: a security runabout peeling off from the barge, cutting its lights, hunting for their upstream tether line. If they found the rope that anchored Rafael's dive point, they'd cut the crew off from the water entirely.

Marisol did the arithmetic she'd been doing her whole life—current speed, hull draft, the angle of the dark—and let out the anchor line three feet more. The skiff swung. The runabout's searchlight passed over water where a boat had been four seconds earlier and found nothing but rain beginning to stipple the surface.

"You old pirate," Tomas breathed.

"Save it," Marisol said. "Tell me when my girl is moving."

* * *

In the vault, Rafael cinched the last strap across the first crate and tested the lift. Forty pounds of paper and iron. She could swim it; barely. Ekwueme had wanted to carry the second crate himself, but the old man's shoulders were seventy years of sermons and bad knees, and the flooded gallery would forgive no weakness. So they'd split the load: Rafael took the master payroll and the testimony folios. Ekwueme took the crew lists—all of them, every erased name, the book of the dead that was also a book of the living.

He was already swimming toward the gallery mouth when the second blast came.

This one was closer. The water didn't shiver—it *shoved*, a flat physical hand against her chest, and Rafael heard her own regulator rattle as the pressure spike squeezed the air spaces in her skull. Somewhere above, the inner gate had died, and the reservoir began to move.

Not rise. *Move.* A slow rotation at first, the whole surface of the valley turning like a thought, then a current, definite and directional, dragging everything toward the breach in the gates. Toward the tunnels. The drowned town's dead leaves—sixty years of them, drifting like black snow—lifted off the streets and began to flow past her, all pointing the same direction, like a congregation finally told where the procession was going.

*Rafael.* Tomas's voice, stripped of all its scholarly calm. *Second gate is open. Water's coming down the upper gorge. You have maybe six minutes before the gallery floods past swimmable.*

She kicked for the gallery mouth.

* * *

The mine tunnel swallowed them the way throats swallow.

It had been dry once—she could feel its history in the walls, the pick-marks, the rusted ore-cart rails running along the floor, the timber supports furred white with mineral bloom. Now the river was reclaiming it from both ends, water thigh-deep and rising, cold enough to make her gasp even through the neoprene. Ahead of her, Ekwueme's dive light bobbed, the old man hauling his crate along the rails where the cart path gave him footing, murmuring something she couldn't hear over the roar building behind them.

The gallery ran nearly straight for eight hundred meters, then bent left toward the sea cave—toward the leeward coast, toward daylight, toward the harbor where this whole story had started with a tide that lied. Eight hundred meters. At a walk, four minutes. Against rising water, carrying forty pounds—

Behind them, the tunnel exhaled.

Rafael felt it before she understood it: a change in the air pressure, a wet wind at her back, and then the sound—not an explosion but a *arrival*, the flood coming around the upper bend like a train entering a station. Her light caught it for half a second: a wall of churning brown water taller than a man, folding around the curve of the tunnel, tearing a timber support loose and simply erasing it.

"*Run!*"

They ran. There was no strategy after that, no craft, nothing but legs and terror and the rails underfoot. Rafael's crate slammed against her spine with every stride. Ekwueme stumbled on a rail tie and she caught his arm without slowing, hauled him upright, felt his weight trust her completely for one full second—and that second, she would think later, was the truest thing in her life. Not the diving. Not the discovery. This: an old man letting her carry him, because there was no time for anything else and he knew she wouldn't drop him.

The water took their heels. Then their knees. Then it wasn't behind them anymore—it was *around* them, and running became swimming became a kind of drowning forward motion, the tunnel ceiling dropping to meet the flood until the gap between was a hand's width of screaming air.

Rafael got one breath. Got another. The crate dragged her down and she let it, used its weight to stay under the collapsing air pocket, pulled herself along the rail line hand over hand in the dark while the flood tried to fold her against the rock. Her shoulder hit a timber post. Something tore—suit, skin, she didn't check. The ledgers were strapped tight. That was the only inventory that mattered.

Ahead: a gray smudge. Faint. Growing.

Daylight. The cave mouth.

* * *

Outside, Marisol watched the cliff face and counted.

Tomas was beside her with the hydrology charts spread across the skiff's bench, rain soaking the paper, his finger tracing the gallery's course underground. "The cave should vent here," he said for the fourth time. "The river carved it. It has to vent here."

"And if it doesn't?"

Tomas didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Then the rock breathed. A gout of spray burst from a seam near the waterline—once, twice—and on the third pulse the seam became a mouth and the mouth delivered Rafael.

She came out sideways in the flood surge, crate still strapped to her back, and surfaced coughing half the reservoir out of her lungs. Ekwueme followed ten seconds later, and Tomas and Marisol were already in the water, hauling them both toward the skiff, and nobody said anything for a full minute because there were no words that fit and everyone knew better than to waste breath on ones that didn't.

Rafael lay in the bilge with the rain hitting her face and laughed—actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound—until Marisol put a hand on her forehead like she was checking a child for fever.

"Both crates," Rafael managed. "Both crates made it."

"Good," Marisol said. "Because we're not done."

She pointed downstream.

* * *

The demolition barge had moved.

While the mountain drowned its own evidence, Cyrus Meridian-Vale's crew had brought the barge up to the reservoir narrows, and now it sat directly athwart the only channel out—floodlights blazing through the rain, the two security boats riding shotgun. And on the barge's deck, visible even at this distance, men were rigging charges along the cliff base. Not the gates this time. The *cliff*. The cave mouth itself. Seal the exit, seal the archive, seal the witnesses—one stone closing three books at once.

"He's burying the entrance," Tomas said hoarsely. "With us on this side of it."

"No," Rafael said. She sat up. Her shoulder screamed. She ignored it. "He's burying the entrance with *Okonkwo* supposedly watching. Where is she? She was supposed to serve the injunction at dawn—"

"She's early," Marisol said quietly. "Look."

Downstream, around the far bend, running lights. Not one vessel. Three. A navy patrol cutter, black hull, guns fore and aft—and flanking it, absurdly, a harbor pilot's launch flying no flag at all, and behind that a flat-bottomed work barge crowded with figures in orange survival gear.

On the pilot launch's bow, alone, holding a battery lantern above her head like a torch in a story, stood Inspector Adaeze Okonkwo.

Her voice carried across the water through a loudhailer, clipped and official and absolutely furious:

"This is Inspector Okonkwo, Concessions Audit Division, warrant number 4471! By authority of the Emergency Heritage Preservation Act I am serving a preservation order on this site. All demolition is to cease immediately. Any charge detonated after this order constitutes destruction of protected evidence and obstruction of a federal audit—which is a crime, gentlemen, that does not expire with a permit!"

Silence on the barge. Rain on the water. Rafael could imagine, perfectly, the arithmetic happening behind Meridian-Vale's elegant eyes: the permits expiring at dawn anyway, the order technically void within hours, the risk of defiance weighed against the cost of obedience. He believed erasure was mercy. He would believe this was mercy too.

The megaphone on the barge crackled. A smooth voice, unhurried, almost regretful: "Inspector. You're trespassing on private concession waters. I'd invite you aboard, but the situation is becoming unsafe."

"The situation," Okonkwo replied, "became unsafe in 1912. Stand down your charges."

And then Marisol did the thing Rafael would never stop telling people about, the thing that would enter harbor legend before the month was out.

She started the skiff's engine, turned the bow toward the barge, and drove—straight at it, slow and deliberate and utterly fearless, an old woman in a leaking skiff putting herself physically between the demolition crew and the cliff face, arms crossed, daring them to light a fuse with her in the blast radius. The security boats broke formation to intercept her. She threaded between them like thread through a needle, sixty-three years of boat-craft spent in eleven seconds, and came out the other side holding position thirty meters off the barge's bow, dead center in their firing solution.

"Come on, then," she said, too quiet for anyone but Rafael to hear over the radio. "I've been invisible my whole life. Let's see them erase me with witnesses."

The cutter's searchlight snapped onto the barge. A navy ensign stepped to the rail. And on the work barge behind, Rafael saw faces she recognized—harbor families, divers, dockworkers, the widows and grandsons of erased crews who had read Tomas's charts and Ekwueme's sermons and come upriver in the rain without being asked.

The demolition crew looked at the fuse. Looked at the crowd. Looked at the guns.

The floodlights on the barge went out, one by one, like a man deciding to sit down.

* * *

They came ashore at the old company landing as the sky went from black to bruise-gray, Rafael carrying the master payroll crate on her destroyed shoulder because some things you don't let other people touch. Okonkwo met her at the top of the ramp, soaked, lantern still lit, and for a moment the two women just looked at each other—the inspector who had chosen the law over her orders, and the diver who had chosen her name over her safety.

"You're late," Rafael said.

"You're early," Okonkwo said. "Dawn's in forty minutes. The permits die at dawn." She held out her hands for the crate. "Give me the payroll. I'm going to start reading it into the record the moment the clock turns, and I intend to still be reading at noon."

Rafael looked down at the crate—her grandmother's testimony inside, the words that woman had spoken into silence a lifetime ago—and then handed it over, and the letting-go felt like surfacing.

Behind them, the reservoir settled into its new shape, and somewhere beneath the gray water the drowned town lay quieter than it had lain in ninety years, its streets empty at last of secrets.

But out on the water, at the far bend of the river, a single unlit boat was slipping away downstream—fast, low in the water, headed for the coast.

Tomas saw it too. "That's Meridian-Vale's tender."

Rafael watched it go, and did not relax. "He's not running," she said. "He's going to get ahead of the story. Dawn's in forty minutes—and so is whatever he's planning next."
