## 21. Open Water

The permit was a single sheet of paper, and it weighed more than any brass I'd ever hauled up.

RESEARCH SALVAGE LICENSE — VESSEL: *MARISOL'S PRIDE* — DIVE SITE: WRECK DESIGNATED NV-114 — ISSUED UNDER AUTHORITY OF THE MARITIME RECORDS COMMISSION. At the bottom, in ink that would not smudge and could not be denied, the signature of Inspector Adaeze Okonkwo. Below that, typed where no one had bothered to type anything for me in eleven years: *RAFAEL THORNBURY, PRINCIPAL INVESTIGATOR.*

Principal investigator. Me. The woman who had spent a decade teaching herself to leave no wake, no shadow, no name on any list. I read the permit four times on the dock before Marisol snatched it out of my hands.

"You'll wear the ink off," she said, folding it with the reverence she usually reserved for her rosary and stowing it in the chart box under the wheel. "It's real, niña. I checked the seal against three others. It's real."

"I know it's real."

"Then stop looking at it like it's going to bite you."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I busied myself with the tanks. Some habits go deeper than the wreck they were built around. For years my whole life had been organized around the principle of not being seen: dive at slack tide when patrol boats changed shifts, run dark running lights, file salvage reports so vague they described nothing but weather. I had been very good at being nobody. I had thought, for a long time, that being nobody was the same as being safe.

The valley had taught me otherwise. So had the flood. So had the ledger vault, and the names inside it, and the particular silence of my grandmother's line burned out of a 1912 crew list. Nobody was not safe. Nobody was just gone, waiting for someone to write them back.

* * *

We went out at midmorning, which would have been unthinkable a year ago. Midmorning was when the navy's harbor launches ran their sweeps, when every pair of official binoculars on the leeward coast was pointed at the water. Now the launch passed us at the channel marker and the coxswain raised two fingers off his cap—not a warning, a salute—and Marisol returned it with the easy insolence of a woman who had decided, somewhere in the last few months, that the sea belonged to her again.

Tomas Reyes sat in the bow with the site survey spread across his knees, weighting the corners with coffee mugs because we had never gotten around to buying clips. He'd grown a beard since the valley. He said it was for warmth; Ekwueme said it was because he'd stopped sleeping and wanted to look the part. Either way, he looked less like a disgraced hydrologist these days and more like what he actually was, which was the only man alive who could read the river gauges from 1911 to 1934 without flinching.

"Current's mild," he said, tapping the survey. "Two knots on the ebb, slack around noon. Visibility should hold at eight meters if the swell stays down."

"And if it doesn't?" Marisol asked.

"Then it doesn't, and we come back tomorrow." He said it simply, without the old hunger in it. That was new too. In the early days Tomas had chased the data like a man chasing a debt—every gauge reading a payment owed to the drowned. Somewhere between the flooded town and the testimony, the debt had been paid, or at least transferred to people better equipped to carry it. What remained was just work. Good work. Work done in daylight.

Father Ekwueme came up from the cabin rubbing his eyes. He still got seasick; he still came anyway. "I've brought the laminated sheets," he announced, holding up a waterproof binder like a relic. "The full roll of the erased. If we find anything down there with a name attached, I want to check it against the record immediately."

"We're diving a navy wreck, Father," Marisol said. "Not another archive."

"The sea is all one archive," Ekwueme said serenely. "It files everything."

He wasn't wrong. That was the thing about NV-114—the wreck the charts wouldn't name, the hull the navy quietly forbade anyone to salvage. We knew now what the forbidding had been for. The concession's permits had covered this stretch of coast like a lid covers a pot, and under the lid, everything simmered: the mine town, the erased crews, the payroll pages written off as lost cargo, and finally the wreck itself—a naval transport that had carried, among its ordinary freight, men who were supposed to have never existed. The navy hadn't sunk the truth. It had just declined to look for it, year after year, decade after decade, until looking became illegal by sheer inertia.

Okonkwo had ended the inertia with paperwork. Of course she had. Not dynamite, not a raid—a license. She'd told me once, over the worst coffee in the archipelago, that institutions don't surrender, they expire. Her job was to make sure the right things expired first.

* * *

The wreck rose out of the green the way it always did, sudden and enormous, a cathedral of rusted plate and open hatchways furred with soft coral. I had been diving her alone for eleven years. I knew her the way you know a house you've robbed a hundred times—which deck plates were sound, which doorways had become teeth, where the brass fittings hid in the silt like coins in a mattress.

Diving her with four other people was a different act entirely.

Marisol took the port side with me, working the lift bag on a section of hull plating we'd flagged for recovery—samples for the maritime museum's conservation lab, part of the permit's terms. Above us, Tomas and Ekwueme hung in the water column mapping the superstructure, their slates scratching. Okonkwo had insisted on a fifth diver, a young commission-trained conservator named Pell who talked too much on the surface and not at all below, and who was even now photographing everything with the patient thoroughness of someone who expected the pictures to matter.

They did matter. That was the strangest part. Every photo, every measurement, every scrap of brass we logged would go into a public record that anyone could read. The wreck's true name—*HMS Corriedale*, lost 1937 with forty-one souls, nine of them concession crewmen whose families had been told nothing for eighty-seven years—would be posted at the harbor office. The families had already begun to come. Two weeks ago an old woman had stood on the dock for an hour, just looking at our boat, before Ekwueme walked over and showed her the roll. Her grandfather's name was on it. She had cried into her sleeve, quietly, the way people cry when grief is finally allowed to be old.

I found the thing I wasn't looking for at forty minutes, wedged behind a collapsed bulkhead on the lower deck: a locker, fused shut, its face stenciled with paint so degraded I almost missed the letters. I worked my knife around the seam and the door gave with a sigh of trapped water, and inside, wrapped in tarred canvas that the sea had failed to fully digest, was a bundle of papers.

My heart did the old thing—the cold drop, the animal certainty that finding documents underwater meant danger, meant erasure, meant someone would come. I held the bundle against my chest and felt the fear move through me like current, and then, deliberately, I let it pass. Nobody was coming. Or rather: everyone was coming. That was the point now.

I signaled Marisol, and we rose together through the green light, the bundle secured in the mesh bag between us, two divers' lights converging on one small piece of the past instead of one diver hiding it from the world.

* * *

On the surface, Ekwueme laid the canvas out on the deck boards and unwrapped it with hands that shook slightly, though he'd deny it. Payroll chits. Dozens of them, waterlogged but legible, each stamped with the Meridian Concession crest and each bearing a name, a rate of pay, a date.

"Corriedale's last manifest listed them as general cargo," Tomas said quietly, reading over the priest's shoulder. "Forty-one souls. Nine unlisted. These are the nine."

Ekwueme checked each name against his laminated roll, his lips moving. On the fourth chit he stopped.

"Here." He held it up. "Amara Thornbury. Deckhand, second class. Embarked for the upper valley run, March 1937."

The wind moved across the water and nobody said anything for a moment. Marisol put her hand flat on my shoulder blade, between the shoulder bones, where a person carries weight they won't show on their face.

"She was twenty-two," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "She survived the mine. She testified. And then they put her on a ship and sank the ship, or let it sink, or simply didn't care enough to keep it floating. And then they wrote her out of the passenger list, and out of the payroll, and out of the parish register, and out of the ground itself. Eighty-seven years of not existing."

"And now?" Ekwueme asked gently.

"Now she exists." I took the chit from him. The paper was soft as cloth, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, but the letters held. Amara Thornbury. Somebody had written those letters with a pen, in an office, on a day in March, expecting them to matter. They mattered now. "That's the whole job, isn't it? Everything else—the permits, the trial, Meridian-Vale rotting in his white-collar cell—it's all scaffolding. The job is that she exists."

Pell photographed the chit from six angles. Tomas logged the recovery coordinates. Marisol wrote the entry in the dive ledger in her big square hand, and the ledger itself would be scanned and filed with the commission within the week. The machinery that had once erased her grandmother was now, gear by gear, being turned to the opposite purpose. It was strange to watch it work. It was stranger to trust it.

* * *

We surfaced from the final dive of the day into gold light, the sun low over the leeward hills, the water so calm it seemed embarrassed by its own reputation. On the swim ladder I paused, treading, and looked back at the boat: five figures against the sky, hauling tanks, arguing cheerfully about decompression tables, Ekwueme already seasick and already refusing to admit it. My family. Found, not given. Kept by practice, not by blood.

For eleven years I had surfaced to an empty harbor. Empty dock, cold lamp, the specific loneliness of a woman who had made herself unfindable and then wondered why no one found her. Tonight the harbor would be full—there'd be fish stew at Marisol's table, and the conservator's questions, and tomorrow's schedule argued over like weather. And next week, the families. More of them every week, coming to the harbor office to read the restored rolls, to find their burned-out lines rewritten in permanent ink.

The wilderness still forgave nothing. The reef would still cut a careless hand, the current would still take a careless diver, the swell would still lie about itself the way it had lied the day the cave opened. The sea had not softened. But standing on the deck with salt drying on my arms and my grandmother's pay chit sealed in a preservation case, I understood something I'd had backwards my whole life: I had never needed the sea's forgiveness. I had needed the right to stand on the water without apology, and that right had never belonged to the sea at all. It belonged to the record. To the names. To us.

Marisol fired the engine and we turned for home across the brightening water, and behind us the *Corriedale* settled back into her long keeping, no longer a secret, no longer a wound—just a grave, tended at last, holding her dead in the open light of a coast that finally knew their names.

Halfway to the harbor, Pell came up from the cabin holding the radio handset, her face doing something complicated.

"Rafael," she said. "Commission's on the line. There's a diver at the cave mouth—says he's found a second strongbox, one the ledgers never mentioned. Says it's addressed to you."
