## 1. The Bottom of the Ledger

The river gave her forty minutes of light and she intended to use every one of them.

Odile Dravenmoor went over the side of the *Kestrel's Debt* at first gray, before the sun had cleared the eastern bluffs and turned the water to hammered tin. The cold came up through her suit in a slow, patient wave, the way it always did — not a shock anymore but a greeting, an old acquaintance putting its hand on the back of her neck. She checked her gauge, checked her line, checked the signal she'd tied off to the rail: three tugs meant *coming up*, two meant *trouble*, one long pull meant *leave me alone*. Bo was on watch above, seventeen years old and half-asleep, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tar and woodsmoke. Sefa stood beside him at the rail, hands loose at her sides, feeling the boat.

Odile let herself sink.

The Vesperine lay on her starboard side in sixty feet of brown-green water, a ship the length of a church street, and the river had been eating her for eleven years with the unhurried appetite of something that knew it would win. Silt had built itself into dunes along her upturned hull. Mussels had colonized her rails in black ridges. A forest of soft coral — the pale, ghostly kind that only grew where the current ran slow — fanned out from her scuppers like the ship had exhaled once, long ago, and never drawn breath again.

Odile swam the outer hull the way an accountant walks a ledger line. Methodical. Left to right. No skipping.

She'd started the ledger four years ago, the first season she'd found the wreck, back when finding it had still felt like luck instead of obligation. A waterproof slate, a grease pencil, and a system of her own devising: hull station numbers, plating condition, rivet loss, silt load, anything anomalous. She'd filled nine slates since. The navy had charts of this stretch of river — she'd seen them, or the sanitized versions sold to licensed salvors — and on those charts the Vesperine appeared as a blank. An unmarked shoal. A hazard to navigation, nothing more. No name. No date. No note about the three hundred souls who had, according to no official record whatsoever, gone down with her.

*The navy won't discuss it.* That was the phrase Tam used later, but Odile had learned it first, in her own way, years before she'd ever met a disgraced cartographer. She'd learned it from a harbormaster who went quiet mid-sentence. From a records clerk who pulled a file, looked inside, closed it, and told her the file didn't exist. From the way licensed fleet captains — men who would gossip about a drowned dog — changed the subject when she mentioned the wreck with the strange lines and the seal on her stern fitting.

So Odile discussed it with herself, underwater, in grease pencil, forty minutes at a time.

She started aft. The stern section was the best preserved, buried to the counter in silt, and she worked along it slowly, trailing her gloved fingers over plating that had been rolled in some mill that officially never existed. The Vesperine hadn't been built like river traffic. River ships were shallow-bellied things, all draft and compromise. This hull had a deep keel, a sheer that swept up like a gull's wing, and plating laid in overlapping courses that Odile had only ever seen in drawings — drawings in books about Calveth, the drowned nation, the country that had been a country right up until the morning it wasn't.

Everyone knew the story the way everyone knows a scar they didn't earn. Calveth had sat in the river delta where three waters met, a city of canals and granite quays, wealthy from salvage and shipping and the peculiar trade of knowing what lay at the bottom of things. And then one spring, eleven years ago, the rivers had risen together — all three at once, against every pattern the rain gauges showed — and the levees had failed, and the city had gone under in a single night, and the government that remained had declared the site a hazard zone and forbidden salvage, settlement, even the speaking of the name in official correspondence. *The Calveth Exclusion.* People still lived around its edges. They just didn't say the word.

The navy said the flooding was an act of God. The navy said a lot of things.

Odile reached the stern fitting at minute twelve. It was brass, green-black with age, a mooring fairlead the size of her torso, bolted through plating that the river hadn't yet pried loose. She'd passed it a hundred times. She'd catalogued it twice: *fairlead, stern port, intact, heavy marine growth.*

Today she stopped.

The growth on its face had begun to slough — winter currents, maybe, or just time working in her favor for once — and beneath the black fringe of weed, catching her lamp's beam, was a stamped device. She scraped at it gently with her thumbnail through the glove, then with the flat of her knife held sideways, careful as a surgeon. Silt clouded and drifted. She fanned it away with her hand and waited, kneeling on the hull in the brown murk, for the water to clear.

A seal. Circular, two inches across, struck deep into the brass so that even the river's slow abrasion hadn't erased it: a walled city seen from above, streets radiating from a central harbor like the spokes of a wheel, and around the rim, in letters worn nearly smooth, a word she could read by shape if not by shine. She knew the word. Everyone who'd grown up within a hundred miles of the exclusion zone knew the word, from smugglers' tattoos and old coins and the songs grandmothers sang too quietly.

*Calveth.*

Not a merchant's mark. Not a shipping line. The state seal of a nation that officially did not exist, bolted to the hull of a ship that officially was not there, sunk in a flood that officially was nobody's fault.

Odile knelt there longer than the ledger required. Her air ticked down. Above her, faint as a heartbeat through sixty feet of water, she felt the *Kestrel's Debt* shift on her anchor — Sefa moving, probably, reading some change in the current that Odile couldn't see yet. The light was strengthening. Shafts of gold were beginning to come down through the surface in long slanted columns, and where they touched the Vesperine's hull the mussels gleamed wetly, thousands of them, like the ship had been studded with dark pearls.

Her sister would have loved this. That was the thought that arrived, uninvited, the way it always did around minute fifteen, when the work slowed enough to let it through. Wren would have loved the light shafts. Wren had been the one who taught her to dive in the flooded quarry behind their grandfather's house, the one who'd hung upside down off the rope swing and made Odile count how long she could stay under, the one who'd said — eight years old, dripping, triumphant — *"the bottom is just another country, Odi, you have to have a passport."*

Wren had gone into a flooded mine eleven years ago too. Different water. Same country.

They'd never recovered her body. The mine company had sealed the adit with concrete and called it closure, and their grandfather had signed the papers because someone had to, and Odile had signed nothing at all, which was why she supposed she'd spent every year since signing other things — salvage contracts, liability waivers, charter agreements, anything with a line at the bottom that kept her moving, kept her wet, kept her from standing still long enough for the silence where Wren's voice should be to catch up with her.

Diving was how she didn't stop. That was the truth of it, and she knew it, and knowing it changed nothing. Every dive was a way of not remembering, and every dive remembered for her. The water always brought it up eventually, the way the river brought up everything — bodies, boats, secrets — given enough time.

She looked at the seal a moment longer. Then she took out her knife, worked the fairlead's lowest bolt loose — it came easier than it should have, the river already loosening it — and put the bolt in her thigh pocket. Not the seal itself; she couldn't take the fitting whole without defacing the wreck, and whatever else Odile Dravenmoor was, she was not a wrecker. But the bolt carried the same stamp on its head, smaller, cleaner, a coin-sized impression of the walled city and its wheel-spoke harbor.

Evidence, she told herself. Cataloguing. The ledger doesn't lie, and neither should I.

It was also theft, and she knew that too, and she pocketed it anyway.

* * *

She worked the rest of the hull in the order she always did: stern to amidships, amidships to bow, the ledger entries coming in short efficient strokes of the grease pencil. *Station 40–55: plating sound, rivet loss approx. 10%. Station 56: fracture, hull breach, silted from inside — origin unknown, pre-sinking? Growth patterns suggest.* She noted the portholes, all of them dark, most of them silted over from within, as though the ship had closed her own eyes. She noted the anchor hawse, empty, cable cut clean rather than slipped — someone had sawed the Vesperine loose of her anchors, and cut cable doesn't happen in a flood by accident.

At the bow she paused at the place where the name should have been. Ships carry their names welded or cast into the stem, proud and permanent. The Vesperine's stem bore a rectangle of brighter metal, a patch, deliberate — the name ground off and plated over before she ever went down. Somebody had wanted this ship anonymous even while she floated.

Odile wrote: *Stem: name removed pre-loss. Deliberate. Navy chart shows no vessel at these coordinates. Ledger entry 9-114: identity confirmed by hull form, stern seal (see bolt sample), construction consistent with Calvethi yards circa—*

She stopped writing. In the green distance, beyond the wreck, something moved.

Not a fish. Too steady. Too straight. She killed her lamp and hung motionless in the water column, and the river closed around her like a held breath, and she watched the shape resolve: a patrol boat, navy gray, running slow along the exclusion boundary a quarter mile east. Running the line the way a guard runs a fence. It wasn't looking for her — licensed divers worked these edges all the time, and the *Kestrel's Debt* flew her permit flag like everyone else. But the patrol boat slowed as it passed opposite the Vesperine's position. Slowed, and held, for the better part of a minute, engines idling, before it straightened and went on.

As if checking on something. As if taking attendance.

Odile finished the ledger with a steadier hand than she felt. On the slate, beneath the day's entries, she added a final line, small, almost private: *Someone is still watching her after eleven years. Ask why.*

Then she tugged the line twice — not trouble, just *ready* — and began her ascent, the stolen bolt riding warm against her thigh like a second pulse.

* * *

The sun was fully up when she broke the surface, and the world above the water was all ordinary business: gulls arguing over the masthead, mist burning off the bluffs, Bo hauling on the line with his whole skinny body while Sefa took her weight at the gunwale with those quick sure hands, reading her condition off the tension in the rope before Odile's head even cleared the water. Sefa couldn't hear the gasp she made coming up, but she felt it, and she had coffee ready before Odile had her mask off. That was Sefa. She heard everything except sound.

"Anything?" Bo asked, meaning treasure, meaning the way seventeen-year-olds mean it — gold, coins, a sword, something you could hold up in the light.

"Hull survey," Odile said. "Same as last month. She's holding."

Bo made a face and went back to coiling line. He was a runaway out of the eastern towns, all elbows and hunger and a head full of dead languages he'd learned who-knew-where, and Odile hadn't decided yet whether he was crew or passenger. Three weeks aboard and he still slept on deck like he expected to be put ashore any morning.

Sefa caught her eye and tilted her chin toward the east, toward the water. Then she held up one finger and rotated it slowly — *boat* — and pointed again, and made a flat hand sinking through her other fist. *Patrol boat. Went down slow. Stopped.*

"You saw."

Sefa shrugged, eloquent. She'd felt the idling engines through the hull, read their rhythm the way other people read faces. She tapped her ear, shook her head, then pointed at Odile's chest: *you felt it too.*

"I felt it," Odile agreed. She drank her coffee. It was terrible, burnt, perfect. "Anything else?"

Sefa's expression shifted — the small tightening around her eyes that Odile had learned meant *something I don't have words for yet*. She held up both hands, palms down, and pushed them apart slowly. Wide. Then she pointed downstream, toward the lower reaches where the three rivers braided together below the exclusion line, and spread her hands wider.

*Big water coming,* she meant. *Spring flood. Bigger than usual.*

Odile looked downstream. The river ran high, yes — it always ran high in the thaw — but Sefa read water the way Tam read maps and Bo read dead script, and if Sefa said big, then big it was. High water meant good diving on the upper wreck, more reach, more access. High water also meant the riverbed would move. It always moved. Things buried for years came up in flood seasons — fence posts, coffin lids, the occasional rowboat, once, horribly, a shoe.

The river gives up what it's done hiding, her grandmother used to say. You just have to be there when it does.

Odile went below to the cramped cabin that served as office, galley, and archive, and pinned the ninth slate to the bulkhead among its eight predecessors. Nine slates of a ship that didn't exist, surveyed plank by plank by a woman with a permit to work an unnamed shoal. She took the bolt from her thigh pocket and set it on the desk beside the slates, and in the morning light the little stamped seal caught the sun and threw it back — the walled city, the wheel-spoke harbor, the name nobody said.

Eleven years the navy had kept this wreck a blank space on a chart. Eleven years, and today the river had handed her a piece of brass with a nation's signature on it.

She got out the tenth slate and wrote at the top, in letters that pressed hard enough to score the surface:

*Entry 10-1. The Vesperine carries the seal of Calveth. Someone cut her anchors. Someone erased her name. Someone is still counting her.*

And underneath, after a pause, because the pencil seemed to want it:

*What else is the river going to give up this spring?*

Outside, far downstream, the water was already rising.
