## 2. What the River Gives Up

The flood came in the night like a debt collector.

Odile woke to the sound of it — not rain, though rain was falling too, but the river itself, a low grinding roar that she felt through the ground before she heard it through the canvas of her tent. Ten years on the water had taught her the river's moods the way other people learned a spouse's breathing: the flat murmur of summer, the glassy silence before ice, and this — this swollen, muscular voice that meant the high country was letting go all at once.

She lay still for a while, listening to her camp take inventory around her. The skiff tugging at its lines. The dive compressor under its oilskin shifting on its pallet. The cook fire hissing out somewhere near the bank. All present. All accounted for.

Then the river took something anyway.

It wasn't a sound so much as an absence — a change in the drag of the current against the pilings of the old fish stage downstream, a note dropped from a chord. Odile had catalogued that sound every night for three weeks without knowing she was doing it, the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was what kept the dark at arm's length. Something had come loose. Something was moving.

She got up, pulled on her wool coat over the shirt she'd slept in, and stepped barefoot into the mud outside the tent.

Dawn hadn't decided anything yet. The sky was the color of wet slate, and the rain came down fine and needling, and the river — the river was unrecognizable. It had climbed four feet up its banks overnight and swallowed the gravel shoal where she'd been ending her dives, and it ran brown and fast with the whole spring's argument behind it, carrying fence posts, half a rowboat, a drowned sapling turning slowly like a man considering his options. The air smelled of torn-up earth and pine resin and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, the smell of places the sun didn't reach.

Odile stood in the rain and watched the water work.

This was the part nobody understood about salvage — the ones who thought it was about diving, about brass and lungs and rope. The real work happened in moments like this one, standing on a bank while a river rearranged itself. Every flood was a hand reaching into the bottom of the world and stirring. Things that had lain quiet for decades got picked up, rolled, set down somewhere new. Wrecks shifted. Hulls that had been buried in silt since before Odile was born shrugged off their blankets. And sometimes — rarely, once or twice in a working life — the river gave something back.

She'd been diving the Vesperine's outer hull for eleven days now, dawn shifts only, cataloguing. That was the word she used even inside her own head, *cataloguing*, because it sounded professional and professional was safe. Rivet patterns. Plating seams. The strange, deep-set ports that no merchant ship needed and no navy would discuss. She'd filled two notebooks with measurements and sketches, and if anyone had asked her why — why spend a season's savings on compressor fuel to document a wreck she couldn't legally touch, in water the harbor office pretended wasn't there — she'd have said science, or salvage rights pending, or some other smooth little lie. The truth was simpler and uglier. Diving was the only time her mind went quiet. Forty feet down, with the cold pressing on her chest like a hand and the world reduced to torchlight and rope, there was no room left for Wren.

Up here, in the rain, with nothing to do but stand — that was when the remembering started. So she didn't stand. She worked.

By full light the flood had begun its slow retreat, dropping an inch an hour, laying fresh silt over everything like a sheet drawn over furniture. Odile walked the new shoreline with a hooked pole, working upstream from her camp, reading the wrack line the way Tam — no, not Tam, she hadn't met Tam yet, she'd been alone then, properly alone — the way she'd taught herself to read it. Driftwood sorted by density. A dead ewe, bloated and dignified. Fishing net gone green with age. And then, forty yards below the fish stage, where the current curled back on itself in an eddy that had been depositing things since before the war:

A chest.

She saw the corner first. A pale line in the brown mud, straight where nothing natural was straight. Then the iron banding, rusted to lace. Then the seal.

Odile stopped walking.

The chest sat half-buried at the eddy's edge, small — the size of a sea chest, the kind a clerk might use for ledgers — and it was faced in lead, which was why it had survived whatever journey it had taken, and across its lid, stamped into a disc of green bronze as big as her palm, was a device she knew better than her own name.

Three waves beneath a sinking star.

Calveth.

The navy wouldn't say the word. She'd learned that in her first week on this stretch of river, when she'd asked a harbormaster — politely, professionally — about the wreck charts for the lower reach, and watched his face do something complicated and closed, like a door with the bolt thrown. *There are no wrecks in that reach worth charting,* he'd said, and looked at her the way men look at a dog that has learned to open gates. The drowned nation. The city that wasn't flooded, officially; it had suffered an *inundation event*, in the language of the compensation bureau, and its people were *relocated persons*, and its ships were *unregistered hulls*, and if you dove them you were stealing from an estate no one would admit existed.

And here one was, delivered to her feet by the same river that had done the drowning.

Odile stood in the shallows for a long moment, rain running off her chin, and felt the vertigo rise in her — not fear exactly, but that tilting sensation she got sometimes at depth, when the torch caught something unexpected and the mind raced ahead of the body into the dark. Because the river didn't give things up. That was the first law of her trade, the law she'd built her whole careful restless life on: the river keeps. You could borrow from it, bleed it a little, but it kept. For it to surrender a sealed Calvethi chest onto a shoal within a mile of her camp —

Well. Either the flood had been violent enough to crack open the Vesperine herself, or something else had let go somewhere upstream. Both possibilities made her hands cold.

She waded in. The water was snowmelt-cold, shoving at her knees, and she braced and got the hook under the chest's leading edge and heaved, and it came free of the mud with a sucking reluctance, heavier than it looked, lead-lined and waterlogged. She dragged it up the bank in stages, resting between pulls, and did not once look away from the seal, the way you don't look away from a snake or a blessing.

* * *

She opened it in the tent, with the flap down and the lamp lit, because some things shouldn't be done in daylight.

The lock was Calvethi work — a lever mechanism she'd seen diagrams of in Tam's future books, though she didn't know that yet either; right now she knew it only as old, elegant, and stubborn. It took her twenty minutes with picks and a boiled-soft strip of leather, kneeling in the mud-floored tent with her breath loud in her ears, and when the hasps finally gave they did so with a sigh, a small exhalation of trapped air that smelled of lead and ink and something faintly sweet, like paper that had dreamed a long time.

Inside, packed in oiled canvas gone stiff as bark: papers.

Not coin. Not plate. Not the salvage-man's dream. Papers — hundreds of them, bundled in twine, wrapped in waxed folios, their edges furred with age but their surfaces, protected by the lead lining, dark and legible. Official paper. She recognized the format without wanting to: the heavy stock, the ruled margins, the small precise numerals of a chancery that had believed, right up until the water came, that it would last a thousand years. State papers. Census schedules, maybe. Shipping manifests. Orders.

Her heart was doing something she didn't authorize, a hard irregular knocking, and she told herself it was the altitude of hope, the simple greed of a salvager who'd just found cargo, and she began lifting the bundles out one by one, setting them on the oilcloth, cataloguing — *cataloguing* — and it was almost working, the professional calm was almost holding, until she reached the third bundle from the bottom and found the letter.

It was folded in thirds, the way people fold letters to carry them in a pocket, not the way clerks file them. It had been handled. It had been carried against a body. The paper was cheap, river-mill stock, nothing like the chancery folios, and across its face, in a hand Odile had last seen on a grocery list pinned to a kitchen door eleven years ago, was written:

*Odi —*

The tent tilted.

That was the only way she could describe it, later, when she tried: the tent tilted, the lamp flame leaned, the river's roar swelled and receded like blood in her own ears, and Odile Dravenmoor, who had hung upside down in black water with her air hose kinked and her torch dead and had never once panicked, sat down hard in the mud of her own camp because a single letter on a page had knocked her legs out from under her.

Wren's handwriting.

She knew it the way she knew her own pulse. The forward slant. The way the O always came out slightly open at the top, unfinished, like Wren had been called away mid-letter her whole life. The little crossbar she put through her sevens, a habit she'd stolen from a schoolteacher in Corvess who Odile had forgotten the face of but whose mark lived on in her sister's numbers forever. Eleven years dead. Drowned in the Marrowfield mine collapse when they were nineteen and twenty-two, when the shaft flooded and the company sealed the level with the night shift still inside it, and Odile had spent eleven years not thinking about it by thinking about everything else, every dive a way of holding her breath against the memory, and here it was, surfacing, physical, wet with river water, addressed to *Odi* in a dead woman's hand.

The past did not stay buried. That was the second law of her trade, the true one, the one she'd been dodging since the mine sealed. Everything goes down; nothing stays down. Sooner or later the flood comes and stirs the bottom and gives up what it took.

Her hands shook so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs before she could pick the letter up.

* * *

She read it four times before she understood it, and she is not sure, even now, that she ever fully understood it — understanding being a thing that arrived in pieces over months, the way light arrives at the bottom of a river, filtered and bent and late.

The letter was dated nine days after the flooding of Calveth.

Nine days. Wren had died in the Marrowfield collapse six years after that date. Which meant that in the space between those two facts — in six years of a life Odile had mourned as short and ordinary, a life cut off in a mining town two hundred miles from any coast — her sister had been here. On this river. Near this water. Doing something that required her to carry a letter against her body, sealed inside a lead chest with the state papers of a drowned nation.

*Odi —*

*If you are reading this then the river has done what I asked it to, which means you finally stopped moving long enough to be somewhere when it did. I know you. You'll have been diving. You're always diving.*

*I don't have long to write this and I don't know how much of it will make sense, so I will say the plain thing first: Calveth did not drown. Calveth was drowned. There is a difference and the difference is a signature, and I am close to it now, closer than is safe, and if I am wrong about the safety then this chest is my insurance and you are my heir.*

*You will want to burn this. I know that too. You will think: Wren is dead, the mine is sealed, the navy forbids the name, what can one diver do. But listen. The census is aboard the Vesperine. Every name. Everyone the water took, written down before the water took them, because someone wanted a count of what they were spending. Someone paid for that count, Odi. Someone signed for it. And when you find the name on the order — and you will find it, because you are the only person alive who cannot leave a thing alone —*

*— I am sorry in advance for what it says.*

*Don't stop moving until you've finished. Then, please, for both of us: stop.*

*Your sister,*
*Wren*

Below the signature, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as if added in haste or fury or both:

*The fitting on the outer hull, port side, third strake. Brass. Look at the stamp. They marked their own work.*

Odile sat in the lamplight with the letter in her lap and the rain hammering the canvas overhead, and the river roared, and somewhere in her chest a thing that had been carefully, professionally still for eleven years turned over and began, horribly, to move.

They marked their own work.

She thought of the Vesperine's hull, eleven days of it, every rivet and seam committed to paper in her neat salvage-hand. She thought of the brass fittings along the third strake, the ones she'd noted as anomalous — replacement work, newer metal, stamped with marks she'd recorded but not read, because reading them would mean asking questions the harbor office answered with bolted doors.

She crawled to her kit bag, dug out the notebook, and found the entry. Third strake, port side, fitting number seven. She'd sketched the stamp without decoding it, the way she sketched everything, collecting facts like ballast.

Three waves beneath a sinking star.

The seal of Calveth, stamped into the hull of the ship that carried the count of Calveth's dead. Marked, as Wren said, like a craftsman signs a cabinet. Like a hand signs a confession.

Odile closed the notebook. Outside, the flood was still falling, inch by inch, uncovering the world it had rearranged, and downriver — she could feel it the way she felt current against a hull, a pressure without proof — the Vesperine lay shifted, stirred, maybe broken open, her hold exposed to whoever reached her first.

Six years Wren had worked this river, and died for it, and Odile had never known. Had spent those same six years diving wrecks up and down the coast, restless, relentless, running from a grief that had apparently been following her the whole time, patient as water, waiting for her to stop moving.

The letter said don't stop. The letter said finish it. The letter said she was sorry in advance.

Odile looked at the bundles of state paper spread across her oilcloth, at the lead chest with its drowning star, at the rain coming down on a river that had just handed her the one thing in the world she could not catalogue and put away.

Then she began, with steady hands now, to pack.
