## 3. Six Tides

The harbor office smelled of lamp oil and old paper and the particular sourness of men who had spent thirty years breathing river fog. Odile stood at the counter with her hat in her hands — a concession to civility she made perhaps twice a year — while behind the desk a clerk with ink-stained cuffs pretended not to know why she'd come.

She hadn't come for anything, that was the truth of it. She'd come because the chest sat in the bottom of her skiff under a tarp, and because she could not sit beside it another hour without either opening it again or putting distance between herself and it, and her hands had chosen distance. So she'd rowed into Grayharbor on the morning tide with no plan beyond motion, and now here she was, listening to a clerk recite mooring fees as though the world were still a place where mooring fees mattered.

"Salvage permit renewal is four silver," the clerk said. "Inspection stamp is one more. You'll want the stamp. The navy's been doing spot checks since the flood season."

"I don't need a renewal."

"Everyone needs a renewal."

"I'm not staying." She set two coppers on the counter anyway, for the courtesy of being allowed to stand there, and turned to go.

That was when the man in the corner cleared his throat.

She noticed him the way you notice anything in Grayharbor: peripherally first, then all at once. He was sitting at a scarred table by the window with a cup of something he wasn't drinking, and he was dressed like a harbor official dressed when he didn't want to look like one — good coat, bad boots, the coat buttoned wrong at the bottom the way a hurried man buttons things. Fifty, maybe older, with the windburned complexion of someone who had once worked decks and now only walked them.

"Diver," he said. Not a question. "You're Dravenmoor. The one who works the outer channel."

Odile did not like being known. It was a small town's habit and a dangerous one. "I work where the work is."

"The Vesperine." He said the name quietly, but he said it whole, without the careful nothing people usually put around it. Nobody said that name. The navy didn't say it; the harbormaster's rolls listed the wreck by grid coordinates and the phrase *unidentified hull, deepwater section*. Hearing it spoken aloud in a government office felt like hearing a dead woman's name in church — a small transgression, and an enormous one.

"You have me confused with someone else," Odile said.

"I don't." He rose, unhurried, and crossed to the counter, and the clerk suddenly found urgent business in a drawer of stamps. Up close the man smelled of tobacco and tide charts. "Name's Corvess. Deputy harbormaster, third district, which means I sign things nobody reads and read things nobody signs." He glanced at the clerk, then back. "Walk with me to the window. It's a long story and I'd rather tell it looking at the water than at him."

Every instinct Odile had said walk out the door. But the chest was in her skiff, and Wren's handwriting was in the chest, and a man who spoke forbidden names in government offices was either a trap or a door, and she had spent enough of her life walking away from doors.

She walked to the window.

* * *

Grayharbor spread below them in its usual gray disorder — fishing smacks, coal barges, the navy patrol cutter tied up at the far quay with its guns covered in canvas as though canvas could make them polite. Beyond the breakwater the river widened toward the sea, brown-green and patient, holding everything it held.

"Six tides," Corvess said.

"That's a number."

"It is." He turned his cup in his hand though he hadn't brought it with him; some habits live in the wrist. "Three days from tomorrow, at slack water after the sixth tide, the Meridian Salvage Fleet's claim on the deepwater section expires. They've held it eleven years. Filed it quietly, worked it never, renewed it every season through a solicitor in the capital who has never once seen the water it concerns." He smiled without any humor in it. "Claims lapse if there's no evidence of recovery activity. Two seasons running, Meridian has filed affidavits claiming active salvage. I signed off on them because signing off was my job. This season the paperwork came through different channels, and my signature wasn't requested, which tells me somebody above me has decided the claim doesn't need to look legitimate anymore."

"Expires how?"

"Cleanly. After slack water on the sixth tide, the section reverts to open claim. First vessel to file recovery intent holds it." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were tired in the way of men who had done arithmetic they didn't like. "Meridian knows the date better than I do. Captain Ilse Varn doesn't let claims expire. She'll have a recovery affidavit drafted already, dated back three weeks, and a magistrate in her pocket to stamp it. By the time anyone objects, the Vesperine will be hers — hull, contents, manifest, whatever your navy buried down there and whatever the spring flood just decided to give back."

The floor tilted. Odile kept her face still, but inside her something dropped away like ballast cut loose. *Whatever the spring flood just gave back.* He knew about the chest. Or he guessed. Or — worse and more likely — he knew nothing specific and everything general, because men like Corvess survived by knowing the shape of things without ever touching their edges.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"No," he agreed mildly. "You wouldn't." He set the untouched cup on the windowsill. "Let me say this plainly, diver, because I'm old and I've got a bad knee and I don't have the patience for the dance. Eleven years ago I processed the original casualty reports out of Calveth province. Before the name came off the maps. I saw the passenger lists. I saw the tonnage figures for a ship that should have floated through anything the river could throw at her, and I saw the weather logs from the night she went down, and the two documents did not agree. I asked a question about it. I was transferred for asking. That was the last question I asked for eleven years." He flexed his bad knee, wincing. "This spring the river moved a bed of silt it hasn't moved since the sinking. Things are coming up out of the shallows that were meant to stay down. My office has received three separate instructions regarding 'material recovered from unidentified deepwater wrecks' — instructions that use the word *surrender* and the word *immediately* and never once explain to whom. And yesterday a Meridian survey launch ran the outer channel at dawn, slow, with her sounder running, taking profiles of a wreck she supposedly has no interest in working."

Odile's mouth had gone dry. Outside, a gull screamed over the fish market and the sound seemed very far away.

"Why are you telling me this?"

Corvess was quiet for a moment. Through the window, the navy cutter's crew washed her deck, water sheeting off the planks, ordinary men doing ordinary work over whatever it was they guarded.

"Because in eleven years," he said finally, "you're the only person who's dived that hull more than twice. Because you dive alone, cheap, and quiet, which means you're not doing it for money, and a person who isn't doing it for money is the only kind of person I can trust with it. And because—" he stopped, and started again, more carefully, "—because my grandmother was born in Calveth. In the low town, by the weir. There are maybe four hundred of us left in the records who can say that, and most of them changed their names when the name became a thing you couldn't say. I didn't change mine because Corvess is a stone-country name and nobody looks twice at it. But I remember the low town. I remember the bells." His voice roughened and he mastered it. "I am not asking you what you found, diver. I am telling you that in six tides, whatever is down there belongs to Ilse Varn, and Ilse Varn sells to whoever pays, and what pays best is the same machinery that put that ship on the bottom. If there's anything in that wreck worth keeping out of that machinery's hands, the clock on it started this morning."

* * *

She made him wait while she thought, or pretended to think, because her mind had already decided and some part of her wanted to catch it in the act.

They stood at the window and watched the harbor work. A barge of pit timber slid past on the ebb. Two boys netted crabs off the pilings. The world performed its ordinariness, and beneath it, six tides ticked down like water filling a lung.

If she walked away — if she took the chest, took Wren's letter, took the brass fitting stamped with the seal she'd pocketed off the hull like a thief taking communion — and went inland, north, anywhere the river's reach ended, she could be free of it. That was the honest arithmetic. She was one diver with a leaking skiff and forty feet of hose line. Meridian was a fleet. The navy was a navy. There was no version of this where she won, and several versions where she drowned, and one likely version where she simply vanished the way inconvenient people vanished, and the river closed over her too, and somewhere a sister-shaped hole opened in no one's life because there was no one left to grieve her.

That last part, she admitted, was almost a comfort. Almost.

But Wren's handwriting was in the chest. She had seen only the first line before her vision had gone white at the edges and she'd had to sit down on the shingle with the river lapping at her boots — *Odi, if you're reading this, then the water gave it up, which means the water wants it found* — and even that single line had rearranged the furniture of her soul. Her sister had been dead nine years. Dead women did not write letters. Unless they had written them before dying, which meant Wren had known, had known enough and feared enough, to seal words against the day the river surrendered them. Which meant the drowning had not been an ending. It had been a beginning Wren had tried to leave instructions for.

And Odile had spent nine years diving that hull not to find anything. She understood that now with a clarity that felt like cold water in the ears. She had dove to keep moving, to keep her hands busy, to keep grief suspended in work the way silt stays suspended in current — harmless so long as nothing lets it settle. Every dive had been a way of not stopping long enough to remember. And now the river had gone and settled it for her, right on the shallows of her camp, wrapped in Calveth's seal, addressed in her sister's hand.

There was no more not stopping. There was only choosing what to do with the weight.

"Deputy Harbormaster," she said. "If I wanted to file a recovery intent on the deepwater section, how fast could it be done?"

Corvess's eyebrows rose a fraction — the only surprise he permitted himself. "The office opens at dawn. The form is one page. The fee is eight silver." He paused. "It would require a vessel, a crew of no fewer than three, and evidence of capacity to conduct recovery. A skiff and a lone diver would not survive scrutiny."

"I have until the sixth tide."

"You have until the sixth tide," he confirmed. "And Meridian will not wait for the sixth tide to start working the site. Varn runs her operations like sieges. She'll want the wreck raised or stripped well before the claim lapses so the lapse becomes academic." He straightened his wrong-buttoned coat, a small fussy gesture that humanized him entirely. "I can't help you beyond this conversation. If I'm seen helping you, both of us end up transferred somewhere with no water in it. But the form will be on my desk at dawn, and I am a man who processes paperwork promptly when the paperwork is in order."

"And if it isn't in order? If I show up at dawn with a crew of—"

"Then I process it promptly anyway," Corvess said, "provided the crew exists and the names are real. I'm an old man, diver. I've spent eleven years watching the water hold its breath. I would like, before I die, to see it exhale."

* * *

She bought supplies the way she did everything now — fast, focused, counting coins against a list she wrote in her head and did not write down. Rope, lamp oil, salt pork, a spare regulator valve from the chandler who cheated her and always would. The chest stayed under the tarp in the skiff. Twice she caught herself standing over it, one hand on the tarp, not lifting it. Not yet. Wren's letter deserved better than to be read shaking on a harbor street with six tides screaming in her blood. When she read it, she would read it whole, and she would need to be somewhere still.

Stillness. The word itself felt like a wound.

By late afternoon she was back at her camp on the shingle downstream of the wreck, and the river did what the river always did, which was continue. The light went long and coppery across the water. Out past the breakwater she could see the deepwater section's marker buoy — a navy buoy, gray, unmarked except by coordinates — riding its tether over the place where the Vesperine lay folded in the dark with her dead.

Six tides. She counted them the way you count stairs in the dark. One: tomorrow's morning flood. Two: tomorrow's ebb. Three and four: the next day. Five and six: the day after, ending at slack water, at the exact hour when the river stops arguing with the sea and everything hangs balanced and possible. Slack water. The moment of least resistance. Somewhere in a harbor office a one-page form waited for her name, and somewhere south a fleet was coaling its boilers, and between those two facts she had to build a crew out of nothing.

A crew. She almost laughed. She had spent nine years perfecting solitude the way other people perfected instruments. She ate alone, dove alone, slept alone in a tent whose second cot she'd sold years ago because keeping it felt like hope and hope was a thing that drowned people. Asking strangers aboard her boat was asking them into the one room of her life that had no furniture and no locks.

But the form required three names. And the wreck required more than one pair of hands — she'd known that for years and ignored it, running lines solo, hauling solo, nearly dying twice in ways she'd told no one. And the papers in the chest—

The papers in the chest were in old Calvethi script. She had turned a page that first vertiginous hour and seen columns of flowing characters she couldn't read, formal and dense, the handwriting of a government that no longer existed. She could dive. She could salvage. She could not read the language of the drowned.

So: a crew. Strays, probably. People the harbor had chewed up and spat out, the ones who drifted along the quays with skills and no berths. She knew the type intimately because she was the type. A disgraced chartman, maybe — she'd heard of one, naval-trained, cashiered over some cartographic sin, drinking his pension at the Anchorline. A rigger — the deaf woman who worked the lift rigs at the coal yard and could splice cable in the dark, because dark was all she'd ever had. Someone young, someone cheap, someone who might, improbably, impossibly, read dead languages — a runaway, a scrapper, one of the kids who haunted the fish market stalls. It was a mad inventory. It was the only inventory she had.

Full dark came down, and she built a small fire because the chest needed drying air and because fire meant a human shape against the night, and tonight she wanted the river to know someone was watching it back. She sat with her boots nearly in the water and watched the marker buoy's gray outline pulse where the navy had fixed a dim light to it, blinking its coordinate code out over the drowned ship like a heartbeat monitored by enemies.

Six tides. Varn would come with steam and steel and lawyers. The navy would come with canvas-covered guns. The river would come with whatever it intended, because the river had already shown it intended something — had coughed up a sealed chest onto the shallows of a grieving woman's camp after eleven years of silence, and rivers did not do that by accident.

Odile fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks climb.

"All right, Wren," she said aloud, to the water, to the dark, to the folded paper waiting under the tarp. "I hear you. Six tides. We'll see what we can raise."

The buoy blinked. The river ran. And far to the south, though she could not have known it, the first smoke of the Meridian Fleet's furnaces was already staining the horizon — but she felt it anyway, the way divers feel weather coming through their scars, and she began, that night, by firelight, to make her list of names.

Dawn would bring the harbor office, and the form, and the first stranger.
