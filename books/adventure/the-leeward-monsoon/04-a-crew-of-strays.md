## 4. A Crew of Strays

The harbor at Greywater Reach smelled of tar and old fish and the particular sourness of money that had changed hands too many times. Odile walked its length with the chest's weight still on her mind, though she'd left it locked in her camp's strongbox under three feet of river silt and a lie about keeping dog meat. Six tides. The number turned over in her head like a stone in a current, wearing itself smooth.

She needed hands. Not just any hands — she needed people who owed nothing to anyone official, who could work without asking why, and who wouldn't run at the first sign that the Vesperine was more than a wreck. That last part was the hard one. She couldn't promise it herself. She only knew that whatever was in that chest had made her sister's handwriting surface after eleven years underwater, and a thing like that didn't let you walk away.

* * *

Tam Quill she found where disgraced men always are: at the end of a bar, early enough that the lamps hadn't been lit, nursing something cheap with the posture of a man who used to hold instruments worth more than the whole tavern.

He'd been a cartographer for the Navy Hydrographic Office — Odile had heard the story secondhand, the way harbor stories travel. He'd charted the Calveth reach before the navy sealed it, back when charts of that stretch were still legal to own. Then he'd refused to amend a survey. Refused to mark a channel as unnavigable when his soundings said otherwise, or the reverse — the versions differed depending on who told it — and they'd taken his commission, his pension, and his name off the rolls. What remained was a man who drank like he was paying off a debt and looked at every piece of paper like it might be lying to him.

Odile took the stool beside him and put a silver bit on the wood between them.

"I need a chart," she said.

"Harbormaster sells charts."

"Not this one." She kept her voice level, unremarkable, the voice of a woman discussing rope prices. "I need the old survey of the Calveth reach. Pre-seal. Your soundings."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes — not hope, exactly. Hope was a luxury he'd clearly stopped stocking. More like the reflex of a man hearing a language he'd been told was dead.

"Those surveys were confiscated," he said. "Every copy."

"You kept one."

It wasn't a question. She'd done her asking around. A man doesn't spend fifteen years memorizing depth contours and then let go of all of them. Some men keep their hands. Tam Quill had kept his memory.

He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the bar. "And if I did? What's it worth to you?"

"A share. Equal. And a reason." She hesitated, then decided the truth was cheaper than any lie she could construct that would hold up against him. "I'm diving the Vesperine. Before Meridian's claim expires."

The glass stopped turning.

For a long moment Tam looked at her the way he must once have looked at a sounding line that came back shorter than the bottom should have allowed — checking the reading against everything he knew, finding it impossible, and finding it true anyway.

"They'll have patrol boats," he said finally. "They've had patrol boats on that reach since before my disgrace, if you can call it mine and not theirs."

"Then we go fast and quiet."

"That's not a plan, that's a wish with a schedule."

"It's what I have." She slid the silver toward him. "You can drink on that tonight and forget me by morning. Or you can come look at what I pulled out of the river and tell me your soundings don't matter anymore."

He didn't touch the coin. But he didn't push it back either, and when she left the tavern an hour later, he was walking beside her, carrying nothing but himself and, she suspected, several hundred fathoms of riverbed held entirely in his head.

* * *

Sefa Marrow worked the rigging yard at Ternholt Locks, two days downstream, and Odile knew of her the way everyone on the river knew of her: as the woman who could feel a frayed hawser through her boots before the splice gave, who rigged cargo lifts that never dropped anything, whose hands were worth triple wages anywhere that would pay them and paid half because the yards were afraid of a crew chief who couldn't hear their complaints.

Odile found her at dawn, standing at the base of a crane, watching a load swing with her whole body angled toward it like a compass needle. When Odile approached, Sefa saw her coming in the vibration of the boards — turned before Odile's shadow reached her.

Up close she was broad-shouldered and weathered, maybe fifty, with grey shot through her black hair and a face that did its talking in small economies: a raised eyebrow, a tilted chin. A slate hung from her belt on a cord, chalk worn to a stub.

Odile had thought about how to do this. In the end she just took the chalk, wrote on the slate:

**VESPERINE. SIX TIDES. NEED RIGGING MASTER. DANGER. PAY EQUAL SHARE.**

Sefa read it twice. Then she took the chalk and wrote beneath:

**WHY THE VESPERINE?**

Odile considered lying. There was something about being written to instead of spoken over that made lies feel heavier, clumsier — chalk didn't flow the way speech did, and every word cost effort, so every word wanted to be true.

**MY SISTER DIED THERE.**

That wasn't precisely accurate. Wren hadn't died there; Wren had died somewhere downstream of it, years later, in water Odile had never found. But the Vesperine was where the thread started, and Odile was tired of untangling it into smaller and smaller lies.

Sefa studied her for a long time. Then she wiped the slate clean with her thumb and wrote:

**THE YARDS THINK DEAF MEANS CARELESS. YOU DON'T. THAT'S WORTH MORE THAN YOUR SHARE.**

She held out her hand, and Odile shook it, and that was the whole contract. Later Odile would learn that Sefa read lips well enough when she chose to, that she'd lost her hearing to a fever at nineteen and had spent thirty years making the world come to her through her palms and her boot soles and the soles of her feet on any deck she stood on. But that first morning, the handshake was enough. Some agreements don't need words. Sefa, of all people, would know.

* * *

Bo was harder.

Bo wasn't at a bar or a yard. Bo was living, as far as Odile could determine, in the loft of the chandlery on Rope Street, having talked the owner into believing they were a nephew from upriver, a fiction that lasted exactly as long as it took the owner to mention it to anyone else. Odile heard about the kid from the chandler's wife, who described "that sharp little runaway" with the exasperated fondness of someone who'd fed them twice and regretted neither meal.

What made Bo interesting wasn't the runaway part. The river produced runaways the way it produced silt. What made Bo interesting was what the chandler's wife said next: the kid spent evenings in the reading room of the Ternholt lending library, copying out inscriptions from a broken gravestone the library kept as a curiosity — a stone fished out of the Calveth reach decades ago, carved in the old script nobody could read.

Nobody except, apparently, Bo.

Odile found the kid there on her third evening of looking, cross-legged on the floor beneath the gravestone's case, copying characters into a battered notebook with the fierce concentration of someone for whom writing things down had once been survival.

Bo looked up when Odile's shadow crossed the page. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Sharp-boned, wary, hair cut unevenly by someone who'd used a knife and hurried. Clothes two sizes wrong in opposite directions.

"You're staring," Bo said.

"You're reading Calvethi."

"I'm copying it. Reading's different." The kid closed the notebook, slow and deliberate, a door shutting. "Who wants to know?"

"Someone who has documents in that script and no one to read them."

Bo's eyes went bright and then immediately, deliberately dull — the kid was good at smothering reactions, which meant the reactions were expensive. "Lots of people claim to have Calvethi papers. Usually they want a free translation and then they leave without paying."

"I pay shares, not fees. Equal share of whatever we raise."

"Raise from where?"

"The Vesperine."

Now the smothering failed completely. Bo sat forward, notebook forgotten. "The Vesperine isn't real. Everyone says it's a story the navy uses to keep divers out of the reach."

"Everyone is wrong." Odile crouched to the kid's level, which felt right rather than condescending — like addressing a crew member, which was what she intended Bo to become. "I have a chest out of the river with Calveth's seal on it. State papers inside. And six tides before a salvage fleet's claim makes all of it theirs."

Bo was quiet for a moment. Then, carefully: "If I come with you, what happens when it's over? When the raising's done and the shares are split?"

"You walk away with yours. No questions."

"That's not what I asked."

Odile understood then, with a small cold clarity, what the kid was actually asking — whether this was employment or abandonment with extra steps, whether Odile was another adult who would be useful until she wasn't. She thought of Wren, who had promised to come back from her last expedition and hadn't. She thought of every dockside farewell she'd ever given, all of them final, all of them chosen.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I'm not good at after. But while it lasts, you eat what I eat and stand where I stand."

Bo looked at her for a long moment, weighing it, the way Sefa had weighed the handshake and Tam had weighed the coin. Then the kid stood, tucked the notebook inside a coat that had probably belonged to three previous owners, and said:

"Fine. But I get the gravestone's inscription copied first. It's the only Calvethi in this town that isn't locked in a government drawer, and I've waited four months for it."

* * *

They gathered on Odile's barge, the *Kestrel*, moored in the shallows below her camp, and Odile laid it out for them the way she'd never laid anything out for anyone: the chest, opened, its contents spread on the deck boards under a lantern. State papers with seals. Shipping manifests with lines struck through in red. And Wren's letter, which she did not open further than she already had, and which she placed apart from the rest, face down, like something sleeping.

Tam went through the manifests with his jaw tight, occasionally muttering coordinates under his breath and correcting them against soundings only he could see. Sefa examined the chest's construction, running her fingers along the joinery, and wrote on her slate: **BUILT TO SURVIVE BEING SUNK ON PURPOSE. FLOATING LID. WHO SHIPS RECORDS LIKE THIS?** Nobody answered, because none of them had an answer yet.

Bo read.

The kid read for two hours without stopping, lips moving, notebook filling, and when Bo finally looked up, the lantern had burned low and the other three had been watching in a silence that had stopped feeling awkward somewhere in the middle and started feeling like something else.

"It's a manifest," Bo said. "But not of cargo. It lists names. Hundreds of them. Passengers, it says — but the columns are wrong for passengers. There's no fares collected, no destinations. Just names, ages, and a third column that repeats the same word." Bo hesitated. "*Drowned.* Every row. The word *drowned* is already printed in the column, like they knew before the ship sailed."

The river lapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, and got no answer.

"Passenger list of the dead," Tam said quietly. "That's not a manifest. That's a census."

"And here—" Bo turned a page, and Odile saw the kid's hand tighten on the paper. "There's an authorization order bound into the front. It's signed. I can't make out the whole name, the ink's run, but the surname is legible." Bo looked up, and the wariness in the young face had shifted into something almost apologetic, as if the news were a debt the kid hated to collect. "It reads *Dravenmoor*."

Odile heard the name land in her own chest like an anchor dropping. Her family. Her blood. Her grandfather's hand, possibly, on an order that filled a column with the word *drowned* before a ship ever sailed.

Sefa wrote on her slate and turned it without ceremony: **THEN WE KNOW WHY IT'S FORBIDDEN.**

"We do," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. She looked around the lantern light at the four of them — a disgraced mapmaker, a deaf rigger, a runaway reader, and herself, a woman who'd spent eleven years diving away from a letter she now owned. None of them belonged anywhere. All of them, apparently, belonged to this.

"Six tides," she said. "We sail on the next one."

Above them the sky was going the color of wet slate, and far down the reach, where the river bent toward the forbidden water, the first wind of the changing tide moved across the surface — heading the same direction they were.
