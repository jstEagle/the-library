## 5. The Navy Won't Discuss It

Tam Vess had spent eleven years drawing coastlines for the Admiralty, and he knew the difference between a map and a lie. A map was a promise: *here is where the water ends and the land begins.* A lie was a blank space with a neat little notation beside it — *unsurveyed*, or *hazardous*, or his personal favorite, *no further information required*. He had drawn three of those blanks himself before they cashiered him, each one a stretch of riverbank some captain had decided civilians didn't need to see. He'd told himself at the time that it was procedure. That was the word they used. Procedure. As if drowning a place were the same as filing it.

Now he sat in the stern of Odile Dravenmoor's leaky salvage barge with a chest of drowned state papers open across his knees, and every blank space he had ever inked came back to him at once.

The lamp hung from the boom above him, swinging gently with the current's push against the anchor line, so that the light moved across the pages like water itself. Around him the barge creaked its old complaints — hull plates ticking as the temperature dropped, the mast wire humming a half-tone under the evening wind. Bo sat cross-legged across from him, chin propped on both fists, watching the papers the way other children watched a fire. Sefa had wedged herself into the corner by the rail with her back to the hull, one bare foot pressed flat against the deck planking, reading the boat through her sole the way she read everything. Odile stood at the rail with her arms folded, not looking at the documents, looking instead downstream, toward the dark.

Tam turned another page with both hands, careful as a priest handling relics.

The paper itself told him things before the words did. It was Calvethi stock — heavy rag bond, that faint gray-green tint, the watermark of the triple wave that had been the drowned nation's seal for four hundred years. The navy had burned most of what it seized after the sinking; Tam had seen the requisition orders for the incinerators once, filed under *sanitation supplies*, which was its own kind of confession. Paper this old, this official, this dry — it should not exist. And yet here it was, fished out of a spring flood like a fisherman hauling up a body.

"Read it again," Odile said, without turning around. "The part about the manifest."

"You've heard it twice."

"I want to hear how you say it the third time."

Tam looked down at the page. His hands were steady — they had always been steady; it was the rest of him that shook — but there was a coldness in his fingertips that had nothing to do with the night air. The document was a shipping abstract, the kind of ledger entry he had processed by the thousand in the Cartographic and Registry Office. Ship's name. Tonnage. Cargo classification. Port of origin. Port of— 

He cleared his throat.

"*Vesperine,*" he read. "Registry tonnage six hundred forty. Cargo classified: census materials, sealed, priority absolute. Departed Calveth-harbor on the last tide before the—" he paused, because the next word in the document was *event*, and the bureaucratic blandness of it made his teeth ache, "—before the event. Destination: leeward registry annex." He turned the page. The following entry was written in a different hand, a faster one, the ink blotted where the pen had pressed too hard. "Received. Condition: intact. Disposition—" 

He stopped.

"TAM," Bo said, too loud, the way Bo said everything when the silence went on too long. Sefa's head came up off the hull.

"Disposition," Tam said, "expunged."

The word sat in the lamplight between them. Expunged. Not lost, not sunk, not destroyed by act of war or weather. Expunged — the registry term for a record removed by authority, wiped backward through time until the ship had never carried anything at all. Tam had used the stamp himself. It sat in a drawer in an office that probably still smelled of ink and floor wax, a brass handle you pulled down and a die that bit the page and a small satisfying resistance, like biting through gristle. He had expunged lighthouse schedules. He had never once asked what else passed under the stamp.

"Sunk deliberately," Odile said. She still hadn't turned around. Her voice was flat in the way Tam had learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. "The manifest erased. The whole ship written out of the books."

"That's what expunged means, yes."

"And the navy won't discuss it."

"The navy," Tam said, "won't discuss it. I asked, once. Early on, when I was new. There was a gap in the coastal charts south of the old Calvethi delta — a big one, thirty miles of river that simply wasn't on any survey I could find. I asked my section chief why. He looked at me the way you look at a man who's asked about another man's wife, and he said, *the navy won't discuss it.* Those exact words. Like a catechism." He closed the ledger carefully. "I thought it was a joke. A hazing thing. You ask, everyone laughs, you learn to stop asking. Except nobody laughed. Nobody ever laughed. They just stopped talking to me for a day, and the next day everything was normal, and the gap stayed a gap, and I drew my maps around it for eleven years like a man walking around a hole in the floor."

Bo had gone very still. "So the ship really did carry the census. All those names."

"It carried something they wanted at the bottom of the river badly enough to sink six hundred tons over it." Tam rubbed his eyes. Lamp oil and old paper; the smell was getting into his skin. "And then they wanted it forgotten even more than they wanted it drowned. That's the part that matters. Drowning is easy. Forgetting takes work. Somebody has been doing that work for years, quietly, in offices like the one I worked in, and they're good at it, because I was good at it and I never noticed."

Sefa signed something, quick and sharp, and Bo translated without being asked, voice dropping half an octave to match Sefa's cadence: "She says if they were willing to sink a ship full of people, they're willing to sink a barge full of us."

Nobody argued with that. That was the worst part, Tam thought later. Nobody argued.

* * *

They weighed anchor two hours before dawn, because Odile didn't believe in sleeping when there was water to cover, and Tam took the first watch at the tiller while the others bedded down among the cargo netting. The river ran wide and black here, the banks dissolved into reed-shadow, and the only lights were the old beacon towers — decommissioned, unlit for twenty years, their lamps rusted open like broken mouths. Navigation by dead reckoning and memory. It was, Tam reflected, exactly the kind of sailing the blank spaces on his charts made necessary. He had built this darkness one notation at a time. Now he was sailing in it.

That was when he saw the running light.

It came up the far channel maybe a quarter mile behind them — a single white lantern low on the water, moving at their speed, no more. A fishing boat would have shown nets, or a work lamp, or the particular drunken zigzag of a man running trotlines. This held a perfect line. Perfectly matched speed. Perfectly placed so that it never closed the distance and never fell behind, hanging in the corner of Tam's eye like a toothache.

He watched it for an hour. It watched back.

At the change of watch he woke Odile with a hand on her shoulder, and she came up out of sleep the way she always did — instantly, completely, no fog, as though sleep were just another dive she surfaced from. He nodded downstream. She looked for a long time. She didn't ask what it was. She asked, "How long?"

"Since the second bell. Maybe longer. I only saw it at second bell."

"Could be coincidence."

"A fishing boat doesn't hold station for an hour."

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't." She was quiet a moment, watching the light. Then: "Wake me if it changes anything. Speed, bearing, distance. Anything."

"It hasn't changed anything in an hour. That's what worries me."

Odile went forward to relieve Sefa, and Tam took the tiller again, and the white light hung behind them all through the gray turn of dawn, patient as debt.

* * *

By full morning the paranoia had settled over the barge like river fog, and Tam found he couldn't shake it, because paranoia was just pattern recognition with the volume turned up, and the patterns were real.

He laid the chest's contents out on the deck boards under an awning of canvas — he'd learned in the Registry Office that sunlight was the enemy of wet paper, and these pages had spent years underwater before the flood gave them back — and worked through them methodically while the crew pretended not to hover. Bo hovered closest, of course, reading over his shoulder in that silent fluent way, lips moving around the old script. Sefa worked rigging nearby with unnecessary industry, close enough to feel the deck through her feet, which Tam understood now was how she listened. Odile steered and watched the water and watched the water and watched the water.

The documents sorted themselves into three piles under Tam's hands.

The first pile was administrative: shipping abstracts, cargo classifications, the expungement order itself with its brass-stamped seal and its signature reduced to initials. Bureaucratic murder, itemized. He'd seen ten thousand pages like it. That was precisely what frightened him — how ordinary it was. No monologue, no villain's flourish. Just routing codes and priority stamps, evil processed in triplicate.

The second pile was correspondence, and it was worse. Letters between registry officials discussing the *disposition of sensitive materials* in language so hedged it took Tam three readings to understand they were discussing whether the census should be destroyed outright or merely buried where no one would dredge. One letter — unsigned, but the hand matched the expungement order — contained a sentence Tam copied out onto a scrap of his own paper because he needed to see it outside the original to believe it:

*"The names are the danger, not the ship. Ships sink every season. Let the hull rot; see that the lists do not surface. A drowned city is a tragedy. A counted one is a question."*

A counted one is a question. Tam read it five times. Somewhere in the middle of the fifth reading, Bo's voice said quietly, "They weren't afraid people would find out the city sank. Everyone knows it sank. They were afraid people would find out someone wrote down who was in it."

"Because a list can be checked," Tam said. "Against pension rolls. Against property claims. Against the descendants who are still alive and don't know why their grandparents' village isn't on any chart." He set the scrap down. "You can drown a city and call it weather. You can't drown a census and call it weather. Names have relatives."

The third pile was small — four sheets, water-warped, written in a hand Tam didn't recognize at first. Then he did recognize it, because he'd seen it on the letter that had started all this, the one folded inside Odile's coat, the one she touched like a talisman and read like a wound. Wren's handwriting. These pages were rougher — notes, fragments, the handwriting of someone working fast and hiding what she found. Dates. Locations. A phrase circled twice: *registry annex, leeward side, iron room, do not trust the harbor master.*

And beneath that, in the same hurried hand, a single line that made the morning go cold:

*If I stop writing, it means they were right to be afraid of me.*

Tam looked up. Odile was at the tiller, and she was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse — not asking, exactly. Waiting to see what his face did. He realized she had already read these pages. She had known since the chest came up out of the flood, and she had said nothing, and she was letting him arrive at it in his own time because that was the kind of woman she was: she handed you the truth the way you handed a diver a line, when you judged they were ready to hold their breath.

"Wren got into the registry annex," Tam said. "After the sinking. She was pulling the same threads we're pulling."

"Yes," Odile said.

"And then she stopped writing."

The river ran. Somewhere behind them, faint as a thought, the white light held its station.

"Yes," Odile said again, and turned back to the water, and that was all anyone said about it, because some cargoes you don't unload on an open deck.

* * *

The patrol boat showed itself at noon.

No more pretending, no more matched speed at quarter mile. It came up the channel flying the Admiralty ensign — a proper cutter, twin screws, the gray paint and the stub gun mount forward, and Tam's stomach dropped through the deck plates because he knew that hull number. He had entered it into a patrol rotation schedule himself, four years ago, in another life, with another man's pen. PC-114. Assigned waters: the lower reaches. Standing orders, per the section chief's memo: *monitor traffic; the navy won't discuss it.*

It didn't run them down. That was the elegant part. It simply adjusted course to parallel theirs, half a mile off, close enough to count their mast rings, far enough to claim it was passing. Twice, signal flags climbed its halyard — routine queries, Tam read them automatically, *state name and business* — and Odile answered them with the correct flags, *salvage vessel, wreck survey, licensed under harbor writ*, all true, all harmless, all exactly what a boat with nothing to hide would say. The cutter acknowledged and did not leave.

"They're logging us," Tam said. He kept his voice low, though at half a mile it hardly mattered. "Every hour, position and heading, relayed upstream. By tomorrow there'll be a file. By the day after, someone in an office will notice that our course, plotted out, points at the last known coordinates of a ship that officially does not exist."

"So we change course," Bo said.

"And they'll log that too, and someone will notice we changed it, and that will tell them more than the original course did." Tam laughed, short and bitter. "That's the trap. You can't outrun paperwork. I know because I was the paperwork. Some clerk somewhere is going to look at a plot of our positions and see a straight line to the Vesperine, and he'll pull the old files, and he'll find the expungement order, and then whatever happens next happens fast."

Sefa signed something long and fluid. Bo watched her hands, then said, slowly: "She says — she says the water will tell us before the clerks do. She says a boat like that draws nine feet and turns slow, and there's a fork in the channel ahead where the deep water goes east and the shallow goes west, and if we take the west fork they either follow us into shoals or let us go, and either way we learn what they want."

"What do they want?" Bo added, in their own voice, young and cracked. "I mean — actually. If we were smuggling brandy they'd have boarded us by now. They're *watching* us. Why watch?"

And Tam, who had spent eleven years inside the machine and knew its grammar the way Sefa knew the water, heard himself answer with the sentence he'd been circling since the lamp-lit stern, the sentence underneath all the others:

"Because boarding us ends the story. Watching us writes it. If they take the chest by force, there's a seizure, a report, witnesses — us. If we simply... fail. Run aground. Sink. Have an accident on some unsurveyed reach of river that no chart shows because the navy declined to discuss it — then there's nothing. Another boat lost on the water. Happens every season." He looked at the gray cutter, patient on the horizon, and felt the full weight of his own former profession settle on his shoulders like wet canvas. "We're not being pursued. We're being *managed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that somebody upstream has already decided how this ends, and they're waiting for us to walk into the ending."

The deck was quiet. The river muttered against the hull. Odile stood at the tiller with Wren's letter in her coat and the whole drowned nation somewhere ahead of them under the brown water, and Tam watched her make the decision he already knew she'd make, because he'd watched her dive, and Odile Dravenmoor did not come up until she had what she went down for.

"Then we stop walking into endings," she said. "Sefa — that west fork. How shallow?"

"Shallow enough to scare them," Sefa signed, and Bo's voice carried it, and for the first time since the white light appeared, something that was almost a smile moved across the barge.

Tam gathered the papers back into the chest and locked it, and as he did, his eye caught the expungement order one last time — the brass seal, the initials, the tidy bureaucratic grave dug for six hundred tons and everyone aboard. He had pulled that same stamp's handle a hundred times. He had never once wondered what happened to the people on the other side of the paper.

Behind them, PC-114 held its distance, and its distance held its meaning, and the river carried them all — the hunted and the hunters, the named and the expunged — toward the fork in the channel where the deep water went east and everything else went west.

Six tides, the harbor man had said. Tam looked at the sun, did the arithmetic, and didn't like the remainder.
