## 6. Downstream, Running Dark

The dark came down like a lid.

Sefa felt it before she saw it — the way the air pressure changed when the last of the dusk drained out of the sky, the way the river's voice deepened. She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Debt* with her bare feet on the deck plates and her palms flat against the spokes, and she listened with everything she had that wasn't ears.

Tam had killed the running lights an hour ago, at the bend below Harrowgate, and since then the world had been reduced to what Sefa could read through her hands, her soles, the bones of her jaw pressed to the wheel's worn wood. The engine talked to her through the hull — a good talk tonight, steady, the old diesel muttering its one long syllable. The current talked too, a low dragging pull against the rudder that told her they were making four knots against two, netting two, maybe two and a half if the channel stayed honest.

And behind them, far back but not far enough, the patrol boat talked loudest of all.

She couldn't hear its engines. She didn't need to. Its wake reached them first, a long rolling pulse in the water that came up through the keel and into her spine, a slow heavy heartbeat about a mile astern. It had been holding that distance for forty minutes. Not closing. Not falling behind. Just walking beside them in the dark, patient as a debt collector.

Sefa lifted one hand from the wheel and signed into the blackness beside her, knowing Bo was crouched at the chart table where the faint hooded glow of the lamp wouldn't show past the canvas skirt. *How far to the first lock?*

Bo caught the motion even in the dark — the boy had learned to watch hands the way other people learned to listen, and Sefa had decided early on that this was the mark of someone worth keeping. His answer came back in the halting signs he'd pieced together from her over six days aboard: *Two miles. Maybe less.*

*Patrol?*

Bo hesitated, then pointed down and aft, toward the water. He'd heard it too, then — or Odile had, up on the bow where she stood wrapped in an oilskin, staring back the way they'd come. Sefa signed again: *I know. I feel it.* And because his face was a pale smudge and she wanted him to understand, she added the sign she'd invented for this night, both fists turning slowly, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. *It is still there.*

Bo nodded. Then he did something he hadn't done before. He reached out and tapped her forearm twice — their signal for *yes*, for *understood*, for *we are doing this together* — and left his hand there a moment before going back to the charts.

Sefa turned her face forward so no one would see what that did to her.

She had run boats through the dark before. She'd run them through weather that peeled paint and through channels that existed only in her grandmother's mouth, and she had always done it alone, because alone was how you survived when every crew you'd ever shipped with got tired of writing notes in the rain and learning to look at your face instead of your hands. Alone meant nobody blamed you when the note-passing slowed the work. Alone meant nobody pitied you.

But alone also meant that when the patrol boat's wake pulsed through the hull, there was no one whose arm you could tap to say *stay low, stay quiet, I have this* — and no one who would tap back.

The river bent. She felt the bend coming three hundred yards before Tam's chart could have shown it, a soft asymmetry in the current's grip, the left bank shouldering out and shoving the main flow against the cut on the right. She brought the wheel over easy, easy, letting the *Kestrel's Debt* swing like a leaf finding its line, and behind her she heard — felt — Odile shift her weight on the foredeck to balance the turn without being asked.

That was new too. Six days ago Odile Dravenmoor steered by shouting. Now she moved like part of the boat.

* * *

The lock appeared as a change in the water's temper.

Above the gates the river pooled and went sullen, backing up against the masonry, and the eddy lines went slack and greasy. Sefa felt the transition under her feet the way a sighted person would see a threshold: the chop died, the current let go of the keel, and suddenly the engine's push was all theirs, unopposed. They were in the slack water above the weir, and dead ahead — she knew it from Tam's chart, from the survey he'd been disgraced for correcting, from the memory of a route she'd never run but had traced a thousand times in her grandmother's stories — were the gates of Lock Nine.

Lock Nine was the problem. Locks had lights, horns, bells, a lockkeeper on night rotation with a telephone wire straight to the harbor authority, and a harbor authority that had already sent one patrol boat after them. Running the lock legally meant hailing, signing papers, being logged, being seen. Being seen meant Varn's claim officers would know exactly which stretch of river they'd be working come morning, and the Meridian Fleet would be waiting at the Vesperine like spiders at a lamp.

So they would go through with the gates shut, the way smugglers did a century ago, the way her grandmother's people had done it when the river was a border and the border had teeth.

They'd planned it at dusk, crouched over Tam's charts by the galley stove. There was a service sluice on the east side of Lock Nine, a maintenance channel used for draining the lock chamber for repairs — a slot eight feet wide between the gate wall and the wing wall, gated by a timber flap that hadn't been maintained since before the war. At normal pool levels it was barred by a chain. But the spring flood had pushed the river a foot above pool, and at flood stage the sluice ran open, a chute of white water dropping the height of a man between stone walls.

Nobody ran a sluice at night. Nobody ran a sluice, period, unless they were insane or desperate or both.

Sefa had looked around the lamplight at the faces — Odile grim and certain, Tam chewing the end of his pencil down to splinters, Bo wide-eyed and trying to hide it — and she had held up her hands and said, aloud, carefully, the words she'd rehearsed: "I can feel the water better than any of you can see it. Let me take us through."

Odile had studied her for a long moment. Then she'd said, "Tell me what you need."

That was the sentence. That was the whole thing, the entire difference between every crew before and this one. *Tell me what you need.* Not *are you sure*. Not *it's too dangerous*. Tell me what you need, as though Sefa's needs were simply the next item on the list, as natural as rope or fuel.

What she needed was this: everyone in position, everyone silent, everyone trusting her hands.

* * *

They came off the bow with fenders rigged and lines coiled, and Sefa took the *Kestrel's Debt* down to a crawl.

At this speed the engine dropped to a murmur she felt more as warmth than sound, a cat purring through the sole plates. The slack water gave nothing back — no current to read, no chop — and so she read the walls instead. Stone spoke differently than water. A lock wall half a mile away announced itself as a subtle flattening of the air pressure on her left cheek, a deadness in the wind, and she steered by that deadness the way a blind man walks by the cold of a window.

Closer, the wall began to talk through the hull. Reflected wash came back off masonry in tight little pulses, and each pulse carried information: near or far, sheer or stepped, weed-hung or clean. She could tell the difference between dressed granite and old brick by the texture of the echo in her wrists. Her grandmother had taught her this on the tidal flats of the southern coast, kneeling in the shallows, pressing Sefa's small palms flat against wet rock while the swell came in. *The water tells you everything,* she'd signed. *People lie. Water doesn't. Your whole life people will assume you're missing the world. Let them. You're getting more of it than they are.*

The gate loomed. Sefa felt it as a vast vertical presence, a cliff of oak and iron that ate the wind entirely, and she swung the bow east, toward the wing wall, toward the slot.

There. The sluice announced itself the moment they entered its reach — a rising tremolo in the hull, a fast bright chatter unlike anything else in the river, the sound of water being squeezed and furious about it. It grew until it filled her whole body, until her teeth buzzed in her jaw and the wheel hummed against her palms, and she knew without looking that the drop was ahead, that the chute was live, that the flood was doing their work for them.

She raised one fist. Felt rather than saw the crew freeze — Odile at the bow with a boathook, Tam at the stern line, Bo flat on the cabin roof holding the anchor light they weren't allowed to light.

Then she put the bow in.

The sluice took them like a hand taking a coin.

For three seconds — she counted them in heartbeats, five, six, seven — the *Kestrel's Debt* was not a boat but a thing being poured, dropped down a throat of standing waves, the hull slamming stone on both sides close enough to touch, spray coming over the rails in a cold slap she felt land on her scalp and shoulders. The wheel fought her with a living strength. She gave it what it demanded, neither fighting the water nor surrendering to it, reading each slam and surge a half-second before it arrived because the water always told her first — always, always told her first — and steering into the chaos the way her grandmother had steered the *Maren Lee* through the Bars passage in a gale, by feel, by faith, by the absolute refusal to be afraid of a river that was only doing what rivers do.

Then the chute spat them out, and the black water below the lock received them almost gently, and they were through.

Sefa realized she was shaking. Not from fear — from the sheer animal expenditure of it, every nerve she owned spent in seven seconds. She leaned on the wheel and breathed, and felt the boat breathe with her, and felt something else besides:

Hands. Small hands, tapping her shoulder blade. Bo, come down from the cabin roof, signing fast and clumsy and radiant: *You — we — alive!* He couldn't find the signs so he just grabbed her hand and pressed it flat against the deck plate, against the hull, as if to say *feel that, feel us moving, we're here, you did it.*

And then Odile's hand on her other shoulder, firm and brief, and Tam's voice — she felt the words more than heard them, felt the shape of them through the man's palm pressed to her back, the way he'd learned to do, tapping out the rhythm of speech against her spine like a telegraph key: *Never doubted. Not for a second.*

He was lying, probably. It didn't matter. It was the first time anyone had bothered to lie to her kindly in eleven years, and she found she'd have run ten more sluices to earn it.

* * *

The patrol boat was waiting below the locks.

Sefa felt it before anyone else knew — a hard regular pulse in the water, quarter mile downstream, dead center in the channel, engines idling at that particular throttle note she'd catalogued an hour ago. They'd come through the lock the legal way, or been waved through by a lockkeeper with a telephone, and now they sat across the river's throat like a cork in a bottle.

She signed to Bo: *Stop. Engine stop.* Bo relayed it to Odile with a whistle-less hand wave, and the *Kestrel's Debt* drifted, silent, into the black shadow of the lock's lower wall, where the overflow from the sluice made a constant turbulence that would shred their own signature into the river's noise.

They hung there in the dark, twenty yards from a navy vessel bristling with searchlights, and Sefa put her bare feet wide on the deck and listened to the enemy with her whole body.

The patrol boat's idling engines. Its generator, a higher whine. Footsteps on its deck — two sets, heavy boots, pacing. The groan of its fenders against its own hull. And beneath it all, the thing that mattered: its searchlight was off. They were sitting dark too, watching the channel, waiting for a boat to come down with its lights on so they could pounce. They expected the fugitives to be nervous. Nervous people lit themselves up like festival lanterns, checking their own position, afraid of the shoals.

Nervous people didn't have Sefa Brightwater at the wheel.

She signed to Bo, slow and clear: *They wait for lights. We give none. Follow the wall, my hands, my speed.* Then she took Odile's wrist and guided it to the rail, pressed the woman's palm flat against the gunwale, and signed against her arm: *When I steer, you will feel the turn in the wood before it happens. Trust the wood. Trust me.*

Odile's answer came back through her own fingers, signed clumsily but unmistakably: *I trust you.*

They went down the channel hugging the north bank so close that Tam swore afterward he could have touched the reeds, at barely steerage way, invisible inside the patrol boat's own blind spot — directly beneath its bow shadow, where no searchlight would ever think to sweep because no sane crew would pass within fifty yards of a warship running dark. Sefa read the patrol boat's wake as they crossed it, a long oily swell that rolled under the keel and tried to slew them, and she caught it with the rudder before it finished forming, feeling the correction in her wrists a full beat before the hull even began to answer.

Fifty yards. Thirty. The footsteps overhead paused — she felt the vibration of a boot stopping on the patrol boat's deck, felt a body lean on a rail almost directly above them — and then the boot walked on, and the searchlight stayed dark, and the *Kestrel's Debt* slid past beneath the guns of the navy like a fish under a heron's beak.

A mile downstream, when the pulse of pursuit engines had faded to nothing behind them, Odile came aft and stood beside her at the wheel. For a while neither of them did anything. The river ran. The stars came and went behind torn cloud.

Then Odile took Sefa's hand and, against her palm, spelled out the words slowly, letter by letter, the old two-handed alphabet Sefa had taught her on the second day: *T-H-A-N-K Y-O-U.*

Sefa smiled and spelled back: *D-O-N'T T-H-A-N-K M-E Y-E-T.*

Because her palms were still full of the river, and the river was telling her something new. Far ahead, past the next bend, past the drowned country's edge where the charts went blank and the leeward passage waited — the water had changed its voice. It had gone deep and strange and expectant, the way it did over wrecks, over big sunken things, over history lying on the bottom with its eyes open.

Somewhere down there, the Vesperine was waiting. And somewhere behind them, gaining, Captain Ilse Varn was waking to find an empty channel and a trail gone cold.

Sefa put the wheel over and ran on into the dark, toward whatever the water was trying to say.
