## 7. The Drowned Census

Bo had learned to read the way other children learned to steal — in secret, quickly, and with the constant expectation of being caught.

The script of old Calveth was not taught anywhere anymore. It lived in the margins of a prayer book Bo's mother kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a trunk, and in the faded signage of river towns that had been Calvethi before they were anything else — a baker's ghost-letters above a shuttered door, a ferry company's name worn into stone until only the shapes remained. Bo's mother had traced them with one finger and made Bo trace them after her, saying the sounds low, like passwords. *This is ours,* she'd said. *They can take everything else but not this.* Then she'd died, or left — Bo had stopped distinguishing between the two — and the reading had become the only inheritance that fit in a coat pocket.

So when Odile Dravenmoor spread the water-swollen papers from the chest across the deck of the *Leeward Girl* and asked, almost casually, if anyone could make sense of the writing, Bo's heart did something complicated and shameful: it leapt.

*They need me.* That was the first thought. The second, arriving on its heels like a debt collector: *they'll stop needing me.*

Bo had been aboard nine days. Long enough to know that Tam slept with his boots on and talked to his charts as though they might contradict him; long enough to know that Sefa communicated in a language of taps and glances that Bo was only beginning to parse — two knuckles on the rail meant *look*, a flat palm meant *stop*, and the rare, astonishing thumbs-up against her sternum meant something so warm Bo had gone red for an hour afterward. Long enough to know that Odile never stopped moving. She coiled rope when there was no rope left to coil. She re-tarred seams that didn't leak. Watching her was like watching someone run from a fire only she could see.

Bo knew about running. Bo had gotten very good at it.

Now the papers lay drying on canvas in the stern, weighted at the corners with brass fittings, and the whole crew stood around them the way people stand around a body.

* * *

The chest had given up its contents reluctantly. Salt had welded the lock; Sefa had opened it with a cold chisel and a patience that seemed almost devotional. Inside, wrapped in oilskin gone stiff as bark: bundles of documents, ledgers, correspondence, all bearing the seal Bo recognized from a hundred half-erased doorways — the leeward wave, curled under a crown of three stars.

"State papers," Tam said, and his voice had gone flat in the particular way it went flat when he was frightened. "These are state papers. Why does a salvage chest full of state papers come floating out of a drowned country?"

"Because the river gives things back," Odile said. "Eventually. To whoever's standing there."

She said it lightly, but Bo saw her hand close around the chest's edge, knuckles whitening, and let go again deliberately, like setting down something hot.

The paper was Calvethi rag-stock, dense and pale, and the ink had survived better than anyone expected — the oilskin had kept the worst out. Bo crouched over the first bundle and felt the old thrill, the secret thrill, of letters resolving into meaning. It was like surfacing. Like breaking through into air after a long time holding your breath.

"This is correspondence," Bo said. "Harbor administration. Dated—" Bo worked through the numerals, which were beautiful and strange, little boats with sails for ones. "Dated eleven months before the flooding."

"Flooding," Tam repeated. "You mean the sinking."

"I mean what it says." Bo turned the page toward him. "*In the year the waters took the city.* That's how they date things after. Before that, it's just years. After, everything is *the year the waters came.*"

Nobody spoke. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, long and mournful, and Sefa turned her head toward it with the unconscious precision of someone who read the world through her skin.

Odile crouched beside Bo. Close enough that Bo could smell tar and river water and, underneath, something clean like iron. "Read me the important parts," she said. "Skip the harbor complaints."

"There aren't any harbor complaints." Bo flipped through the bundle, faster now, the shapes coming easier, the way a current takes you once you stop fighting it. "This is all... requisitions. Cargo manifests. Crew rosters. This one's a passenger list." Bo paused. "It's long."

"How long?"

Bo counted silently, lips moving. "Four hundred names. Maybe more. There are addenda."

"A passenger list for a ship that size would be—"

"It's not a ship's list." Bo had reached the header, and the header changed everything, and Bo sat back on their heels without meaning to, the way you step back from a drop you hadn't seen. "It says—"

*Say it*, Bo told themselves. *Say it and be useful and don't think about what happens after.*

"It says *Census of Souls, Being a True and Full Accounting of All Persons Lost in the Submersion of Calveth, Compiled by Order of the Provisional Authority, for Transport Aboard the Vesperine.*"

The name landed on the deck between them like something dropped from a height.

"The Vesperine," Tam said quietly. "The wreck we're diving."

"The wreck nobody's allowed to name," Odile said.

Bo looked up. Odile wasn't looking at the papers. She was looking at the water, at the brown-green slide of it past the hull, as though she could see down through forty feet of murk to the black ribs they'd spent weeks mapping. Her face did something Bo had never seen it do — it went still. Completely still. Not calm. Still the way deep water is still.

"Keep reading," Odile said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon. Tam brought out his magnifier and his naval training and organized the bundles into a taxonomy on the deck planks: correspondence, manifests, engineering folios, and the census itself, which ran to dozens of pages in a clerk's meticulous hand, each entry ruled and numbered. Name. Age. District. Trade. Next of kin, where known. Some entries had annotations in a second hand, smaller, hurried — Bo began to recognize it as they went, a handwriting that leaned hard to the right as though the writer were always slightly ahead of the line.

"That second hand," Bo said. "It shows up in the margins of maybe a third of these pages. Corrections. Additions. Someone was checking the clerk's work."

"An auditor?" Tam offered.

"Someone who cared," Bo said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

Sefa settled cross-legged near the canvas and didn't touch anything, but she watched Bo's mouth as they read aloud, and Bo realized — with a small, disorienting warmth — that she was following the words. Not the Calvethi, but the cadence. She'd begun learning the rhythm of Bo's voice the way she learned everything: through vibration, through attention, through the patient accumulation of pattern. When Bo hesitated over a numeral, Sefa tapped the deck twice — *keep going* — and Bo kept going.

The names came out of Bo's mouth one after another, and it was strange, it was unbearable and strange, to speak four hundred dead people into the afternoon air of a river they had once called their own. *Maren Ostrevik, age thirty-four, weaver, district of the Lower Terraces. Jem and Ansel Corvane, ages six and nine, next of kin: mother, listed below. Old Halvard, trade unrecorded, district unrecorded, noted only as 'the lamplighter.'* Somebody had written *the lamplighter* and somebody else had let it stand, and Bo found their eyes stinging and blamed the wind off the water.

"These aren't passengers," Tam said at last. His magnifier hung forgotten from its cord. "Passengers board a ship. These people drowned in the city. This is a record of everyone the flood killed, being carried out on the ship that—"

He stopped. Bo watched him arrive at the rest of the sentence and decide, visibly, not to say it yet.

"That's why it was expunged," Bo said instead. They'd heard Tam use the word the night before, hunched over the chest, and had pretended to be asleep. "Not lost. Expunged. You expunge a thing you want to have never existed. But someone put this census aboard anyway. Someone hid it inside the erasure."

"To bring it out," Odile said. "To carry the names somewhere they'd survive."

"Or to bury them deeper," Tam said. "Depending on who won."

The light was going amber. Moths had begun to knock softly against the lantern glass. And it was then, sorting the last bundle — the engineering folios, the ones Tam had set apart with a kind of dread — that Bo found the order.

* * *

It was a single sheet, heavier stock than the rest, folded twice. The seal had been pressed into wax that had cracked but held. Bo unfolded it and the formal script rose up, ornate where the census had been plain, the language of authority rather than grief.

Bo read it once silently. Then Bo stopped being able to see it clearly, because the world had tilted, and it took a moment to understand that the tilting was happening inside.

"Bo?" Odile's voice. "What is it?"

"It's an order." Bo's own voice sounded far away, borrowed. "An execution order. For the flood." The words came out in pieces, like cargo dropped from a great height. "It authorizes the breach of the levees and the canal gates. It cites — I don't — some justification, rebellion, insolvency, the words are legal, they're meant to blur — it schedules the submersion for the night of the spring tide. And it's signed."

Bo looked up. Odile was very close, kneeling on the wet planks, and her eyes had the fixed quality of someone watching a winch cable take a load far beyond its rating.

"Signed by whom?"

Bo turned the page around. Pointed. Said the surname because there was no way not to say it, because it had been sitting in Bo's mouth since the third word of the signature block, heavy as ballast:

"Dravenmoor. First name abbreviated — *A.* Dravenmoor. Below it, countersigned by two others, but the primary seal, the authority—" Bo swallowed. "It's hers. Yours. It's your family's name, Odile."

The river kept moving. That was the terrible thing, Bo thought later. The river just kept moving, slapping gently at the hull, utterly indifferent, while Odile Dravenmoor knelt on the deck of a boat above the grave her family had helped fill.

She didn't recoil. Bo had expected recoiling — had braced for it, the way you brace for a wave. Instead Odile reached out and took the page, slowly, with both hands, the way Sefa handled rigging under load. She read it top to bottom. Bo watched her lips move faintly over the formal phrases, translating nothing, just mouthing the weight of it.

"My grandfather's name was Aldous," she said finally. Her voice was level. Too level, the way a deck is level right before it isn't. "Aldous Dravenmoor. He was — there were stories. Contracts. He built locks. He built *levees*, for God's sake, whole systems of them, he was famous for it, the man who kept the water out." A sound escaped her that was almost a laugh and was not one. "I used to brag about it. As a kid. My grandfather keeps the rivers in their beds."

"Odile," Tam started, "a signature could be—"

"Don't." She said it without heat, which was worse. "Don't soften it yet. Let me hold it the way it is first."

And that, more than anything, was what frightened Bo — not the name on the page, but the discipline of the woman holding it. The same discipline Bo had watched on every dive, every coiled rope, every seam re-tarred at midnight: Odile Dravenmoor could carry enormous weight without showing strain, and Bo understood suddenly that this was not strength. It was a person who had learned that if she ever once set the weight down, she would find out what was underneath her.

Sefa touched Odile's arm. Two fingers, light, on the wrist. Odile looked at her, and Sefa signed something small and private against her own collarbone — Bo didn't know the sign, but Tam did, and Tam looked away, jaw tight.

"What did she say?" Bo asked.

"She said," Tam answered, after a moment, "that a name on a page is not the same as a hand on a gate. And that she's watched Odile dive wrecks for strangers' dead without flinching, so she'll watch her stand under this too."

Odile closed her eyes. Opened them. Folded the order along its original creases, precisely, and set it in the center of the canvas where the lantern light fell fullest, where none of them could pretend they hadn't seen it.

"Then here's what stands under it," she said. "Every name in that census. Four hundred of them, plus the addenda. And my family's name on the order that put them there." She looked at Bo, and her gaze was terrible and steady and, beneath it, pleading in a way she would probably deny to her grave. "Read me the names tomorrow. All of them. If we're going to carry this thing out of the water, I want to know exactly how much it weighs."

* * *

That night Bo couldn't sleep. They climbed into the bow with the blanket from their bunk and sat with their knees drawn up, watching the navigation lights of distant traffic crawl along the far bank like slow, patient insects.

The guilt had come in with the cold, the way fog comes off a river at dusk — not all at once but everywhere at once, until there was no part of the air that wasn't it. Because here was the thing Bo hadn't said, the thing sitting in Bo's chest like swallowed anchor-chain: *I knew the script. I've always known the script. My mother taught me from a prayer book she wouldn't show priests.* Which meant Bo's people had been Calvethi too, or near enough — had been the ones who dated their years by the waters, who lit lamps for the drowned, who passed the letters down in oilcloth because someone, someday, might need to read them.

And Bo had nearly traded that inheritance for a berth and a hot meal. Had stood over the census of Bo's own dead and felt, first, before anything else, the small grubby relief of *being needed.*

"You're awake."

Odile lowered herself onto the foredeck hatch a few feet away. She didn't ask to share the blanket and Bo didn't offer. That was the shape of trust between them so far: adjacent, unsentimental, real.

"Couldn't sleep," Bo said.

"No," Odile agreed. "Me neither." She was quiet a while. The water talked against the hull. "My sister Wren would have liked you," she said at last, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "She collected strays who could read things."

"I'm not a stray."

"No," Odile said mildly. "Of course not."

Bo hugged their knees tighter. Downriver, unseen, the Vesperine lay in her trench of silt with four hundred names sealed in her hold, and somewhere in the dark water a letter waited in a handwriting Bo hadn't seen yet but already dreaded — because Odile carried it everywhere, wrapped in its own oilcloth, and sometimes at night Bo had watched her hold it without opening it, the way you hold a wound you're afraid to look at.

"Odile," Bo said. "The second handwriting. In the census margins. The one making corrections."

"What about it?"

"I've been thinking about it all evening. The lean of it. The way the writer rushes ahead of the rule lines." Bo turned to look at her, and the moon came out from behind cloud cover at exactly the wrong moment, lighting both their faces. "I've seen that hand before. Today. On the letter you keep in your coat."

Odile went very still.

"Whoever checked the clerk's work," Bo said, "whoever added the missing names, who fought to get that census aboard the Vesperine at all — Odile, I think it was Wren."

The river moved. The lights on the far bank crawled. And Odile Dravenmoor, who could carry anything, finally looked like a woman standing in water over her head.
