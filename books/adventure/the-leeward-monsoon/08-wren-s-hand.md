## 8. Wren's Hand

The letter had been waiting for her for three days.

Odile knew it the way she knew weather coming off the water — a pressure behind the ribs, a low gray weight in the air of the cabin. The chest sat open on the table between the lamp and the porthole, its state papers sorted into Tam's careful stacks, its brass seal winking dully whenever the boat rolled. And at the bottom of the stack, set apart from everything else because no one had known what else to do with it, lay the letter in Wren's handwriting.

She had read the first line four times already. *If you are reading this, then you went looking.* Four words, and she'd had to put it down each time, walk out onto the deck, breathe until the river stopped tilting.

Tonight there was nowhere to walk. Rain needled the cabin roof in long silver strokes, and the Vesperine waited below them in forty feet of black water, and Bo was asleep with a dictionary of old Calvethi script open on his chest, and Sefa was up on deck keeping watch by feel, one bare foot hooked under a rail stanchion, reading the river through the soles of her feet. Tam had gone to his charts. Everyone had found something to do with their hands, which was how this crew said *I am here if you need me* — and Odile understood that language perfectly, because it was hers first.

She poured herself two fingers of plum brandy she'd been saving for a victory she hadn't earned yet, and drank half of it standing up, and sat down, and unfolded the letter all the way this time.

The paper was good stock. Calvethi mill paper, watermarked with the leeward current rose — the same mark stamped on the brass fitting in her pocket, the same mark burning like a coal on every page of the census fragments Bo had translated. Someone had given Wren good paper. That detail undid her before the words did: her sister, who had grown up writing sums on slate because ink cost too much, holding government stationery in her hands.

*If you are reading this, then you went looking,* the letter began again, and this time Odile let it keep going.

*I don't know when you started looking or what you think you'll find, but I know you, little sister, so I know you didn't stop at the fence line. You never did. When they told us the mine was flooded and closed, you were the one who walked the perimeter until the guards made you leave. I've thought about that walk for years. I want you to know I'm not sorry I taught you to be stubborn.*

Odile's hand was shaking. She pressed it flat against the table and kept reading.

*You'll have questions about dates, so let me give them to you plainly, because I know how your mind works and you will build a whole architecture out of gaps.*

*I did not drown in the mine.*

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere forward, the hull ticked as it cooled.

*I got out. Eleven of us got out, through the drainage adit on the north face — the one the company sealed after, the one they told everyone was too collapsed to hold survivors. It held us fine. We came up into the gorse at dusk and heard them calling us dead down in the valley. They'd already started the lists. I remember thinking: they're fast at this. They're very fast at telling people who is gone.*

*Eleven of us, Odile. And not one record anywhere that we lived. Not a rescue report, not a hospital intake, nothing. We were dead on paper within a day, and being dead turned out to be useful, because nobody looks for a dead girl. So I stayed dead. I learned to move quietly and I learned to ask questions the way you learn to hold a knife — badly at first, then better.*

Odile set the letter down. Her breath had gone shallow and high in her chest, the way it did at thirty meters when the regulator started to fight her. She made herself breathe low, the way she'd teach a new diver. In through the nose. Out slow. The past has physically surfaced; you do not panic at depth; you work the problem.

She picked it up again.

*Here is what I found, in eleven years of asking. The mine flood wasn't an accident. I know you never believed it was either, but belief isn't proof, and proof is what I went after instead of going home, and I want you to understand that choice even though I know it hurt you. If I'd come back alive, they'd have known someone survived. They'd have known someone was asking. Everything I built would have come apart in a week, and you'd have been standing next to me when it did. Staying dead was the only way to protect you. I'm sorry. I'd do it again. That's the worst thing I've ever written and it's true.*

*The mine was practice. That's the part that took me years to accept. They flooded the Dravenmoor workings to test a method — charges placed along a seam, timed to a water table, engineered so the collapse would look like nature having its way. It worked beautifully. Two hundred and six people died in a hole in the ground and every document called it seepage. And then they took the same engineers and the same method and they pointed them at Calveth.*

The name landed in Odile's chest like a dropped anchor.

*You've seen the wreck by now, or you wouldn't have my letter. The Vesperine wasn't carrying treasure. It was carrying the count. Every soul registered in Calveth when the order came down — names, ages, districts, next of kin. A census of the drowned, compiled before the drowning, filed afterward as manifest. They needed to know exactly how many people they were erasing. Precision was the whole point. You can't falsify a nation's death without knowing its number.*

*I got aboard the Vesperine twice while she still floated. I copied what I could. What I couldn't copy, I hid where I could find it later, and I'm trusting you to be the later. The vault is real. It's intact. I made sure of that myself, and I paid for making sure, and I don't regret the price.*

*Which brings me to the part I've rewritten nine times, so forgive me if it comes out crooked.*

*I'm dying, Odile. Slowly, and on my own terms, but dying. The wet lung, from the adit, from years of it. The doctors in the capital gave me a winter. I'm writing this in autumn, by a window, watching the harbor ice come in early. I have time enough to finish hiding things properly and not time enough to finish the work. That's yours now, if you want it. I won't pretend I'm not handing you something heavy. I carried it alone for eleven years because I chose to, and choosing it nearly broke me more than once, and the only thing that held me together was the thought that someday, somehow, it would end up in your stubborn hands.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you go.*

*Look at the signatures. On the flooding orders, on the census authorization, on the compensation ledgers — look at every signature, and when you find ours, and you will find ours, I need you to promise me you won't let it stop you. Grandfather signed. I know. I've known since year three. He signed for money and then he signed again to keep quiet about the money, and he told himself the story men like him always tell, that the water would have risen anyway, that he just moved the date. I hated him for years. Then I watched what hating him did to me and I put it down like a tool too heavy to carry. Not forgiveness. Just — setting it down. You'll have to find your own way to do that. I can't hand you that part.*

*What I can hand you is this: the names are down there. All of them. Two hundred thousand people who were told, in effect, that they had never existed. As long as the census stays buried, the lie holds. Raise it, and every descendant of Calveth gets back the one thing that was stolen cleanest — the fact of their dead.*

*I love you. I loved you the whole time I was gone. I know you did the arithmetic on that — eleven years, zero letters — and I know what the sum looked like from your side. From mine it was a different equation. Forgive me the mathematics. Sisters shouldn't have to speak in ciphers, but we were raised by a family that dealt in them, and we became what we were raised by, both of us, just in opposite directions.*

*Finish it, or don't. But if you dive the Vesperine, go gently. She's held our secrets a long time. She deserves a careful hand.*

*Wren*

The signature was small and slanted left, the same hand that had addressed Odile's birthday letters once a year until the year it stopped. Below it, in different ink, someone official had written a case number and the word *CLOSED*, and stamped it twice.

Odile sat for a long time without moving.

The brandy had gone warm in her glass. The rain eased to a whisper and then to nothing, and the river breathed against the hull, patient as it had been patient for twenty years, and Odile finally understood that it hadn't been mocking her all this time. It had been holding her sister's work the way it held everything — without judgment, without hurry, waiting for someone strong enough to carry it up.

She had spent her whole adult life moving. Dive after dive, contract after contract, town after town, because stopping meant remembering, and remembering meant the mine, and the mine meant Wren, and Wren meant the day the lists came out and Odile had stood at the fence line at nineteen years old and believed them. Believed the seepage report. Believed the closed verdict. Had grieved a death that never happened and buried a sister who was, at that exact moment, alive somewhere in the world, learning to ask questions with a knife.

Every dive had been a way of not stopping long enough to feel that. And underneath the feeling, all along, had been this: not grief at all, but its counterfeit. Because Wren hadn't died in the water. Wren had died of the wet lung in a rented room in the capital, alone, having chosen to be unfindable, and Odile had missed her by eleven years and never known. There was no grave to visit. There was no body. There was only a letter with a case number stamped over a signature, and the bureaucratic cruelty of it — *CLOSED*, as if a person could be — made something in Odile's chest go quiet and cold and very clear.

She got up. She walked the length of the cabin, touched the bulkhead, walked back. Sat down. Picked up the letter and read it a third time, slower, the way she'd read a survey chart before a dive — marking hazards, noting depths, memorizing the route.

*They flooded the Dravenmoor workings to test a method.*

Her grandfather's name was in these papers. Tam had flagged three documents with red thread and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness, and Odile had known anyway. She had felt the shape of it through the paper the way Sefa felt the river through her feet. She was not surprised. Surprise would have required hope, and she had run out of that particular ballast years ago.

But knowing and reading were different animals. Reading was the moment the cold water closed over your head and you discovered whether you'd trained your body to trust the procedure. Odile sat in the lamplight with her family's crime in front of her and ran the procedure. Breathe low. Work the problem. Do not bolt for the surface.

*Go gently,* Wren had written. *She deserves a careful hand.*

"Okay," Odile said aloud, to the empty cabin, to the rain-washed dark beyond the porthole, to the wreck lying patient in the current below. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Okay."

Bo stirred in his bunk, script dictionary sliding, and caught it without waking — quick hands, a runaway's reflexes. Odile watched him sleep for a moment and felt the strange new weight settle onto her shoulders and found, to her astonishment, that it fit. It was heavier than grief. Grief had made her run. This made her want to stand.

Because here was the thing the letter had done, the thing eleven years of drifting had never managed: it had given her sister back. Not the drowned girl of the lists, not the ghost she'd been diving away from all this time, but Wren entire — stubborn, meticulous, funny even in farewell (*forgive me the mathematics*), alive for eleven more years than Odile had allowed her, working the problem right up until the window iced over. Wren had not been taken by the water. Wren had fought the water's owners and lost only to her own lungs, and she had spent her last autumn folding the truth into oilcloth and good Calvethi paper and addressing it, across the impossible distance, to the one person alive who would never stop looking.

Odile drained the rest of the brandy. Then she took the letter to the chart table, weighted its corners flat, and laid the Vesperine's survey beside it — her own soundings, her own careful lines, drawn over three seasons without ever once letting herself ask why she kept coming back. Now she knew why. The river had known before she did. It had been saving the letter for her the way it saved everything, and the spring flood had simply been the last hand on the clock.

She sharpened a pencil. She began to mark the survey: the hold, the collapsed section aft, the corridor Wren must have swum twice while the ship still floated above her. *The vault is real. It's intact. I made sure of that myself, and I paid for making sure.*

Paid how? Odile's pencil stopped. The phrase sat wrong — too calm, too finished, the way Wren wrote when she was leaving out the worst of it to spare someone. Odile read the paragraph again, hunting the margins of the sentence for what her sister had folded away, and found nothing, and understood that the nothing was itself information. Whatever Wren had paid, she had decided Odile should learn it from the wreck rather than the page. Which meant it was bad. Which meant it was down there, waiting, along with two hundred thousand names and an order bearing the Dravenmoor signature.

Outside, footsteps crossed the deck — Sefa changing her watch, unhurried, reading the night through her feet. A moment later Tam's silhouette passed the porthole, headed below with a lantern, and paused at the cabin door. He didn't knock. He looked at the letter spread under her hands, at the survey, at the pencil marks blooming across her soundings like frost, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once and move on without a word. The crew's language again. *I am here if you need me.* She was beginning to believe they meant it.

Odile bent back over the chart. The tide would turn in four hours; the fleet's claim clock was running; there was no time to waste and, for the first time in her life, no urge to waste it running. She drew a line from the hold to the aft corridor and wrote a single word beside it in her own hand, small and certain:

*Vault.*

Then, beneath it, because some inheritances deserve to be witnessed: *Wren was here first.*

The lamp burned. The river breathed. Down in the dark, the Vesperine held her cargo of names, and somewhere inside that drowned steel, a hidden cache waited with the patience of its maker — a dead woman's careful hands reaching up through twenty years of current to close around her sister's living ones.

Odile blew out the lamp and went to wake the crew. They had a vault to find, and a tide to beat, and a family debt to pay in full.

And she knew, now, exactly where to dive.
