## 9. Tide Two: The Fleet

Tam Voss had spent eleven years drawing coastlines for a navy that lied to him on paper, so he had developed a professional instinct for when a chart was wrong. It was the same instinct that told him, two hours before anyone else saw it, that the smudge on the southern horizon was not weather.

He was at the stern rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* — Bo had named her, to everyone's groaning protest, after a line in a Calvethi ballad about a bird that outlived its owner — pretending to correct their running survey of the channel. In truth he was watching the water. The river here ran wide and brown-green, braided around sandbars that shifted with every flood season, and it carried its secrets the way all rivers did: reluctantly, and only sideways. The wind had come around to the southwest an hour past, hauling the smell of silt and diesel off the lower reaches, and beneath it Tam caught something else — coal smoke, and the particular greasy reek of a steam plant run hard.

Then the smudge resolved.

Three hulls, riding low and heavy, coming upriver against the current in echelon. The lead ship was a salvage tender of the kind Meridian Salvage Fleet kept like hunting dogs — black-bottomed, crane-armed, her deck cluttered with winches and decompression tanks, her funnel laying down a rope of smoke so thick it looked deliberate, a flag planted in the sky. Behind her came a barge with a derrick tall as a church steeple, and behind that, half-hidden in her own wake, a smaller vessel with the clean lines of something built for speed rather than work.

Tam lowered his borrowed glass and did not call out right away. He stood there doing arithmetic instead. Six tides to raise what they'd come to raise; this was the second morning; and Meridian had found them anyway, which meant either Captain Ilse Varn was luckier than any salvager alive or someone had told her exactly where the *Kestrel's Debt* would be. He thought of the patrol boat that had shadowed them through the locks three nights ago, running dark, Sefa reading the water by the soles of her bare feet while Tam held his breath below decks. He thought of the word *expunged* in the navy ledger, inked over the Vesperine's name like a hand pressed flat over a mouth.

Someone had told her. Someone always told her. That was how the machinery worked — he'd seen its gears from the inside, once, before they'd ground his career into paste.

"Bo," he said quietly, because the boy was coiling line nearby with the restless energy of someone who hadn't slept well, "go get Odile. Don't say why. Just get her."

Bo looked up, followed Tam's gaze south, and went pale in the way he did — all at once, like a lamp turned down. Then he ran.

* * *

They gathered in the cockpit while the fleet closed the distance, and Tam laid it out with the flat economy he'd learned in briefing rooms: three vessels, Meridian livery, lead tender flying Varn's personal pennant — a white gull split by a black bar, which Tam knew because he'd once drawn it, badly, for a naval intelligence report nobody read. Estimated arrival at their anchorage: under an hour.

"They can't just take it," Bo said. "There are laws. Wreck claims, salvage rights—"

"There are," Tam agreed. "And there are lawyers who make those laws into a net with holes exactly the size of whatever Meridian wants to pull through them. Varn doesn't break rules, boy. She owns the people who write them."

Sefa stood apart, arms crossed, watching the horizon with her whole body tilted toward it as if she could hear the fleet through the water. Maybe she could. She signed something short and sharp, and Bo translated, his voice reluctant: "She says we should run now, while the tide's still with us."

"And go where?" Tam asked. "The leeward passage is a day north. They'd run us down in open water with those engines. And if we scatter—" he looked at Odile then, finally, because this part wasn't tactics anymore, "—if we scatter, whatever's raised gets picked up piece by piece, and every piece goes into Varn's hold."

Odile hadn't spoken since she'd come up from checking the dive gear. She stood at the rail with the morning light on her face, and Tam watched her do the thing she did — the stillness, the long look at the water, as if the answer were written somewhere below the surface and she only needed to wait for the river to give it up. Her hands were quiet at her sides. Her hands were never quiet. That was how he knew how bad this was.

"No," she said at last. "We don't run. We let her come alongside, and we see what she wants. She didn't send the patrol boat to board us in the night. She wants to talk first." A pause. "That means talking costs her something. I want to know what."

"It could cost us everything," Tam said.

Odile turned and looked at him, and there it was underneath the calm — not fear exactly, but the old grief wearing fear's clothing, the look of a woman who had already lost the one thing that mattered and therefore found it difficult to be impressed by threats. "Then we'll know where we stand," she said. "I'm tired of standing in fog."

* * *

Captain Ilse Varn came across in a gig crewed by four silent sailors, and she came alone, which Tam noted and distrusted. Powerful people traveled with retinues; powerful people who wanted something traveled light, because lightness said *this is friendly, this is informal*, and informality was where the knife went in.

She stepped aboard the *Kestrel's Debt* like she owned the water she crossed, which, in a sense, she did — Meridian held salvage concessions on every major river system in the province, stamped and sealed and renewed without review for thirty years. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, gray going to iron at the temples, with the sun-cured leather of a face that had spent four decades squinting into weather. Her coat was good wool, unadorned. Her boots were salt-stained. Everything about her was calibrated to say *I am one of you, a working woman of the water*, and everything about her eyes said otherwise. They were pale, nearly colorless, and they moved over the little boat — the patched sails, the dive gear lashed under canvas, Bo hovering at the mast, Sefa motionless by the wheel, Tam trying to be furniture — with the unhurried thoroughness of an assessor.

"Captain Dravenmoor." Her voice was low and pleasant, a voice for sharing brandy. "Ilse Varn. You've been making excellent time."

"You've been following me faster," Odile said.

Varn smiled as if Odile had paid her a compliment. "May I?" She gestured at the cockpit bench, waited for nothing, sat. She arranged herself with the ease of a woman who had negotiated in worse places — engine rooms, sinking decks, government offices. "I'll be direct, because I respect your time and mine. You're diving a wreck that my fleet holds claim to. That claim expires in four tides. After expiry, anything raised is finder's keepers — old law, but real law. So you have a window, and I have a problem, and I'd rather we solved both together than at cross purposes."

"We're diving a wreck that's been dead forty years," Odile said. "Nobody cared until we started."

"People care about many things only once someone else touches them." Varn produced, from inside her coat, a folded paper, and set it on the bench between them with two fingers, the way one sets down a card. "Meridian offers a partnership. Fifty-fifty on gross recovery value, our equipment and crews at your disposal, full legal cover for the operation — and full legal protection afterward, which you may find more valuable than the money. In exchange, Meridian handles cataloging, appraisal, and disposition of recovered materials. Standard terms. I've brought a copy of the contract."

Tam, standing at the rail, read the paper upside down from six feet away. He'd spent years reading documents upside down across briefing tables; it was the single most useful skill his disgrace had given him. The contract was dense, well-drafted, and poisoned in three places he could see immediately: clause fourteen gave Meridian sole discretion over "historical materials," defined so broadly it would swallow the census whole; clause nineteen bound Dravenmoor to silence regarding "matters of state sensitivity encountered during operations"; and clause twenty-two, buried in small type, made the partnership voidable by Meridian at will, converting Odile's share into a licensing fee payable — Tam checked — *at Meridian's convenience*. It wasn't a partnership. It was a leash with a coin purse tied to it.

He caught Odile's eye and gave the smallest shake of his head. She didn't need it. He could see that she didn't need it.

"Fifty-fifty," Odile repeated. "On treasure."

"On whatever's down there," Varn said smoothly. "Coin, plate, cargo. The Vesperine was a state vessel; state vessels carry state valuables. Everyone assumes a drowned nation sank with its treasury aboard. Sometimes they're right."

"And if it's not treasure?"

The pause was very small. Tam almost missed it — a half-breath, a flicker in those pale eyes, the pleasant voice recalibrating. "Then fifty percent of nothing is nothing," Varn said, "and you've lost only time. Though I confess I'd be surprised. And if it's papers — manifests, registries, the usual bureaucratic sediment — well." She spread her hands. "Paper has value to collectors. We'd appraise it fairly."

*Fairly.* Tam watched the word land and saw Odile watch it land too. Somewhere below them, forty feet down in the green dark, lay a census of the drowned — thousands of names, an entire nation reduced to a ledger, proof that the flooding had been ordered, signed, engineered. Paper. Bureaucratic sediment. The most dangerous cargo on the river, and Varn was offering to buy it by the pound.

"What aren't you telling me, Captain?" Odile asked.

"I tell everyone the same thing," Varn said, rising, smoothing her coat. "That the river gives up what it gives up, and wise divers take partners. The water down there is deep, cold, and full of collapsed structure. My cranes can lift what your backs cannot. My lawyers can protect what your courage cannot. Think on it. I'll anchor tonight and await your answer through tomorrow's tide." She moved toward the gangway, then paused, and for just a moment the brandy-warmth drained out of her voice and left something older and harder underneath. "One more thing, Captain Dravenmoor. Whatever you think you're looking for down there — names, reasons, ghosts — consider whether finding it serves anyone. The dead don't read. The living sign contracts." She smiled again, and it reached her eyes this time, which was somehow worse. "I'd hate to see a diver of your reputation drown over paperwork."

She went down the ladder, took her seat in the gig, and was pulled away across the bright water, leaving the smell of coal smoke and good wool behind her.

* * *

They didn't speak until the fleet had anchored a half-mile downstream, three black shapes sitting fat and patient on the current like crows on a fence.

"She knows," Bo said. His voice cracked on it. "She knows what's down there. Not treasure — she knows it's the census. Why else offer legal protection? Why else care about 'state-sensitive' papers?"

"Because someone briefed her," Tam said. He heard his own voice come out flat and cold, the old briefing-room voice, and hated it, and used it anyway because it kept his hands steady. "The same someone who expunged the Vesperine from the rolls. The navy pays Meridian to keep this river quiet — I've suspected it since the patrol boat. This offer wasn't a bribe for a share of silver. It was a bribe for silence. Clause nineteen isn't boilerplate, Odile. It's the whole contract. Everything else is decoration."

Sefa signed rapidly. Bo translated, slower: "She says — she says Varn counted our guns. When she walked aboard. She looked at the wheel, the lines, the dinghy. She was measuring how fast we could run." A beat. "She says Varn expects us to refuse. She *wants* us to refuse."

"Why would she want that?" Bo asked.

"Because a partner has to be paid," Tam said quietly, "and an enemy can simply be sunk."

The wind shifted. Downstream, the Meridian tender's crane swung slowly on its moorings, a long dark arm against the sky, pointing at nothing and everything. Tam looked at Odile, expecting — what? Fear? Recalculation? Some sign that the arithmetic had finally grown too heavy?

Instead she was looking at the water, at the place where the Vesperine lay below the silt and the green light, and her face had gone very still and very clear, the way it did in the last minute before a dive, when all the noise fell away and only the work remained.

"She said the dead don't read," Odile said.

"Odile—"

"My sister's down there." Her voice didn't rise at all, and that was the frightening thing about it. "Her handwriting is in that chest in my cabin. Forty years the navy let the river keep her, and forty years Meridian helped hold the lid down, and now the woman who holds the concession stands on my deck and offers me half of nothing to forget her twice." She turned, and Tam saw that her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely settled, the way water settles just before it goes over the edge. "Tell her no. Tell her in writing, so there's a record. Tell her we decline her generous offer and intend to exercise our rights under the expiry law, and that if anything happens to this boat or its crew, the whole river will know exactly what Meridian was afraid of."

"That last part's a bluff," Tam said. "We haven't raised anything. We can't prove—"

"Not yet," Odile said. "Four tides."

He wrote the refusal that evening in his best cartographic hand, the precise, unfalsifiable lettering the navy had drilled into him, and had Bo witness it, and sent it across the water with the boy because Bo volunteered and someone had to go and Tam's face was already known to Varn's officers from worse days. He watched the gig return across the darkening river, small lantern swinging, and he watched Bo climb aboard with a strange expression, half relief and half dread.

"Well?" Odile asked.

"She read it standing in the lantern light," Bo said. "All of it, twice. Then she laughed — not mean, just... like something confirmed. And she said—" the boy swallowed— "she said, 'Tell the captain the tide after next comes in high.' And then she looked at me, really looked, and asked if I was the one who could read the old script. I didn't answer. But she smiled like I had."

Tam stood at the rail as the lights of the Meridian fleet bloomed one by one across the water, three ships' worth of lanterns strung out against the dusk, and felt the shape of the trap close gently around them, courteous and patient and utterly certain.

High tide, the day after tomorrow. Enough water over the shoals for a deep-draught boarding party to come straight up the channel to their anchorage.

Varn hadn't offered a partnership. She'd offered them a deadline with a smile on it.

"Get Sefa," Tam said. "And get me the charts of the leeward passage. If we're going to be enemies of the Meridian Salvage Fleet, we're going to be moving ones."
