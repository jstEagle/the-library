## 10. The Leeward Passage

The chart said *nothing*, and the nothing was the point.

Sefa stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Debt* with Tam's last surviving survey spread across the binnacle box, its margins soft with damp, and watched him trace the coastline one more time as though repetition might make the paper confess. It wouldn't. He'd already told her twice what she already knew: every chart that had ever recorded the leeward passage had been withdrawn, burned, or drowned with the men who drew them. The navy called it a hazard. The fishermen called it the Teeth. Her grandmother had called it *the short way home*, and had run it eleven times in a hull no wider than a coffin.

"You're certain," Tam said. Not a question. A man asking permission to be afraid.

"I'm certain my grandmother was stubborn," Sefa signed with one hand, keeping the other on the wheel. "It runs in the family."

Bo laughed from the foredeck, high and quick, the laugh of someone who needed it. Odile didn't laugh. She stood at the rail with her arms folded, watching the water ahead go from green to that pale, milky jade that meant sand under keel-depth, and her silence had a weight to it that Sefa could feel through the deck boards — through her boots, through the soles of her feet, up into the bones of her hands where the river always spoke first.

They were running for the tide. That was the whole arithmetic of it. Varn's fleet would come around the headland by the deep channel, safe and slow and licensed, and they would lose half a day they did not have. But the leeward passage cut inside the shoals, a ribbon of deeper water threading between bars that shifted with every flood season, and if Sefa could hold it, they'd come out ahead of Meridian by a full tide and be on the wreck before the fleet even cleared the point.

If she couldn't hold it, they'd be a story fishermen told each other in winter.

* * *

She had been eight years old the first time her grandmother took her through. She remembered it in pieces, the way you remember anything that mattered: the smell of tar and strong tea; the old woman's hands moving on the tiller like a pianist's, small corrections, constant, conversational; the sound of her voice saying numbers. Not depths — Sefa hadn't known enough words yet — but a rhythm. *Three-and-a-soft-one. Hard four past the white stone. When the water goes brown, you're already too late.*

Her grandmother had been deaf from a childhood fever, same as Sefa, and she had never once treated it as a loss. *The hearing people listen with their ears,* she'd signed, *and their ears are full of their own noise. You listen with your whole body. That is why the river tells you things it will never tell them.*

Sefa had spent thirty years proving her right, mostly on salvage crews full of hearing men who thought deafness meant slowness, until Odile Dravenmoor had watched her read a lock gate's vibration through a stanchion and hired her on the spot without a single spoken word passing between them. That was the thing about Odile. She looked at what a person could do, not at what the world said they couldn't. Sefa repaid it by getting her boats through places like this.

She touched the wheel and let the hull tell her what it felt.

Every boat has a voice. The *Debt* was an old river tug converted for salvage work, shallow-drafted, heavy in the bow from the winch gear, and she spoke plainly: a low hum through the deck when the water under the keel was generous, a thinner note when it grew mean. Sandbars changed the song. Where the current piled against a bar, the surface wrinkled in long, glassy ridges that looked calm and lied; where the channel ran true, the water went dark and smooth as poured ink. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse, came the percussion of the river working the shoals — a shifting, granular whisper, sand in motion, the sound of the bottom rearranging itself since the last time any human being had dared to look at it.

No chart survived because no chart *could*. The passage redrew itself every spring. Which meant the route her grandmother had run was forty years dead, and what Sefa was about to do was not navigation by memory but something stranger: memory as method. Her grandmother hadn't taught her a line on a map. She'd taught her how the passage *thought*.

*The river keeps its old roads,* she'd signed once, waiting out a fog on the leeward side of the Teeth. *It only buries them. A flood doesn't erase a channel, girl. It moves it a little to one side or the other, the way a snake sheds its skin. Find where the old road ran, and look beside it.*

* * *

"Buoy," Bo called from the bow, and there was nothing there, and that was correct.

There had been a buoy once — her grandmother's charts, the ones burned in the yard stove the winter after she died, had marked it — a black spar on the northern horn of the first bar. The navy had pulled it decades ago rather than encourage anyone. But buoys are moored to the bottom, and moorings leave scars, and scars gather life. Sefa raised her hand and Bo swung the glasses toward the spot she indicated, and there it was, exactly where inheritance said it should be: a smudge of darker water, a faint discoloration, the ghost of a mooring block still holding its little reef of weed while everything around it scoured clean.

"Port ten," Sefa signed, and Odile relayed it with a touch to Tam's shoulder, and the wheel answered, and the *Debt* leaned into the turn like a dog remembering a command.

Behind them, faint on the horizon, the masts of the Meridian fleet were just clearing the headland — three hulls, orderly, patient, flying their claim pennant like a flag over occupied country. Someone on Varn's lead ship would have glasses on them right now. Someone would be watching a salvage tug turn away from the deep channel toward a coast no licensed master would touch, and drawing conclusions. Let them watch. Let Varn think it was panic. By the time anyone understood what the *Debt* was doing, the tide would have carried the answer too far to recall.

The first bar passed under the keel with six feet to spare. Sefa knew the number without sounding — knew it from the note of the hull, from the brown-green seam of the water, from the way the current's push against the wheel went suddenly gentle, the river relaxing around them like a hand opening. Tam exhaled behind her. She didn't. Six feet was comfortable. Comfortable was how men died on the Teeth. Her grandmother had run it at four and called it a lazy morning.

*Hard four past the white stone.* There was no white stone anymore; there hadn't been for a generation. But stones don't vanish, they sink, and a sunk white stone grows a different kind of dark than the mud around it. Sefa found it off the starboard bow — a patch of water the color of wet slate, distinct from the olive of the shallows — and put the helm hard over, counting in her head the way her grandmother had counted aloud, and the *Debt* came around the drowned marker with its paint barely breathing on the bar's edge. Bo whooped. Odile's hand closed briefly on Sefa's shoulder — a grip, not a pat. *I see you,* the grip said. *Keep going.*

* * *

The middle passage was where the grandmother's stories stopped helping and started haunting.

This was the stretch she'd never described clearly — the reach where, in Sefa's ninth summer, another boat had gone in ahead of them. A gull boat, overloaded, skippered by a hearing man with a chart he'd bought secondhand and trusted more than the water. Sefa remembered her grandmother standing at the rail watching the masthead tilt, slide, and go under with nine souls aboard, and she remembered the old woman's face, which had shown no horror at all — only a terrible, weary recognition, the look of someone watching a lesson get learned the hard way one more time. Then she'd turned away and run the rest of the passage in perfect silence, and that night she had burned two charts in the yard stove instead of one.

*Charts kill more than ignorance does,* she'd signed afterward. *Ignorance makes a man careful. A chart makes him confident, and confidence is heavier than cargo.*

So Sefa came into the middle reach with no confidence at all, and that was her armor. She slowed the *Debt* to bare steerage and let the hull become an instrument. Every few lengths she felt for the bottom-note, read the surface seams, watched the birds — terns working a rip line meant baitfish over deeper water, meant channel. The river was talking constantly, the way it always did, and the trick her grandmother had given her was simply this: believe it over everything, including yourself.

Twice the old road failed her. Twice the channel had shed its skin exactly as promised and moved — once to port, where a new cut had opened along a drowned fence line, the pilings still standing in a row like teeth in a jaw, scouring a trench between them; once to starboard, where the flood years had pushed a delta tongue across the old line and the true channel now ran close under the leeward bank, tight against a wall of reeds that whispered against the rigging as they slid past close enough to touch. Both times, the memory gave her the neighborhood and the river gave her the address. Both times, Bo was on the bow calling the color changes before Sefa's hands could ask, learning faster than anyone had taught him, and Tam was aft with the lead line swinging, calling fathoms in a voice that shook less each time.

That was the part nobody warned you about, Sefa thought, easing the wheel through a bend so tight the wake folded back on itself. That a crew becomes a body. That somewhere in the last week — through the chest, and the papers, and the patrol boat's searchlight sweeping the water behind them at the locks — these three strangers had grown nerve endings that connected to hers. She felt Odile's tension in the set of the deck under her feet. She felt Bo's fear coming off the bow in waves she could almost taste. And when the *Debt* finally straightened into the last reach with the shoals falling astern, and the water went deep-dark and honest all the way to the far shore, the cheer that went up — Bo shouting, Tam laughing his rusty, disused laugh, Odile letting out a breath she must have been holding since the headland — came up through the hull and into Sefa's hands like the river itself had joined in.

She allowed herself one moment. One. She closed her eyes and signed into the empty air, small, private, a message with no audience but the dead:

*We ran it, Gran. Eleven times you did it alone. I did it once, and I wasn't alone.*

Then she opened her eyes, because the river doesn't wait, and neither did the day.

* * *

They cleared the eastern gap as the light went long and gold, and the Vesperine's anchorage opened below them — the drowned nation lying quiet under its sheet of green water, forbidden and unnamed, waiting. No masts in sight behind them. The fleet was still feeling its way around the headland, hours back, maybe a full tide if the wind held wrong for them.

Odile came and stood beside her at the wheel, and for a while neither of them said anything, because Odile had learned some signs and Sefa had learned that Odile's silences were not emptiness but weather.

"You knew that passage," Odile signed at last. "Before today."

"My grandmother ran it." Sefa's hands moved slow, giving the words room. "Eleven times. She burned her charts so no one else would die trusting them. I used to think that was cruelty — hoarding the road. Now I think she was keeping it the only safe place a road can live. In a body. In someone who'd feel the water lie."

Odile looked at the water for a long moment. "My sister left me a letter I haven't finished reading," she said. "I keep thinking I need to be ready for it. Maybe readiness isn't a thing you wait for. Maybe it's a thing you run through, like your passage. Blind, mostly, on memory and nerve."

Sefa considered that. Then she reached out and tapped the wheel — the old wood, worn smooth by her grandmother's hands before it was ever hers, salvaged off a boat that should have rotted and didn't.

"Inheritance isn't what people leave you," she signed. "It's what you're willing to carry forward. Everything else is just freight."

Odile nodded slowly, and something in her face shifted — a door unlatching somewhere behind her eyes — and she turned to call the crew together, to plan the dive, to spend the tide they'd stolen fair and square.

Which was when Bo, still up at the bow with the glasses, went very still.

"Sefa," he called down, and his voice had gone strange. "You'll want to see this."

He handed the glasses aft. Sefa raised them, and the triumph in her chest turned over and went cold.

A fourth sail had come around the headland. Not Meridian's boxy salvage hulls — something leaner, lower, gray, running without colors and without lights, cutting the corner Varn's fleet was too careful to cut.

Someone else knew the leeward passage.

And whoever they were, they weren't a day behind anymore. They were closing.
