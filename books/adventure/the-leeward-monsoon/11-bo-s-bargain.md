## 11. Bo's Bargain

Bo had learned to count exits before Bo had learned to read.

That was the first lesson of the road, older than letters, older than the ragged primer with the water-stained Calvethi characters that Bo's mother had pressed into their hands at a crossroads outside Marrowfen and then walked away from without looking back. Every room you entered, find the door. Every kindness you were given, ask what it will cost and when. Every family you were folded into — and there had been three, four, five, a whole litany of temporary families stitched together out of shared fires and shared bread — every one of them would end, and the only question was whether you'd be the one to leave or the one left standing in the cold.

Bo was eleven when they stopped being the one left standing. They had made a rule of it after that: leave first. Leave while it was still your idea. Leave while you could still tell yourself the shape of the leaving.

It had worked for six years. It had worked right up until a woman with river-gray eyes and hands like knotted rope had looked at Bo across a campfire and said, *You can read that?* — not with suspicion, not with the calculating hunger of someone appraising a tool, but with something almost like wonder — and Bo had felt the rule begin to crack like ice underfoot.

Now they sat in the stern of the *Leeward Rose* — Odile's boat, though she never called it hers, just *the boat*, as if naming it would make it something that could be taken away — and watched the Meridian Fleet's lights crawl along the far bank of the river like a procession of patient, patient stars.

Six lanterns. Six tides. Tam had explained it twice, pacing the deck with his ruined cartographer's hands folded behind his back, and Bo had understood both times: the wreck claim expired at slack water on the sixth tide, and after that, whatever was raised from the Vesperine belonged to whoever held it. The fleet didn't need to hurry. The river would do their waiting for them.

But the fleet was hurrying anyway. That was what the lights meant. Captain Ilse Varn did not believe in letting rivers decide things.

Neither, Bo was beginning to understand, did Odile Dravenmoor. And that was the problem.

* * *

The trouble had started, if Bo was honest, with the census fragment itself.

They had translated it by lamplight two nights ago, Bo's fingers moving over the brittle paper while Sefa leaned close to feel the vibration of Bo's voice through the table — Sefa always wanted the words through her palms, said they tasted different that way — and Tam copying the translation into his notebook with the careful, mournful precision of a man writing an obituary for a whole nation.

*A census of souls,* the fragment said. *Every citizen lost in the inundation, recorded by district, by household, by name.*

Names. Thousands of them, presumably, sealed somewhere down in the dark hold of a ship that had been sunk on purpose, its manifest erased, its existence expunged from every chart Tam had ever drawn or seen. A ship full of dead people's names, carried out of a drowning city by whoever had loved them enough to want them remembered — or by whoever had needed the record kept somewhere the flood couldn't reach it.

And somewhere in those thousands of names, Bo knew now, was Bo's own family. They hadn't found it yet — the fragment was only a fragment, a header page, a table of contents for a book still lying in the drowned dark — but Bo could feel it the way you feel weather coming. The script had come up out of them too easily, too naturally, the old Calvethi characters rising to their pen like water finding its level. You didn't learn a dead language that well from a stolen primer. You inherited it.

Which meant Bo was descended from the drowned. Which meant the census wasn't salvage. It was a grave register, and Bo was standing on it.

And which meant — this was the thought that came at three in the morning, lying in the narrow bunk beneath the foredeck, listening to the river slap the hull like a slow hand — which meant Odile was going to sell it anyway.

Bo turned the thought over night after night, examining it from every angle the way you'd examine a coin you suspected was clipped. Odile needed money; the boat ran on money the way hearts ran on blood. Odile had refused Varn's partnership — Bo had been there, had watched her say no to a fortune with a flatness in her voice that had frightened even Sefa — but refusing one bribe wasn't the same as refusing all of them. Refusing a bribe was easy when the thing being bought was still down in the dark, still theoretical, still a rumor. Wait until the vault came up dripping into the daylight. Wait until there were ten thousand names in a box and a fleet captain offering more money than Bo could count to make those names go away.

Odile would hesitate. Bo believed that. Odile would look at the names and think of her sister and her jaw would tighten and something human would move behind her eyes.

And then she'd sell it anyway. Because that was what people did. Because grief was expensive and everyone had bills. Because Odile Dravenmoor had spent her whole life diving into wrecks and pulling out whatever the river gave up, and Bo had yet to meet a single person on this earth who pulled something valuable out of the dark and let it sink back down again.

And when she sold it — when the census went into Varn's ledgers and the navy's furnaces and the long quiet erasure that had already swallowed Calveth once — where would that leave Bo? The Calvethi stray. The useful tongue. The kid who could read the dead language until the reading was done, and then—

Then nothing. Then the road again. Then the crossroads and the cold and the counting of exits.

Unless Bo got there first.

* * *

The Meridian picket boat found them, or Bo found it — afterward Bo was never entirely sure which way it had gone — on the fourth evening, at the fueling dock below Grayharbor Locks, while Odile and Tam argued prices with the lockkeeper and Sefa checked the rigging in the failing light.

Bo had told themselves they were only going to look. That was the lie, and it was a good one, worn smooth from use: *just looking*. Just walking down the dock with their hands in their pockets, past the tar barrels and the coiled line, toward the sleek black hull of the Meridian picket with its running lights burning steady and official. Just curious. Curiosity wasn't betrayal. Curiosity was practically a virtue.

The man on the gangway wore a Meridian coat cut clean as a blade, and he watched Bo come with the unhurried attention of someone who fished for a living and knew the difference between a fish that stumbled into the net and a fish that swam in.

"You're off the little boat," he said. Not a question. "The one that's been keeping ahead of us."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sure." He smiled without warmth. He was maybe forty, with a face like a closed ledger, and Bo catalogued him automatically, the way the road had taught them: *name unknown, rank unknown, dangerous in the patient way.* "I'm Quill. Fleet liaison. Which means I'm the person Captain Varn sends when she wants to know things instead of take them." He tilted his head. "You're the reader."

Bo's stomach dropped through the dock planks and into the river. "I don't—"

"The runaway who reads old script. Yes, we know about you. We know about all of them." Quill said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "Dravenmoor, the disgraced mapmaker, the deaf rigger, and the reader. Four strays in a leaky boat, racing a licensed fleet to a wreck the navy doesn't officially remember." He stepped aside from the gangway, an invitation. "Come aboard. Have something hot to drink. Nobody's going to drag anyone anywhere. I just think we should talk about what happens at slack water on tide six."

Every lesson Bo had ever learned stood up inside them at once and shouted *leave, leave, turn around, walk back down the dock, get on the boat, get small, get gone* —

And underneath all of it, quieter, older, colder, the other voice. The one that said: *they'll sell it. She'll sell it. And when she does, you'll be the stray again, and you'll wish you'd been the one holding the ledger.*

Bo went aboard.

* * *

The picket boat's cabin was warm and smelled of coffee and lamp oil, and that was its own kind of treachery, because Bo couldn't remember the last time they'd been warm on purpose.

Quill poured two cups and set one in front of Bo and didn't touch his own, and Bo thought, absurdly, of Odile, who drank her tea scalding and fast, who did everything fast, as if slowness were a kind of drowning.

"Here's what I know," Quill said. "The census is real. Our translators have been working from fragments recovered twenty years ago — partial pages, water-ruined. What they wouldn't give for a fluent reader with access to the primary documents." He laced his fingers. "Here's what I also know. When that vault comes up — and it will come up, either for us or for you — Dravenmoor is going to be sitting on the most dangerous piece of paper in the nation. Do you understand what I mean by dangerous? Not valuable. Dangerous. There are people in the capital who signed orders that are named in that census. There are families — good families, old families — whose entire position rests on Calveth having been an act of God." His eyes were pale and steady. "Those families pay Captain Varn to keep the water closed. And Captain Varn pays me. And I am paying you, right now, with information, free of charge: when the vault surfaces, your captain will have exactly two choices, and one of them ends with all four of you at the bottom of the channel."

"That's not true," Bo said. "She'd never—"

"Sell?" Quill raised an eyebrow. "I never said sell. I said choose. But since you mention it — yes. She'll sell. Or she'll trade it for protection, which is selling slowly. Or she'll try some heroic third thing and get everyone killed, which is selling to the river." He leaned forward. "You've known her, what, three weeks? I've read her file for nine years. Odile Dravenmoor has never once in her adult life stopped moving long enough to keep anything. She dives, she takes, she leaves. Ask her about her sister sometime. Ask her why the sister isn't here."

The words went in like a fishhook and set deep, and Bo hated him for it, and hated more that he'd chosen exactly the right bait.

"What are you offering," Bo said. Their voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"Protection. Real protection — papers, a place in the fleet, wages, a berth that doesn't leak. And when the census comes up, you translate it. For us. Fully, accurately, everything you can read." Quill finally picked up his coffee. "In exchange, when Dravenmoor makes her choice — and she will make it, soon, probably badly — you won't be standing next to her when she does."

"And if I say no?"

"Then nothing happens tonight. Nothing happens tomorrow. We're not pirates, boy." He smiled again, that closed-ledger smile. "We just wait. Tide six comes for everybody."

Bo looked down at the coffee, at their own reflection trembling in its black surface, broken and reassembled and broken again by every ripple of the boat against the dock.

*I'm sorry,* they thought, and weren't sure whether they were saying it to Odile, or to the drowned whose language lived in their mouth, or to some future version of themselves standing alone at another crossroads holding another piece of paper that used to be a family.

"I'll need to see the fragments first," Bo heard themselves say. "Before I agree to anything. To confirm I can read them."

"Of course." Quill produced a folded oilskin packet from inside his coat and set it on the table between them, and Bo understood that this conversation had been decided before Bo ever walked down the dock — that the net had been woven days ago, and Bo had simply, obediently, swum in.

They took the packet. Their hands didn't shake until they were back on the dark dock, walking toward the *Leeward Rose*'s riding light, and then they shook so hard Bo had to stop and press them flat against a piling and breathe until the world steadied.

Inside the packet: three fragments of Calvethi census pages, genuine, water-marked, heartbreakingly legible. And tucked behind them, a note in Quill's neat hand:

*Tide five. The narrows below Leeward Point. Come alone. Bring nothing you can't explain.*

* * *

Bo didn't sleep. They sat in the foredeck bunk with the fragments hidden inside their shirt, against their skin, feeling them like a fever, and listened to the sounds of the boat: Sefa's rope creaking as she checked the lines one last time, Tam's pen scratching in the cabin, Odile's boots on the deck above, pacing, always pacing, the woman who never stopped moving.

*She'll sell it,* Bo told themselves. *Or she'll die trying not to, and take everyone down with her. Either way you lose her. This way you lose her on your own feet instead of your knees.*

It was the arithmetic of abandonment, and Bo had done it so many times it should have been easy, and it wasn't, because this time the numbers kept coming out wrong. This time there was Sefa teaching them knots by lamplight, guiding their fingers with a patience that needed no words. This time there was Tam explaining tides for the third time without a flicker of condescension, because Tam knew what it was to be thrown away by an institution and had decided no one else on this boat would feel that. This time there was Odile, who had looked at a stray in a market crowd and said *can you read that?* like the answer mattered, like *Bo* mattered.

This time, leaving first didn't feel like winning. It felt like cutting something out of yourself that had only just finished healing.

Near dawn, footsteps on the foredeck, soft, deliberate. Bo froze. The hatch cracked, and Odile's face appeared against the gray light — tired, sharp, unreadable.

"You're awake," she said.

"Couldn't sleep."

Odile studied them for a moment. Bo felt the fragments against their chest like a second heartbeat and made their face into nothing, the way the road had taught them, the oldest skill of all.

"We make the outer wreck on the next tide," Odile said. "Tam thinks the vault's below the third deck, aft of the mainmast. If it's intact—" She stopped. Something moved behind her eyes, quick and unguarded, there and gone. "If it's intact, Bo, those names come up into the light. All of them. Whatever it costs."

*Whatever it costs.* Bo nodded. Said nothing. Watched her go.

And lay in the dark with the stolen pages against their heart, doing the only arithmetic left: *tide five, the narrows, come alone* — two tides away, and every hour between now and then a hour closer to the moment when the family Bo had finally found would learn that Bo had already sold the door shut behind them.

Above, the river ran black and endless toward the sea, carrying everything downstream, keeping nothing, keeping nothing at all.
