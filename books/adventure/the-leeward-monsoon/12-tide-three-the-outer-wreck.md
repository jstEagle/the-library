## 12. Tide Three: The Outer Wreck

The Vesperine's hold opened beneath Odile like a throat.

She hung in the green half-light at the edge of the cargo hatch, her lamp beam swallowed by the dark below, and felt the old familiar pull — not the current, which was slack and gentle this tide, but the other pull, the one that lived behind her sternum and had lived there since Wren went into the flooded mine at Corrow Fen and did not come out. The pull that said *go down*. That said *there is always one more thing below you*.

"Odile." Tam's voice crackled through the line, thin and tinny through the water. "Reading says the vault's forty feet down the central corridor, portside bulkhead. You copy?"

"I copy." She checked her gauges. Air at three-quarters. Bottom time generous, if she didn't linger. She would linger anyway; they both knew it. "Sefa, you've got my topside?"

A double squeeze on the signal line. Sefa didn't waste breath on words she could say with rope.

"And Bo?" Odile said, before she could stop herself.

Another pause. Then Tam again: "Bo's on the winch. Where you put them."

Where she'd put them. She'd put Bo topside because Bo had begged to come down, and because Odile had looked at those young hands — quick, clever, always moving, always reaching for the next thing — and thought of a girl in a mine shaft who had also been quick and clever and had reached for one thing too many. So Bo stayed above, coiling line and sulking about it, and Odile went down alone into the ship that had carried a nation's dead.

She finned through the hatch.

* * *

Inside, the Vesperine was a cathedral of silt.

Her lamp found corridors of settled mud, ribbed with fallen beams, walled with cargo gone to soft gray pulp. Fish scattered from her light like startled thoughts. The ship had been a packet steamer once, proud enough, and now she was a long slow ruin lying on her starboard side with her funnels folded flat and her name scraped off her stern — not weathered away, Odile had seen that up close on their first survey dive, but *chiseled*, gouged by tools, by hands under orders. The navy wouldn't discuss it. The navy had made sure no chart, no registry, no living officer would discuss it. But ships kept their own records, and this one said *they came back for the name*.

Forty feet down the corridor. She counted hatchways as she went, trailing her guideline off its reel, touching each frame the way Sefa had taught her — two taps for sound wood, one long drag for rot. Two taps. Drag. Two taps. The water inside the hull was colder than outside, stiller, holding its breath around her. Somewhere far aft something shifted and groaned, the ship settling a fraction of an inch after sixty years, and Odile's heart answered it with a hard knock against her ribs.

There. Portside bulkhead.

It wasn't a bulkhead at all. It was a door — iron, small, set flush into the frame where a first-class storeroom might have been, and it was different from everything around it because it was *clean*. No silt furred its hinges. No weed grew across its face. Someone had maintained it, or sealed it so well the river couldn't get a grip. Across its center, stamped deep into a brass plate gone green-black with age, was the seal of Calveth: the leeward tower above three waves.

The same seal that had been on the chest the river gave up. The same seal on the fitting in Odile's pocket right now, riding against her thigh like a second heartbeat.

"Tam," she said. "I found it."

Static. Then: "God. Okay. Okay — don't force anything. If it's sealed, we come back with tools."

"It's not sealed." Her fingers had already found the wheel. It turned with a resistance that felt less like rust than like reluctance, like the door itself had been waiting. "It's open."

She should have thought about that. Later, in the long hours after, she would think about it over and over — a vault door standing unlocked at the bottom of a drowned nation, after sixty years, after everything the navy had done to keep Calveth buried. Open meant someone had been there. Or open meant the river had done what rivers do, working patiently at every joint, every weld, until the strongest thing gave way. Or open meant nothing at all meant anything, down here where time ran differently.

She turned the wheel the rest of the way and pulled.

The door swung inward with a sigh of displaced water, and her lamp swept across rows and rows of shelving, and on the shelves, in oilcloth and lead-lined cases, stacked floor to ceiling in the dark —

Names. Thousands of them. A census of the drowned, packed away like seed grain, like something meant to be planted later, when the world was ready.

Odile forgot her gauges. She forgot the guideline, the tide, the crew waiting topside, the fleet that would come hunting on the next ebb. She drifted into the vault room with her arms slightly out, turning slowly, her light trembling across case after case after case, and she thought: *Wren, look. Look what they tried to lose.* And then, underneath that, quieter, in the voice that never quite left her: *you were right to go looking. You were right, and it killed you, and I'm going to finish it.*

She got the first case unhooked from its shelf. Lead-lined, heavier than sin, stenciled in old Calvethi script that Bo could read and she couldn't. She clipped it to her harness lift line and squeezed the bulb twice — send it up — and felt the line go taut above her, felt the case begin its ascent through the black water toward the gray morning light and the people who were, without her ever having planned it, becoming hers.

That was when the ship moved.

Not a groan this time. A *shift* — a long structural shudder that traveled down through the hull like a word passing through a crowd, and Odile knew instantly, with the animal certainty of twenty years underwater, that the door was the problem. Sixty years of silt load pressing on that corridor wall, and the vault door had been holding part of it up. She'd opened it. She'd let the weight find somewhere new to go.

"Odile!" Tam's voice, sharp now. "We felt that topside. Get out. *Get out now.*"

She was already swimming. The corridor behind her was breathing silt — great slow exhalations of it rolling off the shelves, off the ceiling, visibility collapsing from ten feet to five to two. Her guideline was there under her hand, thank God, thank Sefa, and she followed it blind, finning hard, the second case she'd grabbed banging against her hip, and behind her she heard the sound she'd heard once before in her life, at Corrow Fen, the day the world ended the first time:

Timber, letting go.

The collapse came down the corridor like a held breath released. Not on her — the geometry saved her, the fall going portside while she hugged starboard, the shockwave of it shoving her forward through soup-thick water, slamming her against a hatch frame, knocking her regulator loose. She got it back. She got her bearings. She got to the cargo hatch and rose through it into open water and looked back down into the wreck and saw nothing but cloud.

And on the winch platform above, no one answering the signal line.

* * *

They got Bo out of the water coughing, half-drowned, tangled in the lift line.

Later Odile would piece it together: the case rising on the line, Bo unclipping it onto the deck, and then — instead of staying clear, instead of doing the one job they'd been given — leaning over the edge, staring down into the murk where Odile had vanished, and when the collapse shuddered the whole wreck and the signal line went slack and screaming through Bo's hands, Bo had grabbed it. Had hauled. Had leaned out over black water hauling hand over hand on a line loaded with a falling ship, and the winch had paid out wrong, or Bo had simply been too light, too desperate, too much all elbows and fear, and gone over the side into the churn.

Sefa got them. Sefa was in the water before Tam finished shouting, knife out, cutting Bo free of the fouled line, and dragged them to the ladder while Tam worked the winch alone and Odile surfaced into chaos — deck tilting, gear sliding, Tam roaring Bo's name into the fog.

Bo lay on the deck planks vomiting river water and shaking, and Odile knelt over them with her hands hovering, not touching, the way you hover over something you're afraid will break if you touch it wrong.

"You were supposed to stay on the winch," she said. Her voice came out low and terrible. She heard it and couldn't soften it. "One job, Bo. One."

"I had you," Bo gasped. "The line went dead, I thought — I thought you were —"

"So you jumped into a collapsing wreck site with no gear, no line, no plan. Like Wren." The words were out before she could catch them, and she watched them land, watched Bo flinch like she'd struck them. "Corrow Fen took my sister because she went in after something without telling anyone. Do you understand what you just —"

"Stop." Tam's hand on her shoulder. "Stop, Odile."

But Bo was pushing themselves up on their elbows, river water dripping from their chin, and their eyes — wet, furious, terrified — were fixed on Odile's face with an expression she couldn't read yet.

"You want to talk about not telling people?" Bo said. Their voice cracked and steadied. "Fine. Let's talk about it."

* * *

They sat in the cabin afterward, the four of them, while the tide turned outside and the Vesperine's broken corridor settled into its new shape below. The census case sat between them on the table, lead-lined, stenciled, silent. Bo had a blanket around their shoulders and a bruise blooming along one jaw where the ladder rail had caught them, and they would not look at the case.

"The night before we cast off," Bo said, "I sent a message to Meridian."

The cabin went very quiet. Outside, water slapped the hull. Sefa's hands, which had been still in her lap, went stiller.

"They have a translator on staff. An old man who reads Calvethi. I wrote to him months ago — before any of this, when I was just —" Bo's hands twisted in the blanket. "When I was studying the script. He answered. We wrote back and forth. And the night before we left, I told him what we'd found in the chest. I told him about the seal. I told him I'd translate whatever we raised from the Vesperine — for Meridian — in exchange for protection. Papers. A place. Because I thought —" Their voice broke and they pushed through it. "Because I thought you'd sell it anyway, Odile. Everyone sells, eventually. Everyone I've ever — everyone always sells, or leaves, or both. So I figured I'd be the one who chose first. I figured I'd rather be useful to them than abandoned by you."

The silence after that had weight, like deep water.

Odile looked at Bo — at the sharp bones of their face, the runaway's watchfulness worn down to something rawer — and felt her anger do a strange thing. It didn't vanish. It turned over, the way a stone turns in a current, and showed her its underside. *Everyone always sells, or leaves, or both.* How old was Bo? Seventeen, maybe. Maybe younger. Old enough to have learned that lesson thoroughly, from people who had taught it well.

She thought about saying something cold. She had it ready — she always had it ready, the whole armory of distance. Instead she heard herself say: "Did you tell them where we are?"

"No." Fast, immediate. "No. I told them I'd make contact after we raised something worth translating. They don't know the anchorage. They don't know the route we took. Odile, I swear on —" Bo stopped. Swallowed. "I swear on nothing, because I don't believe in anything, but I swear. I haven't written since we left. I couldn't. After the lock, after Sefa got us through running dark — after that, I couldn't."

Sefa reached over and took Bo's hand. Didn't sign anything. Just held it.

Tam cleared his throat. "Varn will know we're on the wreck regardless. She has the claim; she'll assume we're racing her. What matters is whether she knows our position, and she doesn't." He looked at Bo, and his expression was the cartographer's expression, the one that measured things. "You understand that if you write to them now, even one line, we're dead. All of us."

"I know."

"And you understand that what you did wasn't treason, exactly. It was fear wearing treason's coat."

Bo's eyes filled. They nodded.

Odile stood. Walked to the little porthole, where the gray water of the outer roads rolled past, and stood there with her back to all of them for a long moment. She thought of Wren, who had trusted too easily and died for it. She thought of herself, who trusted no one and was dying of it more slowly. And she thought of the vault below, full of names, full of a nation somebody had decided to erase — and how erasure only works if everyone agrees to forget, and how Bo, sixteen years old and terrified of being left, had chosen the exact shape of the wound they knew best.

She turned around.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You don't contact Meridian. You translate for us — for the drowned, nobody else. And when this is over, whatever it turns into, you're still on this boat. Not because you earned it. Because family doesn't audit." She held Bo's gaze. "Do you hear me? Family means staying anyway. Even angry. Especially angry."

Bo made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and nodded, and Sefa's arm came around their shoulders, and Tam pretended very hard to study his charts.

But Odile kept one eye on the horizon through the porthole, because Tam was right about one thing: Varn had the claim, and the claim expired in three tides, and somewhere out beyond the gray line where the river met the sky, a fleet was already weighing anchor.

And now, Odile thought, watching the water, they knew exactly what she'd found.

The case on the table seemed, suddenly, very heavy — and very loud.
