## 13. Varn's Ledger

They took him at the fuel dock at Corren's Reach, in the gray hour between the fourth tide's ebb and its turn, when the water lies flat as poured pewter and a man's judgment goes soft with fatigue.

Tam had gone alone to buy diesel and a coil of new signal line — Odile's orders had been explicit about going in pairs, but Bo was asleep in the hold of the *Leeward Rose* with a fever, and Sefa's hands were needed on the engine mounts, and Tam had reasoned, in the way tired men reason, that a harbor was a public place, that no one would dare touch them inside the jurisdiction of the Reach, that the war they were fighting was fought on water and not on cobbles. He had been wrong on every count. The *Rose* sat anchored two hundred yards out in the roadstead, her riding light burning steady, close enough to see and too far to help, and by the time he understood what was happening — the two men peeling off the fish-market wall with the unhurried gait of professionals, the third drifting up behind him to close the square — the only thing left to decide was whether to make it difficult.

He thought of Sefa reading his footsteps through the deck planking. He thought of Odile's face when she'd refused Captain Varn's offer, the set of her jaw like something forged rather than born. And he thought, with a cartographer's cold clarity, of everything in his head: the decoded manifest, the word *expunged* in navy cipher, the coordinates of the vault room, the route through the leeward passage that existed on no chart but the one folded against his ribs. None of it could be allowed to leave him under duress.

So he made it difficult. Not much — he was forty-one years old and soft from years at a drafting table — but enough. Enough that they put him down hard on the wet cobbles, enough that his lip split against the stones, enough that the fishwives watching from the market awnings would remember it, would talk about it, would make it expensive for Meridian to operate quietly in Corren's Reach again. A small victory. He held onto it all the way into the canvas hood and the smell of tarred rope, all the way across the gangway he knew by the sound of it — heavier timbers than the *Rose*, naval salvage tonnage, probably a converted gunboat — all the way down whatever ladder they walked him, backwards, his shins barking on the treads.

Then the hood came off, and he was sitting in a cabin lit by a single brass lamp, and Captain Ilse Varn was pouring tea.

"Your nose is bleeding," she said. "Hold this." She pressed a folded cloth into his hand without waiting for him to take it properly, and sat down across the little table from him, and for a long moment she simply looked at him the way he imagined she looked at wrecks: assessing structural integrity, estimating what could be salvaged and what should be written off.

The cabin surprised him. He had expected a salvage master's den — charts, prize ledgers, the trophies of a career spent picking corpses. Instead there were books. Real books, leather-bound, shelved with the spines aligned, and a framed hydrographic survey of some estuary done in the old style, ink wash over pencil, beautiful work. A jar of pens sorted by nib width. It looked like the cabin of a man who loved the sea's paperwork more than the sea itself, and Tam, who had been exactly that man once, felt the unwelcome stir of recognition.

"You're the cartographer," Varn said. "Tamwell Ashe. Discharged from the Naval Hydrographic Office four years ago. The record says insubordination." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I pulled the actual file. It says you refused to certify a depth survey of the Calveth basin because your soundings didn't match the official chart, and you wouldn't falsify them."

Tam said nothing. The cloth was soaking red against his palm.

"Which means," she went on, "that you've known longer than most what the navy does with Calveth. You found the discrepancy yourself. Your own numbers told you the bottom of that basin is forty feet shallower than any natural silting could account for." She leaned back. "Forty feet of fill, Mr. Ashe. Somebody put a city under the mud on purpose, and you were the first man in a generation whose instruments were good enough to prove it. And they broke you for it."

"They transferred me to coastal lighthouse survey," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "It was quiet. I liked the quiet."

"No, you didn't." Varn smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was an honest one, which in Tam's experience was rarer and more dangerous. "Nobody who refuses to falsify soundings likes the quiet. They like being right. There's a difference, and it's the difference between you and me, so let's be efficient about this."

She reached beneath the table and brought up a ledger.

Not a prize ledger. Not a salvage log. It was bound in plain black calf, unmarked, the kind of book a bank keeps or a government keeps, and when she opened it Tam saw columns of figures in a fine clerk's hand, dates running back eleven years, entries initialed at the margin in three different inks — three different paymasters, he thought, before he understood what he was looking at.

"I'm going to show you this," Varn said, "because I want you to understand what you're actually racing. Not me. Not my fleet. This." She turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table so the lamp fell full on the page. "Read the entry headers. Go on. You have clearance-adjacent eyes; you'll recognize the format."

He read. The headers were ministry codes — he knew them, had filed against half of them in his old life — and the entries beneath them were payments. Regular, quarterly, substantial. Payments from the Admiralty Salvage Bureau. Payments from the Interior Ministry's Office of Records. One column, smaller but never missed, from an account whose header had been struck through with a single ruled line, leaving only the initials of whoever had once authorized it.

"What am I looking at?" he asked, though his stomach had already begun to tell him.

"Retainers," Varn said. "Eleven years of them. Every quarter, like clockwork, since the year after the flooding. Do you know what a wreck claim costs to maintain, Mr. Ashe? Survey fees, buoyage, patrol coordination, the bribes to harbor officials to keep independent divers off the site — and I do mean bribes, don't look so shocked, you bought diesel from one of mine yesterday. The Vesperine sits in open river water. By every law of salvage, she's fair game for anyone with a hull and a hook. Any diver, any day, for eleven years, could have gone down that outer hull and started pulling artifacts." She tapped the ledger. "Nobody has. Because nobody's been allowed to get near her, and the reason nobody's been allowed near her is that these people pay me to make sure of it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the deck, and the whole cabin flexed with the ship's slow breathing at anchor.

"The navy doesn't want the Vesperine salvaged," Tam said slowly. Working it through the way he'd work through a bad chart, plotting each fix against the last. "They want her *guarded*. Sealed. If she were just a sunken ship, they'd have raised her themselves years ago and burned whatever was aboard."

"Now you're navigating," Varn said. "Yes. A wreck is a problem you solve. But a wreck full of state papers — a census, if your runaway's translation work is as good as I hear — a census is a problem you can never solve, only contain. You can't burn what you can't admit exists. So they do the next best thing. They bury it deeper. They pay men like me to sit on top of it, and they pay clerks to strike names from registers, and they pay officers to write reports saying the site is unstable, hazardous, closed. Eleven years." She turned the ledger back toward herself and closed it, and her hand rested on the cover a moment, flat, almost tender. "Do you know how much money that is? I could have bought my own fleet twice over with what they've paid me to do nothing. That's the joke of it. The most profitable work I've ever done is preventing work."

"And now the claim expires," Tam said. "Six tides. Their own law forces their hand. They can't keep paying you to guard a wreck whose claim has lapsed, because a lapsed claim means anyone can dive her — including your own crews, including mine. So they've sent you to raise her yourselves, or sink her properly, before the clock runs out."

Varn's eyebrows rose a fraction, and Tam felt a small, foolish warmth at having surprised her.

"That's the shape of it," she said. "You'd have made a decent salvage master, Ashe. Cold arithmetic under pressure." She stood, took the ledger, and slid it into a drawer beneath the chart shelf — unlocked, he noticed, deliberately within his sight. "Here's what happens now. In an hour, my launch puts you back on your boat, alive and mostly unbattered, with a message for Dravenmoor. Not a threat. Threats are for people who respond to fear, and I've read her file too — fear just makes that woman dig in. No. My message is arithmetic. Tell her what I'm about to tell you, and let her do the sums."

She poured herself more tea, though Tam noticed her cup had gone cold and untouched, and stood at the little stern gallery window with her back half-turned, looking out at the dark water where the *Rose* rode at anchor, her single light burning.

"Dravenmoor thinks she's stealing history back from the navy," Varn said. "A noble errand. I don't even dispute it — I've read enough of the chest papers your boy translated to know the flooding was ordered, and I know whose grandfather signed the order, and I will give you my professional opinion that Ilse Varn has no love for the Dravenmoor name or the ministry that sheltered it. But nobility doesn't change the machinery. Here is the machinery." She held up fingers, counting. "One: the moment you breach that vault and move the archive, you convert a contained secret into a moving one, and moving secrets get intercepted. Two: the fleet that intercepts you will be mine, because I am paid to be exactly where you'll be, and unlike you I have gun mounts and thirty crew and the law on my side until the claim lapses. Three—" she turned, and her eyes in the lamplight were flat and gray as a winter channel— "three: even if you win, even if you raise the census and hand it to the descendants and stand on the shore reading names until your voice gives out, the people who signed those payments—" she nodded at the drawer— "will still hold their ministries, their fleets, their presses, and their patience. You will have cost them a scandal. They will cost you everything, slowly, over years, and they will sleep fine doing it. I know because I've watched them do it to better people than either of us."

"Is that the warning?" Tam asked. "That resistance is futile?"

"The warning," Varn said, "is that I am the *merciful* option, and you should think very carefully about what it says that I'm the merciful option." She came back to the table and leaned on it, close, and lowered her voice as though the bulkheads might be listening — and Tam understood suddenly that they might be, that there were probably ears in this cabin, that everything she'd shown him might be theater staged for someone else's report. Or might not. With Varn he suspected it was both at once, which was the most dangerous kind of honesty. "There are people above me in this arrangement, Ashe. I've met two of them in eleven years and both times I came away wanting to wash my hands. The quarterly payments come through three separate bureaus so that no single ledger proves anything — except mine, which is why mine stays on this ship and nowhere else. Those people do not want the census raised. They do not want it *found*. And if Dravenmoor gets it into open water, they will not send me. They will send something with no captain who pours tea."

She straightened. The interview, such as it was, was over.

"My men will return your chart case," she added, at the door. "We didn't search it. I want that understood — we didn't need to. I've had the leeward passage on my books for six years; your deaf friend's grandmother wasn't the only pilot who ever ran it. What you have that I don't is the vault location, and I'll have that by tomorrow night regardless, because the Reach is full of loose tongues and I pay better than the tide pays you." She opened the door and the cold corridor air came in, smelling of bilge and iron. "Tell Dravenmoor the arithmetic. Tell her I offered partnership once, honestly, and she chose this. And tell her—" Varn paused, and for just a moment the salvage captain's mask slipped and Tam saw underneath it something tired and old and strangely grieved, a woman who had spent eleven years sitting on a drowned city and had never once been permitted to say so— "tell her the dead don't need the census. We do. The living need it, or we'll do this again. Some other Calveth, some other flood, some other ledger like mine. That's the rot, Mr. Ashe. Not the sinking. The *bookkeeping*."

They walked him up the ladder and out into the gray air, and the launch ran him across the still water to the *Rose*, and hands — Sefa's first, gripping his wrist hard, then Bo's, fever-bright-eyed and shaking, then Odile's, steady, checking his split lip with two fingers and asking nothing yet — pulled him over the rail.

He gave them the message standing there on the deck with the east going pale behind the fleet's masts downstream. All of it. The ledger, the three bureaus, the struck-through account, the arithmetic, the warning about what would come after Varn. And at the end, because he was a mapmaker and believed in complete surveys, he gave them the thing Varn hadn't said aloud but had let him see, plain as a contour line: that she kept her true ledger unlocked in a drawer on her flagship, and that a woman who shows a prisoner her real accounts wants something other than a prisoner.

Odile was quiet a long time, looking south toward the Vesperine's reach, where the river lay flat and waiting for the fifth tide.

"She wanted us to see the price list," Tam finished. "Because she thinks we'll flinch."

Odile shook her head slowly. "No," she said. "She wanted us to see it because she's already flinched — eleven years ago — and she can't stop watching to see if anyone else will." She turned to the tiller. "Get below, Tam. Chart me every bureau in that ledger. If we're going to break their machinery, I want to know every gear in it."

And far downstream, riding lights blooming one by one along the black water, the Meridian Fleet began to weigh anchor for the fifth tide.
