## 14. Tide Four: The Vault Room

The tide turned at four minutes past dawn, and Odile felt it before she saw it — a slackening in the current that had been dragging at the anchor line all night like a hand testing its grip. She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* with her coffee going cold in one hand and the brass fitting stamped with Calveth's seal in the other, turning it over and over until the raised edges printed themselves into her palm.

Below them, forty feet down through water the color of old bottle glass, lay the Vesperine's hold.

Three tides they'd spent getting here. Three tides of running dark through locks, of Bo hunched over translations by lamplight, of Tam's charts spread across the chart table with his fingers trembling on the coastlines he'd once drawn for the navy and now drew against it. Three tides of Sefa reading the riverbed through her bare feet on the deck plates, tapping out warnings against the hull when words were too slow. And now the fourth tide had come, slack and gray and patient, and there was nothing left between them and the vault but water.

"You don't have to be first down," Tam said behind her. He'd come up without her hearing him, which was unlike him; the man moved like furniture. "Bo can take the lead line. I can—"

"I'm going first." She said it without turning around. "I've been diving this wreck since before any of you knew its name."

"That's not—" He stopped himself. She heard him exhale through his nose, the sound of a man choosing his battles. "Fine. But you wait for us at the breach point. You don't go into the vault alone."

Odile looked at the fitting in her hand. The seal of Calveth — a leaping fish above three wavy lines, the mark of a nation that officially had never existed — worn nearly smooth by decades of river silt and her own thumb.

"Tam," she said quietly, "when we open that vault, everything changes. For all of us. Whatever we find in there, we can't put back in the water. Do you understand that?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere below deck, Bo was reciting Calvethi numerals under their breath like a rosary, and Sefa was checking rigging with the methodical tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children.

"I've understood that since the chest came up," Tam said. "I just didn't want to say it out loud and make it true."

* * *

They went down in pairs, Odile and Sefa first, then Tam and Bo, four lines paid out into the green murk while the fourth tide held its breath. The Vesperine's hull rose up to meet them like the shoulder of something sleeping — barnacled, furred with weed, its iron skin blistered and flaking where seventy years of river had worked at it the way water works at stone: slowly, and without malice, and completely.

Sefa swam point, her hands trailing along the hull, reading it. Odile watched her fingers pause over seams and rivets, tap twice here, drag flat there, and knew she was mapping the wreck the way a blind woman maps a face. When Sefa found the breach point — a cargo hatch aft of the main mast, warped half-open by some long-ago collision — she rapped the hull three times, sharp, and hung a marker lamp that burned a sickly amber in the deep.

They gathered at the hatch. Through their masks, in the lamplight, Bo's eyes were enormous. Odile had argued about bringing them down at all — sixteen years old, barely a hundred pounds in gear — but Bo had looked at her with an expression she recognized from mirrors, the look of someone who would rather drown than be left on the surface again, and she had run out of arguments.

Bo signed rapidly: *Which way?*

Odile pointed through the hatch, into the black throat of the hold, and made the sign Sefa had taught them all last week: *Together.*

The inside of the Vesperine was a cathedral that had drowned mid-sermon. Their lamps swept across cargo netting gone to lace, across crates slumped into each other like tired pilgrims, across the ribs of the ship arching overhead into darkness no light could finish. Silt hung in the water so thickly that their beams seemed solid, columns of gold standing in the void. Fish scattered from the lights in silver flashes. Somewhere far off, metal ticked and settled, the ship talking to itself in its sleep.

And there, against the forward bulkhead, exactly where Wren's letter had said it would be —

*Aft of the mail room. Behind the double door with the fish on it. They wouldn't let me touch it, but I saw them open it once. It's real, Oddie. It's all still real.*

— stood a door.

It was smaller than Odile had imagined, which seemed impossible given how large it had grown in her mind. Iron, double-leafed, and though the paint had gone entirely, the seal was still legible: the leaping fish above three wavy lines, cast deep enough into the metal that the river hadn't managed to erase it. A lock plate sat at waist height, crusted with concretion, and beside it, bolted to the bulkhead, a placard whose lettering Bo had taught her to read over three nights of lamplight and cold tea:

**CENSUS OF RECORD — CALVETH PROVINCE — BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY**

By order of the Admiralty. Not of Calveth. Of the navy that had swallowed Calveth whole and then forbidden anyone to say its name.

Sefa touched the door and went very still. Then she turned to Odile and signed, slow and deliberate so everyone could follow: *Strong. Whole. Water-tight.* Her hands hesitated, then added: *Seventy years, and it kept.*

"Because it was built to," Tam said aloud, his voice strange and flattened by the mask intercom. "I've seen vaults like this in naval archives. Double-sealed, pressure-equalized. This wasn't storage." His lamp beam trembled against the iron. "This was meant to survive. Someone wanted this to be found."

"Or someone wanted it to be *kept*," Odile said, "and never asked what keeping means after seventy years."

She got the cutting charge rigged herself. It was small, surgical, placed to shear the lock plate without breaching the inner seal — Tam had spent two sleepless nights calculating it, checking his figures three times because his hands shook and he didn't trust them. Bo hovered at the edge of the lamplight, holding the guide line, and when Odile glanced over, they weren't looking at the door at all. They were looking at the seal. At the fish.

Their lips moved behind the mask. No sound. But Odile had learned enough of the old script by now, and enough of Bo, to know what they were saying. It might have been a prayer. It might have been a name.

"Everyone clear?" she called.

Four lamps. Four faces. The river pressing in around them like a congregation.

"Clear," said Tam.

Sefa struck the hull twice — *yes* — and then, unexpectedly, laid her palm flat against the iron door and left it there for a long moment, as if greeting someone through it. As if knocking gently, first, so as not to startle whoever waited inside.

Odile fired the charge.

* * *

The explosion underwater was less a sound than a feeling — a fist closing around the whole world, a pressure wave that shoved them back and filled the hold with a rolling thundercloud of silt. Visibility died. Odile clung to the guide line and counted heartbeats, ten, twenty, thirty, listening to the debris rain down around them with soft percussive taps, and fought down the animal panic that said *you are buried alive, you are buried alive, you are your sister, you are the mine, you are the flood.*

*Not this time,* she told it. *This time we're the ones opening the door.*

When the water began to clear — slowly, grudgingly, the way rivers forgive — the lock plate hung by a single bolt, peeled back like a lid off a tin. Beyond it, the inner door showed a seam of absolute blackness. Dry blackness. Air blackness.

"It sealed," Tam breathed. "The inner compartment — it's been dry. Seventy years and it's been *dry.*"

Sefa pulled the inner door, and it came with a groan and a sigh, and air came out of the vault — actual air, seventy years old, escaping in a shimmering silver ribbon that rose through their lamplight like a ghost leaving a body. Odile would remember that ribbon for the rest of her life. It bent and curled in the current, caught the light, and was gone.

Then Bo went in first, because nobody could stop them, and Odile followed her crew into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the vault was the size of a modest chapel and packed floor to ceiling with copper-lined cases, each stamped with the same fish-and-waves, each latched, each labeled in that flowing old script that Bo read the way other people read street signs. Their lamps found row after row after row, receding into shadow. Hundreds of cases. Thousands of pages.

Bo knelt at the nearest case with their hands shaking so badly they couldn't work the latch. Odile knelt beside them and opened it herself, and together they lifted out the first bundle.

Paper. Real paper, dry as autumn, soft as cloth. And on it, in ink that had faded from black to the brown of dried blood, in a hand both formal and hurried:

*Name: Aldous Merrick. Trade: netmender. Age: 41. Household: wife, Sera; daughters, Immet and Luce. District: Lower Weir. Recorded: [date]. Status:*

And then a word that stopped Odile's breath.

*Status: Drowned.*

Below it, in a different hand, a clerk's cramped addition: *Cause: provincial flood, engineered per Admiralty Order 114-C. Remains unrecovered. Entry permanent.*

Engineered. There it was. Not implied, not hinted, not buried in cipher the way Tam had found it in the expunged manifest — but written plainly, entered into the official record of every dead man, woman, and child in Calveth, by the clerks who had done the counting. *Per Admiralty Order 114-C.* The flood had not been a disaster. It had been a *procedure*, and this vault was its receipt.

"Oh," Bo said. Just that. One syllable, and their voice cracked down the middle of it. "Oh, there are so many."

Case after case. Bundle after bundle. Names in their thousands — Odile understood that within minutes, the way you understand the size of the sea by looking at it rather than by measuring it. Netmenders and lockkeepers and children recorded in their mothers' arms, whole districts listed street by street, house by house, the census so thorough it included the names of dogs. A nation reduced to paperwork. A people filed away in the dark at the bottom of a river, in a ship the navy forbade anyone to name, waiting seventy years for someone to come down and read them.

Tam had found the engineering orders in the third case — separate folios, wrapped in oilcloth, bearing diagrams and signatures and the cold arithmetic of men who had calculated water displacement against population density and called it planning. He read standing, motionless except for his eyes, and when he finally spoke his voice came out level in the way that ice is level.

"The sluice gates. Here's the schedule. Here's the tonnage calculations. Here's—" He stopped. Turned a page. Stopped again. "Here's the authorization for the second gate. The one they denied ever existed. Signed and countersigned."

"Signed by whom?" Odile asked, though she already knew. She'd known since Bo's translation, since the surname had surfaced from the fragment like a body coming up out of deep water. She'd carried the name down forty feet with her, and it weighed more than her gear.

Tam looked at her over the top of the folio, and did not make her say it, and did not pretend he didn't know either. That was its own kind of mercy.

Sefa had drifted apart from them, down the rows of cases, touching each one lightly as she passed — a fingertip to the copper lids, the way you'd touch the shoulders of mourners at a graveside. She couldn't hear the paper rustle or their voices, but she didn't need to. Odile watched her stop at one case, read the label with slow care — Bo had taught her the shapes of the letters, matching them to carved wooden blocks, letter by patient letter — and then bow her head over it, her hand resting flat, staying a long time.

Later, Bo would tell her: that case held a family named Voss. Sefa's grandmother's family. Runners of the leeward passage, keepers of routes no chart survived. The river had taken them, and the river had also, in its roundabout way, given Sefa back to them — had carried her grandmother's knowledge down through the generations like a current, until it delivered a girl who could feel the bottom of the water with her feet straight to the door of the vault where their names slept.

But that telling would come later, ashore, in daylight. Down here there was only the lamplight and the silence and the terrible gentle weight of all those names.

* * *

They stayed until the tide began to turn. They had to — Tam tracked it on his dive watch, calling the intervals, and none of them argued, though none of them wanted to go. Leaving felt like a kind of abandonment. Odile understood, looking at their faces in the lamplight, that every one of them was thinking the same thing: *we are the first people in seventy years to see these names, and we're just going to swim away and leave them in the dark again.*

"We're coming back," she said aloud, and Sefa tapped the hull twice — *yes* — and Bo signed it too, and Tam nodded, and it became a promise with four witnesses, which is the strongest kind.

At the hatch, pausing with her hand on the frame, Odile looked back one last time. The vault door stood open, its black interior no longer a secret but still a grave, still a country, still the truest record of Calveth that existed anywhere on earth. Seventy years the navy had kept it buried. Seventy years, and one spring flood, and one dead girl's letter, and four strays in borrowed dive gear had been enough to open it.

Her grandfather's signature was somewhere in those folios. She'd seen it — Tam had shown her, wordlessly, turning the page toward her lamp, and she had looked at the strong confident strokes of the name Dravenmoor and felt nothing for a full three seconds, and then everything forever.

History wasn't the past. She'd always known that, in the way divers know the surface is there even at the deepest point of a dive — a pressure, a direction, a place you must return to whether you want to or not. History was a weight on the present, and it had her family's name on it, and now it had hers too, because she was the one holding the rope.

Above her, the surface shimmered like hammered tin. She kicked for it, case strapped to her chest, the first of hundreds, and broke through into gray morning air and the smell of the river and Bo's voice cracking across the water — *"Did you get it? Is it—did you get it?"* — and Tam hauling her aboard with hands that had finally stopped shaking, and Sefa wrapping her in a blanket she hadn't asked for.

And it was only when she was warm, only when the shivering started, that Odile noticed the shape on the horizon.

A smudge, low and dark, where the channel met the sky. Too steady for cloud. Too deliberate for weather.

Through Tam's glass, it resolved into masts. More masts than Meridian should have. And at the fore, a pennant she didn't recognize — navy gray, navy cut, flying from a ship that had no business being this far upstream, this fast, this quietly.

The navy had stopped discussing Calveth.

Now, it seemed, it had come to discuss them.
