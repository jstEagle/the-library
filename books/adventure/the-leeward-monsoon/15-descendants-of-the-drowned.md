## 15. Descendants of the Drowned

Bo had learned to read the way other children learned to steal — in secret, quickly, and with a constant ear cocked for footsteps.

The orphanage at Grayharbor kept its books locked in the matron's office, but the chapel was open, and the chapel held a hymnal so old its pages had gone the color of tea. The hymns were printed in two columns: Harbor Tongue on the left, and on the right a script that curled and hooked like weed in current. Nobody sang from that column. Nobody could. Bo could, though — or nearly — because their grandmother, before the fever took her, had murmured it over the stove like a charm, and some part of Bo's mind had caught the shapes and held them the way a hand holds a shape pressed into wax.

They had never thought much about why. Languages were just things you picked up, like lice or hunger. You didn't ask where they came from. You asked whether they'd keep you alive.

Now, in the lamplit belly of the *Kestrel's Luck*, with the census fragment spread flat under a weight of brass fittings and Tam's magnifying lens, Bo understood for the first time that a language was also an inheritance — and inheritances came from somewhere.

* * *

The vault room still smelled of river. Two days after Sefa and Odile had breached it, the air below decks carried that mineral sweetness, cold and ancient, like licking a stone. The census itself had been brought up in oilcloth bundles and stacked in the main cabin, forty-one bundles, each one wrapped in tarred canvas that the divers had cut away with knives, revealing ledger after ledger bound in gray-blue calfskin stamped with the seal of Calveth: a leeward-facing tower above three waves.

Forty-one ledgers. Tam estimated eleven thousand names.

Bo had been given the job of triage — opening each bundle, checking water damage, flagging anything that looked like more than names. It was slow work, good work, the kind of work that let you stop thinking about the fact that you had sold these people out to Captain Varn less than a week ago. Odile had said she forgave Bo. Sefa had said it too, in her way, by handing Bo the first bundle without ceremony, as if nothing had happened at all, which was somehow worse and better at once. But forgiveness sat strangely on Bo, like a coat cut for someone bigger. They kept waiting for the moment it would be taken back.

So they worked. They worked with the diligence of the guilty.

The cabin lamp swung gently with the river's breathing. Outside, the light was going amber; Tide Five would come tomorrow, and with it whatever Varn intended, and nobody talked about that. Everybody talked about paper instead. Paper was safer than the future.

Bo cut the ties on bundle thirty-three.

* * *

The first pages were household registers — the ordinary miracle of bureaucracy, names entered in a clerk's careful hand: *Aldous Merrin, wife Sabine, daughters Ilse and Petra.* Occupations. Parishes. A column headed **STATUS** that had been filled in later, in a different ink, with a word Bo knew only from this document, a word that appeared beside every single name:

*Thalassé.*

Drowned. All of them. Eleven thousand times, drowned.

Bo copied out the unfamiliar entries mechanically, translating into Harbor Tongue in the margins of a scrap sheet, building a glossary as they went — *parish, hearth-right, next-of-kin* — and it was while writing *next-of-kin* that their eye snagged on something in the left margin of page forty. A notation, smaller than the rest, in that same later ink. Not a status. A cross-reference.

Bo leaned closer. The lamp flame steadied. The letters resolved.

It was a surname. And Bo knew it. Not from any history book. Not from the hymnal. From the chipped enamel plate that had hung beside their grandmother's stove, from the name she'd whispered when she thought Bo was asleep, from the word she used when she meant *home* — the family name she'd told Bo to forget, the one that got people looked at twice in certain harbors, the one Bo had buried under the alias *Bo Tench* the day they ran from Grayharbor.

**Vael.**

Their own name, written in a dead clerk's hand, eighty years old, beside the words *hearth-right confirmed, line unbroken.*

* * *

Bo did not move for a long time.

The river slapped softly against the hull. Somewhere above, Sefa's boots crossed the deck in her particular rhythm — heel-first, deliberate, a walk that announced itself because she couldn't hear herself do it. Tam was up there too; Bo could hear the scratch of his pencil through the deck boards, the sound he made when he was nervous, which lately was always.

Bo's hands were shaking. They watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to someone else — some other kid, some other runaway, sitting in another life.

Because here was the thing Bo had never told anyone, not Odile, not Sefa, not even themselves in the honest dark hours: they had always known they were different, and they had always assumed the difference was damage. That was what the matron said. *That child is wrong somewhere.* Wrong because they flinched at loud bells. Wrong because they read instead of played. Wrong because of the words they muttered in their sleep, words no orphanage in Grayharbor should have put in their mouth.

But the words hadn't come from nowhere. They had come down. Through the fever that took Gran, through whoever had carried the language before her, back and back through eighty years of silence to a flooded nation and a clerk with careful hands who had written down, in the last months of everything, the name **Vael**, hearth-right confirmed, line unbroken.

Line unbroken. Bo read it again. Line unbroken.

Gran hadn't taught them the script out of nostalgia. She'd taught it the way you teach a child to swim — because the water was coming, someday, in one form or another, and the language was the raft. *Keep it,* she'd said, the last coherent thing she ever said, pressing Bo's hand against the hymnal's curled letters. *Keep it, little fish. Someone has to keep it.*

Bo had thought she meant keep it safe. Keep it hidden.

She'd meant keep it *alive.*

* * *

The cabin door opened and Odile came down the companionway, salt-crusted, hair tied back wet from the evening rinse, carrying two mugs of the chicory sludge that passed for coffee aboard. She set one within Bo's reach without comment and stood looking at the open ledger, and Bo watched her face do the thing it always did around the census — that tightening, that grief-with-a-job-to-do look, the look of a woman carrying a mine shaft around inside her.

"Page forty," Bo said. Their voice came out cracked. They cleared their throat. "There's something you should see."

Odile crouched, bringing her eyes level with the page. Bo watched her read the marginal note, watched her lips move slightly as she sounded out the old script — she'd been learning, everyone had been learning, in the evenings, from Bo, haltingly, the way families learn each other's languages.

"Vael," Odile said. "That's—" She stopped. Bo saw the exact moment it landed, saw it move across her face like weather. "That's your name."

"It's my grandmother's name." Bo's throat was tight. "My real one. Before I— before I picked Tench off a barrel in Grayharbor." They laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "I stole my own name away and I didn't even know it."

Odile said nothing. She was good at that — at letting silence be a room you could stand in rather than a door closing. Bo had noticed it early, the first week, when they'd all still been strangers pretending to be a crew. Other people filled silences with questions. Odile just made space.

"I'm Calvethi," Bo said. Saying it aloud for the first time. Testing the weight of it. It fit their mouth strangely and perfectly, like a key that had been cut long ago and carried unused. "That's why I can read it. That's why the script was already in my head before I ever studied it. It wasn't study. It was—" They gestured helplessly at the ledger, at eleven thousand drowned names, at the small marginal note that had reached across eighty years to tap them on the shoulder. "*Inheritance.*"

"The line unbroken," Odile said quietly.

"Through Gran. Through whoever hid her people after the flood. There must have been Vaels who got out, or who were already living outside Calveth when it happened, and they just—" Bo swallowed. "They just went quiet. Generation after generation. You don't pass down a language for eighty years unless you're afraid of something. Unless you're keeping your head down because somebody drowned your country and might come back for the survivors."

And then, because the thought arrived fully formed and terrible, Bo said it:

"Odile. Your grandfather signed the order. My grandmother's family was on the list. We're on opposite ends of the same piece of paper."

The lamp flame bent. The hull ticked. Odile didn't flinch, didn't look away, didn't do any of the things Bo had braced for. She just sat down properly on the bench, mug forgotten, and looked at the page for a long moment.

"Yes," she said finally. "We are."

"That doesn't—"

"It doesn't change what you are to me." She said it simply, the way she said true things, which was without decoration. "You've been reading the dead for us all week. Turns out you're one of theirs. Good. Better than good." She tapped the marginal note once, gently, with one blunt finger. "This isn't a salvage manifest anymore, Bo. It's not cargo. It's a *roll call*, and you're on it. Which means when we give this archive to the descendants — and we are going to give it to them, whatever Varn does tomorrow — you won't be handing it over. You'll be *bringing it home.*"

Bo stared at her. Something in their chest had come loose, some knot tied the day they'd run from Grayharbor with a stolen name and a stolen loaf, the conviction that they were nobody's, that belonging was a story other kids got told.

"You knew," Bo said. "Before I said it. You saw the name and you knew what it meant."

"I grew up with a family that erased things," Odile said. "Names, records, people. I know what it looks like from the inside." Her jaw tightened, just slightly — Wren, Bo thought, she's thinking about Wren — and then eased. "You don't have to be erased. Not if you don't want to be."

* * *

Later, when the lamp burned low and Odile had gone up to take the middle watch, Bo sat alone with the ledger and did the thing they had been avoiding since page forty.

They searched for the rest of the Vaels.

It took an hour. The registers were organized by parish, and the Vaels appeared in the parish of Lowmarsh, a river-district Bo had never heard of and suddenly knew intimately: the clerk recorded fourteen households of Vaels across three pages. Fishers, netmenders, a bell-ringer at the parish church of Saint Aldhelm-of-the-Currents. Hearth-rights confirmed. And in the STATUS column, in that second ink, applied with what Bo could only imagine was the clerk's exhausted, obedient horror:

*Thalassé.* Thalassé. Thalassé. Fourteen households. Sixty-one souls.

Except one.

Near the bottom of the third page, the clerk's pen had hesitated — Bo could see the hesitation, the ink pooling where the nib had rested — and beside the name *Maren Vael, daughter of the house, age 9,* the column was not filled in. Instead, in the margin, cramped sideways as if written in haste or shame or hope:

*taken inland by kin — fate unknown*

Fate unknown. Bo read it until the letters swam. Nine years old. Taken inland by kin. Eighty years ago.

Gran had been nine years old when Calveth drowned.

Bo pressed both hands flat against the page, carefully, the way you touch something sleeping, and felt the absurd, unbearable certainty settle into them like ballast: this was her. This was Gran — Maren, not Marta, Maren Vael of Lowmarsh, parish of Saint Aldhelm-of-the-Currents, taken inland by kin, fate unknown, fate known now, fate sitting in a lamplit cabin reading her own name in a dead man's hand. She had grown up. She had hidden. She had kept the language alive in a kitchen in Grayharbor and passed it to a child she told to forget, and the forgetting had failed, because some things are written deeper than memory.

Bo was not a stray. Bo had never been a stray. They were the last page of a register that someone had risked everything to keep going.

They cried then — not the quick guilty tears of the past week, but something older and stranger, grief and homecoming tangled past separating, sixty-one names' worth of grief arriving all at once in one small body. They cried the way the river moved: steadily, without drama, going where it had always been going.

When it was done, Bo took the scrap sheet where they'd been building the glossary, turned it over, and wrote at the top, in careful Calvethi script, the curling hooked letters Gran had pressed into their hand over a dying stove:

*Maren Vael, of Lowmarsh. Line unbroken.*

Then, beneath it, their own name — their whole name, no barrel-label, no alias:

*Bo Vael.*

It looked right. It looked like coming up for air.

* * *

They were still sitting there, dawn-gray light beginning to seep through the stern windows, when Tam's boots came fast down the companionway — too fast, the pencil-scratch rhythm abandoned, and behind him Sefa's heavier tread, and Bo knew before anyone spoke, the way you know weather.

Tam stood in the doorway, face bloodless, holding a spyglass case like a weapon.

"Bo," he said. "You need to come up. Now."

Beyond him, through the open port, the river lay flat and pewter-colored in the early light — and far downstream, where the channel narrowed toward the sea, a line of black smoke rose against the brightening sky.

Varn had come a tide early.
