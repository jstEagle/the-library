## 16. Tide Five: The Trap

Sefa felt the fleet before anyone saw it.

She was below, coiling line in the hold of the *Leeward Girl*, when the water changed its mind. That was how she thought of it — the river had a mind, and it spoke to her through the soles of her boots and the bones of her hands, through the hull that carried every tremor of the current up into her body like a second pulse. All morning the current had been talking in long, easy sentences, the deep-water draw of the channel running smooth toward the sea. Then, just past midday, the sentences shortened. Chopped. A stutter in the flow, far off, like a door held half-closed.

Engines. Many engines, holding station against the current.

She climbed the ladder two rungs at a time and came out into the grey light with her palms already flat against the deck rail, reading it the way Tam read his charts. Bo was up top, hunched over the translated census pages under an oilcloth, and Odile stood at the wheel with that look she wore when she was pretending not to count things.

Sefa signed fast. *Boats. Downstream. Four. Maybe five.*

Bo translated aloud, voice cracking on the last word, and Odile's jaw tightened. She raised the glass, swept the bend where the channel narrowed between the old pilings, and lowered it again without saying anything, which was answer enough.

"They're early," Tam said from the chart table, where he'd been redrawing the channel by hand because no chart survived the leeward passage and he trusted nothing printed anymore. "Varn said six tides. This is tide five. She's not waiting for the claim to expire. She's ending the argument before it starts."

"She's not here to argue," Odile said.

*Blockade,* Sefa signed, and Bo's hands went still over the census pages. *At the narrows. They'll have a chain or they'll have guns or they'll have both.* She had seen blockades before, back when she ran cargo for men who paid in coin that smelled wrong, and she knew the shape of them: boats spaced across the channel like teeth, one gap left open in plain sight, and that gap was always the trap.

Odile looked at her for a long moment. "How do you know?"

Sefa touched her boot, then the deck, then pointed downstream. *The water told me.*

Bo hesitated, then gave it plainly, without softening, which was why Sefa had started to love the kid — Bo never made her smaller by making her mysterious. "She says the river told her."

Tam laughed once, without humor. "Then the river's a better intelligence service than the navy ever was."

They came about and ran down to see for themselves, engine low, hugging the eastern bank where the willows leaned out over the water and hid their silhouette. What waited at the narrows stopped even Odile's breath — Sefa saw it in the way the woman's shoulders locked, saw it in the sudden silence of her hands on the wheel.

Five hulls across the channel, gunmetal-grey, riding high and empty of cargo. The *Meridian Vow* at center, Varn's flagship, her crane arm folded like a sleeping insect. Two launches patrolling the shallows in slow figure-eights. And strung between the nearest pilings, catching the flat light, a chain — actual chain, links thick as a man's forearm, sagging into the brown water. Not a suggestion. A sentence.

Beyond it, the river widened toward the sea, and beyond the sea was everything: harbors, newspapers, descendants, the world that had spent thirty years pretending Calveth had never existed.

"Well," Bo said quietly. "That's that, then."

"It's not that," Sefa signed, and made Bo say it, because she wanted to hear herself commit. "It's not that. We have something they want more than the channel."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a drowned orchard — the tops of apple trees standing out of the floodwater like the fingers of buried men, a leftover scar from the flooding, from the day the engineers had opened the sluices and let the valley drink. Sefa had always hated this stretch of river. Now she understood why. The whole valley was a grave, and they were sitting in it, and somewhere beneath their keel lay the Vesperine with three thousand names in her belly.

They gathered in the cabin, all four of them, the lamp swinging, the census pages spread out and weighted with brass fittings at the corners. Odile laid it out plainly: the archive could not go back in the water forever, could not be surrendered, could not be sold. Which left one direction. Through the blockade.

"Four against five," Tam said. "And their four are war-surplus. Ours is a salvage tug with a bad injector pump."

"Their five are crewed by hired men," Odile said. "Hired men follow orders until following orders costs more than refusing."

"That's a thin thing to bet three thousand names on."

"It's the only thing I've got." Odile's voice was level but Sefa could feel the tremor coming up through the deck plates, through the soles of her feet — Odile's fear, transmitted steel to steel, the way everything traveled on a boat. "So somebody find me a better one."

Sefa had been turning it over since the orchard. Turning it the way she turned everything — not in words, in weight. She thought about the Vesperine's hull, which she had touched more than any living person. She thought about the sea cocks Tam had found on the engineering drawings, the ones the original crew had never opened. She thought about what a wreck full of paper becomes if you let the river back inside it.

Pulp. Slurry. Ten thousand fragments drifting downstream into the delta mud, past every claim, every court order, every sealed manifest, irretrievable as breath.

She put her hand flat on the table until everyone looked at her. Then she signed, slow, so Bo could keep pace, and watched her own idea turn into words in the kid's mouth and become real and terrible:

*We tell Varn we'll open the hull. Sea cocks. Both holds. If she doesn't let us through, the census goes into the river — all of it, scattered, gone. No claim. No sale. Nothing for her to deliver to whoever pays her.*

Bo's voice shook on *gone*. Tam sat back like he'd been struck. "You're proposing we hold history hostage."

"I'm proposing we make it worthless to steal," Sefa signed, and Bo said it, and the words hung there in the lamplight.

"It won't work," Tam said. "Varn calls the bluff. She has to. If she blinks once, every salvage captain on the coast learns Meridian can be threatened."

"Then we don't bluff," Odile said slowly, and Sefa saw the moment the idea took root in her, saw it flower and darken at once. "We open one cock. One. Let her hear the water going in. Let her stand on her own deck and listen to three thousand names drowning while she decides what she actually serves."

Nobody spoke. The lamp swung. Somewhere above them the apple trees creaked in the current, patient as the dead.

"And if she calls it anyway?" Bo asked. "If she lets us sink it?"

Sefa answered that one herself, signing fast now, sure of it in her hands before her mind caught up: *Then we close the cock, pump her out, and we've lost nothing but time — because Varn will have shown the whole river she'd rather drown the names than free them. Her crew hears it too. Half those men are delta-born. Half of them have grandmothers under this water.* She let that land. *A blockade is only as strong as the men who hold the line. I've read blockades my whole life. It isn't the chain that breaks first.*

Bo translated it all, and by the end the kid's voice had steadied, and Tam was staring at Sefa with something new in his face — not pity, not the careful kindness people wore around her, but respect, raw and unguarded.

"You've been planning mutinies in your head your whole life," he said.

*No,* Sefa signed, and smiled. *I've been listening to them. There's a difference.*

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation was its own kind of vow.

Tam drafted the message in his old naval hand, formal and cold, addressed to Captain Ilse Varn of the Meridian Salvage Fleet, and it read less like a negotiation than a sentence being passed. Odile read it twice and changed nothing. Bo copied it fair. Sefa rigged the signal halyard and checked the dinghy's patch, because someone would have to carry the message out under a white rag, and she volunteered before anyone else could — she was the smallest target, the quietest approach, and besides, she wanted to see Varn's boat up close. Wanted to feel it through her boots. Wanted to know what kind of ship holds a grave for ransom.

While Bo rowed out under the flag, the rest of them worked the Vesperine.

The wreck lay forty fathoms of flooded orchard away, and Odile went down with Sefa tending line, because if this plan was going to live or die on the sea cocks, Sefa wanted her hands on the valve wheels herself. She went down the shot line into the green murk where the light died and the cold closed over her like a hand, and she found the engine room by touch, by memory, by the map she kept in her fingertips. The starboard sea cock sat behind a bracket crusted with thirty years of river stone. She worked the wheel gently. It moved — stiff, grinding, but it moved. She closed it again and marked it with a wrap of red cord, then crossed the tilting deck and did the same to the port side.

Two turns from drowning the truth. That was the arithmetic they were betting everything on.

When she surfaced, Bo was back, white-faced, hauling the dinghy over the rail.

"She read it," Bo reported. "Stood there on her quarterdeck and read it twice. Then she laughed. Actually laughed." The kid's hands were shaking. "She sent back a message. She says — she says, *'Open whatever you like. Paper sinks slower than liars swim. You have until slack water tomorrow.'*"

Slack water. The turn of the tide, when the current dies and the river holds its breath. Varn wasn't giving them a deadline; she was giving them a stage. She expected them to flinch in front of witnesses.

Odile took the note and read it, and Sefa watched her face do something complicated — grief and fury and, underneath it, a strange relief, the relief of a gambler who finally sees the size of the pot.

"Then we open one," Odile said. "Tomorrow. At slack water. In front of her whole fleet."

* * *

Night came down on the drowned orchard, and nobody slept well, and Sefa didn't try.

She sat on deck instead, wrapped in her coat, and listened to the river with her whole body, the way her grandmother had taught her before she'd ever taught her knots. *The water knows who's coming,* the old woman used to sign, hands moving in lantern light on a houseboat that smelled of tar and cardamom. *Feel for the lie in the current. Every lie makes a ripple.*

There were lies in the current tonight.

Two hours past midnight, Sefa felt it first: a launch, running dark, oars muffled, coming up the eastern channel where the willows grew thickest. Not Varn's patrol pattern — the patrols ran wide, confident, lit. This was something quieter. Something that didn't want to be part of any ledger.

She woke Odile with a hand on her shoulder, no lamp, and signed: *One boat. Coming slow. From the fleet, but not for Varn.*

They watched from the shadow of the wheelhouse as the shape resolved — a Meridian launch, yes, but riding low and unlit, and standing in the bow a big man with delta-broad shoulders, the build of someone whose family had farmed the valley before the valley became a lake. He came alongside without hailing, and when Odile leaned over the rail, he spoke low, in the flat accent of the flooded country.

"We heard your message," he said. "Word travels on a blockade faster than you'd think. There are eleven of us aboard the *Vow* born downstream of the sluice gates. Eleven." He held up his hands, fingers spread, then folded them. "Captain Varn thinks she holds the channel. Ask yourself who holds the *Vow*."

"Why are you telling me this?" Odile asked.

The man looked past her, at the dark water, at the drowned orchard, at the place where his grandmother's village slept under forty fathoms. "Because tomorrow, when you open that valve, some of us may be slower to stop you than our orders say." He pushed off from the rail before Odile could answer, oars dipping silent, and the launch faded back downstream into the dark it had come from.

Sefa stood beside Odile at the rail, and through the deck she felt the man's wake pass beneath them and dissolve, and she felt something else underneath it, deeper, older — the long slow pulse of the river carrying its dead, patient as it had been for thirty years, patient as it would be tomorrow.

*They're cracking,* she signed. *From the inside.*

Odile nodded slowly. But her eyes stayed on the black water, and her hand found the rail and gripped it, and Sefa understood that the captain of the *Leeward Girl* was already doing the terrible arithmetic of tomorrow: one turn of a valve wheel, five grey hulls, eleven uncertain hearts, and three thousand names balanced on the edge of either rescue or ruin.

Above them, the sky was going pale at the edges. Slack water was coming.

So was the tide that would decide everything.
