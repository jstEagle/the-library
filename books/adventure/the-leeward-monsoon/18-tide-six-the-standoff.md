## 18. Tide Six: The Standoff

The slack water came at dawn, as Sefa had promised it would — a half hour when the river forgot which way it was going and hung between tides like a held breath. Odile stood at the rail of the *Leeward Rose* and watched the Meridian Fleet come up the channel with the sun behind them, six hulls in echelon, their cranes folded like sleeping birds. Captain Ilse Varn's flagship led them, black-hulled, her name painted over so many times that the older names showed through like scars beneath skin.

"They'll board within the quarter hour," Tam said. He had his charts rolled under one arm and his hands were steady, which surprised Odile; she had expected him to shake. "Admiralty law's on their side until we raise something. Once Varn puts boots on this deck, everything below becomes hers."

"Then we'd best be sure she doesn't want what's below," Odile said.

Bo stood near the mast with the census fragment pressed flat against their chest, both arms wrapped around it like a child holding a cat they weren't sure wouldn't scratch. They hadn't slept. None of them had. The vault room was open now — had been open since yesterday's tide, since Odile's hands found the brass wheel and turned it and the sea gave up four thousand names into lamplight. The engineering orders lay stacked in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, each page bearing the signature of a man whose blood ran in Odile's veins.

*Dravenmoor.* Her grandfather had signed the order to drown a nation, and he had signed it in a hand so careful, so unhurried, that you could see he'd taken pride in it.

"You don't have to do this part," Bo said quietly. "Whatever you're planning. You could let me—"

"No." Odile said it without heat. "This is mine to do. I've been running from my name for thirty years. Time I used it for something."

Sefa came up from below, wiping her hands on a rag, and touched Odile's elbow twice — their signal for *look*. She pointed downstream, then made a fist and opened it slowly. *They're deploying boats.*

Odile looked. Four launches had dropped from the fleet's flanks, each carrying eight men with boarding hooks and naval sidearms that no salvage charter was supposed to carry. The navy's fingerprints were all over this morning, plain as tar on a white deck. Varn wasn't coming to negotiate. She was coming to take.

"Positions," Odile said. "Nobody fights. Nobody draws. We let them aboard, and then we show them what their paymasters drowned."

* * *

Varn came across in the first launch herself, which told Odile everything about how seriously the woman took this. She climbed the pilot ladder with the ease of someone who had been boarding wrecks since before Odile was born — sixty if she was a day, silver-cropped hair, a face weathered down to its load-bearing structures. Her eyes went over the *Rose* the way Odile's went over a hull: fast, total, cataloging every weakness.

"Odile Dravenmoor." Varn's voice carried the flat authority of a woman who had never once needed to repeat herself. "You're anchored over a wreck you have no claim to, in a channel you entered illegally, holding cargo that belongs to the Crown under writ of expungement."

"The Crown doesn't exist anymore," Tam said from the rail. "Neither does the writ. I've read the papers, Captain. Have you?"

Something flickered across Varn's face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "I know what I'm paid to know."

"That's the saddest sentence I've heard all year," Tam said.

"Stand down, cartographer." Varn stepped onto the deck proper, and behind her her crew began to follow, eight, twelve, sixteen of them, spreading along the rails with the practiced geometry of an operation run many times before. Odile counted. Twenty-two aboard. The launches held position alongside, engines idling. "Here is what happens now. My people secure the hold. Your vessel is impounded pending adjudication. You and your crew will be put ashore at Meridian Point, and if you're wise, you'll take the compensation I offered you two tides ago and disappear."

"And if we're not wise?" Odile asked.

Varn looked at her then — really looked, the way one diver looks at another across dark water. "I sank my first wreck at nineteen," she said. "Not raised. *Sank.* Sealed a hull with forty-one men inside because the manifest said they were already dead on paper, and paper is what the navy pays for. Don't make me add you to a ledger, Dravenmoor. I keep very complete ones."

The words landed like cold iron. Odile felt Bo go rigid beside her, felt Sefa shift her weight toward the winch controls without appearing to move at all. But it was Tam who spoke, quiet and terrible:

"Forty-one men. What were their names?"

Varn's jaw tightened. "That's not—"

"Because we have four thousand names down in that hold," Tam said. "Every soul Calveth lost. Every citizen your employers flooded out of existence and then erased from the record. And here's the thing about names, Captain — they don't stay sunk. Ask any of your crew. Ask them where their grandmothers came from."

Silence stretched across the deck. Somewhere below, the river slapped against the hull, patient as ever.

"Secure the hold," Varn said.

Her crew moved. And Odile moved too — not toward them, but aft, to the sea cock housing she'd had Sefa rig three nights ago while the rest of them slept. It was a simple mechanism, brutal in its honesty: a valve assembly plumbed straight into the bilge, connected to the old flooding channels of the *Vesperine* herself, whose broken spine lay directly beneath them. Open it, and the river would come in through the wreck's wounds and take the *Rose* down with it, down into the hold, down among the oilcloth-wrapped pages, down where no crane and no claim and no navy writ would ever reach them cleanly again.

She put her hand on the wheel.

"Captain," she said, loud enough to carry. "Before your people touch that hatch, you should know what's under my feet."

Varn raised a fist. Her crew stopped.

"This barge sits directly above the census vault. The hull below us is already breached — I opened it myself. If I turn this wheel, we flood. All of us. Me, my crew, your boarding party, and every page of that archive, scattered into silt and current where no court, no fleet, and no ministry will ever assemble them again." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. "So here is the choice, and it isn't mine alone to make anymore. Sink the evidence — and drown twenty-six people doing it — or stand down, and let the world read what your paymasters spent fifty years keeping buried."

"You're bluffing," Varn said. But her voice had changed key.

"I buried my sister once," Odile said. "I know exactly what I'm capable of."

* * *

For a long moment nobody breathed. The slack water held. The sun cleared the eastern bluffs and laid a bright road across the channel, and on that road the Meridian launches rocked, their engines muttering.

It was one of Varn's own crew who broke it. A young woman, maybe Bo's age, standing third along the port rail, her boarding hook still in her hand but lowered now, pointing at nothing. "Captain," she said. "My name is Anselm Corvane. My family came out of Calveth. Lower Calveth, the weavers' district." She swallowed. "Is my name in there? In the census?"

Bo stepped forward before Odile could stop them. "Corvane. Yes." Their voice cracked on it. "Maren Corvane, weaver, age thirty-four. Joset Corvane, age seven." Bo's eyes were wet and furious. "I've read them so many times I know them by heart. They're not numbers. They're *people*, and somebody decided they were cheaper dead than heard."

Another of Varn's crew — an older man, gray at the temples, salvage-line calluses on his palms — set his hook down on the deck with a deliberate click. "Rellik," he said. "My grandmother's maiden name. Rellik of the North Terraces." He looked at Varn, and there was no anger in his face, only a kind of exhausted clarity. "Ilse. We've been raising their things for twenty years and selling them back to museums. I always figured the sinking was an accident of war. Tell me it was an accident."

Varn said nothing.

"Tell me it was an accident," he said again, louder.

"I follow orders," Varn said finally, and the words came out of her like splinters. "We all follow orders. That's the whole architecture of it — that's why it works. Nobody has to know anything except the man who signs." Her eyes found Odile's hand on the sea cock wheel, and something moved behind them — not fear, Odile realized. Recognition. "Your grandfather signed it. I've seen the order. Dravenmoor. It's why they sent me after you specifically, did you know that? They wanted a Dravenmoor's name on the recovery manifest too. Symmetry. The navy loves symmetry."

The deck seemed to tilt under Odile. Not the river — the truth. They had sent Varn because of her. Because her family's crime required her family's complicity to stay buried, generation after generation, the sin passed down like an heirloom nobody was allowed to open.

"Yes," Odile said. "I know. I found his signature four days ago." She lifted her chin. "And I'm the last Dravenmoor, Captain, and I am telling you — formally, on the record, with witnesses — that I refuse the inheritance. Turn the wheel or leave it shut, either way, my name stops being theirs today."

The gray-haired man — Rellik — turned to face Varn fully. One by one, without a word spoken, the others did the same. Twenty-two salvagers of the Meridian Fleet, standing in the dawn light, their boarding hooks grounded, their faces turned toward their captain like a jury that had already deliberated.

"I can't order you—" Varn began.

"You can't," Rellik agreed. "But you can decide what you are. Same choice she's offering. Same choice the river's offering." He nodded at the slack water, the bright road of sun, the black hulls waiting beyond. "We stand down, or we drown with the names. Those are the options. I've got grandchildren, Ilse. I'd rather they learn my name from a memorial than from a salvage manifest."

Varn stood very still. Odile watched her the way she'd watch a diver too long down — watching for the signs, the small failures, the moment the body decides for the mind. And she saw it happen: saw the captain's shoulders drop a half inch, saw her gaze travel from the sea cock to the hatch to the faces of her crew and back again, running the arithmetic of a lifetime in the space of three breaths.

"My orders," Varn said quietly, "were to recover the archive intact and deliver it to the Ministry of Interior Records. Unlisted. Unlogged. There's a furnace facility at Harrowgate that's been standing by for eleven days."

"Furnace," Bo whispered. It wasn't a question. It was a wound.

"So you see," Varn went on, and now her voice had gone strange and light, the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on for decades, "if I stand down, I'm not disobeying a salvage writ. I'm committing treason against the office that has owned me since I was nineteen years old." She laughed — one short, hard note with nothing in it. "Forty-one men in my first hull. I told myself they were already dead on paper. I have told myself that every day for forty years."

"Read their names," Odile said. "That's all any of us are asking. Read them, and then decide who you've been working for."

Varn closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked ten years older and twenty pounds lighter, as though she had finally surfaced from a dive begun in another life.

"Stand down," she said to her crew. Then, louder, across the water, raising both arms in the fleet's own signal: "*Meridian elements — stand down. Repeat: stand down. Boarding aborted.*"

Along the channel, the launches throttled back. On the flagship's deck, tiny figures paused mid-task, and then, in ones and twos, set down their gear. Someone — Odile couldn't see who — began, very faintly, to cheer.

Varn walked to the rail beside Odile and looked down at the sea cock wheel, at Odile's hand still resting on it.

"You'd have done it," Varn said. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like respect.

"I'd have hated every second," Odile said. "But yes. Wren taught me that some things matter more than staying dry." She lifted her hand away from the wheel and stepped back from it, and only then did she realize how hard she'd been gripping — her fingers wouldn't uncurl properly, and Sefa had to come and work them loose one by one, wordless, her thumb pressing circulation back into Odile's palm like a promise.

Varn straightened her collar, a small reflexive gesture, a captain putting on her authority one last time before setting it down. "The Ministry will send someone. Within a day, probably less. When they do, they won't come with writs. They'll come with worse." She turned to face the little crew of the *Leeward Rose* — the cartographer, the runaway, the deaf rigger, the last Dravenmoor. "So whatever you mean to do with those pages, do it fast, and do it loud. Names survive in daylight. It's only in the dark that they can be erased."

She signaled her launch. At the ladder she paused, one boot on the rail, and looked back.

"For what it's worth, Dravenmoor — your grandfather's signature was the most beautiful handwriting I've ever seen on an order of execution. I used to think that was the worst thing about it." Her mouth twisted. "Now I think the worst thing is that yours might have been just as beautiful, and nobody will ever know, because you chose better."

She went down the ladder. The launch pulled away. And the slack water ended — Odile felt it in her feet before she saw it in the surface, the river remembering itself, taking up its old argument with the sea.

Tam appeared at her shoulder with his charts clutched to his chest like scripture. "Daylight," he said. "She said daylight. There's a printing house in Meridian Port — the *Ledger-Clarion*, they owe me a favor from before the disgrace. If we can get the archive there by tomorrow's tide—"

"Then we'd best start hauling," Odile said.

But even as she turned to give the order, she was looking downstream, past the standing-down fleet, past the bright water, toward the east — where, far off and closing, a government cutter had just come around the bluff, flying no flag at all.
