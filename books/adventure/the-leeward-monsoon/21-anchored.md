## 21. Anchored

The bell came up on the fifth day after the standoff, when the river had finally gone quiet enough to hear itself think.

Sefa felt it before anyone told her. She was below decks in the *Kestrel's Mercy*, mending a hawser that had no business being mended — Bo had frayed it against the channel rocks during the blockade run and then lied about it with the transparent guilt of someone who had never been good at lying to her — when the vibration changed. The whole hull hummed differently. A new note entered the water, low and round, like a struck bowl still ringing somewhere far off.

She set down the marlinspike and climbed into the gray morning light.

They were all there at the rail: Odile with her arms crossed against the cold, Tam holding the line with both hands as if it might be taken from him, Bo perched on the capstan with their knees drawn up. And rising out of the green water on the end of the crane cable, dripping, turning slowly, hung the bell of the *Vesperine*.

It was smaller than Sefa had expected. Salvage bells usually were — ships lied about their own grandeur, promised in bronze what they couldn't deliver in oak — but this one was beautiful anyway. It was cast in a shape she didn't recognize, wider at the mouth than any navy pattern, and around its shoulder ran a band of lettering in the old Calvethi script, the letters looping into one another like reeds bending in current. The bronze was furred with sixty years of river, but where Bo had already begun to work a soft brush across it, the metal underneath shone pale gold, almost warm.

Odile reached out and stopped the bell's slow swing with one flat hand. She held it that way, palm against the metal, for a long time. She didn't say anything. She never said anything when it mattered most; Sefa had learned to read her hands instead, and Odile's hands were saying something careful and enormous.

Then she looked up at the crew — her crew, though she'd have denied the word if pressed — and said, "We can't put this back."

"No," Tam agreed quietly.

"And we can't sell it." Her jaw worked. "I know what it would fetch. I know who'd buy it. Some collector in the capital would hang it in a glass case and charge admission to grief."

"So what do we do with it?" Bo asked. Their voice cracked on the question, and they hid it by dropping down off the capstan and coming closer, close enough to touch the bell themselves.

Sefa answered before she fully decided to. It happened sometimes — words arriving through her chest before they reached her mind, pushed up by some older instinct. She signed first, out of habit, then made herself speak it aloud because they all needed to hear it:

"We ring it."

Four faces turned toward her. Even Odile's.

"A ship's bell isn't a relic," Sefa said. Speaking was effort, always, each word hauled up like ballast, but this was worth the hauling. "A ship's bell is a voice. You don't put a voice in a glass case. You let it call people home."

The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was the kind of silence a harbor has at slack tide — full of everything, waiting to move.

Tam cleared his throat. "You want to mount it on a boat."

"I want to mount it on *our* boat," Sefa said.

* * *

The boat in question was not much to look at, which was precisely why Sefa loved it.

She'd found the hull two years ago, half-sunk in a backwater off the lower reach, and had bought it from its despairing owner for less than the price of the rope still coiled in its bow. Forty feet, clinker-built, old-fashioned in the way of things built by someone's grandfather rather than someone's accountants. She'd patched it, re-caulked it, and then — because staying anywhere had always felt like standing in rising water — she'd left it. Left it twice, actually. And both times, walking away down the levee road, she had found herself looking back over her shoulder at the little hull sitting patient in the reeds, and both times the looking-back had frightened her more than any shoal passage ever had.

Now the boat sat at the yard in Grayharbor with its deck planks freshly laid, and the whole crew of them stood around it arguing about bells.

"It has to go forward," Tam insisted, sketching on the back of one of his charts with a carpenter's pencil. He'd drawn the deck plan four times already, each version more precise and more wrong. "Aft is where the weight should be — ballast, stores—"

"The mast step won't take it forward," Sefa signed, then caught herself and said it too, because Bo was watching her lips and Odile was pretending not to need the signs but always watched them anyway. "Forward is where the vibration lives. You'll crack the step inside a season."

"Then reinforce the step."

"Then you've added forty pounds of iron to a boat that rides like a duck already."

"Better than a boat that rings like one," Tam muttered, but he was smiling as he said it, erasing his fourth drawing with the side of his hand. This was how they talked now — bickering as a form of affection, disagreement as proof of belonging. Sefa had spent thirty years on crews where disagreement meant fists or abandonment, sometimes both. Here it just meant Tuesday.

Bo solved it, the way Bo kept solving things nobody had asked them to solve. They'd been crouched at the bow reading the bell's inscription with their fingers, tracing the loops of Calvethi script the way other people traced braille, and now they sat back on their heels.

"It says something," they announced. "Around the top. I've got most of it."

"Read it," Odile said.

Bo took a breath. "'I carry the names. Let the water keep the bodies.'" They looked up. "That's — that's what it says. That's the whole thing. That's what the bell was *for*, I think. Not calling sailors to meals. Carrying the census. The bell was the archive's voice. When they rang it, everyone in Calveth knew the names were safe."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Downriver, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Then it goes amidships," Sefa said finally. "Center of the boat. Where a voice belongs."

Tam looked at his ruined drawings, looked at the bell, and nodded slowly. "Amidships," he agreed. "We'll build a proper belfry. Small. Enough to shelter it from spray." He paused, pencil hovering. "And a mounting so it can be lifted out. For—" he hesitated over the word, chose it carefully, "—for readings. If there are more shore communities like the ones downstream. If there are more descendants who want to hear their names."

"You want to take it places," Odile said. Not a question. Her voice had an edge Sefa recognized — the sound of Odile Dravenmoor encountering a future she hadn't planned for.

"I want the names to travel," Tam said simply. "They've been underwater long enough."

* * *

The work took eleven days, and Sefa loved every hour of them in a way that unsettled her more than the blockade had.

There was a rhythm to it that she had never let herself have before. Mornings began with Odile boiling coffee on a spirit stove in the yard's lean-to, strong enough to strip varnish, and ended with Bo reading aloud while the others worked — whatever they could find, mostly, salvaged newspapers and Tam's old charts and, increasingly, pages from the census itself, which Bo handled now with a reverence that had replaced their earlier fear of it. Afternoons were sawdust and argument. Evenings were cards, which Sefa lost at consistently because Tam cheated and Odile pretended not to notice and Bo was unnervingly lucky.

On the fourth day, rain drove them under the lean-to, and Odile taught Sefa a card game from the mining camps where she'd grown up, a game played with a stripped deck, and Sefa discovered that Odile laughed — really laughed, a short startled bark like something escaping — when Bo accused Tam of cheating and Tam, wounded, produced three separate mathematical proofs that he hadn't. On the seventh day, Tam brought out a bottle of plum brandy he claimed he'd been saving for vindication, and they drank it for no reason at all, which Sefa understood was the better occasion. On the ninth day, Odile fell asleep in a deck chair at dusk with the census fragment still open on her lap, and nobody woke her, and Sefa stood guard over her sleep without being asked, the way you stand watch over something you've decided matters.

Sefa had run from crews her whole life. That was the shape of her history: join, belong, flinch, leave. She'd told herself it was the leaving that kept her free, but standing in the sawdust light of that boatyard, she began to suspect the truth was worse and simpler — she'd never once stayed long enough to find out whether the flinching was necessary. Every departure had been a door closed before anyone could show her whether it locked from the inside.

This crew had already seen her deafness, her silences, her hands doing the work her voice couldn't. They'd learned her signs without being taught, badly at first, then better, until Tam could sign *dinner* and *danger* and — embarrassingly, tenderly — *good work today*. Bo had learned to tap rhythms on the deck planks where she could feel them through her boots. Odile had never once done her the insult of pity. They had simply rearranged themselves around her, the way water rearranges around a stone, without making the stone feel like an obstacle.

And they had stayed. Through the patrol boats and the blockade and Varn's mutinous crew standing down in the rain, they had stayed, and none of them had left in the eleven days since, and the world had not ended.

The flinching, it turned out, had never been necessary.

* * *

On the last day, they raised the bell into its belfry.

It took all four of them — Tam and Odile on the tackles, Bo guiding the shackle, Sefa feeling the load through the lines, reading the strain in the rope fibers the way she read everything, through her palms. The bell rose slow and golden into the small wooden shelter they'd built for it, amidships, exactly where a voice belonged, and when it settled onto its mounts the whole hull gave a soft approving shudder that Sefa felt from keel to rail.

Bo looked at her. "Do you want to?" they asked, and tapped the bell's rim. *First strike.*

Sefa shook her head and pointed at Bo. Then at Odile, who shook hers. Then at Tam, who spread his hands — *you're the rigger, it's your ceremony.*

So Sefa took up the striker, a short wooden mallet they'd carved from a piece of the original *Vesperine* decking that Odile had saved from the wreck, and she stood before the bell with the whole gray harbor watching — the yard dogs, the gulls, the far-off cranes of the Meridian fleet sitting idle and disgraced across the water — and she struck it once.

The sound went through her boots, through the deck, through her chest, into the place where her hearing used to live. It was the deepest thing she had ever felt. Not a noise but a fact: *I am here, and you are here, and the names are carried.*

Bo's eyes were wet. So, she noticed, were Tam's, though he blamed the wind. Odile just stood with her hand flat against the mast, feeling it too, and for once her face was entirely unguarded, and Sefa thought: *there she is. There's the woman under all that moving.*

When the ring faded, Odile spoke. "We should decide," she said. "What happens now. Whether we—" She stopped. Started again, in the blunt way she handled anything that scared her. "Salvage crews disband. That's what salvage crews do. Job ends, crew scatters, everyone pretends it wasn't family. I've done it a dozen times. I'm—" her jaw tightened, "—I'm good at it. So if any of you want to walk, walk now, and I'll shake your hand and mean it kindly."

Sefa looked around the deck. Tam was already shaking his head. Bo had crossed their arms in the universal posture of a teenager refusing an instruction.

"I have a counterproposal," Sefa said.

Every face turned to her. She realized, distantly, that she was about to say more words aloud in one breath than she typically spent in a week, and found that she didn't mind.

"We keep the boat. We keep the bell. We take the names to the shore towns — all of them, upriver and down, every place the descendants landed after the flooding. Bo reads. Tam testifies. Odile dives when diving's needed and stays ashore when it isn't." She looked at Odile directly. "And we choose a home port. One port. A place we come back to. Because a crew that only runs isn't a crew. It's just weather moving together."

Silence. The gulls wheeled. Somewhere across the harbor, the idle cranes of the Meridian fleet creaked in the wind like old men grumbling.

"Which port?" Tam asked at last, gently, because he understood what the question cost her.

Sefa had known the answer for six days. She'd known it the morning she'd woken in the boatyard lean-to and heard — felt — the harbor bells of Grayharbor marking the hour, and had lain there realizing that for the first time in her life she knew where she was before she opened her eyes, and that she liked it.

"This one," she said. "Grayharbor. It's ugly, it's expensive, the harbor master hates us, and the chowder is a crime." She let herself smile. "It's home."

Bo whooped. Tam laughed. And Odile — Odile looked at her for a long moment with an expression Sefa had no translation for, and then said, quietly, "Home," as if testing whether the word would hold her weight, and Sefa felt it hold.

They shook on it, all four, hands stacked over the bell's shadow on the deck planks, and above them the *Vesperine*'s bronze swung faintly in the breeze and did not ring, but seemed, Sefa thought, to be listening.

* * *

They launched at dawn two days later, on a rising tide, with the bell polished and the holds provisioned and the first shore town — Reedfall, where the census listed forty-one names — marked on Tam's chart in fresh ink.

Sefa took the helm. The river took them. Behind them Grayharbor's bells rang the hour, ragged and municipal and beloved, and ahead the water lay open all the way to the sea.

Halfway down the reach, Bo appeared at her elbow and tapped her arm. When she turned, they signed clumsily, earnestly, a sentence they had clearly rehearsed: *Good work today.* Then, grinning: *Every day, probably. Forever.*

Sefa laughed — out loud, unguarded, a sound she barely recognized as her own — and signed back the truest thing she knew:

*Home,* she said. *We're going home.*

And the bell rode steady amidships, carrying the names, letting the water keep the rest.
