## 22. Where the Water Stops Moving

The morning of the last dive, Odile woke before the gulls and lay still in her bunk, listening to the river breathe against the hull.

That was the thing she had never learned to do — lie still. Twenty-two years of moving, of filling every hour with rope and brass and the cold arithmetic of depth, because stillness was where Wren lived, and Wren was a wound she could not touch and could not leave alone. But this morning she lay in the gray half-light of the *Leeward Bell* — Tam had painted the name on the transom himself, in letters that wobbled because his hands were better with charts than brushes — and she let the stillness come, and it did not kill her.

It was just quiet. The river against the hull. Sefa's kettle ticking on the galley stove, three decks down, because Sefa was always first up and always pretended not to be. Bo's hammock swinging empty in the forehold; Bo had gone ashore the night before to help set the benches for the memorial, and had come back smelling of cut pine and river mud, and had slept like something finally anchored.

Odile rose. She did not dress for salvage.

The suit she laid out on the bunk was her old one, patched at the knee where the Vesperine's rail had torn it on the third tide, but the weight belt she left coiled in its bin. No knife. No lift bags. No slate. Tam, who was up in the wheelhouse pretending to study a chart of water they would never sail again, watched her through the glass and asked nothing. That was the gift of this crew, the one she still marveled at, the one she expected to wake from: they knew when a thing was not for talking.

She carried only the letter.

It had traveled with her through everything — the six tides, the standoff, the long slow months of testimony and hearings and men in gray uniforms saying *the record is under review* while the census went out into the world like seeds on wind. Wren's handwriting on navy paper, folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. *If you're reading this, little fish, then you went looking, which means you never could leave a thing alone.* She knew it by heart now. She had read it in the vault room by lamplight while Bo wept over their own family's name, had read it the night Varn's fleet stood down, had read it aloud once, only once, on the shore at the memorial, her voice cracking on the last line the way a rope cracks before it parts.

Today she would read it one last time. Down there. Where Wren had meant it to rest.

* * *

The Vesperine lay as they had left her, and the river had already begun the work of taking her back.

Odile went down the shot line hand over hand, slow, no urgency in her body at all — a strangeness she kept noticing, the way you notice a missing tooth. The water at this depth was the color of old tea, of memory, and her lamp swung a soft cone through it, and the wreck rose up to meet her the way it always did: vast and patient, a cathedral with its roof fallen in.

She had first seen this hull at dawn, years of tides ago, cataloging what the navy would not discuss, pocketing a brass fitting stamped with a seal she wasn't supposed to know. She had been restless then. She could feel that old restlessness now like a coat that no longer fit — the itch to be moving, cataloging, counting, anything but stopping. She let it pass through her like the cold did, and kept descending.

The census vault stood open. It would stand open forever now; the navy had wanted it sealed again and the courts had said no, and the names had come up into the light, all four thousand and some, read aloud in shifts by descendants who had traveled from three coasts to hear them. The vault room was scoured, emptied, its shelves bare — but the river was already draping it in silt the color of pale wool, softening the edges of everything, and in the lamplight it looked less like a wound and more like a room. A room where something had been kept safe.

Odile settled to the deck beside it, knees in the silt, and unfolded the letter one final time.

The paper was waterproofed — Wren had been a diver's sister, she had known — but the ink had faded to the brown of dried kelp. Odile read it by lamplight anyway, though she did not need to. *They will tell you I drowned in the mine. I didn't. I drowned here, later, asking questions, and I have never been sorry.* And the last line, the one that had cracked her voice on the shore: *Stop diving for me, little fish. Dive for yourself. And then — and I mean this — come up.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it along its soft old creases, and she slid it into the empty vault, into the place where four thousand names had waited out their long dark, and she pressed it flat with her palm against the shelf as if setting a stone on a grave.

"There," she said. The word came out in a silver blur of bubbles and rose away from her, and she watched it go, and she did not say anything else, because there was nothing else. Wren was not down here. Wren had never been down here. Wren had been in the stillness all along, in every hour Odile had refused to sit in, and now that Odile could sit in it, Wren was there too — not as a wound, but as a sister, the way you carry the living.

She stayed a while longer. Not salvage-long, not working-long. Just a while. She looked at the vault, and at the silt settling like snow, and at the far doorway where the hold's black opened into black, and she felt the wreck the way you feel an old house you grew up in: familiar, finished, no longer yours.

Then Odile Dravenmoor took one last look at the Vesperine, turned her back on it for the first time in her life, and went up the line toward the light.

* * *

She broke the surface into a morning so clear it seemed the sky had been rinsed overnight. The *Leeward Bell* rode at her mooring a hundred yards off, and on her deck three figures were waiting, pretending not to wait — Tam with his chart rolled up under his arm as a prop, Sefa leaning on the rail with her arms crossed and her eyes doing that thing they did, reading Odile's wake the way she read water, by feel. Bo was hanging halfway over the side, seventeen going on thirty, hair tied back with a scrap of signal flag.

Odile swam the distance in. She did not hurry.

Hands came down for her — Bo's first, gripping her forearm with the fierce certainty of someone who had once tried to trade their family away out of fear and had learned, at the bottom of a collapsing hold, what family actually did. Sefa's hand next, steady as a cleat. Tam hovering, useless and earnest, with a blanket he'd clearly warmed by the stove. She came over the rail into the middle of them and stood dripping on her own deck, and nobody said anything, because there was a kettle on and that said everything.

They were not disbanding. That had been decided the way everything on the *Leeward Bell* was decided — sideways, over food, without anyone calling it a decision. The navy wanted Tam back and Tam had said, with a formality that had made Sefa laugh her silent laugh, that he would consult his crew. Bo had school now, real school, funded by the settlement and taught half the time by the Calvethi descendants who had adopted the whole lot of them with the brisk, unsentimental ferocity of people who knew what it was to be given back to themselves. And Sefa — Sefa had chosen a home port. Odile had watched her do it, watched her stand on the quay at Leeward Harbor and look at the moorings for a long time, and then point, once, at the berth beside the fish market, and that was that. Sefa, who had never stayed anywhere, had a berth. Had a kettle. Had them.

"Port side berth's open," Sefa signed, nodding at Odile's dripping suit. *Yours. Still. However long you want it.*

"Indefinitely," Odile said, and heard how the word sat in her mouth without tasting like a cage.

They ate on deck because the morning was too fine for the galley — bread and smoked fish and the honey Bo had traded half their coffee for ashore, and Tam read aloud from the morning sheet, from the story that had run under a headline three inches tall, which for a navy story was a shout: *CENSUS RETURNS COMPLETE. INQUIRY TO HEAR SURVIVING CHARTS.* Tam's testimony was scheduled for the autumn. He was terrified. He was also, Odile could see, quietly alight, a disgraced cartographer about to lay his old charts on the table of record and say *here, here, and here — this is where they lied.* She put her hand on his shoulder on her way below and he went pink and pretended to study his bread.

Afternoon came, and with it the tide, and with the tide the habit came too — the old habit, the itch, the reach for the weight belt. Odile felt her body move toward the gear bin the way a river moves toward the sea, without thought, without choice.

She stopped.

She stood in the middle of the deck with the sun on her shoulders and the belt coiled in the bin at her feet, and she looked at it — twenty pounds of lead, the thing that had made her sink, the thing she had strapped on every morning of her adult life so she could go down and down and down, looking for a sister, looking for a name, looking for anything that would let her stop looking. The fleet was broken. The census was raised. Wren's letter was home in the vault, and Wren was home in the stillness, and there was nothing down there.

There was nothing down there, and up here there was everything: a kettle, a berth, a boy arguing with a cartographer about the correct way to fold a chart, a deaf woman laughing at them both without a sound.

Odile closed the bin. She latched it. She did not know if it was forever — she was honest enough with herself to know she couldn't promise that, that the water would call her again someday, maybe for work, maybe for nothing, maybe just to visit an old wreck the way you visit a grave with flowers instead of grief. But today the belt stayed in the bin, and today that was enough, and she had learned — at forty-one years old, on a salvaged ship with a stolen name and a bell that had once belonged to the drowned — that *enough* was a thing you could choose, one morning at a time, the way you chose a home port.

She went and sat on the rail with her back to the water.

That was the whole of it, and it took her breath, how small the whole of it was: she sat with her back to the river, facing her crew, her strange ragged family, the sun warm on the deck boards, and the water moved behind her without her watching it, and the world did not end, and Wren did not vanish, and nothing was lost. The current went on doing what currents do, carrying silt and light and the slow weather of seasons past a hull that was, for now, a home. Somewhere downstream the Vesperine settled a little deeper into her keeping, her letter safe in the vault, her names safe in the world. Somewhere upstream the inquiry waited with its gray rooms and its gray men, and Tam's charts, and the truth, which turned out to be heavier than any weight belt and easier, in the end, to carry.

Bo leaned against her, bony shoulder to bony shoulder, and pointed at the horizon where the river widened toward the sea.

"Race you to the fish market," Bo said. "Sefa's buying."

"She is not," Sefa signed indignantly, already reaching for her coat.

Odile laughed — a real one, from the bottom, from all the way up — and pushed off the rail, and went with them, up the quay, into the noise and the light, leaving the water behind her to do the only thing water has ever known how to do.

It kept moving. She didn't.
