## 1. Dead Water at Dawn

The river gave nothing away before sunrise. That was the first thing my father ever taught me, and the last thing I still believed without question: a river at dawn is a liar with a beautiful face, all glass and gold, hiding its snags and its grudges beneath.

I stood at the wheel of the *Marta B.* — forty feet of riveted stubbornness that had hauled fuel drums, mail sacks, livestock, and the occasional fugitive up and down this water for longer than I'd been alive — and watched the mist burn off the shallows like a curtain being drawn by someone who didn't want to be seen doing it.

"Rosalind," I said to the empty pilothouse, just to hear it. Not my name. My name was a coin I kept in a locked box under the floorboards, along with my father's compass and a photograph gone soft at the edges from handling. Rosalind Villalobos belonged to a girl who drowned when she was twelve, in every way that mattered. The woman at the wheel was plain Marta Reyes, licensed, bonded, forgettable. Forgettable was a profession. I was very good at it.

The engine ticked over beneath my feet, that familiar arrhythmia on the port cylinder that no mechanic in three hundred miles could cure and which I had come to think of as the boat's heartbeat. A boat with a perfect engine is either new or lying. The *Marta B.* did neither.

Downriver runs were the easy ones. Empty drums clanking in the hold where full ones had been, the current doing half my work for me, the whole day stretched out like a hammock. I had six drums of diesel to deliver to the depot at Puerto Cobre, an invoice to collect on, and a debt — the boat's mortgage, held by the Banco Provincial with the warmth of a fish hook — that I paid down one run at a time, the way you eat an elephant: reluctantly, and in small pieces.

At the bend below Caño Seco I passed the morning fishing boats going out, and old Tomás raised his hat to me the way he always did, two fingers, no wave. Tomás had fished this stretch for fifty years and had never once asked me a personal question, which is why he was my favorite person on the river. We understood each other. The river keeps what you tell it; better not to tell it anything.

My father's compass sat in its bracket by the wheel, brass gone green at the seams, the needle steady as a conscience. He'd bought it secondhand the year I was born and engraved the case himself, badly, with a tool meant for something else: *EL RÍO SABE* — the river knows. As a child I'd thought it was romantic. As an adult I understood it as a warning. The river knows where you've been, what you've sunk, whom you've lost. It knows the town of San Esteban is still down there, thirty feet under the flood pool behind the dam, church bell and schoolhouse and all, and it has never once mentioned it out loud.

Neither had I. Twenty-two years of silence, practiced daily until it fit me like skin.

* * *

Puerto Cobre smelled of diesel and overripe mangoes, as usual, and the depot manager, Fierro, was waiting at the dock with his clipboard held against his chest like a breastplate. Fierro was a small man made entirely of rules, and I liked him more than I admitted.

"Marta." He squinted at my cargo. "Six drums. You're light."

"I'm always light. You're always surprised."

"The invoice—" he began, and then stopped, because a man had stepped out of the depot office behind him, and Fierro went quiet the way a dog goes quiet when a stranger walks past the gate.

I knew government men the way sailors know weather. This one wore his rank the way some men wear cheap suits — correctly, but without conviction. Gray shirt, gray trousers, a leather folder under one arm, and shoes too clean for the river. He was perhaps fifty, built like a man who had once been athletic and now merely disciplined, and he looked at the *Marta B.* the way an auditor looks at a ledger: itemizing.

"Captain Reyes?" His voice was pleasant. Pleasant voices on this river were worth more than any other kind of lie.

"That's me. You need fuel?"

"I need a captain." He produced papers from the folder with the smoothness of long practice. "Corrigan. Bureau of Water Resources." He said the bureau's name the way other men say their own names — as though it were larger than he was and he stood in its shadow comfortably. "There's a survey expedition upstream. Scientific. They've missed two scheduled check-ins."

"So send a radio man."

"We sent a radio man." A pause, measured out like medicine. "He came back. He says the upper river is fine. He also resigned the same afternoon and took the train south, and we can't find him. So now I'm hiring a boat."

"And mine's available because?"

"Because your reputation says you'll go anywhere for money and ask nothing after." He smiled. It was a good smile, maintained regularly, like the shoes. "Triple rate. Half now."

Triple rate. I thought about the mortgage, and the port cylinder, and the new propeller shaft I'd been promising the *Marta B.* since Christmas. I thought about how rivers punish curiosity — not out of malice, but out of architecture; curiosity is a channel with no depth soundings, and the river does not forgive drafts it hasn't measured. I had spent twenty-two years being a woman who took the measured channels.

But triple rate was triple rate, and dead expeditions paid the same as live ones if the paperwork was right.

"What's the survey studying?" I asked. "Silt?"

"Sedimentation. Erosion patterns." He handed me the advance in banded notes without counting them, which told me either honesty or urgency, and with government men it is never honesty. "You'd resupply them and confirm status. Nothing more. You're not to involve yourself in their work."

"Captain Reyes doesn't involve herself in anyone's work. She moves drums."

"Then we should get along well." He watched me sign the chit — *M. REYES*, the letters small and neat, a signature worn smooth as river stone from twenty years of signing a name that wasn't mine — and something in his watching made the hair on my arms lift, though I couldn't have said why. Perhaps it was only that he counted things for a living, and I was a thing that did not want counting.

"One condition," I said. "If they're dead, I bring back what's theirs. Personal effects. That's not involving myself. That's decency."

For just a moment — a flicker, gone as fast as a fish turning — something moved behind Corrigan's pleasant face. Not grief. Calculation, maybe, or recognition. "Of course," he said. "Decency."

Fierro loaded my drums in silence, and when Corrigan walked off toward the agency launch moored at the far dock, the depot manager leaned close and spoke for the first time in an hour.

"That one," he said quietly, "asked about you before he asked about the job. How long you'd been running the river. Whether you worked alone." He straightened his clipboard. "Government men don't hire boats, Marta. They hire alibis."

I laughed, because Fierro expected it, and because laughing is cheaper than thinking sometimes. But the laugh sat wrong in my chest all the way back to the boat, and while I cast off I found myself looking at my father's compass, needle steady, pointing north upriver — pointing home, if a drowned town can be called home — and thinking that the river knows, and that sooner or later everything it knows comes downstream.

I turned the *Marta B.*'s bow upriver, into the current, into the narrowing green distance where an expedition had gone silent, and told myself it was only the money.

The river heard me say it. Rivers punish liars too.
