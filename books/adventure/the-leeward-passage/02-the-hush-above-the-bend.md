## 2. The Hush Above the Bend

The river above the bend had a name the maps didn't bother with, and like most unnamed things it was honest about what it wanted: to be left alone.

Rosalind Villalobos — or rather, the woman the fuel ledgers called Rosa Marsh, which was close enough to true to pass a handshake — stood at the wheel of the *Perdiz* and let the current argue with her. The boat was forty years old, held together by paint, prayer, and an engine she'd rebuilt twice with parts that had started life as other machines. She talked to it the way some people talked to horses. It responded better than most people did.

"Easy," she murmured as the channel narrowed and swung east. "I know. I know."

The banks closed in like a conversation getting quieter. Bamboo gave way to stands of alder, alder to dark walls of ceiba whose roots gripped the clay the way old men grip a bar at closing time. The water went from brown to tea-dark, then to something like black glass, and her wake — the only rude thing for miles — unrolled behind her and vanished.

She had been running this stretch since she was nineteen, first as deckhand, then as pilot, then, when old Captain Ferro's heart quit him mid-river and she'd brought his boat and his body home in the same day, as captain. Eleven years under a name that wasn't hers. Long enough that *Rosa* answered faster than the other one did. That was the trick of borrowed things: wear them daily and they fit better than your own skin.

Her father's compass sat on the shelf beside the wheel, brass-cased, its glass clouded with a hairline crack from a night she didn't think about. It pointed north the way her father had pointed north — stubbornly, and slightly wrong, but wrong in a consistent direction, which he'd claimed was all any honest instrument could promise. *A compass doesn't tell you where you are,* he used to say, tapping the case. *It tells you which lie you've chosen.* She kept it because arguing with a dead man was the only argument she ever won cleanly.

The expedition she was hunting had gone silent eleven days ago. A government survey, four people, upriver past the last fuel depot, past the last anything. Corrigan — the man who'd hired her at triple rate without blinking, which was itself a reason to blink — wanted a status report. *Check on them. Bring back word.* He'd said it the way a man says *check on them* when what he means is something with more edges.

Rivers punish curiosity. Her father had taught her that too, though not on purpose; he'd been a curious man about water, about gates and gauges and pressure heads, and look where it had gotten everyone.

She pushed the thought down where she kept it, under the floorboards of herself, weighted like ballast.

* * *

By midmorning she began reading the river the way other women read letters.

There was a sandbar at the mouth of the Quebrada Honda that hadn't been there in June — new, pale, greedy, already colonized by a single optimistic heron. She noted it, adjusted her line, and made a mental mark: the dry season was biting early this year. Water this low this soon meant the upper reaches would be mean by month's end. Whatever Corrigan was paying, it wasn't enough for a low-water run in October.

An hour on, she found the first camp.

Or what was left of one. A flat gravel shelf on the outside bend, the kind surveyors loved because you could land a skiff and pitch tents without negotiating with the jungle. Someone had been here. Someone had left in a hurry or been made to. A tent pole leaned against a tree like a drunk against a lamppost. A crate marked with the agency's stencil — the same eagle-and-waves stamp that was on her contract of hire — lay open and empty, its lid pried, not unlatched. Scattered around it: a boot, one boot, size large, laces still tied, which was somehow worse than if it had been torn. A tin cup, dented. And cigarette ends, many, stamped out with a heel twist she recognized because a certain kind of man did it everywhere, in every country, the same little violent signature.

Not the expedition's leavings. Somebody else's. Somebody who'd found this camp and moved through it.

Rosalind stood on the gravel a long moment, hands in her pockets, and let the place talk. Rivers carried news downstream — silt, branches, dead fish, rumors — but silence traveled upstream, and she could feel it coming off the bank like cold off ice.

"Well," she said aloud, to the heron, to the boot, to nobody. "That's not ominous at all."

The heron flew off, offended.

* * *

She ate lunch underway, one hand on the wheel, bread and salt fish and a lime she'd been saving, and the eating settled her nerves the way food does when the alternative is thinking. The *Perdiz* chugged. The jungle watched. Somewhere overhead a hawk rode a thermal, going nowhere expensively.

And because the river was quiet and her hands were busy and there was nothing between her and the next bend but water, memory came up the way floodwater comes — not all at once, but finding every low place.

She was twelve. San Esteban was a town of four hundred souls and two churches and a market square where her mother sold cloth and argued cheerfully about prices, winning more than losing. Her father worked the dam — the big government dam twelve miles upstream, concrete and proud, built to tame the river that had built the town. He came home smelling of cement dust and engine oil, and on Sundays he took her walking along the levee and taught her the river's moods the way other fathers taught psalms.

*"Water always tells you before it takes,"* he said. *"Listen long enough and it confesses."*

The rain fell for nine days that October. On the ninth night the sirens — no, there were no sirens; that was the thing she could never make the memory hold still about. There should have been sirens. There was a bell, one bell, rung by the priest, and by the time it rang the water was already in the streets, already brown and fast and full of the river's whole opinion of them.

She remembered her mother's hands on her shoulders, steering her up the stairs of the church, and then not remembering, because after that there was roofline and current and the sky doing something enormous overhead, and her father's voice somewhere below calling a name — hers, or her mother's, or God's, the acoustics of that night never sorted themselves out.

Four hundred souls. The number they printed afterward was three hundred and eleven recovered, and the rest written down as *lost to the flood*, which is a phrase that sounds administrative until it's your family inside it.

They said the dam had failed. They said it gently, in official language, with a commission and a report and a conclusion so thick with engineering terms it might as well have been a quilt thrown over the town's face. *Structural inadequacy compounded by unprecedented precipitation.* Her father's name appeared once, in an appendix, noted as deceased, and she had stared at that line for hours as a girl, feeling that it was wrong, that names in appendices don't drown towns, that somebody somewhere had turned a key or opened a book or signed a paper, and that the paper was still warm somewhere.

Then she'd grown up, and grown careful, and become Rosa Marsh, and put the warm paper in the same underwater room where she kept everything else.

The lime was gone. She noticed she'd eaten it rind and all.

* * *

The cache was two miles past the second bend, tucked in a cutbank the coordinates had promised, and it told its story plainly, the way looted things do.

Three steel drums of diesel: two empty, one punctured, weeping a rust-colored stain into the mud. Crates of tinned food: opened, sorted through, taken selectively — the meat gone, the fruit left, which told her the raiders had either been hungry or professional, possibly both. The expedition's spare radio mast was not fallen. Fallen masts break at the base, splinter, lie in the direction of their dying. This one had been snapped at the midpoint, clean as a wishbone, and the halves laid side by side with a neatness that was worse than wreckage. Deliberate. Methodical. *We know how to reach you, and we have decided you shouldn't.*

Beside it, half-buried in mud, a canvas satchel. Rosalind crouched, checked the ground — boot prints, several sets, heading upriver, none returning — and opened it.

Inside: a field journal, water-spotted, and wedged beneath the flap a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around something flat and heavy. She unwrapped it just far enough to see the corner of a photographic plate, glass-edged, and a strip of paper with handwriting cramped and urgent:

*If found — do not take to authorities. A.O.*

Dr. Amara Osei. The expedition lead. The woman whose file Corrigan had shown her with a photograph and a list of credentials and a tone suggesting Osei was a formality, a line item, a problem already solved.

Rosalind sat back on her heels in the mud of the cutbank and looked upriver, where the water narrowed into shadow and the hush got thicker, and she understood — not guessed, *understood*, the way you understand a current by the pull on your hull — that the silence ahead was manufactured. That four people had walked into the upper river carrying something worth snapping radio masts over, and that whatever they'd found, it was the kind of finding that got erased. She knew erasure. She'd been raised by it. Erasure had drowned her town and filed the report and handed her a borrowed name without ever once meeting her eyes.

The sensible move was to turn the *Perdiz* around, go back downriver, and tell Corrigan she'd found an empty camp and a broken mast and no survivors, sorry for the trouble, keep the advance. Sensible had kept her alive for twenty-three years. Sensible was the whole architecture of Rosa Marsh.

But her father's compass sat in the wheelhouse pointing its consistent wrongness toward north, and her hands, she noticed, had already begun coiling the mooring line.

"Damn it, Papá," she said quietly. "You and your listening."

She fired the engine. The *Perdiz* grumbled awake, and Rosalind Marsh — who was not, and had never quite managed to be, Rosa Marsh — turned her bow upriver into the hush, toward whatever the surveyors had kept, moving against the current and every instinct she owned, at a speed just slow enough to hear the water confess.
