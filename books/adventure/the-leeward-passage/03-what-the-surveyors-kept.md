## 3. What the Surveyors Kept

The letter was nailed to the mission door with a rusted surveying pin, which told me two things before I read a word: the writer had no nails, and she expected visitors she couldn't trust.

*M/v* whatever-your-name-is, *it said. If you are reading this, you are not the men in the gray launch, because the men in the gray launch do not read. They shoot at paper.*

I liked her already, whoever she was. I liked her considerably less when I read the next line.

*Come in alone. Leave your crew aboard. If you are armed, leave the gun in the boat, and if you cannot bear to do that, at least leave the safety on, because our nerves are gone and we will shoot first and apologize in the morning.*

The mission ruin crouched on the bluff above the east bank, all that remained of some parish's ambition a century gone — arched doorways opening onto nothing, a bell tower missing its bell, the whole thing strangled in fig vine. Somebody had strung fishing line with tin cans across the approach, which would have stopped exactly no one, but it was the thought that counted.

"Miss Osei?" I called up the path. "I'm the resupply. Alone. Unarmed, mostly."

A pause. Then a woman's voice, hoarse as a crow: "Mostly?"

"I kept the boat hook."

Another pause, longer. "That's fair. Come up slow. And mind the cans."

* * *

There were three of them, and they looked like the last three candles on a birthday cake somebody had forgotten about.

Dr. Amara Osei was tall and thin as a survey stake, with gray at her temples and the particular stillness of a person who has spent eleven days listening for footsteps. The junior — a boy of maybe twenty-two, name of Ferro, who flinched when the wind moved the vines — sat against the wall hugging a rifle he clearly did not know how to love. And on a cot of saplings and tarp lay the wounded one, a broad-shouldered man whose leg had been bound with a splint made from a tripod. His name, Osei said, was Halloran, and his leg, she said, was a problem with a deadline.

"You're late," she said.

"River's low. I had to walk the boat through the second bend." I set down the crates. "Your radio mast was snapped."

"Snapped," Osei repeated. "Yes. That's the word for it."

"Fallen masts break at the base. Yours broke a meter up, and the break was white wood, not gray." I looked at her. "Somebody climbed it."

"Somebody climbed it," she agreed. "And then somebody looted our cache at the confluence, and took the fuel, and the spare radio, and — thoughtfully — every can of food, but left the theodolite. Because they weren't thieves, Captain. They were tidiers. They were removing evidence."

Ferro made a small sound. Halloran, from his cot, said, "The lady's got eyes," in a voice like gravel in a bucket, and then coughed for a long time, and I decided his deadline was closer than Osei wanted to admit.

"I was hired by a government man," I said. "Corrigan. Triple rate to check on you."

The effect was immediate. Ferro went gray as river clay. Osei didn't move at all, which was worse. Halloran stopped coughing.

"And did he tell you," Osei said carefully, "what we were surveying?"

"Flood mapping. Channel depths above the bend."

"That's what we told him, too." She studied me the way I'd study a sandbar at low water — looking for what was underneath. "You came alone. You left the gun. You read my letter and didn't burn it. So I'm going to do something stupid, Captain, because I've run out of clever. I'm going to show you why three people are hiding in a ruin eating river mussels."

* * *

The maps were under the altar.

There was a certain poetry in that, I suppose — the mission had been stripped of everything holy decades ago, and now the holiest thing in it was a steel case of sonar plates and a notebook gone soft with damp. Osei knelt and drew them out and laid them across the altar slab, and Ferro held a lantern, and Halloran propped himself up on one elbow because he was the kind of man who would not miss this lying down.

"Standard bathymetric survey," Osei said. "That's what the contract said. But the funding came through a discretionary line, and the discretionary line came from Corrigan's agency, and the area they wanted mapped —" her finger traced a stretch of river I knew the way you know the back of your own hand — "was not the channel. It was the old town site."

I did not say anything. My father taught me that. *When the water's deep, girl, keep your mouth shut and your lead line down.*

"San Esteban," Osei said. "Drowned in the flood of '61. Two hundred and six people, if you believe the official count, and the official count is wrong, because the parish registers went under with the church and nobody ever dove for them." She turned a plate toward the lantern light. "Here's what the government told everyone: the rains came, the river came up, the dam upstream failed, God's will, terrible tragedy, rebuild downriver. Here's what the sonar says."

The image swam up out of the plate, ghostly and gray. A riverbed. And in the riverbed, unmistakable, the grid of streets. Foundations in rows. A church with its tower still standing, leaning like a drunk against the current. And upstream of the town, upstream of everything —

"That," Osei said, "is a breach pattern. You see how the scour fans out? That's not a dam that crumbled. That's not a dam that overtopped and washed out slow. That is a dam that was *blown*, Captain. Charges set low on the sluice side, in three places, and I will show you my math if you want it, but the shape of that hole doesn't lie. Concrete doesn't fail like that on its own. Concrete fails like that when somebody helps it."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the vines a bird complained and shut up.

"In '61," I said, and my voice came out flat as a chart table, "there was a land dispute. The valley above San Esteban. The government wanted it for —"

"Resettlement scheme. Grazing leases. It was never fully documented, because documentation has a way of drowning." Osei sat back on her heels. "The town was in the way of the story they wanted to tell. So they drowned the town, and the story, and the people, all in one night, and called it weather."

Ferro was staring at me. "Captain? You've gone — you've gone a funny color."

"I'm fine," I said, which was a lie, and I was apparently in a season of telling them.

Because here is the thing I had not said out loud in twenty-six years: I was twelve years old in San Esteban. I was twelve years old, and my father was the dam's assistant engineer, and my mother put me on the supply boat that evening to ride down to my aunt's landing because I'd had a fever and the river air was supposed to be good for me, and I watched from the water as the lamps of my town went out one by one, not like lights being turned off but like lights being *put under*. My father had argued with the chief engineer for a week about the sluice gates. My mother wrote him a letter about it. I know because I found the draft in her handwriting, years later, in a Bible that floated.

I had spent my whole adult life believing the river did that. Believing the river, which I loved, which fed me, which carried my living on its back — believing it had taken my family in a fit of temper the way rivers do.

And here was a stranger from upriver, kneeling at a dead altar, telling me the river had been *used*.

"Captain." Osei's voice was gentle now, and I hated that, because gentle voices are for people who are falling apart, and I had a boat to run. "You knew the town."

"I knew the town," I said.

"Then you should know the rest. There's more than maps." She drew a folded paper from the notebook — not sonar, but handwriting, dense and furious. "A letter. My predecessor on this survey, a man named Ibáñez, found the dam maintenance logs in a provincial archive. The sluice gates were ordered closed the day before the flood. Closed, and locked, and the keys signed out by the chief engineer. The town's evacuation order was never issued because the order would have required a reason, and the reason was the one thing they couldn't put on paper." She held it out. "Ibáñez was going to take it to a journalist in the delta. He didn't get the chance. The fourth member of our team — the men in the gray launch came for the plates, and he —"

She stopped. Halloran finished for her, quiet. "He wouldn't hand them over. So they took the mast down on him." A pause. "He was a good swimmer. It didn't matter. The current up there is a liar this time of year."

I took the letter. I did not read it then. I held it, and it weighed nothing, and it weighed everything, the way paper does.

* * *

We sat afterward on the mission steps, the four of us and the dark coming down, and Osei brewed something on a spirit stove that claimed to be coffee and I was too polite to dispute.

"What happens now," I said, "is that I take you downriver."

"They'll be watching the main channel."

"They'll be watching the channel they can see. There's water down this river that isn't on any chart made in the last thirty years, and I grew up on it." I turned my cup. "But understand what you're asking. If I carry your plates, I'm not a resupply captain anymore. I'm a courier for the thing that got Ibáñez killed."

"Yes," Osei said simply. "I know. Which is why I'll not lie to you about the odds." She looked out at the river, silver-black below the bluff. "Corrigan funded us because he wanted to know how much was findable. When we found all of it, he wanted it buried with the town. He's spent his whole career keeping that flood a weather event, Captain. Men like that don't stop at a snapped mast."

"I know men like that."

"Then you know they also have a habit," she said, "of knowing things about the people who cross them."

I felt that one settle in my chest like cold water finding a seam. Because Corrigan had looked at me across his desk with those pale, patient eyes, and he had hired me at triple rate, and triple rate is not a wage, it is a *price* — and I had been so busy counting the debt off my boat's back that I hadn't asked myself the obvious question. Of all the captains on this river, all the drunk ones and desperate ones and cheap ones —

Why me?

Down at the landing, my boat rode at her lines in the dark, and the current whispered against her hull the way it had whispered against the levee the night the lamps went out, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like the river itself turning me by the shoulders, that I had not been hired despite who I was.

I had been hired *because* of it.

"Dr. Osei," I said. "How fast can Halloran travel?"
