## 4. Corrigan's Ledger

Amara Osei had kept a journal since she was nine years old, when her mother bought her a red notebook and told her, *A scientist who doesn't write things down is just a person with opinions.* The habit had survived three continents, two divorces, and one near-drowning in Lake Malawi. It had not, however, survived the last three weeks intact. The entries had grown shorter. Then they had stopped being entries at all, and become a ledger of a different kind.

*Day one of silence: 41 days. Men on the ridge again. Tomas says we should bury the plates. I say we should bury Tomas's optimism, it's taking up room.*

She read it by candlelight in the mission ruin, in the corner she had claimed as her office — a collapsed archway, a broken pew for a desk, and a view through the shattered window of the river, where a stranger's boat sat at anchor with its running lights doused. Rosalind Villalobos. Or whatever her name was. Osei had hired enough river pilots to know when a person's name fit them like a borrowed coat.

The woman had gone up the ridge at first light with the junior, Dev, to check a trap line of survey markers. Which meant Osei had an hour alone with her conscience, and with the tin box under the floorboard.

She lifted it out. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: the sonar plates. Eleven of them. Each one a portrait of the river bottom above the old dam, and beneath the silt, the drowned bones of San Esteban — streets, foundations, a church roof, a schoolhouse bell still hanging in its frame forty feet down, patient as a fossil.

And on the upstream face of the dam, rendered in the clean gray language of sonar: the fracture pattern of high explosives. Four charges, placed by someone who knew exactly where the concrete was thinnest.

She had shown Rosalind the maps yesterday. She had watched the woman's face do something complicated and then go very still, the way water goes still before it drops over a ledge. Osei had spent thirty years reading faces in the field, and she had thought: *she knows this town.*

Tonight she would show her the rest. Not the plates — the ledger. The human one.

* * *

It had begun, as most disasters do, with money and a polite letter.

*March:* The Ministry of Water and Territorial Survey had approved their grant in eleven days — a speed Osei had noted at the time with the naive pleasure of a woman who had spent her career begging for funding. Eleven days. She should have framed the approval letter as a warning.

*April:* They had come upriver on the *Cormorant*, four of them: Osei, her old friend and surveyor Tomás Reyes, a hydrologist named Priya Chandrasekaran, and Dev Okonkwo, twenty-four years old and so relentlessly cheerful that Osei had privately considered throwing him overboard by week two, and had come to love him like a nephew by week four. Their mandate: map the riverbed above the decommissioned Verruga Dam as part of a "historical hydrology initiative."

"Nobody funds historical hydrology," Tomás had said, laughing, as they loaded the gear. "Amara. Nobody. We are being paid real money to look at mud. Say nothing, take the check."

"I'm saying nothing," she'd said. "I'm just also looking at the mud."

*May:* The mud had looked back.

The first plates came up in the third week. Osei had been in the survey tent at midnight, alone with the processor, when the image resolved. She had made Dev come look, then Priya, then Tomás, and the four of them had stood around the screen in the small hours saying nothing at all, which is what scientists say when they have just seen a ghost with coordinates.

A town. Streets in a grid. A plaza. And the dam — blown from the upstream face, four charges, a textbook demolition.

"The flood of '61 was an accident," Dev had said. "It's in every textbook. Rainfall event, structural failure, tragedy."

"Rainfall events don't cut clean fracture cones into concrete," Priya had said. She was the quiet one, the one who folded her maps into perfect squares. "Somebody did this."

*Day 9:* *Tomas wants to notify the Ministry immediately. I want to know why the Ministry paid us to find this. Dev has stopped joking. That frightens me more than the dam.*

* * *

The Cormorant had come back on a Tuesday, unannounced, with Corrigan aboard.

Osei had met the type before — the government man whose smile arrived a half-second after his eyes finished assessing you, whose shirts were always pressed in a climate where nothing stayed pressed. He had come up the bank with two aides and a cooler of cold beer, the universal currency of men who wanted something.

"Dr. Osei. Corrigan, Territorial Water Authority." He'd handed her a beer like they were old friends. "How's the mud?"

"Fascinating," she'd said. "Silty. Deep. Full of surprises."

"I love surprises." He hadn't. She could tell by the way he'd said it.

He'd stayed two days. He'd looked at their equipment with the unhurried attention of a man inventorying it. He'd asked, casually, over dinner, whether the survey had produced "anything anomalous," and Osei had said the river was exactly as boring as rivers are, and he'd smiled his late-arriving smile and said, "Good. Boring rivers are safe rivers."

*Day 14:* *He knows. I don't know how he knows, but he knows. Priya wants to duplicate the plates and hide the originals. Tomas wants to go public the moment we're downriver. Dev says we should finish the survey — complete data is unimpeachable data. I keep thinking about my mother's red notebook. Write it down. A scientist who doesn't write things down is just a person with opinions.*

*Day 19:* *Corrigan's men came while we were on the ridge. Went through everything. Took nothing — because there was nothing to take. Priya had already moved the plates. She won't tell me where. I think I love her.*

*Day 23:* *They came back. This time Corrigan came with them. He had a document. He said the survey's findings were "a matter of strategic water security" and that the data belonged to the Authority. Tomás told him where he could file his document. I have never been so proud and so frightened in the same breath.*

The night it happened, Osei had been asleep when the voices woke her — Tomás's, rising, and Corrigan's, level as a spirit gauge. She'd come out of her tent to find them at the water's edge, Tomás holding the tin box against his chest like a man holding a child above a flood.

"Dr. Reyes," Corrigan had said, and his voice had done something terrible then — it had gone gentle. "Four charges on a dam that failed sixty years ago. You're a reasonable man. Think about what reasonable men do with unreasonable information."

"I think about the four hundred people in that town," Tomás had said.

"I think about them too," Corrigan had said. "That's the difference between us. I think about them, and you only think about the number."

He'd reached for the box. Tomás had turned, and the bank was mud and rain-slick rock, and Osei would replay the next two seconds for the rest of her life: Tomás's heel skidding, the box flying free, Tomás going down the bank into the high brown water with a sound she refused, even now, to describe in her journal.

They'd found him two days later, nine miles downriver. The box had washed into the roots of a fig tree a hundred yards from camp, dry inside, because Priya had sealed it like the careful, perfect-square woman she was. Corrigan's men had been gone by then. So had Corrigan's document, though not his men's footprints, which Dev had photographed, and Priya had measured, and Osei had written down.

*Day 26:* *Buried Tomas on the ridge above camp. Dev spoke. He said, "He was the funniest man on this river and the stubbornest, and the river didn't beat him, it just got lucky." I don't have anything to add. My mother would tell me to write it down anyway. So: they killed him. Not with their hands. With their reaching. I will carry this the way the river carries its dead — all the way down.*

* * *

Now she closed the journal and sat in the candle smoke, listening to the river and the small sounds of the mission ruin: Dev's snoring, arrhythmic, like a man arguing in his sleep; Priya's breathing, shallow and fast, the wound in her side keeping its own ledger of pain.

Rosalind had come back down the ridge at dusk with Dev, said little, eaten less, and gone to check her boat's lines in the dark. A careful woman. A woman who doused her lights at anchor. Osei had decided something on the ridge today, watching her read the water the way Tomás used to read a room — instantly, completely, and without sentiment.

She was going to trust her. Not because trust was safe. Nothing safe remained. But because eleven plates in a tin box under a floorboard were just evidence, and evidence could be confiscated, drowned, filed under *strategic water security*. What Corrigan feared — what a man who drowned towns feared — was a witness. A living one, with a boat and a river's worth of reasons.

Osei heard the deck of the ruin creak, soft as a confession, and turned. Rosalind stood in the archway with her father's brass compass in her hand, turning it over, not looking at it.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Osei said.

"Your junior snores like a landslide."

"He does. It's his one flaw. I've decided to find it charming, since the alternative is smothering him." She patted the pew beside her. Rosalind came, slowly, the way wild animals approach a baited trap, and sat.

Osei reached down, lifted the floorboard, and set the tin box between them.

"I'm going to tell you about the fourth member of my team," she said, "and then I'm going to ask you for something no one's ever asked you for, because I don't think anyone's ever trusted you enough to ask."

Rosalind's jaw tightened. "And if I say no?"

"Then you'll say no, and I'll have been wrong about you, and I'll bury these plates so deep even I can't find them." Osei met her eyes. "But I watched you read that channel at the bend today, Captain, and I don't think you've been wrong about a river yet. So. Tomás Reyes. Forty-nine years old. Funnier than he had any right to be. And the reason I need you to do the one thing you've spent your whole life avoiding."

The candle guttered. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Osei had been dreading for three days: an engine, running without lights, coming up against the current.

Rosalind's head turned toward it. Her hand closed over the compass.

"That," she said quietly, "is going to have to be a very short story."
