## 5. Running the Narrows

The patrol boat showed itself an hour after we cast off from the mission ruin, which told me two things: Corrigan had been watching the river longer than I'd liked to think, and he wanted us to see him.

"Gray hull, twin screws," I said, not turning around. "Don't stare at it. Staring is rude."

The junior one — Ferro, all elbows and terror — whipped his head around anyway, then back again so fast his cap nearly went in the water. Osei stood at my shoulder with her arms folded, wearing the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"How long before they catch us?" she asked.

"That depends on whether you'd like the honest answer or the captain's answer."

"The captain's."

"They never catch us." I eased the throttle forward a notch and felt the old girl answer through the deck plates, that deep contented shudder she made when asked for more than courtesy. "The honest answer is they're faster, they're newer, and their engines haven't been held together with prayer and baling wire since 1987. So we're going to go somewhere their charts say doesn't exist."

Izquierdo — the wounded surveyor — lay on the bench under the awning with his leg propped on a coil of line, gray as river clay. He'd been shot three days ago, badly enough that the field dressing had soaked through twice, and every hour of heat and jostling was a tax on a body already overdrawn. He opened his eyes when I said *charts*.

"You know a channel," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know a place where the river keeps a secret," I said. "There's a difference."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rivers: they are not roads. A road stays where you put it. A river is a living thing that moves its furniture every season, rearranges its rooms, forgets where it left the door. The maps are lies with good intentions. Every pilot worth her salt knows this, and every pilot who forgets it ends up as a story other pilots tell at night with the lamps low.

When I was fourteen — two years after San Esteban drowned, two years after I stopped being Rosalind Villalobos and became whoever the next town needed me to be — I spent a dry season running supplies for a man named Baptiste who paid in food and taught me in silence. One August the water dropped so low the main channel above the Narrows simply gave up, and Baptiste, instead of waiting like a sensible person, took us through a side passage between two bluffs that no chart acknowledged. He called it the Leeward Passage, because the wind couldn't reach it there, and he made me take the wheel while he sat smoking and saying nothing, which was his way of screaming.

I ran it four more times that season. Then the rains came, the water rose, and the passage closed itself like a held breath. I never found it again. But I remembered what it looked like from the water — the leaning fig tree on the north bluff, split by lightning, its trunk bent over the current like a man peering down; the pale seam of limestone where the cliff had sheared; the smell, absurdly, of wild mint growing thick on the bank where nothing else grew.

Rivers change. Bluffs don't. And mint, in my experience, is loyal.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the sky ahead had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong — yellow-green at the horizon, stacked dark above it, the whole western sky organizing itself into something with opinions. The air went heavy and sweet. My knees ached, which was more reliable than any barometer.

"Storm's coming early this year," Ferro said, hugging himself.

"Season's first," I agreed. "She'll be a rude one. Everyone's first storm is rude; it hasn't learned manners yet."

Osei didn't laugh. She was watching the gray boat astern, patient as a debt. "It's gaining."

"It's gaining because I'm letting it think it's gaining." I checked the compass — my father's compass, brass-cased, the glass scratched where a flood had tried to take it and failed — and then I did something I hadn't done in years. I turned off the channel the chart offered and pointed the bow at a wall of green that no sane person would call a route.

Ferro made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"Captain—" Osei began.

"Dr. Osei. Do you trust me?"

"No," she said honestly. "But I trust your boat, and I've watched you read water for two days like other people read letters. That will do."

"Good enough. Everybody hold onto something that loves them back."

* * *

The entrance, when we reached it, was exactly as memory had drawn it and nothing like hope had feared. The fig tree still leaned, older now, its lightning scar grown into a black seam down the heartwood. The limestone seam gleamed pale under the bruised light. And there — God bless it — the mint, gone feral, covering the bank in a green riot that filled the wheelhouse through the open window and smelled like every summer I'd ever survived.

"There you are," I said softly, to the passage or to the girl I'd been. Hard to say. Both of them had waited a long time.

The channel was narrow — twenty meters, maybe less where the sandbar shouldered in from the east — but deep, because the bluffs funneled the current and the current keeps what it digs. I took us in at half throttle, hands light on the wheel, reading the surface the way Baptiste taught me: the smooth glassy tongue where the deep water runs, the wrinkled boil where a bar hides just under, the V of a snag pointing downstream like an accusation. The banks slid past close enough to touch. Ferns brushed the rail. Somewhere overhead a heron complained about our manners.

Behind us, the gray hull shrank. Not because we were fast — we weren't — but because the patrol pilot had charts, and charts said this water was a hillside. Let him sit out there in the main channel with his twin screws and his certainty, waiting for us to reappear downstream. We wouldn't. Not tonight.

For forty minutes I steered by mint and memory, and the river carried us like it remembered me too.

* * *

We anchored at dusk in a cut behind a willow island, engines off, lines doubled, everything lashed down before the storm arrived — because it would arrive, and it would arrive angry. Izquierdo had gone quiet in a way that frightened me more than shouting would have. Quiet men with gunshot legs are spending something they can't earn back.

Osei changed his dressing by lamplight while Ferro held the lamp and tried very hard to be somewhere else. The wound had gone angry at the edges, red climbing past the bandage in thin streaks toward the knee.

"Fever's settling in," Osei said quietly, when she came to the wheelhouse. She rubbed her hands clean with spirit and stared out at the first fat drops hitting the river. "He needs a hospital, Captain. Real medicine. In two days, maybe three—"

"In two days we're in the delta," I said. "One day if the river behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

I watched the lightning walk along the western ridge, counting seconds out of habit. Four. Getting closer. "Then we make it behave anyway. Go sit with him. Tell him about the delta — the fish markets, the ice, whatever hospitals have that makes people want to reach them. Hope is ballast. Load him up."

She almost smiled. "You're a strange woman, Captain Reyes."

"I'm a riverboat captain. Strangeness comes with the license."

* * *

The storm hit at full dark and hit like it held a grudge. Rain came sideways, then downward, then in quantities that suggested the sky had simply given up pretending to be sky. Thunder rolled up and down the valley without pause, one long continuous argument. I kept a double anchor watch, engine idling against the lines, and rode it out in the wheelhouse with tea I never drank and the compass glowing on the binnacle like a small faithful heart.

Somewhere past midnight, in a gap between squalls, I heard footsteps and found Ferro standing in the wheelhouse doorway, soaked despite the awning, holding two mugs of terrible coffee.

"For the watch," he said. His voice cracked on *watch*, and he pretended it hadn't.

I took the mug. We stood together looking at the rain hammering the river into white static.

"My brother was on the survey team," he said finally. "Before. Before they sent the second group. He told me the upper river was beautiful. He said—" He stopped. Started again. "They never found him either."

I didn't say anything clever. There wasn't anything clever to say. I handed him back the mug, empty, and said, "Get some sleep, Ferro. Tomorrow I need eyes. Yours work fine."

He nodded like I'd given him a medal, and maybe I had. You learn, running a boat, that people are cargo too — some of them fragile, all of them shifting — and the trick isn't keeping them still. It's stowing them where the motion does the least harm.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, the river swollen and brown and moving with new purpose. I gave the storm another hour to sulk offshore, then weighed anchor and took us out into the current.

The Narrows proper lay ahead — the real ones, the white-water reach where the valley pinches shut and the river drops through like a coin through a slot. At normal water it was a hard run. At flood water it was a rumor people didn't survive. But the side passage let me out below the worst of it, into a stretch I knew like my own pulse, and the flood that would kill a careless boat would also hide ours — churned water, no wake to follow, no trail but luck.

Izquierdo woke as we got underway, fever-bright, and insisted on being carried to the wheelhouse. Osei propped him in the corner with blankets and he watched me work with the fixed attention of a man memorizing something.

"You've run this water before," he said.

"Once or twice."

"Liar," he said pleasantly, and closed his eyes, and smiled like a man who has decided to trust the lie.

We made nine miles by noon, then twelve, the current doing half our work, the boat running light and eager, and when I glanced astern at the long empty brown water there was nothing behind us at all — no gray hull, no wake, no one. A night's lead, maybe more. Maybe enough.

It was Osei who spotted it first, late afternoon: smoke rising thin and straight from the fuel depot bend ahead, the depot that marked the last safe anchorage before the delta channels. Too much smoke for a cook fire. Too neat for an accident.

I put the glasses on it and felt the day go cold in my hands.

Three boats at the dock. Men unloading crates with military economy. And standing on the fuel apron, hands clasped behind his back, watching the river come to him — a lean figure in a dry gray suit, patient as arithmetic.

Corrigan hadn't followed us through the Narrows.

He'd simply known where we'd come out.
