## 6. The Man Who Drowned a Town

The fuel depot at Puerto Ceniza was the kind of place that existed because the river had nowhere else to put it: a corrugated shed, a leaning pump, a dog asleep in the shade of a rusted barge with no name and no future. Rosalind had bought diesel there a dozen times over six years, and the man who ran it — old Ferro, half-blind, entirely deaf when it suited him — had never once asked her name beyond "captain," which was the highest compliment the river paid anyone.

She brought the *Marta B* alongside at mid-morning, running on fumes and nerves. Behind them lay two days of hard water and one night of listening for engines that never quite came. Ahead lay the narrows, and after that the delta, and after that — if the river was kind, which it never was — a journalist named Ibarra who printed things the government wished he wouldn't.

Osei stood at the rail with the surveyor's charts wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a sleeping child. The wounded man, Teodoro, slept below with fever climbing his spine like ivy. The junior one, Pell, was scrubbing the deck with the frantic energy of a young man who had discovered that fear looks exactly like industry.

"Fuel first," Osei said. "Then we don't stop again until salt water."

"Fuel first," Rosalind agreed, and that was when she saw the black launch tied up at the far end of the dock, and her stomach performed a slow, deliberate roll, like a barrel going over a weir.

* * *

Corrigan sat on an upturned crate outside Ferro's shed, drinking coffee from a tin cup as though he owned the crate, the shed, the dog, and possibly the river itself. He wore civilian clothes today — a linen jacket gone soft at the elbows — but clothes don't change what a man is, any more than a borrowed name had changed what Rosalind was.

"Captain Villalobos," he said pleasantly.

"Mr. Corrigan." She made the lines fast herself rather than let Pell do it, because Pell's hands were shaking and Corrigan would notice. "You're a long way from your office."

"I'm told rivers are good for the constitution." He gestured at the pump with his cup. "Please. Don't let me interrupt commerce."

Rosalind did not move toward the pump. "Ferro's not open till noon."

"It's ten past." Corrigan checked a watch that probably cost more than the *Marta B*. "I asked him to make an exception. He was very accommodating. Deaf men usually are, once you speak loudly enough about their licenses."

Behind her, she heard Osei come down the gangway and stop. She didn't turn around. You didn't turn your back on a man like Corrigan, but you also didn't give him the satisfaction of a formation.

"You've been following us," Rosalind said.

"Following you? Captain." He set down the cup and spread his hands, a man wounded by the very suggestion. "I came to *help*. There's a storm coming down from the high country — you'll have seen the clouds. The agency is offering full amnesty to Dr. Osei and her people. No charges, no questions, transport home on government steam. All we ask is the return of certain survey materials that were, frankly, misappropriated."

"Misappropriated," Osei repeated, in the tone of a woman testing whether a floorboard would hold her weight.

"Dr. Osei." Corrigan rose, and up close he was smaller than his reputation, which Rosalind found oddly comforting. "You're a brilliant scientist. Your career is a candle worth protecting. Don't burn it down over some paper maps of a riverbed nobody will ever look at twice."

"They're not paper," Osei said quietly. "They're sonar plates. And they're not of a riverbed. They're of a town."

The dog lifted its head, considered the three humans, and went back to sleep. Somewhere under the brown water, forty years downstream and forty years down, San Esteban kept its own counsel.

"Amnesty," Rosalind said. "That's generous. What happens to the boat?"

"The boat?" Corrigan smiled. "Why, nothing. The boat goes free."

"Everything goes free except the truth," Rosalind said. "That's not amnesty, Mr. Corrigan. That's a receipt."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; she'd expected anger. Interest. The interest of a man who has just found a page in a ledger he thought he'd torn out.

"Villalobos," he said slowly. "Yes. That is the name on your license, isn't it. Bought secondhand from a shipping clerk in Santa Rosa, six years ago. A woman named Villalobos who emigrated the year before and never knew she'd sold her identity to a riverboat captain." He picked up his coffee again, unhurried. "Clean work. I'd have to compliment whoever arranged it, if I didn't already know."

The world went narrow and quiet, the way it did before a bar shifted underwater. Rosalind heard her own heartbeat and, distantly, Ferro's radio playing something with too much accordion in it.

"Whoever arranged it," she said.

"Your papers were processed through the provincial registry in '61. After the flood." Corrigan drank. "There was a program. Resettlement documentation for survivors of the San Esteban disaster. Very efficient. It gave new names to orphans so they could be placed without — complications. Sentiment. Lawsuits." He tilted his head. "It also meant the agency always knew where those orphans were. Every one of them. Except, for a long time, you."

"Then you didn't know," Rosalind said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Until now. So this whole conversation is you telling me you've been looking."

"Captain—" He sighed, genuinely regretful, which was worse than the smile. "Your father was Mateo Reyes. Chief engineer, San Esteban headworks. Do you want me to go on?"

"No."

"Because I can. I have the file memorized. I wrote part of it." He set the cup down on the crate with a small click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning. "On the night of March ninth, 1961, the dam upstream of your town failed. The official finding was structural fatigue. Rainfall. An act of God, whom we thanked publicly and billed privately. But the gates, Captain. The spillway gates. They were dynamited at eleven-forty that night, and the flood that took San Esteban took four hundred and six people, and it took them because the land under the town had been sold three times over to three different buyers, and a drowned town settles its own title disputes."

Pell had stopped scrubbing. Osei's hand found Rosalind's shoulder — light, steadying, an anchor line thrown in white water.

"And your father," Corrigan continued, softer now, almost kind, which was the cruelest register he owned, "was the man who refused to open the gates early. There was a plan, you understand. A controlled release, days before, dressed up as maintenance. Empty the valley, save the lives, lose only the town — and the town was going to be lost regardless, that was decided above all our pay grades. Your father refused. He said the gates were his responsibility and he would not be the hand that drowned his neighbors. So they waited until he was asleep, and they did it without him, and then they needed him to be a coward in the record instead of a martyr, because a martyr asks questions and a coward closes them." He looked at her with something that might, in a better man, have been shame. "He signed a confession, Captain. Two days before he died. Did you know that?"

"I know my mother burned everything he owned," Rosalind said. "I was twelve. I thought she was grieving."

"She was protecting you. The resettlement program wasn't charity. It was a firewall. Every orphan given a new name was a witness erased. Yours was just the most thorough erasure on the list, because of whose daughter you were." He spread his hands. "So you see my problem. You are not a neutral party I can pay off. You are a liability with a boat and a grudge, and I am offering you the last decent deal you will ever be offered: the plates, the amnesty, and you keep sailing under whatever name you like, invisible forever, safe forever. Or—"

"Or I'm a liability," Rosalind finished. "And liabilities get handled."

"I prefer the word *resolved*."

Above them the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had begun to talk to the palm fronds in that low voice the high country used when it meant business. Rosalind thought about her father — a man she had rebuilt out of fragments, a compass, a smell of machine oil, a story her mother told once and never repeated: *he loved that river more than he feared any man in a suit.* She had spent twenty years being careful. Careful had been presented to her as a personality. It had been a cage with the door painted shut.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said. "You take your launch back downriver. When the storm clears, you read the morning paper like everyone else."

Corrigan studied her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, genuine, almost admiring. "You know what your father said to me? The night before. He said, *'One day the river will give it all back.'* I thought he was being poetic." He straightened his jacket. "He was being a schedule."

He walked past her toward the black launch, then paused, close enough that she could smell his coffee and the faint institutional soap of government men everywhere.

"For what it's worth, Captain — and it isn't worth much — I was thirty years old and I filed the paperwork. I didn't press the plunger. I've told myself that for forty years." He glanced back at Osei, at the oilcloth bundle. "The offer stands until the storm makes landfall. After that, everyone involved becomes a rescue statistic. Rivers are so convenient that way."

* * *

They watched the launch recede around the bend, black paint swallowing gray water, and then Rosalind turned to Ferro's pump and began fueling with hands that only shook a little.

"He knows your name," Osei said.

"He knew it before I did, maybe." Rosalind watched the diesel gauge climb. "Twenty years I thought I chose to disappear. Turns out I was filed away." She laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, though there was something underneath it that might become humor later, once they were alive. "My mother always said I got my stubbornness from him. Turns out I got my paperwork from him too."

"Rosalind." Osei's voice was very steady. "If he knows who you are, he doesn't need the plates anymore. He needs you dead either way. The offer was theater."

"I know." Rosalind screwed the cap down and looked north, where the clouds had stacked themselves into a wall the color of a bruise, and the first fat drops were already spotting the deck around Pell's abandoned scrub brush. "Which means we don't wait for the storm to decide anything. We run the narrows tonight, ahead of it."

"Through the channel that isn't on any chart?"

"The one that isn't on any chart," Rosalind said, "because I'm the only person alive who remembers it. My father drew it for me. In pencil." She stepped to the wheel and rang the engine bell, loud and bright, and the *Marta B* woke beneath her feet like something that had been waiting twenty years for permission. "Hold on to your plates, Doctor. We're going home the leeward way."
