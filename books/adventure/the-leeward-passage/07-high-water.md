## 7. High Water

The barometer had been falling all morning, and by noon it fell the way a drunk falls — slowly at first, then all at once.

I stood at the wheel of the *Marta Luz* and watched the sky over the eastern ridge turn the color of a bruise going bad. The river had opinions about weather, and it was already voicing them: a brown muscle flexing under the hull, current running a knot and a half harder than it should for this stretch, driftwood riding past us in nervous little dashes like commuters who'd heard something on the news.

"Captain," Osei called from the deckhouse, "the junior wants to know if we're stopping."

"We're not stopping."

"He'd like to know why."

"Because stopping is how you die in floodwater." I said it loud enough to carry. The junior — his name was Pell, and he was twenty-three and had the survival instincts of a moth — appeared at the door with his face arranged into an expression of polite disagreement. "The banks go soft," I told him. "You tie off to a tree that's standing in soup, and by two in the morning the tree is lying down and taking your boat with it. You keep moving. You keep her bow into the worst of it and you keep moving."

"And if the worst of it is ahead of us?" Osei asked quietly.

I looked upriver, where the channel narrowed between bluffs the old charts called the Throat, and then behind us, where a gray smudge of diesel smoke hung over the bend like a signature on a bill.

"Then we go through it," I said, "and hope whatever's chasing us has worse sense than we do."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about storms on the river: they don't arrive. They *assemble*. First the wind comes down out of the hills in long scouting parties, testing the water, flattening it one minute and shredding it the next. Then the light goes strange and greenish, the way it does before hail, and every bird you've ever ignored suddenly becomes very visible because they are all leaving. And then, last, with a sound like a freight train deciding against its schedule, the rain comes — not falling but arriving, sideways, in sheets thick enough to drown in.

We were forty minutes into the Throat when it arrived.

I had Reyes — the wounded surveyor, feverish but stubborn as a fence post — lashed into the pilot seat beside me because he knew these waters too, once, and because a second pair of eyes is worth more than comfort. He kept pointing left. Left, left, always left, away from the charted channel and toward a seam of darker water hugging the bluff where the current ran slack.

"You're sure," I shouted over the rain.

"I fished here as a boy," he shouted back. "Before your time, girl. Before anybody's maps."

The *Marta Luz* is sixty feet of pre-war river steel with the turning radius of a cathedral, and she does not like being told things twice. I brought her left. The rain hit like applause from ten thousand wet hands, and the world shrank to the width of my windshield wipers, which were losing their argument with dignity.

Behind us, through a tear in the weather, I saw the launch.

Corrigan's boat was bigger than mine and newer and faster in flat water, and none of that mattered now. It came into the Throat doing what fast boats do in floods — fighting the current instead of reading it — and I watched it swing wide around the first bluff with the casual arrogance of a man who had never once been told no by geography.

"He's coming in after us," Osei said at my shoulder. She had to put her mouth nearly against my ear.

"He's coming in blind." I shook my head. "He doesn't know the seam along the bluff. He doesn't know the eddy below the second point flips when the water's this high. He's driving a government budget through a living river."

"Will he make it?"

"That depends entirely," I said, "on whether the river is feeling merciful today."

And here I will confess something, because a logbook is no place for vanity: some part of me — a small, hot, shameful part that had been fed for three days on the memory of San Esteban and the word *deliberate* — wanted the answer to be no.

* * *

The river decided at the second point.

I felt it before I saw it, the way you feel a lie in a room before anyone speaks. The current under my keel went lazy and then wrong, sliding backward along the hull, and I understood that the high water had drowned the old eddy line and built a new one, meaner, spinning counterclockwise where the charts said calm.

Reyes saw it a half-second later and grabbed my arm. "The launch—"

Corrigan's boat came around the point exactly where the old map said safe water lived, and the new eddy took it by the stern like a dog shaking a rat. I saw the helmsman correct hard — the worst thing, the textbook-worst thing — and the correction turned a skid into a spin, and the spin put them broadside to the current, and the current, which weighs more than any engine ever built, simply picked them up and introduced them to the rock shelf that lives three feet under the surface there.

The sound carried even through the storm. A crunch like God biting a cracker.

Then the launch was listing hard to starboard, its engines screaming a note engines are not meant to scream, and men were spilling onto its deck in orange life vests, and the river began, unhurried, to fold the whole arrangement under.

"Captain." Osei's voice. Steady. Terrible. "There are four of them."

"I can count."

"They tried to kill us."

"They tried to *stop* us. There's a difference, and it matters less right now than you'd think." My hands were already on the wheel, and my hands, traitors to every sensible argument, were bringing the *Marta Luz* around.

Every instinct I owned voted no. The instinct that had kept me alive since I was twelve years old and swimming away from my own hometown; the instinct that said *invisible, uninvolved, unnamed*; the instinct that whispered, with real warmth, *let the river settle your accounts, Rosalind, let it do what it did before, let it take Corrigan the way it took everything else and never once apologized.* That instinct had a strong case. It had decades of evidence.

But I had watched a town drown when I was twelve, and I remembered — this is the thing, this is the whole thing — I remembered what the water sounded like from inside it. I remembered people. I did not remember politics or ledgers or dam gates. I remembered a man on a roof holding a child above the waterline, and I remembered thinking, even then, even drowning, *somebody should help him.*

"Osei," I said. "Get lines ready. Throw ropes, not lectures. Pell — forward, get the searchlight working if it kills you."

"You're going back," Reyes said. Not a question. He was smiling, fever-bright, like I'd confirmed something he'd bet on years ago.

"I'm going back," I said, "because justice isn't drowning a man in the dark and calling it the river's idea. If Corrigan answers for San Esteban, he answers in daylight, in front of witnesses, with his own name attached. That's the only verdict worth anything. The river doesn't testify. The river just erases."

I put the bow into the flood and drove at the wreck.

* * *

It took eleven minutes, though it felt like a season and a half.

The launch was down by the stern, wedged on the shelf, water walking steadily across her deck. Two of Corrigan's men had made it to the bluff-side shallows on their own and stood there waving their arms with the frantic gratitude of the recently unbaptized. The other two were still aboard — one clinging to the cabin roof, and one in the water.

The one in the water was Corrigan.

I don't know how he lost his grip. It didn't matter. Floodwater doesn't care how careful you were. He came past our bow face-down, turning slowly, arms making the wide stupid circles of a man who had read about swimming in a report somewhere, and I did the thing every captain is told never to do in flood stage: I put the rail to him.

Osei went down the ladder herself, rope around her waist, and I'll say this for the woman who spent six weeks cataloguing a drowned town: she caught him by the collar on the first pass and held on while the river argued. Pell and I hauled. Corrigan came over the rail like a landed fish, coughing half the Throat out of his lungs, and lay on my deck in his ruined suit looking smaller than any man I'd ever pulled from water.

His eyes found mine. Even half-drowned, even blue-lipped, they did their arithmetic. He knew whose deck he was on. He knew what I could do about it — which was anything, which was nothing, which was whatever I pleased, with four witnesses and no questions ever asked.

"Don't," he rasped.

"Don't what?"

"You know what." A cough. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't."

I crouched next to him, rain hammering the deck around us, and I gave him the truth straight, because he'd earned exactly that much and not a drop more.

"Mr. Corrigan," I said, "if I wanted you dead, I'd have saved myself eleven minutes and a bent railing. Get him below," I told the others, "before he drowns on dry wood out of sheer habit. And somebody find him a blanket — the ugly one."

Pell laughed. It was the first sound any of us had made in hours that wasn't shouting, and it broke something loose in the air.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the bluff while the storm spent itself, the *Marta Luz* swinging gentle on the line, the wrecked launch groaning against the rocks like an old man complaining about stairs. Two of Corrigan's men sat wrapped in blankets, drinking the coffee Reyes brewed with the solemnity of a sacrament. The fourth had broken his arm on the rocks and slept under sedation in the galley, snoring like a landslide.

Corrigan sat across from me at the little table under the wheel, dry at last, holding coffee he hadn't touched. Between us sat the silence of two people who each knew exactly what the other was.

"Why?" he asked finally. It wasn't gratitude. It was bookkeeping. He needed the entry explained.

"Because I was in that water once," I said. "A long time ago. Somebody didn't come back for me, and I've been paying the interest ever since." I looked at him until he looked away. "And because a drowned man can't testify, Mr. Corrigan. I need you alive, healthy, and terrified — in that order — for about another week."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition — the look of a man discovering the ledger he thought he controlled has a second set of books.

"The storm's passing," he said quietly. "My office will have boats on the water by dawn. Whatever you think you're carrying south—"

"I'm not carrying anything," I said, and smiled, and for the first time in fifteen years the smile belonged to someone whose name I actually knew. "I'm just a fuel boat running flood stage, Captain. Ask anyone."

Above us, the rain thinned. Through the wheelhouse glass, far downriver, the clouds were tearing open over the delta, and beneath them the water ran high and brown and fast — running, the way rivers do, toward the sea, carrying everything they're given.

Including, if I did my job right, us.

I went to the wheel and checked my father's compass, sitting in its bracket where it had ridden through every mile of this. The needle swung, settled, pointed south.

"Hold on to something," I told the room. "The leeward passage runs at night, and we've got a lead to spend."
