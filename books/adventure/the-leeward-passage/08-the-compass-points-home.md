## 8. The Compass Points Home

I have been a scientist long enough to know that the worst arguments happen not in lecture halls but in small boats, where no one can walk away and everyone can hear you chew.

We were anchored in a backwater off the main channel — Rosalind called it a "sleeper," a slough of black water and overhanging willow where the current barely breathed. She had killed the engine two hours before dawn and let us drift into it sideways, quiet as a leaf settling, while the patrol boat's searchlight swept the main channel half a mile east. Now we sat in the green dark with the engine cold and the rain starting again, and I was learning that four frightened people in an eighteen-foot skiff is one more than a functioning committee.

"We burn them," said Teo.

Teo was the junior — twenty-three, a cartography student who had joined the expedition because he thought river surveys were romantic. The river had spent three weeks correcting that impression. He held the tin case against his chest like a life vest, which was ironic, since the case was the reason we couldn't have life vests, or fuel, or sleep, or anything else a hunted person might want.

"Nobody is burning anything," I said.

"They're *plates*, Dr. Osei. Photographs. If Corrigan gets them, he doesn't just bury the survey — he buries us. We're not witnesses to him, we're loose ends." Teo's voice cracked on *loose ends* and he hated himself for it; I watched him hate himself for it. "Burn them, scatter the boat, tell them we found nothing. They'll believe it. Nobody believes a junior anyway."

"That last part is true," said Marcus from the bow, "but the rest is cowardice talking."

Marcus was our wounded surveyor, which meant he had earned the right to be insufferable. He lay propped against the gunwale with his leg splinted and wrapped, feverish but lucid, running the argument like a man refereeing a match he intended to win from a stretcher. "Burying it is what they want. Burning it is what they'd prefer. Both are surrender with different paperwork."

"And selling it?" Teo shot back. "You said it yourself last night. There are papers in the capital who'd pay—"

"Papers pay for stories," Marcus said. "They don't pay for protection. We'd be rich and dead, which is at least a novel combination."

I let them go on. This was partly strategy — an argument left alone often argues itself into a corner — and mostly exhaustion. Three days since Halvorsen died on the bank above the mission ruin with the agency's men shouting across the water. Three days of running by night, hiding by day, eating crackers and canned peaches out of the same tin. My hands still smelled of the generator fuel we'd used to keep the wound clean. Somewhere behind us, upstream, Corrigan was regrouping, and somewhere ahead of us, downstream, the delta waited with its journalists and its lawyers and its terrible indifference.

And in the stern, Rosalind Villalobos said nothing at all.

She sat with one boot up on the tiller mount, cleaning the compass her father had left her — I'd seen her do it every night, a ritual as fixed as prayer: unscrew the bezel, wipe the glass, hold it level, watch the needle settle north. She did it now in the dark, by feel, without looking, while the other three of us conducted democracy.

"You're quiet, Captain," Marcus observed.

"I'm driving."

"The boat is anchored."

"I'm driving it later," she said, and there was just enough dryness in it that Teo laughed despite himself, a short startled sound, like a bird flushing. That was her gift, I was beginning to understand. She could put a hairline crack in terror without breaking anything.

But then she stopped polishing. And she looked at the compass needle instead of through it, and when she spoke again her voice had changed key entirely.

"Before you vote," she said, "you should know who you're voting with."

The rain ticked on the canopy. Downriver, faint as memory, the patrol boat's engine throbbed and faded.

"My name isn't Villalobos."

Teo blinked. "It — what?"

"It's Reyes. Rosalind Reyes. My father was Tomás Reyes, chief engineer of the San Esteban dam." She turned the compass in her hand so the brass caught what little light there was. "You've all seen the maps. You've seen the date stamped on the demolition charges in Osei's sonar overlays. November 1961. I was twelve years old that November. I stood on the levee with my mother and my brother and watched the water come down the valley, and I watched San Esteban go under, and I was told — everyone was told — that the rains were freak, that the dam failed, that God or geology did it." A pause. "My father opened the gates. Did you know that? The record says he didn't. The record says he panicked, or he wasn't there, or he froze. The record was written by the same office that wrote your funding grant, Doctor."

I felt something move in my chest that had no scientific name. Because I had read those records. I had read every scrap the archive would release, and the released scraps said exactly that: Engineer T. Reyes, negligence under pressure, gates unopened, tragedy unavoidable. I had written a footnote about it. A footnote. I had reduced a man's death and a town's drowning to a parenthetical.

"Your father refused," I said quietly. "He refused to open the gates, because opening them would have confirmed the flood was coming from the dam — from the demolition. So they drowned him twice. Once in the water and once in the file."

Rosalind looked at me, and in the dark her face was very still. "He opened them," she said. "That's the whole crime, Doctor. He *did* open them — late, too late, after they'd already blown the spillway and he understood what was coming down the valley. He opened them to try to bleed the surge, knowing the record would say whatever Corrigan's people wanted it to say. Knowing he'd hang for it either way. He chose the town over his own name." Her thumb moved once across the compass bezel. "This was his. It points north no matter how badly the world lies about everything else. I figured somebody in this country ought to keep one honest instrument."

Nobody spoke. Teo had gone white around the mouth. Marcus had pushed himself up on one elbow, fever forgotten, staring at her the way I imagine men stare at a chart that suddenly shows a reef where they'd sailed all their lives.

"So when you vote," Rosalind finished, "understand what you're voting on. Those plates aren't somebody else's scandal. That's my town on them. My family. You want to sell it, fine — name your price and I'll row you to the papers myself. You want to burn it, fine — but you'll do it knowing whose grave you're burning. I'm done being invisible. Whatever you decide, decide it looking at me."

* * *

Here is what I understood in the next hour, sitting in that black slough while the rain came down and the argument finally, mercifully, changed shape:

I had been thinking about the plates all wrong.

For three weeks — for the whole expedition, honestly — I had treated them as evidence. Evidence is a thing you possess. Evidence can be seized, stolen, burned, lost overboard. Evidence lives in tin cases and dies in courtrooms, and its whole miserable existence is a contest of custody. That was why Teo wanted to burn it: evidence in enemy hands is a weapon. That was why Marcus wanted to sell it: evidence in friendly hands is currency. Both of them were right, within the logic of evidence, and both of them were wrong, because the logic itself was the trap. As long as the truth lived only in those plates, Corrigan only had to win once.

But testimony is not evidence. Testimony is a person. It cannot be confiscated, because it walks, eats, sleeps, and answers questions. It cannot be burned, because burning a witness creates ten more. It cannot be bought, because — well. Usually.

"Teo," I said. "Give me the case."

He hesitated, then passed it over. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the photographic plates: the sonar overlays, the demolition charge signatures, the survey photographs of the dam's blasted spillway, and — the item Corrigan's men had killed Halvorsen for — the original 1961 engineering drawings with the alteration marks, the ones showing the flood model that predicted, to the meter, what the water would do to San Esteban. Predicted in advance. Filed in advance. Dated in advance.

I took out the oldest plate, the valley survey, and held it flat on my knee.

"Rosalind. Point to your house."

She leaned over. For a moment her finger hovered, and I saw her go somewhere else — twelve years old, levee, rain — and then she touched a spot on the river bend below the church. "There. Blue shutters. My mother grew peppers on the roof."

"Marcus. Your leg. Can you testify to the condition of the mission ruin, the looted cache, the cut radio mast?"

"Sawn, not snapped," Marcus said. "I'll swear to it on any book you like."

"Teo. You logged the supply inventory. Can you show what was taken and what was left?"

"Every crate," Teo said. His voice had steadied. Something was happening to him — the same thing that happens to a junior when he realizes the senior staff need him, that he is not ballast but crew. "Signed, dated, photographed. I kept the log even after—" He stopped. "Yes. I can."

I closed the case and set it on the thwart between us, deliberately, where anyone could reach it.

"Then listen to me," I said. "Corrigan has spent forty years maintaining a story, and stories are cheap to maintain as long as they're only opposed by things. Papers can be stolen. Plates can be burned. Files can drown, conveniently, in floods." I looked at each of them. "But there are four of us, and now there are five, because the woman steering this boat is not a hired captain — she is the primary witness, the living daughter of the man they erased, and she has been carrying the truth in her pocket this entire time in brass and glass." I picked up the compass and held it out to her. "Testimony needs a witness. The witness needs the testimony. Neither survives alone. That's why we don't sell it and we don't burn it. We *deliver* it — all of it, together, to someone who prints."

"To whom?" Marcus asked. "Half the delta press is owned by people who drink with Corrigan."

"Not half," I said. "I taught a seminar in the capital two years ago. One of my students now works at *El Faro del Delta*. Elena Brandt. She fact-checks like a surgeon and she hates the Ministry of Water with a passion I always suspected was personal. If anyone will print this and stand behind the printing, it's her." I paused. "Also she owes me for a grade I regret."

"Grievous academic corruption," Marcus said solemnly. "The republic is doomed."

"It got you a splint and antibiotics," I said. "The republic provides."

Rosalind took the compass back. She held it a moment, then laughed — low, brief, real, the first time I'd heard it — and screwed the bezel home.

"All right, Doctor," she said. "Delta it is. But El Faro's offices sit on the waterfront past the customs basin, and the customs basin is where Corrigan's launch will be." She tapped the chart rolled beside her knee. "Which means we come in the way nobody comes in. The leeward channels. Marsh, mud, mosquitoes the size of your thumb, and water shallow enough that a patrol draft draws six inches more than we do." Her eyes came up, and there was something in them I recognized from the maps — contour lines around a thing too deep to fathom. "It's a night run. It'll cost us. Maybe the boat. Maybe more."

"Captain," Marcus said, "with respect — you've been describing my entire career."

Teo snorted. Even I smiled. And outside the willow curtain, far downstream toward the delta, the sky had begun — very faintly, the way beginnings do — to turn the color of tin.

Rosalind lifted the anchor without a splash, and the compass needle settled north, honest as ever, while we swung south toward the marshes and whatever the leeward passage meant to make of us.
