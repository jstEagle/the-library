## 9. The Leeward Passage

The delta at night does not look like water. It looks like a country that drowned and never admitted it, black channels threading between black islands, the mangroves standing in the shallows like mourners who have forgotten why they came. I had run these leeward passages since I was old enough to reach the throttle standing on a beer crate, back when my father would let me steer only on the straight stretches, which in the delta meant almost nowhere.

Now I steered them with three passengers, a satchel of photographic plates wrapped in oilcloth, and two boats' worth of men behind us who wanted all of it.

"They're still there," Osei said. She stood at my shoulder, one hand on the rail, watching the darkness astern. "Headlight. Maybe half a mile."

"Maybe less," said the junior one — Delgado, his name was, though for six days he had been so frightened he'd barely qualified as a person, more of a trembling outline. "They were gaining before we turned off the main channel."

"Then they'll be surprised," I said, "when we stop existing."

I killed the running lights. The world went absolute black, and then, slowly, my eyes remembered how to see: the pale seam of current where channel met channel, the ghost-gray crowns of the mangroves, the fireflies stitching themselves into the reeds as if the marsh had decided to help. My father's compass sat open on the chart box beside me, its needle glowing faintly, pointing home out of pure habit.

"Rosalind." Osei's voice was low. She had learned, over six days, when to use that voice. "Whatever you're about to do — tell me first."

"I'm going to do what my mother did with the church collection money," I said. "Split it up so nobody gets everything."

* * *

Here is the shape of it, which I laid out for them while idling against a mudbank no wider than a coffin:

The delta had two ways through. The main passage was wide, deep, marked by the government's own buoys — which meant Corrigan's men knew every bend of it, and could wait at the mouth like spiders at a doorway. The leeward passages were the other way: narrow, shifting, unmarked, known only to smugglers, fishermen, and girls whose fathers taught them to read water instead of books. But even the leeward passages converged, eventually, at the fishing village of Puerto Ceniza, where a journalist named Beatriz Salas had been waiting three days for me, drinking coffee she didn't like and asking questions the cantina owner couldn't answer.

One boat could not carry both the plates and the pursuit. So the boat would carry neither.

"You want us to leave you," Osei said. Not a question.

"I want you to take the skiff." I nodded toward the little punt lashed to the stern, the one I used for fuel runs in shallow water. "Delgado rows you through the reed cut — it's too shallow for anything with an engine, and too dark for anyone to follow who doesn't know the stakes. You come out at Puerto Ceniza from the north. Nobody watches the north; there's nothing there but mosquitoes and my aunt's second husband."

"And you?"

"I take the *Marta* down the main passage with every light burning and the engine loud enough to wake the dead. They follow me. That's the whole point of me."

"That is *not* the whole point of you," Osei said, with the particular fury of a woman who has already buried one colleague this month. "That is a fraction of the point of you, and I object to spending it."

"Dr. Osei." I turned and looked at her, and I found, to my surprise, that I was smiling. Six days of running will do that to you — either wring the fear out or boil it down to something else entirely. "You've spent your whole career proving a town was murdered. I've spent mine pretending I wasn't born in it. Tonight we can only save one thing, and it isn't going to be the boat."

The wounded surveyor, Herrera, lay propped against the wheelhouse with his leg strapped straight, gray-faced but listening. He raised two fingers off his chest — a salute, or a blessing, hard to say. "The girl's right," he rasped. "Maps don't row."

"The plates aren't maps," Osei said quietly. "They're testimony. And testimony needs a living witness." She looked at me a long moment, and something passed between us that I did not fully understand until much later — the recognition of one stubborn woman by another. Then she took the oilcloth satchel and stepped down into the skiff, and Delgado followed, and Herrera was lowered after them with considerably less grace than any of us pretended.

"North through the reed cut," I told Delgado. "Keep the fireflies on your left. When the water starts tasting of salt, you're nearly there."

"How do you know when it tastes of salt?" he asked, genuinely.

"You'll know," I said. "It's the river saying goodbye."

Osei gripped my hand once, hard, and said nothing, because there was nothing left that hadn't been said in the dark over six days. Then the skiff slid into the reeds and became a rumor, and I was alone on the *Marta* with a full tank, a loud engine, and a plan that depended entirely on being worth chasing.

Which, I reflected, pushing the throttle forward and lighting every lamp I owned, had rarely been in doubt.

* * *

They took the bait the way fish take anything shiny — immediately, greedily, without one thought for the hook.

I heard their engines come alive behind me within ten minutes, two boats this time, running the government buoys with the confidence of men who believed the river belonged to their employer. I gave them a show. I ran the marked channel at a speed the *Marta* disapproved of deeply, wallowing through the bends, my lights blazing, my silhouette unmistakable. Once, passing a fishing camp, I even shouted a cheerful good evening to the family on the dock, because if you are going to be a decoy you may as well be a convincing one, and terrified decoys attract scrutiny.

For an hour it worked beautifully.

Then the river narrowed, and the storm that had been sulking offshore all week finally made up its mind.

The rain came sideways, sudden and total, as though someone upstream had pulled a plug in the sky. Visibility went from poor to theological. And in that white roaring blindness, I did the thing I had been planning since the mudbank: I swung the *Marta* hard to starboard, out of the marked channel, into the flooded marsh beyond it, and shut everything down.

No lights. No engine. Just the rain hammering the deck and the boat drifting on floodwater that, in this season, let a shallow-draft hull slide over country that was technically land eleven months of the year.

The pursuit roared past me in the dark — I felt them more than heard them, two throbbing presences in the rain, running on toward the sea and the empty channel ahead. I counted to two hundred, which is longer than you think when your heart is keeping time, and then I started the engine at a whisper and began to feel my way south through the drowned mangroves toward the reed cut and Puerto Ceniza.

This was the part I had not confessed to Osei, because she would have objected on professional grounds: the flooded marsh between the main channel and the reed cut was not a route. It was a memory. I had crossed it exactly once, at fifteen, with my father, in a flood very like this one, and he had narrated it the way he narrated everything — *watch the crown of that dead tree, mija, it means the sandbar underneath runs east; count your turns, the marsh forgives nothing twice.*

I counted my turns. One, past the dead tree. Two, where the current ran contrary. Three—

Three was where the marsh tried to keep me.

The flood had moved things. A snag I remembered as lying clear of the channel now reached across it like a toll gate, and I saw it too late — a black arm of wood just under the surface — and the *Marta* struck it with a crack I felt in my teeth. The wheel kicked. The engine screamed and caught and screamed again. Water came aboard over the transom in a single cold insult, and the current, finding a wounded boat, took hold of her with both hands and began to swing her stern-first into the mangroves.

I have nearly died perhaps four times on that river, and each time I have noticed the same strange thing: the mind goes quiet and practical, like a deckhand who has seen it all before. *Bilge pump,* said the quiet mind. *Fenders off the hooks. Paddle if the pump fails.* I got the pump running, got the bow pointed downstream, got the *Marta* limping through the last mile of marsh with water sloshing around my ankles and the compass needle shivering in its glass, pointing home, pointing home, pointing home.

When the reed cut opened ahead of me — a black corridor breathing the smell of salt and mud — I laughed out loud, alone, in the rain, which should tell you something about the state of me.

* * *

Puerto Ceniza at dawn is a village built on stilts over brown water, smelling of fish and diesel and frying plantains, and I have never in my life been so glad to see anything. I brought the *Marta* in listing gently to port, tied her to Beatriz Salas's own dock because I knew she'd want to write that detail down, and found the others already there — Osei sitting dry and composed in the journalist's kitchen, the oilcloth satchel on the table between them like a loaf of bread.

Herrera was being fed soup by my aunt's second husband, who had taken one look at the man's leg and appointed himself chief surgeon, nurse, and critic of the expedition's footwear.

"You're bleeding," Osei said, standing.

"I'm *damp*," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the boat."

"Rosalind—"

"She's holed below the waterline and pumping slower than she leaks. She'll need a careening and two days' work." I set my father's compass on the table next to the satchel, because it seemed right that they should meet. "She held. That's all I'm reporting."

Beatriz Salas was a small woman with ink-stained fingers and the patient, unnerving gaze of someone who had waited twenty years for a story like this and intended to enjoy it responsibly. She unwrapped the oilcloth with the reverence of a woman handling relics, studied the first plate a long moment — the sonar image of the dam, the charge patterns, the surveyor's annotations in Dr. Osei's precise hand — and then looked up at me.

"These names," she said. "On the record. All of them. Including yours."

And here is the thing I had been running from for twenty years, the thing Corrigan had held over me like a hook through the gill, the name I had left at the bottom of a drowned town along with everything else I loved.

I looked at Osei. Osei looked at the compass. Outside, the tide was turning, and the river was carrying the last of the storm seaward, the way rivers carry everything eventually, given time.

"Villalobos," I said. My voice cracked on it, and then steadied. "Rosalind Villalobos. Of San Esteban. My father was Tomás Villalobos, and he was the engineer at the dam, and he refused to open the gates, and they drowned him with the town. Write it exactly like that."

Beatriz wrote it exactly like that.

Then she looked past me, out the kitchen window toward the dock, and her expression changed — sharpened, the way a blade sharpens — and she said, very calmly:

"Captain. There's a government launch coming up the channel. And the man standing in the bow — is that the one who offered you amnesty?"
