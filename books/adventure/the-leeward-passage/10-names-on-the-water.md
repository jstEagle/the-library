## 10. Names on the Water

The hearings were held in the provincial capital, in a hall built for shipping disputes and too small for what they'd become. By the third week there were people standing along the back wall, and a line outside that wrapped around the fountain, and vendors selling roasted plantains to the crowd as though truth were a festival.

Rosalind watched most of it from the gallery, in a gray dress she'd bought for the occasion and hated immediately. She sat beside Amara Osei, who took notes even when nothing was being said, and beside Tomás, the wounded surveyor, whose arm had healed crooked and who had decided this was fine, since it gave him an excuse to rest it on every railing in the building.

On the fourth day, Corrigan testified.

She had wondered how it would feel, seeing him again under fluorescent light instead of river glare. Smaller, was the answer. He wore a suit that fit him badly now, as if he'd lost weight in the months since the floodwater spat him out, and his voice had the flat, careful quality of a man reading from a script he'd written himself and no longer believed.

He did not confess, exactly. He *unraveled*. That was the phrase the delta journalist used — the one Rosalind had given the plates to at dawn, standing knee-deep in marsh water with her boat half-sunk behind her. The journalist's name was Beatriz Salgado, and she had spent twenty years being told her stories were too local, and she had printed this one anyway, all nine thousand words of it, with the sonar maps reproduced across two full pages like the ribs of something enormous surfacing.

Corrigan unraveled the way old rope does: strand by strand, each one fraying separately until the whole load-bearing lie simply parted. The dam. The '61 order. The valuation scandal underneath San Esteban that had made drowning the town cheaper than compensating it. And then, when the committee chairman asked him whether he had known that the engineer responsible for the gates had refused the order —

"I knew," Corrigan said, and looked, for one moment, directly up at the gallery.

Not at Osei. Not at the chairman.

At her.

* *

Afterward, in the corridor that smelled of floor wax and rain coming off the plaza, he waited for her by the window. She could have walked past. She considered it. But there is a particular kind of conversation you can only have with a man you have pulled out of a river, and she found she wanted to have it.

"You didn't have to do that," he said. "The flood. You could have let me go down."

"Yes."

"They'll give me years for this."

"Fewer than my family got."

He flinched as if she'd struck him, and she supposed she had, gently, with the flat of her hand. He looked out the window at the traffic, at the ordinary city going about its ordinary morning, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter.

"Your father," he said. "Emiliano Villalobos. I read his file after. All of it. He wrote letters, you know. For eleven months before the flood he wrote to the ministry warning them the gates were failing, and every letter came back stamped and unopened. They kept them. They're evidence now." He turned to look at her. "I thought you should know your father was right, on paper, the whole time. Someone will want to tell you that properly, someday, so I'm telling you first."

Rosalind stood very still. Somewhere below, a bus sighed at its stop.

"He named you," Corrigan went on. "In the last letter. 'My daughter watches the water better than any gauge I own.' I didn't understand that sentence until the narrows." He almost smiled; it was a terrible, ruined thing of a smile. "You run a channel blind that my pilots won't take with charts."

"The river remembers me," she said. "It's the only witness who never needed convincing."

She left him at the window. She did not shake his hand, and she did not hate him anymore either, which surprised her more. Hate, she was learning, was just another kind of ballast, and she was done carrying weight that wasn't hers.

* *

The renaming happened on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were cheap at the yard and Captain Villalobos had never been a superstitious woman about days, only about rivers.

Tomás came with his crooked arm and a bottle of something amber that he claimed was rum and everyone pretended to believe. Osei came with chalk dust still on her cuffs from lecturing that morning — they'd made her a professor of applied hydrography, which she described as "the government finally paying me to say what I said for free while being shot at." A handful of others came too: Beatriz Salgado with her notebook; the junior surveyor, Mateo, who had stopped trembling somewhere around the second hearing and started growing a beard of tremendous ambition; and a dozen strangers from the capital who had read the story and simply showed up, the way people do when a thing belongs to them now.

The old name had been sanded off the transom weeks ago. *Leeward Passage* — the borrowed name for the borrowed life, gone the way of the alias, the dyed hair, the careful flat vowels. In its place, fresh white paint, still tacky at the edges:

**SAN ESTEBAN**

"It's not unlucky?" Mateo asked. "Naming a boat after a drowned town?"

"She wasn't drowned," Rosalind said. "She was buried. There's a difference, and the difference is a crime, and crimes can be answered." She set her palm against the paint. "Also, boats come back. It's the whole point of them."

Osei broke the bottle against the hull — gently, the way you'd tap a shoulder — and the crowd made the sound crowds make, and Rosalind felt something in her chest unlock that had been rusted shut for thirty years.

Her father's compass rode in her breast pocket, as it always did. Its needle swung once, settled, and pointed downriver, toward home, which was also away from home, which on a river is the same direction.

* *

They ran her past the drowned town in October, when the water was low and honest.

It was just her aboard — she'd wanted it that way, and the others, bless them, had understood without being told. The channel into the old basin was marked on no chart but hers, the one drawn in pencil inside her head by a twelve-year-old girl who had memorized the rooftops before the water took them.

San Esteban rose around her the way it always did in low water: the church tower first, leaning, crowned with tenacious green; then the ridge of the plaza fountain; then, if the light was right, the long dark spine of the market street where her mother had sold cloth and argued cheerfully about prices. Fish moved through the windows of houses. Sunlight came down in columns and lay along the streets like the memory of afternoon.

Rosalind idled the engine and let the current carry her, hands off the wheel, the way her father used to stand.

"I brought the name back," she said aloud, to the tower, to the street, to whoever was listening down there in the green. "I know it took me thirty years. I'm told rivers don't keep time."

A bird — a kingfisher, electric blue — lifted off the church belfry and flew ahead of her, downstream, as if showing the way.

She laughed, and then she cried, briefly and without shame, and then she wiped her face on her sleeve because captains are practical people, and she opened the throttle, and the *San Esteban* rose onto plane and ran the old basin fast enough to throw spray over the fountain, christening it a second time.

* *

There was a letter waiting when she got back to the dock. It was from the ministry — a real letter, on real paper, signed by a deputy minister whose name she would forget by dinner.

Inside was an apology, carefully worded, legally hedged, worth exactly the paper it was printed on. And beneath it, folded separately, was something else: a photocopy of her father's final letter, the eleventh one, the one that had come back stamped and unopened. The ministry was returning it. *For closure*, the covering note said.

Rosalind read her father's handwriting at the galley table with the lamp lit, and found the sentence Corrigan had quoted, and read it a dozen times.

*My daughter watches the water better than any gauge I own.*

"Well," she said to the empty cabin, "he wasn't wrong."

She pinned the letter above the chart table, next to the compass, where the whole river could see it.

* *

Spring came early that year, and with it, work — good work, honest work, the kind that pays in money instead of silence. Osei was organizing a proper survey of the basin, mapping the town street by street, and wanted Rosalind as pilot. Hearings continued; there would be years of them; reparations were being discussed in rooms where Rosalind's name now appeared in the minutes, spelled correctly.

And then, on an ordinary morning in March, with the paint barely dry and the season's first cargo loaded — fuel drums, flour, a piano of all things, bound for a mission upstream that had heard the river was safe again — a girl came running down the dock.

Fifteen, maybe sixteen, barefoot, breathless, with the particular urgency of someone who has been sent by adults who are too frightened to come themselves.

"Captain," she gasped. "Captain Villalobos — they said ask for you by that name —"

Rosalind set down the mooring line. Hearing it spoken by a stranger, casually, as if it had always been hers, still landed like a blow, a sweet one.

"What is it?"

"There's a camp up past the Narrows," the girl said. "New people. Surveyors, they say, but they fly no flag and they pay no tolls, and they've got equipment nobody recognizes, and—" she swallowed— "and last night my brother saw lights on the water where the old dam used to be. Lights, captain. Underneath."

Rosalind looked upriver, where the morning mist was lifting off the bends, and felt the old familiar pull in her chest — not dread this time, or not only. Curiosity. Her father's compass, warm in her pocket, pointed straight at it.

"Well," she said, stepping aboard. "Then we'd best go see what the river's found this time."
