## 1. The Weight of Miles

Edmund Yarrow measured his life in miles the way other men measured it in years. Fifteen thousand, four hundred and six — that was the number he carried into the Leeward Range this season, tallied in a greasy notebook he kept in the inside pocket of his shell jacket, where no client would ever think to look. He did not count birthdays. He did not count anniversaries, because there had been none worth the ink. He counted ground covered: the switchbacks above Kettle Pass, the moraine fields below the Serrac icefall, the long grey trudge along the leeward flank where the wind never quite stopped talking to itself.

Fifteen years since the government had taken his name away. Fifteen years since the inquiry had concluded — with the tidy finality of men who had already written their report before the hearings began — that Edmund Yarrow had led his party into hazard through negligence, that the death of nine climbers on the Col de Vire was attributable to guide error, and that his certification was revoked effective immediately. They had not used the word *blacklisted*. Blacklisting implied someone had made a list. What they had done was quieter than that: they had simply let his name go stale, like milk left out, until every outfitter in three provinces had heard the story secondhand, thirdhand, tenth-hand, each retelling adding a fresh coat of rot. *The one who walked them off the mountain.* *The one who came back alone.*

He had come back alone because everyone else was dead. But grief does not argue well in a hearing room, and neither does a man with mud on his boots and nothing in his pockets but a rope he'd cut to save himself.

So now he guided the wealthy instead of the serious — bankers and surgeons and software men who wanted the Leeward Range the way they wanted a vintage watch: as proof they could afford difficulty without being harmed by it. They paid well. They complained about freeze-dried coffee. They took photographs of themselves standing on summits they had been hauled up, and Edmund stood slightly outside the frame, as he always did, counting.

This season's party was four of them, bound for the high plateau via the Thorne Creek approach, and by the fourth day Edmund had added thirty-one miles to the ledger and lost whatever patience he'd started with. The clients slept at the Thorne Creek lodge their first night back down — a low timber building with a wood stove, a liquor shelf, and a radio that crackled more than it spoke. Edmund ate in the kitchen with the cook, as was his habit. Guides ate apart. It was one of the few rules of the trade he still honored.

He was on his second cup of tea when the man came in out of the rain.

Edmund noticed the boots first. That was how you read people in the mountains — feet before faces. These were city boots, expensive, leather gone soft from disuse, but the man wore them with the deliberate ease of someone who had been told he looked outdoorsy and believed it. His coat was newer than any garment had a right to be within fifty miles of a glacier. He stood in the doorway a moment, letting the water run off him, and then crossed the room directly to Edmund's table, which told Edmund everything else he needed to know. Nobody comes straight across a lodge to a stranger's table unless they've been watching him through glass.

"Yarrow." Not a question. The man sat down uninvited, set a leather folder on the table between them, and kept two fingers on it the way a gambler keeps fingers on his cards. "Corben Ashe. Provincial Liaison Office."

There was no such office. Or rather, there was, but it existed on letterhead only, a drawer in some ministry where inconvenient terrain got filed. Edmund had heard the name whispered once or twice over the years, always by men who went quiet afterward. He turned his cup slowly on its saucer.

"We don't have business," he said.

"We might." Ashe smiled. He had the kind of face that had learned smiling as a professional skill, the way a butcher learns sharpening. "You're guiding the Hollis party. Four clients, Thorne Creek to the plateau. I watched your route plan on the board." He tapped the folder once. "Impressive mileage, Mr. Yarrow. You must know every drainage in the western range by now."

"I know what guides know."

"You know more than that." Ashe leaned forward. Up close he smelled of wet wool and something underneath it, antiseptic and cold, like a clinic. "Fifteen years ago you knew the Col de Vire better than the men who died on it. That's why they sent you up there in the first place. And that's why we're here tonight."

Edmund drank his tea. Outside, the rain worked at the roof with patient fingers. In the main room he could hear his clients laughing at something, the bright careless laughter of people whose worst weather was financial.

"A survey team went missing eleven days ago," Ashe said. "Four personnel. Oduya, Brandt, Kessler, and Dr. Ilves, the senior geologist. Last check-in was from a camp below the Meridian Glacier, coordinates verified, all systems nominal. Then nothing. No distress call, no movement on satellite, no tracks leaving camp when the weather window opened on day six." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Aerial reconnaissance can't get under the cloud deck. Ground teams are—" he spread his hands, "—unavailable."

"Unavailable," Edmund repeated. "Meaning what? Meaning nobody will go?"

"Meaning nobody is *cleared* to go." Ashe's voice dropped half a register, and Edmund heard the thing beneath the words, the thing that had been underneath every government sentence he'd ever heard. "The upper Meridian basin falls inside a restricted survey zone. Permits suspended. Your name came up."

"My name doesn't come up," Edmund said. "That's the point of what you people did to my name."

Ashe didn't flinch. If anything he looked pleased, as though Edmund had recited a line correctly. "Which is precisely why it works. A restricted zone requires a cleared individual. The clearance list has exactly one entry, and it isn't there because anyone trusts you. It's there because fifteen years ago, when the Ministry needed someone deniable to run a line up the Col de Vire, they put your name on a document, and documents don't expire, Mr. Yarrow. They just wait." He slid the folder an inch across the table. "You're the last name on a list nobody remembers writing. Congratulations. You're cleared."

Edmund looked at the folder and did not touch it. "Find another guide."

"There is no other guide. There is no other *name*." Ashe said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "And there is no other offer. Full reinstatement. Certification restored, record expunged, the Col de Vire finding formally reopened and revised. You'd guide again. Real parties. Real mountains. Your name cleaned down to the bone."

The kitchen was very quiet. Edmund became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and traitorous, quickening at the word *reinstated* the way a dog's ears lift at its own name after years of neglect. He hated that. He hated that after fifteen years of measuring his life in miles because miles asked nothing of him, some man with city boots could walk in out of the rain and offer him back the thing he'd stopped letting himself want.

"What's the catch," Edmund said. "There's always a catch. With your kind the catch is usually the whole arrangement wearing a hat."

"The catch," Ashe said, "is discretion. The Meridian basin is closed for reasons that predate your involvement and will outlast it. You find the team. You assess their condition. You bring them down if they can be brought down." He stood, buttoning his absurd coat, and left the folder on the table. "You report what you find to me. To me personally. Not to the press, not to the families, not to the ranger stations. Me."

"And if I say no?"

Ashe paused at the door, rain-light silvering his shoulders. For a moment the professional smile was gone and something older showed underneath — something tired, almost regretful, the expression of a man doing arithmetic he'd done too many times.

"Then the list gets shorter," he said. "It always gets shorter, Mr. Yarrow. That's what lists are for."

The door swung shut behind him. Edmund sat a long while with the folder untouched, steam rising off his forgotten tea, listening to the rain and the distant laughter of his clients — people who trusted him, in their shallow and expensive way, to keep them alive through tomorrow.

At last he opened the folder. Inside: a map of the upper Meridian basin with a red circle drawn around a campsite; a photograph of four faces, three strangers and one woman whose eyes looked directly into the camera as though she already suspected what the lens was for; and a single typed sheet bearing the seal of an office that did not exist, authorizing an expedition that had not been requested, for a man whom no one trusted, into country no one was meant to see.

Edmund read it twice. Then he took out his greasy notebook, turned past fifteen years of miles, and wrote on a clean page:

*Meridian basin. 62 miles in.*

Below it, after a moment, he wrote: *Reason unknown.*

He closed the book. Somewhere above the cloud deck, under the Meridian Glacier, four people were either dead or hiding, and Edmund Yarrow — who had spent fifteen years learning that mountains keep no secrets, only evidence — pulled his boots back on and went to tell his clients the trip was cancelled.

The mountain, he thought, had been waiting for him long enough.
