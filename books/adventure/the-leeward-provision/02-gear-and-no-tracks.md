## 2. Gear and No Tracks

Edmund Yarrow had spent fifteen years learning to read a camp the way other men read a face, and the face of this one was wrong.

He came up on it from the east, out of the long shadow of the Col de Serré, where the wind funneled through the notch like water through a throat and made even the ravens fly sideways. The survey team's site sat in a shallow bowl below the ridge line — sheltered, sensible, chosen by someone who understood mountains. Four dome tents, olive-drab and government-issue, staked in a neat crescent with their backs to the prevailing weather. A cook shelter strung between two ice axes. A solar panel angled at the pale afternoon sun.

Everything in its place. That was the first wrongness. In Edmund's experience, camps that ended badly did not end neatly. Panic scattered things. Cold made people abandon gear they should have kept. Fear left a signature — a slashed tent wall, a dropped glove, a trail of small desperate choices written across the snow. This camp had no signature at all. It had been *tidied*.

He stood at the edge of the bowl for a full minute before entering, letting his eyes do the walking. Fifteen years of guiding wealthy clients through the Leeward Range had taught him patience as a survival trait; you did not step into a room until you'd read what the room was telling you. The snow around the tents was wind-scoured but unbroken. No tracks led out. None led in, either, except the faint ghost of their own arrival days ago, already half-erased, and his own fresh prints behind him like a sentence he couldn't take back.

He unslung his pack and went in.

The nearest tent's zipper ran smooth when he tested it. Inside: sleeping bags rolled tight, foam pads squared, a headlamp clipped to the pole in exactly the position of someone who expected to come back to it. He opened the second tent and found the same grammar repeated — order, intention, departure without haste. On the floor of the third lay a paperback novel, spine cracked at the midpoint, a pencil marking the page. Nobody fleeing for their life brought a book halfway through and left it.

The cook shelter told him more. Under the tarp, the stove sat cold but clean, its fuel bottle full to the shoulder. Food stores were stacked in crates against the rock wall: ration packs, chocolate, a bag of dried apples clipped shut against scavenging birds. Enough for six people for ten days, by his rough count. Untouched. Beside the stove, a kettle. Beside the kettle, four enamel mugs arranged mouth-down on a flat stone, the way careful people leave them so nothing drifts inside.

Four mugs. The liaison's file said five missing.

Edmund crouched over that arithmetic for a long time, breath steaming, gloved fingers hovering above the mugs without touching them. Either one of them had already gone somewhere — ahead, or under the snow — or the fifth had never drunk from these cups at all. He found no answer in the camp. He found something else instead, half-buried in the scree at the bowl's edge where the latrine trench had been filled and marked with a neat cairn of stones: a boot.

One boot, size eleven, laces tucked inside it with a precision that looked almost ceremonial. He turned it over. The sole was worn at the ball of the foot, the heel scuffed from crampon work. It was not abandoned. Abandoned boots lay on their sides, half-swallowed by drifts. This one had been *placed*, upright, stone-weighted against the wind, like a grave marker for a man who wasn't dead.

He looked up then, really looked, at the ground leading away from the camp — north toward the glacier tongue, south toward the scree slopes, west into the teeth of the range. Nothing. Wind had softened everything, but wind didn't erase five sets of boot prints in three days unless there had been no boots making them. And here was a boot, empty, pointing north like a compass needle some dying hand had set.

*They walked out barefoot,* he thought, and the thought was so absurd he almost laughed, and then he stopped laughing because he checked the snow again, closer, kneeling, and saw what he'd missed: a scatter of shallow dimples beyond the camp's perimeter, spaced too far apart for footprints, too regular for rockfall. Crampon marks. Someone had crossed the bare ground on iron, leaving almost nothing, moving fast, moving deliberately — and every mark pointed north, toward the glacier.

The missing team hadn't been taken. They had packed nothing, eaten nothing, taken no food, left their boots, and walked onto the ice.

*The missing don't want to be found.*

The words arrived in him not as thought but as temperature — a cold that started in the gut and climbed. Fifteen years ago he had carried a boy down off the North Arête with a broken back, and the boy had died anyway, and the government men who debriefed him had asked questions that weren't about the rescue at all, and after that his name had become a thing stamped *unreliable* in every office that mattered. He knew what it meant when people in the high country stopped behaving like people. It meant someone had taught them fear.

He searched the camp properly then, methodically, the way he'd once searched avalanche debris for heartbeats. In the fourth tent, taped beneath the sleeping platform — a hiding place chosen by someone who thought in layers, who assumed any searcher would check the obvious places first — he found a field journal. Water-stamped cover, PROPERTY OF THE LEWARD TERRITORIAL SURVEY. Most of the pages had been torn out, ragged stubs remaining, but near the back a single sheet survived, folded twice, and he unfolded it under the tarp with hands that were steadier than he expected them to be.

* * *

**From the field journal of Maren Oduya, Survey Team Lead — recovered fragment, undated:**

*Day 9. We voted last night. Five for, none against, Tomas abstaining because he says his hands shake too badly to hold a pen and a conscience at the same time. I record it here only because someone must write down what we decided, and if this page survives me, let it say we were not fools and not cowards.*

*We are not lost. Write that plainly, whoever reads this. We walked away. There is a difference between being missing and choosing to disappear, and the difference is the whole point.*

*What we pulled from the ice on Day 6 cannot go back down the mountain in our pockets. We know now why the liaison's earlier expeditions came home short-handed. We know what "survey" means when the survey is paid for by the same ministry that signed the Harrow Vale compensation orders. The cores don't lie. Charges leave signatures in the ice, and the glacier has kept theirs for eleven years like a woman keeping letters she means to use someday.*

*So we stay. Above the cloud, in the cold, where their helicopters can't loiter and their searchers will find an empty camp and draw the easy conclusion. Let them think the mountain took us. The mountain is the safest liar we have.*

*If a searcher comes — and one will come, because they always send one man they can disown — we will watch him longer than he watches us. If he comes for the ministry, we stay ghosts. If he comes for us — truly for us — then perhaps the mountain sent him, and the mountain has better handwriting than any of us.*

*Boots stay behind. A camp without footprints tells a truer story than a camp with lies in it.*

*M.O.*

* * *

Edmund read it twice. Then a third time, slower, while the light went bronze on the western faces and the temperature began its evening fall, the kind of cold that got into metal and made zippers brittle and turned mistakes permanent.

*A searcher they can disown.* He heard the liaison's voice from the lodge, smooth as poured concrete: *Your name is the only one cleared to enter the zone, Yarrow. Cleared by us. You understand what that means.* At the time he'd taken it for bureaucratic arrogance. Now it sounded like exactly what it was — a man explaining, in plain language, that Edmund was expendable and deniable both, the perfect instrument for a job whose outcome could be buried as easily as the valley had been.

And they had watched him come up the col. He was certain of it now, certain the way you're certain of eyes on your neck. Somewhere above the cloud, five people who had voted to become ghosts were deciding whether he was the ministry's man or the mountain's.

He repacked the journal page inside his jacket, next to his skin, where paper stayed dry and where a man kept the things he wasn't ready to admit he believed in. He left the camp as he'd found it — mugs mouth-down, boot standing its lonely watch — because destroying their story would tell them everything about him, and he wasn't yet sure which story he wanted to tell.

Then he turned north, toward the glacier tongue, toward the blue-black shadow at its base where meltwater went underground and the ice kept its own counsel. The wind had risen, combing spindrift off the ridges in long white banners, and somewhere under that moving white were five people betting their lives that no one would come looking.

One of them was wrong. One of them was already climbing.

He reached the first pressure ridge as the sun dropped behind the Serré massif and the world went the color of old pewter. Below him, at the glacier's root, he could see it — a seam in the ice, a darkness where darkness shouldn't be, breathing out a faint shimmer of warmer air that frosted and vanished at the lip of a crevasse wide enough to swallow a tent.

Meltwater channels. His hunch, and the dead geologist's likely door.

Edmund checked his headlamp, checked his anchors, and started down into the blue.
