## 3. What the Glacier Keeps

The meltwater told him before anything else did.

Edmund had learned to read water the way other men read faces, and the stream running out of the glacier's toe was lying. It ran too clear for July, too cold, too steady — a pulse without variation, the kind of flow that came not from surface melt but from a reservoir held somewhere in the dark interior of the ice. And it emerged from a seam on the *leeward* side of the glacier's snout, the shaded face, where the sun's work was slowest and the ice kept its secrets longest.

He crouched at the outflow and let his bare fingers break the surface. The cold bit down to the bone in seconds, honest as a lie detector. He watched the water's clarity — no rock flour, no grit, nothing ground from the glacier's belly. This water hadn't traveled. It had been waiting.

"Someone's dammed you," he murmured, "or you're coming from somewhere the sun doesn't reach."

He'd spent two days circling the last camp, fighting the same conclusion over and over. Tents pitched with care. Food cached, not abandoned. Boots gone — every pair — which meant the team had walked out under their own power, deliberately, into terrain that would kill an unshod goat. No tracks because they'd wanted no tracks. Fifteen years ago he'd have called it desertion. Now he recognized it for what it was: the discipline of people who had decided that being found was more dangerous than being lost.

The question that had kept him awake in his bivouac, staring at the stars through the mesh of his hood, was simpler and worse: *found by whom?*

He followed the stream upstream along the glacier's flank, moving on the old lateral moraine where the rock was stable, testing each step with his pole. The seam widened into a slot — a blue-black fissure in the ice wall, breathing cold air outward with a faint, rhythmic exhalation, like something sleeping. Air moving *out* meant air moving *in* somewhere else. A cave system. A living one.

He should have marked it, reported it, turned back. That was the protocol he'd been paid to follow, the protocol of a man who did what he was told and asked nothing except to be left alone afterward. Instead he clipped his headlamp, checked the knife at his belt out of fifteen years of habit, and went in sideways through the slot, shoulders scraping ice that wept cold down his collar.

* * *

The dark inside was total and then, gradually, not. His lamp found walls of ancient blue ice veined with ash layers — the mountain's own diary, one grey line for every eruption or fire or buried century. The passage descended in a spiral, the stream chattering somewhere below him, and the air grew warmer by degrees, which was wrong, which meant ventilation, which meant an opening to the outside somewhere beyond the glacier's mass.

Then the light changed ahead of him. Not daylight. Lamplight, low and amber, bleeding around a bend in the corridor.

Edmund killed his own lamp and stood still, listening. Voices — no. One voice, low, reading aloud? Numbers. Someone reciting numbers into the dark like a prayer. And beneath it, a wet, labored breathing that he knew from high camps and bad seasons, the sound of lungs filling with fluid.

He could have withdrawn. He thought about it honestly, standing there in the cold with his hand on his knife. Whatever was in this cave, it wasn't a rescue anymore; it was a door, and doors opened both ways. But his feet were already moving, because fifteen years of distance had never actually taught him how to walk away.

He rounded the bend with his lamp raised and his free hand open and empty.

"Easy," he said. "I'm alone."

The cave chamber opened around him like the inside of a throat — thirty meters across, ceiling lost in black above, the floor a terrace of gravel beside the buried stream. Four figures. Two men huddled near a camp stove, one of them half-rising with an ice axe held wrong, grip too high, terror in every line of him. A woman sitting against the ice wall with a rifle across her knees — not aimed, but *available*, which was its own kind of answer. She was gaunt, brown-skinned, frost-nipped at the cheekbones, with eyes that assessed him the way he assessed weather: for what it was going to do next.

And behind them, wrapped in every layer they owned, a fifth shape that didn't move except to breathe — that terrible, tidal breathing.

"You're Yarrow," the woman said. Not a question.

"That's a problem, then."

"It's a problem." She didn't lift the rifle. "Maren Oduya. Survey team lead. The man dying is Dr. Ilves, our geologist. The two who look like rabbits are Okonkwo and Reyes, technicians." The rabbit with the axe lowered it slowly, ashamed. "We voted on whether to shoot you if you found us. It was close."

"I'm flattered."

"Don't be. We voted before we knew you'd come alone." Her gaze went past him, to the tunnel mouth, counting the darkness for the men who might follow. "How did you find us?"

"The water. It was too clean. Somebody built a check-dam of scree to pool the outflow and mask the flow rate." He nodded toward the dark downstream. "And the air moves. You've got a chimney open somewhere above the bergschrund."

Something flickered across her face — respect, quickly suppressed, like a match cupped against wind. "Ilves said you would. He said if anyone came, it would be Yarrow, because you know this ice better than the people who fired you from it." She paused. "He wants to talk to you. He doesn't have much talk left."

She rose, slung the rifle, and led him across the gravel to the bundled shape. Up close, Dr. Ilves was a ruin — a big man gone hollow, skin waxy and grey-blue, lips cracked, each breath dragging through him like a saw through green wood. High-altitude pulmonary edema, Edmund guessed, complicated by whatever had brought him here half-dead already. Weeks of this. Weeks of lying in the dark reciting numbers.

But the old man's eyes were clear. Clearer than anyone's Edmund had seen in years.

"Yarrow." The voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "Sit. My legs are done but my mouth works fine."

Edmund sat on the gravel, uninvited by courtesy, and waited.

"They sent you to find us." Not a question either. These people dealt only in certainties. "Voss sent you. Tell me what he promised you."

"My name cleared. Back on the roster. Money I don't need."

"And your reputation. Don't leave that out. Voss is good at finding what a man is missing." Ilves coughed, a long, tearing effort, and Maren moved forward with a cloth, and Edmund watched her tenderness and understood it was the most dangerous thing in the room. "Fifteen years ago," Ilves went on, "you guided a government survey into the upper Leeward. You were the only one who objected to the charge pattern. You said the snowpack above Harrow Vale couldn't take it. They did it anyway. The avalanche came down. Three hundred and eleven people. And when the inquiry started asking questions, someone needed a scapegoat with mountain credentials and no political family. So they made the guide the story. *Reckless local guide triggers disaster.* You were perfect. You were designed."

The cave was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere above, the glacier groaned through its thousand-year settlement, patient as geology.

"I know what happened to me," Edmund said finally. His voice came out flat, which cost him more than shaking would have. "I've had fifteen years to memorize it."

"You don't know all of it." Ilves fumbled beneath his layers and produced a waterproof case, and Maren took it from his hands and opened it with the care of a woman handling relics. Inside: core samples sealed in tubes, their labels written in a precise, dying hand. Photographs printed on field paper. Seismic traces. "Our survey wasn't a survey. It was a retrieval. The government has known for fifteen years that the charges they set above Harrow Vale left signatures in the ice — detonation residue, fracture patterns, everything a real investigation would need. Every few years they send a team up to find and destroy the evidence. And every team that found too much and hesitated had an accident on descent. Crevasse fall. Rockfall. Weather." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Edmund had ever seen, a smile with no floor under it. "We found everything. All of it. And then we read the file on the previous teams, which our own liaison helpfully provided, thinking we wouldn't understand it. And so we chose to disappear instead of report. Because reporting means descending, and descending means meeting them on the ice, where accidents happen."

Maren spoke from beside him, quiet. "We've been down here six weeks. We ate the dogs first. Then most of the food. Ilves is dying because he gave his oxygen and his ration share to keep the rest of us strong enough to carry this out." She touched the case. "This is three hundred and eleven people, Mr. Yarrow. This is your name, given back. And the moment we start down off this mountain with it, we become five witnesses walking into a kill zone."

Edmund looked at the core samples. At the photographs — the fracture fans radiating from charge points, unmistakable, deliberate, the mountain's wound mapped in forensic detail. Fifteen years he had carried the word *reckless* like a stone sewn into his chest, and here it lay, cut out of the ice, dated and labeled, proof that he had been right — that he had *objected*, that his objection was recorded, that they had buried it along with the valley.

He felt grief and fury arrive together, fused at the root, indistinguishable, and understood distantly that this was what purpose felt like when it finally came: not clean, not noble. Just heavy, and his.

"Why tell me?" he said. "You could keep hiding until the season turns. Wait us out."

"Because Ilves is dying," Maren said, "and dead men can't testify. Because we can't stay — the food's gone in days. And because—" she stopped, and for the first time she looked uncertain, this woman with a rifle and a vote on his life. "Because Voss chose you. And we need to know why. What does a man like you cost, Mr. Yarrow? Everyone has a price. We need to know yours before we trust you with the only thing we have."

Edmund was silent a long moment. The stream muttered past in its channel of ice. Above them, faint through a hundred meters of glacier, came a sound none of them wanted to hear: the flat, mechanical thrum of helicopter blades, sweeping the ridge, patient as a searchlight.

He looked at the dying geologist, at the case in Maren's hands, at the two frightened technicians who had eaten dog meat and drunk meltwater for six weeks rather than give up three hundred and eleven names.

"I'll tell you my price," Edmund said. "It's not money, and it's not my name back. My price is that nobody else dies carrying this but me, if it comes to that." He stood, brushing the gravel from his knees, and nodded upward, toward the sound of the blades. "Now — how fast can you move, and how well do you know the route Voss thinks you'd take? Because we're going to go a different way. And we need to move tonight."

Maren looked at him for a long moment, weighing him still, the rifle's sling loose in her grip. Then, quietly, to the others:

"Pack everything. He's either the best thing that's happened to us—"

"—or the trap working perfectly," finished the technician Okonkwo, with a shaky laugh that nobody joined.

Edmund checked his watch against the rhythm of the rotors overhead, doing arithmetic of shadow and distance, and began, in a low voice, to describe the corridor of crevasses that no map in Voss's office had ever charted — the long white corridor that ran down the mountain's blind side, through country only he had ever crossed alive.

Outside, the light was failing. The storm, when he tasted the wind at the cave mouth, was two days out at most.

They would need every hour of it.
