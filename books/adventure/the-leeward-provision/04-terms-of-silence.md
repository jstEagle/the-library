## 4. Terms of Silence

The cave breathed cold the way a church breathes incense — slowly, and with the sense that something enormous was listening.

Maren Oduya sat with her back against the ice wall and watched Edmund Yarrow across the blue light of the carbide lamp. He had been in the cave for six hours now. Long enough for the shock to wear off his face. Long enough for him to have seen the core samples laid out on the tarp like relics, the photographs fanned in their oilcloth sleeves, the geologist Halvard lying grey-lipped in his sleeping bag with the sound of water working through his chest. Long enough, she thought, for a man to decide what he was worth.

He was worth something. That was the problem. Everyone was worth something, and the government had learned long ago how to read price tags.

"You keep looking at me," Edmund said. His voice was low, pitched under the drip of meltwater. "Like I'm an invoice."

"I'm trying to work out your number."

A ghost of a smile moved through his beard. "And?"

"Too high," she said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

Eleven days earlier — though it felt like a season — they had voted by lamplight in this same cave, with Halvard already coughing blood into a rag and the glacier groaning overhead like a ship in ballast.

The vote had not been close. It had been terrible anyway.

Maren had argued first for going down. Report it. Photographs, cores, her own field notes — take it all to the provincial office, to the press if necessary, let the machinery do what machinery did. She had believed in machinery then. She had been trained to believe in it; surveying was nothing but faith that measurement would outlast men.

It was Tomas, the senior technician, who had laid out the arithmetic. Not loudly. He had done it the way he did everything, flat and precise, unrolling the record of the liaison's previous expeditions across the tarp beside the evidence as if comparing two kinds of cargo.

"Kessler's team, four years ago. Found seismograph anomalies in the north passes. All five died in a cornice collapse. The report called it weather." He tapped the page. "Brandt's crew, two years back. Water samples from the Harrow Vale watershed. Their raft swamped on a river rated class two. Both drowned were strong swimmers." Tap. "Last year, a single ranger, Osei, filed an informal complaint about blasting permits in a restricted zone. Fell while soloing a route he'd done eleven times."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere deep in the ice, a crack traveled and settled, the glacier clearing its throat.

"You're saying coincidence," Maren had said.

"I'm saying pattern," Tomas said. "I don't believe in patterns. I believe in them being arranged."

Halvard had spoken last, which was unlike him; he was a man who spoke first and apologized after. But he was dying even then, though he hadn't told them yet, and dying men spend words carefully. "They didn't just bury Harrow Vale," he said. "They buried it twice. Once with charges, once with paperwork. And now we're standing on the second grave, holding shovels." He looked around the circle of faces. "If we go down and hand this over, we become a line item. If we stay dead, we stay dangerous."

"We're not dead," Priya, the younger technician, said quietly.

"No," Halvard agreed. "But we can be. That's the one card we hold that they can't take. As long as nobody knows where we are, we're a rumor. The moment we surface, we're a target. So we choose: rumor or target."

Maren had wanted to scream at the logic of it. Instead she had asked for her journal and written the tally herself, because someone had to make the cowardice official: four votes to vanish, hers included, cast with her teeth shut so hard her jaw ached for two days afterward.

She had told herself it was strategy. She was still telling herself that. But some nights, listening to Halvard breathe, she understood they had done something else entirely. They had chosen to let the valley stay buried a little longer so that they could live to dig it up properly. Whether that was courage or arithmetic, she could no longer say. Probably it was both. Probably everything worst in the world was both.

* * *

Now she studied Edmund Yarrow by lamplight and tried to fit him into the ledger.

She knew his file. Of course she knew his file; before the expedition she had read everything the ministry would give her about the Leeward Range, and half of what it wouldn't. Fifteen years ago, a rescue on the western face. A party of climbers officially lost to avalanche, unofficially — according to three pages that had been redacted so thoroughly the paper wore thin — something else. Yarrow had come down alone with a survivor and a story that contradicted the official one. The survivor had recanted. Yarrow had been blacklisted for "gross negligence in the field," which was the ministry's way of hanging a bell around a man's neck so that every door he approached rang warning.

She had assumed, reading it, that he was guilty of something. Now she wasn't sure. Now she suspected the blacklist itself was part of the architecture — a wall built in advance against exactly the kind of man who might one day climb over it.

Which made him either their best ally or their most valuable asset to destroy.

"Tell me about the rescue," she said.

Edmund didn't move. In the lamplight his face was all planes and patience, a face carved by weather into something economical. "No."

"It matters."

"It doesn't matter to you whether I tell you. It matters whether I'd tell *them*. And the answer's the same either way." He turned his hands palm-up, calloused, scarred along the thumbs where rope had bitten for decades. "You want to know if I can be bought. Ask me directly. I've had fifteen years of people asking sideways."

The directness unsettled her more than evasion would have. She made herself match it. "Can you?"

"I don't know." He said it without shame, which was somehow worse and better at once. "Fifteen years ago, no. Five years ago, I'd have said no faster. Now?" He looked at Halvard's sleeping bag, at the shallow lift of it. "There's a version of me that takes reinstatement and tells himself the mountain needs guides more than it needs martyrs. I've met that version. He lives in my head and pays rent in excuses. So I won't swear to you. Swearing is cheap and I'm expensive."

Priya stirred by the radio, alarmed, but Maren lifted a hand. This was the conversation. This had always been the conversation.

"Then what good are you?" she asked.

"The good kind of dangerous," Edmund said. "The kind that knows it might turn. You watch me like I'm a knife. Fine. A knife that admits it's a knife is safer than a spoon that lies." He leaned forward, and the lamplight climbed his face. "Here's what I'll give you instead of an oath. Facts. They offered me this job because I'm the only guide cleared for the restricted zone — meaning the only guide they thought they controlled. They sent me in expecting to find bodies or silence. I found neither. That makes me a loose end, same as you. Loose ends get tidied. So whatever my price was last week, the market's changed." He paused. "Also: I know a way off this ice that isn't on any map they'll be carrying. That's not loyalty. That's just geography. But geography will keep you alive longer than loyalty does."

Maren almost laughed. Almost. It was the most honest sales pitch she'd ever heard, and honesty from a desperate man was its own kind of weapon.

"And when the liaison offers you your old life back?" she said. "Because he will. He'll have worked out your number the way I just did, except he'll have a budget."

Edmund was quiet a long moment. The glacier ticked and settled above them, patient as a clock.

"Then you'll have to decide fast," he said finally, "whether you trust me more than you trust him. Because one of us is going to be lying, and I'd rather it got found out on the ice than in a courtroom."

* * *

She took first watch because she couldn't sleep, and because watching was the only form of prayer she still practiced.

The others slept in the geometry of exhaustion — Priya curled against the supply crates, Tomas sitting up even in sleep, spine against stone as if rest were a vulnerability he permitted only partially. Halvard's breathing came in long torn intervals. Maren counted them the way she counted paces, because counting was how she kept the world from dissolving.

Across the cave, Edmund did not sleep either. He lay on his side facing the entrance, boots on, pack within reach of his hand, and she understood that this was simply how he lived — a man who had spent fifteen years learning that safety was a rumor told by people who slept well.

Near midnight he rose without sound and came to sit a body's length away, close enough to talk, far enough to move.

"Halvard," he said softly. "How long?"

"Days. Maybe a week. High-altitude pulmonary edema, and he hid it until we were underground." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "He won't survive the descent."

"He'll survive longer moving than staying. There's a storm coming — three days, maybe four. When it breaks, every helicopter in the province will be over this range looking for smoke, heat, movement. We want to be below the cloud deck before it clears." He glanced at her. "You voted to disappear. I'm offering you the opposite. An appearance. Loud, public, at the border, with cameras."

"That's not what we decided."

"No. What you decided was to stay a rumor until the rumor killed Halvard and froze the evidence into folklore." His voice stayed gentle, which made it worse. "Rumors don't testify, Ms. Oduya. Dead men in caves don't testify. Cores in a mail sack don't testify. Only living witnesses testify, and living witnesses require corridors, and corridors require a guide." He looked toward the cave mouth, where the night beyond the glacier was beginning, very faintly, to snow. "So here are my terms. Not silence. Speed. We move tomorrow night, ahead of the storm, down the corridor of ice the maps forgot. And you keep your knife behind your back the whole way. I'll keep mine."

Maren looked at him for a long time — this ruined, careful man the government had built a wall against and then, in its arrogance, hired to walk through it.

"All right," she said. "Terms accepted."

Above them, the glacier groaned, and somewhere far to the south, too far to hear but not too far to feel, a helicopter lifted off into the dark.
