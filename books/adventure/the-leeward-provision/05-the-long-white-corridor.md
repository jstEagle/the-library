## 5. The Long White Corridor

They moved out at 2300 hours, when the light had gone the color of old pewter and the glacier's surface stopped throwing shadows. Edmund went first, as he always did, but for the first time in fifteen years he was not alone on the rope.

He'd spent two hours teaching them to walk again — shorter steps, weight over the balls of the feet, poles planted before commitment. Maren learned fastest. She moved like someone who had spent her life reading ground, even if it was ground of a different kind: soil strata instead of seracs, core samples instead of crevasse bridges. The technicians, Okonkwo and Reyes, were slower, their city legs uncertain on the crust, but they listened and they repeated what they heard back to him, which was more than most clients ever managed.

It was Halloran who frightened him.

The geologist weighed nothing anymore. Edmund had felt that when he'd helped rig the man's harness that evening — the way the webbing cinched down through its last holes and still hung loose, the shoulder straps standing off his frame like scaffolding around a building already condemned. Halloran's lungs made a wet sound at the bottom of each breath, a sound Edmund knew from avalanches, from men pulled out too late with ice packed in their throats. The mountain had already begun keeping him.

"Halloran," Edmund said now, over his shoulder, keeping his voice low under the wind. "How are we doing?"

"Ask me in an hour." The old man's grin was audible. "I've been asked worse questions by better doctors."

* * *

The corridor ran three kilometers between the glacier's medial moraines, a lane of blue ice scoured clean by wind, and it was the only sane route down from the cave. Every other line dropped into serac fields or funneled into the icefall where the glacier broke itself apart annually like a thing trying to remember how to be water. Edmund had come up this corridor eleven years ago, alone, running from a client's family and a coroner's report he still hadn't read. He knew it the way you know a scar — intimately, unwillingly, every contour mapped in the body rather than the mind.

The trouble with knowing a route that well is that you know exactly where it will kill you.

Two hundred meters in, the first crevasse opened beneath them like a held breath. It was bridged — had been bridged since Edmund last crossed it, probably longer — but the summer melt had been eating at the snow arch all season, and the bridge's lip showed a dark stain where meltwater had been working downward through the grain. He probed it with his axe, then his full weight, then called them across one at a time, spread along the rope so no single failure could take more than one of them.

Maren crossed last before him, and paused at the far lip, looking down into the black.

"How deep?"

"Fifty meters. Maybe sixty."

"And below that?"

"Glacier till. Then bedrock. Then whatever the mountain keeps under everything else." He looked at her. In the flat night light her face was all planes and patience. "You want to take a sample."

"I always want to take a sample." But she shouldered her pack and kept walking, and Edmund understood that she was leaving things behind her on purpose now — curiosity, ambition, the whole apparatus of the scientist — because none of it would help them survive the next six hours. There was something almost holy in watching a person set down the thing that defined them.

The helicopter came at 0110.

They heard it first as a change in the wind, a wrongness in the rhythm of the air, and then the sound itself arrived, rolling down the valley walls like a barrel dropped on stone. Edmund killed his headlamp and dropped flat against the ice, and heard the others do the same — four soft impacts, four held breaths. Above them, along the ridgeline they'd descended from, a searchlight swept the skyline in slow arcs, painting the rock faces white, then shadow, then white again.

Edmund pressed his cheek to the glacier and felt it breathing under him. That was the thing people never understood about ice: it wasn't dead. It moved, groaned, settled, sighed through its own weight. Right now it was telling him something — a long, low complaint somewhere deep in the corridor ahead, the sound of a serac adjusting itself, or a crevasse widening, or nothing at all. Fifteen years ago he would have ignored it. Tonight he lay there and listened until the helicopter's light swung away north and the engine note faded toward the lowlands, and only then did he let himself breathe.

"They're not searching," Maren whispered beside him. Her face was close enough that he could see the frost in her eyelashes. "They're herding. Pushing us toward the river crossing."

"You've been thinking about this."

"I've been thinking about nothing else since the radio." She didn't say which radio, and he didn't ask. The technician's transmission was a wound they were all walking around carefully, not looking at directly. "Edmund. If they expect us at the river—"

"Then we won't be at the river." He stood, brushed the ice from his front, and pointed down-corridor with his axe. "There's an old meltwater channel, left side, half a kilometer on. Runs parallel under the moraine. We can move in daylight inside it if we have to."

"In daylight," Reyes repeated, and there was something between hope and horror in his voice.

"If Halloran needs daylight, we'll take daylight."

* * *

Halloran needed daylight.

It happened gradually, the way bad things do on mountains — not a crisis but a subtraction. First the old man's pace dropped, then his pauses lengthened, then he began to lean on the rope between rest stops in a way that meant his arms were doing work his legs couldn't. By the time the sky began to lighten in the east — a thin gray promise, nothing more — they had covered less than half the corridor, and Edmund stood at the mouth of the meltwater channel doing arithmetic he hated.

Safety margins were simple things. You calculated your window — weather, light, pursuit, the body's own fuel — and you stayed inside it. He had built his whole life on staying inside margins, on never being the man who pushed past the point where the numbers turned. It was why clients hired him. It was also, though he rarely said it aloud, why he had never once risked anything that mattered.

Now the numbers said: leave Halloran. Not cruelly — build him a shelter, leave food and the satellite phone, mark the position for recovery. It was what any competent guide would do. It was what the liaison's people would expect a competent guide to do, which made it almost safe.

Halloran sat against the channel wall while Edmund worked the calculation, and watched him with eyes that had gone clear and calm as the deep ice.

"You're going to carry me," the geologist said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to shorten the rope intervals and adjust the pace."

"You're going to put me on your back like a sack of cores and haul me down this channel, because you've done the math and the math says a slow group survives and a split group doesn't." Halloran coughed, wetly, patiently. "I ran those same numbers forty years ago in the Andes. Left a man behind myself. He lived. I've regretted it every day since, including the days when regretting it was the only thing keeping me upright." He patted the ice beside him. "Sit down, Yarrow. You look tired."

Edmund didn't sit. He stood there in the growing gray light with the wind moaning through the channel mouth, and he felt something shift in his chest that had not shifted in fifteen years — some plate of armor grinding loose, some load redistributing. He thought about Harrow Vale, which he had not yet seen, whose name he had only heard spoken in the cave like a password into grief. He thought about the photographs of the detonation charges, the core samples with their shock-fractured layers, the tidy government report that had ended his career before it began. He thought about the fact that these four people had voted, in the dark, to trust him — a blacklisted guide, a man whose name was a synonym for failure — and that he had not yet given them a single reason to regret it.

That was the dangerous part. Not the helicopter, not the crevasses, not the armed men converging on the river. The dangerous part was that when he looked at Maren checking Halloran's pulse with hands that pretended to be clinical and weren't; when he looked at Okonkwo sharing out the last of the chocolate in equal fifths without being asked; when he looked at Reyes, twenty-six years old and terrified and still walking — when he looked at them, he wanted them to live. Not professionally. Not as a job completed and invoiced. He wanted it the way you want things for family, for people whose deaths would leave a hole shaped exactly like them in the world.

Caring was the most dangerous thing he had done in fifteen years. Caring meant there were outcomes he could no longer survive.

"All right," Edmund said. "Here's how it goes. We stay together. We move in the channel through the daylight hours. I carry Halloran on the steep sections, and Halloran doesn't argue, because arguing costs oxygen." He turned to Maren. "And when we reach the river valley, we don't cross where they expect. There's a ford two kilometers upstream, under the old slide path. Bad ground, but nobody watches bad ground."

"Why not?" Reyes asked.

"Because everyone believes bad ground kills you." Edmund shouldered his pack and took Halloran's rope onto his own harness, shortening the interval until the old man was practically tethered to his back. "I've spent fifteen years learning exactly how much bad ground a person can survive."

They moved. The light strengthened by increments, and the meltwater channel took them down through the glacier's gut — walls of layered ice glowing faintly green where the sun struck them, the floor a streambed of gray cobbles polished round by ten thousand summers. Halloran rode Edmund's shoulders on the steeper pitches, weighing almost nothing and somehow everything, and murmured geological commentary the whole way, identifying strata, dating eruptions, narrating the mountain's deep history as though determined to spend his last strength teaching it to whoever would listen.

Somewhere above and behind them, another engine started. Closer than the first. Edmund counted the seconds of its approach, judged its bearing, and did not stop walking.

Then the radio in Maren's pack crackled — the technician's radio, the compromised one, the one they should have destroyed — and a voice came through, calm and familiar and terrible, a voice Edmund had heard across a lodge table ten days ago offering him a deal he couldn't refuse.

"Yarrow." A pause, engineered, comfortable. "I know you can hear me. The offer stands. Bring them to the river crossing by tonight, and you fly out reinstated tomorrow morning. Your name cleared. Your permits restored." Another pause, softer. "Fifteen years, Edmund. All you have to do is finish the job you were actually hired for."

Edmund stopped. The others stopped with him, roped to him, watching.

He looked down the green-lit channel, toward the river he had no intention of reaching, and found — to his own surprise — that the voice had made the decision for him. Because the man on the radio had just told him, plainly, what Edmund was worth: four strangers' lives, traded for a name.

And Edmund Yarrow, who had spent fifteen years believing he deserved nothing, discovered that he disagreed.
