## 6. Radio Silence, Broken

Maren Oduya had learned to sleep in the dark the way other people learned to swim — badly at first, then with a kind of grim competence that passed for ease. In the cave it was never truly dark anyway. The glacier breathed light through its walls, a blue so deep it seemed less like color than like memory, and it moved all night with the slow pulse of meltwater somewhere beneath them. She had stopped hearing it as noise weeks ago. Now it was simply the sound of being alive in a place that did not care whether they were.

She woke because the breathing of the cave had changed.

Not the water. The men. One of them was gone from his bedroll.

She knew before she lifted her head which one. Ilya slept like a stone and snored like an avalanche; Devraj slept curled tight as a fist; Edmund slept on his back with his boots within reach of his hand, and even unconscious he looked like a man waiting to be dismissed. But Anselm — Anselm had been restless for three nights, turning over, muttering fragments of transmission protocols into his collar like prayers. Anselm was not in the cave.

Maren rose without a sound. Fifteen years of fieldwork had taught her that the body could be quiet even when the heart was hammering, and hers was hammering now, because she had watched Anselm's hands during dinner, the way they kept drifting to the canvas satchel where the radio lived, wrapped in fleece against the cold, dead by unanimous vote since the day they'd voted to disappear.

The vote had been hers to call, technically. She had called it. *We do not transmit. We do not exist.* And every night since, she had lain awake doing the arithmetic of what that silence cost, and every morning she had woken still believing in it, the way you believe in a rope bridge you have not yet stepped onto.

She found him forty meters down the secondary passage, where the ice narrowed to a throat and the blue light came through thin enough to read by. He had the radio out of its fleece. He had it assembled. His fingers were moving over the dials with the tenderness of a man touching something he loved, and the little power indicator glowed amber in the dark like a single lit window in a buried city.

"Anselm." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost gentle, and she hated herself for the gentleness, because gentleness would not stop him.

He didn't flinch. That frightened her more than flinching would have. "The window opens at 0400," he said. "Atmospherics are good tonight. Cold, dry, high pressure behind the front. It'll carry."

"You swore."

"I swore to a plan made by terrified people in the first week." He turned and looked at her, and in the glacier's light his face was older than his thirty-four years, hollowed and patient, the face of a man who had done his grieving already and come out the other side into something worse than grief: certainty. "Maren. Tomas is dying. You know it. He knows it. We've been sitting in this beautiful blue tomb for six weeks guarding paper while the man who found those samples for us gets quieter every day. If we die here, the evidence dies here, and Harrow Vale stays a weather event forever."

"The liaison—"

"—is not listening at four in the morning on a frequency band nobody has used since the last survey cycle. I checked the intercept schedules myself, Maren. I built half of them." A ghost of his old smile. "I'm not calling for help. I'm sending mail."

"To whom?"

"A journalist. Priya Raghunathan, the *Coastal Ledger*. She's been writing about Harrow Vale for two years. She's the only person in the lowlands who never accepted the avalanche story, and she's too stubborn and too unimportant for them to have bought." He held up a folded page — coordinates, frequencies, a compressed fragment of the core-sample log, the photographs' checksums. "A fragment. Enough that if we vanish completely, something survives outside. Not enough to tell them where we are or what we hold. Insurance, not surrender."

Behind them, a boot scraped stone.

Edmund stood at the mouth of the passage, silhouetted against the fainter blue, and Maren watched him take in the scene — the radio, the amber light, Anselm's bare hands on the controls — with an expression she could not read, because it was not anger. It was recognition. As if he were watching a film of something he himself had once done.

"How long have you known?" she asked him.

"That Anselm wanted to transmit? Since the second week. He talks in his sleep." Edmund came forward slowly, and his eyes went to the radio the way a starving man's eyes go to bread. "How long has it been powered?"

"I trickle-charged it off the solar panel," Anselm said. "Ninety minutes a day, under the tarp, when the others were sleeping."

"And you're transmitting at 0400."

"If no one stops me."

Maren heard her own heartbeat in the silence of the ice. She thought about the vote, taken by lamplight, five raised hands, her own among them. She thought about Tomas coughing in the dark, thinner every day, insisting he was fine in a voice like wind through wire. She thought about the liaison's name — Voss — and the file Edmund had described, the three previous expeditions that had ended in *weather*, in *equipment failure*, in bodies recovered too late to explain anything.

And she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *the vote was wrong.*

"Send it," she said.

Anselm stared at her. Edmund did not move at all.

"We voted," Anselm said carefully.

"We voted to hide, and hiding is killing Tomas and changing nothing else. They erased a valley, Anselm. Four hundred people. And they will erase us just as quietly unless someone outside knows we exist." She crouched beside him, took the folded page from his hand, checked the frequency against her own memory of the survey bands. "Send your fragment. Then we decide, together, what comes next — but we decide as people who have spoken, not as ghosts."

* * *

The transmission took eleven minutes.

They gathered around it the way people gather around a fire — Edmund standing slightly apart, arms crossed, Devraj and Ilya woken and silent, Tomas propped against his pack with a blanket over his shoulders, watching the radio with an expression of fierce, exhausted satisfaction, as though the machine were a son who had finally come home. Anselm's fingers moved through the burst protocol in short, disciplined sequences: header, fragment, checksum, sign-off. The amber light pulsed with each packet. Outside, the glacier said nothing. The mountain said nothing. Somewhere far below and south, in a city none of them could see, a machine in a dark room wrote their words into existence.

When it was done, Anselm killed the transmitter but left the receiver live, out of some technician's instinct neither he nor anyone else questioned. Static filled the passage, soft as snowfall.

"He'll verify the checksums against the published survey data," Anselm murmured. "If they match the anomalies — and they will — she'll start digging by morning. Within a week there'll be questions asked in the lowlands that can't be unasked."

"Within a week," Edmund said, "we need to be a hundred miles from here."

It was Ilya who heard it first. The big technician had been staring at the receiver with his head cocked, the way he listened to engines, and now he raised one hand without looking away from the radio. "Quiet. There's — listen. Under the static."

Maren leaned in. At first there was nothing, only the glacier's white noise. Then, threading through it, a carrier tone. Steady. Deliberate. Someone keying a microphone on a band adjacent to theirs, close enough in frequency that the receiver's bleed-over caught it — which meant whoever was transmitting was close enough, powerful enough, deliberate enough.

Then the voice came through, and Maren felt the temperature of the cave drop ten degrees.

It was a calm voice. Cultivated. The voice of a man accustomed to boardrooms and briefing rooms, softened now for the medium, pitched low, almost kind. It used no names.

*"To the party on the glacier. Good evening. I'll keep this brief, since I imagine batteries are a concern."*

Devraj made a small sound. Ilya's hand closed around the radio's casing as if he might crush the voice out of it. Edmund had gone very still — a stillness Maren had seen in him only once before, at the crevasse edge on the night march, when the ice under his feet had begun to talk.

*"You've had a busy evening. So have we. Our monitoring station picked up a burst transmission at 0402 hours — short, encrypted fragments, amateur handling, but identifiable origin. You should know that the equipment you're using was manufactured under government contract, and every unit carries a resonant signature. We've known roughly where you are for some time. What we haven't known is your intent."*

The voice paused. Static breathed.

*"Here is my intent. Mr. Yarrow — I know you're listening, because you're the only one of them with any sense of terrain, and sensible people stay near the exits. Fifteen years ago you were the finest guide this range ever produced, and the record that ended your career was, I'll say this plainly, unjust. I have read the file. I have the authority to amend it. Full reinstatement. Certification restored. Your name cleared in every registry from here to the coast — and a consulting retainer that would make the rest of your life comfortable."*

Maren turned her head very slowly and looked at Edmund.

He was watching the radio the way a man watches a door he had believed permanently locked. His face in the blue light was stripped of everything — no irony, no wariness, none of the armor she had spent weeks learning to read around. Just the raw thing underneath. Fifteen years of exile, looking at itself.

*"All of that is yours," the voice continued, "for a simple act of professional judgment. You are a guide. Guide. Bring me the four of them — alive, unharmed, delivered to the lower trailhead — and walk away clean. Or stay with them, and share whatever outcome they've chosen for themselves. The storm coming in behind this front is rated severe. I'd think carefully about how many people you're willing to bury in it."*

A click. The carrier dropped. Static rushed back in like water filling a hole.

No one spoke. The meltwater ticked somewhere in the ice. Tomas pulled the blanket tighter and looked, not at Edmund, but at Maren, and his eyes said what his ruined lungs could not: *there it is. Now we learn what he is.*

Edmund moved. He crossed the passage in three steps and put his hand flat on the radio, and for one terrible moment Maren thought he was going to switch it back on, answer the voice, negotiate.

Instead he switched the receiver off entirely. Killed the light. The amber glow died, and the darkness came in blue and total.

"They triangulated the burst," he said quietly. "Which means they don't need to search anymore. They know the sector. They'll sweep it at first light, and they'll bring people this time, not helicopters." He turned to face them all, and his voice was steady, but Maren heard the effort underneath it, the sound of a man holding a door shut against himself. "We leave tonight. All of us. Down the corridor, before the storm, before they arrive."

"And the offer?" Anselm asked softly.

Edmund was silent long enough that the glacier seemed to lean in.

"There is no offer," he said at last. "There's a price list. And I've spent fifteen years learning exactly what I'm worth to men like Voss." He reached for his pack. "Get Tomas up. We move in twenty minutes."

But Maren had seen his hand shake as he buckled the straps — a fine, fast tremor, quickly mastered — and she understood that the voice on the radio had told the truth about one thing, at least. It had offered him his old life, whole and gleaming, for four strangers.

And it had been, God help them, a real offer. That was what frightened her. Not that Edmund might take it.

But that part of him — the exiled, starving, fifteen-years-buried part — had wanted to.
