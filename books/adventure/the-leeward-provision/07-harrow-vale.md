## 7. Harrow Vale

They came down out of the high country in the grey hour before dawn, when the light was still more memory than fact, and the valley opened below them like a wound that had never been allowed to close.

Edmund had known it was there. He had known for fifteen years, the way a man knows about a tumor he refuses to have looked at — a shape on every map of his life, avoided by route-planning and silence. But knowing a thing from above was not the same as walking into it.

Harrow Vale lay below the terminal moraine like something swallowed. The avalanche had come off the eastern face at four minutes past three on a morning in late spring, and it had taken the village whole: forty-one houses, the kirk with its slate roof, the school where they'd found the children's boots arranged in rows because the teacher had lined them up for fire drill the day before and no one had moved them since. What remained now was rubble sorted into windrows by the meltwater, a plain of grey stone and splintered timber that stretched two miles to the river, and scattered across it — absurdly, unbearably — small cairns. Provisional memorials, raised by hands that had no bodies to bury. Someone had tied ribbons to a fence post that stood alone in all that emptiness, holding up nothing, fencing nothing, and the ribbons had bleached white as bone.

Maren stopped beside him at the moraine's crest. Below, the other three were already picking their way down, Ilse carrying the sample case strapped to her chest like an infant.

"You don't have to do this," she said.

"Yes I do." He heard his own voice come out flat, the guide's voice, the voice he used for crevasse fields and weather. "There's a crossing at the old mill race. It's the only way south that doesn't put us on the helicopter's line of sight. And—" He stopped. Started again. "And I've never been down there."

Fifteen years. He had guided clients past this valley eleven times, taking the western ridge so they wouldn't see it, telling himself the detour was about terrain. Once, a wealthy American had asked what the scar below them was, and Edmund had said *old slide, nothing down there now*, and the man had nodded and gone back to his coffee, and Edmund had carried that sentence for a decade and a half like a stone in his boot.

He went down into it now.

* * *

The rubble made its own acoustics. Every footstep rang against stone, and the sound seemed to come back wrong — too loud, then muffled, as if the ground itself couldn't decide whether to speak or keep its mouth shut. They moved single file along the course of the buried main street, which Maren had identified from the survey photographs: here the bakery, here the cooper's yard, here the well that had been dug in 1811 and was now a depression full of milky meltwater. At the well someone had left a child's tin cup on a flat stone, upright, waiting.

Tam, the younger technician, kept stopping to photograph things, and Edmund let him, though every shutter-click went through him like a pick strike. This was evidence too, Maren had said. Not just cores and documents. The valley itself was the document.

It was Ilse who found the woman.

She was kneeling at one of the cairns near the river line, an old woman in a man's wool coat, laying fresh-cut heather on the stones with the deliberation of someone performing a rite she had performed ten thousand times. She heard them before she saw them — the valley gave no cover for approach — and she rose slowly, turning, and Edmund watched her take in five strangers with packs and ropes and a metal case, and watched her decide, in the space of a breath, that whatever they were, they were not the ones in the helicopter.

Because she'd seen the helicopter. Of course she had. Everyone in the lowland settlements had been seeing it for weeks.

"You're from up there," the woman said. Her voice was roughened by cold and years. "From the ice."

"We're coming down," Maren said carefully. "We're trying to get home."

"Home." The woman said the word the way you'd spit out gristle. Then her eyes moved past Maren, past Tam and Ilse and the dying geologist Anders, propped between them on a litter of pack frames, and settled on Edmund, and stayed there.

Edmund knew the look. He'd spent fifteen years learning to recognize it — the flicker of a name surfacing, the half-second of *where do I know that face* — except this time it wasn't his face she was searching. It was his name, and she hadn't heard it yet.

"I'm Edmund Yarrow," he said, because there was no point anymore in being anyone else. "I'm the guide."

Something happened in the old woman's face. It didn't collapse; it *set*, like mortar going off, every line hardening into place around an expression he could not read until she spoke again.

"Yarrow," she said. "The Yarrow. The one they blamed."

The words went through him colder than any wind off the ice. "Blamed?"

"They said you led them wrong." She was looking at him now without fear and without kindness, the way you look at weather. "That spring, after. There were men in the settlement asking questions — government men, quiet ones, staying at the inn, buying drinks. My son-in-law talked to them. He was a quarryman; he'd worked the eastern face; he told them the charges they set for the road survey were set wrong, set deep, right in the blue ice where it holds the headwall together. He told them the mountain didn't fall on its own." Her hand closed on the fence post, on the bleached ribbons. "Two weeks later my daughter got a letter saying the witness had withdrawn his statement. Except he hadn't. He'd had an accident on the ice, they said. And the only name attached to that expedition — the guide who supposedly took those men up there, the guide whose rope they said failed —"

She stopped. She didn't need to finish. Fifteen years fell on Edmund all at once, the blacklisting, the inquiry that never called him, the newspaper line — *GUIDE'S ERROR CITED IN FATAL EXPEDITION* — the clients who stopped calling, the lodge managers who suddenly had no work, the slow erosion of a man's livelihood conducted so politely he had almost believed it was weather, bad luck, the mountain's own indifference.

"They needed the witness discredited," the woman said. "So they discredited his mountain first. Took away the man who knew that face better than anyone alive. Made sure that if he ever spoke, no one would listen." She tilted her head. "You did know that face, didn't you. Better than anyone."

"I surveyed it," Edmund said. His voice sounded far away to him, like a call across a glacier. "For the roads ministry. Before."

"And then they used your name to bury the question, and used the question to bury my boy." She turned back to the cairn, laid the last of the heather with fingers that did not shake. "Forty-one houses. Sixty-three people. Eleven of them children. And the men who set the charges are pensioners now, somewhere warm."

Nobody spoke. The meltwater ticked among the stones. Somewhere upslope, very faint, a helicopter engine — there and gone, a ghost of a sound, but enough. Maren's head came up like a bird dog's.

Edmund knelt at the cairn. He didn't know the name carved into the fieldstone marker — the letters were shallow, done by hand — but he read it anyway, and then he reached down and picked up a piece of the fallen rubble, a chunk of grey granite the size of a fist, and set it on the pile. One more stone. It was nothing. It was the only thing he had.

"I didn't fail them," he said, to the cairn, to the woman, to the valley entire. "I need you to know that. Whatever they wrote. I wasn't even there. I never met those men. They took my name and put it on their accident like a coat."

"I know," the old woman said simply. "My son-in-law knew it too, at the end. That's why they had to kill him twice — once with ice, once with paper." She looked at him a long moment. "You're carrying that name up and down these mountains like a debt. Well. It isn't a debt. It's a weapon somebody handed you and told you it was a chain. Time you found out the difference."

* * *

They crossed at the mill race in the early afternoon, the water thigh-deep and furious with snowmelt, roped in pairs while Edmund chose each footing. On the far bank, in the lee of the ruined mill wall, they regrouped, and Maren spread the route sketch on a flattened carton and weighted the corners with stones.

Anders was worse. The geologist lay against the mill wall with his eyes closed and his breathing doing the wet, labored work of a body closing its accounts, and when Ilse checked him he opened his eyes and said, clearly, "Don't stop for me at the border. Mail goes faster than funerals." Nobody laughed. Everybody understood.

"The radio traffic's changed," Tam said quietly, tapping the receiver. "Since dawn. Encrypted bursts, short intervals. They're coordinating something. If we stay on this side of the river—"

"We don't stay anywhere," Edmund said.

He heard his own voice and recognized it, and was surprised. It wasn't the guide's voice anymore. Something had burned off in the valley below, some sediment laid down over fifteen years, and what was left underneath was simpler and harder.

He looked south, toward the border, thirty miles of broken country between them and the wire. Then he looked back north, up-valley, toward the ice and the cave and the core samples that proved everything — and he thought about the old woman's hand on the fence post, and the tin cup at the well, upright and waiting, and sixty-three names that had been filed under *natural disaster* by men who signed their memos in triplicate.

His blacklisting hadn't been punishment. It had been maintenance. A loose thread cut before it could unravel anything — except the thread had walked back onto the mountain after all, carrying a survey team, and now the unraveling was finally due.

"Maren," he said. "How many copies of the evidence exist?"

"Three sets. Cores split, photos duplicated, documents copied. Why?"

"Because from here on, we stop moving as one group." He crouched over the sketch, and began, in the ash-grey light, to draw three routes. "They'll be watching for five people traveling together. So we won't be five people traveling together."

Maren studied him across the map, and he saw her arrive at the same arithmetic he had — saw her understand that the routes converged nowhere, that one of them ended at a border post where a man with a ruined name would be standing in the open, drawing every eye, while the mail sack crossed the wire unexamined.

"You're volunteering," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm guiding," Edmund said. "It's the only thing I've ever been good at. Turns out I finally know what for."

Above the valley, far off, the helicopter came back — closer this time, deliberate, sweeping the river line in long patient strokes, like something reading.

Edmund folded the map.
