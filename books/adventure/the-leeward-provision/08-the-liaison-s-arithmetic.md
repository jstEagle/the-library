## 8. The Liaison's Arithmetic

Maren heard the river before she saw it — a gray, muscular sound threading through the fog, the kind of water that didn't negotiate. They came down through the dwarf pine in single file, Edmund first, then the technicians, then her with the geologist's weight distributed across her shoulders and her own guilt distributed everywhere else. Aldous Voss had stopped speaking two days ago. He weighed less than his pack now, and that was its own kind of arithmetic, the terrible kind, the kind Maren had been doing since the cave: how many days of him were left, and what each one cost the rest of them.

The crossing was a braided floodplain of slate-gray channels, cold enough to kill a man in four minutes of immersion. On the far bank the trail climbed toward the border passes — three of them, three routes, the plan they'd built in the dark like children building something out of matches.

Edmund stood at the water's edge and did not move.

Maren had learned to read him the way you learn to read weather: not by any single sign but by an accumulation. The set of his shoulders. The way he counted things with his eyes — ridgelines, exits, minutes. He was counting now, and whatever total he arrived at made him go very still, the stillness of a man listening to something no one else can hear yet.

"What is it," she said. Not a question. She'd stopped phrasing them as questions days ago; he answered faster that way.

"Helicopter," he said. "Shut down. Upstream, maybe two kilometers. They landed because they don't want us to hear them coming." He turned, and in the flat pewter light his face looked carved out of the same material as the mountains behind it. "They're already on this side of the river."

* * *

They came out of the trees twenty minutes later, and they did not hurry, which told Maren everything about how confident they were. Six men. The liaison — Halloran, though he'd introduced himself weeks ago over the radio with the warm anonymity of a man reading from a card — walked at the front without a weapon visible, which was its own kind of weapon. The five behind him carried rifles slung low and casual, the posture of men who had done this before and expected it to be brief.

Halloran was smaller than his voice. That was the first thing Maren noticed at forty meters, close enough now to see details the radio had flattened: gray at the temples, a government windbreaker zipped to the chin, hands folded behind his back like a man touring a facility he owned. He smiled when he saw them, and the smile reached all the way up, professionally, like a lamp switching on.

"Dr. Oduya." He said her name correctly, carefully, the way you handle evidence. "And Mr. Yarrow. I have to say, you've made my job difficult."

"You should try doing it without helicopters," Edmund said.

"I should." Halloran's gaze moved past them, counting — Voss wrapped in the emergency bivvy, barely conscious; the technicians, Pell and Sandoval, standing too close together the way frightened people do; Maren herself, the sample case strapped across her back like a second spine. His eyes rested on the case for exactly one second too long, and Maren felt the weight of it settle there, proprietary, patient.

"Here's where we are," Halloran said pleasantly. "You're on the wrong side of a very cold river, in restricted terrain, in possession of material that belongs to the Crown Survey Authority. Behind me are six people whose overtime I'm paying for personally. Ahead of you is nothing but border and weather." He unfolded his hands. "I'm authorized to resolve this. I'd prefer to resolve it simply."

"Simply," Maren repeated.

"There's a version of today where everyone walks away." He said it looking at her, not Edmund, and she understood the tactic even as it worked on her: address the reasonable one. Address the scientist, the one who believed in procedure, in institutions, in the idea that somewhere above all this there were adults who would do the right thing once shown the proof. "The samples come with us. The photographs. You four come with us — comfortably, I promise you, better than you've eaten in weeks. And Mr. Yarrow—" the smile tilted, became almost intimate— "Mr. Yarrow gets exactly what he asked for at the start of all this. His name back. Clean. Reinstated. Fifteen years erased from the ledger, Dr. Oduya. Doesn't every man deserve that?"

She watched Edmund take that sentence the way a man takes a blow he saw coming — absorbing it, letting it pass through. But she also watched the half-second where it lodged. Where some old, starved part of him reached for it before the rest of him could stop it. She had spent three weeks deciding whether Edmund Yarrow could be bought, and here was the price tag held up to the light, and the awful thing was not that the number was high. It was that the number was exactly right.

"That's your offer," Edmund said.

"That's my offer."

"And if we refuse?"

Halloran sighed, genuinely, theatrically regretful. "Then we're into the other version of today. And Mr. Yarrow, having spent time in these mountains, you know what the other version looks like. Accidents happen here. You taught this government that lesson yourself, or it taught you — I've never been clear on the history."

* * *

It happened fast after that, so fast that Maren would spend the rest of her life replaying it frame by frame, trying to find the moment she could have stepped in.

Edmund laughed. Short, harsh, real. "Fifteen years," he said. "Do you know what I did with fifteen years? I carried rich men up hills and listened to them complain about the wine in base camp lodges. You think you're offering me my life back? My life was the thing you took to punish me for being right about Harrow Vale. There's nothing in your ledger to erase, Halloran. There's just the hole where it used to be."

"Then we're at an impasse."

"We're at a river." Edmund turned and looked at Maren, and something passed between them that had no words in it, or rather had too many — the whole three weeks of distrust and grudging respect and the night in the ice cave when she'd finally slept because he was watching the entrance. "The water's high," he said to her, conversationally, loudly, a man giving a briefing. "Braided channels. The main channel's chest-deep and moving. Nobody crosses it loaded like this without a guide who knows the ford points."

"Maren," Halloran said softly. "Don't."

But Edmund was already talking, and his voice had changed — dropped into the register she'd only heard him use once before, explaining crevasse rescue to Pell, patient and absolute, a man transferring knowledge he intended to survive him. "There's a ford ninety meters downstream, under the bluff. Gravel bar, knee-deep, safe even loaded. You can't see it from here. I can put all four of them across it in ten minutes, and there isn't a thing your six riflemen can do about it except follow, and the bluff overlooks the crossing, and I know every rock on that bluff."

Halloran's smile stayed fixed but something behind it recalculated. Maren watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she was doing, arriving at the same sum from the opposite direction.

"You're offering to trade yourself," Halloran said slowly. "For their crossing."

"I'm offering to save you a helicopter fuel budget and a week of mountain searching," Edmund said. "Me, cooperative, walking out with you, telling you everything — where the samples are, where the routes run, all of it, my full confession, on record. In exchange, those four walk to the border unmolested. Every man of yours stays on this side of the river until they're across."

"They'll be arrested at the post."

"They'll be *seen* at the post," Edmund said. "In front of witnesses. With journalists already asking questions because someone transmitted evidence off this mountain nine days ago — you've been chasing that signal ever since, haven't you? The story exists now, Halloran. All you can control is who's holding the samples when it breaks. And you want the samples more than you want four tired witnesses."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstream the river ground a boulder against another boulder, slow and enormous, like the mountain thinking.

And in that silence Maren understood — fully, horribly, with the clarity that comes too late to be useful — that this was not improvised. That Edmund had been building toward this since the cave, maybe since the trailhead lodge, that every kindness and every hard lesson and every mile he'd led them had been leading here, to a gravel bar and a bargain, and that he had never once intended to cross the river himself.

He caught her looking at him. He didn't deny it. That was the worst part — he didn't deny it, didn't soften it, just gave her the smallest nod, the one he gave when a route decision was made and debate was over.

"The samples," she said aloud, because someone had to say it, because the plan needed saying to become real. "Split. Three cases, three routes. Nothing travels together." She unslung her pack with steady hands, hands that shook only when she let them. "Voss goes with me. He doesn't have two days in him and he knows it, and he wants to cross the line carrying his own work. Pell takes the western pass. Sandoval the high route with the duplicates. If any one of us makes it, the truth makes it."

"No one says goodbye properly," Edmund said quietly. "That's the point. Goodbyes are testimony. Anyone who watches you cry knows what you're carrying."

* * *

They split the evidence on the gravel bar with the efficiency of people who had rehearsed grief so long it had worn smooth. Core samples divided. Photographs copied in triplicate back in the cave, weeks ago, when none of them had admitted even to themselves what the copies were for. Maren took the originals and Voss. Voss, conscious now, propped against a boulder, insisted on signing the chain-of-custody log with a hand that could barely hold the pencil, and Maren held the log steady for him and pretended that was routine, that scientists did this every day, signed their life's work over to a dying colleague on a riverbank while armed men watched from the treeline.

Pell hugged her quickly, hard, face buried in her shoulder, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't testimony. Sandoval shook Edmund's hand and held it a beat too long and still said nothing.

Then it was Maren and Edmund at the edge of the water, and the river filled the silence between them the way it filled everything, totally, without effort.

"You knew," she said. "From the beginning. From the lodge."

"I knew the deal was rotten. I didn't know what shape the rot was." He looked at the water. "I owed a debt to Harrow Vale. Fifteen years of interest. This is just the payment clearing."

"It's not justice. They'll bury you deeper than the valley."

"Probably." He almost smiled. "But the mail crosses the border tomorrow morning, and the inquiry opens next month, and somewhere in a hearing room a lawyer is going to say my name out loud in front of the whole country, and it won't be the blacklisted version." He shrugged, the small shrug of a man adjusting a pack strap. "That's more belonging than I ever figured on. Don't waste it feeling sorry for me. Cross the river, Maren. Ford's at ninety meters. Knee-deep. Count your steps — thirty-two to the far bar."

She stepped toward the water and then, breaking her own rule, breaking his rule, turned back. "Edmund."

"Don't," he said gently.

"Yarrow." She made herself say it plainly, a scientist stating findings. "You were right about Harrow Vale. Fifteen years ago. You were right, and they punished you for it, and I am going to spend the rest of my career making sure everyone knows that was the finding. That's not goodbye. That's data."

For a moment something moved through his face that fifteen years of mountains had failed to erode — something young, and unguarded, and grateful beyond speech. Then he nodded once, turned, and walked up the bank toward Halloran's men with his hands loose at his sides and his head up, a guide leading a party into terrain only he knew.

Maren crossed the river counting her steps. At thirty-two her boots hit the far gravel bar, and she did not look back, because looking back was testimony, and the evidence had to travel clean.

Behind her, faint under the river's roar, she heard Halloran say, pleasantly: "Right then, Mr. Yarrow. Show us the way."

And the fog closed over the crossing, and the mountain kept its own count.
