## 9. Whiteout

The storm came down the Leeward Range like a debt being collected.

Edmund felt it before he saw it — a pressure change in his ears, a taste of iron in the wind off the col — and he knew, standing at the river crossing with the liaison's men fanning out behind him, that every hour of careful planning had just been spent like a coin tossed into meltwater.

"Storm's early," said Voss, the liaison's senior man, squinting up at a sky that had gone the color of dirty wool. "Can you still move?"

"I can move," Edmund said. "The question is whether you can follow."

He meant it as a warning. He watched it land instead as reassurance — Voss glancing back at the other three men, the rifles slung across their packs, and nodding as if Edmund had just confirmed their good fortune. That was the arithmetic they'd all agreed to: four armed men, one disgraced guide, and a bargain struck on the riverbank with the water roaring too loud for anyone to hear the fine print. Edmund in exchange for Maren and the others' safe passage to the border. The liaison, a narrow man named Corrick with a voice like a filing cabinet closing, had accepted it with the ease of someone who believed he was buying a map.

Let him believe it, Edmund had thought. Maps are only true until the weather changes.

* * *

They climbed north from the crossing into the teeth of it.

By the second hour the world had been erased. Not dimmed — erased. Snow came sideways in sheets so dense that Edmund could not see Voss ten feet behind him, only hear the crunch of his boots and the occasional curse when a hidden rock caught a shin. There was no horizon. No ridge line. No sun, no shadow, no slope he could read by eye. The whiteout did not obscure the mountain; it abolished it, leaving each of them alone inside a spinning grey room the size of a grave.

Which was exactly what Edmund had been counting on.

For fifteen years he had walked this range alone, blacklisted, unwelcome, mapping it with his feet because no one would pay him to map it any other way. He knew the Leeward Provision — the long saddle below the eastern icefield where the wind funneled and swirled in ways that defied every compass — better than he had ever known a human face. In clear weather it was merely treacherous. In a whiteout it was a labyrinth built by God specifically for this purpose, though Edmund suspected God had never intended it for him.

"Guide!" Voss shouted through the wind. "You're drifting west!"

"I'm not," Edmund called back, and turned two degrees further west.

It was a small thing. It was everything. Without visual references, a man walking what feels like a straight line describes a slow arc toward his dominant side — every mountaineer learns it, most learn it by dying. But Edmund could correct for it instinctively, could hold a bearing by the feel of wind on his left cheekbone and the grade of snow underfoot, and the men behind him could do none of those things. They were navigating by him entirely. Their radios were useless in the interference; their GPS units showed them a blinking dot adrift in a featureless field; their rifles were slung muzzle-down against the storm. Every step they took was a step Edmund chose.

So he began to choose them badly.

Not obviously. Never obviously. A dogleg around a serac that added six hundred meters. A contour line followed a quarter-mile longer than it needed to be, descending when they thought they were holding elevation. At the third hour he took them through a shallow basin and out the far side on a heading forty degrees off true, and when Voss checked the compass against Edmund's shoulder-line, the needle disagreed with itself in the way needles do near the iron-rich schist of the Provision, and Voss swore and put it away and trusted the guide.

Because that was the thing about hunters, Edmund thought, breaking trail into the rising wind. They trust the hunted to know the country. It never occurs to them, until it is far too late, that this is precisely why the hunted always have somewhere to run.

* * *

He thought about Maren then, the way you think about a hand you've let go of in a crowd.

Three hours ahead of them — if the timetable held, and the storm had already murdered the timetable — her group would be splitting at the cairn below Saddle Pass. Three routes to the border, the evidence divided three ways: core samples sealed in a mail sack with Oduya, photographs taped flat against Halvard's chest beneath his coat, the journal pages memorized and carried in Maren's own head, where no search could find them. Any one route might be caught. All three could not be stopped. It was Maren's plan, cold and elegant, and she had explained it to Edmund in the blue light of the glacier cave with the dying geologist breathing shallowly beside them, and she had asked him one question:

"And you? Where do you fit in this plan?"

Nowhere, he'd told her. Nowhere was exactly where he fit. A man who trusts no one, offered a deal he cannot refuse — he'd spent fifteen years learning that the only safe place in any bargain is the part of it that can be given away.

He gave himself away now, step by step, leading four armed men deeper into a maze only he could see the walls of.

* * *

The first man went down just after noon.

Not dramatically — the mountain rarely bothers with drama. Rennick, the youngest, stepped onto a snow bridge over a crevasse that Edmund had crossed safely an hour earlier by a line six feet to the east, and the bridge sighed and let him through to the waist. His scream was thin and strange in the wind, more surprise than pain, and the others hauled him up in a tangle of rope and panic while Edmund stood upwind and shouted instructions he made deliberately half-wrong — *pull him left, left!* — knowing that left was the broken side of the bridge, knowing they'd fight the rope the wrong way, knowing it would cost them twenty minutes and whatever remained of their confidence.

Rennick came up bleeding from the hip, hypothermic, done. Voss looked at Edmund with naked accusation.

"You knew that crevasse was there."

"I know where *I* am," Edmund said. "You're asking me to know where *you* are, in a whiteout, on ground I haven't walked since the government closed the zone. I told you at the river this was a gamble."

It wasn't true. He had walked this ground eleven days ago, alone, in the dark, laying the escape route with the same care a man lays snares. But Voss had no way to check, and the storm pressed in around the lie like snow around a body, sealing it in.

They left Rennick in a dug shelter with a bivouac bag and a radio that wouldn't reach anyone, which was its own kind of sentence, and Edmund noted without satisfaction that he had not hesitated. Fifteen years ago he would have hesitated. Fifteen years ago he had hesitated, on a different slope, over a different man, and the hesitation had been recorded in a government report as negligence and used to bury him. Now he understood that the report had never been about hesitation at all. It had been about making sure that when the day came — this day — no one would listen to anything Edmund Yarrow said.

Well. He was saying nothing now. He was only walking. And the walking was testimony enough.

* * *

By late afternoon he had brought them in a full circle.

He knew it the moment he recognized the serac — the leaning blade of ice he'd passed at midday, now shouldering out of the murk on their right when it should have been far behind them. Voss's GPS, consulted with shaking hands, placed them impossibly: a dot moving in a slow spiral, like something going down a drain.

"We've lost an hour," Voss said hoarsely. "Maybe two. Where's the pass? Where are they?"

"Gone," Edmund said. It was the first completely honest word he'd spoken since the river, and he savored it. "If they moved at dawn, they're over Saddle Pass or into the trees below it. You're not catching three groups on three routes. You never were. Your boss sent four men to catch smoke."

Voss's hand went to his rifle sling, and Edmund watched the calculation move behind the man's eyes — the calculation Corrick had surely briefed into him: *the guide is expendable once the terrain is crossed.* But the terrain was not crossed. The terrain was everywhere, and it was Edmund, and Voss knew it. In a whiteout, shooting your guide is suicide with extra steps.

"Keep moving," Voss said finally.

"With pleasure," said Edmund, and turned them west again, into the spiral.

* * *

The storm took the second man near dusk. Aldous, the quiet one, simply sat down during a halt and did not get up, and when they shook him he looked at them with the mild blue astonishment of the freezing and said he'd rest a moment, just a moment, and Voss — exhausted, half-blind, his authority dissolving with the daylight — made the decision commanders make: leave him, mark the spot, press on. Edmund marked the spot with a wand he planted upside-down, pointing nowhere, a small monument to the arithmetic of men who measured other people in numbers.

And then, in the last grey thread of light, Edmund heard it.

Faint, torn by wind, almost imaginary: a voice on Voss's radio, Corrick's filing-cabinet tones flattened to a whisper of static. Edmund caught only fragments — *...border post... mail sack... nothing crosses... seal the road...* — but the fragments were enough, and they were wrong, all wrong. Corrick wasn't pulling men back to hunt the routes. He was massing everyone at the border. Which meant the border was no longer an exit. Which meant Maren, somewhere ahead in the dark with a mail sack full of the truth, was walking straight into a closed fist.

Voss was shouting into the radio, demanding position reports, and getting static, and Edmund stood behind him in the screaming white and felt the plan turn over in his chest like a stone in a streambed.

There was one way to warn her. One way to reach the border post before the net closed — a way down the eastern icefall that no government man would take, because no government man could, a route that existed only in the private atlas of a blacklisted guide who had spent fifteen years learning every illegal door in the range. It would mean abandoning the hunters here, high on the Provision, in a storm that would keep them alive or kill them according to its own indifferent bookkeeping. It would mean breaking the bargain he'd struck on the riverbank — the bargain that was supposed to buy Maren's safety with his own surrender.

Or it would mean keeping the bargain the only way it could still be kept: not by giving himself up at the border, but by arriving there first.

Edmund pulled his hood tight, set his bearings by the wind on his cheekbone, and walked away into the whiteout, leaving four armed men spinning slowly in the dark behind him like a compass needle with no north.

The border was nine miles distant. The storm would arrive in three hours.

He intended to win.
