## 10. The Leeward Provision

The border post sat in a saddle of grey scree where the Leeward Range finally gave up its argument with the sky, and Edmund Yarrow walked toward it the way he had once walked away from everything — alone, unhurried, counting his steps because counting was the only prayer he knew.

Behind him, three days back up the corridor of ice and stone, Maren Oduya had shaken his hand and said nothing at all. That had been the agreement. No goodbyes. Goodbyes were for people who believed an ending was real, and none of them could afford to believe that yet. The evidence was already moving — split across three routes like water finding its way downhill: photographs sewn into the lining of a technician's coat, core samples packed as mineral specimens in a prospector's pack, and the last of it, the heaviest of it, sealed in oilcloth inside a mail sack bound for the lowlands on the weekly supply run.

Edmund's job was simpler. His job was to be seen.

He came down out of the last rocks at midmorning, hands empty, hood back, walking the open ground in full view of the post's watchtower because a man who wanted to be caught did not hide. The wind off the glacier carried the smell of stone dust and old snow. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and went quiet.

The border guards were young. They always were. The one who stepped out to meet him had a rifle slung carelessly and eyes that kept flicking to the ridge behind Edmund, as if the mountain itself might produce more men.

"Name," the guard said.

"Edmund Yarrow."

The name landed on the boy's face like weather. Even here, even this far from anywhere, it meant something. Fifteen years of meaning something in all the wrong ways — *the guide who lost them*, the file said, though the file had never said who *them* was, or why a rescue party of six had come home as one. Edmund had spent a decade and a half letting that name sit on him like a stone slab, and now, standing in the wind with his surrender already written into the government's own arithmetic, he found the slab lighter than it had ever been.

"You're expected," the guard said, and there was something almost apologetic in it.

"I know," Edmund said. "I'm the one who told them where I'd be."

They took his pack, his knife, the small brass compass that had been his father's. He let them. A man traveling light traveled fast, and he had no intention of being anywhere else ever again. In the post's single heated room, while a clerk wrote down facts about him that were all wrong, Edmund looked out the barred window at the range he had given his life to and taken his life from, and he thought about miles.

Fifteen years of miles. Miles to outrun the inquiry that never happened, miles to stay ahead of the whisper that followed him through every guide's office and every hiring hall in three provinces. Miles measured in clients' satisfied handshakes and empty beds and the particular silence of high camps where no one asked him anything personal because no one stayed long enough to want to. He had told himself distance was freedom. It had taken four strangers in a glacial cave, a dying geologist named Aldous Wren who had held his hand on the ice and made him promise nothing except to remember, and a valley of rubble called Harrow Vale to teach him that distance was only another word for exile.

The liaison arrived by helicopter two days later.

Edmund heard it first — the flat percussion of rotors echoing off the cirque walls — and felt the old animal calm settle over him, the calm that had served him on corniced ridges and in collapsing couloirs. Voss came into the room still wearing his city boots, grey and immaculate, and looked at Edmund the way a man looks at a ledger that has finally balanced.

"Yarrow." He set a folder on the table. "You've cost me a great deal."

"You cost Harrow Vale forty-one people," Edmund said. "We're not going to compare accounts."

Voss smiled thinly, sat, opened the folder. "The survey team is dead. All of them. Exposure, the official finding will say. Tragic. You led them into terrain you knew they couldn't survive — which, I'll grant, has a certain historical symmetry." He tapped the folder. "Cooperate with that narrative and your reinstatement survives you. Your name gets cleaned. Posthumously, of course, but names matter to men like you."

"They do," Edmund agreed. "That's rather the point."

Voss studied him for a long moment, and Edmund watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes — the same cold column-keeping Maren had described, lives weighed against careers, a valley weighed against a ministry. He saw the moment the numbers failed. There was nothing left to buy. Nothing left to threaten. A man who had already handed himself over owned nothing worth taking.

"The mail sack," Voss said quietly. "It went south yesterday."

"Yes," Edmund said. "It did."

* * *

Maren Oduya stood at the window of a rented room above a print shop in the lowland capital, watching rain silver the streetlamps, when the story broke.

She had been in the city eleven days. Long enough to see the photographs developed — Aldous Wren's core samples laid out on a laboratory bench like the ribs of some buried animal, each stratum dated, each detonation residue identified. Long enough to watch the journalist, a tired woman named Kessler who had waited three years for documents she'd half stopped believing in, go very still over the seismic records and then work for thirty-six hours without sleeping. Long enough to understand that the thing they had carried out of the ice was heavier than any of them had known, and that weight, once set down in public, could not be picked back up.

The broadcast came at seven. She listened to the minister's statement — *deeply troubling allegations, a full independent inquiry, the individuals responsible will be held accountable* — and heard in its careful hollowness the sound of a government discovering that some avalanches cannot be covered by later snow.

Her field journal lay open on the desk. She had written in it that morning for the first time since the cave:

*Day 61. They are calling it the Harrow Vale Inquiry. Forty-one names read aloud in a chamber with good acoustics. Aldous would have hated the acoustics and loved the names. Edmund Yarrow is in a holding facility awaiting charges — obstruction, unlawful entry into a restricted zone, aiding fugitives. He surrendered so that a mail sack could cross a border unmolested. I have been asked whether I will testify. As if there were a version of me that wouldn't.*

She testified on the fourth day of the hearings.

The chamber was oak-paneled and cold, and the ministers sat behind a raised bench with the posture of men who had expected a weeping witness and received instead a surveyor with her notes in date order. Maren spoke for two hours. She described the last camp, the deliberate disappearance, the vote in the dark of the cave — four hands raised to choose silence over the fate of the liaison's earlier expeditions. She described the charges, the timing, the seismic signature of demolition rather than collapse. And near the end, when the chairman asked her, gently, how a blacklisted guide of Edmund Yarrow's reputation had come to lead them out, she looked directly at the bench and answered.

"His reputation was manufactured," she said. "By the same office that manufactured the avalanche. He was the one witness positioned to connect the original rescue to the original crime, and so he was made into a cautionary tale instead. Fifteen years ago, Edmund Yarrow brought six people down off that mountain alive, and the truth of what killed the other rescuers was buried with them under his name. Everything you have heard about him is load-bearing masonry over a hole. We have the photographs of the hole. Take the wall down."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that she heard the rain start again outside.

* * *

The sentence came down in late autumn, and it was measured in months.

Eighteen months, reduced to fourteen for cooperation. Obstruction, unlawful entry, the aiding of fugitives — the fugitive charge dropped entirely once the inquiry formally recognized the survey team as witnesses rather than deserters. The newspapers ran his photograph beside the old one, fifteen years apart, and readers wrote letters asking how such a man had ever been blacklisted at all. The answer, when it finally appeared in the inquiry's interim report, ran to forty pages and ended four careers.

Edmund served his time in a low-security facility in the foothills, within sight of the range on clear days. Visitors came. This was the part that undid him, more than the verdict, more than the vindication — visitors came. Maren, monthly, with survey maps and complaints about the bureaucracy of testimony. The technicians, once, awkward and grateful, bringing tobacco he didn't use and news he did. A woman from Harrow Vale, the survivor he had met in the rubble, who sat across the table from him and said, "My father's name is on the memorial now with the true date under it. That's yours. I wanted you to know someone knows that."

He counted the months the way he had once counted miles, and found the arithmetic transformed. Miles had been a way of never arriving. Months were a promise of arrival — a fixed number, diminishing daily, at the end of which stood a door and beyond the door a world that had, improbably, made room for him. He took to marking the days on the cell wall not with desperation but with something dangerously close to patience. Fourteen months. Thirteen. Twelve. Each one crossed off was not distance fled but time *kept*, time honored, time owed to Aldous Wren and the forty-one and paid in the only currency he had left.

On the day of his release, it was snowing — light, dry, leeward snow, the gentle kind.

Maren was waiting at the gate with a hired truck and a thermos, and she did not ask where he wanted to go, because they both already knew. They drove north through the falling white until the road ended and the trail began, and they walked the last miles together in the blue hush of afternoon, up past the tree line, along the corridor he knew better than any living soul, to the saddle above Harrow Vale.

The valley lay below in winter dress, rebuilt in part, scarred in part, the provisional memorials replaced now by a single stone wall with forty-one names cut deep enough to hold snow and shadow both. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone was ringing a small bell — evening chores, or a school, or just the hour. The sound climbed the slope to them thin and clear.

Edmund stood at the top of the trail with the cold on his face and the whole inhabited valley breathing below, and understood that he had reached the end of every route he had ever run.

"Well," Maren said. "Here you are."

"Here I am," he said.

And for the first time in fifteen years, Edmund Yarrow did not reach for the map, or the compass, or the count of miles still between him and somewhere else. He simply stood, in the falling snow, in the place where he was — a man who belonged, at last and entirely, to something.
