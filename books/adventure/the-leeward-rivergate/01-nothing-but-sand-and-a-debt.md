## 1. Nothing but Sand and a Debt

The thief was three days ahead of me and losing ground, and I knew it the way you know a storm is coming — not by looking at the sky but by the taste of it.

I'd found her first camp on the eastern lip of the Sereen salt flats: a cold fire ring, a heel-print in the alkali crust, and a single clue she probably thought was clever. She'd buried her tracks by dragging a thornbush behind her, the way smugglers do in the coastal ranges. But thornbush doesn't grow within a hundred miles of the flats. She'd carried it with her. Which meant she was smart enough to hide her trail and green enough to leave the tool of the hiding lying in the open like a confession.

I picked it up, weighed it in my hand, and felt something I hadn't felt since my father died. Not rage. Rage had been riding shotgun for eleven days, ever since I'd come back from a two-week patrol circuit and found the cabin door standing open and the strongbox emptied of exactly one thing: his brass compass, the one he'd held in his hand the night the fever took him, pressing it into my palm like it was a key to something.

*Find it,* he'd said. Not the compass. I'd assumed he meant the compass. He was mostly gone by then, his voice a dry whisper of itself. *Find it, Sebastian. It points where it points.*

I'd spent eleven days assuming that was fever-talk. Now I wasn't so sure.

The second camp told me more. She'd made it at a dry wash forty miles further east, and this time there were no tricks at all — no buried fire, no dragged brush, no effort to hide. Just a bedroll pressed into the sand and a scatter of food wrappings, and beside the fire ring, deliberate as a signature, a small cairn of three stones.

Thieves don't leave cairns. Pilgrims do. Or people who want to be followed.

I stood there a long time with the wind pulling at my coat collar, doing the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Three days ahead. Traveling light, moving fast, but not running — a running thief drops everything that slows her, and this one was carrying a thornbush and building monuments. She was heading east across the flats toward the coast, and the only thing east of the flats toward the coast was the Rivergate country, and the only reason anyone went to the Rivergate country was a reason I had sworn off at seventeen with my father's blood still on my hands.

I watered the mule, checked my rifle, and kept going.

* * *

The salt flats don't forgive. By the third day of my pursuit the crust had gone from white to a blinding silver, and the horizon dissolved into a shimmer that made distance meaningless. I traveled at dawn and dusk and slept through the killing hours of noon under a canvas shade rigged off the mule's pack. My water ration tightened. My thoughts loosened.

I thought about the compass. I'd wound it every morning of my father's last year, a ritual he'd insisted on even when his hands shook too badly to hold the thing — he'd guide my fingers over the crown, click by click, like teaching a child to pray. It was an old instrument, older than him, brass gone green at the seams, with a hinged lid and a needle that didn't sit right. It always swung a few degrees off true north, and my father had never once corrected for it. When I'd asked him why, back when I was small enough to ask questions like that, he'd smiled and said, *Because it's not lost, son. It's going somewhere.*

Then he'd died, and the compass had sat in the strongbox for six years, and I'd let it sit, because winding it every morning felt like a debt I didn't know how to pay. And then a stranger had climbed through my window and taken the one thing I couldn't replace, and the debt had a shape at last: rage, and a rifle, and a trail of thornbushes across the end of the world.

On the fourth day I found blood.

A few drops, brown and dried on the crust, spaced like a walking man's. Then a scrap of cloth — dark wool, good quality, torn on something sharp. Then, half a mile on, the thornbush itself, abandoned. She'd stopped hiding. She was hurt, or she'd stopped caring, or both.

The blood bothered me more than I wanted to admit. I'd spent eleven days building a thief out of my own anger — some hard-eyed professional who'd picked my cabin because it was easy, who'd taken the compass because it was valuable, who deserved whatever came to her. But a professional doesn't bleed across the flats. A professional doesn't carry a thornbush. A professional doesn't leave a cairn like a woman marking stations on a route only she understands.

I caught up with her at dusk.

* * *

The flats end at the Verrin Escarpment, a long red wall of rock that runs north-south like a scar, and where the wall meets the last of the salt there's a bowl of broken stone — old landslide country, house-sized blocks tumbled against each other, full of shadows and dead ends. She'd gone to ground there. I saw her fire before I saw her: a tiny red eye winking in a gap between two slabs, the only warm color in a world gone blue and gray.

I should have waited for full dark. That was the smart play — come in on her blind side, take the compass, ask questions after. But I'd been four days on salt and eleven days on anger, and smart had worn thin. I tied the mule, checked the rifle out of habit and left it slung, and walked into the rocks with my hand on my knife and my heart going like a drum.

The fire was in a hollow no bigger than a grave. She sat with her back against the stone, one leg stretched out stiff, and she heard me before I cleared the last boulder — of course she did — but she didn't reach for a weapon. She just turned her head, and the firelight found her face, and every story I'd built about her came apart at once.

She was younger than me. Maybe twenty-five. Sun-cracked lips, a week of grime, a bandage wrapped around her left calf that had soaked through and dried brown. Dark hair cut short and ragged, like she'd done it herself with a knife. And her eyes — she looked at me the way you look at weather. Not afraid. Calculating. Deciding what kind of storm I was.

"Ranger," she said. Her voice was rough with thirst. "Took you long enough."

"You left a trail a child could follow."

"I left a trail a *ranger* could follow." She nodded at the ground beside her fire, an invitation or a dare. "There's water in the skin by your foot. I'd offer, but I'm told it's rude to poison a guest."

I didn't sit. I didn't take the water. I crouched at the edge of the firelight, out of lunging range, and held out my hand. "The compass. Give it here, and I'll bandage that leg, and in the morning we'll both walk out of this and I won't have to decide what the law says about thieves who lead me four days across the Sereen."

"You don't want the compass."

"The compass is mine."

"It's not, though." She said it mildly, the way you'd correct a small error of fact, and something in the mildness made the hair stand up on my neck. "That's the whole problem, ranger. It's not yours, and it's not mine, and I'd bet everything I own — which at the moment is one bad leg and a poisoned waterskin — that you don't actually know what it is."

"My father's compass. Stolen from my father's house."

"Your father." Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt. Recognition. She looked at me the way you look at a name you've heard read off a list. "Montrose. You're a Montrose."

"You know my father?"

"I know his name." She shifted, winced, pressed her palm flat against the stone behind her. "It's on a document. In a place I've been trying to reach for two years."

The wind came through the rocks then, cold off the escarpment, and the fire flattened and flared, and for a moment neither of us spoke. I heard myself say, "What document," in a voice that didn't sound like mine.

"Show me the compass and I'll show you."

Everything in me said no. Everything in me said take it off her, break her fingers if she argues, ride home, be done. But I'd spent four days following a trail she'd built by hand, stone by stone, and I was tired of not knowing why. I reached into my coat and drew out the compass.

She held out her hand. I hesitated — then set it in her palm, because I wanted to see what she'd do with it more than I wanted to keep it.

She opened the hinged lid with a thumbnail, tilted the brass face toward the fire, and turned it slowly, deliberately, until the needle settled. "Watch," she said. "Don't watch me. Watch the needle."

I watched the needle.

It swung. Not the few degrees of drift I'd grown up with, not the lazy wobble of an old instrument finding its rest. It swung a quarter turn, smooth and certain, and settled pointing east-southeast — out of the rocks, across the darkening flats behind us, toward the escarpment and the country beyond it. Toward the coast.

Toward the Rivergate.

"Your father wound that thing every morning for years," Isla Reyes said quietly. "Do you know why?"

"Because it was broken."

"Because it was *pointing*." She turned the compass so I could see her face over the top of it, firelit and fierce and suddenly very tired. "There's a lodestone in the base that shouldn't be there. Someone built this compass to answer to something, and that something is east of here, under about forty fathoms of seawater, in the hull of a prison ship called the Leeward Rivergate. Your father knew. I think he spent his whole life knowing. And I think he died before he could do anything about it."

The name went through me like cold water through a cracked hull. The Leeward Rivergate. I hadn't heard it spoken aloud since I was seventeen, since the night my father had come home white-faced from a meeting in Port Calloway and burned a stack of papers in the stove and told me, the only time in his life he'd ever raised his voice at me, *Whatever anyone ever offers you, Sebastian — whatever they promise, whatever they threaten — you never, never go near that ship.*

I'd asked him what ship. He'd looked at me like a man looking over a cliff, and he'd never said another word about it, and eleven years later he'd died with the compass in his hand telling me it pointed where it pointed.

"You knew him," I said. It wasn't a question. I didn't know how I knew, but I knew.

"I never met him." She closed the lid and held the compass out to me, and her hand was steady and her voice wasn't, quite. "But my mother was a prisoner on the Rivergate. She died there when I was nine. That compass was built to find her — or what she left behind her. And a man I've been hunting for two years has it in his ledgers that your father tried to deliver proof of what happened on that ship, and died for the trying, and that the proof is still down there, sealed in the hold, waiting for someone to come and get it."

The flats were dark now, all the silver gone out of the world, and the fire was the only light for a hundred miles, and I sat down in the sand across from her because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

"Who," I said. "Who has it in his ledgers."

"Aldous Crane."

The second name in an hour to come off a cliff. Crane. My father's old rival, the salvage man with the silver voice and the ledger of favors he collected like other men collected debts — and I owed him, God help me, I owed him four thousand crowns against my ranger's commission, a debt I'd signed in a bad winter two years back and been drowning under ever since. A debt he'd been very, very patient about.

I looked at the compass in my hand. The needle lay still now, but I could feel it the way you feel a held breath.

"Why steal it?" I asked her. "Why not come to me? I'd have helped. I didn't know any of this, but I'd have helped."

"Would you?" Isla Reyes leaned her head back against the stone and closed her eyes, and for the first time she looked as tired as I felt. "You spent eleven days hunting me across a salt flat to take this thing back and put it in a box. I didn't have eleven days. Crane's salvage fleet sails in three weeks, and when it reaches the Rivergate, whatever's in that hold burns with the ship, and my mother's name burns with it, and every name on every roll she smuggled out piece by piece for six years — gone. I stole the compass because the compass is the key, ranger, and I'm the only one alive who knows what it opens."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the flats, the mule shifted and stamped.

"And now," she said, opening her eyes, "you have a choice. Take it and go home and wind it every morning and never ask it where it's pointing. Or come with me and find out why your father died with this thing in his hand."

I didn't answer. I turned the compass over in my palm, and the brass was warm from her grip, and the needle trembled in its housing like something alive, like something patient, like something that had been waiting eleven years for me to stop running the other way.

In the morning, she said, there was an inn at the foot of the escarpment where a man was waiting to buy passage to the Rivergate — a man with a silver voice and a ledger full of favors. She'd been avoiding him for two years. She figured it was time somebody walked in and asked him what he knew.

I should have told her no. I should have taken my compass and my mule and my four thousand crowns of debt and ridden west until the sea was a rumor.

Instead I heard myself say, "This inn. Does it have a name?"

She almost smiled. "The Rivergate."
