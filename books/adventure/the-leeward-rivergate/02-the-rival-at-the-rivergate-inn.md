## 2. The Rival at the Rivergate Inn

The inn sat where the escarpment gave up and the salt began, a low stone building with a slate roof gone green and a sign that had once read THE RIVERGATE in gold leaf, now reading something closer to THE RIVERG TE. Smoke curled from its chimney the way smoke does in wind country — sideways, apologetic.

Isla stopped at the edge of the yard and looked at it the way you look at water you're about to cross.

"Two years," she said. "You want to know why I avoided him for two years?"

"I assumed it was because he wanted to arrest you."

"He wanted to *hire* me." She pulled her hood back. Her hair was still full of salt flat dust, and she didn't bother with it. "Aldous Crane doesn't chase people, Montrose. He arranges for them to arrive. The only question is whether you walk in on your own feet or he carries you."

"And which are we?"

She looked at me sidelong. "That's what we're here to find out."

The common room was dim and smelled of tallow and old fish stew. A fire burned low in a hearth big enough to roast an ox, and around it sat the particular population of places like this: two drovers playing dice, a woman mending a net that had no business being this far inland, and a boy of maybe ten hauling a bucket of water with the grim determination of someone who'd been told twice.

And at the corner table, alone, a man drinking tea.

I knew him before I saw his face. I knew him by the way he sat — spine straight, hands folded around the cup like it was a document he was reviewing — and by the fact that the drovers' dice game had gone quiet, and the woman with the net had turned her chair a few degrees toward him without seeming to notice she'd done it. Rooms rearranged themselves around Aldous Crane. They always had. My father used to say Crane could walk into a funeral and have everyone checking their pockets before the first hymn.

He was older than the last time I'd seen him. That was six years ago, at the graveside, standing at the edge of the mourners with his hat in his hands and an expression I'd spent six years deciding was either genuine grief or excellent craftsmanship. He'd put a hand on my shoulder then. I'd stepped away from it. He'd nodded as if I'd said something wise.

Now he looked up, and there was no surprise in his face at all.

"Sebastian." My name, warm as a hearthstone. "And you've brought company. Sit down, sit down. Marta!" — to the boy, who was not Marta — "two more of whatever this is, and the good cups."

"We haven't agreed to anything," Isla said, and sat down anyway.

"Of course not." Crane poured nothing; the tea came to us. It was terrible, oversteeped, bitter enough to strip paint. He drank his like it was a vintage. "Agreements come later. First, grief." His eyes went soft, and I hated how well they did it. "Six years, and you still carry him like a wound. Your father was the finest salvage man on this coast, Sebastian. Finest I ever competed against. Finest I ever lost to."

"You didn't lose to him," I said. "He retired."

"He won," Crane said simply. "There's a difference, and we both know it. He got out with his name clean and his hands empty, and I stayed in the game with everything but my soul. Some days I think he took the better bargain." He set the cup down. "Some days I know it."

Isla watched him the way a card player watches a dealer's hands.

"You sent for me," she said. "Two years of letters through three intermediaries. Here I am. Say it."

Crane smiled at her — genuinely, I thought, or with such craft I couldn't split the seam — and then he reached into his coat and laid three things on the table between us.

A chart. A coin. And a photograph, if you could call it that — one of those salt-print images, faded to the color of weak tea.

The chart showed the coast north of the calving grounds. The coin was a crown, minted the year I was born. The photograph showed a ship.

I had seen drawings of her before. Every kid who grew up on this coast had heard the story around fires: the *Leeward Rivergate*, prison ship, three hundred souls aboard when she went onto the rocks inside the fjord during the great storm of the last century. No survivors worth counting. No wreck worth finding, because the fjord ate everything and kept it. The glacier above her had been calving into the water for a hundred years, grinding the site under ice, under rock, under cold black water that would kill a diver in four minutes.

Except the chart wasn't a hundred years old. It was new. Survey-fresh, ink still sharp, depth soundings marked in a hand I recognized.

My father's hand.

The room went very quiet, or I went very quiet inside it, which amounts to the same thing.

"Where did you get this?" I asked. My voice came out level. I'd learned that from him too.

"From your father," Crane said. "Eleven years ago. Six months before he died." He tapped the soundings. "He surveyed the fjord himself. Alone, in a boat barely bigger than that ketch Reyes is so fond of. He came to me afterward with this chart and a proposal, and I turned him down, and he never spoke to me again, and eleven months later he was dead on a road outside Corvane with a debt on his books that nobody could explain."

Isla's head came up slowly. "What kind of proposal?"

"The kind I refused." Crane leaned back. "Your father believed the *Rivergate* wasn't just a wreck. He believed she was a sealed room. Prison ships keep records, Miss Reyes — manifests, rolls, correspondence. Everything a magistrate wants hidden goes aboard a prison ship, because dead men don't file complaints and drowned paper doesn't testify. Elias believed there was a manifest in that hull that would hang a man who is currently a magistrate. He wanted to raise it. I wanted no part of it. And now—" he spread his hands, and the lamplight caught the rings on his fingers, "—now I find myself wanting very much to be part of it after all."

"Why the change of heart?"

"Because the man on that manifest ruined me too." For a moment — just a moment — the silver voice dropped its polish, and underneath it was something old and scoured. "Magistrate Halloway. You'll have heard the name. Everyone has. He owns half the coastal trade, a third of the courts, and all of the salvage licenses north of the capes. Including, as of last month, the license on the *Leeward Rivergate* herself."

Isla went still in a way I was learning to read. Stillness, with her, meant calculation running hot.

"He's mounting a fleet," she said.

"He's mounting a fleet." Crane nodded. "Three ships, professional divers, steam winches, and a salvage master out of Vell who's raised wrecks in worse water. They'll be underway within the fortnight. When they bring up whatever's in that hull, it will go into a furnace in Corvane harbor, and every name on that manifest will stay dead forever. Along with every claim, every grievance, every family still waiting to learn what happened to their people." He looked at me. "Unless someone gets there first."

The fire popped. Out in the yard, the mule stamped.

"You're asking us," I said carefully, "to beat a licensed salvage fleet to a wreck inside a glacier fjord, in calving season, with—" I looked at Isla, who supplied it:

"—six tidal windows. Maybe five, if the ice comes early. The fjord mouth only opens on the big spring tides, and after the sixth, the calving starts in earnest and nothing goes in or out until summer."

"There," Crane said, pleased, as if she'd recited a lesson well. "You see? She knows the route. Do you know how many people on this coast know that route, Miss Reyes?"

"One other," she said. "And he's sailing for Halloway."

Crane's smile thinned by a degree. "Captain Sloane. Yes. Which is why speed matters more than strength. We will not out-muscle that fleet. We will out-*time* them."

I looked down at the chart again, at my father's soundings, at the little annotation in the margin I hadn't let myself read yet. I made myself read it now.

*Fjord holds her whole. God forgive me for looking.*

My father had found her. Eleven years ago, he'd found the *Leeward Rivergate*, intact, in cold black water, and he'd come ashore carrying proof, and he'd been refused, and then he'd been dead. And I had spent six years hating the debt that killed him without once asking who wrote it.

"Here is my offer," Crane said, and his voice came back up to its silver register, smooth as poured oil. "Sebastian. Four thousand crowns. Cancelled. Today, on my word, witnessed by—" he gestured pleasantly at the room, at the net-mender, at the dice players who weren't playing, "—witnessed by whoever you like. Your father's books cleared. Your name clean. In exchange: you captain this salvage. You take Miss Reyes and whatever crew she can raise, you run the fjord on the spring tides, and you bring me that manifest before Halloway's fleet drops anchor."

"And if we don't beat them?"

"Then you'll have had an adventure, and I'll still clear the debt, because I'm generous with failures." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Though of course, if the fleet arrives first, none of us will be in a position to discuss generosity."

Isla laughed once, short and sharp. "You're asking them to die for your leverage."

"I'm asking them to dive for justice," Crane said, "and I'm paying for the privilege. Those aren't mutually exclusive, Miss Reyes, whatever your politics tell you." He turned back to me, and now the warmth came off him like heat off a stove, deliberate, engineered, aimed. "I knew your father thirty years. We built each other, he and I — every trick I know, I learned losing to him. He died owing money he never borrowed, to men he never met, and it has eaten at me for six years that I did nothing. Let me do this one thing. Let me finish what he started. Let me give him back his good name."

It was a beautiful speech. That was the trouble with Aldous Crane; everything he said was beautiful, and beauty is the most reliable disguise in the world.

Because here is what I knew, sitting there with bad tea going cold in front of me: my father had refused this job. Not declined — *refused*. I'd grown up on the story the way other kids grow up on lullabies. *The Rivergate stays down*, he used to say, when I asked about the fjord, and he'd say it in a voice I never heard him use for anything else, and my mother would change the subject fast, and once, only once, I saw him burn papers in the yard and stand watching them until they were ash and then some.

He surveyed that wreck. He carried the chart ashore. And then he spent the rest of his life making sure nobody went back — including, especially, me.

So why was his chart on Aldous Crane's table?

"Who else has seen this?" I asked.

"No one living."

"Then why do you still have it? If he wanted it buried, why give it to the one man on this coast who'd want to dig?"

Crane held my gaze, and for the first time since the graveside, I couldn't read him at all. Something moved behind his face — regret, or memory, or arithmetic; with Crane the three were cousins.

"Because he didn't give it to me, Sebastian. He left it to me. In his will. With a letter I have never shown anyone, saying that if the day ever came when Halloway moved on the fjord, I was to put this chart in the hands of whoever had the best chance of beating him." He paused. "He named you."

The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked. Isla was watching me, and I realized she'd been watching me this whole time, cataloguing, the way she'd tracked me across forty miles of salt flat — except now she wasn't hunting, she was waiting, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened me more than the chart did.

Four thousand crowns. My father's name. A wreck he died keeping closed.

And a compass in my pocket whose needle, when I'd checked it at dawn, wasn't pointing north at all. It was pointing northeast. Toward the coast. Toward the fjord. Steady as a heartbeat, like it had somewhere to be.

"Six tides," I said finally. "One ketch. A crew that doesn't exist yet. Against three ships and the best salvage master money can hire." I looked at Crane. "If I say yes, I say yes for the manifest — for the names on it, for the families. Not for you. You get your leverage out of whatever's left over, and if I find out you've played us, Crane, I will spend the rest of my life making sure the coast learns exactly what kind of man you are. My father's rival or not."

For just a moment, Aldous Crane looked delighted.

"Oh, Elias," he murmured, to no one, to the dead, to the tea. "He talks just like you." Then, aloud, briskly, businesslike, the mask of the merchant sliding back on: "Terms are drawn tonight. Provisioning tomorrow. Miss Reyes — your crew. How soon?"

Isla drained her terrible tea and stood.

"Tam's in Port Lachlan, drunk and brilliant. Okafor's diving the kelp beds off Sere Point, if he's speaking to me. Brannigan's cooking at a mining camp and hates it." She pulled her hood up. "Give me four days."

"Three," said Crane.

She looked at him for a long moment. "Three," she conceded, and walked out into the wind.

I stayed one minute longer. Crane was gathering his chart, his coin, his ghost of a photograph, and as he slid my father's soundings back into their oilcloth sleeve, he said, without looking up:

"For what it's worth, Sebastian — your father's last letter to me ended with a sentence I've thought about every day since." He tucked the sleeve inside his coat, over his heart, or where a heart would be. "It said: *If my son asks you why, tell him the ship isn't holding prisoners anymore. It's holding witnesses.*"

I walked out into the wind with those words rattling in my chest like loose shot, and found Isla already at the gate, already facing northeast, toward water I couldn't see.

"Well?" she said.

I should have told her no. God knows my father had.

Instead I heard myself say, "How fast can that ketch of yours actually sail?"
