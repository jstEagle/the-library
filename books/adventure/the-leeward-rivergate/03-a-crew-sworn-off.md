## 3. A Crew Sworn Off

Isla Reyes had a list of three names, and she'd spent the whole ride south from the Rivergate Inn pretending it was a list of none.

The compass rode in her coat pocket, warm as a coal. Sebastian rode half a length behind her, silent as a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the sum of. He'd agreed to this — to Crane's job, to her crew, to her — with the expression of someone signing a confession. She understood. She'd signed a few herself.

The first name was two days south, in a shipbreaking yard at the mouth of the Saltwash, where they stripped dead hulls down to their bones and sold the bones by the ton.

* * *

They found Tam Voss up to his elbows in a boiler that had died a decade before he was born to it, if his face was anything to go by. He was forty now, maybe forty-one, and the years since she'd last seen him had salted his hair gray at the temples and carved a line between his brows deep enough to lose a coin in. He heard their boots on the grating and spoke without turning around.

"Yard's closed to salvagers. We got nothing worth your time."

"Hello to you too, Tam."

The wrench stopped. He turned slowly, the way a man turns when he already knows what he'll see and is giving himself one last chance to be wrong. His eyes went to her face, then to Sebastian, then back to her, and whatever he read there made him set the wrench down with exaggerated care, like it was a loaded thing.

"Isla." He said her name the way you'd say the name of a storm that had already taken something from you. "They told me you were dead. I was glad, for about a week. Then I felt bad about being glad."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair. It's generous." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago. "The Meridian went down with nine souls, Isla. Nine. And you walked out of the water."

"Three of us walked out."

"Three." He laughed, and there was no warmth in it, just the shape of a laugh worn hollow. "You counted. That's the part I can't get past. You stood on that beach and you *counted*, and then you kept walking."

Isla had rehearsed this on the road. She'd had speeches. She'd had the whole architecture of apology laid out, load-bearing walls of contrition and windows of explanation. Standing in the boiler-smell and the ringing heat of the yard, all of it collapsed into one plain sentence.

"I'm not here to ask you to forgive me. I'm here to ask you to work."

Tam looked at Sebastian. "Who's this?"

"Montrose," Sebastian said. "My father was Elian Montrose."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes — recognition, and under it, the quick flicker of a man doing his own arithmetic. "The ranger. The one who died refusing a salvage contract." He looked back at her. "And let me guess whose contract you're carrying now."

"Crane's."

"God help us." Tam sat down on an anvil that had outlived its trade, and for a long moment he just breathed the yard air, oil and rust and hot metal, the smell of things being unmade. "You know why I left the Meridian crew, Isla? It wasn't the wreck. Wrecks happen. It was that after, when we were pulling survivors out, you went back down. Fourth time. Fifth. Your hands were bleeding through the gloves and Okafor had to physically drag you up, and you looked at me — I was holding the line, I was the one holding your line — and you said, *one more*. You said *one more* like the sea keeps books and we were ahead."

He shook his head slowly.

"I left because I couldn't be the man holding your line anymore. Not because I stopped believing you'd find what you were looking for. Because I believed it too much, and it was going to kill us all, one *one more* at a time."

Isla held his gaze. "This job has a number on it. Six tides. Hard stop. The fjord closes after that and it closes for a season, and nobody — not me, not Crane, not the sea itself — gets an *one more* out of it."

"Six tides." Tam's mouth twisted. "You've counted the tides now. That's new."

"That's what the last three years bought me."

He was quiet a long time. Somewhere across the yard a crane groaned and dropped a hull plate, and the sound rolled through the ground like distant thunder.

"The engine on your boat," he said finally. "What is it?"

"Vagrant Mercy. Old ketch, auxiliary single-screw. She's held together with prayer and baling wire."

"She'd want to be, if you're sailing her." He stood, and Isla felt the moment tip — felt it the way you feel a scale balance, that held-breath instant before it commits. "I'll look at her engine. *Look* at it. That's not a yes."

"It's not a no."

"It's not a no," he agreed, and picked up his wrench, and turned back to his dead boiler, and that was as much farewell as he would give her.

* * *

Okafor was harder to find and easier to read, because Okafor had never learned to hide anything, which was the whole problem with him.

They tracked him to a fishing village on the Graywater coast where he worked the mussel beds and, the locals said, dove the winter wrecks for scrap when the money ran thin. He was in the water when they arrived. Isla stood on the shingle and watched the dark shape of him move along the bottom of the cove, patient and unhurried, a man at home in the one place that had nearly killed him, and felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name.

He surfaced twenty minutes later, saw her, and went back under.

He stayed under long enough to make his point. When he finally waded out, dripping, he didn't greet her. He stood there with the water running off him and looked at her the way you look at a tide chart — assessing, unimpressed.

"Three years," Okafor said.

"Three years."

"You didn't write."

"You didn't either."

"I was ashamed." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said everything. "You were the one who should have been ashamed, so I assumed the shame was taken."

Sebastian made a sound that might have been a cough. Isla shot him a look and turned back.

"I need a diver, Okafor. Cold water, deep water. A wreck in a fjord under a glacier."

"I know the fjord." He wrung out his sleeve. "I surveyed the meltwater channels there, two summers back. Before the fishing consortium pulled out." He paused, and Isla saw him decide to give her the next part. "I know things about that fjord that aren't on any chart."

"Then you know why the window's six tides."

"I know why the window is *generously* six tides, if the calving holds to pattern, which glaciers do not do." He crouched, picked up a flat stone, and skimmed it out across the cove — four skips, five, clean and effortless. "You want to know what I said when I left the Meridian crew?"

"You didn't say anything. You just left."

"I left because I pulled you out of that water five times, and the sixth time I looked at you in the water and I thought: she will not come up. She has decided. And I could not spend the rest of my life diving after a woman who has decided." He stood. "So. Has she decided?"

The question hung there. Sebastian, to his credit, said nothing. This was hers to answer.

"Isla Reyes has decided," she said carefully, "that the Leeward Rivergate has something in her that three years of running couldn't drown. And she's decided to go get it with a hard stop and a crew that can call the dive off — *I* will call it off — if it goes wrong. That's the offer. That's the whole offer."

"And the compass?" Okafor's eyes flicked to her coat pocket, sharp as a gaff hook. "You think I didn't hear? A ranger's compass that points where it shouldn't. You think the whole coast doesn't talk about the thief who stole a dead man's compass and then didn't sell it?"

"The coast talks too much."

"The coast is right sometimes." He looked at her a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a small, tired, real smile. "You still carry your mother's knife."

Isla's hand went to her belt before she could stop it. He nodded, satisfied, like he'd gotten the answer to a different question than the one he'd asked.

"I'll dive your fjord," Okafor said. "On one condition. When I say *up*, we go up. All of us. Even you. *Especially* you."

"Agreed."

"Say it like you mean it."

"Okafor." She held his eyes. "When you say up, we go up."

He stuck out his hand, and they shook, diver's fashion, and that was that — easier than Tam by miles, and somehow heavier for it, because Okafor had never stopped being her friend, and friends were the ones you could actually hurt.

* * *

Brannigan they found where Brannigan always was when the world ended: in a kitchen.

He had a galley of his own now, a dockside hash house in the port of Leeward's Landing that smelled of fry-bread and black coffee and the particular grease of feeding sailors on the cheap. He was enormous and gray and moved around his stove with the grace of a much smaller man, and when he saw Isla come through the door he did not drop anything, did not shout, did not even slow down. He finished the order he was plating, set it on the pass, called the next one, and then untied his apron and told his line cook he was taking ten minutes.

They sat at the corner table, the one with the wobbly leg. Brannigan folded his huge hands on the table and looked at her.

"Before you say anything," he said, "I want you to know I dreamed about it. The Meridian. Every night for a year. You know what's in the dream? Not the sinking. The *soup*. I was making soup when she hit, and in the dream I never finish the soup. I just keep stirring it while the water comes in." His voice was steady, which was somehow worse. "Three years, and the dreams stopped last spring. You know why I'm telling you that? Because I'm about to say yes to you, and I want you to know exactly what it costs."

Isla's throat was tight. "Brannigan—"

"You fed nine people on that boat. You kept them alive through a bad season and a worse one, and when the hull went, you were the last one up the ladder because you were making sure everyone else was through it. I know that. I've always known that." He leaned forward. "But here's what I need you to understand, girl. It wasn't your fault, and I left anyway. Not because of guilt. Because I couldn't be the one ladling out hope every morning to a crew that was following you into the deep water. A cook's job is to send people out warm. You kept sending them out into the cold."

"So why say yes now?"

He was quiet for a moment. Through the pass window they could see the harbor, and at anchor in it, small and patched and stubborn, the Vagrant Mercy, with Tam already swarming over her engine housing like a man reading an old letter.

"Because of him," Brannigan said, nodding at Sebastian. "The ranger. His father turned down this job and died for the turning down. And because of her." He nodded at Isla's pocket, at the compass. "And because I make soup, Isla, and I have been making it for strangers for three years, and a man can only feed strangers so long before he needs to feed his own again."

He stood, retrieved his apron, and tied it back on.

"I'll need two days to close the hash house. Marta can have it, she's been carrying it anyway. And I'm telling you now —" he pointed a spoon at her, and it was the old spoon, the Meridian spoon, she'd have sworn to it — "six tides or sixty, everybody eats. That's the rule. Nobody makes a hard decision hungry. Not on my boat."

"Your boat?"

"Our boat," Brannigan said. "That's how this works. Everybody's boat, everybody's grave. You taught me that too."

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide, the four of them and the ghost of a fifth — the Meridian herself, riding in the hold of every one of them like ballast they couldn't pump out.

Isla stood at the wheel and felt the strange, terrifying lightness of a crew again. Tam had the engine running smooth enough to purr and had already started a list of things wrong with it, delivered in a tone of grim delight. Okafor was forward, coiling line with the economy of a man who had never once wasted a motion. Brannigan had claimed the galley inside an hour and was already complaining about the state of her stores, which meant he was already planning meals, which meant he was already home.

Sebastian came up beside her and watched the coast fall away.

"They all said yes," he said. "I kept waiting for the no."

"The no comes later," Isla said. "It always does. The trick is being worth the yes by the time it does."

He looked at her — that long, measuring ranger's look — and then out at the water, where the morning haze was burning off the swells. She followed his gaze and felt her stomach drop, because there, far out on the gray line of the horizon, sails. More than one. Big ones, ranked and steady, moving with the unhurried confidence of a fleet that had already been paid.

Sebastian saw them too. He said nothing. Neither did she.

The compass in her pocket swung gently against her hip, needle steady, pointing north — toward the fjord, toward the wreck, toward whatever her mother had left in the dark water waiting for her.

Six tides. She'd counted them herself.

She just hadn't counted on being counted.
