## 4. Six Tides

The Vagrant Mercy smelled of tar, old coffee, and Tam's engine grease — a smell Sebastian would come to think of as the smell of being committed to something stupid.

He sat in the cramped stern cabin with the chart table bolted down under his elbows, and he spread out everything Crane's courier had delivered in the oilskin packet: coastal charts going back forty years, a hand-inked glacier calendar annotated by someone with a surgeon's patience, and six pages of tidal calculations for the Leeward Fjord, each one dated, each one signed with a single looping initial — *A.C.*

Isla stood across the table from him, arms folded, watching him read the way a card sharp watches a mark count his chips.

"Well?" she said.

Sebastian didn't answer right away. He was looking at the fjord chart, at the long blue throat of water cutting twelve miles into the icefield, narrowing at its mouth to a channel less than a quarter mile wide. The wreck was marked — not on any official survey but penciled onto this copy in faded graphite, at a depth sounding that made his stomach tighten. Ninety feet. Maybe more, if the glacier had shifted since whoever drew this line had drawn it.

"Six," he said finally.

"Six what?"

"Tidal windows." He turned the calendar around so she could see it. "The mouth of the fjord is choked with a sill — a moraine ridge the glacier dumped there centuries ago. At low water there's maybe nine feet over it. The Mercy draws seven with her keel patched the way Tam patched her." He tapped the numbers. "We need a spring tide. A big one. And according to this—" he flipped to the glacier calendar, where someone had marked the calving season in red hatching like a wound, "—the icefield starts shedding bergs into the fjord in nineteen days. After that, nothing goes in or out. Not a ketch. Not a rowboat. Not a prayer."

Isla leaned over the chart. Her hair fell forward and she pushed it back with an impatient knuckle. "So we go in on the first window, dive on the second and third, come out on the fourth, and keep two in reserve."

"That's the plan if you're an optimist."

"And if I'm not?"

"Then you plan for five dives in three windows and pray the ice is late."

She looked up at him. In the lamplight her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, and they were doing that thing they did — measuring him, weighing whether he was the kind of man who'd fold under pressure or the kind who got sharper. He still hadn't decided what she was. Thief, certainly. Liar, probably. But she'd vouched for this route in front of Crane, and she'd done it with the flat certainty of someone who had actually been inside that fjord before.

"You've seen it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I've seen the mouth." She straightened up. "Three years ago, running cargo nobody asks about. We came down the coast in fog and I saw the ice wall from offshore, blue as a bruise, and the sill breaking white across it. My skipper wouldn't go near it. Said the fjord eats ships that are already dead." She shrugged. "He wasn't wrong about much, that man."

* * *

Tam found them an hour later, or rather announced himself from the companionway with a clatter of tools and a stream of profanity aimed at some component only he could see.

"The injector pump," he said by way of greeting, dropping into the bench seat across from Sebastian like a sack of mail. Tam was sixty if he was a day, built like a barrel stave, with forearms tattooed in faded anchor ink and hands that shook slightly unless they were holding a wrench. "Half rebuilt. She'll run, but she won't run pretty. You want speed, you pray. You want reliability, you don't push past four knots."

"We don't need speed," Isla said. "We need precision."

"Precision." Tam laughed, a dry rattle. "Girl, I've known that engine since before you were stealing things. Precision is not in her vocabulary. Grit is in her vocabulary. Spite, maybe."

Okafor came down next, ducking under the deckhead beam, carrying a coil of dive line over one shoulder like a stole. He was the quietest of them, a broad, deliberate man who moved through the world as though it were made of water and he'd simply learned to swim through air too. He set the coil down, studied the chart without being invited, and put one thick finger on the pencil mark of the wreck.

"Ninety feet," he said. "Cold water. Silt bottom, if the river runoff is what I think it is. Visibility down there will be an arm's length on a good day."

"You've dove cold silt?" Sebastian asked.

"Fifteen years. Lakes, harbors, one reservoir they didn't tell us had a town at the bottom until we were down in it." He said it without drama, the way another man might mention weather. "Ninety feet in glacial water, you get maybe twenty minutes of work per diver, and your hands stop being hands after ten. Whatever's in that hold, we bring it up fast or we don't bring it up."

Brannigan arrived last, because Brannigan arrived last everywhere, carrying a pot of something that claimed to be stew. He was round and gray and cheerful in the way of men who had survived enough disasters to find them funny. He wedged himself into the corner, ladled out bowls unasked, and peered at the charts upside down.

"So how long have we got, then?" he said. "Before the big ice does the big thing."

"Nineteen days to calving season," Sebastian said. "Six usable tides before then."

Brannigan slurped his stew thoughtfully. "And Crane worked all this out for us. Kind of him."

Nobody answered that. But everybody heard it.

* * *

It was Brannigan's comment that kept Sebastian up that night, sitting alone in the stern cabin with the lamp turned low and the tide tables fanned out in front of him like a losing hand.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop turning over: the calculations were perfect.

Not good. Not close. *Perfect.* Crane's figures matched the almanac to the minute — Sebastian had checked them twice against his father's own battered tide tables, the ones he kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his kit, the ones he'd sworn he'd never open again. Every spring tide, every neap, every correction for the fjord's odd resonance, where the water sloshed in that deep stone throat like water in a bottle and ran twenty minutes behind the coast. Whoever drew these pages knew the Leeward Fjord the way a man knows his own heartbeat.

And that was the problem.

His father had refused this job. Sebastian knew that the way you know things that were never said aloud in a house — through doors held shut, through his mother's silence, through the one night he'd heard his father shouting in the yard at a visitor whose voice he now, with a cold turn of his gut, recognized as Aldous Crane's. His father had died a year later chasing a debt that never should have existed, in a country he shouldn't have been in, carrying papers nobody would ever let him show anybody.

And now Crane — who had hated his father, who had competed with him for twenty years across half the salvage grounds on the coast — handed Sebastian a packet containing flawless tide work for the exact job his father died refusing, timed to the day, timed to the *tide*, with a bonus payment schedule that cleared Sebastian's debt on delivery of the manifest.

You didn't get that kind of precision from hatred. Hatred was sloppy. This was care.

Sebastian spread the six tide pages out in a row and looked at them the way he'd once looked at tracks in sand — reading not the animal but the animal's intention. First window: entry, dawn tide, slack water at the sill for forty minutes. Second and third: dive days, midday slacks, longest windows of the cycle. Fourth: exit. Fifth and sixth: reserve. It was a beautiful plan. It was a plan drawn by someone who wanted them to succeed.

Or someone who wanted them to be exactly where those pages put them, exactly when those pages put them there.

He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Above him, through the deck planks, he could hear the small sounds of the ship at anchor — the halyards ticking against the mast, Tam's muttering, Brannigan's off-key humming from the galley. Four strangers and a thief, floating on a patched hull above sixty feet of dark water, about to sail into a stone throat full of ice because a man who owed his family nothing good had asked nicely.

A knock. Soft. Then Isla's head appeared in the doorway without waiting for an answer, which told him everything about how the knock was meant.

"You're up," she said.

"I'm working."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Brooders sit in the dark; workers light the lamp." She came in anyway, dropped into the opposite bench, and pulled one of the tide pages toward herself. For a while she just read, and he watched her read — quick, hungry, the way she did everything, like the world might take the page back if she slowed down.

"Crane's good," she said finally.

"He's too good."

"Mm." She set the page down and looked at him. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of what?"

"The rest of the thing you've been chewing since dinner. You've got a face like a man chewing gravel. Say it."

So he said it. He laid it out the way he'd lay out a trackline: the perfection of the figures, the knowledge of the fjord's resonance that no chart showed, the timing so exact it felt less like planning than like scheduling. "Either Aldous Crane has spent thirty years studying a fjord he claims he's never worked," Sebastian said, "or somebody gave him these numbers. Somebody who knows that water from the inside."

Isla was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "Or Crane's been planning this longer than he told us. Long enough to send people in ahead of us. Long enough to know things about that wreck he hasn't shared."

"That's the other track," Sebastian admitted. "That he's not sending us to salvage the Rivergate. He's sending us to do the dangerous part of somebody else's salvage."

"And when we've opened the hold and bled on the ice, whoever's waiting outside collects." She nodded slowly. "That's a shape I recognize. That's the oldest shape there is."

They sat with that, the lamp hissing between them, the ship breathing underneath.

"So why are you still here?" Sebastian asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "You're a thief. You're quick. If you smelled this same shape, you could be over the rail and gone before Brannigan finished the dishes."

Isla turned the tide page over, ran her thumb along the crease, and didn't look up.

"Because my mother's name is somewhere in that hold," she said quietly, "and I've spent eleven years not knowing which prison ship took her. Now I know. So either Crane is using me, or Crane is useful to me, and honestly, Montrose, I don't care which, as long as that ship opens." She looked up then, and there was nothing soft in her face at all. "But I'm glad you're paranoid. Keep being paranoid. One of us has to count the silver."

She left. Sebastian sat a while longer, then blew out the lamp and lay in his bunk listening to the water slap the hull, counting tides instead of sheep.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

* * *

They weighed anchor on the third morning, and the coast came apart around them into the gray-on-gray of early sea mist, and by noon the wind had backed around and Tam's engine settled into its four-knot grudge, and the Vagrant Mercy ran north with the land falling away on their left like a page being slowly turned.

Sebastian took the tiller after midday. Isla worked the foredeck, re-bending a jib with Okafor, and he found himself watching the two of them — the easy shorthand between them, the way she pointed and he understood without words — and feeling the strange double weight of it: these were people he'd have arrested three weeks ago, and now their lives were lashed to his decisions like dinghies behind a boat.

Toward evening the mist thinned, and the coast ahead resolved into something none of the charts had quite prepared him for: a white line along the horizon, low and hard, like the edge of a blade laid flat against the sea. The icefield. Even from fifteen miles out you could feel it — the air coming off it was colder than the air around it, a private climate, a warning written in temperature.

Brannigan climbed up beside him with two mugs of tea and handed one over without asking.

"There she is," the old cook said. "Biggest thing on earth that doesn't care about us."

"Aye."

"You've got your sums done, then? Six tides and all?"

"Sums are done."

Brannigan drank his tea and squinted north. "Funny thing about sums," he said comfortably. "Man gives you numbers, you check 'em. Man gives you *perfect* numbers—" he shook his head slowly, "—that's when I start checking the man."

Sebastian looked at him. "You too, huh."

"Forty years afloat, boy. Nobody's ever handed me anything perfect that didn't cost extra later." Brannigan drained his mug and started back down the deck, then paused. "Oh — nearly forgot. Tam says come look at the wireless before you turn in. Says it's been picking up traffic since noon. Ship chatter, working frequencies. Lots of it." He scratched under his cap. "More than fishing boats make, he says. More than anybody makes, way out here."

The white line lay on the horizon, patient as arithmetic.

Six tides, Sebastian thought. And somebody else was already counting them too.
