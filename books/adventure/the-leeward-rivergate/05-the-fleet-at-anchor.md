## 5. The Fleet at Anchor

Isla saw the smoke before she saw the ships.

Three threads of it, thin and black as spider silk, standing off the horizon to the northeast where no honest vessel had any business burning coal that dirty. She was on the foredeck of the *Vagrant Mercy* with a mug of Brannigan's terrible coffee going cold in her hand, and she had been watching that smudge for the better part of an hour before she let herself name it.

A fleet. Under way. Running the same coast they were.

"Tam," she called down the hatch. "Come look at this and tell me I'm wrong."

Tam came up wiping grease from his hands with a rag that only made it worse, squinted northeast for a long moment, and said nothing. That was worse than anything he could have said. Tam filled silences the way other men filled rooms — completely, and with visible effort.

"That's three funnels," he said finally. "Maybe four. Steam colliers, by the color of it. Nobody burns that filth unless they're buying fuel by the ton and don't care who sees them coming."

"Salvage fleet," Isla said.

"Salvage fleet," Tam agreed.

The wind came off the water cold and clean, carrying the smell of salt and kelp and, underneath it, the faint mineral bite of glacier melt still forty miles distant. Six days out from the Rivergate Inn. Five days since Crane had laid his offer on the table like a hand of cards he already knew the value of. Four days since Okafor had come aboard without a word, dropped his sea bag on the deck, and started checking the dive gear as if the intervening years hadn't happened.

And now this.

"How far?" Tam asked.

"At their speed? A day ahead of us. Maybe less." Isla set the coffee down on the deck rail. Her hands were steady. She made them be steady. "They're not racing us, Tam. They don't know we exist. They're just... ahead."

"That's better, isn't it?"

"It would be," Isla said, "if I didn't recognize that formation."

She did. God help her, she did. Three ships in echelon with the big one — the one burning the dirtiest coal — holding back half a mile so her funnel smoke wouldn't foul the survey vessels' air intakes. That was textbook. That was *Verity Sloane's* textbook, because Verity Sloane had written half of it herself, back when Isla had been her second and the two of them had been going to own the northern salvage routes together, and —

Back when Isla had sold her out.

She picked the coffee back up and drank it cold. It deserved to be drunk cold.

* * *

Sebastian found her at the wheel an hour later, which meant he'd been avoiding her for an hour first, which meant Brannigan had told him about the smoke. The man moved around a boat like someone perpetually surprised to find himself on one — sure-footed enough, but always a half-beat late, a desert animal doing sums about water. He'd spent most of the voyage so far either studying tide tables or studying her, and she hadn't decided yet which she liked less.

"You've seen it," he said. Not a question.

"Three steamers in echelon, big one trailing. Salvage configuration." She kept her eyes on the compass — on *his father's* compass, bolted now to the binnacle where everyone could see it, its needle swinging lazy and certain toward the fjord. "Crane said we had time. Crane said the fleet was still fitting out in the southern yards."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed what was useful to believe." She said it flatly, because lying to Sebastian Montrose was a waste of breath and they both knew it. He had a face built for catching lies, all angles and attention. "That's the whole trade, ranger. You believe what's useful until the facts get expensive enough to change your mind."

"The facts just got expensive."

"They usually do."

He came and stood beside her at the wheel, close enough that she could see the salt drying white along the seams of his coat. He didn't touch the wheel. He never touched the wheel. It was one of the few things about him she'd decided might be a virtue rather than a flaw — a man who understood that a boat had one captain or none.

"So who commands it?" he asked.

"Why do you think anyone does?"

"Because nobody runs three ships in echelon by committee. Somebody's making decisions over there. Somebody good." He nodded at the smoke. "You recognized something. Tam said you went quiet, and Tam says you never go quiet."

"I'm going quiet right now."

"Isla."

She sighed and gave the wheel a needless correction, two degrees to starboard, the kind of thing you did when your hands wanted something to betray. Below them the *Vagrant Mercy* shouldered through a long grey swell, patched sails drawing well enough, Tam's resurrected engine thumping below decks like a heart with a murmur.

"Her name is Verity Sloane," Isla said.

Something shifted in Sebastian's posture. Small, but she caught it. He'd been a lawman's son and a lawman himself too long not to know what a name meant when it landed like that.

"She commanded the *Kestrel*," Isla went on, because stopping halfway was worse than finishing. "Salvage brig, fast, mean, the best hull on this coast. I was her diving master for four years. We ran the Sable Shoals together, the Corvane wreck, the whole drowned length of the war coast. There wasn't a job on the water we couldn't do." She paused. "There wasn't a wreck we couldn't open."

"What happened?"

"I did."

The word sat between them. Sebastian, to his credit, didn't fill it. He'd learned that much about her already, or maybe he'd been born knowing it — that some confessions needed room the way a wound needed air.

"There was a wreck off Cape Merrow," Isla said. "Prison transport, same era as the Rivergate, same magistrate's fingerprints all over the manifests — though we didn't know that part then, or I tell you honestly, I don't know what I'd have done. Sloane wanted to report the find to the coastal authority. Standard practice. Wrecks with government dead belong to the families, and the families pay recovery fees, and it's clean money and it keeps the guilds off your back." Her jaw tightened. "I told her the cargo hold was empty. I told her the manifest had burned. And then I went back at night with my own boat and stripped the strongboxes myself and sold what was in them to a broker in Salt Harbor who didn't ask questions."

"What was in them?"

"Letters. Prisoners' letters. Sealed correspondence the transport was carrying home from the colony — last words, mostly. People pay obscene money for last words, ranger. Grief is the richest market there is." She heard her own voice go dry and clinical and hated it, and made herself stop. "Sloane found out. Of course she found out. She tracked me to Salt Harbor and stood in the broker's doorway while I counted the take, and she looked at me the way you'd look at a hull breach — like something that had been sound right up until the moment it killed everyone aboard."

"What did she do?"

"She didn't turn me in. That's the worst of it. She could have. Guild tribunal, blacklisting, the works — I'd have earned every hour of it. Instead she took the broker's ledger, burned it in front of me, and told me that if I ever worked another wreck she was commanding, she'd put me through her own personal court, and her court didn't have appeals." Isla laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "Then she paid the families' fees out of her own share. Every one. Took her six years. I checked."

Sebastian was quiet a moment. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because in about a day, that woman is going to look across the water at this boat, and she is going to see me standing on the deck of a ship racing hers to a prison wreck full of sealed documents, and everything she ever suspected about me is going to stand up and wave." Isla turned and faced him fully. "So you need to hear it from me first, and you need to decide, right now, whether you can sail with a thief who's already stolen once from the dead. Because I stole from those families, Sebastian. I sold their grief by the pound. And I am on this ship for reasons that start with my mother and end somewhere I can't see yet, but I will not pretend to you that my hands were clean before Crane ever found me."

The swell lifted the bow and set it down. Somewhere below, Brannigan was singing something tuneless and cheerful about a girl from the estuary, and the engine thumped, and the compass needle held its line toward the ice.

"My father carried debts that weren't his," Sebastian said at last. "I've spent nine years paying them. So I know exactly how heavy a thing gets when you carry it long enough — you stop being able to tell where it ends and you begin." He looked at her, and there was no softness in it, which she was grateful for; softness would have been unbearable. "I'm not asking you to be clean, Isla. I'm asking you to be pointed. Same as the compass."

"And if Sloane comes for us?"

"Then we'll deal with Sloane."

"You say that like it's simple."

"I say that about everything. It's a character flaw." But his mouth twitched, almost, and he turned away toward the bow, and Isla stood at the wheel and felt the old shame settle into its familiar place under her ribs — heavier than ballast, more useful than ballast too, because shame kept a heading when nothing else would.

* * *

They raised the fleet properly at dawn.

Not smoke now but shapes: three hulls grey against a pewter sea, running parallel to the coast with the easy confidence of ships that owned the water they floated on. The big one was a converted gunboat, black-funneled, her lines cut down and her deck cluttered with crane gear and compression chambers. The two smaller vessels were survey steamers, hydrographic launches slung at their davits, sounding wires paying out astern like fishing lines cast for continents.

Okafor came up from the dive locker when Brannigan shouted down to him, and stood at the rail with his arms folded, looking at the fleet the way a man looks at weather he has sailed through before and survived, barely.

"Magnus Line," he said. "The gunboat. That's *Iron Verity* — they renamed her after she changed hands. Compression chamber for sixty meters, mixed-gas rig, a diving bell if they've kept it maintained." His voice was level, professional, and underneath it Isla heard the other thing, the thing he never said aloud: the last disaster, the one that had scattered the crew, had happened in water not so different from what waited in the fjord. "They're equipped for deep salvage. Properly equipped. We are not."

"We're equipped for one dive," Isla said. "One dive is all we need."

"One dive is all we'll get," Okafor agreed, "if they don't see us first."

But the sea had already decided otherwise. As the light strengthened, the lead survey steamer altered course — a small thing, five degrees, ten — and began a long sweeping arc that would bring her within hailing distance of the *Vagrant Mercy* inside the hour. Routine. A merchantman sighted on a salvage ground got hailed. Papers, cargo, destination. Routine, unless you were carrying a forged charter, a stolen compass, and a crew of ghosts.

"Options," Sebastian said. He'd appeared at her shoulder without her hearing him, which was starting to feel less like clumsiness and more like habit. "We run. We run south and lose them in the coastal traffic and come back around through the outer shoals — costs us a tide, maybe two."

"We don't have two tides."

"Or we stand on, let them hail us, and lie."

"Lying to a survey steamer is easy. Lying to whoever's *aboard* her—" Isla stopped.

Through her glass, she watched a figure step to the survey steamer's wing bridge. Tall. Grey coat. Hands clasped behind her back in a stance Isla had seen ten thousand times across four years of shared decks — the stance of a woman watching the water and finding it wanting. Even at this distance, even as a smear of glass and grey wool, Isla knew her. You didn't spend four years diving on a person's voice and forget the shape of her silence.

Verity Sloane raised a signal flag. Not a challenge pennant. Not the Magnus Line house colors.

A single square of yellow cloth, hoisted alone, and Isla's stomach dropped through the hull and into the sea, because she knew that signal too. Everyone on this coast knew it. It was the old *Kestrel*'s private code, invented over cheap rum in a fo'c'sle bunk, used between two women who had once trusted each other with their lives at sixty meters:

*I see you. Come alongside. Alone.*

"She knows," Isla said. The glass trembled slightly in her hand and she steadied it against the rail. "It's not routine. She's been watching for us. Or watching for *me*."

Sebastian took the glass and studied the steamer, then the gunboat beyond, then the flag. "Can she force the issue? Board us?"

"Not legally. We're outside territorial water, our papers are — " Isla grimaced. "Our papers are Crane's problem, and Crane's paper has a way of being worth exactly what Crane wants it to be worth in the moment. No. She won't board. She doesn't need to. That flag isn't a threat, it's an invitation, and Verity Sloane doesn't send invitations twice."

"Will you go?"

The question hung there. Behind them, Tam had come up silently and Brannigan had stopped singing, and the whole crew was listening the way crews listen — pretending not to, missing nothing.

"If I go aboard her deck," Isla said slowly, "she'll know everything. What we're after, who hired us, how many tides we think we have. She'll strip it out of me in an afternoon, because she always could, because I taught her how I lie and she's had eight years to get better at it than me."

"And if you don't go?"

"Then she stops being curious and starts being thorough, and thorough looks like putting a launch on our stern and shadowing us into the fjord mouth, and we lose the race by a day we don't have." Isla lowered the glass. Her hands were steady again. She noticed, distantly, that they'd been steady since she'd said her mother's name in the dark hold two nights ago, and hadn't shaken since, and didn't know what to make of that. "There's a third option."

Sebastian waited.

"I go across. And I don't lie to her." She turned to face him. "I tell her one true thing. Just one. Enough to buy us the day."

"Which true thing?"

Isla looked at the compass on the binnacle, its needle faithful and unbothered, pointing at the ice and whatever slept beneath it.

"That my mother is down there," she said. "That's the one she'll believe. Because it's the one thing about me Verity Sloane never thought I was lying about."

Ahead, the yellow flag snapped in the morning wind, patient as a hook, and the *Vagrant Mercy* drove on toward the fleet at anchor, carrying Isla Reyes toward the one woman on this coast whose forgiveness she had never dared ask for — and whose judgment, she was beginning to understand, might be the cheapest price the sea was going to charge her.
