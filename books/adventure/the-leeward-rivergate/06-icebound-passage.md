## 6. Icebound Passage

The fjord announced itself before we saw it — a cold breath rolling off the water at dawn, the kind of chill that gets into your lungs and stays there like a tenant who pays no rent.

I stood at the bow of the *Vagrant Mercy* with the compass in my hand and watched the mountains come up out of the sea mist like the backs of sleeping animals. Grey stone. White ice. Water the color of a bruise. Somewhere ahead of us, past the pack ice that Tam had been cursing about since midnight, lay the Leeward Rivergate, sunk in forty fathoms of black water, holding a manifest that could hang a magistrate.

And somewhere behind us, gaining with every hour we lost, was Captain Verity Sloane's fleet.

"Sebastian." Isla came up beside me, hair tied back, hands wrapped around two mugs of Brannigan's coffee. She handed me one without asking. It tasted like tar and ambition. "Tam says the ice is moving."

"Pack ice always moves."

"Not like this. He says it's breathing."

I drank the coffee and looked back toward the stern. Tam Voss stood over his engine hatch like a man arguing with a sick animal, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, forearms black to the elbow with grease. He'd been down in that engine room for six hours straight. The *Vagrant Mercy* was his patient, his religion, and possibly his alibi for whatever he'd done before Isla found him again, and he did not appreciate any of us offering opinions on his work.

"He said that last night too," I said. "Breathing."

"And?"

"And I told him ice doesn't breathe."

Isla smiled, but it didn't reach her eyes. "It does here," she said. "The tide goes out, the pack opens like a lung filling. Tide comes in, it closes. You have maybe ninety minutes on each exhale to get through. After that the channel seals and you're either inside it or you're part of it."

Okafor appeared at the rail below us, coiling rope with the unhurried precision of a man who had decided long ago that panic was a luxury item he couldn't afford. He was the biggest person aboard and moved like the smallest, quiet as snowfall. He looked up at us once, said nothing, went back to his coils.

Brannigan leaned out of the galley doorway, dish towel over his shoulder. "If we die out here," he said, "I want it noted that my last meal was excellent."

"Noted," Isla said.

"I'm serious. There should be a plaque."

"Tide turns in forty minutes," Tam called from the stern, not looking up. "Either we commit now or we anchor outside and watch Sloane sail through our window while we eat Brannigan's plaque."

* * *

We committed.

The first hour was almost beautiful, which is how these things get you. The pack opened as the tide fell, exactly as Isla had promised, a winding lane of open black water threading between plates of ice the size of village squares. The *Vagrant Mercy* slipped into it under partial sail, engine idling low, and for a while it felt less like navigation and more like being swallowed slowly by something enormous and indifferent.

The ice talked the whole way. That was the thing nobody warns you about. Groans, cracks, long wet sighs, the occasional sharp report like a rifle going off somewhere behind the fog. Every sound meant something — pressure, movement, a plate shifting against its neighbor — and none of us knew the language except Okafor, who stood at the bow with a boathook and translated in a low murmur.

"That one's settling," he'd say. "Fine."

Crack.

"That one's new. Watch it."

Boom, distant.

"That's the glacier. Far off. Fine."

It should have been terrifying. Instead it was strangely calming, having one voice aboard that wasn't guessing. I found myself steering toward his murmurs the way you steer toward a lighthouse.

Isla worked the chart table in the cockpit, calling depths and bearings, and I noticed she'd stopped performing for me somewhere around the second day out. No more swagger, no more thief's grin deployed at strategic moments. Just work. She was good at it. Better than good. Her marks were clean, her arithmetic fast, and when she caught an error in her own plotting she said so out loud, flatly, without drama, and fixed it. My father used to say you learn more about a sailor watching them correct a mistake than watching them make ten perfect ones. By that measure Isla Reyes was the finest sailor I'd ever crewed with, and I hated how much that pleased me.

Then the engine died.

No warning sputter, no cough, no dramatic shudder. One moment the *Vagrant Mercy* was ghosting through the lane under sail and idle power; the next there was a metallic clank from below, deep and wrong, like a bone breaking inside a body, and the vibration through the deck simply stopped. The silence afterward was worse than any noise. On a boat, silence from the engine is a scream.

"Tam?" Isla called down the companionway.

A pause. Then, very calmly, in a voice that told me exactly how bad it was: "Don't come down here."

"What is it?"

"Don't—"

The rudder stopped answering the wheel.

I felt it before I understood it. The helm went light, then dead, the boat's response lagging, then gone entirely, and we were no longer sailing so much as drifting with intent, carried by the current between two ice plates that were, at that moment, beginning their slow inhale. The tide had turned. The lung was closing.

I tied off the wheel and went down the companionway three steps at a time.

The engine room was a coffin with gauges. Tam was on his knees in the bilge, arm buried to the shoulder in machinery, grease-slicked face turned up toward me. "Prop shaft's fouled," he said. "Something's wrapped around it — line, net, I don't know. And the rudder stock is jammed. Ice or debris, can't tell from in here. I can't fix what I can't see, and I can't see anything from inside this hole."

"How long to clear it?"

"If I could get at it? Twenty minutes. From in here? Never."

Above us, through the deck, I heard Isla's voice go tight: "The plates are closing. We have minutes, not hours."

I looked at Tam. Tam looked at me. Neither of us said the obvious thing, because saying it made it real: someone had to go into that water.

The water in the fjord was a few degrees above freezing. Men died in water like that in four minutes. Five if they were lucky. Six if they were Okafor.

Because Okafor was already coming down the ladder.

He didn't ask questions. He didn't volunteer dramatically or make speeches. He simply appeared at the bottom of the ladder, already pulling off his boots, and started stripping down to his wool base layer with the same calm he brought to coiling rope. Tam stared at him. I stared at him.

"The water will kill you," Tam said.

"Yes," Okafor agreed, folding his shirt.

"Four minutes. Maybe five."

"Yes."

"You've been in water like that before."

Okafor paused, one boot in his hand, and something moved behind his eyes — not fear, exactly. Memory. "Once," he said. "Off a trawler in the Snaefellsnes channel. Line fouled the screw, skipper froze, I didn't. I learned two things that day." He set the boot down. "One: the cold doesn't hurt as long as you keep moving. It only hurts when you stop. Two:" — he looked at me, and held the look — "the cold doesn't care why you're in it. Only whether you get out."

He took the dive knife from his belt, clamped it in his teeth, and tied a line around his waist. I took the other end and looped it twice around the engine mount and back around my forearm, because I wanted him to feel that someone was holding it, even if the knot would hold without me.

"One pass," Tam said. "Cut the prop clear, check the rudder stock, come up. Don't be a hero. Heroes float."

Okafor almost smiled around the knife. Then he climbed out through the lazarette hatch, onto the transom step, into the black water between the closing plates of ice.

* * *

I have been in bad places. I've crossed salt flats hot enough to blister paint. I've watched a man die of thirst with a full canteen he was saving for someone else. But nothing I had ever seen prepared me for watching Okafor go under.

The water took him without a ripple worth the name. The line paid out through my hands, slow and steady, and I counted. Everyone counts. I counted in seconds because minutes felt like an insult.

Ten. Fifteen. The line kept running out.

Twenty-five. Thirty. Isla crouched at the hatch above me, one hand on the line, feeling it the way you feel a pulse. Tam had his head out the transom, scanning the water.

Thirty-five. Forty.

"Pull him," Tam said.

"No," Isla said. "Wait."

"How do you know?"

"Because he told me once. In another life, on another boat, before everything went wrong between us. He said: don't pull me unless the line stops. If the line moves, I'm working. If it stops, I'm dead, and pulling won't change that." Her jaw was set hard enough to crack teeth. "The line is moving."

Forty-five. Fifty. My forearm burned where the line crossed it. The ice groaned overhead, a long descending note, and through the hull I could hear the plates grinding closer, the channel narrowing, the lung contracting. Spray drifted across the transom and froze on contact, building a thin white fur along the rails.

Fifty-five. Sixty.

The line jerked. Twice. Hard.

"That's him," Isla said. "Haul."

We hauled. All three of us, hand over hand, and Okafor came up out of the water like something surfacing from a deeper world — streaming, grey-lipped, eyes wide and shockingly calm. He got one arm over the transom and we dragged him the rest of the way, and the first thing he did, before we could wrap him in anything, was speak through chattering teeth:

"Prop is clear. Cut the line away. Rudder stock—" he sucked in a breath that sounded like tearing canvas, "—rudder stock has a bight of cable jammed in the gudgeon. Starboard side. Needs a bar, not a knife. I marked it. Scratch on the hull."

"Get him below," Tam snapped. "Now. Move."

Brannigan materialized out of nowhere with blankets and a thermos of something that smelled like it could strip varnish, and we poured Okafor into the galley bench and wrapped him until only his face showed. His lips were blue. His hands, when Brannigan took them to rub warmth into them, were steady. Steady hands, blue lips. I thought about that for a long time afterward.

"Second pass," Okafor said.

"No," said everyone at once, including himself, and then he closed his eyes and let the brandy do its work.

That left the cable in the gudgeon, and the channel closing, and nobody left willing to say the next obvious thing out loud. So I said it.

"I'll go."

Isla turned to look at me. Not surprised — that was the strange part. She just studied me for a second, the way she'd studied the tide charts, checking my math. "You've dived in freezing water?"

"No."

"In water at all?"

"Lakes. Warm ones."

She nodded slowly. "Okay." No argument, no lecture, no offer to go herself instead. Later I understood why: she'd seen me count the seconds on that line. She'd seen that I hadn't flinched, and she knew — the way sailors know things about each other without evidence — that I'd come back up. "Tam, give him the bar. Show him the mark. Sebastian — the cold doesn't hurt while you're moving. It only hurts when you stop. So don't stop."

I stripped down. I took the iron bar Tam pressed into my hands, still warm from the engine room, and I tied the line around my waist, and Isla tied it herself, double-checked the knot, and put my hand on it. "I'm holding the other end," she said. "Same rules. If the line stops, they pull. If it moves, you work."

I went out onto the transom step. The water was the color of slate and it looked exactly like what it was: a door that opened one way.

I went in.

The cold was not pain. That was the first miracle. It was absence — a vast, total subtraction of everything, heat and feeling and the memory of my own body, replaced by a single bright instruction: *move*. I pulled myself down the transom, hand over hand along the hull, following Okafor's scratch mark, a short white gouge in the weed-dark paint. Found the gudgeon. Found the cable — old mooring wire, rusted, wedged into the pintle like a splinter in flesh. I jammed the bar in, braced my boots against the hull, and levered.

Nothing.

Levered again, harder, and the cold began to whisper to me, softly, reasonably, that stopping for just a moment would feel wonderful. I heard Okafor's voice in my head: *it only hurts when you stop.* I didn't stop. I screamed into the water instead, bubbles streaming past my face, and put everything I had into the bar, and the cable gave with a jolt that nearly tore the bar from my frozen hands, and the rudder stock swung free.

The line tugged — Isla, feeling me move, feeling me alive. I clawed my way up it.

They hauled me over the transom and the air hit me like a blessing and a beating all at once, and I lay on the deck shaking so hard my teeth rattled loose words I didn't mean to say, and Brannigan wrapped me in blankets and poured scalding tea down my throat, and Tam was already below, slamming the engine back to life.

The starter caught on the third try. The prop bit water. The wheel answered my hand like a living thing.

"Channel's closing," Isla called from the cockpit, reading the gap between the ice plates the way other people read weather. "Full ahead. Now, now, now."

We went through with thirty yards to spare. I know because I watched it from the helm, wrapped in three blankets, steering the *Vagrant Mercy* through a gap that sealed shut behind our wake like a wound closing. The plates met with a boom I felt through the soles of my feet, and then we were through, in the wide dark water beyond, and the fjord walls rose on either side of us a thousand feet high, streaked with glacier ice, silent as a cathedral.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The engine hummed. The sails filled. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, ticking softly against the deck.

Then Brannigan, from the galley doorway, in a voice thick with emotion he'd deny to his grave: "Dinner's at seven. Both of you are eating double. Don't argue with the cook."

Okafor, thawed and wrapped beside me in the cockpit, extended one enormous steady hand. I took it. His grip was careful, the grip of a man who knows exactly how strong he is.

"You counted," he said.

"Everyone counts."

"You counted right." He squeezed once, gently, and let go. "That's the whole job, ranger. Counting right, and not letting go of the line."

Isla stood at the wheel beside me, snow gathering in her hair, and she didn't say anything at all. She didn't need to. Something had shifted on this boat in the last hour, some load-bearing wall moved into place, and we all felt it settle. Yesterday these people were a thief's collection of damaged strangers I'd agreed to tolerate. Tonight they were the reason I was alive, and I was theirs, and the knowing of it sat in my chest like a coal.

I was still warm from it when I went below to stow the bar, and found Isla's chart bag open on the table, and saw the corner of a folded paper sticking out of it that hadn't been there before.

A letter. Old paper, water-stained, addressed in a woman's hand.

Addressed to Isla. Postmarked the year the Leeward Rivergate went down.

She came down the companionway behind me and stopped when she saw what I was looking at, and the look on her face told me the ice outside wasn't the coldest thing aboard the *Vagrant Mercy* anymore.
