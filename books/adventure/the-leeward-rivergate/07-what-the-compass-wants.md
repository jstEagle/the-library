## 7. What the Compass Wants

The Vagrant Mercy rode at anchor in a cove the charts called Hollowbone, a notch of black water between two shoulders of granite where the wind couldn't reach them. Tam had dropped both anchors and then, because he was Tam, checked them twice. Okafor was below with his hands wrapped around a mug of Brannigan's pepper tea, trying to coax feeling back into fingers that had spent an hour in the fjord's edge-water. The rudder still bore the scratches from the ice. Everyone had earned sleep.

Isla couldn't find hers.

She sat in the hold instead, on a crate of salt pork, with the compass open in her lap. The lantern was turned down to a coal. Around her the ship creaked its slow arithmetic — hull against water, water against stone — and above her feet she could hear Sebastian moving through the deck watch, his boots deliberate, a man who walked like he was still tracking something even when there was nothing left to track.

The needle swung. It didn't point north. It hadn't pointed north since she'd lifted it off his table in that rented room in Saltmere, three weeks and one lifetime ago. It pointed east-northeast, out past the cove mouth, past the glacier's white shoulder, toward a stretch of water marked on no chart she'd ever bought: *Leeward Deep*. Where the Rivergate lay.

She'd told herself it was coincidence for exactly as long as she could stand to.

Boots on the companionway ladder. She knew the weight of them now. Sebastian came down into the hold with the lantern from the galley and stopped when he saw her sitting in the dark like a woman keeping vigil, which was, she supposed, exactly what she was.

"You're not sleeping," he said.

"Neither are you."

"I'm on watch." He set his lantern on a barrel and lowered himself onto the crate across from her, knees up, forearms draped over them. He did this thing where he looked at her sideways first, deciding whether to look at her straight. "Okafor's going to live. Tam says the engine will run another forty hours if we don't push it. Brannigan made too much stew again."

"That's not why you're down here."

"No," he agreed. "It's not."

The silence between them had changed since the ice passage. It used to be a wall. Now it was more like a room they both happened to be standing in. He waited. He was good at waiting — a desert ranger's whole trade was patience sharpened into a weapon — but she could feel him choosing not to ask, and the effort of it filled the hold like smoke.

She turned the compass so he could see the needle.

He looked at it the way he'd looked at it every night since Crane's parlor, and every night before that, back when it sat on his father's shelf in a house full of dust and grief. He knew what it did. What he didn't know — what he'd never asked, because asking would have meant admitting he cared about the answer — was *why*.

"Why does it do that?" he said finally. Quietly. Like he was afraid of waking something.

"It doesn't do anything," Isla said. "It's just a compass. Brass case, agate bearing, card balanced for southern latitudes. My mother's name is engraved under the bezel." She paused. "Not yours."

His jaw tightened. "Montrose is engraved under mine."

"I know. I read it. By lamplight, in your room, while you were downstairs arguing with the innkeeper about hay prices." She turned the compass over in her hands. "That's how I knew whose it was. That's how I knew it wasn't mine. And I took it anyway."

* * *

Sebastian didn't move. That was the thing about him — she'd learned it on the flats, chasing him across three days of salt and mirage — he went very still when something mattered. Stillness as attention. Most men got loud. Sebastian Montrose got quiet, and the quiet had edges.

"Tell me," he said.

So she did.

"My mother's name was Maren Reyes. She was a clerk. Not a glamorous one — customs manifests, cargo ledgers, the paperwork nobody reads until somebody dies. We lived in Corvane Harbor, in a room above a chandler's shop that smelled forever of tar and hemp. She worked for the port authority. She was good at it. She could spot a falsified tonnage figure across a crowded office." Isla smiled, and it hurt. "She taught me figures before she taught me letters. Said numbers don't lie, but men who write them down do."

"She sounds like someone I'd have liked."

"You'd have liked her. Everyone liked her. That was the problem." Isla looked at the needle. "Because the magistrate liked her too."

"Halden Voss."

"You know the name."

"Everyone on this coast knows the name. He signs the salvage charters. He owns half the fleet Sloane is sailing with right now."

"He owned my mother's office. Her wages. Her future. And in the year I turned nine, he started owning her evenings." Isla kept her voice level. It was an old discipline, learned young: if you tell it flat, it can't cut you while you're saying it. "There were documents that crossed her desk. Prisoner transports. Supply requisitions for a vessel most people thought was a rumor — a prison hulk moored somewhere north, beyond inspection range. The Leeward Rivergate. Officially decommissioned. Officially a ghost."

"But it wasn't."

"It was a ledger with a hull around it. People went in. Nothing came out. No release papers, no death certificates, no bodies. Just requisitions — flour, salt meat, chain, lamp oil — year after year, signed by a warden who officially didn't exist. My mother noticed that the flour orders fed four hundred men, and the burial barge permits covered none of them." She swallowed. "She started copying things. Manifests. Transfer rolls. Names."

The lantern flame leaned in some draft only it could feel. In the dark of the hold, with the hull ticking around them, it felt less like a ship and more like a throat.

"One night she didn't come home. I waited up. I was eleven. I waited up a lot of nights after that, but that was the first." Isla's hands were steady on the compass; she made them be. "They said she'd been arrested for embezzlement. There were papers. Very official papers, with seals, saying Maren Reyes had stolen from the port authority and been sentenced to transport. To the Rivergate. They let me visit once, through a grate, before the ship sailed. She told me she loved me. She told me to count everything twice. She did not tell me she was innocent, because she thought — she still believed, right up to that grate — that the truth would come out on its own. That systems like hers corrected themselves. That's what she'd spent her life believing."

"And they were wrong."

"They weren't wrong. They were *bought*." The word cracked despite her. "The Rivergate sailed north with three hundred and eighty-one prisoners aboard, and my mother was number two hundred and six on the transfer roll, and it never came back. It went into Leeward Deep and stayed there. And every record of everyone aboard was sealed by order of Magistrate Halden Voss, in perpetuity, for reasons of state."

Sebastian's breath went out slowly through his nose. "How do you know all this? If the records are sealed."

"Because my mother taught me to count everything twice." She met his eyes. "And because I've spent nineteen years doing nothing else."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long moment. Above them, his boots' echo had gone; he must have handed the watch to Tam without either of them noticing. The ship felt emptied out, just the two of them and the dead.

"The debt," he said at last. "Crane's offer. You vouched for the route. You pushed us onto this job."

"Yes."

"You didn't do it for salvage rights."

"Salvage rights are paper. Paper burns." She closed her hand over the compass. "I did it because your father's compass points at my mother's grave, Sebastian. Do you understand that? Some instrument-maker put a lodestone in this case that swings toward iron, and there is enough iron on the bottom of Leeward Deep — chains, guns, a hull three hundred feet long — to pull every needle on this coast. Your father carried a compass that couldn't find north because it was always finding *her*. Finding them. All of them. And he never knew why."

Sebastian stared at the needle. She watched him do the arithmetic — she could see it moving behind his face, the same way it moved behind hers, figures refusing to lie.

"How did my father get it?" he asked. "Her compass."

"I don't know." It was the honest answer and it cost her. "That's part of what I came to find. Here's what I have: nineteen years ago, a man named Elias Montrose filed a petition with the coastal tribunal requesting the unsealing of the Rivergate's prisoner manifest. It was denied. He filed again. Denied. A third time, and after that third denial he stopped filing petitions and started chartering boats." Her voice dropped. "He made four runs toward Leeward Deep. The first three turned back — weather, ice, a patrol cutter that suddenly appeared where no cutter had any business being. On the fourth run, his boat came back and he didn't. Drowned, they said. Lost overboard in fair weather, they said. His body was never recovered, they said."

"My mother told me he died in a storm."

"Your mother was told that by the same people who told the world my mother was a thief." Isla gentled it as much as she could, which wasn't much. "I'm sorry. I've had years to stop believing the comfortable version. You've had weeks."

He absorbed it the way he absorbed everything — silently, completely, the blow taken standing. Then: "The compass. How did it get from her to him?"

"A week before he died, he sent a letter to a woman in Corvane Harbor. It arrived two months later, forwarded twice, and it reached the chandler's shop where I was working rigging jobs by then. Nineteen years old, orphaned twice over on paper and once over in fact." She heard her own voice go thin and hated it. "The letter said: *If anything happens to me, the enclosed belongs to Maren Reyes's daughter. Tell her the manifest exists. Tell her it's sealed in the strongroom, port side, below the waterline. Tell her I'm sorry I was slow.*"

"The compass was enclosed."

"The compass was enclosed. With no note about the lodestone, no explanation of how he came to have my mother's compass in the first place. Maybe he bought it from the confiscation auctions — Voss sold off prisoners' effects like scrap. Maybe someone gave it to him. Maybe—" She stopped. Breathed. "Maybe my mother sent it to him herself, as proof of who she was, so he'd trust what she'd found. I've turned it over ten thousand ways and I don't know. But I know what the letter said, word for word, because I've had it memorized since the day it nearly broke me. *The manifest exists.*"

"Port side. Below the waterline."

"Below the waterline." She nodded. "Which is where Okafor's survey puts the strongroom. Which is why I stood in Aldous Crane's parlor and swore an impossible route existed through the ice. Which is why I'm here."

* * *

Sebastian rose. Not to leave — she saw that in the set of his shoulders — but because staying still had become impossible for him, the way it always did when the ground shifted under a belief. He paced the length of the hold, three steps, turn, three steps back, the lantern throwing his shadow long against the salt pork crates.

"My father," he said, "spent the last year of his life obsessed. I thought it was grief. He'd lost his brother, his partner, his reputation — Crane saw to that last one. He sat up nights with charts and tide tables and that compass, and I was sixteen and furious at him for it, because he was there but he wasn't *there*, and then he was gone entirely, and I decided the obsession had killed him. I decided the sea took him because he chased something that wasn't real." He stopped pacing. "And now you're telling me it was real. The manifest. The sealed names. Three hundred and eighty-one people erased by a signature."

"Two hundred and six," Isla said. "My mother was two hundred and six."

"And your father—" Sebastian caught himself. "Your mother. And mine spent his last year trying to dig them out, and I inherited his debts and his shame and I never once thought to ask what he was actually digging *for*." He laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it. "Crane told me my father refused this job. Refused to salvage the Rivergate, back when they were partners. I've hated my father a little bit for twenty years for dying, and hated Crane for surviving, and it turns out my father didn't refuse the job. He refused *Crane's version* of it. Because Crane wanted leverage and my father wanted names."

"You don't know that."

"I know my father's handwriting. I found his copy of the tribunal denials in his effects after he died — I thought they were just papers, I was a kid, I burned half of them in the stove to heat the house." His voice roughened. "I may have burned evidence of a massacre to boil water."

"You were sixteen."

"I was his son, and I didn't ask." He sat back down across from her, heavily, like a man setting down cargo. "You stole from me, Isla. I tracked you across the flats to take it back and maybe break your fingers. And the whole time, the thing you stole was pointing at the reason my father died."

Their eyes held in the low light. Something passed between them that wasn't forgiveness yet — forgiveness needed more distance than three weeks allowed — but was adjacent to it. Recognition, maybe. Two people discovering their wounds were cut by the same blade.

"Your debt," she said carefully. "To Crane. What is it, actually?"

"Fourteen hundred crowns. Contracted against my ranger's commission after his lawsuit gutted my father's estate. I've paid it down for twenty years. It's the shape of my whole life." He rubbed his face. "Why?"

"Because Crane is too precise." She stood, restless now herself, the confession having loosened something that wouldn't settle. "Six tides. Exactly six. A route through the pack ice he knows to the hour. A salvage fleet that hasn't sailed yet but will arrive precisely when he needs it to. Men like that don't leave things to weather, Sebastian. They build the weather." She looked at the compass in her hand, then at him. "My mother counted everything twice. I'm asking you to help me count him."

Above them, faint through the deck planks, Tam's voice called the hour, and beneath it, far off toward the cove mouth, came a sound neither of them could name at first — a deep, grinding exhalation, like the sea clearing its throat.

Ice, shifting. Or a ship's engine, a long way off in the dark.

They looked at each other.

"Counting starts now," Isla said.
