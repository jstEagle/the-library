## 8. Sloane's Parley

The white flag went up an hour after dawn, and it went up on a launch that moved like it had been built by people who had never once been in a hurry.

Sebastian watched it come across the grey water from the deck of the *Vagrant Mercy*, arms crossed, breath fogging. The launch was painted the same slate-grey as the three ships anchored at the fjord's mouth — three ships where yesterday there had been two. They'd come in overnight, silent as weather, and now they sat in a loose crescent across the only exit, masts bare, steam curling lazy from their stacks, the whole formation radiating the patience of something that had already won.

"That's not a warship," Tam said at his shoulder. The engineer had a wrench tucked in her belt out of habit, and her knuckles were white around the rail. "Warships don't send boats out at first light. That's a negotiation."

"Negotiations are just wars with better manners," Okafor rumbled from the stern. He was coiling line with slow, deliberate care, the way he did when his hands needed something honest to do.

The launch carried six people. Four were rowers, broad-backed, in matching oilskins. The fifth sat at the tiller and did nothing at all. And the sixth stood at the bow with one hand resting on the gunwale like the boat belonged to her, a tall woman in a dark coat with a collar turned up against the spray, hair cropped short and going silver at the temples.

Sebastian had never seen Captain Verity Sloane before. He knew her anyway. Isla had said her name exactly twice in the whole voyage, both times the way you say the name of a fire you once walked through.

"I should be the one to meet her," Isla said, coming up from below with her coat half-buttoned. Her face was pale. Not afraid, exactly. Braced.

"No," Sebastian said.

"It's my history—"

"Which is exactly why she wants you on that deck." He kept his eyes on the launch. "She's not coming to talk to us. She's coming to talk to *you*, in front of us, and every word will be a blade with your name on it. You stay below. Tam, you're with me. Okafor, Brannigan — on deck, armed, visible. We look like a crew that can't be bought and can't be scared."

"And if we can be both?" Brannigan asked mildly, from the galley door, a cleaver in one hand and a heel of bread in the other.

"Then we lie well," Sebastian said.

The launch bumped against the hull. A boarding ladder came down. And Captain Verity Sloane climbed aboard the *Vagrant Mercy* like she was stepping into her own parlor.

* * *

She was smaller up close than the water made her, and older, and her eyes did the thing that Sebastian would remember for years afterward: they went around the deck once, unhurried, cataloguing everything — the patched planking, the jury-rigged engine housing, the depth of the freeboard, the number of crew — and then came to rest on him with an expression of mild, professional disappointment.

"Sebastian Montrose." She said it the way a doctor reads a chart. "Your father had better lines. This thing handles like a drunk barge."

"She handles fine," Tam said.

Sloane smiled without warmth. "And you'd be the engineer who walked out of the *Cormorant Queen* with a toolbox and a grudge. I've heard of you." Her gaze slid past them all, down the companionway, deliberate as a hand. "I'd hoped to see Isla Reyes. I had so looked forward to it."

"She's not available," Sebastian said.

"Pity." She turned back to him, and the smile dropped away like a mask on a string. "Let's not perform for the crew, Mr. Montrose. You're a desert ranger. You've spent your life chasing thieves across sand, and now you're crewing a salvage job in a glacier's throat for a man who ruined your father. You're smarter than this arrangement. So let me make you an offer that respects your intelligence."

She reached into her coat and produced a folded paper, and did not hand it over — just held it, letting them all look at the wax seal on it. The magistrate's seal. Sebastian's stomach went cold before his mind caught up.

"Full payment for the Rivergate job," Sloane said. "Double what Crane is offering, and I'll be honest with you — Crane isn't offering you money. He's offering you a debt cancelled, and debts that can be cancelled can be reissued. I'm offering coin. Real coin, in a real account, under names I'll help you bury so deep no creditor will ever dig them up. You walk away today. The fleet takes the wreck. Nobody drowns, nobody starves, nobody freezes on an ice shelf because their employer decided they'd become inconvenient."

"You know a lot about how Crane operates," Sebastian said carefully.

"I know everything about how Crane operates." Sloane folded the paper away again. "That's rather the point of this conversation."

"Then say the rest of it."

She studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because she sighed — a small, genuine sound, almost tired — and glanced at the fjord walls rising around them, blue ice streaked with grey moraine, the glacier's snout a white wall at the far end of the water.

"Your route," she said. "Through the Serrated Reach, along the meltwater tongue, into the fjord on the second tide of the cycle. The ice leads are open for six windows. Crane gave us all of it. Charts, timings, the condition of your hull, the names of your crew." She let that sit. "He sold you to us before you ever cleared the harbor, Mr. Montrose. You're not his salvage crew. You're his insurance policy. If we take the wreck, he's been paid either way. If you somehow beat us, he's been paid *more*. The only person on this ship with nothing to gain is you."

The wind came down the fjord and moved the water in long silver seams. Nobody spoke. Sebastian was aware of Tam beside him, very still; of Okafor's hands stopping on the coil of line; of Brannigan setting down the bread.

"That's a lie," Sebastian said.

"Of course you think so."

"Crane is a snake, but he *needs* us. The fleet can't dive that wreck — the hull's sealed, the holds are flooded to the bulkheads, and your divers would take a month your timetable doesn't have. He needs a crew crazy enough to go through the prisoner's door. He wouldn't torch his own advantage."

"Wouldn't he?" Sloane's voice was almost gentle, and that was worse than cruelty. "Mr. Montrose, I have been in this trade for thirty years. I have watched men sell their own brothers for less than what Crane is selling you for. He doesn't need your crew. He needs the *manifest*, and he needs it deniable. A crew that dies in the ice is the most deniable instrument ever invented." She drew her coat tighter. "The offer stands until the fourth tide. After that, we go in over you, and I will not enjoy it, but I will do it. Talk to your people. All of them. Including the one below decks pretending she isn't listening at the hatch."

Sebastian didn't turn around. He heard the hatch ease shut anyway, soft as a breath.

"Tell her," Sloane said, quieter now, and for one moment something moved behind her eyes that wasn't strategy — something old, and salted, and hurt. "Tell her Verity Sloane keeps her accounts current. Tell her I remember the *Kestrel*. Tell her I remember what was left in the water."

She went down the ladder without waiting for an answer. The launch pulled away, oars dipping in rhythm, and the white flag stayed up, and the three grey ships sat in their crescent across the sea like a verdict not yet read aloud.

* * *

The argument started before Sloane's launch was even a hundred yards out, and it did not stop for two hours.

"She's lying." Sebastian had the wheel and gripped it like it might bolt. "It's what she does. She poisons the well and then sells you the antidote."

"She knew the route," Tam said. She'd spread the charts on the chart table and was staring at them like they might confess. "The Serrated Reach. The six tide windows. That's not guesswork, Sebastian. That's *our* route. Down to the day."

"Crane wrote the salvage contract. Of course the fleet has the route — he filed it with the harbor office like any salvage claim. That's public record."

"Is it?" Isla's voice came from the companionway. She came up slowly, and her face had a stillness on it that Sebastian didn't like at all. "Because I checked the harbor office in Rivergate before we signed. Crane never filed. He said filing would tip the magistrate. He said secrecy was the whole job." She crossed to the table and put one finger on the chart, on the narrow blue thread of the Serrated Reach. "Nobody has this route. I drew half of it myself from my mother's notes. If Sloane has it, she got it from one person."

"From you," Sebastian said. "You sailed with Sloane. You know her methods. Maybe this is the long game — split us, buy us cheap, take the wreck without a shot fired. You told us what she is."

"I told you what she is," Isla said, and her voice cracked down the middle, and she steadied it. "I told you because I know exactly how good she is at this. And this—" she tapped the chart, "—is how good she is at this."

Okafor spoke from the stern, quiet, which was how you knew he meant it. "On the *Cormorant Queen*, my last captain sold our route to pirates for a percentage of what they took from us. I survived it because I got in the water fast. I have been on the ship that gets sold, Montrose. I know the smell." He looked at Sebastian with those deep, unhurried eyes. "It smells like this."

"Then what do you want?" Sebastian's voice came out harder than he intended. "You want to take Sloane's coin? Fine. Vote. Right now. I'll row you to her myself."

"Nobody said that," Brannigan said.

"Nobody had to. It's in the room."

"It's in *you*," Isla said. "You're the one who can't stand it. We're all just standing here."

That landed, because it was true, and Sebastian hated that it was true. He let go of the wheel and looked at his hands. Desert ranger's hands, rope-scarred, a compass callus on the thumb from years of carrying his father's instrument everywhere — the compass that was, at this moment, in Isla's coat pocket, because she carried it now and he had stopped asking why.

"Crane cleared my father's debt," he said. "That's the whole reason I'm on this boat. If that debt was a lie—"

"Then you're a free man," Tam said, "and you can walk away."

"And if it's true," Sebastian said, "then Sloane's whole speech was theater, and the moment we believe her, we're done."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the hull ticked against a drifting floe, small and patient, like something knocking.

"We need to know," Okafor said finally. "Not believe. *Know*. Is there anything aboard this ship that would tell us what Crane actually intends? Paper. Letters. Anything."

Sebastian thought about the cabin Crane had used for two days at the start of the voyage, before he'd transferred to his own tender and begun running ahead of them, meeting them at each anchorage like a man checking on an investment. Crane traveled light. Crane traveled *careful*. But he was also vain, and vain men kept records.

"He has a strongbox," Sebastian said slowly. "He kept it under his bunk. He moved it to the tender when he transferred." He frowned. "But he left a sea chest. Locked. He told us it was charts and said anyone who touched it would swim home."

Tam's grin had no joy in it. "I've opened a lot of things people told me not to open."

* * *

Crane's sea chest yielded in forty minutes, because Tam was Tam, and because vanity had made the lock ornamental — a merchant's lock, showy, built to impress rather than resist. Inside: charts, as promised. And beneath the charts, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger bound in green leather, and a packet of letters sealed with the magistrate's crest.

Sebastian read the letters first, because they were short.

They were invoices.

Not for salvage. Not for charts or permits or harbor fees. Invoices from Aldous Crane to the Office of the Magistrate, itemized with a bookkeeper's dry precision: *Route survey, fjord approach — 400. Crew engagement, Montrose — 250. Insurance, contingent — 600.* Dated three weeks before Crane had ever appeared at the Rivergate Inn with his offer of a debt cancelled.

The last line of the last invoice was dated nine days ago.

*Balance due on delivery of manifest — 2,000.*

"He sold us," Tam said flatly. "Before we even signed. Before the inn. Before any of it." She looked up at Sebastian. "The debt. Your father's debt. He wrote it into the contract as a line item. *Crew engagement, Montrose, 250.* He paid himself to hire you with a debt he—"

Sebastian wasn't listening. He had the ledger open, and his hands were shaking, and the shaking was not fear.

The ledger was coded — a simple substitution, the kind Crane would think clever, the kind Tam cracked over tea — but the entries didn't need cracking. The columns of figures ran down the page in Crane's narrow, upright hand, and beside each figure was a date, and beside each date was a name, and Sebastian's father's name was there, dated eleven years back, with a notation in the margin that Sebastian read four times before the words would hold still:

*M. Montrose — debt instrument created — 1,200. Note: the son will carry it. They always carry the father's debts. It makes them docile.*

Docile. The word sat on the page like a slug.

Eleven years. Eleven years he had carried it. Eleven years of hard seasons and harder choices, of turning down charters that paid because the debt came first, of watching his father's name sit on a creditor's roll like a stain he was born to scrub and never scrub clean. His father had died with that debt outstanding. Sebastian had built his whole life in its shadow, and the shadow was a *line item*. A margin note. A trick an old man had played on a grieving boy because grieving boys were docile.

The compass. Even the compass — Crane had known his father carried it, had known the son would want it back, had let the theft happen, had let Isla take it, because a crew with a wound was a crew that could be steered by pressing on the wound.

He didn't remember standing up. He was at the companionway with the ledger in his fist and the fjord wind hitting his face, and the *Vagrant Mercy* rocked under him, and out at the mouth of the fjord the three grey ships waited in their patient crescent, and somewhere between them and the glacier's white wall, Aldous Crane's tender was anchored in the lee of an ice shelf, warm lamplight in its cabin windows.

"Sebastian." Isla's voice. Her hand on his arm, light, careful, the way you touch a man standing on ice that might go. "Sebastian, look at me. What are you going to do?"

He looked at her. Her face was the one steady thing in a world that had come loose from its moorings, and that was its own kind of revelation — that the thief was the anchor, that the woman who'd stolen his father's compass was the only person on this water who hadn't sold him.

"He's going to get us all killed for a payday," Sebastian said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Level and cold, like deep water. "He sold the route. He sold *me*. The moment the manifest is in his hands, we're witnesses, and witnesses on Crane's books have a column too." He looked past her to the crew gathered at the hatch — Tam, Okafor, Brannigan, all of them watching, all of them waiting on him, which was a thing he hadn't earned and couldn't escape. "Fourth tide, Sloane said. That's when her offer expires. That's two days."

"And?" Isla said.

"And Crane's tender is sitting alone in the ice with a strongbox full of every lie he ever told." Sebastian rolled the ledger closed and felt the green leather warm in his grip like something alive. "I think it's time we had a conversation with our employer."

Isla held his eyes, and something passed between them that had no words in it — an agreement, or the beginning of one, or maybe just two people who had both been sold deciding, together, to stop being merchandise.

The glacier boomed at the far end of the fjord, long and low, ice shifting in its sleep. Two days. One tide less than they'd thought they had, and a traitor in the lee of the ice, and a fleet across the water with a captain who kept her accounts current.

Sebastian went to get the boat hook.
