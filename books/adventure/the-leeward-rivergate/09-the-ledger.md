## 9. The Ledger

Isla found the ledger on the second night after Sloane's ships appeared on the horizon, and she found it by accident, which was the only way anything true ever got found aboard the *Vagrant Mercy*.

Crane kept his cabin at the stern, a cramped space he'd surrendered half of to the chart table and filled the rest of with a traveling strongbox, a folding cot, and a smell of camphor and old ink. He slept like a man who had trained himself to sleep in short, profitable intervals — two hours, then awake, then two again. Isla had watched the rhythm for three days from the companionway, telling herself she was only learning the ship's habits, learning everyone's habits, because that was what a captain did. She was lying, and she knew it. She'd been lying since the moment Sloane's lead ship had raised its colors and Crane had gone very still at the rail, stiller than a man should be at the sight of a rival fleet, and then said, "Well. They're ahead of schedule," in a voice that held no surprise at all.

Ahead of schedule. Not *they found us*. Not *they followed the trade lanes*. *Ahead of schedule* — as if there was a schedule, as if Crane knew it, as if the fleet's arrival were a line item in someone's accounts.

So on the second night, when the wind backed around and Tam called all hands to shorten sail, Isla made sure she was the last one down the companionway and the first one into Crane's cabin. The strongbox was iron, old, locked with a key she didn't have. But Crane was a man of ledgers before he was a man of anything else — she'd learned that much about him in the inn at Rivergate, watching his fingers drum the account-book spine while he talked about debts — and men of ledgers carried their current book on their person or close to it. She found it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his spare sea chest, beneath a folded suit of shore clothes that smelled of a better life than this one.

It was a plain book, board covers, cloth spine, the kind sold in any port for a few coppers. The pages were dense with figures. Columns of sums, dates, initials. And threaded through the arithmetic, in a cramped cipher of Crane's own devising, a second text — letters standing in for other letters, numbers standing in for words, the whole thing looking, to a casual eye, like nothing more than a merchant's obsession with precision.

Isla had cracked ciphers before. Not many, and not well, and never alone. She wrapped the book back in its oilcloth, slid it inside her coat against her ribs, and went up on deck to help Tam fight the sail, her heart going like a drum in a parade.

* * *

"You want me to what."

Tam didn't look up from the engine. He rarely did. He was a small, square man with forearms like dock rope and a permanent smudge of grease along one jaw, and he had spent the last four days in the engine room the way other men spent them in church. "Because if you want me to steal it, I'll remind you we already have one thief aboard and she's standing in front of me asking me to be a second one, and I don't like the arithmetic of that."

"I'm not asking you to steal it. I already stole it." She set the ledger on his workbench, next to a jar of bolts and a half-disassembled fuel pump. "I'm asking you to read it. Or rather, to read what isn't in it."

Tam wiped his hands on a rag with the slow deliberation of a man buying time. Around them the engine ticked as it cooled, and the hull worked against the swells, that endless groan and settle that Isla had fallen asleep to for ten years of her life and was only now falling asleep to again. "Crane's book."

"Crane's book."

"If he finds it gone—"

"He'll find it gone whether we read it or not. He'll notice eventually. The question is whether we know what's in it before he notices, or after." She pulled the oilcloth free and opened the cover, turning pages until she found the cipher columns, and watched Tam's eyes change — the merchant's boredom draining out of them, the engineer's hunger coming in. Tam could not leave a mechanism alone. A locked mechanism least of all.

"That's a substitution cipher," he said. "With numeric substitution for the high-frequency words. Look — that figure repeats every time he writes about money changing hands, and that one every time he writes about a person." He pulled the ledger toward him and was already reaching for his pencils. "It's not a good cipher. It's a *busy* cipher. He made it complicated because he uses it every day, and nobody makes an everyday cipher simple, but nobody makes an everyday cipher *hard*, either, because then he couldn't read it himself at two in the morning by lamplight." He glanced up. "Give me until tomorrow night."

"Tam."

"I know." He was already writing, transcribing columns onto scrap paper, his lips moving. "I know what you think is in it. I've been thinking it too. Since the fleet showed up exactly where we were going to be, exactly when we were going to be there." His pencil paused. "I left this crew once because a captain sold our route out from under us. Four of us went into the water. I said I'd never sail with anyone who'd do that again." He looked at her, and his face was very still. "So let's find out if I've made a mistake."

* * *

He didn't need until tomorrow night. He needed until two in the morning, and he came up to the aft deck where Isla sat a false watch she'd assigned herself so she'd have an excuse to be awake, and he set the ledger on the deck between them and sat down across from it like a man sitting down across from a body.

"It's not one cipher," he said. "It's two. The columns are the real book — grain futures, harbor fees, a warehouse in Corvane he's renting under a false name, boring, forget it. The cipher is the other book. And the other book—" He stopped. Started again. "Isla, the other book is about you."

The wind came off the glacier fields and it was cold enough that she could see her breath, and she saw it hang there and dissolve and thought, absurdly, *that's what the last four days have been.*

"Start," she said.

"Start where?"

"Start with the fleet. Start with why Sloane found us."

Tam opened the ledger to a marked page and turned it to face her. In the lamplight from the companionway she could see his transcription notes in the margin, his neat engineer's hand annotating Crane's cipher the way he'd annotate a schematic. "Entry dated nine days before we left Rivergate. I'll read it as he wrote it. 'V.S. confirmed. Route and timetable delivered per agreement. V.S. to hold off direct approach until fourth window, allowing C.M. to commit fully. Payment on delivery of manifest.'" Tam tapped the page. "V.S. is Verity Sloane. C.M. is Crane himself. He sold her our route before we ever signed on. The whole fleet at anchor out there — he invited them. He wanted them behind us, pressing us, so we'd have no time to think and no way to turn back. We're not racing the fleet, Isla. The fleet is his whip."

Isla found that her hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind. She thought of Sloane's signal across the water, the deliberate, personal flash of the lamp. *This will be personal.* Sloane had known. Sloane had known everything, from the first day, and had been waiting, patient as ice, for Crane's whip to drive them into her reach.

"What else," Isla said. Her voice came out flat and hard, and she was glad of it, because underneath the flatness there was something moving that she did not want to look at yet.

Tam turned pages. "The warehouse in Corvane. It's not a warehouse. It's where he's been keeping papers. There's a list — I think it's a list of payments, or bribes, or — Isla, there are entries going back nineteen years. Names. Dates. Sums. And every few pages, the same initials recurring. M.H."

"Magistrate Harrow," Isla said. The name tasted like copper. Everyone on this coast knew Harrow; he'd built his career on the prison ships, on the efficiency of the Leeward routes, on a reputation for order that he'd polished for two decades.

"Magistrate Harrow," Tam agreed. "Crane's been collecting against him for nineteen years. Every entry is a debt Crane thinks Harrow owes him. And then—" Tam's pencil stopped, and Isla saw his hand was not quite steady, and she braced herself the way you brace before a wave breaks over the bow. "Then there's this."

He turned the ledger around and put his finger on a page near the middle. The cipher here was looser, hurried, and Tam's transcription ran down the margin in his neat hand, and Isla read it twice because the first time the words refused to mean anything.

*'S. Montrose. Debt of 4,200 crowns, secured against the Vagrant Mercy and the son's freedom of movement. Originated: fabricated. No instrument exists. Purpose: leverage. The son will not take the job for wages or for the manifest. He will take it for the debt. The father's name is the key that turns him. Note: the debt must never be papered. An unwritten debt cannot be disproven.'*

The wind moved. The rigging sang its one long note. Isla sat with the ledger open on her knees and felt the cold come up through the deck planks and into her bones, and what moved underneath her flatness finally had a name, and the name was fury — but not her fury. Not at first.

"Fabricated," she said.

"Fabricated," Tam said. "There's no debt. There never was a debt. Crane invented it — or he let Montrose believe in it, which amounts to the same thing — to get him on this ship. Four thousand crowns, secured against—" Tam shook his head slowly. "Do you know what a debt like that means for a man like him? A ranger's pay? He could work twenty years and not clear it. He's been *owned*, Isla. He's been rowing toward this fjord for however long with Crane's hand on his neck, and he thinks it's his father's ghost pulling him."

Isla looked aft, toward the stern cabin, where Sebastian Montrose was sleeping — or not sleeping — with his father's compass on the shelf beside his hammock and his father's debt in his chest, and she thought about every conversation they'd had since Rivergate. Every time he'd mentioned the debt, and it had come up often, always in the same tone: not complaint, exactly. Obligation. A man carrying something heavy and refusing to put it down because putting it down would mean his father had carried it for nothing.

*The father's name is the key that turns him.*

And she understood, then, the shape of the whole machine. Crane hadn't just bought a salvage crew. He'd built one. He'd taken a ranger with a manufactured debt and a thief with a dead mother and a grudge, and an engineer with a drowned crew in his past, and he'd assembled them into an instrument pointed at the Leeward Rivergate, and every one of them believed they were sailing for their own reasons. That was the elegance of it. That was the thing that made her cold all the way through. Crane hadn't lied to any of them, not exactly. He'd just made sure the truth they each carried was the truth that served him.

"Does he know?" she asked. "Montrose. Does he know there's no paper?"

"Crane's note says the debt must never be papered." Tam closed the ledger with great care, as if it might bite. "So no. There's nothing to show him. That's the cruelty of it. You can't disprove a debt that only lives in a man's belief."

* * *

They sat in the cold until the middle watch, and Isla turned the problem over the way Tam turned a seized engine over — listening for where it caught, where it slipped.

Here was the thing. Here was the thing she kept coming back to, the reef under her own keel: she could tell him, and the mission might die. Not because Sebastian was weak — he was the opposite of weak, he was a man made entirely of load-bearing stone — but because a man who learns his whole purpose was fabricated does not always convert that knowledge into resolve. Sometimes he converts it into rage, and rage points in every direction at once, including backward, including at the people who knew and said nothing. She thought of him learning it from her, on this deck, with Sloane's lamps burning on the horizon and four tides left, and she tried to see his face when he heard it, and she couldn't. She couldn't see it at all. And a thing you can't see the other side of is a thing that can kill you.

And here was the other thing: she could say nothing, and they'd finish the job, and Crane would get his leverage over Harrow, and Sebastian would go on carrying a debt that didn't exist, rowing for a man who'd built him like a tool. And she would be Crane. She would be exactly what Tam had sworn off, exactly what Sloane had every right to hate her for — a captain who managed her people with held-back truths, who decided what they could bear, who spent them.

"I stole his compass," she said at last. It came out of her before she'd decided to say it, the way confessions do.

Tam looked at her.

"Back at the start. He thinks I stole it for coin, and then he thinks I stole it because it pointed somewhere strange, and both of those are true, but they're not the *true* true. The true true is in that ledger too, probably, or in whatever Crane knows about the prisoner rolls. Her name was Maren Reyes. She was on the Rivergate. She died in the water when it went down, and the compass was hers before it was his father's, and I took it because it was the last thing of hers that existed outside that fjord." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "So I know what it is to find out that the story you've been carrying isn't the whole story. And I know what it did to me, finding it out. And I know that when I found it out, what I wanted — the only thing I wanted — was for someone to have told me sooner."

The glacier fields groaned out in the dark, that deep tectonic complaint of ice under strain, and the sound rolled across the water and into the hull and up through the deck into their spines.

"So you'll tell him," Tam said.

"I'll tell him." She took the ledger from between them and held it against her ribs again, where it had ridden out of Crane's cabin, and its edges dug into her like a rib of her own. "But not tonight, and not on this deck, and not with the fleet in sight and his hands full. He's on first watch at dawn. There's a stretch after that, mid-morning, when the water goes flat and there's nothing to steer. I'll tell him then. And then—" She looked north, toward the fjord mouth, a darkness within the darkness. "Then he can do whatever a man does when he finds out the weight he's been carrying was put on him by someone else. But he'll do it knowing, and he'll do it as crew. That's the only way I'll have it. No more managed men on this ship. Including me."

Tam nodded, slow, and gathered his transcription papers, and paused at the companionway. "You know he's going to want Crane's blood."

"I know."

"And we're four tides from the wreck, and Crane's blood is the only one of the three of you that knows the manifest's provenance — the seals, the registry marks, how to authenticate it for a tribunal. We need him functional."

Isla smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Then we'll keep him functional. Cold, and functional. There's a word for what you do with a tool you need and can't trust."

"Aye," Tam said. "There is." And he went below.

Isla stayed on deck until the sky went grey at the edges. She put the ledger back through Crane's cabin port while he stood his own false watch below, and she arranged it exactly as she'd found it, oilcloth and all, and she stood a moment in the dark of that cabin looking at the man's sleeping face and thinking: *you built your machine well, Aldous Crane. Every gear in its place. Every man turning for his own reason.*

*But you made one mistake. You put the gears where you could see them, and you wrote everything down, because men of ledgers always do.*

Dawn came up the color of old bone. Sebastian Montrose came up the companionway for his watch, nodded to her, took the wheel, and said, "Quiet water today," in a voice that had no idea at all what was waiting for it at mid-morning, and Isla Reyes looked at him and began, in her chest, the slow rehearsal of a sentence that was going to break a man's world in half.

*Sebastian. There's something you need to know about your debt. There is no debt.*
