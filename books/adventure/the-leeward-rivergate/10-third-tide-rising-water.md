## 10. Third Tide, Rising Water

The third tide came in gray and mean, the kind of water that looked like it had already made up its mind about you.

Sebastian stood at the rail of the *Vagrant Mercy* and watched the fjord swallow the morning. Above them the glacier hung in its ancient silence, a wall of blue-white ice two hundred feet tall, and below them—forty fathoms down, if Okafor's soundings were right—the *Leeward Rivergate* waited in the dark like something buried alive.

"Bottom's colder today," Tam said, crouched over the winch with a wrench in his teeth. He spoke around it the way he did everything, sideways and grudging. "Thermocline's dropped. Your man's going to feel it in his hands inside ten minutes."

"He knows," Sebastian said.

"That's not what I asked."

Sebastian didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer that helped. Okafor knew the cold. Okafor had been diving these waters since before Sebastian had ever seen salt. What Okafor couldn't know was what waited down there, because nobody knew—not after forty years, not since the prison ship went down with her prisoners locked below decks and her officers rowing away in the longboats.

Isla came up from the hold with the dive plan rolled in her fist like a weapon she hadn't decided to use yet. She'd barely slept. None of them had, since Sloane's parley, since Crane's ledger had turned out to be coded and Crane himself had turned out to be smiling too easily at nothing. The crew had held together through all of it the way a rope holds—frayed, kinked, still bearing load.

"Tide turns in twenty minutes," she said. "Slack gives us maybe fifty of usable bottom time before the current comes back through the narrows. Okafor goes down first, runs the guide line to the hull breach on the port side. You follow at three minutes."

"I know the plan."

"You don't like the plan."

"I don't like diving into a flooded wreck on a line I've never run, behind a man whose breathing apparatus is older than both of us." Sebastian checked his own rig anyway, third time, the way his father had taught him: valves, straps, gauges, knife. "But I like the alternative less."

"The alternative being?"

"Explaining to Crane we quit."

Isla almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Crane can drown for all I care."

"No," Sebastian said quietly. "He can't. Not until we know what he actually wants. And we won't know that until we're holding what's down there."

Okafor emerged from the forward hold in his gear, moving slow and deliberate, a big man made bigger by the suit. He'd said almost nothing all morning, which wasn't unusual—Okafor spent words like a miser—but this morning his silence had a different texture. Sebastian had noticed it last night, when they'd eaten Brannigan's stew in the galley and Okafor had stared at the bulkhead as though he could see through it, through the hull, through the water, all the way down to the thing he'd been avoiding for six days.

"You don't have to go," Sebastian told him now, low, just between them.

"Yes, I do." Okafor pulled his hood up. His face disappeared into rubber and glass until only his eyes remained, and even those were shadowed. "I surveyed this fjord eight years ago. Salvage survey, honest work, before I knew who was paying for it. I found the Rivergate then. Marked her position. Took the money and never came back." He shook his head once, slow. "A man leaves a debt like that sitting on the bottom, it doesn't rot. It waits."

Brannigan killed the engine noise to a low idle. Tam paid out the anchor line. The tide began its turn, and the current in the narrows slackened from a river to a breath.

"Line's rigged," Tam called. "Fifty meters to the drop point, then free descent on the shot weight. Buoy's marked."

Okafor stepped to the rail. Before he put his regulator in, he looked at Sebastian one last time, and what was in his eyes wasn't fear. It was older than fear. It was the look of a man walking back into a room he'd walked out of years ago and never forgiven himself for leaving.

"Three minutes," Okafor said. "Then you come."

"Three minutes," Sebastian promised.

Okafor went over the side without a splash, which was its own kind of skill, and the gray water closed over him like a door.

* * *

Sebastian counted.

It was the only thing he could do with his body while his mind ran ahead of him into the dark. One minute: Tam watching the line spool, calling depth in a flat voice. Two minutes: Isla at the rail beside him, her knuckles white on the wood, saying nothing, which from Isla was louder than screaming. Three minutes—

He went.

The cold hit him through the suit like a hand closing around his chest. Forty degrees, maybe colder, the thermocline Tam had warned about lying across the water column like a threshold. Below it the light died fast, from gray to green to a black so complete his lamp became the whole world—a cone of drifting silt and nothing else, the guide line running down ahead of him into the deep.

At thirty fathoms he felt it: a shape rising out of the blackness larger than anything a mind could properly hold. First the rail, crusted white with forty years of marine growth, an iron fence grown over with pale coral-like bloom. Then the deck beneath it, tilted ten degrees toward the fjord wall. Then the name, and even under the crust he could read it by touch as much as sight, letters half a foot high:

**LEEWARD RIVERGATE**

She was big. Prison ships were built to carry shame in bulk, and the Rivergate had carried four hundred souls, and she'd gone down with most of them still aboard. Sebastian had read the histories. Everyone had. The magistrate's tribunal had called it a navigational error in a storm. The survivors' families had called it murder. The sea had called it hers, and kept her.

His lamp swept along the hull and found the breach—port side, aft of midships, a wound torn in the plating wide enough to drive a cart through. Okafor's line ran taut into it and vanished.

Sebastian followed.

Inside, the wreck was a cathedral of ruin. Corridors opened off the breach like ribs; his lamp caught doorframes, collapsed bunks, a boot standing upright on the deck as if its owner had stepped out of it and walked away into the water. Silt hung everywhere, stirred by their movement, turning his beam into a brown fog. He swam the corridor following the line, checking his air—half a tank, plenty—and listening for Okafor's bubbles somewhere ahead.

He heard them. Then he heard something else.

A sound. Metallic, rhythmic. A man striking steel with steel, over and over, deliberate—the signal they'd agreed on. Three strikes, pause, three strikes. But wrong somehow. Faster. Frantic.

Sebastian kicked hard down the corridor.

He found Okafor twenty meters in, past a junction where two passages met, and the sight stopped Sebastian's heart for one full beat.

The corridor beyond Okafor had come down. A section of the inner bulkhead—rusted through, weakened by forty years and disturbed by their entry—had let go, and the collapse had brought a shelf of the deck above with it. Steel plate and fallen structure filled the passage nose to stern, and Okafor was on the wrong side of it.

Through a gap no wider than a hatch cover, his lamp found Sebastian's. Okafor's face behind the glass was calm in the way of a man spending enormous effort to be calm. He pointed at the collapse. Pointed at his gauge. Held up fingers.

Ten minutes of air. Maybe twelve.

Sebastian's own gauge said he had more, but not enough to dig through forty tons of wreckage with a dive knife. He tried anyway—one plate shifted, groaned, and the whole mass settled another inch with a sound like a coffin lid, silt boiling up around them until his lamp showed nothing but cloud.

When it cleared, Okafor was looking at him steadily. He tapped his wrist where a watch would be. Then he pointed back the way Sebastian had come.

Go. Get help. Save yourself.

Sebastian shook his head.

Okafor's eyes narrowed. He struck the bulkhead twice—*no*—and pointed again, insistent now, and Sebastian understood: Okafor wasn't asking to be brave. He was doing arithmetic. One diver might make it out and bring tools, a second tank, Tam with the cutting gear. Two divers burning air against a collapse meant two funerals instead of one rescue.

It was good math. It was the math any sensible man would do.

Sebastian had done that math his whole life. His father had taught it to him on the desert patrols: weigh the cost, count the lives, spend none you cannot afford. His father had also died refusing a job because refusing it was right, and the world had called that bad math ever since.

Sebastian looked at Okafor through the gap, at the big careful man who had gone down first because the debt on the bottom was his, and he took out his dive knife, jammed it into the seam of the nearest plate, and began to work.

Okafor stared. Then, slowly, he reached through the gap and gripped Sebastian's shoulder—once, hard, the way men grip each other when words are underwater—and turned to the plate on his own side. Together, from either side of the collapse, they started pulling the wreck apart with their bare hands.

* * *

On the surface, the slack tide was dying.

Tam felt it first, in the line tension, the way he felt everything first. "Current's coming back through the narrows. Eleven minutes to full flow."

"They've got time," Isla said.

"They had time ten minutes ago." Tam's wrench stilled in his hands. "Isla. Look at the bubbles."

The bubble trail from the breach had changed—instead of the steady march of a working diver, it came in bursts, ragged, scattered down-current. That wasn't digging. That was exertion. That was two men breathing hard in the dark.

Isla was already moving. "Get the spare bottle rigged. Brannigan, warm blankets, every one we have. Tam—" She stopped herself, because Tam was already rigging the bottle, because they were all already doing the things that needed doing, because this crew, broken and rebuilt and barely holding, moved as one body when the body was bleeding.

"Current hits full in nine minutes," Tam said. "Anybody coming up after that gets dragged into the narrows. We lose the line, we lose them."

"Then we don't lose the line."

Isla stripped off her jacket and started checking the second rig.

* * *

Below, the plate came free.

It moved all at once, the way rusted things do—nothing, nothing, nothing, then a shriek of parting metal and a gap wide enough for a man. Okafor came through it like water finding a channel, and for a moment the two of them hung in the brown fog, lamps finding each other, and Okafor did something Sebastian had never seen him do.

He laughed. One bark of it, muffled by the regulator, half disbelief.

Then his lamp swung past Sebastian's shoulder and stopped, and the laugh died.

Beyond the collapsed section, the corridor continued into the heart of the ship—and the walls of it were lined with doors. Cell doors. Dozens of them, small and square and bolted from outside, each one marked with a number stenciled in paint that forty years of seawater had not entirely erased. Okafor's light traveled down them the way a man's eyes travel down a list of names.

And on the nearest door, scratched into the rust with something sharp, someone had left words. Sebastian swam close and angled his lamp, and the letters resolved out of the corrosion:

*WE WERE HERE. TELL THEM WE WERE HERE.*

Four hundred souls. Locked below. Rowed away from, written off, called a navigational error. And some of them, in the last dark, had spent their final strength carving a message into a door for whoever came next.

Sebastian pressed his glove flat against the door for one second. Just one. Then he tapped his gauge at Okafor—low now, seven minutes, the current above them already beginning to pull—and they turned for the breach and swam for their lives.

The guide line was singing. Sebastian felt it in his fist before he understood it: a vibration, a strumming, the current coming through the narrows and taking the line with it. They hauled themselves along it hand over hand while the water around them thickened and leaned, silt streaming past them like wind-driven snow, the wreck groaning around them as the tide took hold of forty thousand tons of dead ship.

They broke the surface into gray daylight and driving spray, and hands were on them instantly—Isla and Tam hauling them over the rail, Brannigan wrapping them in blankets that smelled of woodsmoke, the ketch's deck heaving under the building current. Sebastian coughed half the fjord out of his lungs and lay on the boards gasping, and Okafor lay beside him doing the same, and for a long moment neither of them could speak, so they just breathed, together, in the same rhythm.

Finally Okafor rolled his head over. His face was gray-blue with cold and there was blood in the water dripping from his hairline where a plate had caught him, and he was grinning.

"You came back down," he rasped. "Against the math."

"Your math was wrong."

"My math was fine. You're just bad at it." He coughed, laughed, coughed again. "Good. Bad at math is how crews get made."

Isla crouched over them both, and Sebastian saw that her hands were shaking—not from cold. She'd been ready to come down after them herself; he could see it in her, the dive rig half-checked at her feet, the fury of almost having needed to. She looked at Okafor, then at Sebastian, and whatever she saw in his face made her look away quickly, out at the glacier, jaw tight.

"The cell block," Sebastian managed. "Port side, past the junction. It's intact. The cells are intact. And Isla—" He caught his breath. "There are markings on the doors. Writing. Things the prisoners left."

Isla went very still.

"And there's more," Sebastian said. "Past the cell block, the corridor keeps going. Deeper into the ship. Toward the strongroom, if the plans are right. The collapse bought us time—Sloane's people will have to cut through it, and that'll take them days." He pushed himself up on his elbows, the blanket falling away, the cold forgotten. "Fourth tide is tomorrow. We go back down."

Okafor, wrapped in wool and shivering, nodded slowly. "We go back down," he agreed. "Together."

Above them the glacier boomed—a calving crack, distant, a warning shot from the ice—and every head on deck turned toward it. The season was thinking about starting early.

They had four tides. Maybe fewer.

And forty fathoms down, in the dark, four hundred voices were waiting to be told that somebody had finally come back.
