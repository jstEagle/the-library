## 11. Crane's Confession

Isla found him at the stern, past midnight, feeding scraps of dried fish to the gulls that followed the Vagrant Mercy like creditors. He didn't startle when she came up beside him. Old men like Aldous Crane never startled; they simply recalculated.

"You should be sleeping," he said without turning. "Divers need their rest before the third tide."

"Divers who've been lied to sleep badly." She leaned on the rail beside him, close enough to smell the camphor on his coat, the faint sweetness of the tonic he sipped from a flask when he thought no one watched. "So do thieves. And I've been both."

The glacier walls slid past in the dark, blue-black and groaning, shedding small ice into water so still it rang like a struck bell. Somewhere below decks, Tam was arguing with the engine again, a low percussion of wrench on steel. The ketch smelled of bilgewater and Brannigan's stewed tea and the particular sourness of men and women pushed too long against a deadline.

Crane took his time answering. That was his way. Every silence was a door he wanted you to open yourself, so the blame for what lay behind it would be partly yours.

"Montrose put you up to this?" he asked finally.

"Sebastian doesn't know I'm here. He's asleep with the tide charts under his cheek like a pillow. He trusts you." Isla let that sit. "That's the part that interests me. A man like you doesn't survive forty years of salvage by being trusted. You survive by being useful right up until the moment you're not."

The gulls wheeled off toward the ice. Crane set down the fish scraps and folded his hands, and for a moment — just a moment — Isla saw the man her mother's generation had spoken of in half-whispers: the salvager who could read a wreck like a psalter, who'd pulled bodies and treasure out of the northern fjords when other men wouldn't even look at the charts.

Then the moment passed, and there was only the rival, the creditor, the man with a ledger full of other people's sins.

"Say what you've come to say," he said.

"The manifest." She kept her voice level. "You told Sebastian it was proof. Justice for the prisoners who died aboard the Rivergate. A reckoning." She turned to face him fully. "My mother was one of those prisoners, Crane. So when I ask you what you actually want with those papers, understand that I'm asking as someone with skin in the grave."

For the first time since she'd known him, Aldous Crane looked genuinely tired. Not old — he'd been old since the day she met him — but tired, the way a man is tired after carrying something heavy for a very long distance and realizing the road doesn't end.

"You want the truth?" He laughed, dry as salt. "Truths are cheap at sea. Everyone has one, and none of them float."

"I want the truth."

He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to decide something. He reached inside his coat and drew out the flask, unscrewed it, drank. Offered it to her. She shook her head.

"Magistrate Halloway," he said. "That's the name you're looking for. The man whose seal is on the Rivergate's charter, whose signature sent three hundred souls north in chains, whose fleet is even now anchored outside the fjord mouth pretending to be a salvage operation." He screwed the cap back on. "Twenty-two years ago, Halloway and I were partners. Not friends — partners. We ran the northern routes together: my ships, his warrants. Prisoner transport, supply runs, salvage rights. It was profitable and filthy and we both knew exactly what we were."

Isla said nothing. The glacier calved somewhere behind them, a sound like the world cracking its knuckles.

"Halloway got ambitious," Crane went on. "There was a shipment — gold, mostly, seized assets from the southern rebellions — being moved north on the Rivergate along with the prisoners. Officially it was to fund the new penal colony. Unofficially, Halloway meant to skim it, sink the evidence, and blame the weather. He needed a captain who could make a ship disappear properly. Someone with my reputation for finding wrecks — because no one questions a wreck found by the man famous for finding them."

"And you refused."

"I *agreed*." The word came out flat and ugly. "That's your truth, girl. I agreed. I took his money, I gave him the route through the fjord, I told him which tides would cover the scuttling. Three hundred prisoners went into that water because I drew the map." His voice didn't waver, but his hands had gone white around the flask. "The gold went down with them. Halloway kept his cut, I kept mine, and for twenty years I told myself the dead didn't mind, being dead, what we did with their story."

Isla's breath had gone shallow. She'd come looking for leverage or lies and found instead a confession so complete it felt like standing at the edge of an airlock, watching the water rise.

"But something went wrong," she managed.

"Something went wrong." He nodded slowly. "Halloway didn't just want the gold gone. He wanted the *witnesses* gone — every soul who could tie him to the shipment. The prisoners were the perfect solution: convicts, dissidents, troublemakers. No families worth bribing, no tribunals asking questions. Except some of them had families. And some of them wrote things down." He looked at her then, directly, and Isla felt the weight of twenty-two years settle onto the rail between them. "Your mother was one of the writers. We learned that too late. There were documents aboard the Rivergate — a sealed manifest, prisoner rolls, letters smuggled over years by women who knew they were going to die and wanted the record to outlive them. Halloway has spent two decades making sure nothing surfaces. Because if it does, it doesn't just prove he sank a ship. It proves he murdered three hundred people to hide a theft, and that the theft itself was state-sanctioned."

"So the manifest isn't justice to you." Isla heard her own voice go cold and distant, the way it did when grief got too large to feel. "It's a weapon."

"It's *leverage*." Crane spread his hands. "Halloway ruined me eight years ago. Stripped my charters, froze my accounts, blackened my name in every port from here to the capital. Why? Because I made the mistake of keeping copies of my correspondence with him. Insurance, I called it. He called it blackmail material waiting to happen. He couldn't prove what I had, so he destroyed me preemptively. And I have spent eight years with nothing but my name and my grudges, waiting for a way to make him bleed." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Isla had seen all voyage. "The manifest gives me that way. With those papers, I don't go to a tribunal. Tribunals can be bought. I go to Halloway himself, and I offer him a trade: the documents for everything he took from me. Charters, fortune, reputation. Or I sell them to his enemies in the capital and watch him burn slowly, publicly, while I stand on the dock and wave."

* * *

The wind had come around while they talked, and now it carried the smell of open water ahead — the deep channel where the fjord bent south toward the third dive site. Isla gripped the rail and made herself think. This was the discipline her mother had taught her, in the few years they'd had: *when the ground moves, count what stays still.*

What stayed still: the crew, asleep below. Sebastian, trusting. Okafor, who'd bled for this job. Tam, who'd left a good contract to come back. The mission itself — the manifest, the truth, the record her mother had died trying to preserve.

What moved: everything else.

"You used us," she said. "Sebastian's debt—"

"Is real enough in the eyes of the law, whatever its origins." Crane shrugged. "His father owed me. Debts pass down. That's not cruelty, that's custom."

"That's not what I asked." She stepped closer. "Did you manufacture it? Did you engineer Montrose's ruin so you'd have a leash on him? Because his father died refusing this job, Crane. Refusing *you*. And now his son is sailing straight into it with a smile on his face, thinking he's paying an honest debt."

Something flickered across the old man's face — not guilt, quite. Something adjacent to guilt, the way a vulture is adjacent to an eagle.

"I gave opportunity," he said. "What a man does with his debts is his own affair."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer you'll get." He turned back to the rail, dismissing her, and that dismissal more than anything else hardened something in Isla's chest. "Here's what matters, Reyes. The job hasn't changed. The manifest comes up from that wreck on the next tide, and we carry it out of this fjord, and what happens after—" He gestured vaguely at the dark water. "Negotiable."

"No," Isla said. "It's not negotiable. Those papers aren't yours to trade. They belong to the dead. To the families. To a tribunal, if any tribunal is still clean enough to hear them. My mother spent six years smuggling words out of a prison hold so that the *record* would survive — not so that a man like you could ransom it back to the man who killed her."

Crane's jaw tightened. "And how exactly do you propose to stop me? You're a thief on a borrowed boat, diving on a wreck you have no legal claim to, funded by my coin, provisioned by my credit. I own this voyage, girl. I own every nail in the Vagrant Mercy."

"You own the debt," Isla said quietly. "You don't own the crew."

She left him at the rail and went below, moving quiet as breath through the narrow corridor, past the galley where Brannigan slept sitting up, past the engine room where Tam had finally surrendered to exhaustion amid a scatter of tools. Sebastian's cabin was the last one aft. Through the door, she could hear him breathing, slow and even, the tide charts still crumpled beneath him.

She stood there a long time, hand on the latch, and understood that she was holding two truths and they were tearing her in half.

The first truth: if she woke him, if she told him what Crane had confessed, the voyage might end tonight. Sebastian would want to turn the ketch around, or worse, confront Crane directly, and Crane held their purse strings, their provisions, their legal cover. They were four days from the wreck with no fuel reserve and no allies. The mission would die in this corridor, and her mother's words would stay in the dark another twenty years, until Halloway's fleet or the glacier swallowed them for good.

The second truth: if she stayed silent, she became complicit. Every mile closer to the wreck was a mile closer to handing three hundred dead souls to a man who saw them as currency. She would be doing to her mother's memory exactly what Halloway had done — reducing the dead to instruments of the living.

*When the ground moves, count what stays still.*

The crew stayed still. The mission stayed still. Those were the things she could protect, tonight, in this corridor, with a closed mouth and an open plan.

Because there was a third truth, the one she'd been circling since Crane said *leverage*: they didn't need Crane anymore. Once the manifest was aboard — once it was physically in their hands — ownership would shift. Paper in hand beats paper promised. Crane's coin had bought the approach, but the dead would decide who carried their testimony home. And between Aldous Crane and the crew of the Vagrant Mercy, Isla liked their odds.

She just had to keep the old man confident, keep the crew united, and keep Sebastian from learning the shape of the leash around his neck until the manifest was safe in the strongbox and it no longer mattered.

She eased away from his door without waking him, and the guilt of that sat in her chest like swallowed ballast.

Up top, the sky was going grey at the edges. Third tide in six hours. She climbed to the wheelhouse, where the chart of the fjord lay open under the lamp, and began, methodically, to study the water they'd have to cross in the morning — and the water they'd have to cross after, once the manifest was theirs and Crane discovered that the crew of the Vagrant Mercy had opinions of their own about who owned the dead.

Behind her, unheard, the deck boards creaked.

Aldous Crane stood at the top of the companionway, watching her bent over the chart, and his eyes — pale, patient, bottomless — moved from the back of her head to the chart case bolted beside the wheel, where the crew's copy of the salvage agreement hung in its oilskin sleeve.

He smiled to himself, the smile of a man adding a line to a ledger, and went silently back down the stairs.
