## 12. Mutiny Weather

The storm came out of the north like something with a grudge.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind his ears, a taste of iron in the spray. The sky over the fjord walls had gone the color of a bruise, and the wind that came down off the glacier carried cold sharp enough to cut through oilskin like it was linen.

"Sail!" Tam's voice cracked across the deck. "Reef the main — now!"

The *Vagrant Mercy* heeled hard as the first gust hit, and Sebastian threw his weight against the line, hauling with Okafor while the canvas fought them like a living thing. Isla had the wheel, boots braced wide, hair whipping loose from its braid, and she was laughing — that wild, teeth-bared laugh she got when the sea stopped being a job and started being a fight.

"There!" Brannigan shouted from the bow, pointing a thick finger south along the fjord. "That cove — the notch in the cliff — I saw it on the survey charts!"

"Too narrow," Isla called back.

"Too drowned if we stay out here!"

The waves were building fast now, short and steep, the kind that came when wind ran against a deep channel. Sebastian had seen water like this swallow ships twice the *Mercy's* size. He'd seen it swallow his father's boat, though he'd been a boy and they'd told him it was weather, just weather, nothing anyone could have done.

He looked at the notch in the cliff wall. A black seam in the gray stone, barely wider than the ketch's beam, with white water boiling across its mouth.

"Isla." He moved to the wheel beside her, shouting against the wind. "You want to live to dive that wreck or not?"

She looked at the seam. Looked at him. Something passed between them in that half second — the same thing that had been passing between them since the salt flats, a conversation conducted entirely in glances and held risks.

"Hold on to something," she said, and turned the wheel.

* * *

The passage was thirty yards of pure violence. The cove mouth narrowed to a slot barely wider than the hull, waves detonating against the rock on either side, and Tam killed the engine at Isla's signal because a propeller in that channel was a promise of a broken shaft. They sailed it on canvas and nerve, the *Mercy* sliding sideways through the gap with a fathom of clear water on the port side and stone on the starboard, and then the cliff walls fell away and the wind died all at once, like a door closing.

Silence. Real silence, the kind Sebastian hadn't heard in weeks.

They drifted into a pool of black water ringed by cliffs two hundred feet high, sheltered so completely that the rain fell straight down instead of sideways. A shingle beach of gray pebbles at the far end. A waterfall threading down the rock face, thin as wire. The storm raged somewhere above the rim, a distant roar, a rumor.

Okafor sat down on the deck planks and put his face in his hands. Tam lit a lantern with hands that shook only a little. Brannigan said, "Well," and then didn't say anything else for a full minute.

Isla killed the sail and let the ketch drift to the middle of the pool, then dropped anchor. The chain ran out with a sound like a bell.

"Everyone hurt?" Sebastian asked.

"No," Okafor said into his hands. "That's the problem. I keep surviving things I shouldn't."

Sebastian understood that more than he could say.

* * *

They ate in the hold because the cabin was too small for five and the rain on deck came down like nails. Brannigan produced stew from somewhere — the man's ability to conjure hot food out of nothing was, in Sebastian's opinion, the closest thing to magic on the ship — and they ate in the lantern light with the storm muttering overhead.

It was Tam who broke the quiet.

"I heard them. Before the squall hit. Two long whistles off the headland." He stirred his stew without eating it. "That's a fleet signal. I ran salvage six years; I know a fleet signal."

Sebastian set down his spoon. "Sloane?"

"Or her scouts." Tam's jaw was tight. "Which means she's not behind us anymore. She's ahead. She knows the fjord. And now she knows roughly where we are, because I answered that signal before I thought about it. Old habit. Answer the fleet."

The silence got heavier.

"It was a mistake," Tam said. "I'm telling you because it was a mistake and you deserve to know."

"It's all right," Isla said.

"It's not all right." Tam looked up, and Sebastian saw something raw in the engineer's face — not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, old and worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket. "The last time I ran with a crew that got caught between a fleet and a deadline, people died. I left this life because of that. I came back because Isla asked, and because I owed her, and because I told myself this time was different." He set the spoon down. "I need to know it's different. I need to know what we're actually doing here."

"We're salvaging a wreck," Sebastian said.

"We're racing a fleet backed by a magistrate," Tam said, "for a sealed manifest that could hang half the government, with a glacier about to close the only door out. And our employer—" he glanced at Isla, then at Sebastian "—our employer is a man neither of us trusts, on a ship with a fuel reserve that won't get us home if we lose one more day. So no. I need to know what we're doing here. All of us do. And I think we should say it out loud, tonight, before the storm ends and we can't anymore."

Nobody spoke. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, thunder rolled along the cliff tops like a barrel being dragged across a floor.

It was Brannigan, of all people, who said it.

"Call the vote, then."

* * *

They did it properly, because Isla insisted on proper. She'd explained it to Sebastian once, on the second night out, when he'd asked why her crews lasted as long as they did despite the odds she ran them against. *A captain decides fast,* she'd said. *A crew decides true. You want both, you let the captain decide the sailing and the crew decide the staying.* Her mother's rule, she'd said, and hadn't elaborated, and he hadn't pushed.

They gathered in the hold. The lantern hung from the deck beams, swinging a little with the ketch's gentle motion, throwing their shadows up the walls like a fifth, sixth, seventh crew member. Sebastian stood with his back against the mast step. Isla sat on the cargo hatch. Tam, Okafor, and Brannigan arranged themselves along the port rail, and for a moment nobody said anything, and the silence had the specific weight of a thing about to break.

"Here's the truth of our position," Isla said, and her voice was steady, captain-steady, but Sebastian had learned to hear the seams in it. "Sloane has us outgunned, outmanned, and possibly outmaneuvered. Crane is a question mark I stopped trusting three days ago. The manifest is still down there, in the hold of the Rivergate, behind two fathoms of black water and a corridor that already tried to kill Okafor once. We have two usable tides left before calving season, maybe three if the glacier's late, and the glacier has never once been late." She let that sit. "The alternative is that we signal Sloane, take her buyout, keep our skins, and walk away with enough coin to disappear for a year. That's the choice. Finish it, or sell it. And I'm not going to choose for you. My mother taught me better."

Sebastian heard the name land in the hold like a dropped anchor. *My mother.* He looked at Okafor and saw the diver's hands go still in his lap. He looked at Tam and saw the engineer look down.

"Sebastian," Isla said. "You first. You've got the most to lose and the least reason to stay."

He'd been turning it over since the cove mouth, honestly, since before that — since Sloane's parley, since the ledger, since Crane's smooth voice had started sounding less like an ally and more like a trap with the door already closed. He could take Sloane's coin. He could walk away from the debt that had ridden him for six years, the debt that had shaped every choice he'd made since the funeral, the debt that had put him on a salt flat chasing a thief with his father's compass in her coat.

But the ledger had told him something else, too, something he hadn't said out loud yet: that the debt was manufactured. That Crane had built it, brick by brick, to leash him. And if the debt was a leash, then the thing at the other end of it — the thing Crane wanted, the manifest — was worth leashing a man for. Worth killing his father over, maybe, years ago, on a night they'd all called bad weather.

"My father refused this job," Sebastian said. "That's the part nobody's said plainly yet. Crane offered it to him, years back, and my father said no, and then he died running an errand nobody's ever explained to me." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I used to think the debt was what tied me to all this. Now I think the debt was just the rope Crane used to drag me here. But the reason I stay—" he looked around the hold, at the four faces in the lantern light, strangers six weeks ago, something else now "—the reason I stay is that I want to know what my father died carrying. I want to hold it in my hands. That's not a job. That's not coin. I don't have a word for it. But I'm in. Whatever the vote says, I'm in."

"Tam?" Isla said.

Tam was quiet a long moment. The lantern swung. "I want to say no," he said finally. "I want to tell you all that I'm too old for this and the smart move is the coin and a long road south. And every one of those things is true." He rubbed his face. "But I rigged the engine that got us through the ice, and I've been lying awake listening to it run, and it runs sweet. Best work I've done in years. And I keep thinking — if we sell out, that engine's last job is carrying us away from the truth. I didn't spend thirty years turning wrenches to end up a courier for liars." He exhaled. "Aye. Finish it."

"Okafor?"

The diver didn't answer right away. He was looking at his hands, turning them over, palms and backs, as if reading something written there.

"That corridor," he said at last. "When it collapsed. When the water came in." He looked up. "I wasn't afraid of dying. I want you all to know that, because it sounds like the kind of thing a man says to be brave, and it isn't. I was afraid of dying *in there* — in that ship, in a cell, in the dark, with my name on somebody's list and nobody ever coming." His voice caught, steadied. "There were people in those cells. For years. Real people, with names, and the ship's been their grave since before any of us signed on to anything. If I sell out, I leave them down there with their names still buried. I can't do that. I've been down there. I know what it's like." He nodded once, sharp, like closing a latch. "Finish it."

"Brannigan?"

The cook had been uncharacteristically silent, arms folded, staring at the deck. Now he looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't try to hide it.

"I cooked for the last crew," he said. "The one that didn't come back. I cooked their last meal, the night before, and I didn't know it was their last meal, and I've cooked ten thousand meals since trying to make up for it." He swallowed. "I came on this voyage because Isla asked, and because I'd have followed her voice out of a burning building, and I've been waiting the whole way for a reason to jump ship that didn't feel like running. This vote is that reason, one way or the other." He looked at Isla. "I'm not voting for the manifest. I'm not voting for coin. I'm voting because if we quit now, we quit scared, and I've been scared since the last crew and I'm tired of it being the loudest thing on this boat. Aye. God help us. Aye."

Four votes. Sebastian felt the arithmetic of it settle over the hold, and it was enough — four of five, a majority, done. But Isla held up a hand.

"Not done," she said. "One more."

"Isla," Tam said gently, "you don't have to—"

"My mother was a prisoner on the Leeward Rivergate." The words came out quiet and absolute, and the hold went so still Sebastian could hear the waterfall through the hull, thin and far away. "Eleven years. I was nine when they took her. I stole for a living because she taught me locks before she was taken, and I stole the compass because it was hers before it was Sebastian's father's, and I've been sailing toward that wreck since I was old enough to know it existed." Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible thing about it; it didn't shake at all. "Sloane offered me coin and I thought about it. I'll be honest with you — in the dark, on the night watch, I thought about it. A year of disappearing sounds like heaven when you've been running since you were nine." She stood. "But my mother spent eleven years in a cell writing down names. Names of everyone they brought in, everyone they moved, everyone who didn't come back. She smuggled them out a scrap at a time for a decade, and the last scrap she got out was a compass heading, and it led to a man who kept it, and that man's son is standing in this hold." She looked at Sebastian, and now her voice did shake, just barely, just at the edge. "I am not selling my mother's eleven years to the woman who runs Sloane's fleet for a magistrate. I would rather sink in this cove. That's my vote. That's the only vote I have."

Nobody moved. The lantern swung. Above them the storm roared on, indifferent, enormous.

"Then it's five for finishing," Brannigan said at last, gruff, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Unanimous. Somebody put the kettle on."

* * *

Later — much later, when the others had gone to their bunks and the ketch creaked at her anchor like a sleeping animal — Sebastian found Isla at the rail, looking up at the black rim of the cliffs where the storm showed itself in occasional flickers of lightning.

"Heading," he said quietly. "You said the last scrap she smuggled out was a compass heading."

Isla didn't turn. "North by northwest, off the coast. Toward the fjord."

"That's why the needle pointed where it pointed. On the flats. That's what you were following when I caught you."

"My mother's handwriting was on the backing plate," Isla said. "Under the brass. I'd have shown you, that first night, if you hadn't been pointing a rifle at me." A ghost of a smile. "You were very dramatic about it."

"You stole my father's compass."

"I recovered my mother's compass," she said, "from the man who was carrying it when he died." She turned then, and in the dark her eyes were bright and unflinching. "Your father and my mother were working the same job, Sebastian. From opposite ends, years apart, and both of them died for it. The manifest isn't Crane's leverage and it isn't Sloane's prize. It's theirs. It's the only thing they finished together."

Sebastian looked at her, and the whole shape of the last six years turned over in his chest like a keel coming free of the mud. "Two tides," he said. "Two tides, and then we go get it."

"Two tides," Isla agreed. And then, nodding upward, toward the storm, toward the glacier beyond it: "Listen."

He listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the thunder, there was a new sound — deep, rhythmic, like a heartbeat under the floor of the world. A cracking. A groaning of something vast beginning to move.

"The glacier," Isla said. "It's starting early."
