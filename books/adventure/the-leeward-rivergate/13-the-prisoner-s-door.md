## 13. The Prisoner's Door

The water at forty feet was the color of old bruises.

Isla hung on the downline and let the cold find her — first through the seams of her drysuit, then through everything else. Above her, the Vagrant Mercy's hull cut the fjord into a long green shadow. Below her, the Leeward Rivergate waited in the dark like a thing that had been holding its breath for twenty years.

"Comms check," Okafor said in her ear, his voice flattened by the regulator and the distance. He was ten meters up the line, safety diver, watching her back the way he'd promised he would after the third tide nearly took him. "Say something, Reyes."

"I'm here," she said.

"You don't have to do this one," he said. "Sebastian's suited up. Tam can run the lift bags. You could work the surface."

"She has to," Sebastian said over the channel, quieter. Not arguing. Just stating it, the way you'd state the weather.

He was right, and she hated that he was right. She'd spent two days since the storm cove not talking about why, and everyone had let her not talk about it, which was its own kind of mercy, and now the fifth tide was rising and there would be no more not talking about it.

The manifest was somewhere in the wreck. Crane wanted leverage; Sloane wanted salvage rights; the tribunal in Port Calloway wanted paper they could hang a magistrate with. But Isla wanted a door. A specific door, low in the hold, with a number stenciled above it that she'd only ever seen in a smuggled letter her mother had written in ink made of ash and spit: *Cell 44 faces the bilge. If anything happens, look where the water comes in.*

Her mother had been dead for nineteen years. The letter had taken eleven more to reach her, passed hand to hand through prison porters and fish buyers until it arrived at an orphanage in Saltmere smelling of brine and other people's pockets. Isla had been nine when her mother was transported aboard the Rivergate. She'd been twenty when the letter came. She'd read it four thousand times since, and every reading ended the same way, at the same line, written smaller than the rest as if the writer had been running out of light:

*They think we're cargo. We are not cargo.*

Isla spat into her mask, rinsed it, and pulled it down over her face. The world went green and close.

"Diving," she said.

She dropped the line and sank.

* * *

The Rivergate lay on her starboard side, which meant her decks were walls and her walls were floors, and the whole geometry of the ship had been turned ninety degrees like a sentence rewritten by someone who hated the author. Silt hung in the water column in slow brown veils. Her lamp carved a cone out of the dark and the dark took it back the moment she turned away.

She'd studied Tam's survey sketches for two days. She knew the route: through the gash Okafor's collapse had opened on the third tide, along the outside of the hull to the scupper line, then in through the bilge intake — *where the water comes in* — because the main hatchways were crushed flat under two hundred tons of settled iron.

The hull rose up to meet her, furred with white bacterial mats that looked, in the lamplight, like frost. She put her glove against the plating.

Cold came through it. And something else. Something that felt absurdly, embarrassingly like recognition.

"Easy," Okafor said. "Breathe. You're at the scupper."

"I know where I am."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway. That's the job."

She almost laughed. The laugh turned into a shiver on the way out. She found the bilge intake — a mouth of rusted grating half-buried in silt — and worked her knife around the edge until a section of bars came away soft as wet bread. The opening beyond was barely wider than her shoulders.

Prisoners had come through this hole. That was the thought that stopped her, hand on the grate, bubbles ticking up past her chin. Not divers with tanks and lights and a ketch waiting above. Women in chains, or near enough, wrung through this gap on some smuggled errand, passing letters and tools and — if the letter was true — years of testimony out into the current, one scrap at a time, betting their small lives on strangers downstream.

*I'm the stranger downstream,* Isla thought. *Nineteen years late.*

She went in.

* * *

Inside, the ship was a cathedral built by spite.

Her lamp found corridor bulkheads standing at wrong angles, deck plates buckled into ridges, a rain of sediment so fine it moved when she moved, closing behind her like water remembering itself. She followed Tam's line markers — orange tags every five meters — through a maze that had once been the hold's lower passage. Cell doors lined what had been the port wall and was now the ceiling, hanging open like mouths in a row, and she made herself count them as she passed. Thirty-one. Thirty-seven. Forty-one.

Forty-four.

The number was still legible, stenciled in faded white above a door that no longer opened, because the door wasn't a door anymore — the deck collapse had folded the frame inward, sealing the cell except for a gap at the top, maybe forty centimeters, where the frame had torn free of the plating.

Forty centimeters. She'd freedived through gaps half that in the kelp off Saltmere, but that was in warm water with a whole sky overhead. This was inside a grave, at depth, alone except for a voice in her ear and a line back to the living world.

"Reyes." Okafor again. "Your breathing rate just spiked. Talk to me."

"There's a gap. It's tight."

"How tight?"

"Tight."

A pause. Then Sebastian's voice, low: "Isla. The manifest matters. You matter more. If it doesn't feel right—"

"It's my mother's cell, Sebastian."

Silence on the channel. Long enough that she checked her gauge to make sure the comms hadn't died. When he spoke again his voice had changed — stripped down, no ranger command in it at all.

"Then go slow. We'll hold the line as long as your air allows. Ten minutes to the turn."

"Twelve."

"Twelve," he agreed, and she heard Tam mutter something in the background that was probably a protest and probably went ignored.

She pulled herself up the buckled plating, hand over hand, until she floated level with the gap. Below it, through the opening, her lamp showed her the cell.

A bunk frame bolted to what had been the outer wall. A shelf. Scratches on the plaster — hundreds of them, thousands, tallies and words and lines of writing too worn to read, scored into the surface by someone with a nail or a button or sheer time. The prisoners had written on their walls. Of course they had. Where else does a person write when everything portable is taken from them?

Isla's eyes stung inside the mask. She blinked it away and told herself it was salt water.

She fed her lamp through first, then her shoulders, then worked herself through the gap in a slow exhale, suit whispering against rusted metal, one snag from a torn drysuit and a flooded glove at thirty-eight feet in a fjord that killed people in minutes. She did not think about that. She thought about the tally marks. She thought about hands making them.

And then she was through, and she was inside.

* * *

The cell was small enough that her bubbles couldn't rise properly; they pooled against the collapsed deck above her and slid sideways in silver sheets, and her lamplight caught them and threw moving shadows across the writing on the walls, so that the whole room seemed to flicker with handwriting, alive, agitated, a room full of voices trying to finish their sentences.

She found the bunk frame and held it. The metal was cold through both gloves. Somewhere under the silt on the frame there might still be the shape of a mattress, the shape of a woman who had slept there for six years before the ship went down — because her mother hadn't died in the wreck, not exactly. She'd died *of* it, slowly, the way prisoners die of ships: of the voyage, the fever, the rationing, the years. The Rivergate had been condemned and anchored in this fjord as an overflow prison for three years before the storm that broke her moorings, and the prisoners aboard had been the last cargo anyone bothered to account for.

Except they had accounted for themselves. That was the whole point of the letter. That was the whole point of the scratches.

Isla swept her lamp along the walls. Tallies, yes — days, months, years counted in groups of five. But also names. Rows of names, each with a date beside it, and she understood with a lurch that these were not the living counting themselves. These were the survivors recording the dead. Someone in this cell had kept a ledger of everyone the ship took, scratched into plaster in a room the size of a coffin, because if no one else would remember them, then plaster would have to do.

She found the name halfway up the wall, at eye height for a seated woman, written larger than the others and boxed in with a careful double line, the way you'd frame something precious:

*MARA REYES. Taken 14th of Thawmonth. Wrote for us. Remember her writing.*

Isla stopped breathing. Her body did it for her, automatic, the regulator hissing, and she knelt there on the folded deck of a sunken prison with her gloved fingers an inch from her mother's name and could not make herself close the distance.

"Talk to me, Isla." Sebastian. Soft. "Please."

"She's here," Isla said. Her voice came out wrecked even through the comm. "Not — her. Her name. They wrote her name on the wall. They wrote *remember her writing.*"

"Her writing," Okafor repeated. "What writing? The letter?"

"No." Isla's heart was suddenly slamming. "The letters. Plural. She wrote for all of them — she was the one who could write, she taught school before — oh, God, Okafor, she didn't just send a letter out. She ran it. The whole smuggling chain. The documents. *Look where the water comes in* — that's not where the strongbox is, that's how they got things OUT, and if she was the writer, then her cell is where the writing lived—"

"The shelf," Sebastian said. "Check the shelf."

She checked the shelf.

Under a skin of silt, bolted to the underside where no inspector's torch would ever sweep, there was a box. Iron, small, the size of a bread loaf, with a seam around its lid and a padlock that had spent nineteen years underwater and looked it. But the box sat in a bracket, and the bracket was bolted with four nuts, and nuts, unlike padlocks, could be persuaded.

Her knife and wrench did the persuading. Two minutes that felt like two hours, her air gauge falling, her hands steady in the way hands get steady when the alternative is shaking. On the last nut her lamp caught something scratched into the bracket's underside, tiny, deliberate, in a hand she knew from eleven years of reading one letter over and over:

*For whoever comes. Thank you for coming.*

Isla Reyes, who had stolen a compass, betrayed a captain, survived a mutiny, and never once cried in front of her crew, cried inside her mask at thirty-eight feet in a sunken prison ship, and the water took the tears the moment they left her eyes, so that no one would ever see them, which was somehow exactly right.

She got the box free. It was heavier than it looked. Documents, sealed in waxed canvas layers, dense as brick. Nineteen years of testimony. Names, dates, orders, signatures — everything the magistrate had shipped into this fjord thinking the sea would keep his secrets better than any grave.

"I have it," she said. "I have the box. I'm coming out."

"Air?" Okafor asked.

She checked. "Six minutes to reserve."

"Then stop sightseeing," Tam said, and for once nobody told him to mind his tone.

* * *

Getting out was harder than getting in. The box wouldn't fit through the gap held against her chest, so she ran it through on a short tether ahead of her, feeding it into the corridor, then backing out after it shoulder by shoulder, the rust whispering against her suit, her lamp beam jinking wildly because her hands weren't steady anymore and she'd stopped pretending.

In the corridor she clipped the box to her harness, checked her line, and began the long pull back toward the gash and the green light beyond it. The orange markers ticked past. Behind her the cell doors hung open in their row, mouths in the dark, and she did not look at forty-four again because she was afraid that if she looked she'd want to stay, and staying was one thing the dead could not ask of her.

At the gash, daylight. Thin, glacial, blue-green — but daylight. She rose through it with the box against her sternum like a second heartbeat, and the surface came down to meet her faster than she expected, and then her head broke into air and wind and the sound of Tam shouting and Brannigan's big hands hauling her over the gunwale.

She lay on the deck of the Vagrant Mercy, breathing hard, mask pushed up, and the sky wheeled gray and enormous above her, and she wept — openly, ugly, in front of all of them — and not one of them looked away, and not one of them said a word.

Sebastian crouched beside her. He didn't touch the box. He touched her shoulder, once, briefly, the way you'd steady something you intended to keep.

"What's in it?" he asked.

"Everything," she said. She sat up, dripping, and put her palm flat on the waxed canvas. "Names. Orders. His signature. Nineteen years of what he did to them — to us." She looked up at him, and her voice came out level, which surprised her more than the crying had. "My mother helped build this. Every page. She was the writer, Sebastian. She was the whole network. And the last thing she scratched into that cell was a thank-you for whoever came next."

Sebastian's jaw worked. "Whoever came next."

"That's us." She stood, legs unsteady, and lifted the box, and the crew gathered around it without being asked — Tam wiping his hands on his rag first, Okafor pulling off his hood, Brannigan bending his big gray head over it like a man at a graveside. "We take it to the tribunal. All of it. Not Crane's leverage. Not Sloane's salvage claim. The tribunal, the families, the public record. That's what it was built for. That's what she built it *for*."

"And Crane?" Tam asked quietly. "When he finds out we're not handing it to him?"

Isla looked north, toward the head of the fjord, where the sixth and final tide would open tomorrow — and where, far out beyond the calving ice, she could just make out the masts of Sloane's fleet, closer than they'd been yesterday. Closer than they should be.

"He'll find out," she said, "the moment he checks his charts. Because those masts are inside the outer marks, and there's only one reason a fleet moves that fast overnight."

She met Sebastian's eyes.

"Somebody sold us the tide window. And I think I know who."
