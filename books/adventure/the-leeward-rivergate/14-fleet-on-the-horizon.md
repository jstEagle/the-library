## 14. Fleet on the Horizon

Sebastian saw the sails before anyone else did, because he'd been watching the horizon the way a man watches a wound.

Two tides left. That was the arithmetic he'd been carrying in his chest since the fourth dive — two windows of slack water before calving season turned the fjord mouth into a churning graveyard of icebergs the size of cathedrals. Two chances to bring up the manifest and run. And now, far out where the gray water met the grayer sky, three masts' worth of canvas, running lights burning even in daylight, which meant Sloane wasn't hiding anymore.

He didn't call it out right away. He stood at the bow of the *Vagrant Mercy* with his hands jammed in his coat pockets and let himself have ten more seconds of not knowing. Ten seconds where they were still just a patched ketch crawling north through a corridor of pack ice with a dead woman's documents sealed in oilcloth below decks, and the world was still wide enough to lose yourself in.

Then Isla came up the companionway, hair salt-stiff, eyes red-rimmed from the night before, and he said it plainly, because she'd earned plain speech.

"Three ships. Northeast. Making eight knots if they're making one."

She didn't gasp or curse. She leaned on the rail beside him and looked for a long moment, the way she looked at everything — like she was pricing it. "Sloane's lead ship is the one with the tricolor at the masthead," she said finally. "The other two are hired hulls. She'll hold them back until she's sure of us."

"She's sure of us."

"She's sure of *me*." Isla's jaw tightened. "There's a difference."

* * *

They gathered in the main cabin an hour later, all six of them around a chart table that had been Brannigan's galley shelf until Tam bolted it down. The manifest sat between them in its oilcloth wrapping like something that might bite. Okafor's hands were still bandaged from the third tide; he kept flexing them without seeming to notice, testing what the cold had taken and given back.

"Options," Sebastian said. "Lay them out."

"Run south through the shallows again," Tam said. He was a small man built entirely of angles and grease stains, and he spoke the way he worked — fast, precise, no wasted motion. "It bought us twelve hours last time. It won't buy twelve more. Sloane's got a local pilot aboard now; I saw the boat transfer off Cape Merrow."

"Fight," Brannigan offered, around a mouthful of bread he was eating mostly to keep his hands busy. The old cook had a crossbow he'd won in a card game and a faith in it that bordered on liturgical.

"With what?" Okafor said gently. "We're six. They're sixty, and their sixty have cannons."

"Give her the manifest," Crane said.

Everyone stopped. Aldous Crane had been quiet since the cove, since the vote, since Isla had stood in the lamplight and spoken her mother's name and the crew had chosen — barely, four to two — to finish the job. He'd cast one of the two votes against. He'd accepted it with a smile that hadn't reached anywhere near his eyes, and he'd spent the following days being helpful in small, unremarkable ways: coiling line, reading the ice, brewing tea nobody asked for.

Now he folded his hands on the chart table and said it again, reasonable as a magistrate. "Give Verity Sloane the manifest. Negotiate. We keep salvage rights, she keeps her fleet, everyone sails home rich enough. It's the only arithmetic that ends with all of us alive."

"The manifest isn't ours to trade," Isla said quietly. "It belongs to the dead."

"The dead," Crane said, "are remarkably poor negotiators."

Sebastian watched him across the table. Something had been itching at him for days — small things, the kind of things you noticed when you'd grown up learning to read men the way your father read weather. Crane volunteering for anchor watch alone. Crane asking Tam casual questions about the engine's fuel reserve. Crane standing at the stern rail every evening with a signal lamp wrapped in cloth, ostensibly checking the bulb.

"You've been very calm," Sebastian said. "Since the cove."

"I'm always calm. It's why I'm alive."

"No," Sebastian said. "You've been *settled*. Like a man who's already placed his bet." He turned to Tam. "Check the fuel bunkers. Now."

Tam went. The silence he left behind was the worst kind — the kind where everyone starts doing arithmetic they don't want to do. Isla's hand drifted toward the knife at her belt, not drawing it, just resting there, the way a person rests a hand on a doorframe in an earthquake.

Tam came back down the companionway two minutes later moving very fast, and he wasn't talking. He held up the sounding rod he used to gauge the reserve tank, and the rod came up dry except for a slick black smear near the tip.

"That's not empty-empty," Tam said, voice tight. "That's *drained*. Somebody siphoned the reserve down to fumes sometime in the last two nights. We've got maybe forty miles of steam in the main tank and nothing behind it. If we burn what's left getting away from Sloane, we drift into the calving fields with no way out."

The cabin went very still.

"Forty miles," Okafor repeated softly. "And Sloane is how far?"

"Thirty," Sebastian said. "And closing."

* * *

Crane didn't run. That was the thing Sebastian remembered afterward, the detail that made it worse than violence would have been. When Sebastian took the lamp from the stern locker and unwrapped the cloth and found the signal shutter set to Sloane's private code — the code Crane would have known from thirty years ago, when he and Sebastian's father had been rivals instead of whatever this was — Crane simply stood by the rail with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the three ships grow on the horizon, and said:

"I wondered when you'd look."

"When?" Sebastian's voice came out low and strange. "Or whether you'd get caught?"

"Those are frequently the same question." Crane turned. Up close, in the flat northern light, he looked older than he had at the Rivergate Inn — older than he had a week ago. There were new lines around his mouth, deep ones, carved by something that had been eating him from inside for years. "Sit down, boy. You'll want to sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will. You're your father's son down to the boots." Crane sighed, and for one naked moment he looked tired — genuinely, humanly tired — before the polish slid back over him like ice over water. "I sold your route to Sloane. I drained the fuel. I signaled her our position each night. And yes, before you ask, I forged the debt paper that dragged you onto this deck. All of it. Every piece."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped down a well. Sebastian heard them, counted them, felt each one hit bottom.

"Why."

"Because the magistrate ruined me twenty years ago and no one will say his name in a courtroom without laughing. Because justice is a luxury good, boy, and leverage is the only currency that buys it. Because that manifest is worth more than gold to a man who needs a knife at a magistrate's throat — and worth nothing at all in the hands of a tribunal full of his appointees." Crane spread his hands. "I needed this done, and I needed it done by people who couldn't be bought off halfway. Your father refused me once. I made sure his son wouldn't be able to afford to."

"My father died refusing you."

"Your father died *broke*, refusing me, on a job that would have fed his family for a generation." For the first time, Crane's composure cracked, and underneath it was something hot and ancient and wounded. "I offered him partnership. He called me a vulture. And then he went off alone to deliver that same manifest to a tribunal that buried it, and he drowned for nothing, and his son grew up owing money to moneylenders who—" He stopped. He'd said too much, and he knew it, and something almost like regret crossed his face before the shutters came down again. "Well. Now you know whose signature was on those notes."

Sebastian's hands were shaking. Not with fear. With the specific, white-hot fury of a man discovering that the weight he'd carried his whole life had been strapped to him deliberately, by someone standing close enough to smile while he buckled it.

"You manufactured my father's ruin," he said. "And then you manufactured mine."

"I gave you a purpose."

"You gave me a leash."

On deck, the shout came up from the crow's nest — Isla, glass raised, voice carrying flat and hard across the water: "They've launched boats! Three of them, boarding parties, half a mile!"

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and Sebastian would spend a long time afterward deciding whether he was proud of it.

The boarding boats were fifteen minutes out. The *Vagrant Mercy* had forty miles of fuel and a hundred miles of ice between her and anything resembling safety. And Aldous Crane stood at the stern rail with the wind pulling at his coat, looking — Sebastian realized with a lurch — not frightened but *expectant*, the way a man looks when his plan has entered its final movement. If Sloane took the ship, Crane would be standing beside her captain when the manifest changed hands. His leverage, delivered. If somehow they escaped, well — he'd deny everything, and half of it would be deniable.

Unless he wasn't aboard.

"Okafor," Sebastian said. "The ice shelf off the western point. The big one, where the seals haul out."

Okafor followed his gaze. The shelf lay a quarter mile to port, a pale blue slab grounded on a shoal, fifty feet high at its seaward edge, streaked with guano and old meltwater channels. Stable — for now. Calving season would take it apart within the week, but a week was longer than any rescue was going to take.

Okafor looked back at him. He didn't ask the question. He'd pulled men out of flooded corridors; he understood exactly what Sebastian was proposing, and exactly what it cost.

"Sebastian." Isla had come down from the nest, and her voice was careful. "The boats are coming. Whatever you're going to do—"

"He sold us to Sloane," Sebastian said. "He drained our fuel so we'd have no choice but to deal. So I'm taking away his choice too." He turned to Crane. "You get the shelf. You get the tent canvas, the stove, a week of rations, and the signal lamp — use it to flag down whoever comes first, if anyone ever does. The glacier will open this coast in spring. Someone salvages wrecks along here every year. Someone always finds the bones."

"You can't—" For the first time, Crane's voice climbed. "Boy, think. Without me you know nothing about the magistrate's network, nothing about—"

"We have the manifest," Isla said. "We don't need your network. We never did. That was the lie, wasn't it? The whole job was the lie."

Crane looked at her, then at Sebastian, and Sebastian watched him do the final calculation — the one where a man realizes the arithmetic has turned against him and there is no clever entry left in the ledger. What he saw in Crane's face then wasn't fear. It was something sadder: recognition. The look of a man meeting, at last, the exact shape of the choice his own ruthlessness had made inevitable.

"You're marooning an old man on the ice," Crane said quietly, "to save a stack of paper."

"I'm saving my crew," Sebastian said. "You taught me the difference. Just not on purpose."

* * *

They put him ashore at the shelf's foot in the dinghy, because Sebastian wouldn't make anyone else row him over and wouldn't trust Crane with an oar within reach of the hull. The transfer took four minutes. Crane stepped onto the blue ice without assistance, straightened his coat, and stood there among the seal bones and the guano-white rock like a monument to some forgotten, discredited virtue.

"One thing," Crane said, as Sebastian pushed off. His voice carried easily across the still water. "Verity Sloane's brother was on the Rivergate's prisoner rolls. Third cell block. She doesn't want that manifest for the magistrate's enemies, boy. She wants it *buried deeper than he does*. Remember that when she smiles at you."

Sebastian rested the oars. "Why tell me that?"

"Because I ruined your father's name and yours," Crane said, "and it turns out that at the end, I'd rather be a monster who told the truth than a friend who lied. It's the only inheritance I have left to give." He smiled, thin and terrible. "Go. Beat her by one tide. Make my ruin worth something after all."

Sebastian rowed back and didn't look at the shrinking figure on the ice, because looking would have meant feeling something about it, and there was no time to feel anything, because the boarding boats were closing and the wind was rising and somewhere under the gray water the Leeward Rivergate waited with one last secret in her hold.

He came over the side and Isla met him at the rail, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Then she put her hand flat against his chest — not tenderly, exactly; more like taking a bearing — and said, "Two tides. One wreck. Three ships. And no fuel to run."

"So we don't run," Sebastian said.

Tam appeared at the companionway, face gray with a different kind of arithmetic. "Seb. You need to see the barometer. It's falling like a stone, and that front off the glacier—" He swallowed. "Calving season isn't waiting for the calendar. It's starting *now*."

Beyond the bow, at the head of the fjord, the glacier's white face gave a sound none of them had heard before — a crack like the world's spine breaking — and the first iceberg of the season calved into the sea.
