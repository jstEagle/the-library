## 15. Fourth Tide: The Race

The bilge pump coughed twice and died.

Isla heard it from the deck, where she was coiling line with hands too numb to feel the rope, and the sound went through her like a blade between ribs. Not because the pump mattered — a pump could be fixed — but because Tam had rebuilt that pump three times since Rivergate Inn, and Tam did not build things that died without help.

"Sabotage," she said, and the word came out flat as slate.

Below, in the engine room's amber lamplight, Tam was already elbow-deep in the machinery, grease to the shoulder, his face doing something she'd never seen it do: nothing at all. Tam's face was always moving — muttering, calculating, cursing in three languages. This stillness was worse than rage.

"Fuel line's been nicked," he said finally. "Not cut. Nicked. Just enough to weep. And the spare cans are—" He pulled one up, tipped it. A thin thread of diesel ran out and vanished into the bilge water. "Drained through a pinhole. Someone wanted us to find out at the worst possible moment."

"Crane." Sebastian's voice came down the companionway like a dropped anchor. He stood there with the coded ledger still rolled in his fist, knuckles white around it. "It has his handwriting all over it."

"It has his *silence* all over it," Isla corrected. "He's been standing watches with us. Eating Brannigan's stew. Laughing at Okafor's card tricks." She looked at the weeping fuel can and felt the old shame rise again, the shame of chapter three of her life, when she'd trusted the wrong person on the *Kestrel* and lost everything but her own two hands. "That's how he does it. That's how they all do it."

Sebastian came down the ladder and crouched beside the engine, studying the nicked line. His father's compass hung at his neck now; he hadn't taken it off since the hold, since her confession, since the night the debts between them stopped being separate things. The compass swung gently with his breathing, needle steady toward the fjord, toward the wreck, toward whatever truth waited in the flooded dark.

"How much fuel do we have?" he asked.

Tam did arithmetic with his lips. "Main tank, maybe four hours of running if I nurse her. Reserve is gone. Gone-gone. We make the fourth tide on what we have or we don't make it at all."

Above them, muffled by hull planks, came the sound Isla had been dreading for six hours: oars. Many oars, in rhythm, coming out of the fog bank where Sloane's longboats had been lurking since dawn like wolves testing a fence.

"They're boarding," Okafor called from the cockpit. "Two boats, maybe three. They're not shooting. Sloane wants us alive — she wants the manifest *and* an audience."

Isla climbed up into the grey morning light. The fjord walls rose sheer on either side, glacier-scoured granite streaked with meltwater, and ahead the channel bent north into the shallows Tam had been charting off Okafor's diving surveys — water no salvage captain would risk, unmarked, unsounded, studded with drowned moraine. Behind them, half a mile back through the thinning fog, the boarding boats pulled hard. She could see Sloane in the lead boat, standing in the stern like she was born there, and even at this distance Isla felt the woman's eyes find her.

Sloane raised one hand. Not a wave. A promise.

"I betrayed her once," Isla said quietly, to Sebastian, who had come up beside her. "On the *Kestrel*. I took the salvage money and left her crew stranded at Port Meridian for a winter. Two of them didn't see spring." She swallowed. "She has every right—"

"She doesn't have the right to take the manifest." Sebastian's jaw was set the way his father's jaw must have been set, the way men's jaws set when they've decided the world owes them a debt and intend to collect. "Whatever you owe her, this isn't payment. This is her being bought."

"That's what frightens me. Being bought is the only honest thing left in her."

The first arrow — no, not an arrow, a grappling iron trailing line — arced out of the fog and splashed ten feet short. Then another, closer. The Vagrant Mercy's patched sails hung slack; the wind in the fjord came in gusts that died against the ice walls, useless, teasing.

"We can't outrun them under sail," Sebastian said.

"No," Isla agreed. "But we were never going to outrun them anyway. Were we, Tam?"

Tam's head appeared in the hatch, greased to the eyebrows, grinning the wild grin he only wore when the mathematics were terrible and beautiful at once. "The shallows," he said. "Okafor's survey. There's a channel through the drowned moraine — eight feet at low slack, maybe nine on the flood. Sloane's longboats draw four feet, but her ship draws twelve, and her *other* ships draw more. If we run the shallows, she can't follow in anything bigger than the boats."

"And the boats?" Okafor asked.

"The boats have to row against the flood tide to reach us. The flood starts in forty minutes." Tam's grin widened. "The same flood that lifts us over the moraine pushes them backward. It's a door that opens for us and shuts on them."

"And if we touch bottom?" Brannigan asked from the galley doorway, flour on his forearms, having abandoned bread mid-knead, which told Isla exactly how bad he thought this was.

"Then we're aground in front of the fleet," Tam said cheerfully, "and this becomes a very short chapter of our lives."

Isla looked at the channel ahead — brown-green water braiding over pale shapes beneath, the drowned bones of a glacier's retreat, uncharted by any chart she'd ever stolen. She looked back at the boarding boats, closing. She looked at Sebastian, who was watching her the way he'd watched her since the hold: not with suspicion anymore, but with the terrible attentiveness of a man who had decided to trust her and couldn't take it back.

"Helm's mine," she said. "Tam, give me everything the engine has and pray the nicked line holds. Okafor, sounding pole, bow, call me depths. Brannigan—" she hesitated, then smiled despite everything, "—finish the bread. If we die, we die fed."

Brannigan snorted and disappeared below, and Isla put the Vagrant Mercy's wheel over and ran for the door.

* * *

The first hour was the purest terror of her life, and Isla Reyes had once jumped a burning brig in a hurricane.

The channel was a serpent. It bent where the soundings said it would bend and then bent where they said it wouldn't; the flood tide came in lurches, lifting the ketch and dropping her, and twice the keel kissed mud with a shudder that traveled up through the wheel and into Isla's teeth. Okafor stood at the bow with the sounding pole, calling numbers in his deep calm voice — "eight and soft, seven and a half, SEVEN, come right—" — and Isla came right, and the ketch groaned, and lived.

Behind them, the boarding boats gave up at the channel mouth. She heard Sloane's voice carry across the water once, clear and cold: *"You'll run out of world eventually, Reyes!"*

"Not today!" Isla shouted back, and meant it so fiercely her throat hurt.

They gained ground. God help her, they gained ground — the shallows that should have killed them became a moat, and the moat became a road, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge they were through the worst of the moraine and running free across the flooded flats toward the fourth tide window and the wreck of the Leeward Rivergate, whose masts they could see now, black fingers breaking the green water two miles on.

That was when Tam came up the ladder slowly, wiping his hands, and his face had gone still again.

"We made it," Isla said. "Say it. We made it."

"We made it on fumes." He held up the main tank's gauge glass, empty. "The nicked line bled us faster than I thought. Main's dry. We're sailing only now, and the wind in this fjord is a rumor." He looked at the reserve locker, which they'd already opened and found gutted, and his voice dropped. "We burned our reserve getting here, Isla. Every drop. When the fifth tide comes, we'll need the engine to hold position over the wreck against the current. Diving operations need a station-keeping engine. Without fuel—"

"Without fuel we drift," Sebastian finished grimly. "We drift while we dive, and the current takes us onto the ice, or out to sea, or onto Sloane's guns."

Nobody said anything. The ketch slipped through the green water, sails full of almost-nothing, and the wreck grew larger ahead, and somewhere behind them beyond the shallows the fleet would be finding its way around, patient as tide itself.

Isla gripped the wheel and felt the exhilaration of the run curdle into something colder. They had beaten the boats. They had beaten the shallows. But the race wasn't against Sloane anymore, and it never really had been. The race was against arithmetic — tides, fuel, hours — and arithmetic did not parley, did not turn its fleet around, did not show mercy to anyone's mother's memory.

"Then we don't waste a minute of the fourth tide," she said. "We dive now. All of us. We get the manifest on this tide, because there may not be fuel enough for a fifth."

Sebastian met her eyes. "And after?"

"After," Isla said, watching the black masts rise against the glacier, "we invent a miracle. It's what crews do."

She spun the wheel and ran for the wreck, running on nothing but wind and stubbornness, and behind her the fjord narrowed, and the ice waited, and the tide began to fall.
