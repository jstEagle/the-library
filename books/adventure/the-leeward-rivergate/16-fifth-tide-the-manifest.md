## 16. Fifth Tide: The Manifest

The fifth tide came in gray and mean, the water in the fjord running the color of old pewter, and Sebastian Montrose stood at the rail of the *Vagrant Mercy* with his father's compass open in his palm and no idea why his hands were shaking.

He'd been cold before. He'd been scared before. He'd gone down after Okafor on the third tide into a flooded corridor that should have killed them both, and he'd come up laughing, half-mad with relief, and something between him and the big diver had been settled ever since — a wordless thing, a brotherhood sealed in black water. That hadn't shaken him.

This did.

"Two hours of window," Tam said at his elbow, squinting at the tide gauge she'd rigged from salvaged brass and stubbornness. "Maybe less. The glacier's been groaning all morning. You hear it?"

He heard it. Everyone had been hearing it since dawn — a deep, tectonic complaint from up-fjord, like the mountain clearing its throat. Calving season was supposed to be three days out. The ice had other opinions.

"Isla ready?"

"She's been in the water since first light." Tam's voice went careful. "She asked me to tell you something when you woke. Said you'd want to hear it down there, not up here."

Sebastian looked at her.

"She found the hold," Tam said. "The prisoner cells. She found her mother's door, Sebastian. She's been down twice already this morning and Okafor finally hauled her up because she wouldn't stop looking through the silt." Tam paused, choosing words the way you chose footholds on scree. "There's a strongbox. Documents. Years of them, smuggled cell to cell. And she says one of the names on the outer wrapping is Montrose."

The compass needle trembled against its glass, swinging toward the wreck below as if it had always known.

His father had died eleven years ago with a debt on his name and a lie in his mouth, drowned in a river crossing that everyone called an accident and Sebastian had spent a decade calling bad luck. He had carried that debt since he was nineteen years old — carried it into the ranger service, carried it across the salt flats chasing a thief who turned out to be carrying his father's compass toward the coast for reasons neither of them understood yet.

*One of the names is Montrose.*

"Rig my gear," he said.

* * *

The descent was different this time.

On the fourth tide they'd gone down fighting — dodging Sloane's boarding boats, working by feel while the surface above them churned with hulls and shouting. Today the fjord was strangely quiet. Sloane's fleet sat beyond the glacier's shoulder, blocked by the early calving warnings, licking their wounds after Isla's run through the shallows had cost them two boats and their patience. Crane was gone — marooned on an ice shelf with a tent, a flare gun, and Sebastian's contempt — and without his sabotage the *Vagrant Mercy* rode steady over the wreck, her patched engine ticking like a contented animal.

Which left only the water, and the dark, and the ship.

The *Leeward Rivergate* lay on her starboard side at forty fathoms, a prison ship gone down with her cargo of the condemned still locked below, and she was the largest thing Sebastian had ever seen underwater. She didn't feel like a wreck. She felt like a building — corridors and stairwells, bulkheads and barred doors, a whole vertical city tipped into the dark. Their dive lights swept along her flank and the hull plating threw back pale reflections, sheets of drifting silt glowing like snowfall in reverse.

Isla waited at the broken stern gallery, tethered to the mizzen channel, her lamp angled down into the gash where the prisoners' hold had split open to the sea. When his light touched hers she turned, and even through the mask he could read her face. She'd been crying down here. The salt hid it from the ocean but not from him.

She tapped her slate. *Ready?*

He tapped back. *Show me.*

They dropped through the gash together.

Inside, the hold was a cathedral of ruin. Bunk frames rusted to lace. Chains fused to ring bolts, trailing off into the dark like kelp made of iron. Cell doors stood open or hung by one hinge, and the numbers were still legible on some of them, stenciled in paint that forty years of seawater had faded to ghosts: 14, 15, 22, 31.

Isla led him to a door near the forward bulkhead. Number 9.

She put her gloved hand flat against it and held it there, and Sebastian hovered behind her in the green-black silence and understood that he was trespassing on something sacred. Her mother had lived in this box. Had scratched whatever people scratch into walls to prove they existed. Had died in it, or near it, when the ship went down — one of the condemned, one of the magistrate's erased, one of the names nobody was ever supposed to say again.

Then Isla moved aside and showed him the strongbox.

It was an ammunition case, naval issue, wedged behind a loose plate in the bulkhead where generations of searching hands would never think to look. The prisoners had wrapped it in oilcloth and tarred canvas and bound it with wire, and they'd labeled it — because they had known, all of them, that someday someone would have to find it, and finders need directions. Names ran down the oilcloth in rows, written in different hands, some in ink, some in grease pencil, some scratched with nails:

*Delivered by M. Montrose. If not him, then whoever he trusted. If no one, then let the sea keep it better than the land did.*

Sebastian stopped breathing.

The water pressed in. His own heartbeat filled his ears, huge and slow, a drum in a locked room. He reached out and touched the letters — *M. Montrose* — and his father's name dissolved under his glove into a smear of forty-year-old ink, and somewhere far above him the glacier groaned again, long and low, like the world mourning in advance.

*I know,* Isla's slate said, when he couldn't write anything at all. *I know. I'm sorry.*

*My father was here,* he wrote. His letters came out jagged. *Before he died.*

*He was more than here.* She hesitated, then turned her slate so the lamp caught it fully. *Okafor and I opened the box this morning. There are hundreds of pages. Testimony. Shipping manifests. Prisoner rolls. And one document sealed in wax, separate from the rest, marked for delivery to the coastal tribunal. It's the master manifest, Sebastian. Every name. Every sentence. Every payment the magistrate took to make people disappear. Your father wasn't carrying a debt when he died.*

Her lamp held steady on the last line.

*He was carrying this.*

* * *

They brought it up slow, the way you bring up anything that matters — wrapped, weighted, checked at every stop for decompression and for damage. Okafor met them at the ladder and took the case in both arms like a sleeping child. Tam had the hold lit and warmed and Brannigan had tea going, because Brannigan believed that no catastrophe could survive contact with a hot drink, and for once Sebastian was grateful for the superstition.

They laid everything out on the mess table under lamplight. Pages and pages of it, dried and pressed flat between boards, the ink ghostly but legible. Tam worked with tweezers and a magnifier. Isla read aloud in a low voice, name after name after name, and the cabin filled with the dead the way a room fills with smoke.

"Here." Tam's voice cracked on it. "Page forty-one. Cross-referenced against the tribunal's original warrant — the one that was quashed. Look at the annotation."

Sebastian looked.

His father's handwriting. He knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a crowd — the narrow slanted script, the way he crossed his sevens. Marcus Montrose had annotated the manifest himself, years ago, in the margin beside the magistrate's signature:

*Obtained from source inside Rivergate. Delivery arranged via river crossing, north bank, autumn crossing. If I do not reach the coast, my son knows nothing. Keep it that way. He carries enough.*

"He knew," Sebastian said. The words came out flat, strange, like someone else's voice run through a bad radio. "He knew he might die doing it. He wrote instructions. He—" His throat closed. He stared at the page. "He told them to keep it from me. He thought knowing would be a burden."

"It was," Isla said quietly. "Just not the one he meant."

Sebastian laughed. It came out wrong, a single harsh note with nothing behind it. Eleven years. Eleven years he had hated his father a little, in the secret place where sons keep the things they can't say — hated him for the debt, for the recklessness, for dying stupid in a river with his affairs tangled and his name stained. Sebastian had built his whole life in the negative space of that death. Careful where his father was careless. Solvent where his father was ruined. A ranger who followed rules because rules were the opposite of whatever had killed Marcus Montrose in the north-bank current.

And the whole time, the truth had been sitting in a steel box at the bottom of a fjord, waiting.

His father hadn't died fleeing debt. He'd died delivering evidence — evidence that named the magistrate who'd manufactured the very debt Sebastian inherited, the forged paper Aldous Crane had used as a leash, the lie that had dragged Sebastian across the salt flats after a thief and onto this deck and down into this hold. The chain ran unbroken from cell nine to the north-bank crossing to the salt flats to tonight. His father's unfinished work had simply been waiting for someone to pick it up.

"He didn't fail," Sebastian said. "Everyone said he failed. The tribunal records say the courier never arrived. But he got it out of the prison. He got it to a cache, or a contact, and the contact got it aboard—"

"The supply runs," Isla said. "Tam found the shipping logs. The Rivergate took quarterly supply barges out of the same port your father worked. Someone on those crews was moving documents both directions for years. Your father was the outside half of it. The prisoners were the inside half." She touched the strongbox, gently, the way you touch a wound that's finally healing. "My mother was the inside half. They never met. They probably never knew each other's names. And they built the same thing anyway, from both ends, in the dark, for years."

Sebastian looked at the table. At the names. At his father's handwriting sitting three inches from a dead woman's grease-pencil marks, two strangers collaborating across a wall of steel and sea.

The debt he'd carried his whole adult life — the number, the shame, the weight that had bent him into a careful man — had never been his. It had been manufactured, first by the magistrate to bury his father's work, then by Crane to weaponize it. And underneath the forgery, underneath all of it, was the real inheritance: not money owed but truth owed. A debt of witness. His father had paid everything he had into it and gotten eleven fathoms short of the coast.

"We finish it," Sebastian said. "Tomorrow. Sixth tide. We take the manifest up, we take the *Mercy* out through whatever hole Okafor finds in that ice, and we put this on the tribunal's desk if we have to carry it through the calving itself."

"That's the spirit," Brannigan said, refilling his tea. "Now drink that before you argue with a glacier."

But Tam wasn't listening anymore. She'd gone still over the shipping logs, magnifier forgotten in her hand, and the look on her face was the one she wore when an engine made a sound it shouldn't.

"Sebastian," she said slowly. "If your father was running documents out on the supply barges — quarterly, for years — then the magistrate has known about a leak since before the ship sank. He didn't sink the Rivergate to punish the prisoners." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible. "He sank it to destroy the evidence. Your father's evidence. Which means the sinking wasn't an accident either."

The cabin went very quiet. Outside, the glacier spoke again — longer this time, closer, a sound like a continent deciding something.

"And there's one more thing," Tam said. "The last entry in the barge log. The final supply run before the sinking. The manifest lists a passenger transferring aboard at the north-bank crossing. One passenger, no cargo, paid passage." She turned the log around and pushed it across the table so the lamplight fell on the line.

*A. Crane, north bank to Rivergate anchorage, private business.*

Crane hadn't just known about the wreck. Crane had been aboard the *Leeward Rivergate* the season she went down. Whatever he wanted the manifest for — leverage, he'd said, confession, he'd claimed — he'd been standing on her decks when the magistrate's plan came for her, and he had rowed away, and he had let forty years pass and a dozen lives get spent before he hired a grieving son and a thief's daughter to go down and fetch what he'd swum away from.

Sebastian closed his father's logbook with hands that had finally stopped shaking, because fury, he discovered, steadies a man better than grief ever did.

"One tide left," he said. "Let's not waste it on him."
