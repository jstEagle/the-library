## 17. Calving Season Opens

Isla felt it before she heard it — a tremor in the deck plates under her boots, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. She was in the galley at dawn, warming her hands on a tin mug Brannigan had pressed into them without a word, and the mug's surface shivered in rings.

"Tam," she called up the companionway. "Tam, you feel that?"

The engine room hatch banged open and Tam's head appeared, grease-streaked, eyes already wide. "Felt it an hour ago. Thought it was my pump bearings." He climbed out, wiping his hands on a rag that only spread the black around. "It's not my bearings."

They came up on deck together and stood at the rail, and the fjord opened before them like a held breath.

The Leeward Rivergate lay two hundred feet below, her masts furred with silt, her hull cradled in the cold dark where they'd spent five days hauling out crates and sealed tubes and one iron strongbox that Isla still hadn't been able to open. The manifest was aboard the *Vagrant Mercy* now, wrapped in oilcloth, lashed under Sebastian's bunk. They'd done it. Fifth tide, done. All that remained was to sail out the way they'd sailed in, past the glacier's snout, into open water where Sloane's fleet waited — and Isla had spent three sleepless nights deciding what she would say when Verity Sloane's guns covered them.

The glacier had other plans.

She saw it first as a change in the light. The ice face at the fjord mouth — a blue-white wall eight hundred feet high, the color of deep water and old glass — had begun to shed. Not the small calving they'd watched on the way in, house-sized blocks peeling off with a crack like a rifle shot. This was different. This was the whole face breathing. Fissures she could see from a mile away were opening along the top of the ice, running back toward the icefield like veins filling with ink, and the morning sun caught the spray of meltwater pouring off every edge of it.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, that's early."

"How early?" Sebastian was beside her now. He'd come up silent, the way he did, compass in his pocket and the manifest's weight apparently riding on his shoulders too, because he looked like a man who hadn't slept either.

"Calving season should open in nine days," Tam said. "Maybe ten. That's what the charts said. That's what *Crane* said." He stopped, and Isla heard the thought land in him before he finished it. "He knew the exact windows. Six tides, he told us. Precise as scripture."

"He also sold our route to Sloane," Sebastian said flatly. "So maybe we stop taking his word for anything."

The glacier groaned. It was not a sound Isla could describe to anyone who hadn't heard it — a sound with weight, with intent, the sound of something vast deciding to move. A section of the ice face the size of a cathedral began to lean.

They all watched it go. Nobody spoke. It fell for what felt like ten seconds, a white cliff surrendering to gravity, and when it hit the water the wave came at them across the whole width of the fjord, and Tam was shouting to get the ketch's head into it, and Okafor was already at the wheel, and the *Vagrant Mercy* rose and fell like a leaf on a dropped bucket of water, and then it passed, and the silence after was worse than the roar.

Where the ice face had been, there was open water. For now.

Beyond it, through the gap, Isla could see the sea — gray, choppy, ordinary — and beyond that, faint on the horizon, the shapes of ships. Sloane's fleet, anchored off the headland, exactly where they'd been two days ago. Waiting for the fjord to give them up.

"They're outside," Okafor said quietly. He'd come up from checking the anchor line, and his voice had the careful evenness he used when things were bad. "And we're in here."

"And in about—" Tam squinted at the ice face, doing arithmetic no one wanted done, "—two days, maybe less, that gap is going to be a wall of brash ice fifty feet thick. Maybe a hundred. Nothing sails through that. Nothing swims through that. Nothing gets through that until the melt channels open in spring."

Isla turned and looked back up the fjord, the other direction. Twenty miles of water ending in the glacier's root, walls of granite rising sheer on either side, scoured smooth by ice ten thousand years dead. No beaches. No passes marked on any chart she'd ever stolen or read. The *Vagrant Mercy*, patched and stubborn and carrying the most important paper any of them had ever touched, sat in a stone bottle with a cork of ice closing behind her.

"We beat the fleet by one tide," Sebastian said. There was no triumph in it. He said it the way a man reads his own sentence.

"We beat the fleet by one tide," Isla agreed, "and the fjord just took the tide back."

* * *

By midmorning the calving was continuous. It came in waves now, every twenty minutes or so, each collapse bigger than the last, and the waves they threw off kept the whole crew at the lines keeping the ketch from grinding against the dock pilings of the abandoned survey station they'd tied up alongside. The station was a rotting timber shack on a gravel shelf — Crane's people had used it, years ago; there were still rusted ring bolts in the rock and a stove that Brannigan had coaxed back to life because Brannigan could coax fire out of wet newspaper if the mood took him.

They gathered in the shack around noon, all six of them, because there was nothing left to load and nowhere left to dive, and the manifest sat on the table between them like a guest of honor nobody had invited.

Okafor laid it out plainly, because plainness was his gift. "Options. One: we wait for spring. Four months, maybe five, in this shack, eating what Brannigan can stretch, and Sloane's fleet goes home and tells everyone we died, and the magistrate sleeps easy, and everything we bled for rots in a tin box." He paused. "Two: we run the gauntlet tomorrow, first light, before the brash closes. Full speed, straight through the gap, and pray we punch through before it thickens. And if we don't punch through, we die slow in floating ice with Sloane picking over whatever washes out."

"Three?" Isla asked.

"There is no three. I've been through every survey I ever ran in this water. The fjord has one mouth."

Brannigan set down a plate of hardtack and salt pork that nobody reached for. "There's a fourth thing," he said, "but it isn't an option, it's just a fact. Fact is, the old man Crane had charts. Paper charts, locked in his cabin trunk, and I saw him reading them the night before he went up the mast to cut our own halyards like the cowardly—"

"He didn't cut the halyards," Sebastian said. "That was me."

"You know what I mean."

"The trunk went down with the pinnace when we marooned him," Tam said. "On the ice shelf. With Crane standing next to it."

"Then somebody dives for the pinnace," Brannigan said, "because I watched that man fold a chart along a line that wasn't the fjord mouth. I watched him do it. Folded it twice, tucked it in his coat, and looked pleased with himself, which for Aldous Crane is a facial expression I have spent eleven years learning to dread."

The shack went quiet. Outside, the glacier boomed, and dust of ice glittered down through the light like snow falling upward.

"A submerged channel," Okafor said slowly. "A meltwater outflow. Under the ice."

"I've heard of them," Tam said. "Glacier rivers. They run under the snout and out beneath the terminus. Some of them are big enough to float a boat at high melt. But you'd need to know where it comes out, and how deep, and whether the roof of it has collapsed, and—" he shook his head, "—and you'd need to be insane."

Isla looked at the manifest. Her mother had spent eleven years in the hold of the *Leeward Rivergate*, and in eleven years she had smuggled out documents, page by page, hidden in laundry and sewage barrels and once, according to the prison letters they'd found in the strongbox, sewn into a dead woman's burial shroud. Eleven years, and she had never stopped looking for a way out that everyone else said didn't exist.

"We dive for the trunk," Isla said.

Sebastian looked at her. In five days she had learned to read his silences the way sailors read weather, and this one meant *I was already thinking it and hated myself for it.*

"It'll be under the ice shelf by now," he said. "Or crushed."

"Crane marooned himself rich," Isla said. "That trunk is the most valuable object within thirty miles and Crane knew it when he packed it. He wouldn't leave it loose on the ice. He'd have weighted it, cached it, sunk it somewhere he could come back for. Which means it's findable. And Okafor—" she turned to him, "—you dove that shelf two days ago. You know the bottom there."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did when he'd already decided and was just letting his mouth catch up. "Bottom drops to forty feet off the shelf edge. Silt and rubble. Cold enough to kill in fifteen minutes, but I've worked worse." He stood. "Get me the good line and the spare tank. And somebody make coffee worth drinking, because if I'm going back in that water I'm not doing it sober on Brannigan's dishwater."

* * *

The dive nearly killed him, and Isla never learned all of what happened down there, because Okafor was a man who kept his terror the way other men kept money — close, counted, and never displayed. What she knew was this: he went down at slack water in the lee of the ice shelf, in water so cold the pressure gauge iced over, and he was down nineteen minutes when his safety line went slack, and Sebastian was stripping his coat before anyone could speak, and Tam had to physically hold him at the rail while the line twitched twice and then pulled taut and steady, the signal they'd agreed on, *coming up, have it.*

Okafor surfaced with the trunk strapped to his chest and blood in the water around him — a gash on his thigh from the rubble, nothing deep, but the cold had taken his feeling of it and he hadn't known until he saw red. They hauled him over the rail and Brannigan wrapped him in every blanket aboard and Isla put the trunk on the table and broke the lock with a pry bar while her hands shook in a way she decided was from the wind.

Inside: Crane's papers. His real ledger, the coded one, its secrets already spent. Letters. And folded twice, tucked in an oilskin sleeve, a chart.

Not a chart of the fjord. A chart of the *glacier*.

Hand-drawn, decades old, in faded ink — drawn by someone who had surveyed the ice in another era, when the snout stood further back and the melt ran differently. Across it, in Crane's precise hand, were annotations. Soundings. Depths. A dotted line running from the upper fjord, under the ice, and emerging — this was the part that made Tam sit down slowly on a crate — emerging in a cove on the seaward side, four miles north of the fjord mouth, behind a headland the fleet couldn't see past.

"There really is a channel," Tam whispered. He traced the line with one finger, not touching the paper. "Under the whole snout. Look — he's written depths. Twelve feet at the entrance shoal. Nineteen at the bend. Roof height forty feet, 'as of'—" he squinted at the date, "—thirty-one years ago."

"Thirty-one years," Okafor said from his nest of blankets. "Ice moves. Melt cuts. That roof could be five feet or fifty. Could be gone entirely."

"Could be," Isla said. "But it's a door. It's the only door. And Crane drew it because Crane always keeps a way out for himself." She thought of the man they'd left on the ice shelf with his charts and his schemes, and felt something between hatred and a terrible kind of understanding. Even ruined, even marooned, Aldous Crane had made sure the map to his escape route was somewhere he could retrieve. He just hadn't planned on her retrieving it first.

Sebastian stood at the window, looking up the fjord toward the glacier's root, where the channel's entrance must lie. "Twelve feet at the entrance shoal," he said. "The *Vagrant Mercy* draws six and a half."

"With cargo out and ballast trimmed, five and a half," Tam said. "I can get her to five if we strip everything that isn't hull, crew, and that table."

"The manifest rides light," Isla said. "It rides in my coat."

Nobody argued that.

Outside, the glacier calved again — the biggest yet, a whole shoulder of the ice face going, and the shockwave rattled the shack's one window in its frame, and when the echo faded they could hear it: the new rhythm of the ice, faster now, hungry, the season arriving like a debt collector who doesn't knock.

Tam did the arithmetic aloud, because someone had to. "At current rate, the mouth closes day after tomorrow, nightfall at latest. The channel entrance is twelve miles up-fjord. We sail tonight, take the entrance at slack water before dawn, and run the channel blind, by lead line and prayer, following a thirty-one-year-old map drawn by a man who hates us."

Silence.

"And if the roof has come down," Okafor said, "we die in the dark under a mountain of ice, and nobody ever finds us, and the magistrate wins forever."

More silence.

Isla picked up the chart. Beneath Crane's annotations, half-erased, there was older writing — the original surveyor's, in a fine copperplate hand. She tilted it to the light and read the signature at the bottom corner, and the floor of the world shifted under her.

*M. Reyes.*

Her mother's name. Her mother had surveyed this glacier — before the arrest, before the *Rivergate*, before the hold and the cells and the eleven years. Her mother had mapped the way out of this fjord a decade before she ever needed one, and the map had ended up in Crane's possession, and Crane had never known whose hand he was holding.

"She knew," Isla said. Her voice came out strange, thin. "She knew there was a way out. She drew it. She was a prisoner on a ship at the bottom of this fjord, and the whole time, the way out was in her own handwriting, sitting in a rival salvager's trunk." She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Eleven years. She was eleven years from her own map."

Sebastian crossed the shack and put his hand over hers on the paper. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. That was the thing about him she'd stopped fighting weeks ago — he understood debts, the inherited kind, the kind that outlive the people who owed them.

"We sail tonight," he said to the room. "All in favor."

Six hands. Even Okafor's, raised from under three blankets, moving like a slow flag.

* * *

They stripped the *Vagrant Mercy* until she was bare as a bone. Cargo out. Spare spars out. Half the water out, over Brannigan's loud objections, and half the fuel, over Tam's louder ones. The manifest went into an oilskin wallet, and the wallet went inside Isla's coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

They cast off at dusk, into a fjord that thundered on both horizons — the glacier behind them shedding itself into history, the mouth ahead closing like a wound. Ice ground against the hull in whispers. The last light showed them the gap at the fjord mouth, narrower now, choked with drifting brash, and beyond it the distant lights of Sloane's fleet, patient as vultures, waiting for a door that was about to stop existing.

"Let them wait," Isla murmured, and felt the manifest shift against her ribs. *You drew the door, Mama. Show me the way through.*

They came to the channel entrance at midnight, where the glacier's root met black water, and Tam killed the engine, and Okafor went down with a lantern to look at the throat of it — and came up in ninety seconds, hauling himself over the rail fast, eyes enormous in the lantern light.

"It's open," he breathed. "It's *open*. Forty feet of clear water under a roof of blue ice, and a current running out to sea like a river." He grabbed Sebastian's arm. "But there's something else down there. Something big, wedged in the bend. And it's got a name on it."

Isla's blood went cold. "What name?"

Okafor looked at her, and then at the chart in her hands, and said the words that turned the whole night sideways:

"*Leeward Rivergate.*"
