## 18. The Back Channel

The glacier calved at dawn, and the sound was the end of the world.

Sebastian stood on the deck of the *Vagrant Mercy* and watched a wall of ice the size of a cathedral shear off the glacier's face and fall into the fjord. The wave it threw came down the channel like a moving hill, and Tam had the engine screaming before anyone gave the order, turning the ketch's bow into the swell so they rode up it and down the other side instead of being rolled like a toy boat in a bathtub. Behind them, where the fjord mouth had been open water two days ago, there was now a dam of brash ice and bergs grinding against each other, sealing them in.

"Nobody's getting out that way," Okafor said quietly. "Not us. Not Sloane."

"Not for weeks," Tam added. "Maybe months. The channel won't clear until the spring melt."

Sebastian looked at Isla. She stood at the rail with her mother's compass open in her palm, the needle steady, pointing not toward the sea but inland — north, up the fjord, toward the glacier itself. She'd been staring at it since the calving stopped, and when she finally looked up, she wasn't despairing. She was thinking.

"We're not going out that way," she said. "We're going through."

* * *

Okafor remembered it over breakfast, or what passed for breakfast — Brannigan's last real meal, hard bread and salt fish, eaten in silence because nobody had the stomach for talk. He set his mug down mid-sip, and the look on his face made Sebastian stop chewing.

"The meltwater channel," Okafor said. "Two years ago. I did survey dives along the glacier terminus for the coastal authority — before I swore off diving, before all of it. There's a river under the ice. Meltwater from the interior, running out to the fjord through a tunnel in the glacier. It was big enough to swim when I found it. Big enough, maybe, to float a raft."

"A raft through a glacier," Brannigan said flatly. "Through the dark. Under a hundred feet of ice."

"It drains the whole icefield. In summer it runs fast, but right now, with calving season just opening—" Okafor spread his hands. "Right now it might be walkable in places. Wadable. The exit is a mile downstream of the fjord mouth, behind the headland. Sloane's fleet can't see it from their anchorage. They'll be watching the sealed mouth, waiting for us to do something stupid."

"And if we don't come out where they're watching," Isla said slowly, "they'll think we're still trapped inside. Which buys us time even after we're gone."

Tam had already pulled out the charts, spreading them across the chart table, weighing the corners with brass fittings and a tin of Brannigan's tea. There was nothing on the charts — no channel marked, no exit. But Okafor took a pencil and drew from memory: the line of the tunnel as he'd swum it, the chamber where it widened, the bend where the ceiling dipped low, the final stretch where daylight leaked down through crevasses in blue shafts.

"I only went in four hundred yards," he admitted. "Past that, I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the honest truth of it."

Silence around the table. Sebastian did the arithmetic every one of them was doing: a mile of unknown tunnel under living ice, carrying a sealed manifest worth more than all their lives, with the glacier actively shedding itself into the sea. Against it: Sloane's three ships outside, Crane marooned on an ice shelf with a mouth full of threats, and a magistrate whose retainers would kill them all for what lay wrapped in oilcloth in the hold.

"We could wait," Brannigan said. "Weeks. Months. Eat the ship, eat each other—"

"No," Sebastian said. It came out harder than he meant. He looked around the table. "Every day we wait is a day the magistrate's people get closer to figuring out we have the manifest. A day Crane thaws out enough to bargain. A day Sloane gets desperate enough to try blasting the mouth." He paused. "And my father waited twenty years to deliver this thing. I'm done waiting."

Isla met his eyes across the table and nodded once.

Okafor stood. "Then I'll lead. It's my channel. My mistake to remember, my risk to take first."

Brannigan laughed — a short, bitter bark with no humor in it. "You're all mad. Every one of you." He pushed back his chair. "Get me an hour. If we're doing something suicidal, nobody does it hungry."

* * *

They stripped the *Vagrant Mercy* to essentials by midmorning. The manifest went into a waterproof satchel strapped to Sebastian's chest, and Isla checked the knots twice, then a third time, then made him stand while she checked them again. The compass went around her neck. Rope, two hundred feet of it. Ice axes from the climbing kit Tam had never explained and nobody asked about. The raft was Tam's masterpiece of desperation: two closed cargo hatches lashed to a frame of spars, light enough for four people to carry over ice, buoyant enough — supposedly — to float a flooded tunnel.

"You'll carry it to the tunnel mouth," Tam said. "After that, it carries you. Or it doesn't." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and somehow that was worse.

The five of them went over the side onto the ice at noon, and Sebastian looked back once at the ketch riding alone in the trapped water, sails furled, looking small and stubborn as a gull on a rock. They'd left her provisioned and ready. If they lived, they'd come back for her. If they didn't, she'd sit there until the ice crushed her, and no one would ever know what she'd carried.

He didn't let himself finish that thought.

The first mile was easy going — old sea ice, wind-scoured, solid underfoot. The glacier face grew ahead of them until it filled half the sky, a cliff of blue-green ice streaked with gray moraine, groaning continuously now, a deep tectonic complaint that Sebastian felt in his back teeth. Chunks calved off as they watched, some the size of wagons, and each fall sent cracks spidering across the surface around their boots.

"There." Okafor pointed.

The tunnel mouth opened at the glacier's base where a meltstream ran out — a black arch thirty feet high, its ceiling hung with icicles like the teeth of something ancient, its floor a river of gray water running fast and shallow over gravel. Cold poured out of it. Not cold like winter — cold like a crypt, air that had never seen the sun, and standing at the threshold Sebastian understood that they were about to walk into the throat of the same creature that had built the fjord.

"Four hundred yards, I know," Okafor said. "After that, we find out together." He looked at each of them in turn. "Rope stays on. Ten-foot spacing. Nobody wanders. If the water rises, we climb — there'll be ledges, there are always ledges, the river drops in summer but the walls remember where the high water goes." He hesitated, then: "And if someone falls in the current — you don't go in after them. You hear me? The water wins. Always. You mark the spot and you keep moving, because dead men don't need avenging more than living ones need delivering."

"That's a grim speech," Brannigan said.

"It's a true one."

They went in.

* * *

The dark was total within fifty yards. Their lanterns turned the tunnel into a shifting cathedral — walls of glassy ice veined with trapped stones and ancient air bubbles, the river chattering over its bed, their breath smoking in the lantern light. The sound was enormous and intimate at once: water echoing off ice, multiplied into something like voices.

Sebastian walked third in line, manifest strapped tight, rope humming faintly between him and Isla ahead of him. At two hundred yards the ceiling began to drop. At three hundred they were bent double, wading shin-deep in water so cold it burned, and the walls pressed close enough that his shoulders brushed ice on both sides.

At four hundred yards — Okafor's edge of the map — the tunnel split.

Left: low, wide, the sound of faster water. Right: narrower, higher, a draft moving through it that smelled faintly of open sky.

Okafor crouched at the junction, lantern held low, and for a long moment didn't move. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "The draft's right. Moving air means another way through. But the right passage dips — I can see it dipping — and if it floods, there's no ledge."

"The left?" Isla asked.

"The left runs fast and low. We'd be swimming. In this water, swimming means minutes. Maybe less."

Sebastian felt the weight of the satchel against his chest and thought about his father walking into a magistrate's court with these papers and never walking out. "Right," he said. "We go up toward the air. And we go fast."

They went fast.

The dip was worse than it looked — the river dropped through a chute barely four feet high, and they passed the cargo hatch-raft through it piece by piece, on their backs in the current, faces inches from the ceiling ice, while the water tried to peel them off the gravel and take them somewhere none of them wanted to name. Isla went through last, and when she came out the far side, coughing, Sebastian hauled her up by the rope and held on a beat longer than he needed to.

She didn't pull away. "Still here," she said.

"Still here," he agreed.

Beyond the chute the tunnel climbed, and the ceiling rose, and then — impossibly, wonderfully — there was light. Thin threads of it, falling from cracks overhead, blue-white shafts that turned the darkness into something almost holy. They stood in the glow and looked up at a hundred feet of ice between them and the sky, and Brannigan started laughing, great whooping laughs that echoed down the tunnel, and for a minute nobody told him to stop.

Then the glacier groaned — a long, rising shriek somewhere above and behind — and dust sifted down through the light shafts, and everyone remembered where they were.

"Move," Okafor said.

* * *

They heard the fleet before they saw anything — a thin horn note rolling down the fjord, distorted by ice, unmistakably a signal. They had come out of the tunnel's final bend into a widening chamber where the roof had partially collapsed sometime in the last century, letting down a ramp of broken ice and rock, and beyond it, framed like a picture, was the headland pass: a notch in the rock, a slope of scree, and past it the flat gray gleam of open sea.

Free. One mile of bad ground away.

The horn sounded again, closer, and Okafor wormed forward to the chamber mouth and flattened himself against the rock, studying the water below.

"Boats," he reported. "Sloane's pinnaces, working the shoreline. Two of them, sweeping south along the headland. They haven't seen the tunnel exit — it's hidden behind that spur — but they're close, and they will sweep past it within the hour. If they round the point before we cross the scree, they'll pick us off on open ground with nowhere to run."

Sebastian joined him. Below and to the south, two longboats pulled steadily along the base of the cliffs, oars flashing, a figure in the bow of each scanning the shore. Beyond them, hull-down past the point, the masts of Sloane's ships stood against the horizon like a forest of gallows.

"We could wait for dark," Isla said. "Cross at night."

"Dark is fourteen hours off," Tam said. "And the glacier above this chamber has been shedding since dawn. I don't love our odds of standing under it for fourteen hours."

It was Brannigan who broke the silence, and he broke it strangely — calmly, the way men do when they've already decided something and are only waiting for the words to catch up.

"How much would it cost them," he said, "to believe the exit was somewhere else?"

Everyone looked at him.

"Sloane knows we're in the fjord. She knows the mouth is sealed. She's hunting the shoreline because she thinks we'll try to break out near the wreck, near the old landing stage on the north shore — that's where she'd have come out, if it were her." Brannigan's eyes were bright and terrible. "So we give her a show. Fire on the north shore. Smoke. Something that looks like people trying to launch a boat off the ice. Her pinnaces will run north, and her ships will shift their anchorage to cover it, and this whole southern shore goes unwatched for hours."

"And how do we light a fire on the north shore," Sebastian said slowly, "when we're here?"

Brannigan smiled at him, and it was the saddest smile Sebastian had ever seen.

"Somebody stays behind. With the signal lamp, the spare powder, and enough of Tam's slow-match to keep a fire fed for a few hours. Somebody climbs the north ridge, lights it up good and loud, and lets Sloane's boats chase the smoke." He shrugged, a cook's shrug, a man who'd spent his life making other people comfortable. "And then that somebody walks back along the shore, quiet as anything, and waits for the ice to clear. Weeks, maybe. Eating gulls. Talking to himself." His grin widened. "Which, granted, isn't much of a change."

"No," Isla said immediately.

"Yes," Brannigan said. "Listen to me, girl. I'm sixty-one years old. I've been cooked dry on three ships and buried one wife and buried one captain I loved better than most men love their brothers. I can't dive, I can't climb that scree with this knee, and I can't row worth a damn anymore. What I can do — better than any of you, better than anyone alive — is make a fire look like a fleet." He tapped his chest. "This is the one job on this crew that's mine. You don't get to steal it from me like I stole your father's compass, Isla Reyes. Some things you give."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above them the glacier groaned, patient and vast.

Sebastian thought about the salt flats, eleven days ago, and the rage that had carried him across them — rage with nowhere to go, aimed at a thief who'd turned out to be the truest person he'd met in years. He thought about a crew sworn off, gathered one by one at cost, and what it meant that every yes had cost something, and what it meant that this was the cost coming due.

"We draw lots," he said. "Fair is fair."

"No," Brannigan said again, gently. "Lots are for men with equal stakes. You've got a manifest and a father's ghost. Isla's got a mother's grave down in that wreck and a debt to the living. Tam and Okafor are the only ones who can get that raft and those ropes over the scree. Me?" He spread his hands. "I'm the cook. Cooks stay with the fire. It's practically scripture."

Isla crossed to him and took his face in both hands, the way you'd hold something breakable and beloved. "You come back to us," she said. "You hear me? Spring melt. The *Vagrant Mercy*. We will come back for you, and you will be there, fat and insufferable, or I will dive into that wreck myself and haunt you personally."

"Promises, promises," Brannigan said, and his voice cracked only a little.

* * *

They gave him everything they could spare: the signal lamp, the reserve powder, half the slow-match, a week of food, the whetstone, and — after a short argument Sebastian lost — the flask. Tam rigged the lamp with a shutter so it could flash code across the water; Brannigan knew the merchant signals better than any of them, having spent thirty years cooking for merchants. He'd light the north ridge, flash distress-and-launch sequences at Sloane's pinnaces, feed the fire until it looked like an armada preparing, and then vanish into the rocks like smoke himself.

At the chamber mouth, with the others already hauling the raft pieces up toward the notch in the headland, Brannigan gripped Sebastian's forearm, cook's hand, iron underneath.

"You watch her," he said, nodding toward Isla. "Both of them — the living one and the dead one. They've waited too long for somebody to finish this."

"You watch yourself," Sebastian said. "That's an order."

Brannigan grinned. "Last time I took orders from a Montrose, I ended up on this mad voyage." He squeezed once, hard, and let go. "Go. Make it worth the flour."

Sebastian climbed. At the top of the notch he turned back once. Brannigan stood alone in the blue light of the collapsing chamber, a small broad figure with a lamp in one hand and a coil of match-cord over his shoulder, already turning north toward the ridge and the fire that would buy their lives. He raised the flask in salute, drank, and walked into the shadows without looking back.

Then the headland swallowed them, the scree tore at their boots, and below and behind them — right on time, right on script — the first thread of black smoke rose off the northern ridge, and a horn sounded from the fleet, and the pinnaces turned away from the true exit and ran toward the fire.

Sebastian put his shoulder under the raft frame, beside Isla, beside Tam and Okafor, and they hauled for the sea.

Behind them the glacier calved again, and again, and the smoke stood straight up in the windless air like a pillar marking the place where a good man was keeping a promise — and ahead, past the point, the open water waited, and somewhere on it, Captain Verity Sloane was about to learn she'd been looking in the wrong direction.

One tide. That was all they needed now. One tide, and the world would hear what the dead prisoners of the *Leeward Rivergate* had spent twenty years trying to say.
