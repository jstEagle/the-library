## 19. Verdict at Sea

The fog burned off an hour after dawn, and there they were.

Three ships, spread across the open water like a hand closing. Sloane's flagship in the center — the *Iron Verdict*, black-hulled, her gunports painted to look like eyes — and two escorts fanning out to port and starboard, cutting off the northeast run toward the tribunal coast. Isla stood at the Vagrant Mercy's wheel with the manifest wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and did the arithmetic she'd been doing all night.

No wind worth the name. No shallows left to run through; Tam had spent their last charted trick two days ago. The back channel had spat them into open sea four hours before dawn, half-drowned, half-frozen, Brannigan's signal fire still burning on the far shore behind them like a promise they couldn't afford to keep. And now this.

"They've been waiting," Tam said from beside the engine housing, wiping grease from his hands with a rag that was filthier than his hands. "That formation isn't a search pattern. It's a receipt."

"She knew where we'd come out." Isla kept her voice level. Okafor was below with Sebastian, both of them wringing seawater out of everything they owned, and she was glad of it. Glad not to have an audience for the next few minutes. "Brannigan told her fleet where to wait and not where we were. That was the deal. That was what he traded himself for."

"Then she guessed the rest."

"Sloane doesn't guess." Isla watched the flagship come about, slow and deliberate, presenting her broadside the way a duelist presents his empty hand. "She remembers."

* * *

They'd run the channel by raft and rope for eleven hours. Isla could still feel the meltwater in her bones, a cold that lived under the skin now, that had opinions of its own. She'd carried the strongbox against her body the whole way, strapped down with the same harness Okafor had rigged for the dive, because if the ice took anything else it was not going to take that. Her mother had spent nine years aboard a prison ship smuggling paper out through the hands of dying women, and Isla had carried it through a collapsing fjord on a raft made of hatch covers and prayer. She was not going to lose it in the last hundred yards of flat gray sea.

Sebastian had helped her up the Vagrant Mercy's side with his own hands, and then he'd gone aft and been sick over the rail, quietly, the way men do when they think no one is counting. She'd counted. She always counted.

Now he came up on deck, hair frozen stiff at the temples, and looked at the three ships without surprise. That was the thing about Sebastian Montrose that had unsettled her since the salt flats: nothing surprised him for long. He metabolized catastrophe the way other men metabolized breakfast.

"How long?" he asked.

"Twenty minutes to gun range. Less if they push."

"And our options?"

"We have one," Isla said. "It's a bad one."

He turned to look at her. Up close, in the flat light, she could see how thin he'd gotten, how the last six tides had carved him down to something essential. His father's compass hung at his neck on its cord, and even now — even here — she caught herself glancing at it. Old habit. Old grief.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

They gathered in the cockpit: Sebastian, Tam, Okafor, dripping and hollow-eyed. No Brannigan. The space where Brannigan should have been was its own kind of passenger, taking up room, breathing cold air into every silence.

Isla unwrapped the oilcloth.

The manifest inside was smaller than the story of it. A sealed tube of lead sheeting, prison-stamped, the wax unbroken after twenty years. The prisoners of the Leeward Rivergate had rolled their testimony tight as a wick and hidden it in the one place no salvage crew would ever think to gut: the ship's own keel ballast, behind a plate only a diver who knew the wreck's original builder's marks could find. Her mother had known. Her mother had died knowing, and had gotten the knowledge out the only way she could — sewn into a daughter's coat pocket, disguised as a child's nonsense rhyme about a compass that pointed home.

"I'm going to give it to her," Isla said.

Tam laughed once, sharp, without humor. "Give the magistrate's crimes to the magistrate's captain. The woman hunting us across two hundred miles of open water. That's the plan."

"That's the offer," Isla corrected. "There's a difference."

Okafor leaned on the rail, looking at the ships. He'd nearly died twice in that fjord and moved like a man who had decided death would have to work harder than that. "Sloane took a parley flag once before," he said. "At Corvo Anchorage. I was on her crew then. A man came out under white cloth and she heard him out for an hour and let him row back." He paused. "Then she sank his ship. But she heard him out."

"That's comforting," Tam said.

"It's meant to be honest."

Sebastian hadn't spoken. He was watching Isla with that stillness of his, the tracker's stillness, the look of a man reading sign. When he finally spoke, his voice was low. "You knew her brother."

It wasn't a question. Isla closed her eyes for half a second. Of course he'd seen it. She'd told him about her mother in the dark hold, weeks ago now, and she'd told him about the rolls — the prisoner manifest Sloane's fleet had been chasing in one form or another for years, because everyone in the trade knew the Rivergate's papers were worth more than her hull. But she hadn't told him the name on the roll that mattered to Sloane.

"Elias Sloane," Isla said. "Deckhand on the Rivergate, transported for stealing grain during the famine year. He's on the list of the dead. Fever, winter of the third year. Verity was sixteen when the transport record reached her." Isla opened her eyes. "I know because I stole that record. Years ago, when I was working the archive houses. I read it because it was valuable, and then I sold it because it was valuable, and I never thought about the girl whose brother's name was in it until she started commanding ships."

"You never tried to find her?"

"To tell her what? That her brother starved in a hole while a magistrate built a courthouse with the profits?" Isla shook her head. "Grief like that doesn't want a messenger. It wants a target. I made sure I wasn't one."

"And now you're going to row out to her," Sebastian said, "and hand her the names of every person the magistrate buried. Including Elias Sloane. Including—" he stopped.

"Including your father," Isla finished. "Yes."

The wind shifted. Somewhere behind them, gulls argued over whatever the fjord had given up. Tam looked between the two of them like a man watching a fuse burn toward his feet.

"She'll take the manifest," Tam said. "And then she'll take us, because that's what captains do with leverage and witnesses, and we'll be two more names on somebody's roll."

"Maybe," Isla said.

"That's your argument? *Maybe?*"

"My argument," Isla said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, harder than she'd allowed herself in weeks, "is that I have spent my whole life selling things that belonged to grieving people because grief pays well. I stole a dead woman's compass. I sold a dead boy's record. I ran crews until they scattered, and I lied to every one of them, and the last time I told myself a job was just a job, people drowned." She held up the lead tube. "This is the first thing I've ever carried that I don't intend to sell. So yes, Tam. *Maybe.* Maybe mercy is a bad bet. I'm placing it anyway."

Nobody spoke. The flagship fired a line gun — not a shot, a line, the harpoon-thump of a message rocket arcing over the water and bursting white above them. Standard signal. *Heave to or be hove to.*

Okafor straightened up. "For what it's worth," he said, "the woman I served under at Corvo cried the night we got the transport records aboard. Thought nobody saw. I saw." He shrugged. "Row out. I'll cover the boat."

"With what?" Tam demanded. "We have four swivel guns and a cook's temper, and the cook's marooned on an ice shelf."

"With being visible," Okafor said. "Ships don't sink boats when their whole crew is watching. Even magistrates' captains care who's telling the story after."

Sebastian put his hand on the rail, next to the compass, and looked at Isla. "You'll need someone in the stern sheets they'll respect," he said. "And someone they'll shoot first if it goes wrong. I can be both."

"No," Isla said.

"Yes. You carry the manifest. I carry the debt." He almost smiled. "It's the only thing I've ever owned outright. Let me spend it."

* * *

The gig's oars creaked in the thole pins, and the sea did what open sea does, which is nothing, endlessly. Isla sat in the bow with the tube across her knees. Sebastian sat astern, oars shipped, hands visible, the compass hanging openly at his throat where any glass trained on them would find it. A flag of pale cloth — Tam's last clean shirt, which Tam had surrendered with the expression of a man donating a kidney — flew from the boat's staff and declined, in the windless air, to fly at all. It just hung there, a white rag on a stick. Honest enough.

The *Iron Verdict* grew as they pulled toward her, and she was worse up close than she'd been at distance: scarred black oak, gun muzzles run out but blanketed, marines along the rail in the magistrate's slate-gray coats. And at the break of the quarterdeck, alone, a woman in a plain dark uniform with no braid on it at all, as if rank were something she carried rather than wore.

Captain Verity Sloane watched them come the whole way. She didn't call out. She didn't send a boat to meet them. She waited with the patience of a woman who had already won and wanted to see how they'd behave about it.

The gig bumped her hull. Lines came down. They climbed.

The quarterdeck smelled of tar and cold iron. Sloane was older than Isla remembered from the archive portrait — of course she was; the portrait had been ten years old — with gray coming in at her temples like frost finding a window's edge. Her eyes went to Isla first, and Isla watched recognition arrive there, cold and total, the way water finds a seam.

"Isla Reyes," Sloane said. Her voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. "You have some nerve wearing that face on my deck."

"You know me."

"I paid good money for your description in three ports." Sloane's gaze flicked to Sebastian, to the compass, and something moved behind her eyes — recalculation, fast and thorough. "Montrose's son. Your father refused Crane's contract for this same wreck, you know. I dined with him the week before he sailed. He told me the Rivergate should stay shut until the law was strong enough to open it." Her jaw tightened. "I told him the law was the reason it needed opening. We disagreed pleasantly. Then he went out and died trying to prove us both wrong."

Sebastian's breath caught — Isla heard it, small and involuntary, a man taking a hit he'd braced for all his life and finding it landed anyway.

"You're here for Crane's money," Sloane said. "Or Crane's pardon. Whichever he promised you. I'm prepared to double it, and I don't even have to threaten you, though I retain the option." She extended one gloved hand, palm up. "The manifest. Now. My marine officers will verify the seal, and then we'll discuss terms like civilized people, and if you're very lucky I'll forget the Corvo contracts you defaulted on, Reyes, and the two of my boats you burned at Halden Reach."

The deck was very quiet. Somewhere below, a ship's bell marked the half hour. Isla found that her heart was not pounding. That surprised her. All this way, all these tides, and at the end of it she felt strangely calm, the way she'd felt in the flooded hold with her mother's cell door swinging open — past fear, into something cleaner.

"You're right about me," Isla said. "Everything you've heard is true or worse. I stole for pay. I burned boats. I sold a transport record once that had your brother's name in it, and I knew whose it was, and I sold it anyway, and I have owed you that apology for fifteen years without ever once planning to deliver it."

Sloane went very still.

"So I'm not going to sell you the manifest," Isla said. "I'm going to give it to you. Free. No terms, no conditions, no price. Because it was never mine to sell. It belongs to a hundred and forty dead people who smuggled it out of that hull with their fingernails, and the only thing they'd want from it is exactly what you'd want." She stepped forward and laid the lead tube in Sloane's gloved palm. "Elias Sloane. Transported for grain theft, famine year. Died of fever, third winter. He's on the roll. So are a hundred and thirty-nine others, with the magistrate's signature on every order that killed them, in his own hand, sealed in his own wax, which he used because he never believed anyone would ever get it out of the ice."

The silence stretched so long that the bell rang again.

Sloane looked down at the tube. She didn't open it. Her thumb moved once across the prison stamp, slowly, the way a person touches a grave marker.

"He wrote letters," she said, and her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the command stripped off it like paint off a hull. "Every month for two years they came. Then they stopped, and the stoppage letter said *fever*, and I was sixteen, and I believed it, because believing it was easier than what the alternative required." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I joined the magistrate's coastal service because it was the closest thing to him I could reach. I hunted smugglers for eleven years in his name. Do you understand what you're handing me, Reyes? You're not handing me evidence. You're handing me eleven years of my life and asking me to burn it."

"Yes," Isla said.

"And if I refuse? If I throw you all in irons and deliver this to the magistrate myself and let him bury it deeper?"

"Then you'll be the last lock on his door," Isla said. "And I'll spend the rest of my life picking it. But I don't think you will. I think you've been sailing toward this document your whole career without knowing it. I think that's why Crane hired a fleet instead of divers — because he needed someone whose grief would do the work greed couldn't. And I think he counted on you never reading what you recovered." She paused. "He was wrong about you. He's been wrong about everyone on this water. It's the only consistent thing about him."

Sloane turned away from them, to the rail, to the gray water, and stood there for the length of ten slow breaths. Sebastian's hand found Isla's shoulder — not gripping, just resting, an anchor that weighed nothing and held everything.

When Sloane turned back, her face was a captain's face again, but rebuilt around new keel timbers.

"Mr. Montrose," she said. "Your father and I disagreed about method, never about the crime. I'd be honored to have his son stand witness at the tribunal." She raised her voice, and the quarterdeck snapped to attention around her. "Marines! Strike the boarding parties. Signal the escorts: course reversal, all ships. We make for the tribunal coast under parley colors, and these—" she nodded at the Vagrant Mercy, riding her anchor a cable's length off, "—are under my protection until the verdict is read. Anyone who touches that ketch answers to me personally."

She looked at Isla one last time. Whatever was in her face, it wasn't forgiveness — Isla hadn't earned that, and didn't expect it. It was something rarer. Recognition. One carrier of dead men's papers acknowledging another.

"The manifest stays in my cabin safe tonight," Sloane said. "Tomorrow we sail together. And Reyes —" she hesitated, and for a moment she was sixteen again, holding a stoppage letter, "— thank you is insufficient. But it's what I have."

"It's more than I expected," Isla said. "It's more than I deserve."

"Don't tell my crew that. They think I'm infallible." Sloane turned to her signals officer. "Run up the parley. And get me a chart of the tribunal coast — I want to know exactly what forces the magistrate keeps at the courthouse before we're in sight of it."

The flags went up. Across the water, the escort ships answered, wheels going over, sails filling as the fleet came about — the whole armed weight of the magistrate's salvage operation turning, in one long groaning minute, toward the man who'd bought it.

Isla stood at the rail and watched it happen and felt the strangest sensation, like a debt being cancelled that no ledger had ever recorded.

Sebastian leaned close. "There's a problem," he murmured.

Of course there was. There was always a problem. "Tell me."

"Sloane's chart table." He nodded, subtle, toward the cabin hatch. "Crane's route charts are aboard this ship. He sold them to the fleet at Halden Reach — Sloane admitted as much at the parley. Which means Crane's coded ledger pages are probably filed right alongside them." His jaw tightened. "And if Sloane's clerks decode those pages the way Tam did, they're going to learn that Crane fabricated my debt — and that Crane also kept a second set of books on *them*. Payments. Bribes. Names."

Isla stared at him. "You're saying the proof that damns Crane is sitting in the enemy's cabin, and we sail for the tribunal tomorrow morning with the man who forged your life's ruin as our—"

"Witness," Sebastian said grimly. "Against the magistrate. While carrying the evidence that ruins him too." He looked out at the turning fleet, at the gray horizon beyond it, at the courthouse that waited somewhere past all that water. "One trial. Two defendants. And only one of them knows yet."

Behind them, the *Iron Verdict*'s bell began to ring for the tide.
