## 20. The Magistrate's Court

The harbor town of Saltmere smelled of fish and tar and old money, and the money was the part that made my skin crawl.

We came in on the morning tide with the manifest sewn into a canvas satchel strapped across Isla's chest, because she'd earned the carrying of it, and I'd earned the watching of it. The Vagrant Mercy sat low in the water behind us, her hull scarred from the fjord, her rails still bearing the salt-burn of the calving glacier. Behind us — far behind us, beyond the headlands we'd threaded through the back channel — Sloane's fleet lay at anchor under a flag of truce, held there by a promise and by the word of a woman who had every reason not to give it.

Sloane had given it anyway. That still sat strange in my chest, like a bone set slightly wrong.

"Six blocks," Tam said, squinting up the hill from the quay. "Tribunal's six blocks up. You want me to say it or you want to pretend you haven't noticed?"

"Say what?" Brannigan asked, though his eyes had already found them.

The retainers. They stood along the seawall in pairs, gray-liveried, armed with short truncheons and longer faces, and every one of them was pretending to be furniture while watching us the way a cat watches a door. Magistrate Corven's men. I counted nine before we reached the first corner and stopped counting at twenty-two.

"They know," Okafor said quietly. He walked with the rolling gait of a man whose knees had been frozen twice in one month, and he carried himself like someone who had already decided how he would die if it came to it, which was either grim or comforting depending on the hour. "Word travels faster than sail when the word is worth enough."

"Then they've had time to think about what happens if they touch us," Isla said.

"And what happens?" Tam asked.

Isla smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. It was the smile she wore diving into black water. "They become evidence."

We went up the hill anyway.

* * *

Saltmere's courthouse had been built by men who wanted justice to look expensive. White stone gone cream with salt air, columns out front wide as mast steps, a staircase broad enough for a funeral procession. The tribunal sat inside three days out of seven, hearing salvage disputes and customs appeals and the small grinding grievances of a coast town, and today — by luck or by Sloane's doing, I never learned which — it was sitting.

We were halfway across the square when the retainers moved.

Not all of them. Eight of them, peeling off the seawall in loose formation, closing the gap between us and the steps without hurrying, because men who hurry look afraid and these men had been told they wouldn't need to be. Their sergeant was a thick-necked man with a magistrate's warrant folded in his breast pocket, and he planted himself at the base of the stairs like a fence post driven deep.

"That's far enough," he said. "Tribunal's closed to unregistered parties."

"It's closed to everyone who isn't bought," Isla said. "We're registering."

"You're carrying stolen documents."

"We're carrying *recovered* documents," I said, "from a wrecked vessel, under lawful salvage claim, witnessed by Captain Verity Sloane of the salvage fleet *Corven himself commissioned*. Your magistrate paid for that fleet, sergeant. Ask him why he's so eager to keep its findings out of this building."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face. Doubt, maybe, or just the arithmetic of a man realizing his employer hadn't told him the whole shape of the job. Behind him, the other retainers had spread into a line across the stairs, and above them the courthouse doors stood open, and through those doors I could see clerks pausing over their ledgers, watching.

The square was filling too. That was the thing about Saltmere — half the town had kin who'd gone through the Rivergate system. Prisoners shipped north on that floating hell and never listed, never sentenced publicly, never returned. For years the families had been told their people drowned, deserted, ran off. For years the manifests had been sealed by order of the magistrate's office.

And now eight of us stood in the square holding the reason why.

"Last chance," the sergeant said. "Walk away. There's no shame in it."

"There's a great deal of shame in it," Isla said. "Just none of ours."

He reached for his truncheon.

* * *

I want to say I calculated it — weighed odds, counted blades, chose the ground. I didn't. What happened next happened the way weather happens.

Because the crowd moved.

It started as a murmur at the edges of the square, the way a tide starts as a wetting of the sand. A woman in a fishmonger's apron stepped out of the market row and planted herself between two retainers, arms crossed, and I recognized the set of her jaw because I'd seen it on Isla's face a hundred times since the fjord. Then a dockhand. Then an old man with a crutch. Then a dozen more, then fifty, flowing down the side streets with the particular quiet fury of people who have spent years being lied to politely.

"Those are Corven's men," someone shouted. "Those are the ones who took my brother!"

"The rolls are sealed—"

"She has the rolls! She has the *rolls!*"

The sergeant looked at his line of retainers, and his line of retainers looked at the crowd, and I watched the whole balance of the morning tip in the space of three heartbeats. Twenty-two armed men against eight tired sailors was one fight. Twenty-two armed men against a town was something else entirely — something no warrant covered.

"Clear the square," the sergeant said, but his voice had lost its footing, and everyone heard it.

A retainer grabbed the fishmonger woman's arm. She spat in his face. He raised his hand—

—and Okafor was there. I don't know how a man with frozen knees crossed thirty feet that fast, but he caught the retainer's wrist mid-swing and held it, just held it, and said something too low for me to hear. Whatever it was, the man let go. Maybe it was the size of him. Maybe it was the look in his eyes, the one of a man who had swum out of a flooded prison corridor and wasn't impressed by anything on dry land.

"No blood," Isla said, loud enough for the square. She climbed two steps so she stood above the sergeant, above the crowd, and the strangest thing happened: the noise began to die, because people wanted to hear her. "You hear me? All of you. No blood on these steps. We didn't carry that manifest out of a glacier so it could drown in a riot. We carried it here to be *read*."

She pulled the satchel around, undid the stitching with her teeth, and held the sealed roll high over her head — the Rivergate's manifest, wax seals intact, the names of four hundred prisoners and eleven years of Corven's crimes in a clerk's careful hand.

"This is a court document!" she called. "So let's take it to court!"

The cheer that went up rattled the courthouse windows. And the sergeant — to his lasting credit, I'd say later, when I could say it without wanting to hit him — looked at the crowd, looked at the doors, and stepped aside.

"Register your claim," he said flatly. "If the tribunal throws you out, that's the law's business. Not mine."

* * *

Inside, the courtroom was cool and dim and smelled of lamp oil and paper. Three justices sat the bench: an old woman with a surveyor's squint, a fat man who looked like he'd rather be fishing, and in the center a lean, gray-faced justice named Halloway whom I knew by reputation only — the one judge on this coast Corven had never managed to buy, mostly, people said, because Corven had never believed he needed to.

The clerk read our salvage claim aloud. Tam had drafted it at three in the morning somewhere south of the back channel, and it was beautiful — precise, tedious, bulletproof. Vessel *Leeward Rivergate*, lost with all hands, wreck located in glacial fjord, cargo recovered under distress conditions, witnesses: the crew of the ketch *Vagrant Mercy*, Captain Verity Sloane commanding the chartered salvage fleet, and — appended in Sloane's own hand, sworn before her officers — a statement that made the fat justice sit up straighter.

Corven's advocate arrived twenty minutes into the reading, red-faced and breathless, with two junior clerks and a stack of motions he began shuffling like a card sharp. He objected to everything. Our standing. Our custody of the documents. The validity of a witness under Sloane's own investigation. The seaworthiness of our vessel, which had nothing to do with anything and told us exactly how thin he was scraping.

Halloway let him run out of air, the way good judges do.

"Advocate," he said finally, "the tribunal will hear the contents of the recovered manifest now. Unsealed. On the record."

"Your honor, the seals—"

"Were affixed by the office of the magistrate," Halloway said, "who is named in these proceedings. Break them."

Isla broke them. Her hands were steady. Mine weren't, and I was only watching.

The clerk read. And the room changed.

Four hundred and twelve names. Sentences never entered into any public record. Transfers signed by Corven's own seal — prisoners sold north as labor, deaths recorded as escapes, fees collected from families for pardons that were never granted. And then the entry that stopped even the fat justice's pen:

*Sebastian Montrose, ranger, deceased — intercepted attempting delivery of documents to coastal tribunal, [date]. Effects: one brass compass, retained.*

My father's name. In my father's handwriting, in effect — the manifest he'd died trying to deliver, copied into the very record that had buried him. Eleven years I'd carried a debt I thought was mine. It had never been mine. It had been his, unfinished, and the compass in my pocket had been trying to finish it the whole time.

Isla's hand found my arm under the table. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Her mother's name was on that same page, forty lines up.

The advocate was on his feet, voice cracking. "Your honors, these documents are forgeries! Fabricated aboard a private vessel by persons with obvious grudges—"

"Captain Sloane's sworn statement attests to their recovery location," Halloway said. "Does the advocate wish to call Captain Sloane?"

A silence. Because calling Sloane meant putting her under oath in front of the whole coast, and everyone in that room knew what she'd found in the fjord, and what her brother's name was doing on page nine of the manifest.

"I — the tribunal should consider—"

"The tribunal," Halloway said, "considers. Warrant for the arrest of Magistrate Edran Corven. Seal the manifest as primary evidence. And advocate — sit down before you dig the hole deeper than the one we just pulled these people out of."

* * *

We came out onto the steps into sunlight and a crowd that had doubled, tripled, spilled down the hill toward the harbor. Someone had gotten hold of the news faster than the clerk could file it — Tam swore later it was the fishmonger woman, who turned out to have a sister on the prisoner rolls — and the sound that met us wasn't a cheer exactly. It was bigger than a cheer. It was the sound of a weight coming off four hundred families at once, and it pressed against my ears like deep water.

Okafor laughed, actually laughed, a great booming thing. Brannigan cried openly and claimed it was the wind. Tam stood with his arms crossed pretending to be unmoved while grinning like a boy.

Isla stood beside me at the top of the steps, looking out at all of it, and her face did something complicated — grief and relief tangled together, the expression of a woman setting down something she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it had weight.

"He'll fight it," I said. "Corven. He's got retainers, ships, friends inland."

"He's got nothing," Isla said. "Look at them." She nodded at the crowd, at the gray-liveried men along the seawall who had quietly removed their livery and were drifting away like men leaving a losing table. "Loyalty's just a wage, Sebastian. And nobody's paying anymore."

Down at the quay, a boat was pulling away from Sloane's flagship, oars flashing, coming in fast with a signal pennant flying — and not a friendly one. Red over black. The fleet's code for urgent, unofficial, and don't ask questions on open water.

Tam saw it the same moment I did. "That's Sloane's runner."

"What's wrong?" Brannigan asked. "We won. What could possibly be wrong now?"

The boat grounded on the shingle and a fleet officer came up the hill at a jog, saluted Sloane's colors on his sleeve like an apology, and handed me a sealed note. I cracked it open.

Three lines, in a sharp, furious hand I was beginning to know well:

*Corven's taken ship south with two retainers and a strongbox. Crane's with him — alive, thawed, and talking. If you want the man who forged your debt, meet me at the southern mole within the hour. We sail on the evening tide.*

Isla read it over my shoulder. Then she looked at me, and there it was again — that diver's smile, the one that goes down into the dark on purpose.

"Well," she said. "What comes next?"
