## 21. Debts Cancelled

The tribunal's holding cell smelled of salt and old stone, and Aldous Crane sat in it like a man who had already rehearsed his own ruin.

Isla stood on the wrong side of the bars with her arms crossed, and she made herself look at him. Really look. The ice shelf had taken three fingers from his left hand — the surgeon in the tribunal town had taken two more — and whatever the cold had left of him, the magistrate's arrest had finished. He was smaller than she remembered from the Rivergate Inn, smaller than the man who had bought a debt and a compass and a crew with a smile like a knife laid flat.

"You came," Crane said. His voice had a rasp in it now, wind-scoured. "I half expected you'd let me freeze for the gulls."

"The gulls would have complained." She leaned against the wall. "Sebastian wanted to leave you. Okafor voted to. Brannigan—" she stopped, because Brannigan wasn't there to vote anymore, and the pause cost them both something. "Brannigan would have gone back for you anyway."

Crane looked at his bandaged hand as if it belonged to a stranger. "Your cook. The one who stayed behind to pull Sloane's fleet off your trail." He shook his head slowly. "I sabotaged that ship. I sold your route twice over. And you still sent men back up the fjord for me."

"Okafor went back for you," Isla said. "Not us. There's a difference, and he'll tell you about it at length when he's ready."

Silence filled the cell the way tide fills a channel — patient, total. Somewhere above them, the tribunal bells were ringing the hour, and beneath the ringing she could hear the crowd: still gathered on the courthouse steps three days after the magistrate's arrest, families of the Rivergate dead who had come to see a name they'd only ever whispered spoken aloud in daylight.

"Why did you come?" Crane asked finally. "To gloat? I would have."

"No." She studied him. "I came because my mother spent eleven years in a hold full of people nobody would listen to, and the only man who tried to get their story out died on the water for it. I've had enough of letting men drown in their own consequences. It doesn't bring anyone back. It just makes more silence."

Crane laughed — a dry, cracked sound with no pleasure in it. "You sound like Elias Montrose."

"I never met him."

"No. You wouldn't have. He came to me once, years ago, before the Rivergate job. Asked me to help him reach the wreck. I told him the fjord couldn't be cracked, that the fleet owned every approach, that he'd die out there and his boy would inherit nothing but debts." Crane's good hand closed around the bar. "He said, 'Then I'll owe the right people.' I've thought about that sentence every day since. Every single day, Miss Reyes. And then I manufactured a debt against his son to drag him out to do what his father refused to do with me."

"I know," Isla said. "We found the ledger."

Something moved through Crane's face — not surprise. Relief, maybe, or its ugly cousin. "Then you know everything, and you're still standing there talking to me instead of spitting through the bars. You're either a better person than me or a stranger one."

"Both days," Isla said, and pushed off the wall. "The tribunal convenes your hearing next week. Sloane has given testimony. So have we. Cooperate, and maybe they let you keep the hand you have left."

She was almost to the door when he spoke again.

"The salvage rights," Crane said. "They'll award them. Wreck of record, first recovery, witness testimony — it all points to your crew. You could sell those rights for more money than any of you have seen in a lifetime. The Rivergate's copper alone—"

"We know what it's worth," Isla said. "That's why we're not selling it."

* * *

They divided the salvage rights in the back room of the Leeward Rivergate Inn — where else — around the same scarred table where Crane had first set his offer down in front of Sebastian like a trap dressed as a gift. Tam had claimed the seat nearest the window, where he could watch the harbor. Okafor sat with his hands wrapped around a mug of Brannigan's terrible tea recipe, which Tam had attempted from memory and gotten wrong in a way that somehow made it worse. Isla took the chair facing the door, because old habits kept you alive, and Sebastian stood at the head of the table with a document in front of him and a candle burning low beside it.

The forged debt paper.

Isla had seen it a dozen times now — in Crane's ledger, in the tribunal evidence chest, and finally, this morning, returned to Sebastian by the tribunal clerk with an apologetic nod, as though handing a man the instrument of his own enslavement were a courtesy. It was a fine piece of work. Crane had always been thorough. The seal was right, the signatures were right, the interest schedule was right. Eleven years of Sebastian Montrose's life, bound and notarized, and every line of it invented in a rented office while his father's body was still warm somewhere in the sea.

"Read it aloud," Tam said quietly. "All of it. Then burn it. That's how you kill a thing like this properly."

Sebastian read it. His voice stayed level through the principal, through the compounding clauses, through the paragraph that transferred the debt to "heirs and assigns in perpetuity" — the clause that meant Isla's children would have inherited it too, if he'd ever had any, if things had gone differently, if a thief hadn't stolen a compass that pointed somewhere it shouldn't. When he reached the signature — Crane's, elegant and confident, dated four days after Elias Montrose drowned — his voice did not shake, but his hand did, just once, and Isla watched him press it flat against the table until it stopped.

He held the corner to the candle.

The paper caught slowly, the way old documents do, flame crawling up the margin past the seal, past the signatures, past the clause about perpetuity. Nobody spoke. Okafor bowed his head. Tam watched the fire with the expression of an engineer watching a load come off a stressed joint. The ink curled black, the words that had owned a man for eleven years turned to ash and smoke, and Sebastian held it until the flame touched his fingers, then dropped the last scrap into the dish and ground it out under his palm.

"Well," he said. His voice was rough. "That's that."

"That's that," Isla agreed.

He looked up at her across the table, and there was ash on his knuckles and something new in his face — not lightness exactly, but space, the way a cabin feels bigger once you've thrown the ballast overboard. "What comes next?"

And every eye at the table turned to her, because apparently that question had been addressed to her, and apparently everyone had known it before she had.

* * *

The salvage rights took an evening and an argument, which by the Vagrant Mercy's standards was remarkably efficient.

The tribunal's award was generous — the Rivergate's hull, fittings, and cargo rights, minus the manifest itself, which was seized as evidence and would eventually go to the families. On paper, the crew of the Vagrant Mercy had performed the recovery, so the rights belonged to the crew. But crews, Isla had learned long ago, are not paper.

"Equal shares," Tam said immediately. "Five ways."

"Four ways," Okafor corrected, and the room went quiet, because everyone understood he meant Brannigan's share, and no one wanted to be the one who said it. "His family gets a share. Full share. He earned it on the ice."

"Full share," Sebastian agreed at once.

"His sister's in Port Calloway," Isla said. "Maren. I'll take it to her myself. She shouldn't hear it from a ledger."

Tam nodded and scratched figures into the table with a pencil stub. "Five shares. Now — the ketch. Vagrant Mercy needs a refit that'll eat half the award by itself. New planking below the waterline, rigging throughout, and I want an engine that isn't held together with prayer and rivets."

"Get the engine," Sebastian said. "Get two engines."

"One good engine," Tam said, "and a spare we can actually lift. And before anyone asks — yes, I'm staying." He said it fast, looking at the window, not at any of them. "In case anyone was going to ask."

Nobody had been going to ask. Everybody had been going to ask.

"Okafor?" Isla said.

The diver turned his mug slowly on the table. "I told myself in the fjord I was done after this one. Told myself again when the corridor collapsed on me. Told myself again when I went back up the ice for Crane." He looked around the table, and his deep voice was steady. "I'm a liar. Wherever this ship goes next, I'm on it. But I want it written somewhere — actual ink, actual paper — that nobody dives alone. Not for cargo, not for speed, not for anything. We lost Brannigan to speed. Never again."

"Written in the articles," Sebastian said. "First article. Before shares, before discipline. No one dives alone."

"And the shares," Okafor pressed. "Equal?"

"Equal," Isla said. "But I don't want mine in coin."

Everyone looked at her.

"The Rivergate's fittings are worth a fortune, and the copper holds will take months to strip. Fine. Strip them. Sell them. Pay for the refit, pay Maren Brannigan, put the rest in the common purse. But my mother's cell block — the prisoner decks, the wardroom where they hid the strongbox, the whole section where they lived for eleven years — I want that preserved. Sealed, documented, left as it lies. And I want the tribunal to fund a memorial marker on the cliff above the fjord. All nine hundred names from the manifest. Carved in stone where the sea can't argue with it."

The table was silent for a long moment.

"They'll fight you on it," Tam said. "Preservation clauses cut into resale value. The tribunal's merchants will scream."

"Let them scream," Sebastian said. "It goes in the articles. Second article, right behind Okafor's."

Isla felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since she was nineteen years old. "Third article," she said. "The compass stays with the ship. Not with any one of us. With the ship."

* * *

They walked down to the harbor at dusk, all four of them, because the Vagrant Mercy was tied at the public quay and none of them could stay away from her for a whole day yet. She looked battered and beautiful in the last light — scorched rail patched with pale new wood, the jury-rigged forestay replaced with proper wire, Tam's new spare engine crated on the dock beside her like a promise.

Sloane was waiting at the gangway.

Isla stopped. Old reflexes fired — the angle of escape, the weight distribution, the lie already forming — and then she let all of it go and simply walked up to the woman who had hunted them across six tides and half a frozen sea.

"Captain Sloane."

"Reyes." Sloane looked older in civilian clothes, or maybe just honest. Her fleet lay at anchor out in the roads, five ships riding quiet, their salvage gear struck down and covered. "I signed my deposition this morning. The magistrate's advocates didn't enjoy it." A thin smile. "My brother's name is on page forty-one of the manifest. Deck prisoner, third year of the voyage. Died of fever in a hold the magistrate certified as 'humane conditions' in his own hand." She looked out at the water. "Twenty-two years I worked for that man's courts. Twenty-two years, and a thief and a ranger and a broken ketch brought him down in a season."

"Not alone," Isla said. "Your fleet turned around. Your testimony counts."

"My fleet turned around," Sloane agreed, "because a cook lit signal fires in a pattern only fleet officers would recognize, telling us the true exit was blocked and the fugitives were running south. Which was a lie. Which saved your lives." She turned back, and her eyes were wet and furious and calm all at once. "I've been offered the tribunal's commission to oversee the Rivergate memorial site. Preservation oversight. Someone competent, someone who knew the waters." A pause. "I said yes."

"Good," Isla said, and meant it, and Sloane seemed briefly unmoored by the absence of a fight.

"Heard you're keeping the wreck rights," Sloane said, recovering. "And heard you're not stripping the prisoner decks. That's — " she searched for the word, failed to find a hard one, and settled for a true one — "right."

"It's in the articles."

"Articles." Sloane almost smiled. "Elias Montrose sailed under articles. Did you know that? Everyone thought he was a lone wolf. He had a crew sworn to him deeper than most marriages." She glanced past Isla to where Sebastian stood watching from the foot of the gangway, and something passed between the two of them — grief, acknowledgment, the particular respect of people who had both lost him. "Take care of his son's ship, Reyes. He'd have liked the company it keeps."

She walked away up the quay, straight-backed, toward her fleet, and Isla watched her go and thought about how many kinds of surrender there were, and how the best ones looked like walking away upright.

* * *

That night, the inn's common room was fuller than it had any right to be. Half the town had heard about the tribunal by now; the other half had heard about the salvage award, which traveled faster. Families of the Rivergate dead kept coming to the table where the crew sat — not to ask for anything, mostly, just to look at them, to touch the table, to say a name and have it heard. One old woman pressed a knitted cap into Okafor's hands and refused to explain. A boy of maybe ten asked Sebastian whether the prison ship was haunted, and Sebastian, solemn as a judge, said, "Only by the truth," and the boy nodded like this was the correct and expected answer.

Isla slipped out the side door when the room got loud.

The harbor was dark and still, the tide halfway up, the masts knocking gently against the sky. She stood on the quay and breathed the cold salt air and let herself feel all of it at once — her mother's name carved soon in stone above a fjord, a debt burned to ash, a crew that had voted to stay, a woman she'd betrayed standing watch over her mother's grave-site. Grief and relief arrived together, the way they do, indistinguishable in the dark.

Footsteps behind her. She didn't turn. She knew the weight of them now, the way you know a rope by its lay.

"She'd have liked tonight," Sebastian said, coming to stand beside her at the quay's edge. "My father. All those names being said out loud."

"He'd have hated the attention," Isla said.

"He'd have hated it terribly," Sebastian agreed, "and then stayed for every minute of it."

Below them, the Vagrant Mercy rocked at her lines, lamplit through the stern port, Tam's silhouette moving inside as he inventoried something for the fourth time. The compass sat in the chart drawer below, needle resting, pointing nowhere in particular because nowhere needed pointing at tonight.

"So," Sebastian said.

"So," Isla said.

He turned the ash-stained hand over, looked at it as if checking whether the burning had left a mark. "Eleven years I carried that debt. It organized my whole life. Where I went, what jobs I took, how far I could run. This morning I woke up and for the first time since I was sixteen, nobody owned anything of mine." He looked at her. "I don't entirely know what to do with that. The weight's gone and I keep reaching for it."

"I know the feeling," Isla said. "I stole a compass to fill a hole that size. Didn't work, for the record."

"What did work?"

She considered the question honestly, because he'd earned an honest answer. "People who stayed," she said finally. "That's the whole secret. Everything else is rigging."

Sebastian nodded slowly, looking out at the dark water. Then: "There's a charter. Came in with the tribunal post this morning — I haven't shown anyone yet. Northern waters. A wreck off the Skerren Islands, sixty years down, carrying records nobody's ever recovered. Court archives, sealed manifests, the whole rotten history of a colonial governor. The families petitioned for ten years and got refused ten times." He paused. "They heard what happened here. They wrote to the crew of the Vagrant Mercy."

Isla felt it land in her chest — not excitement exactly, something older and quieter than that. Purpose with the smell of salt on it.

"Sixty years down," she said. "Cold water. Unsurveyed approaches, probably. Fleet interests in the way, certainly."

"Almost certainly."

"An impossible job for an impossible price."

"For the truth," Sebastian said. "Which is the only price we seem to deal in."

Isla looked at the ketch rocking in the dark, at the lamplight in her ports, at the compass sleeping in her chart drawer — her mother's compass, pointing wherever they chose to point it now. She thought about articles, and shares, and a stone with nine hundred names on a cliff above a frozen fjord. She thought about what her mother would say, and decided she already knew, because she'd raised a daughter who stole ships instead of crying about it.

"When do we sail?" Isla said.

Sebastian smiled — the first easy one she'd seen on him, unweighted by anything owed to anyone. "Tide turns at dawn."

"Then we'd better wake the crew," Isla said, and turned toward the inn, toward the noise and the lamplight and the people who stayed, already reaching for the door.
