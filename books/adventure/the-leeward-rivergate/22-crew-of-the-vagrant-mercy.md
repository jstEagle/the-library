## 22. Crew of the Vagrant Mercy

The Vagrant Mercy sat on the slipway at Rivergate like a patient that had finally agreed to be healed.

Sebastian walked her length every morning before the yard crews arrived, coffee in hand, boots ringing on the planking. It had become a ritual he didn't examine too closely — the way a man checks a wound not because it hurts but because it's his. Three weeks of work had transformed her. Tam had pulled the engine apart down to the block and rebuilt it with parts scavenged from two wrecks and one very apologetic harbormaster. New strakes amidships where the ice had chewed her. A proper windlass instead of the hand-cranked relic Okafor swore had nearly cost him an arm on the third tide. The hull below the waterline was black with fresh pitch, and when the morning sun hit it just right, she gleamed like something newly born rather than something dragged out of a fjord by her fingernails.

He stopped at the bow, where the shipwright's boy was carving the nameplate fresh. The old letters had been worn to ghosts.

*Vagrant Mercy.*

His father had named her, back when she'd been a survey ketch and not a salvage runner. Sebastian had been eleven. He remembered asking what it meant and his father laughing, saying something about how mercy was always vagrant — it never stayed where you put it, you had to go chasing after it.

He hadn't understood then. He thought maybe he did now.

"You're blocking my light, ranger."

Isla came up the slipway ladder with a coil of new line over one shoulder and sawdust in her hair, which she'd stopped trying to keep out of about a week ago. She wore the compass on a cord around her neck these days. He'd noticed that. He noticed most things about Isla Reyes and had given up pretending otherwise.

"Your light," he said. "This is my ship."

"Our ship." She dropped the coil at his feet. "You signed the articles same as the rest of us. Article one: shared hull, shared risk, shared blame when Tam's engine explodes."

"The engine will not explode," said Tam, appearing from the engine hatch with grease to his elbows and an expression of profound offense. "I have rebuilt this engine better than the factory that birthed it. If anything explodes, it will be beautiful."

"Comforting," said Okafor, hauling himself up over the rail. His hands were still stiff in the cold mornings — they'd been stiff since the flooded corridor, since the twenty minutes Sebastian had held him against the bulkhead plate while the water rose and neither of them said a word about dying. The doctors said the stiffness would fade. Neither of them talked much about those twenty minutes anyway. They didn't need to. Something had been settled down there in the dark that didn't require discussion.

Okafor clapped Sebastian on the shoulder as he passed, which was also new. Before the fjord, Okafor had touched no one.

"Brannigan sent word," he said. "He'll make the tide."

Sebastian felt the whole deck shift slightly under his feet, though the ship wasn't even in the water yet. Brannigan. Who had stayed behind on the ice shelf with a signal lamp and a thermos of terrible coffee, blinking false courses at Sloane's fleet while the rest of them threaded the meltwater channel with the manifest strapped to Isla's chest. Who had spent four days alone on the ice waiting for a pickup that might never come, and who had greeted the returning boat by complaining that the coffee had gone cold, as if that were the true hardship of the expedition.

"He's coming back," Sebastian said. Not a question. He'd asked it enough times that Okafor just looked at him now, patiently, the way you look at a man learning to trust a thing that keeps proving itself.

"He's coming back," Okafor confirmed. "Said the north has no decent bread. Said someone has to fix that."

* * *

They launched on a Tuesday, on a high tide, with half the town of Rivergate on the quay.

That still surprised him — the crowd. Three months ago, Sebastian Montrose had been a debt-ridden desert ranger chasing a thief across salt flats, a man whose name meant nothing outside the county sheriff's office. Now dockworkers tipped their hats to him. Now old women pressed bundles of food into Isla's arms and called her *the manifest girl* like she was a saint in a stained-glass window. Now children played at diving the Rivergate in the shallows, shouting names — Okafor! Tam! — that had become stories told in taverns up and down the coast.

The tribunal had done that. Eleven days of testimony, the sealed manifest read aloud in a courtroom so packed people stood three deep at the windows. Forty-one names on the prisoner rolls. Forty-one victims of Magistrate Corvane's private prison economy, worked to death or drowned or simply erased — and among them, read out in the tribunal clerk's flat voice on the fourth day: *Elias Montrose, surveyor, died attempting delivery of evidence, date uncertain.*

Sebastian had sat in the gallery and listened to his father's name spoken as a fact of record instead of a wound of rumor, and he had not wept, though Isla's hand had found his knee under the bench rail and stayed there the whole session.

Corvane was in custody now, awaiting transport to the capital. Crane was in a hospital bed in Rivergate, having lost two fingers to frostbite and, by all accounts, considerably more than that to whatever happened inside a man when his leverage turns to ash. Sloane's fleet had escorted them through the last stretch of open water like an honor guard, and Sloane herself had signed the salvage claim as witness, her brother's name from the prisoner rolls written in her own hand on the affidavit.

Justice, it turned out, was less a verdict than a cargo. You carried it as far as you could, and then you handed it to the next crew.

"Cast off when ready," Isla said from the helm, and there was such ordinary command in it, such easy ownership of the wheel his father had once gripped, that Sebastian's throat tightened for no reason he cared to admit.

Tam's engine caught on the second try — "a miracle of engineering, do you see, it *hesitates* out of respect" — and the Vagrant Mercy slid off the slipway cradle and took the water like she'd been waiting for it. The crowd cheered. Brannigan, who had come aboard an hour earlier without ceremony and immediately annexed the galley, rang the ship's bell twice, which meant nothing in any naval tradition and everything in theirs.

They cleared the harbor mouth as the sun climbed. Behind them, Rivergate shrank to a smudge. Ahead, the sea ran long and green-gray toward the north, toward the deep-water grounds where the charts went thin and the wrecks went uncounted.

Sebastian stood at the bow and let the wind take his coat.

* * *

The charter papers were in the chart table drawer, and he took them out that first evening because he liked the weight of them.

It was Isla's doing, mostly. After the debts were cancelled — after he'd burned Crane's forged paper in the inn's hearth and watched thirty years of manufactured obligation curl into nothing — she had gone to Sloane with a proposal instead of a celebration. Wrecks, she'd said. The coast was littered with them, and every one of them held something somebody wanted buried: manifests, ledgers, logs, testimony sealed in iron boxes at the bottom of cold water. The tribunals needed evidence. The families needed proof. And nobody else alive knew how to dive a wrecked prison hulk in moving ice.

Sloane had funded the charter herself. "Consider it reparations," she'd said, "for aiming my fleet at you." Then she'd added, quieter, "And consider it the only useful thing I've done in ten years."

So the articles were drawn. Shared hull, shared risk, shared findings — the salvage rights divided honestly, the evidence delivered to the tribunals regardless of price. No more Cranes. No more debts manufactured to bend men into tools. Just a ship, a crew, and a coast full of sunken truths.

Sebastian read the articles again by lamplight, though he knew them by heart. At the bottom, six signatures. His own sat between Isla's sharp slanted hand and Tam's careful engineer's print, and seeing them together did something to his chest that he decided not to name.

A knock at the cabin door. Isla leaned in, hair loose, the compass swinging gently at her throat.

"Okafor found something in the survey archives," she said. "An old wreck report. Prison transport, lost off the Skerries forty years back. Never salvaged. Records say the hold was sealed when she went down." Her eyes had the light in them that he'd first seen across the salt flats at dusk, when she'd held his father's compass up between them and said, *it points somewhere it shouldn't.*

"Sealed holds mean sealed papers," Sebastian said.

"Sealed papers mean names." She stepped into the cabin and sat across from him, elbows on the chart table, close enough that he could smell salt and pitch and the particular soap the Rivergate inn used. "Families are still waiting on some of those names. There's a woman in Harrow Bay who's written to the tribunal nine times about her grandfather. Nine times." She tapped the table. "We could be her tenth letter. Or we could be her answer."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere above them, Brannigan was singing badly and Tam was arguing with the engine in a low affectionate murmur, and the ship rocked with the kind of easy rhythm that only came when a hull was right and a crew was settled and the sea had decided, for tonight, to be kind.

"When I caught you on the flats," he said, "I wanted to break your wrist."

"I remember. Very romantic."

"I want you to know I've revised my position."

She grinned, and it was the grin that had gotten under his skin somewhere around the second tide and never left. "Noted for the record, ranger."

He slid the charter papers back into the drawer and drew out the chart of the northern coast instead, spreading it flat between them. The Skerries lay a hundred miles north-northeast, a scatter of black teeth in white water. Uncharted approaches. Spring gales. A wreck nobody had touched in forty years.

Perfect.

"We make the Skerries in four days if the wind holds," he said. "Okafor will want a survey dive first. Tam will want to inspect the engine —"

"My engine needs no inspection —" floated down faintly from the deck, as if summoned.

"— and Brannigan will complain about the provisions by tomorrow noon. So we should probably leave tonight."

Isla was already standing, already reaching for the lamp. But she paused at the door, and she lifted the cord over her head, and she held the compass out to him across the little cabin.

It was the first time she'd offered it since the fjord. Since she'd told him, in the dark hold of the Vagrant Mercy with the ice groaning overhead, that the compass had belonged to her mother — that it had pointed, all those years, at the place her mother died, and that stealing it had been the only way Isla knew to follow her.

"Take a bearing," she said.

Sebastian took it. The brass was warm from her skin. He held it level and watched the needle swing — past north, past the harbor, past every fixed point on the chart — and settle, quivering, on the open water to the north. On the Skerries. On the next wreck, and the next truth, and the next family waiting for a name.

But that was the thing he'd learned about this compass, the thing his father had known and Isla had known and the whole mad crew of them had come to know: the needle didn't point at fate. It pointed at choice. Wherever they steered, it followed. It always had. It always would.

He passed it back to her, and their fingers overlapped on the case, and neither of them hurried.

"Wherever we choose," he said.

"That's the idea," said Isla Reyes, captain of the Vagrant Mercy, and went up to give the order.

Above, the bell rang once — the signal they'd invented themselves, the one that meant *underway, all hands, look alive* — and the engine turned over, and the sails filled, and the ship leaned north into the darkening water with a crew of five aboard and room in the articles for whoever needed saving next.

Sebastian stood at the helm beside her and watched the coastline fall away astern, and for the first time in longer than he could measure, he was not chasing anything at all.

He was going somewhere. With his crew. On his father's ship, under a name that meant mercy never stays where you put it —

— and far to the north, past the edge of the lamplight, past the edge of the chart, a light was blinking on the water. Steady. Deliberate. Three flashes, pause, three flashes.

Nobody used that code anymore except one man, and that man was supposed to be dead.

Isla saw it the same moment he did. Their eyes met over the compass, and the needle swung hard to starboard, trembling, pointing straight at it.
