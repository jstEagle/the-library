## 1. Six Tides

The tide came in at 6:14, and Zephyrine Reyes counted it the way other men counted birthdays.

He was on the water before it turned, as he always was, running the skiff out from Port Anse with the engine idled low so the sound wouldn't carry across the flats. The Leeward Shoals spread ahead of him in the gray half-light—three miles of coral heads and sand channels that changed their minds twice a day, a labyrinth the charts had never quite gotten right because the sea here refused to hold still long enough to be mapped. Good, he thought. Let it refuse. A place that couldn't be mapped couldn't be searched.

He had been counting tides for three years now. Not days—days were what men measured when they expected to arrive somewhere. Tides were what you measured when you only needed to know how much water stood between you and the bottom, and Zephyrine's whole life had become a negotiation with the bottom. Six tides to the dry season front, if the forecasters were honest, and they rarely were. Six tides of workable water left before the swells closed the shoals like a fist and every diver in the archipelago went home to wait out the winter.

Six tides. He said the number to himself the way he said all his numbers: without feeling. That was the craft of it. Anyone could feel something; feeling was cheap and everywhere, like sargassum. What took discipline was going down into forty feet of green dark with your heart rate at sixty and your hands steady and nothing in your head but the task. Zephyrine had made invisibility a trade, the way other men made carpentry or sailmaking. You learned to move through the world without displacing anything. You learned to want nothing loudly enough for anyone to hear it.

He anchored over the wreck at first light.

* * *

The navy had sealed her two years ago, and nobody would say why.

Officially she was the *Verity Ann*, a coastal freighter lost in the '09 hurricane with eleven souls aboard, all accounted for, case closed, nothing down there but rust and fish. Officially the salvage exclusion buoy—painted naval gray, stenciled RESTRICTED WATERS, riding the swell a hundred yards off the site—was a formality, a courtesy to the dead. Unofficially, everyone who worked the Leeward Shoals knew three things: the *Verity Ann* had gone down carrying more than freight; the navy had spent four weeks on the site with equipment no storm-salvage operation required; and the hull had been sealed with welded plates rather than blown open, which was what you did when you wanted to keep something in, not keep divers out.

Zephyrine knew a fourth thing, because he'd been diving the perimeter since the buoys went in, working the debris field the navy hadn't bothered to claim. Cargo shifts in a sinking. It scatters. Whatever the *Verity Ann* carried had shed pieces of itself across a quarter mile of reef like a molting animal, and the fleet's survey crews, so thorough inside the exclusion line, had never once looked outside it. Their loss. His living.

He went down on the flood tide, when the current pushed him along the debris line instead of against it, and worked by feel more than sight—the visibility this close to the coral was eight feet on a good day, and today wasn't good. His gloves moved over the sand the way a blind man's fingers move over a page. Winch block. Chain run. A cargo hook furred with coral, beautiful in its uselessness. He logged each find mentally, marked it with a zip tie, moved on. Methodical. Patient. A man who wanted nothing loudly.

Forty minutes in, his hand found something that wasn't coral.

It was a locker—a footlocker, steel, maybe thirty inches long, crusted so thick with calcification it looked grown rather than manufactured. It had wedged itself under a slab of hull plating, and the plate had done it a service, hiding it from two years of fleet surveys. Zephyrine worked it free with his pry bar, taking his time, checking his air, checking the current, checking everything, because discipline was not a mood, it was a schedule. The locker came loose with a sigh of sand and rose against his grip, heavier than its size explained.

There was a stencil on the lid. Most of it had gone to the crust, but the crust had cracked along one edge, and through the crack he read four letters of a name:

**R-A-M-O-S.**

His breath caught—not much, a hitch the size of a coin, but underwater even a small thing echoes. Ramos. Elena Ramos. Second mate on the *Verity Ann*, the one name on the crew manifest that had never sat right with him, because he'd pulled her personnel file once, quietly, from the harbor office in Port Anse, and the file had been too clean. Too new. Nobody's file got reprinted six months after they drowned unless somebody upstairs wanted the paper trail to say something specific.

He didn't let himself think further than that. Thinking was wanting, and wanting was loud.

He clipped the locker to his lift bag and sent it up.

* * *

Delphine was waiting on the skiff, feet bare, hair bound back with a strip of red cloth, gutting the morning's fish over the side with quick economical strokes. She didn't look up when he broke the surface. She never did until he was actually aboard—she'd told him once that watching a partner surface was how you learned to worry, and worrying was a habit she intended to die without acquiring.

"Anything?" she said.

"Chain. A winch block." He hauled himself over the gunwale, breathing normally, keeping his voice level, and felt the locker's weight shift in the water behind the boat where he'd staged it on the descent line. "Reef's giving up. Two more tides on this field and we're done."

"You said that last week."

"Last week I was wrong about the weather." He took the knife from her, finished the fish she'd started, handed it back. Their hands had done this exchange a thousand times; it required no eye contact, which suited them both. Delphine Marchand had been his partner for two years and his friend for none, in the sense that neither of them had ever asked the other a question whose answer might require trust. They split the take fifty-fifty, covered each other's dives, and kept their pasts moored offshore like derelict hulls—visible, acknowledged, never boarded.

She glanced at the descent line. "What's on the bag?"

"Nothing worth pulling."

"Zeph."

"Sand weight. I'll cut it loose."

She looked at him then, fully, with those flat gray eyes that missed less than anyone's he'd ever known, and he watched her decide—not to believe him, but to bank the disbelief. That was Delphine. She collected debts of honesty the way a moneylender collected interest, silently, and you always knew the total was growing even when she never mentioned it.

"Two more tides," she said finally, turning back to her fish. "Then the season closes and we go ashore like normal people."

"Like normal people," he agreed.

But his eyes went once, briefly, to the shadow of the locker hanging below the hull, turning gently on its line in the green water, and he thought: *Ramos.* He thought: *a file too clean is a door someone paid to close.* He thought: *six tides.*

And then he put it away, the way he put everything away, in the flooded compartment at the back of his skull where he kept Mayagüez and the inquiry and the name he'd stopped using, and he took up the tiller, and he ran them home across the falling tide, invisible, disciplined, counting.

* * *

That night, alone in the lean-to behind Delphine's boat shed while she slept, Zephyrine knelt over the locker with a chisel and a bucket of fresh water and took the crust off in flakes, working by lamplight the way an archaeologist works, or a man defusing something. The steel beneath was pitted but sound. The latch had fused; he freed it with penetrating oil and patience, and near midnight the lid came up with a groan and a smell of old salt and older paper.

Inside: a seabag of rotted cloth. A tin cup. And wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day it was packed, a ledger—ship's station bill, crew assignments, watch rotations for the *Verity Ann*'s final voyage. Routine. Except that the final page had been torn out, raggedly, in a hurry, and the remaining pages carried annotations in a second hand, pencil, small and precise, noting cargo weights that matched no manifest any harbor office held. Eleven tons of discrepancy. Eleven tons the *Verity Ann* had carried that officially did not exist.

And tucked into the binding, a single sheet of stationery, water-stained at one corner, written in a careful seaman's script and signed *E. Ramos*. Three lines. He read them twice, then a third time, and the third time his hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed him, because steadiness was the whole of his craft.

*I have put the truth where the truth will keep,* the note said. *If you are reading this, the navy found me before I found a listener. Ask the deep shoal. R.A.M.O.S.*

The deep shoal. Everyone on the Leeward Trail knew the phrase the way children knew a curse—there was water out past the third bank where the soundings dropped off the chart entirely, where the fleet's boats turned back and the navy's buoys mysteriously ended, and where, ten years ago, a prospector named Augustin Vex had spent a season diving alone and come ashore with white hair and a fortune he never spent and a silence he never broke.

Zephyrine sat back on his heels in the lamplight and listened to the tide turn outside, and understood, with the cold clarity he trusted more than any feeling, that the locker in his hands was not cargo. It was a message. It had been waiting two years under a slab of hull for exactly one kind of man to find it, and it did not care whether that man wanted to be found.

Six tides. He looked at the note again. *Ask the deep shoal.*

Tomorrow, he'd row into Port Anse for fuel and stores, same as every week, face as blank as ballast. But Augustin Vex kept a room above the cooper's shop on Rue des Pêcheurs, and everyone said the old man was dying, and dying men, Zephyrine knew, were the only people left in the world who told the truth for free.

He folded the note back into the ledger, closed the locker, and blew out the lamp.

One tide down. Five to go.
