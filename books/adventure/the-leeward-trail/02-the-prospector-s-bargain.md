## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

Port Anse smelled the way all port towns smell when the fleet comes in to refit: diesel and salt and rotting fruit, sweat dried into canvas awnings, the sweet-sour breath of rum shops opening at hours no church would bless. Zephyrine walked through it with his collar up and his eyes down, a man practicing invisibility so long it had become less a skill than a posture, the way a crab carries its shell without thinking.

He had come ashore for three things only. Diesel. A new cutting torch tip, because the old one had gone soft as butter against the navy wreck's hull plating. And ice, because Delphine had asked, and Delphine did not ask for things often enough that he could afford to pretend he hadn't heard.

The market street was loud with the fleet's arrival—deckhands spending wages they hadn't finished earning, a woman selling fried jackfish out of a basket balanced on her head, two men from the *Cormorant* arguing about compressor pressure in voices that carried across three rooftops. Zephyrine bought what he needed from vendors who knew him as nothing more than a face that appeared every few weeks, paid in small bills, and never lingered. He was halfway back to the dock road when he saw the boy.

The boy couldn't have been more than twelve, barefoot, holding a paper bag of bread against his chest like something rescued from a fire. He stood at the mouth of an alley between the cooperage and a boarding house whose paint had surrendered to salt years ago, and he was crying in the silent way children cry when they've learned that noise brings worse than comfort.

Zephyrine should have walked past. In three years he had become excellent at walking past. But the boy looked up, and there was something in his face—not fear exactly, but the specific helplessness of someone who has been sent to do a thing and cannot do it—and Zephyrine heard himself say, "Who's sick?"

The boy wiped his nose on his shoulder. "Monsieur Vex. Room four. The landlady says if he don't pay by tomorrow—" He stopped, because adults always stopped when they realized they were telling their troubles to strangers.

"Vex." The name sat strangely in Zephyrine's mouth. Augustin Vex. Every diver who worked the Leeward Shoals knew the name the way farmers know the name of a drought year. Twenty years the old man had worked the outer banks alone, half mad or fully mad depending on who told the story, chasing wrecks the charts didn't admit existed. "You're family?"

"No, m'sieu. I run errands. He gives me a gourd sometimes." The boy looked down at the bread. "He asked for this yesterday but he ain't woke up today."

Zephyrine stood in the alley mouth with diesel oil soaking through his bag and the tide clock ticking somewhere behind his ribs—he'd learned long ago to feel the turn of water even standing on dry land, a habit that was either professionalism or sickness—and made the kind of small decision that men make without knowing they are deciding anything at all.

"Show me the room," he said.

* * *

The boarding house stairs complained under his weight, each step announcing him to the building like a town crier. The hallway smelled of kerosene and mildew and something else underneath, something sweetish and wrong that Zephyrine recognized before he could name it: the smell of a body beginning its long negotiation with leaving.

Room four was unlocked. The boy hung back at the threshold, and Zephyrine understood why.

Augustin Vex lay on a cot too short for him, one arm hanging to the floor, and he was not asleep. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling where the plaster had bloomed with water stains shaped like continents nobody had charted. His chest moved in shallow, irregular pulls, the rhythm of a man drowning slowly in air. His skin had gone the color of old rope. Around him the room held the archaeology of a life spent alone: a dented hurricane lamp, a tin cup crusted white, clothes folded with a precision that surprised Zephyrine until he remembered that men who work alone often keep order the way other men keep company.

"Monsieur Vex," Zephyrine said. "I brought nothing. I'm not the boy."

The old man's eyes came down from the ceiling with visible effort, like cargo being winced off a deck. They found Zephyrine's face and stayed there, and something moved through them—recognition, or the hope of recognition, which is nearly the same thing in a dying man.

"You dive the shoals," Vex said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey, the voice of a man who had spent decades shouting over engines and then decades more whispering to himself. "Leeward side. I've seen your boat. Black hull. You dive quiet."

"I dive quiet," Zephyrine agreed, because denying it would be louder.

"The navy wreck." Vex tried to lift the arm that hung to the floor and failed, and the failure cost him something; Zephyrine watched it go out of his face like air from a punctured suit. "They sealed her. You know why?"

"I know they sealed her."

"That's not knowing why." The old man smiled, and the smile was terrible because it used muscles that had forgotten how. "Nobody knows why. That's the whole trick of it. A secret needs a door, and they built theirs underwater where nobody thinks to knock."

Zephyrine set his bag down. He told himself he was staying because the boy couldn't carry the bread up alone, because the landlady deserved to know, because any of a dozen decent reasons. The truth was simpler and uglier: he could not stop looking at the man's hands. They were enormous, scarred to the wrist, the hands of someone who had pulled himself along reefs by fingertip for longer than Zephyrine had been alive. And they were empty. Dying men's hands should hold something. These held nothing, and it looked obscene.

"There's a doctor in town," Zephyrine said. "I can send the boy."

"A doctor." Vex laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Zephyrine crossed the room and put a hand behind the old man's shoulders without deciding to. The ribs under his palm felt like a birdcage after the bird has gone. "A doctor would tell me what I already know, and charge me for the telling. No. What I need—" He caught his breath. "What I need is a witness."

"For what?"

"Not what." Vex's eyes closed. Opened again. "Who. Sit down, diver. Standing men leave. Sitting men stay."

There was one chair, a wooden thing with a woven seat gone brittle in the middle. Zephyrine sat in it, and the sitting felt like a decision much larger than it was, though he would not understand how large until later, until the tides had turned six times and turned everything else with them.

* * *

Vex talked the way water finds cracks: slowly, patiently, following the path of least resistance toward the deepest place.

He had come to the islands forty years ago with a survey crew and stayed when the survey ended, because the shoals had shown him something on a bad-weather day—a reading, a shadow, a shape on the fathometer that the official charts said should be open water. He had spent four decades and two marriages and whatever savings a prospector ever manages to keep proving that shadow was real. The first wreck everyone knew about—the navy one, the sealed one—had been his discovery, though no record said so. The navy had taken his coordinates in 1961 and thanked him with a letter and a check so small it was an insult dressed as courtesy. Then they had dropped a buoy line around the site and told the world it was a hazard to navigation.

"But she wasn't the deep one," Vex said. His voice was failing now, fraying at the edges like old line. "She was the *sentinel*. Wrecks travel in pairs, diver, same as men. One sits where you can see her, and one lies deeper, past the sand channels, where the cold water comes up. The visible one guards the true one. Always has. The Spanish knew it. The English knew it. The navies of the world have known it since men first started sinking things they wanted forgotten."

"And the deep one," Zephyrine said carefully, "you've found it."

Something kindled in the old man's face. It was the worst thing Zephyrine had seen yet, worse than the rope-colored skin, worse than the birdcage ribs: hope, arriving decades late, burning anyway.

"Found her? Boy, I *courted* her. Thirty-one years. I dove her twice." He raised two fingers, trembling. "Twice, and both times the sea took my price. First time she took my partner, Émile, in 'forty-nine. Second time she took my lungs—too deep, too long, and I came up knowing I'd traded the rest of my breath for ten minutes in her hold." He touched his own chest almost tenderly, the way you'd touch a wound you'd decided to stop hating. "Emphysema, the doctor called it, five years ago. I call it rent. The sea collects. She always collects."

Outside, the afternoon had thickened toward evening. Somewhere down the street the fleet's generators were spinning up, a sound like distant thunder that never broke. The boy had gone; Zephyrine didn't remember him leaving. The light through the room's single window went amber, then rust.

"Why tell me?" Zephyrine asked. "You don't know me. For all you know I'm the worst man on this island."

"I know what you are." Vex's eyes were sharp suddenly, lucid in a way that made Zephyrine's neck prickle. "You walk like a man counting exits. You buy diesel in three different shops so no one shop knows your consumption. You flinch when I say the word *navy*, just here—" he tapped the skin beside his own eye, "—just a flicker, quick as a lizard. You're hiding, diver. From what, I don't ask. A man's past is his own ballast. But I know hiding when I see it, because I've been doing it thirty years myself. Hiding from a wreck instead of a crime, but the posture's identical."

Zephyrine said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie, and lying to a dying man felt like a different category of sin than the ones he practiced daily.

Vex reached beneath the cot. The motion took him a full minute and visibly emptied him; when he drew his arm back out, he was holding a bundle wrapped in wax cloth, tied with tarred twine, the kind of wrapping seamen used for charts they meant to survive weather. He laid it on the blanket between them like a man laying down a heavy tool.

"Open it."

Zephyrine hesitated. Then he untied the twine—it came away stiff, reluctant, the knots swollen with years—and unfolded the wax cloth.

Inside was a chart. Hand-drawn, ink on linen-backed paper gone soft as cloth with handling, the edges worn round as river stones. It showed the Leeward Shoals as no hydrographic office drew them: every channel named in a cramped, patient hand, every reef marked with depth soundings in fathoms, hundreds of them, the record of a lifetime spent dropping lead lines into green water. And in the lower quadrant, past the sand channels, past the marked edge of the navy exclusion zone, a single shape drawn in careful profile: a hull, broken amidships, resting in what the notation said was eleven fathoms and a half. Beside it, in letters pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper:

*LA PERDUE — 1724 — THE DEEP ONE — SHE KEEPS HER CARGO.*

Zephyrine stared at it. He had seen charts his whole working life, good ones and forged ones and everything between, and everything in his bones told him this one was true—the soundings were too numerous to fake, the corrections too consistent, the little marginal notes in a diver's shorthand (*foul ground here, anchor holds, current runs hard on the ebb*) that no forger would bother inventing. This was not a treasure map. This was a man's entire life, folded into wax cloth.

"It resolves against nothing," Zephyrine heard himself say. "Your latitudes are dead reckoning. Without a fixed reference—"

"Without the fleet's master survey, yes." Vex smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it, wolfish and delighted. "You see it already. Good. I needed a man who'd see it in under a minute, and I've been watching you three weeks to be sure." He began to cough again, deeper now, and Zephyrine waited it out with his hand hovering uselessly over the old man's shoulder. When Vex could speak, his voice had dropped to a thread. "The Cormorant carries the master survey of these shoals. Marchetti bought it from the Admiralty, back when he still pretended to be legitimate. Your chart and their chart together make one true chart. Apart, they're half a riddle each. Which means, diver, that what I'm giving you is worth nothing—and everything—and the difference between those two things is whether you're willing to go get the other half."

"I'm not a treasure hunter."

"No." Vex's eyes were closing. "You're a man who wants nothing. I was that once too. It doesn't last. Wanting always comes back, like tide."

The lamp needed trimming; the flame had gone low and yellow. Zephyrine got up to tend it, grateful for the task, and when he turned back Vex had gathered himself against the wall with the last of some reserve Zephyrine couldn't locate, and his huge ruined hands were folded on the wax-cloth chart, and he was looking at Zephyrine with an expression that had shed all its cunning and left only the naked thing underneath.

"One promise," the old man said. "That's the price. Not money—I have none. Not labor. One promise, and the chart is yours free, and everything she holds."

"Don't." Zephyrine heard his own voice go flat and cold, the voice he used on clients who tried to renegotiate mid-contract. "Don't dress it up. Say what you want."

"What I want." Vex breathed for a while, the effort of breathing apparently taking everything. "At Sainte-Rose, on the hill above the village, there is a cemetery. My daughter lives below it—Colette, she keeps the little house with the blue shutters, you'll know it. I have not seen her in nine years. I wrote and she wrote and then we both stopped writing, because that's what pride does, it starves you politely." His voice cracked and steadied. "When I die—and it will be soon, tonight perhaps, tomorrow if the night goes slow—I want my ashes on that hill. Not scattered at sea. Everyone scatters divers at sea; it's lazy and it's free and it means nothing. I want *land*. Ground that stays where you put it. I spent my whole life on water that moves, and I want to finish on something that holds still." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Carry me there, diver. That's all. Carry me up the hill and put me down, and tell Colette her father kept her letters even the ones he didn't answer. That's the whole of the price. One trip. One hill."

The room was very quiet. Down the street the fleet's generators thundered on. Zephyrine sat in the brittle chair and felt something arrive in him uninvited, unwelcome, unwilled—the way weather arrives, the way guilt arrives, which is the same mechanism wearing different clouds. He knew what promises were. He had made them professionally for years: salvage contracts, salvage law, the whole architecture of *I will recover what you lost or forfeit my fee.* He broke them professionally too, when the fine print allowed, when the sea intervened, when the arithmetic favored it. A promise, in his experience, was a rope with a slipknot in one end, and he had spent three years keeping his hands empty of ropes.

But Vex was dying in front of him, and the chart lay between them smelling of tar and time, and the old man's hands were empty, and no one else in the world was going to climb that hill.

"I break promises," Zephyrine said. "It's fair to tell you that."

"I know," Vex whispered. "Everyone does. That's why the promise matters. If promises were easy to keep they'd be worth nothing, same as wrecks in shallow water." He fumbled at the chart, pushed it an inch across the blanket toward Zephyrine. "Take it. Take it and say yes, or take it and say nothing, but take it, because I can't hold it anymore and it shouldn't die in this room."

And Zephyrine—who measured his life in miles precisely so that he would never again have to measure it in obligations, who had buried a name and a country and a man's death under three years of disciplined silence—heard himself speak before the part of him that calculated risks could intervene.

"Yes," he said. "Sainte-Rose. The hill. I'll carry you."

Vex exhaled, a long rattling sound, and something in his enormous frame let go—not death yet, but the loosening of a knot held for decades. He slid down against the wall, and Zephyrine moved the chart clear and eased the old man flat onto the cot, and Vex's hand found his wrist and gripped it with shocking strength, the grip of a man who had held lifelines in black water.

"She keeps her cargo," the old man murmured, eyes already glazing toward the ceiling and whatever continents grew there. "Eleven and a half fathoms. Cold water rising. And diver—if anyone asks—you found the chart on my body. You robbed a dead man. Say it like you mean it. Dead men can't dispute you."

Then he slept, or stopped sleeping and started something else, and Zephyrine sat in the darkening room holding a dead man's life's work and wondering which of them had just drowned.

* * *

Augustin Vex died at 3:40 in the morning, between the ebb and the slack, and Zephyrine knew the hour because he was awake to watch it, sitting in the brittle chair with the lamp trimmed low, listening to the old man's breathing space itself out like a tide running down. At the end there was no drama. The intervals simply lengthened—one breath, then a gap, then a breath—until the gap became the rule and the breath the exception, and then the exception lapsed, and the room held only the sound of the fleet's generators and the sea working the harbor stones below.

Zephyrine closed the old man's eyes. He did it clumsily; he had never done it before. Then he sat for a long time with the wax-cloth chart on his knees, feeling the weight of it, which was nothing, and the weight of the word *yes*, which was considerable.

Near dawn he went downstairs and found the landlady, gave her enough money for the room and the burial paperwork and her silence, and asked her where a man in Port Anse could find a cremation done quietly. She looked at him with the flat appraisal of a woman who had rented rooms to dying men for thirty years and said, "Père Lucien handles it. He won't ask questions. None of us do, in this town."

By full light the deed was done and Zephyrine stood on the dock road with a coffee tin under one arm—he had bought the tin specifically, a proper tin with a tight lid, because ashes deserved better than a bag—and the chart inside his jacket against his chest, and Delphine waiting at the boat with her arms crossed and her eyes already asking questions he had no intention of answering honestly.

Later, alone in the cabin with the door shut, he unrolled the chart under the lamp and traced the drawn hull with one finger, down past the sand channels to the notation in its furious pressed ink. Eleven and a half fathoms. Cold water rising. *She keeps her cargo.*

He had promised a dying man a hill. He had inherited a riddle with half its pieces missing. And somewhere out in the anchorage, riding her lights at anchor like a hotel that had wandered to sea, the *Cormorant* held the other half—and her director, Ivo Marchetti, held files on every diver in the Leewards, filed and cross-filed, the way men like Marchetti held everything.

Zephyrine folded the chart away, and lay in his bunk listening to the tide turn, and did not sleep at all.
