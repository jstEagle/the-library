## 3. Half a Map

The lamp in Zephyrine's room at the Pension Caraïbe had a bad wick. It burned with a stutter, throwing light in pulses, so that everything it touched seemed to breathe—the peeling wall, the tin basin, the wax-cloth map spread flat and weighted at its corners with four coins he would not be able to spend if things went the way he suspected they might.

He had not slept. Sleep was a country he'd stopped holding citizenship in years ago, and tonight the border was closed entirely.

Vex had died an hour after dawn, quietly, between one breath and the next, like a man stepping out of a conversation he'd grown tired of. The flophouse owner had wanted him gone before noon; Zephyrine had paid for the week anyway, because the dead deserved a room as much as the living did, and because he hadn't yet decided what to do with the body or the promise or the map. He'd folded Vex's hands over his chest and sat with him until the smell of the harbor came through the window and reminded him that the living have schedules.

Now the map lay under the lamp's failing pulse, and Zephyrine leaned over it the way a man leans over a wound—close enough to understand it, careful not to make it worse.

* * *

Wax cloth was a prospector's medium: cheap, waterproof, tough enough to survive a shirt pocket through forty years of sweating and swearing. Vex had drawn on it in three inks—a black that had gone brown, a red that had faded to the color of old blood, and a blue that remained stubbornly, almost defiantly itself. The hand was shaky but precise. This was not a drunkard's treasure map. This was the work of a man who had spent decades learning how the sea kept its secrets and had decided, near the end, to give one up.

The shoals were there, rendered in Vex's cramped cartography: the Leeward chain, the broken spine of reef running southwest from the point at Anse Colibri, depth soundings penciled along the channels like a sentence written in numbers. Zephyrine knew those soundings by heart. He had been reading them off his own charts for three years, diving them for two, bleeding into them more times than he could count.

But Vex's map went where no chart he owned went.

Past the navy buoy. Past the sealed wreck with its hull number stenciled in white and its perimeter of silence. The map continued southwest, into water marked on every official chart as *unsurveyed*—which was a lie, because all water is surveyed by someone, sometime; the phrase only means the surveyor didn't want you to know.

There, in the blue ink, a cross.

Not an X—the theatrical kind, the kind drawn for tourists and children—but a surveyor's cross, two bearing lines intersecting, each line annotated with a reference point and an angle. And beneath the cross, in letters small enough to require the lamp held close:

*HOLD — 14 FATHOMS — SAND OVER CORAL — SHE LIES ON HER PORT SIDE.*

Zephyrine read it four times. Then he did what he always did when the sea offered him something too good: he looked for the trick.

* * *

The trick revealed itself slowly, the way most tricks do—not with a flourish but with a small wrongness that grows.

The bearings were correct. He checked them against his memory of the reef line, against the sun angles he'd logged across two dry seasons, against the soundings he carried in his head the way other men carried debts. Vex's references were good. The angle off the northern cay, the back-bearing from the deep channel marker—all of it consistent, all of it professional.

But the numbers didn't close.

A fix needs to resolve. Two lines cross, you get a point, the point sits on the chart, done. Vex's lines crossed beautifully—and the point they made fell in open water where nothing could lie at fourteen fathoms, because the sounding there, on every chart Zephyrine had ever seen, was twenty-two. Either Vex had drawn a map to a shipwreck floating in midwater, or—

Or the map was half of one.

Zephyrine sat back on the room's single chair, which complained under him, and stared at the pulse of the lamp. Half a map. It was an old con, older than salvage: split your knowledge so no one man can steal it. Prospectors did it with partners they didn't trust. Captains did it with owners they trusted less. And governments—

Governments did it with wrecks they sealed.

He stood up. Paced the four steps the room allowed. Sat down again. Pulled the map closer.

Because here was the thing he should have seen on the flophouse floor, with Vex's fingers cold around his wrist and the promise still wet in his mouth: the bearings weren't incomplete at random. They were incomplete *systematically*. Every reference point Vex used was a feature of the first wreck—the navy-sealed one, the one whose locker Zephyrine had hauled up last week with a name stamped into its corroded face that he had lied to Delphine about. The sealed wreck wasn't just a neighbor on the map. It was the anchor. Its position, its dimensions, its exact orientation on the seabed—those were the fixed points from which Vex's bearing lines sprang.

And who held the exact position of the sealed wreck?

Not Vex. Vex had never gotten within a mile of it; the navy's patrol skiff saw to that. Not any chart sold in Port Anse; the official surveys showed the wreck as a hazard symbol and nothing more. No—the precise coordinates, the hull orientation, the survey-grade detail that Vex's bearings required to resolve—

That lived aboard the *Cormorant*.

* * *

Everyone at the shoals knew the *Cormorant*, the way everyone knows the weather. She was the salvage fleet's tender: hundred and ten feet of rust-streaked steel, a crane on her aft deck like a gallows arm, generators humming day and night. She anchored over the sealed wreck for weeks at a stretch, lowering divers on umbilicals, raising things in closed crates that never passed through customs, and when anyone asked what she was salvaging, the answer changed depending on who asked and how much they'd had to drink.

Ivo Marchetti ran her. Marchetti ran the whole fleet—eleven boats, two hundred men, a payroll that flowed through three banks and left no fingerprints. He was a small man with large hands and a voice pitched so low that people leaned in to hear him, which was, Zephyrine suspected, the entire architecture of his power.

And somewhere aboard the *Cormorant*, in a survey room behind a door that opened to Marchetti's key alone, hung the master chart of the Leeward Shoals. Every diver in the fleet knew it existed. None of them had seen it. It was said to contain soundings from before the war, positions corrected to the centimeter, and the true location of everything the fleet had ever found and chosen not to announce—including, presumably, the sealed wreck, surveyed to the inch during whatever arrangement the fleet had with the navy that let a civilian tender work a military exclusion zone without ever being boarded.

Vex's map needed that chart. Without it, the cross on the blue ink pointed at empty water. With it—

With it, the lines would close, and fourteen fathoms of sand over coral would give up whatever she'd been keeping on her port side for two centuries.

Zephyrine understood then, with the particular clarity that comes at the end of a sleepless night, why the navy had sealed the first wreck. Everyone assumed the seal was about the wreck itself—cargo, ordnance, embarrassment, some combination. But the sealed wreck sat in thirty feet of water over sand, and in thirty feet of water there is very little worth a naval exclusion zone. Unless the wreck wasn't the prize. Unless the wreck was the *key*—a fixed point, surveyed and guarded, from which someone intended to resolve a bearing line toward something deeper.

They weren't protecting the shallow wreck.

They were using it to hunt the deep one.

And Vex—poor, dying, patient Vex—had figured out the same geometry from the outside, over forty years, one bearing at a time, and had drawn the answer on a piece of wax cloth and traded it to a stranger for a grave on a hill.

* * *

The lamp pulsed. Outside, Port Anse was waking: fish carts on cobblestone, the gull argument over the market roof, a radio somewhere playing the same six notes of a song it had played every morning since Zephyrine arrived in these islands. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a life he had built carefully, plank by plank, out of the wreckage of the one before.

He thought about what the map was asking of him.

Three years ago he had come south with one rule, self-imposed, non-negotiable: want nothing. Wanting was how men got caught. A man who wants nothing can't be leveraged, can't be followed, can't be predicted—he drifts like silt and settles wherever the water lets him. Zephyrine had been very good at it. He worked, he ate, he dove, he slept alone, he measured his life in miles traveled and fathoms descended and tides turned, units that asked nothing of him and promised less. When the locker came up off the sealed wreck with that name stamped in the corrosion, he had felt the old current tug at his ankles, and he had lied to Delphine—easily, smoothly, the way you lie when the truth would cost more than the lie—and put the locker in the channel the same night, weighted with chain, and told himself the numbness he felt afterward was peace.

It had worked. For three years it had worked.

And now a dying man's handwriting was on his table, and the trap of it was not that the map promised gold. Gold meant nothing to him; he'd seen what gold does to fleets and navies and partners, and he wanted no part of that arithmetic. The trap was smaller and worse than that. The trap was that for the first time in three years, Zephyrine Reyes *wanted to know*.

Wanting to know was the gateway drug. Wanting to know became wanting to see, wanting to touch, wanting to have, wanting to matter—and a man who matters can be found, and a man who can be found can be named, and his name was already sitting in a file somewhere with a photograph stapled to it and a date and a word like *negligence* or *falsified* written underneath in an investigator's careful hand.

He pressed his palms flat against the wax cloth, feeling its faint greasiness, its stubbornness. Forty years of a man's patience, bequeathed to him free of charge, which meant it was the most expensive thing he had ever been given.

"You should have charged me," he said aloud, to the room, to the dead man across town, to nobody. "Then I could have refused."

The lamp stuttered and steadied. The blue cross waited under his hands, pointing southwest at nothing, at everything, at fourteen fathoms of sand over coral that might hold coin or cargo or simply the bones of a ship and the end of a long patience.

* * *

He worked until the light through the shutters went from gray to gold, because work was the only prayer he knew. He copied the map onto tracing paper in case the original was taken. He memorized it—every bearing, every sounding, every annotation—because paper burns and memory drowns slower. He wrote out the unresolved geometry on the back of a dive log page, the equations neat as a confession, and stared at the gap where the second datum belonged, the hole in the middle of the fix shaped exactly like a locked room on a rust-streaked tender.

Which raised the question he had been circling all night the way a shark circles a light.

To resolve the map, he needed the master survey. To get the master survey, he needed to go aboard the *Cormorant*. And the *Cormorant* was not a place a man wandered onto by accident—it took contracts, credentials, a reason. The fleet hired freelance divers in the dry season, when the workload doubled and their own men burned out; that was known, that was a door. But doors that open easily are doors someone is watching.

And beyond that question sat a larger one, patient as a stonefish: if the navy was hunting the deep wreck using the shallow one as its anchor, and if Marchetti's fleet was working the shallow wreck under some arrangement with the navy, then Marchetti either had the deep wreck's position already—or was looking for it. And if he was looking for it, then the moment anyone resolved Vex's bearings, the race would begin, and it would not be a race conducted with charts.

It would be conducted the way these things were always conducted. Quietly. With money first and water after.

Zephyrine looked at the map a long time. Then he folded it along Vex's original creases, wrapped it in oilcloth, and slid it inside the false seam of his dive bag, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose and cannot afford to explain.

He would need help. That was the thought he'd been refusing since the flophouse, and it would not stay refused. The geometry was beyond solo work—two boats, a shot line, a decompression schedule for fourteen fathoms with no margin for error. There was exactly one person in the islands he trusted with his life underwater, and he had spent three years making sure she trusted him with nothing else.

Delphine would look at him and know. She always knew. She read him the way she read current—by what bent, by what held.

Zephyrine picked up his hat, checked the street through the shutter slats out of a habit older than his exile, and went down the stairs into the noise of morning, carrying half a map and all of a promise, toward the one conversation he had been avoiding for three years.

She was already waiting on the dock. She was leaning against her boat with her arms crossed, and beside her feet, dripping seawater onto the planks, sat a corroded locker stamped with a name—and she was looking at him the way the sea looks at a man who has just thrown something overboard and thinks nobody saw.
