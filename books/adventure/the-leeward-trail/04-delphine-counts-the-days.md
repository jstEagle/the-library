## 4. Delphine Counts the Days

Delphine Marchal had a rule about counting: never count what you couldn't afford to lose. She counted tanks, she counted fathoms, she counted the minutes of bottom time with a watch strapped inside her wrist where no current could steal it. She did not count money—money came and went like weather—and she did not count days, because days were just miles wearing different clothes.

Zephyrine counted miles. She'd noticed it three years ago, back when he first showed up at her dock in Port Anse with salt-cracked hands and a résumé he refused to discuss, asking for work on her boat in a voice like a man apologizing for existing. He measured everything in distance. How far to the shoals. How far down to the wreck. How many miles of open water between here and wherever he'd come from—a question he answered by not answering it. He never said *three years ago*. He said *a thousand miles back*. He never said *I've been doing this a long time*. He said *I've covered a lot of water*.

Men who measured their lives in miles were men running from something, or toward something, or both at once along the same road. Delphine had grown up around fishermen and smugglers and one memorable gunrunner, and she knew the difference between a man at rest and a man holding still. Zephyrine held still beautifully. He was the best diver she'd ever put in the water, calm as ballast, precise as a tide table. But holding still was a skill, and skills were practiced, and nobody practiced that hard unless the alternative was motion they couldn't survive.

Lately he'd stopped practicing.

She saw it in small things first. He'd started checking his knots twice—not the anxious double-check of a green diver but the deliberate redundancy of a man building something to last exactly as long as he needed it. He'd begun sketching in the margins of his tide tables, little triangles and bearing lines, and covering them with his forearm when she leaned over. He'd taken to eating facing the harbor mouth, watching the fleet's tender *Cormorant* swing at its moorings across the anchorage like a dog chained too close to a butcher shop.

And then there was the locker.

She'd seen him pull it off the navy wreck two weeks ago—a corroded steel box stamped with letters he'd gone pale over, though his face hadn't changed, because his face never changed; the pallor lived somewhere behind it. He'd told her it was empty. She'd helped him carry it aboard. Empty lockers don't weigh what that locker weighed. Empty lockers don't get wrapped in oilcloth and stowed under a bunk instead of opened on deck. And empty lockers don't make a man start sleeping in shifts, one eye on the harbor, the way soldiers sleep.

So on the morning the dry-season front first showed itself as a bruise on the eastern horizon, Delphine decided she was done counting his silences, and she went to collect what she was owed.

* * *

She found him at the north end of the shoal flats, where the tide had pulled back far enough to expose the coral bones of the reef. He was crouched over a tide table weighted with four shells, drawing those triangles again, and when her shadow fell across the paper he covered it so fast he knocked two shells into the sand.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning." He didn't look up. He was re-anchoring the table with the remaining shells, buying himself time with busy hands. She let him have ten seconds of it. She was generous that way.

"You've been drawing maps on my boat," she said.

"It's navigation."

"It's navigation you hide." She sat down on an old mooring post, worn smooth as driftwood, and looked out at the water rather than at him. Confrontation worked better sideways with Zephyrine. Head-on, he turned into a porthole—all glass, nothing behind it. "You check the *Cormorant*'s boats every time they move. You've sold half your good gear—I found the receipt for your backup regulator in the trash, and Zeph, nobody sells a backup regulator unless they're planning to need money more than air. And you've got a locker under your bunk that you told me was empty."

The wind moved through the mangroves behind them. Somewhere out on the flats a heron stabbed at the shallows and missed.

"I told you," he said quietly, "it's nothing."

"You're a liar," Delphine said, "and I've known it since the day we met, and I hired you anyway, because a man who lies well about himself usually tells the truth about everything else. The currents, the depths, the work—that's all true with you. It's only ever yourself you falsify." She finally turned to look at him directly. "So here's my truth, since yours is apparently under a tarp: I dove the sealed wreck."

That landed. She watched it land—the way his whole body went still, stiller than still, the practiced stillness cracking just enough to show the machinery underneath.

"You what?"

"Two years ago. Before the navy buoyed it off. There was a week in October when the patrol boat went to Fort-de-France for refit, and the seal buoys were down for maintenance, and I'd been curious about that wreck since I was nineteen years old and my father used to point at it from the deck and say, *that one ate a government ship and nobody talks about it.*" She shrugged. "Curiosity is a current. You can fight it or ride it. I rode it."

"You could have been arrested."

"I could have drowned. That was the bigger worry." She hugged her knees, looking out at the reef flats, at the place beyond them where the water darkened over the shoal. "You want to know what I saw down there? Because I think you already know, and I think that's why you've been sleeping in shifts."

He said nothing. The heron cried out once, flat and ugly, and gave up on the shallows.

"The hull's torn open on the port side, aft of the superstructure," she said. "Not storm damage. Storm damage tears things outward or peels them. This was blown—from inside, or from very close alongside. And in the corridor behind the tear—" Her voice caught, not from grief but from the effort of saying it plainly after two years of carrying it alone. "There were bodies, Zeph. Nine that I counted before my nerve went. In a ship that officially lost four crew in a squall. Nine bodies, and they weren't dressed for a squall. Two of them were in civilian clothes. One was a woman. And every one of them—" she made herself finish— "had been dead longer than the official sinking. Weeks, maybe. Long enough that the sea had already started its accounting."

The tide whispered against the flats. Zephyrine sat with his elbows on his knees, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to someone whose story he'd been told secondhand.

"Why didn't you report it?" His voice was rough.

"To whom?" She laughed, short and without humor. "The navy that sealed the site? The harbormaster who takes the fleet director to church? Ivo Marchetti's boats were working that shoal within a month of the sinking, 'salvaging,' and everything they lifted went into holds that never got inventoried. You don't report a thing like that, Zeph. You either forget it or you carry it. I carried it." She stood, brushing sand from her legs, and now she did face him head-on, because the sideways part was done. "And now I'm watching you carry something too, and draw triangles, and sell your regulators, and lie to me about an empty locker, and I need to know which kind of trouble you're in. Because there are two kinds on this coast. The kind that drowns you, and the kind that drowns everyone standing near you."

* * *

For a long moment he didn't answer, and she understood that whatever he said next would cost him, and that he was doing the arithmetic of cost the way he did everything—silently, precisely, with no expression spent on it.

"It's a map," he said finally.

The word fell between them like a dropped tool.

"A dying prospector gave it to me. Augustin Vex—you might remember him, the old man who used to buy diesel off Teo and talk to gulls. He's dead now, or near enough. He had bearings to a second wreck, deeper in the shoals than the sealed one. Deeper than anything the fleet's touched." Zephyrine's jaw worked. "The bearings are incomplete. They only resolve against a master survey chart, and the only master survey on this coast is aboard the *Cormorant*."

Delphine felt the pieces click together, ugly and inevitable. "So the navy isn't guarding a grave."

"The navy is guarding a search. They sank that ship—or someone did—to stop whatever it was carrying, and now they can't find the rest of it, and neither can Marchetti. And Vex's map says the rest of it is still down there." He looked up at her, and for the first time in three years she saw him entirely unmasked—not calm, not blank, but hungry, and frightened of his own hunger, the way a starving man is frightened of bread. "I know how this sounds."

"It sounds like a man about to do something desperate," Delphine said. "It sounds like a man who's been measuring miles for three years and has finally picked a destination." She crouched so they were level, eye to eye. "Here's what I know about destinations, Zeph. My father chased one his whole life—a wreck off Dominica he swore held Spanish silver. Died with the chart in his hand and the boat unpaid for. A destination is just a way of spending the present on a future that doesn't love you back."

"And what would you have me do?" he asked. "Forget it? Go back to lifting engine parts for tourists' salvage money until the dry season closes the shoals and I do this for another year, and another?" Something cracked in his voice, hairline-thin. "You don't know what I left behind. You don't know what it costs me to stay invisible. Some prices you pay forever, Delphine. This map is the first thing that's ever offered change."

"No," she said. "This map is the first thing that's ever offered you a trade, and you haven't read the fine print yet, because you're looking at the treasure and not at the water between here and there." She stood. "Marchetti owns this coast. The navy does his patrolling whether they admit it or not. You go near that survey chart, you go near that deep wreck, and you won't be a diver anymore. You'll be a problem. And problems on this coast get solved."

"I know."

"You don't know. Knowing is what I did when I swam into that corridor and counted nine bodies. Knowing is cheap. Surviving knowing is expensive." She took a breath, and made the offer before she could talk herself out of it, because this was the part that scared her—not the wreck, not Marchetti, but the words coming out of her own mouth. "If you do this, you don't do it alone. That's not a question. You've been alone for three years and it's made you stupid in exactly one direction, and I'm going to be that direction's chaperone."

He stared at her. "Delphine—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it's dangerous. Don't tell me to stay out of it. Don't give me the speech where you protect me by excluding me, because I've watched you rehearse it and it's a bad speech." She turned and started back across the flats toward the dock, then stopped, because there was one more thing, and partners told each other things even when the things were terrible. "One more piece of fine print, since we're trading truths. When I dove that wreck—when I was in that corridor with the nine—one of the bodies wore a fleet uniform. Not navy. Fleet. Marchetti's company colors on the shoulder patch, rotted but readable." She watched him absorb it. "Whatever sank that ship, Marchetti was there, or his people were, and whatever's on the deeper wreck is worth killing for, because somebody already did."

The bruise on the horizon had thickened while they talked. The dry season was coming, six tides at most, and the water between here and the deep shoal was already filling up with everything that wanted them not to cross it.

Zephyrine gathered his tide table, folded it once, and slid it inside his shirt against his chest, where people keep the things they've decided to die for.

"There's something else you should know," he said. "Tomorrow I'm going to try to board the *Cormorant*."

Delphine closed her eyes. Above them the first front-runners of the season—clouds like gray sails—came up over the rim of the sea, and she thought: *there goes the counting. From today we count hours.*
