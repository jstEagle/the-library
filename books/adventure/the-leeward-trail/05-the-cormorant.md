## 5. The Cormorant

The Cormorant rode at anchor off Port Anse like a knife laid flat on the water—white hull, black boot-top, a crane arm folded against her superstructure with the patience of something that had learned to wait. She was bigger than the harbor deserved. Two hundred feet of salvage tender, survey masts bristling, a helipad aft scarred with tie-down rings, and along her rail the kind of floodlight arrays that could turn night into confession.

Zephyrine stood on the quay in the gray hour before dawn and studied her the way he'd once studied reefs: for the way she breathed. The rhythm of her deck crew. Where the gangway watch changed. Which portholes glowed at four in the morning and which went dark—the survey room would be one that never slept, because charts were made by men who worked while everyone else dreamed.

He'd spent two days becoming someone else. It was a craft he knew well; three years of it had honed him finer than any dive knife. His papers said Étienne Roche, contract diver out of Saint-Pierre, certified for deep salvage, available immediately. The certification was real—it belonged to a man who had died of fever in Dominica two seasons back, a man roughly his build whose documents Teo had acquired through channels Zephyrine preferred not to imagine. The face above the papers was his own, but faces change in ten years. Salt does that. Grief does that. He'd counted on both.

What he hadn't counted on—and this, later, he would count as the first true mistake of the race—was Ivo Marchetti's memory.

* * *

The gangway watch was a boy of maybe nineteen with a clipboard and a hangover, and Zephyrine walked up it at 0640 with a canvas tool bag over his shoulder and the particular gait of a man who has been summoned and resents the early hour. Confidence, he had learned, was not swagger. Swagger drew eyes. Confidence was the assumption that you belonged where you stood, worn so plainly it became invisible.

"Roche," he said before the boy could ask. "Contract dive team. Marchetti's office sent for me Tuesday."

The boy checked his list. Zephyrine watched his finger slide down the column and prepared, in the space between heartbeats, to be either on it or not. Teo had a cousin who did clerical work for the fleet's shore agent; whether the name had been inserted or whether Zephyrine was about to improvise, he didn't know. That ignorance was part of the design. A man who doesn't know his own script can't be caught reciting it wrong.

"You're expected in the survey room at seven," the boy said, and waved him through.

So the cousin had earned whatever Teo paid. Zephyrine climbed aboard the Cormorant and felt the deck take his weight with the faint give of a working ship, and something in his chest tightened—not fear, not yet, but the old animal awareness that he was now inside the thing that wanted him dead or gone, and everything from here was borrowed time measured in footsteps.

The ship smelled of diesel and brine and, underneath, something institutional: floor wax, coffee, the particular staleness of government-adjacent money. Navy contracts left a smell. He'd worked enough sealed sites to know it.

A bosun with forearms like mooring line pointed him aft. "Survey room's midships, below. You'll sign in with the mate. Don't touch anything on the tables."

"I'm a diver," Zephyrine said. "I don't touch anything that isn't mine."

The bosun grunted, which might have been approval.

* * *

The survey room was everything Vex's map had promised it would explain.

It occupied the compartment directly beneath the bridge—a long room, windowless, lit by overhead fluorescents that buzzed at a frequency just shy of headache. Along the port side ran a bank of drafting tables under glass covers, each holding a chart weighted at the corners with brass bars. Along the starboard side, cabinets with combination locks, and above them a pegboard of instruments: sextants, parallel rules, depth sounders, a hydrophone rig coiled like something sleeping.

And in the center, dominating the room the way an altar dominates a church, a plotting table.

Zephyrine signed the log with Étienne Roche's careful hand and let his eyes do what his hands must not. The plotting table held a chart of the Leeward Shoals at a scale that made Vex's map look like a child's drawing. Every channel, every sand spur, every coral head named and numbered. And across the eastern deep—past the navy-sealed wreck, past the marked limits of the fleet's licensed ground—a region of blank paper with a single penciled annotation in red:

**SURVEY PENDING — RESTRICTED — REF MARCHETTI**

Restricted meant someone above Marchetti had told him to stay out. Survey pending meant Marchetti intended to go in anyway, when the tide of permission turned. And the red pencil sat almost exactly where Vex's wax-cloth bearings resolved into nothing without a second chart—which meant the master survey, the one chart in the world that could complete Vex's map, was either in those locked cabinets or rolled in the tube rack beside the door.

There were forty tubes in the rack. He had perhaps six minutes.

He moved to the drafting tables first, because a man signing in as a diver had a plausible reason to study bathymetry and no reason at all to open cabinet number three. The charts under glass were fleet work: salvage plots, wreck dispositions, the navy site itself rendered in loving detail with its exclusion ring drawn in the same bureaucratic blue the government used for things it wanted forgotten. He memorized the seal coordinates. He memorized the crane schedule pinned beside the door—diving rotations, tender assignments, the names of the fleet's boat captains. Information was ballast; you took all you could carry.

Then the mate looked away toward the radio, and Zephyrine slid the fourth tube from the rack.

He unrolled it on the empty table at the room's end, weighting the corners with his palms, and gave himself thirty seconds. Master survey, Leeward Shoals, full hydrographic detail. His eyes went straight to the eastern deep and began transcribing—not with a pencil, which would be noticed, but with the method his father had taught him on fishing charts when he was nine years old: fix the reference points first. Two headlands. The distinctive double reef. Then the depths, read fast, committed in strings of numbers the way other men remembered phone numbers. Forty-one fathoms on the bearing. Thirty-eight. The shelf break at—

"Roche."

The voice came from the doorway behind him, and it was not the mate's.

Zephyrine finished reading the depth—thirty-six, with a hard rise beyond—and let the tube roll closed under his hands before he turned. The motion cost him nothing outwardly. Inwardly, every nerve he owned had gone to battle stations.

Ivo Marchetti stood in the doorway with a coffee cup in one hand and ten years of Zephyrine's life in the other.

He was smaller than his reputation. Fleet directors were supposed to be big men, loud men, men built like their cranes. Marchetti was compact and neat, silver at the temples, dressed in a white shirt open at the collar, and his eyes were the problem. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the eyes of a man who read people the way Zephyrine read water—for current, for pressure, for what lay beneath the surface color.

"You're early," Marchetti said. "That's rare, in your trade."

"The tide doesn't wait."

"No." Marchetti crossed the room slowly, and Zephyrine registered the geometry of it: the mate suddenly very busy at the far end, the door behind Marchetti, the door beside the instrument cabinets. One exit worth having. "Neither does the dry season. We're racing it, you and I. All of us."

Zephyrine held the silence a half-beat too long, then filled it the way Roche would. "My orders said survey support. Nobody's briefed me yet."

"They will." Marchetti set his coffee on the plotting table, directly on the restricted chart, and Zephyrine understood that the disrespect was deliberate—a man marking territory. "Tell me about yourself, Roche. Saint-Pierre, is it? Deep work mostly?"

"Mostly."

"For whom?"

"Whoever pays. It's why they call it contract."

Something flickered at the corner of Marchetti's mouth—not quite a smile. He picked up the coffee again and drank, watching Zephyrine over the rim, and the pale eyes did their slow work, moving from his face to his hands and back. Hands, Zephyrine thought. It's always the hands. A dockworker's hands, a clerk's hands—they tell the truth the mouth won't. His own were a diver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, and there were a thousand divers in the islands with hands like his, and only one of them had done what he'd done, and Marchetti was deciding, right now, with the patience of a man stirring sugar, whether the face matched the file.

"You've worked the islands long?" Marchetti asked.

"Long enough."

"Ever work Puerto Rico?"

The question dropped into the room like a weight into still water, and Zephyrine felt the shockwave pass through him and out, and kept his face level by an act of will that cost more than any dive he'd ever made. Ten years. Ten years since anyone had said those words to him looking for an answer rather than making conversation.

"Once," he said. "Harbor work. Fajardo. Nothing deep."

"Fajardo." Marchetti nodded slowly. "West coast man myself, mostly. Mayagüez." He pronounced it carefully, the Spanish rolling soft in his mouth, and set the cup down. "Bad season, some years, that coast. Bad accidents. Divers lost. Records misplaced." He paused. "Files, even."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the bridge overhead, four steps, unhurried. Zephyrine stood in the eye of it and understood with perfect clarity that Marchetti had known since the moment he walked in—that the contract order, the name on the list, the whole convenient arrangement might have been bait, or might have been coincidence, and that it no longer mattered which, because the pale eyes had already found what they were looking for.

"You have my face confused with someone," Zephyrine said quietly.

"I never confuse faces." Marchetti came closer, close enough that Zephyrine could smell the coffee and the starch of his shirt. "Ten years ago I read an incident report out of Mayagüez. A diving fatality. A falsified depth log. A diver who walked out of the inquiry before it concluded and vanished south." The pale eyes held steady. "The report had a photograph attached. Younger man. Different hair. But faces—" he tapped his own temple with one finger— "faces keep."

Every instinct Zephyrine owned screamed run. Run was the old reflex, the Mayagüez reflex, the one that had carried him onto a ferry with a duffel bag and a new name and nothing else. But run was also death, here, now, in a locked compartment forty feet beneath a hostile deck, and so instead he did the harder thing: he stood still, and he let the cold move through him until it became something usable. Fear, refined, is focus. Delphine had taught him that without ever saying it—she dove wrecks with her heart rate steady and called it respect, not courage.

"You should check your files again," Zephyrine said. "That diver drowned."

"Did he." Marchetti smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing Zephyrine had seen in ten years, because it was patient. "Funny. So does everyone, eventually, in my business. The sea keeps excellent records. Better than governments." He stepped back, and his voice lifted into something ordinary, administrative, pitched for the mate's ears. "Roche—you'll dive the north site tomorrow, first rotation. Report to the bosun. Welcome aboard the Cormorant."

But his eyes, as Zephyrine gathered his tool bag and walked the length of the survey room, said something else entirely. They said: *I know where you're going, and I will be there first.*

* * *

He did not run down the corridor. Running was confirmation. He walked, at the pace of a tired contract man heading for breakfast, past the galley with its smell of fried bread, past the engine-room hatch breathing heat, up the ladder to the main deck and the clean gray morning wind. Behind him the Cormorant went about its business—crane tested, generators thrumming, a diver's bell being swung out for inspection—and none of it, he knew now, was about salvage.

At the gangway the boy with the clipboard glanced up. "Done already?"

"Finished what I came for," Zephyrine said, and it was truer than the boy could know.

He waited until he was three streets into Port Anse before he allowed himself to think, really think, standing in the fish market's noise with the morning catch bleeding on the scales around him. He laid it out the way he'd laid out the chart, point by point.

Marchetti knew. Not suspected—knew, or knew enough that certainty and suspicion made no practical difference. Which meant the quiet hunt had already begun, and every hour Zephyrine spent in port was an hour the Cormorant spent closing on the eastern deep. Marchetti had the master survey in his own cabinets; he needed no map, only permission, and permission was a matter of paperwork and patience unless—unless he decided he couldn't afford patience. Unless a diver asking questions in his survey room convinced him the clock mattered more than the signatures.

Zephyrine had gone aboard to steal a head start. He had come out having started a race he'd meant to begin in secret, with the starting gun fired in front of the only man who could beat him.

And yet. He turned his hand over and looked at his palm as if the numbers might still be written there. *Forty-one. Thirty-eight. Thirty-six.* The shelf break, the hard rise, the double reef off the western headland. He had the depths. He had the references. Combined with Vex's bearings, scrawled in a dying man's hand on waxed cloth, they might resolve to a position within a cable's length—and a cable's length was findable, if you were willing to search bottom in water that deep with the dry season coming down on you like a lid.

Marchetti had better charts. Zephyrine had a promise, a deadline, and nothing left to lose, which was its own kind of chart.

He bought a paper of salt fish he didn't want, to have a reason for standing there, and watched the harbor. The Cormorant's launch was lowering away. Two men in it, and one of them small and neat, silver at the temples, coming ashore with a folded paper in his breast pocket—to the harbormaster, most likely, or the police annex, or the telegraph office. Making calls. Pulling threads. Quietly posting a price on a diver's whereabouts, the way men like Marchetti did everything: below the waterline, where no one could see the work.

Zephyrine turned away and walked, unhurried, toward the south end of town where Teo kept his boats, and behind his calm his mind was running numbers at speed: tides remaining, fuel, the hours it would take to strip his camp and get the skiff to open water before the net finished closing. Cold fear, sharpening. By the time he reached the sea wall it had become something cleaner—focus, direction, the terrible clarity of a man who knows he is hunted and has decided exactly where he is going.

Behind him, out in the roadstead, the Cormorant weighed anchor.

She came around slowly, her crane arm swinging like a compass needle finding north, and stood out of the harbor on a heading that took her—not toward the navy seal, not toward the fleet's licensed ground—but east. Toward the deep shoal. Toward the blank space on the chart where Vex's map ended and the truth began.

The race, Zephyrine thought, watching her wake lengthen across the bay, had already started. And he was losing.
