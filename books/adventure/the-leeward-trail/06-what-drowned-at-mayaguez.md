## 6. What Drowned at Mayagüez

The sea off Mayagüez was the color of hammered tin that morning, and Zephyrine Reyes had lied about it before he ever got wet.

He remembered that with a clarity that never dulled, never softened, never once in ten years let him pretend it hadn't happened exactly the way it happened. The sea report. The depth log. The two small lines of ink he wrote himself, standing on the deck of the *Doña Elena* while Tomás Oquendo checked their tanks a second time because something in the water made him uneasy and Tomás always trusted his unease.

"Bottom's at forty," Zephyrine had said, tapping the log. "Forty-two, maybe. Easy work. We're just running the survey line."

Tomás had looked at the log, then at the water, then at Zephyrine. He was thirty-one years old then—two years older than Zephyrine—and he had a wife named Marisol who packed his lunches in cloth napkins and a daughter who was learning to whistle through her front teeth, the gap where the new tooth hadn't come in yet. He had a scar on his left forearm from a moray eel and a habit of humming boleros underwater, tuneless little vibrations that carried through a regulator like a heartbeat.

"You sure?" Tomás said. "It looks deeper."

"It's forty-two," Zephyrine said. "I checked it twice."

He had not checked it at all. He had written the number because the number needed to be there—for the contract, for the client, for the record that would show Zephyrine Reyes completing the deepest survey line of the season without a safety stop overage, without a decompression penalty, without a single mark against the flawless profile he was building toward a certification that would take him out of coastal salvage and into deep-water recovery, where the money lived, where the respect lived, where the man he intended to become lived.

The real bottom was sixty-eight meters.

* * *

They went down at seven-forty in the morning, into water that went from tin to green to a darkness that arrived too early, the way night arrives in a room when someone draws a curtain. Zephyrine led. Tomás followed at his shoulder, humming.

Zephyrine could still map every meter of that descent if you woke him from a dead sleep and asked. The drop-off at thirty meters where the reef shelf ended and the wall began. The thermocline at forty-five, cold as a hand closing around the back of the neck. The moment—he could not say at what depth, that was part of the lie's cruelty, the lie had taken even his bearings from him—the moment Tomás's humming stopped.

He turned. Tomás was looking at his gauge. Then at Zephyrine. Then down, into the blue-black below them, where the actual bottom lay waiting, twenty-six meters deeper than any number Zephyrine had put on paper.

Tomás didn't panic. That was the thing Zephyrine could never make anyone understand, later, at the inquiry, when they asked him to describe the dive. Tomás Oquendo did not panic. He pointed downward, then at his own gauge, then made the slow, flat gesture—the one that meant *we go up now, we go up slowly, we do this right*—and began his ascent, calm as a man climbing stairs, because Tomás believed the way you survived the sea was by doing things correctly, one step at a time, no matter what the paper said.

And Zephyrine, ten meters above him already, rising too fast because his own body understood before his mind admitted what he'd done—Zephyrine watched him go up correctly. Watched him stop at the intervals. Watched him hang in the green light doing his decompression stops like a man keeping an appointment, faithful to a schedule that was built on a false number, faithful to a partner who had fed him that number with a straight face and steady hands.

At fifteen meters, Tomás stopped moving.

Zephyrine saw it happen from above. Saw the small confusion in the set of his shoulders. Saw him look at his gauge again, and this time hold it, and this time the calm broke—not into thrashing, nothing so useful as thrashing, but into a terrible stillness, the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. Bends. Nitrogen loading his blood like soda fizzing in a sealed bottle, bubbles forming in the places bubbles should never form, because the dive profile on the log said this ascent was safe and his body knew better and he had trusted the wrong one.

Zephyrine went down to him. Of course he went down to him. He took Tomás's arm and they came up together, fast, too fast, breaking every rule a second time because the first rule had already been broken and broken rules multiply like wounds infect. They surfaced and Tomás was gray-lipped and slurring his words and laughing, actually laughing, saying *Marisol's going to kill me, I told her I'd be home for lunch,* and Zephyrine held him upright in the swells and screamed at the *Doña Elena* until the deckhand heard and the boat came and they hauled Tomás aboard and ran for Mayagüez harbor with the engine wide open.

Tomás Oquendo died in the hyperbaric chamber at four hours and eleven minutes after surfacing. The technician said he was talking near the end. Not words anyone could make out. Maybe a bolero. Maybe a name.

Maybe a lunch order for a woman who waited with a cloth napkin folded around an empanada, going cold on a table in a house in Aguadilla, while a six-year-old girl whistled through the gap in her teeth and asked when Papa was coming home.

* * *

The inquiry took nine days.

Zephyrine learned things in those nine days about how institutions grieve, which is to say: how they protect themselves. The diving contractor wanted no finding of negligence—their insurance hung on it. The client wanted no finding of falsified survey data—their contract hung on it. The port authority wanted the whole file thin enough to staple. Everyone in the hearing room in Mayagüez had an interest in the truth being smaller than it was, and Zephyrine discovered, sitting in the witness chair with his hands flat on his knees, that he was the only person present whose interest required the truth to stay whole.

So he did the arithmetic of cowardice. He weighed it. The full truth: *I wrote the depth myself. I never sounded the bottom. I falsified the log to protect my record.* Against the partial truth: *the log showed forty-two meters; I dived per the log; the accident appears to have involved a miscalculated ascent profile.*

The partial truth fit neatly inside everyone else's partial truths, like ballast stones seated in a hull. It sank cleanly. It found its resting place on the seafloor of the record, and the inquiry closed with a finding of *tragic operational error, contributing factors environmental,* and a recommendation for improved gauge calibration procedures, and Tomás Oquendo's death was filed under weather.

Except.

There was a man at the inquiry—a consultant, brought in by Marisol's lawyer, a salvor-turned-investigator with a reputation for pulling drowned facts out of drowned wrecks. Heavyset, silver-templed, with an accent that had crossed more than one ocean and eyes that read documents the way other men read faces. His name was Marchetti. Ivo Marchetti. He sat at the back of the hearing room for all nine days and never said a word until the last afternoon, when he stood during the recess and walked past Zephyrine's chair and stopped, close enough that Zephyrine could smell coffee and salt on him.

"The bottom off that shelf is sixty-eight meters," Marchetti said quietly. "Every fisherman between Mayagüez and Aguadilla knows it. Your log says forty-two." He looked at Zephyrine the way a man looks at a chart he has already memorized. "Somebody wrote that number, Reyes. Paper doesn't sound itself."

Then he walked out, and Zephyrine sat alone in the emptying hearing room with the fan turning overhead and the finding of *environmental factors* drying into official ink, and understood that somewhere in a filing cabinet there was now a document with his name on it that would never fully close. A file. A thin one, maybe. A marginal note. But files are like anchors—they don't have to be heavy to hold you. They only have to be attached.

* * *

He stayed three more months. He told himself it was to settle affairs, but affairs don't take three months, and the truth was he couldn't leave because leaving felt like the second half of the crime. Then Marisol came to see him.

She came to the yard where he worked, on a Sunday, in her church clothes, with the little girl holding her hand and sucking on a frozen paleta that stained her mouth cherry-red. Zephyrine saw them coming across the gravel and his whole body went cold, colder than the thermocline, colder than anything the sea had ever done to him.

Marisol stood in front of him. She did not raise her voice. She did not weep. She looked at him with an expression he would spend ten years trying to drown and never quite succeed, and she said:

"They told me it was the water. They told me it was nobody." She glanced down at her daughter, then back. "But I know my husband. He checked everything twice. He would not have gone down on a bad number unless somebody he trusted gave him one."

The little girl looked up at Zephyrine and smiled, gap-toothed, cherry-mouthed, and waved.

He did not remember answering Marisol. He did not remember what he said, or whether he said anything at all. What he remembered was the paleta stick falling into the gravel, and the two of them walking away down the row of moored boats, getting smaller, and the understanding arriving in his chest like water filling a boot: *there is no penance here. There is no sentence you can serve that gives her back her husband. There is only distance, and you owe her all of it.*

He left Puerto Rico that week. He sold his gear—kept one mask, one knife, one slate—and took a berth on a freighter south, and when the captain asked his name he gave the name of a cousin who had died young, and when the freighter stopped at Saint-Pierre he stepped off and kept walking, and he did not stop walking for a long time.

That was how the miles started. Not as travel. As measurement. Every mile he put between himself and Mayagüez was a unit of penance he could count, and counting was the only prayer he trusted anymore. Miles to the next island. Miles of survey line. Miles of bottom time converted to fathoms, fathoms to leagues, leagues to a life measured entirely in distance covered rather than time lived—because time was what Tomás had lost, and Zephyrine had no right to spend it, so he spent distance instead, and called the spending discipline.

Three years ago he had washed up, more or less literally, at Leeward Shoals, and found the one trade where a man could work alone, live alone, and be paid for going down into places where names didn't follow. Salvage. The perfect profession for the self-buried. The sea asks no history; it only asks your hands and your air, and it pays in objects that belong to no one, recovered by a man who belongs to no one, and both parties find the arrangement honest.

He had almost convinced himself it was peace.

* * *

Now, lying in his bunk at Port Anse with Vex's wax-cloth map folded under his shirt and the lamp turned low, Zephyrine did the thing he did perhaps twice a year: he took out the memory of Mayagüez and looked at it straight, the way you check a wound for infection.

Ten years. Ten years of clean dives, honest logs, numbers sounded twice and written true. He had rebuilt himself the way you rebuild a wrecked hull—plank by plank, caulk by caulk, knowing the ship would never again be what it was, only seaworthy. And it had held. It had held through storms and salvage seasons and the long grey silence of the shoals, and he had begun to believe—almost—that the ballast was finally stowed, that shame was cargo you could lash down and carry without it steering the ship.

But tonight he had seen a name on a corroded locker and lied to Delphine about it, and tomorrow he would need to board a salvage tender wearing a borrowed identity, and somewhere in the fleet's records—Marchetti's records—there was a file with his name on it, ten years old, never closed. *Paper doesn't sound itself.* The man had known in nine days what the inquiry took nine days not to find, and men like that did not forget, did not misfile, did not let a leash go slack just because the dog had run far.

And beneath the fear, quieter and worse, sat the other thing. The thing with a gap in its teeth and a cherry-stained mouth. Because Vex had asked him for a promise today—a promise about bones and land and being claimed by somewhere—and Zephyrine had heard himself say yes, yes, the way he had once heard himself say *it's forty-two, I checked it twice.* Promises were how the damage started. Promises were numbers written on paper for other people to trust with their lives. He had sworn off them the way he'd sworn off falsified logs, and here he was, ten years later, signing another one in the dark.

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw the green of the descent, the wall, the place where the humming stopped.

Tomorrow, the *Cormorant*. Tomorrow, a face that might be remembered. He rolled onto his side and did what he always did when the ballast shifted: he counted miles. Port Anse to the shoals, four. Shoals to the deep water, nine. Nine miles of open sea between him and a wreck that might hold everything, guarded by a man who held the one document that could end him.

Outside, the tide turned against the pilings, patient as a debt coming due.
