## 7. Borrowed Boat

The smuggler's dock sat behind a ruined salt works on the windward side of Port Anse, where the mangroves had grown through the walls of the old evaporation pans and the air smelled of brine and rot and diesel. Zephyrine came in on foot, before dawn, carrying everything he owned in the trade in two canvas bags: his dive harness, his brass regulator rebuilt twice over, the good mask with the prescription ground in Saint-Denis, three hundred feet of braided line, the lift bags, the crowbar with the chiseled tip, and the little anvil-weighted slate he'd used for ten years to write his findings in water.

He set the bags down on the planks and waited.

Teo appeared the way he always appeared, out of a doorway that hadn't looked like a doorway, a small man with a boxer's flattened nose and hands that were soft as a priest's. He looked at the gear for a long time without touching it. That was Teo's way. He made you watch him not touching your life.

"Regulator's old," he said.

"Rebuilt in March."

"Everything's rebuilt in March. March is when men rebuild things." He crouched, turned the harness over with one finger, checked the stitching at the shoulder joints, the buckle tongues. "Line's good. Slate's good. Mask—" He held it up to the gray light, squinted through it. "You're blind as a bat without this."

"I manage."

"You manage badly." Teo set the mask down with something close to gentleness. "You know what you're asking for. Not just the boat."

"I know."

"The skiff is nothing. I have three skiffs. The silence is the expensive part. A week of me not hearing things, not saying things, not answering when certain people ask me certain questions." He straightened up and looked at Zephyrine the way he looked at cargo he suspected of being alive. "A week of silence costs more than the gear. The gear is what you're giving me so I can pretend the arithmetic works out."

"Then pretend."

Teo laughed, a short bark with no joy in it. He walked the length of the dock to where the skiff sat low on its mooring lines, a gray-hulled thing with no name and no registration and an engine under a canvas shroud that Zephyrine knew was worth more than everything in his two bags combined. Teo pulled the canvas off and patted the cowling like a man touching a horse he loved.

"Forty knots in flat water. Thirty in the chop you'll find out there. Range to cross to Dominica and back twice if you don't push her." He turned. "She has no name because a name is a question. You don't answer questions for a week. You don't fuel at the town dock. You fuel at the point, at Baptiste's, and you pay cash, and you tell Baptiste nothing, because Baptiste tells me everything, and I will be pretending not to know it. Are we clear?"

"We're clear."

"And the deep shoal." Teo said it lightly, the way a man says a woman's name to see if her husband flinches. Zephyrine didn't flinch, and Teo nodded slowly, as if a theory had been confirmed. "You're asking about the deep shoal. Don't bother lying. You traded me your eyes, Zeph. A man doesn't give up his eyes for a boat he means to fish from."

Zephyrine said nothing. The mangroves clicked and dripped behind them. Somewhere out in the channel, the first ferry of the morning ground its way toward the harbor, and its wake came in flat and gray against the pilings.

"Sit down," Teo said.

They sat on the edge of the dock with their feet hanging over the water, which was what men did in Port Anse when they were about to say something that would cost them. Teo took a tin of cigarettes from his shirt pocket, shook one out, and didn't light it. He just held it, rolling it between his fingers, an old habit from the years he'd been trying to quit.

"Three days ago," Teo said, "a man named Prosper Lan came to the point. You knew him?"

"I knew of him. Prospector. Worked the outer banks."

"He'd been working the outer banks for years and finding nothing, and then three weeks ago he came into money. Small money, but real. Bought a new tank compressor, paid his bar tabs, bought his mother a stove. And he started asking questions. Quiet ones. The kind you ask over rum, one at a time, so nobody notices the shape of them all together." Teo rolled the cigarette between his fingers. "He was asking about the deep shoal, Zeph. About water depth and bottom type and whether the old charts marked a rise out there. About whether anyone had ever tried to run a line to it."

The water under the dock was the color of old pewter. A crab moved sideways along a piling and stopped, feeling the vibration of their voices through the wood.

"And?" Zephyrine said.

"And two nights ago his boat was found drifting off the outer banks with the tanks still lashed down and his mask on the deck. Dry. He wasn't in it." Teo finally lit the cigarette, and the match flared orange in the gray. "They found him yesterday, on the falling tide, caught in the wire coral off Grosse Pointe. Drowned. A man who'd been diving these banks for fifteen years, drowned in six fathoms of water he knew like his own kitchen."

"Could've been a cramp. Could've been his heart."

"Could've been." Teo exhaled smoke. "Except the fleet posted a quiet bounty the same morning. Not posted — that's the wrong word. *Circulated.* A thousand dollars to any captain or harbor man who reports a diver asking after the deep shoal. Five thousand if the diver can be held until the fleet's people arrive. It's not written anywhere. It's in the way bartenders stop talking when you walk in. It's in the way the fuel dock at the town pier suddenly needs to see papers. You can't see a thing like that, Zeph. You can only feel the water getting colder."

Zephyrine watched the crab work its way down the piling and slip under. Prosper Lan. He'd seen him twice, years ago, across a bar and across an auction floor — a big soft-spoken man with reef scars on his forearms who bought his tanks secondhand and treated them like pets. A man who'd found something, or a piece of something, and had been too slow or too proud or too hopeful about what finding it would cost him.

Vex had seen the same map Prosper Lan had asked about. Vex had traded it away the day before he died, and Vex was dead of his lungs and not of the water, and the difference between those two deaths was a coin toss Zephyrine had won without knowing he'd flipped it.

"You think it was the fleet," Zephyrine said.

"I think the fleet is a company with a director who smiles too much and a payroll that includes men whose job titles don't exist. I think a man drowned with a dry mask on his deck. And I think you're going out there anyway, because you've already traded me your eyes, and men don't do that for a boat they mean to keep tied up." Teo stubbed the cigarette out on the plank and put the butt in his pocket, because Teo never littered, Teo never left a trace of anything. "So here is my silence, and here is what it buys, and here is what it doesn't."

"Tell me."

"It buys you the skiff and a week of me being deaf and dumb. It doesn't buy you Baptiste's silence, or the barkeeps', or the ferry crew's, or the harbor master's, and the harbor master eats out of the fleet's hand since Marchetti came. It doesn't buy back Prosper Lan's death, and it doesn't buy you a different ending. You understand what I'm telling you? The price already went up. It went up in six fathoms off Grosse Pointe, and it'll go up again, and the next man who pays it might be paying with more than his eyes."

Zephyrine looked at the skiff. Gray hull, no name, an engine that could outrun anything the fleet kept at the anchorage except maybe the fast launch he'd seen once at dusk, running dark, running quiet, the kind of boat a company buys when it wants to arrive somewhere without being asked why.

"I need the gear to come back to me," he said. "When the week's up. If I come back."

Teo looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind the flattened face — not warmth exactly, but the memory of it, the way a wreck shows through clear water.

"If you come back," Teo said, "the gear's in the loft over the salt works, wrapped in the blue tarp, where it's been since you handed it to me. If you don't come back, I sell it in Martinique and drink the proceeds, and I will say a kind word about you while I drink, and it will be the only kind word anyone says." He stood, and offered his soft priest's hand, and Zephyrine took it. "Fuel at Baptiste's. Tell him nothing. And Zeph — the tide turns at nine. Whatever you're racing, race it now."

* * *

He ran north with the sun coming up on his port side and the islands stacked blue and gray along the horizon like a fleet of their own. The skiff handled the way Teo had promised, cutting the morning chop with a low, even thrum, and Zephyrine kept her outside the traffic lanes, outside the fishing grounds, outside the sightlines of the anchorage where the Cormorant sat fat and white with her survey cranes folded like sleeping birds.

He'd spent the night before at his camp doing arithmetic, because rage was a luxury and arithmetic was a tool. Marchetti had recognized him. That meant the file was open. That meant the fleet's director — a man who owned survey ships and salvage contracts and, if Teo was right, men without job titles — had a ten-year-old incident report from Puerto Rico with Zephyrine Reyes's name on it and a photograph that was still, at a distance and in bad light, his face. Marchetti hadn't moved on him yet. That was the part Zephyrine kept turning over. A man with that kind of hold doesn't wait out of mercy. He waits because he's deciding what the hold is worth, or because he's waiting for you to lead him to something. Prosper Lan had led someone to something, and Prosper Lan was in the wire coral.

So the race had a clock inside the clock. Six tides until the dry season closed the shoals — and inside that, a shorter clock, the one that ran from the moment Marchetti decided the file was worth cashing in. Zephyrine had bought a week of silence from Teo. He wondered how many days of silence Marchetti had bought from everyone else.

At Baptiste's fuel point, a concrete slab under a leaning palm at the northern tip of the island, he paid cash and said nothing and Baptiste, a heavy silent man who pumped fuel like a man administering last rites, said nothing back, and both of them performed the whole transaction like a mime show, and it was perfect.

He anchored in a pocket cove through the middle of the day, when the fleet's patrol circuit would pass the outer banks, and slept in two-hour shifts with the duplicate chart — the tracing he'd made of Vex's map before leaving it in a waterproof sleeve inside his camp's false floor — spread on the skiff's sole under his hand. Bearings to a rise that didn't appear on any chart he'd ever bought. Soundings in an old hand, corrected in a newer one. Vex had been out there. Vex had stood over that water and written those numbers, and then Vex had spent twenty years not going back, and Zephyrine was starting to understand why. Some men don't avoid a place because there's nothing there. They avoid it because there's too much, and they're not ready to be the one who finds it.

Near dusk he moved again, running the last leg low and slow along the leeward edge of the shoal bank, and killed the engine a half mile out and let her drift while he took bearings with the hand compass and checked them against the tracing three times, four times, the way he'd checked depth logs once upon a time, and the thought arrived uninvited — *you were careful with the wrong numbers* — and he put it down the way you put down a tool that's too hot to hold.

The rise was there. He could feel it before he could confirm it: the water color changing over the bank, the swell standing up slightly where the bottom shouldered toward the light. He dropped a weighted line and counted fathoms, and the bottom came up to meet the lead twenty feet sooner than any chart said it should.

Vex hadn't been wrong. Vex had been exactly right, and dead, and the man who'd asked the same questions was in the wire coral, and somewhere behind him in the dark the fleet's fast launch was running its dusk circuit with its lights off.

Zephyrine sat in the drifting skiff with the compass in his hand and the first star coming out over the shoal, and he made himself say it plainly, the way Teo would have: the price of survival had gone up. It would keep going up. Prosper Lan had paid in full, and Augustin Vex had paid in installments, and the ledger was open now with Zephyrine's name at the top of the next page.

He started the engine and turned the skiff's bow away from the rise, out toward deep water and the dark anchorage of a cove he knew, because a man who found the wreck tonight would die tonight, and a man who was merely lost out here, fishing, running from a squall, might live to dive it tomorrow.

Behind him, far off across the black water, a single white flare went up over the outer banks — one, two, three — the fleet's signal, the one every harbor man in the Leewards knew without anyone ever teaching them.

Someone had found something. Or someone was being found.

Zephyrine opened the throttle and did not look back.
