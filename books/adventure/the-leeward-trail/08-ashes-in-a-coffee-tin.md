## 8. Ashes in a Coffee Tin

The squall came down out of the east like something with a grudge, and Zephyrine ran the skiff into the mangrove cut on the leeward side of Îlet Chancel with two minutes to spare. Rain hit the water so hard it seemed to bounce, a white roar that erased the horizon, the shoals, the fleet anchored somewhere behind him like knives in a drawer. He got the anchor over, rigged the tarp, and sat in the sudden green dark under the mangroves listening to the world get beaten flat.

He had not meant to open the tin.

It had ridden in his duffel since Port Anse, wedged between the spare regulator and a coil of line, and he had carried it the way he carried everything now — carefully, without looking at it. A coffee tin. Café Monté Carlo, the paint worn to ghosts of red and gold, the lid taped down with three layers of electrical tape gone brittle as cicada shells. Augustin Vex's bones, or what was left of them after the crematorium man's crude work: he could feel the weight of it when the skiff rolled, a soft shifting mass, lighter than it should be. Ash weighed almost nothing. That was the thing nobody told you. A whole man reduced to something you could forget you were carrying until the boat tilted.

The rain kept coming. There was nothing to do but wait it out, and waiting had always been the hardest work Zephyrine Reyes did.

He told himself he was checking the map. That was the lie he used, and it was a good one because it was half true — Vex's wax-cloth chart was folded inside the tin, tucked beneath the letters, and he did need to study it, needed to burn the bearings deeper into his memory before someone took the tin off his corpse. So he peeled the tape, worked the lid loose with a thumbnail, and the smell came up at him: coffee, faintly, decades of it soaked into the metal, and under that something sweeter and older, like a drawer in a house where someone had once been happy.

The ashes were wrapped in a flour sack. On top of them lay the letters, bound with string, maybe forty of them, envelopes gone soft at the corners from handling. *Colette Vex, Sainte-Rose, Grande-Terre.* The same address over and over, year after year, in handwriting that started tall and careful and ended cramped and slanting, the script of a man whose hands were failing faster than his pride.

Zephyrine meant to check the map. He told himself that again, firmly, the way you tell a dog to stay.

Then he opened the first letter anyway.

*Ma fille,* it began. The date was eleven years old. *I have found work on the dredges at Port Anse. The pay is honest if the hours are not. I am too old for diving and too stubborn for anything else, which you know is the Vex way.*

He read it twice. It was nothing. It was groceries and weather and a complaint about the foreman's boots. It was a man writing to prove to a daughter three islands away that he still existed, and Zephyrine understood that kind of arithmetic better than he wanted to. He almost put it back. Instead he untied the string and read the next one, and the next, and the rain hammered the mangroves and the light went from grey to green to the color of old bottle glass, and somewhere in there he stopped pretending he was doing anything but reading every word.

* * *

The letters told a story Vex had never mentioned in the flophouse, dying by inches on a mattress stained with rust marks from the bed frame above. Zephyrine had assumed the old man was alone — prospectors usually were, men who'd traded their families for a rumor of gold and spent thirty years paying the difference. But Vex had a daughter who wrote back. Some of her replies were in the bundle too, and Colette Vex's hand was quick and sure, and she had not been kind to him in the early ones.

*You left when I was nine,* she had written. *You may send all the letters you like. I am not obligated to answer them.*

And then, four years later: *Papa, I am not angry anymore. I am just tired of being angry. Write me about the water. You never write about the water, and it is the only part of your life I believe.*

So Vex had written about the water. Page after page of it, in that deteriorating hand. The color of the deep channels at dawn. The way a school of jacks will turn all at once, ten thousand bodies, like a single thought passing through them. How the wrecks sleep. *A wreck is not dead, ma fille. A wreck is a held breath. Everything on board is exactly where fear put it.* Zephyrine read that line and had to set the letter down and look at the rain for a while.

Because it was true. He knew it was true. Ten years of salvage work and he had never found the sentence for it, and a dying prospector in a flophouse had written it home to a girl who didn't dive, and she had kept it, kept all of it, kept forty letters tied with string the way other people keep jewelry.

There was one from six years ago that was different. Shorter. The handwriting had begun its final collapse.

*Colette. I have found something. I cannot say what in a letter. When I am done I will come home and we will walk up the hill together and I will show you what a man can do with one good season. Save me a place on the hill. Not beside your mother — she would not want it, and I would not presume. Just above her, where I can see the sea. Tell me there is room. Tell me the hill remembers me.*

*Tell me Sainte-Rose will take me back.*

Zephyrine read that last line four times.

The rain slackened, then came back harder, a second cell rolling through, and the mangroves creaked and groaned around the skiff like a ship working loose from its moorings. He sat with the letters spread across his knees, careful of the wet, and felt something move in his chest that he recognized and hated.

Tenderness. That was the word for it, and he resented the word, resented the feeling, resented the old man for planting it in him with nothing but paper and a dying wish. Tenderness was a leak. Tenderness was ballast you couldn't cut loose, weight you carried for no operational reason, and Zephyrine Reyes had spent ten years stripping himself of everything that wasn't operationally necessary. No home port. No real name on any lease. No woman who knew more than his schedule. He measured his life in miles because miles could be logged and closed out, and years — years were what other people accumulated, people with hills that remembered them, people who could write *tell me Sainte-Rose will take me back* without their hands shaking.

Vex had shaken. Every letter after that one shook a little worse.

And the terrible thing, the thing Zephyrine sat with while the squall spent itself against the mangroves, was that Vex had gotten his answer. He must have. Because here was the reply, near the bottom of the bundle, undated but late, in Colette's sure hand:

*There is room on the hill, Papa. There has always been room. Come home whenever you are ready. I will not ask what you found. I only ask that you come while I can still walk up with you.*

He hadn't come. Whatever he'd found — whatever the map led to, down in the deep shoal past the navy's sealed wreck — it had kept him out here six more years, grubbing the shallows, selling what he could lift, getting older and poorer and sicker, unable to go home empty-handed and unable to die until he'd earned his place on the hill. Until the last week, when dying had finally made the decision for him, and he'd traded his life's one secret to a stranger in exchange for a ride he wouldn't live to take.

*Carry my ashes to the hill at Sainte-Rose.* That was the promise. Zephyrine had said yes the way he broke promises professionally — smoothly, meaning to manage it later, meaning to find the angle. Sitting in the mangrove dark with forty letters across his knees, he understood for the first time that there was no angle. There was an old man who had wanted, more than gold, more than the thing he'd found, to be claimed by a square of grass above his wife's grave. And a daughter who had left the door open for twenty years. And a promise that was not geography at all.

The map pulled two directions. It had been pulling two directions since Port Anse, and Zephyrine had been refusing to feel it, and now, in the rain-smell and the coffee-tin smell, he felt it plainly. One bearing ran south and east, down into the deep shoal, toward the second wreck and whatever Vex had found that made a dying man weep over a wax-cloth chart. Toward money enough to vanish forever, or trouble enough to finish what Mayagüez started. The other bearing ran north, to a hillside cemetery on Grande-Terre, to a woman who had written *come while I can still walk up with you* to a father who hadn't managed it.

Both bearings ran through him. He was the instrument now. Vex had seen to that.

* * *

Near dawn the squall died and the sky came back the washed, bruised grey that precedes dry-season mornings, and Zephyrine climbed out of the mangrove cut into a sea lying flat as poured metal. He made coffee on the single burner, and while it heated he did the thing he'd been avoiding: he took the flour sack out of the tin and looked at what was actually in his boat.

Ashes. Maybe four pounds of them. A man's whole remainder, grey-white, fine as reef sand. Somewhere in there were the hands that had drawn the map, the lungs that had failed, the heart that had written *a wreck is a held breath* to a girl who believed him. Zephyrine held the sack a moment longer than necessary, then retied it, seated it back in the tin, and pressed the lid down.

"You picked a bad courier," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange after a night of silence, rough, like it belonged to someone else. "I lose things. It's what I'm good at."

The tin said nothing. Coffee tins are good at that.

He drank his coffee and studied the map one more time in the strengthening light, and this time he saw what grief and squall-darkness had blurred: the deep-shoal bearings resolved against nothing he owned. They needed the fleet's master survey — the Cormorant's charts, Marchetti's locked survey room — and every hour he waited out weather was an hour the fleet used to close the water. Teo's warning sat in him like a swallowed hook: they've posted quiet money on anyone asking after the deep shoal. Another prospector already drowned for it. Vex's secret had a body count, and Zephyrine was carrying the only remaining copy of the reason why, along with the only remaining copy of the man.

He broke camp as the sun cleared Guadeloupe's shadow and lit the shoals gold, and the skiff ran fast and clean toward the anchorage roadstead, where the Cormorant lay at her moorings with her survey antenna turning slow against the morning.

Two things, then. He could hear Vex's voice laying them out, patient, the way the old man had explained tide tables in the flophouse. First the hill — because a promise made to a dying man is the one contract even a professional breaker honors, or finds out what he's made of. Then the survey — because the map was half a map, and half a map was a dare the sea always collected on.

He throttled down at the edge of the roadstead and looked at the Cormorant for a long moment, at the white hull and the busy deck cranes and the divers' flag snapping from her mast.

Then he checked his fuel, turned the bow away from the fleet, and pointed it north — toward Sainte-Rose, toward the hill, toward a woman who had been waiting twenty years to bury her father — telling himself the detour would cost him nothing he couldn't win back.

He was lying, and he knew it. But some lies you have to sail first, to see how they handle.

Halfway across the channel, a tender with no name painted on her bow fell in behind him, holding his wake at exactly four hundred meters, and stayed there.
