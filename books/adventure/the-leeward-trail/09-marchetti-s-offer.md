## 9. Marchetti's Offer

The anchorage at Anse Fidèle was a half-moon of black water cupped between two headlands, and Zephyrine chose it because it was the kind of place a man went to disappear. Teo's skiff sat low on the swell, riding light without the salvage gear he'd traded away, and he lay in the bottom of it with the wax-cloth map folded against his chest like a second heartbeat, watching the stars wheel and counting the hours until dawn would let him run for the deep shoal.

He had been at anchor four hours when the lights came.

Not the fleet's working lamps—those were white and industrial, strung along the Cormorant's superstructure like a floating cannery. These were different. Two points of green navigation light, moving slow and deliberate through the pass between the headlands, followed by a wash of deck illumination that bloomed across the water like something surfacing. A launch. Forty feet, maybe more, running without running lights until it wanted to be seen, which meant it had never once worried about being seen.

Zephyrine did not move. That was the first discipline of his exile: stillness as craft. In ten years he had learned that men who moved first were men who were seen first, and men who were seen first off Mayagüez ended up as entries in incident reports. He lay flat against the skiff's wet planking and let his breathing go shallow and watched the launch come on with the patience of a man who had already decided there was nowhere worth running to tonight.

It came alongside without hurry. A fendered hull, varnished brightwork gone dull with salt, and standing at the rail with a glass of something amber in his hand, Ivo Marchetti.

"Reyes." The name crossed the water easily, unhurried, the way you'd call to a neighbor over a fence. "Or should I say Baptiste? I've always wondered which one you answer to."

Zephyrine sat up slowly. There was no point pretending otherwise; the man had said the name, and names once spoken over water could not be taken back. He kept his hands visible on his knees, an old habit from rooms where men carried knives.

"You have me at a disadvantage," he said. "You know both my names."

"I know a great deal about you." Marchetti gestured, and one of his crew—there were three, big men in matching dark shirts, the kind of uniform that pretended not to be a uniform—tossed a line across. It landed in the skiff's bilge and lay there coiled, an invitation or a leash. "Come aboard. Have a drink. The water's getting cold and you've been lying in the bottom of that boat since sunset like a man waiting to be arrested."

"I'm comfortable."

"You're freezing. You're also out of gear—I hear you sold your dive rig to Teo for this skiff, which is either desperation or commitment, and I'm curious which." Marchetti smiled. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, built like a man who had once been a swimmer and now merely supervised them. His voice had the flat warmth of someone who had negotiated in a dozen ports and buried men in at least two. "Ten minutes, diver. Then I'll leave you to your cold anchor and your map."

At the word *map*, something behind Zephyrine's ribs went very quiet and very still, the way the sea goes before a waterspout.

*He knows.*

Of course he knew. He had known since the Cormorant's survey room, since the moment their eyes had met over the master chart and recognition had moved through Marchetti's face like weather. Zephyrine had swum away from that ship knowing the race had started. He had simply hoped—hoped the way a drowning man hopes, irrationally, against all arithmetic—that he'd bought himself more than two days.

He took the line.

* * *

Marchetti's launch was warmer than the night had any right to allow. A cabin aft with leather benches gone soft with humidity, a chart table bolted to the deck, and a bottle of aged rum that had clearly crossed an ocean to get here. One of the dark-shirted men took the helm; another went forward; the third stood by the door with his arms crossed and the patient posture of furniture. Marchetti poured two glasses and set one on the table across from Zephyrine, then sat, unhurried, and looked at him for a long moment before speaking.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Reyes?"

"My silence."

"Your discipline." Marchetti lifted his own glass but didn't drink. "I've read the file. All of it. Mayagüez, 2014. Diver Émile Fontaine, lost at forty-one meters during a wreck assessment. Your depth log showed thirty-two. The inquiry found the discrepancy—" he paused, savoring it, a man turning a card face-up, "—but by then you'd already signed onto a freighter out of San Juan under the name Baptiste, and the case went cold because nobody spends money chasing a man who has made himself cheap."

The cabin was very warm and very quiet. Zephyrine heard his own pulse in it.

"You've done your reading," he said.

"I do my reading. It's cheaper than making mistakes." Marchetti finally drank, a small measured sip. "Here's what interests me. Most men who bury a thing like that spend the rest of their lives keeping it buried. They avoid water. They avoid wrecks. They take jobs on land and drink themselves polite. But you—" he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate, "—you went back to diving. Wreck diving. Salvage. You work the Leeward Shoals, of all the water in the Caribbean, within sight of a navy-sealed site. Do you understand how that reads, from where I sit? It doesn't read like a man hiding. It reads like a man doing penance in the exact church where he committed the sin."

"That's not what it is."

"It doesn't matter what it is. What matters is what it looks like, and what it looks like is leverage." Marchetti set his glass down and produced, from inside his jacket, a slim folder. Not thick—a dozen pages, maybe, in a government watermark. He laid it on the chart table between them but kept two fingers on it, the way a man keeps fingers on a dog's collar. "This is a copy. The original lives in a filing cabinet in San Juan that I pay rent on. Your real name, your real history, the inquiry transcript, Émile Fontaine's sister's address in case anyone ever wants to send her closure—or a summons. Ten years of your life, reduced to paper. And here is the interesting part, diver: I don't want it."

"No?"

"I want it *gone*. Which brings me to why I'm sitting in your anchorage buying rum instead of sending three men into your skiff with a gaff hook." He slid the folder an inch closer to Zephyrine, then stopped. "Augustin Vex drew you a map. Don't insult us both by denying it—he was seen leaving Port Anse with his papers in order and his face pointed at you, and yesterday morning a man named Castera who'd been asking questions about the deep shoal turned up floating off Pointe Noire with his lungs full of harbor. I don't love that part of the business, but it happens, and I'd rather it stop happening. So."

"So."

"Dive it with me. The deep wreck—the one Vex found twenty years ago and was too old and too alone to reach. My divers have the fleet's resources: surface-supplied air, decompression chambers, a tender that can hold station in anything short of a hurricane. You have the bearings. We go down together, we bring up whatever's down there together, and we split it sixty-forty in your favor." He let that number hang in the humid air. "Sixty-forty, Reyes, on a colonial cargo that could buy every boat in this archipelago. And when it's done, I burn the file. Not sell it back to you—burn it. You walk away with no past, no leash, no name in any cabinet anywhere. You can be Baptiste forever, or Reyes again, or nothing at all. Freedom, cash, and a clean ocean. All I ask for is the map."

Zephyrine looked at the folder. Under the cabin lamp its watermark caught the light, and he thought: *that's me in there. That's the worst hour of my life, laminated.* He thought of Émile Fontaine's face in the last clear moment before the silt took him—not angry, worse than angry, *understanding*, looking up through forty feet of green water at the partner who had falsified the log that put them both too deep, too long, to save him. He thought of ten years of measuring life in miles so he'd never again have to measure it in years, in anniversaries, in the age a dead man would have been.

And he thought of Augustin Vex in the flophouse, gray as driftwood, pressing the wax-cloth into his hands and saying: *Not for money. For a promise. Everything else is just ballast.*

"You want the map," Zephyrine said slowly, "and in exchange you'll make my crime disappear."

"I'll make your *past* disappear. Cleaner word."

"They're the same word where I come from." He reached out and touched the folder—not taking it, just resting a fingertip on its cover, feeling the weight of it, the terrible lightness of it. "Tell me something true, Marchetti. Why does a salvage director with a fleet and a navy contract need a dying prospector's hand-drawn chart? Your master survey covers these shoals. I've seen it. Sonar, side-scan, the whole liturgy. If the wreck were findable with your equipment, you'd have found it."

Something flickered behind Marchetti's eyes—the first honest thing Zephyrine had seen in him.

"The deep shoal doesn't scan," Marchetti admitted. "There's a thermocline and a silt layer that eats returns below fifty meters. Vex found it the old way—dead reckoning, lead line, and thirty years of talking to fishermen who remembered their grandfathers' stories. The old ways are the only ways down there. Which is why I need the man who has the old map." He smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "That, and the fact that the navy sealed the wrong wreck. They're diving a decoy forty meters shallower and calling it progress. I'd rather not correct them. I'd rather be finished before they notice."

"And what's down there? Gold? Coin? Church plate?"

"What's down there," Marchetti said, "is cargo. That's all you need to know. Cargo, and your share of it, and a burned file." He pushed the glass of rum closer. "You're a man with nothing, diver. No gear, no partner, no country, no future except six tides and a dry season that will close these shoals like a door. I'm offering you everything. Think about what refusing costs."

Zephyrine thought about it. He thought about it honestly, the way you're obliged to think about any offer that buys back your soul at retail price.

Then he thought about Castera, floating off Pointe Noire with his lungs full of harbor, and understood exactly what partnership with Ivo Marchetti cost, and who paid it.

* * *

"No," Zephyrine said.

The word came out quieter than he'd expected. Not thrown, not hurled—set down, the way you set down something heavy you've been carrying too long.

Marchetti didn't move. "Say it again."

"No." Louder this time, and steadier. "I'm not giving you the map. I'm not diving your wreck. And I'm not shaking your hand on a deal where the other signatory floats in the harbor a week later." He stood. The cabin felt suddenly small, the leather and the lamplight and the silent man by the door all of it shrinking around him, and he found—to his own surprise—that he wasn't afraid. Or rather, he was afraid, coldly, thoroughly, the way a man is afraid at altitude, and the fear felt less like a warning than like an altitude. Like coming home to a country he'd renounced. "You keep the file. Send it to San Juan. Send it to Émile Fontaine's sister, if that's what you do. I spent ten years letting that paper own me from inside a cabinet I'd never seen. You've shown me the cabinet now. It's smaller than I imagined."

Marchetti rose too, unhurried, and for a moment the two of them stood across the chart table with the folder between them like a border.

"You understand," Marchetti said, and his voice had lost every degree of warmth, "that this makes it open. Not quiet anymore. Open. Every boat in my fleet, every diver on my payroll, every port from here to Trinidad. Six tides, diver. You have six tides and I have thirty boats."

"I counted the tides before you did."

"You'll die out there. Or you'll surface with something worth killing for, and then you'll die slower." Marchetti picked up the folder and tapped it once against the table, a small dry sound like a knuckle on a coffin lid. "Last chance. The map."

"The map," Zephyrine said, "is buried somewhere you'll never find it. And even if you did—it's half a map. Vex was smarter than you give a dead man credit for."

Something moved in Marchetti's face at the word *dead*—a flicker of recalculation, quick as a fish turning—and Zephyrine logged it and filed it and gave no sign he'd seen it. *He doesn't know if Vex is dead. He's not certain what Vex told me, or whether there's a second copy.* Good. Let the uncertainty work. Uncertainty was the only weapon left in the skiff.

"Put him over the side," Marchetti said—not to Zephyrine, but to the room.

The two dark-shirted men moved, and Zephyrine moved faster. Not toward them—toward the stern. He'd noted the transom door on his way aboard, the way he noted exits in every room he'd entered for a decade, and he went through it in three strides and hit the water in a flat dive that barely raised spray.

The sea took him like a struck bell.

Cold, after the cabin—cold enough to sharpen every nerve he owned. He swam down and away on instinct, angling beneath the launch's keel, and above him the water began to strobe. Searchlights. They swept the surface in long white blades, cutting the black water into panels of silver and shadow, and Zephyrine swam through them the way a man walks through falling knives—timing each sweep, flattening against the swell when the light passed, pulling hard in the dark between. Once a blade caught him full across the shoulders and he hung motionless in it, arms out, lungs burning, a pale starfish on the surface of the night, while somewhere above him a voice said something he couldn't hear and the light moved on.

They didn't shoot. Of course they didn't shoot—a body with bullets in it was evidence, and evidence was the one cargo Ivo Marchetti could not afford. They wanted him taken whole, or drowned clean. So they searched, and he evaded, and the distance from the launch grew by strokes: fifty meters, eighty, a hundred and twenty, until the searchlight blades fell short of him and only their glow remained, a bruised luminance on the underside of the clouds.

He surfaced beside his skiff at last, gasping, and hauled himself over the gunwale with arms that shook. Below in the bilge, soaked but sealed in its oilcloth, the wax-cloth map pressed against his chest where he'd stowed it before boarding—because he had never intended to surrender it, and had never intended to let them search the skiff either, and had spent the entire negotiation knowing that the swim back might be the price of the evening.

He started the engine low, idled out of the anchorage on the dark side of the eastern headland, and only when the launch's lights were blotted out by rock did he let himself breathe fully.

His hands, he noticed, were steady. That was the strange thing. Ten years of numb discipline, of invisibility practiced as a craft, and it had taken Ivo Marchetti and a folder and a glass of imported rum to make him feel, for the first time since Mayagüez, entirely awake. Defiance, it turned out, fit him like an old jacket. It had been hanging in the back of the closet the whole decade, waiting.

*Six tides,* Marchetti had said. *Thirty boats.*

Zephyrine turned the skiff's bow toward the open shoals and did his arithmetic. The fleet would blanket the anchorages and the known passes by dawn. Which meant the deep shoal had to be reached not through any channel a chart would recommend, but over the coral spine north of Caye Brûlée, where the water ran three feet over live coral and no boat heavier than his skiff would dare try it. Teo hadn't asked why he'd wanted a shallow-draft hull. Smugglers rarely did.

Behind him, far off across the black water, the Cormorant's working lamps came on one string at a time, a floating city waking up angry.

Ahead, the dry-season front was already building on the horizon—a long bruised line of cloud stacked like masonry, with lightning ticking inside it, patient as a clock.

Between the two, one skiff, one map, one promise to a dead man, and five tides remaining.

Zephyrine opened the throttle.
