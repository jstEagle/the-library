## 10. The Deep Shoal

The bearings made no sense until the third night, and then they made terrible sense.

Zephyrine sat in the skiff's stern with Vex's wax-cloth map spread across his knees, a pencil stub behind his ear and the duplicate page from Colette folded into his shirt pocket like a second heartbeat. The wind had gone soft and southerly, which was wrong for this time of year—wrong the way a held breath is wrong—and he had learned to read that kind of wrongness as a warning written in a language older than charts.

Vex had drawn his map drunk or dying or both, but the man had been a surveyor before he'd been a prospector, and surveyors don't forget their arithmetic even when they're forgetting their own names. The numbers resolved against Colette's page the way two halves of a broken plate resolve: not perfectly, never perfectly, but with a seam you could feel with your thumb.

*Three hundred fathoms south-southeast of the sealed wreck. Past the coral shelf where the water goes from green to ink. Look for the anchor that isn't an anchor.*

Zephyrine had spent two days telling himself it was coin down there. Gold, silver, pieces of eight, something heavy enough to buy a new name in a country where nobody asked questions about old ones. That was the story he told himself because the other story—the one about why the navy had sealed a wreck on a shoal nobody commercially valued, why Marchetti's fleet crawled over these waters like ants over spilled sugar—was a story he wasn't ready to be inside of.

He anchored at dusk on the fourth day, over water so deep the lead line ran out its marks and came up cold.

* * *

He dove at first light, alone, which was stupid, and he knew it was stupid, and he did it anyway because Delphine was still somewhere out there running her decoy lights through the fleet's traffic and every hour she bought him was an hour he couldn't waste waiting for company.

The descent was long enough to become a kind of meditation. At thirty feet the green light thickened. At sixty the temperature dropped and his skin remembered the Mayagüez water, cold as a closed door. At ninety the bottom rose to meet him—a plain of white sand rippled like a bedsheet, featureless, indifferent.

He swam east by his compass, counting kick cycles, watching the sand for anything that didn't belong.

At four hundred kicks, he found it.

It looked, at first, like a ridge of coral. Then the ridge took on geometry no coral owns: straight lines, right angles, a curve of iron furred with two centuries of growth. An anchor—but not an anchor. A windlass. The iron heart of a ship that had died here so long ago the sea had finished digesting everything except its bones.

Zephyrine hung in the water above it and felt something move through him that had nothing to do with current.

He had dived wrecks for eleven years. He knew what a wreck was: a problem, a payday, a set of hazards with a dollar figure attached. This was different. This was a ship that had come here on purpose—or been brought here on purpose—and settled into the deep shoal like a secret settling into a locked drawer. No storm had done this. Storms scatter; storms break masts and open hulls and leave wreckage strewn across a mile of bottom like a confession. This ship lay whole. Settled. Placed.

He followed the hull outline north, fins kicking slow, and found the entry point Vex had marked with a small X and a word in Creole French that Zephyrine had needed three readings to parse: *la porte des noms.* The door of names.

A cargo hatch, rusted shut, half-buried. He worked his pry bar into the seam and leaned into it, and the bar bent, and he stopped, breathing hard through the regulator, and made himself look at the hatch properly.

There were letters cut into the iron. Old lettering, hand-chiseled, filled with coral crust he scraped away with his knife point by point while his air gauge watched him with its small patient face.

*LA SIRÈNE — NANTES — 1788.*

A slave ship. He knew the trade routes, everyone in these islands knew them the way you know the shape of a scar on your own family. Nantes had been one of the great mouths of the triangular trade, and 1788 was the last fat year before the revolutions started eating the profits. A slaver out of Nantes, sunk whole in the deep shoal, two hundred years under the sand.

And the navy hadn't sealed *her*. They'd sealed the other wreck—the shallow one, the decoy—because someone wanted divers' eyes pointed anywhere but here.

Zephyrine hung in the blue dark above a ship that had carried human beings as cargo, and the awe he'd felt curdled slowly into something heavier. Not gold. Whatever was in that hold, it wasn't gold. Nobody sealed a slave ship to protect her treasure. You sealed a slave ship to protect her paperwork.

* * *

The hatch gave on the third dive, after he'd surfaced twice to swap tanks and let the nitrogen drain out of his blood like poison from a wound—which it was, which it always was, and he thought of Teo's voice saying *the fleet posted quiet money on any diver asking about the deep shoal*, and dove again anyway.

Inside, the hold was a cathedral of dark.

His light swept across rows of copper sheathing gone green, across ballast stones stacked with a precision that spoke of a disciplined crew, across—and here his breath caught audibly in his own ears—wooden shelving built along the starboard side, tier upon tier, and on the shelves, cases. Dozens of them. Long boxes of lead-sealed wood, most rotted to lace, some collapsed into their contents, but a few—maybe one in ten—still intact, wrapped in tarred canvas that the sea had never quite gotten its teeth into.

He pulled himself along the shelving, light close, and found where the wood had split on one ruined case and the contents had spilled into the sand below: paper. Paper, after two hundred years. Waxed and lead-lined, packed in tallow, sealed against the water the way the dead are sealed against the earth—and it had worked. Sheets of it lay fanned across the ballast stones, browned to the color of old tea, and Zephyrine lifted one with a gloved fingertip and angled his light onto it.

Columns. Names. He recognized the structure instantly because he'd seen manifests before, salvage manifests, customs manifests, the bureaucratic skeletons of commerce. But these columns were different. These weren't bales and barrels.

These were people.

*Adele, age 19, taken at Ouidah. Kossi, age 24, marked with three cuts left cheek. Marie-Claire, age 31, with infant, name unrecorded.*

Names. Ages. Marks. Origins. A ledger of the stolen, kept with the meticulous horror of men who counted everything, who insured their cargoes, who needed receipts.

Zephyrine's hands were shaking inside his gloves and he could not make them stop.

This was the cargo. Not coin. Not specie. Records—sealed manifests naming families, naming ships' masters, naming the ports and the factors and the buyers, and somewhere in those pages, if Vex's dying hints meant anything, names that had grown into fortunes. Names that had laundered themselves across two centuries into respectability, into shipping concerns, into salvage fleets, into government ministries. Records someone powerful had paid—was still paying—to keep lost.

The navy hadn't sealed the wrong ship by accident. Someone had known exactly which ship held the truth and had buried the truth under a decoy, and the decoy was guarded, and the truth was merely deep.

He understood, then, with the clarity that comes only underwater, why Marchetti's face had changed when he'd recognized Zephyrine's. It wasn't just the file. It was the territory. The fleet wasn't salvaging the Leeward Shoals. The fleet was erasing them.

* * *

He chose the case the way he chose everything now: fast, cold, without ceremony. Bronze—not wood—one of only two on the shelves, its lead seals intact, its weight wrong for documents and right for documents packed in wax and oilcloth around a rigid frame. The kind of case a captain keeps his master papers in. The kind of case that gets carried off a sinking ship last.

It weighed maybe forty pounds. His gauge said he had twenty minutes of bottom time left and a long ascent ahead, and forty pounds of bronze through a rusted hatch at ninety feet was the kind of arithmetic that killed careful men and careless ones alike.

He rigged a lift line anyway. He'd brought the gear because part of him—the part that had survived ten years by never trusting the easy plan—had insisted.

The ascent was slow, deliberate, staged. At the safety stop, hanging at fifteen feet in water lit from above like stained glass, Zephyrine held the bronze case against his chest and felt it through his wetsuit, cold and dense and certain, and he thought: *this is the heaviest thing I have ever carried, and it weighs less than my partner's body did.*

He hadn't thought about Étienne in nine days. A record. And here, at fifteen feet, with the surface shimmering overhead like the skin of a drum, the thought arrived whole and uninvited, and he let it pass through him the way you let a cramp pass—acknowledging it, riding it, refusing to fight it, because fighting it underwater is how men drown.

Then he kicked for the light.

* * *

He surfaced on the seaward side of the skiff, out of habit, out of the animal caution that had kept him alive through a decade of other people's greed. He blew his tanks, grabbed the gunwale, and hauled the case up over the transom with arms that burned, and he was reaching for the rail to follow it when he saw the smoke.

Not engine smoke. Cook-smoke—galley smoke, greasy and black, the signature of a ship running her stoves at cruising stations. It stood up off the water beyond the southern point of the shoal in a thin gray column, and beneath it, coming around the headland with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once needed to hurry, was the Cormorant.

White hull. Green boot-top. The fleet's house flag snapping at the masthead, and on her foredeck, glinting in the morning sun, the davits of her dive launch already swung outboard.

Zephyrine went over the rail without splashing—hands, then body, lowered into the skiff's bilge like a man lowering himself into his own grave—and lay flat among his tanks and line coils, heart slamming, salt water pooling under his chin.

They shouldn't have been able to find him. He'd anchored in the lee of the shelf, engines cold since dusk, no lights, no smoke, nothing. Which meant they weren't looking for him. They were looking for the wreck. They'd run their own survey, or bought someone else's, and the Cormorant had come to the deep shoal the way a creditor comes to a debtor's door—certain of the address.

Which meant the site was burned. Which meant whatever window he'd opened down there was closing, and closing fast, and the bronze case sitting six inches from his ribs was suddenly the most dangerous object in the Caribbean.

Through a gap between the fuel drums he watched the tender settle, watchful, a quarter mile off. A boat dropped from her davits. Figures in it—four, five. Dive gear. Hard hats, no—modern rigs, commercial umbilicals. They were going down today. Maybe going down now.

And then, smaller, colder: a second boat, fast, running a search pattern off his position. Quarter-mile legs. Boxing the water.

They were sweeping for a skiff.

Zephyrine slid the bronze case beneath the false floor he'd built into the bilge—Teo's workmanship, smuggler-grade, worth every coil of gear he'd traded for it—laid his dive flag and spare tank over the panels, and started the engine at lowest idle, letting it warm without sound, the way Teo had taught him, the way a poacher warms a rifle stock in his hands.

The search boat turned onto a leg that would bring it within two hundred yards in four minutes.

Zephyrine put the helm over and ran for the western channel, low and slow, hull barely kissing its own wake, threading the coral heads he'd memorized on the way in, while behind him the Cormorant's shadow lengthened across the deep shoal and her divers went down to a hold that was now, thanks to one missing bronze case, missing exactly one thing.

He did not look back. Looking back is how you hit the reef.

But he could feel it back there—the whole weight of it, the ship of names, the paper families, the minister's fortune, Marchetti's leash and his own—all of it rising behind him like a swell with no horizon, and the case under the deck plates pressing up against his spine through the fiberglass as if it knew.

Four minutes later the search boat found his anchorage: cold engine, cold coffee, a chart table with the corners of a wax-cloth map still pinned under the lamp clamp.

Zephyrine heard the faint crackle of their radio through the morning air, reporting contact, reporting sign, reporting a heading guessed from a tide-slack drift he'd already contradicted.

He opened the throttle.

Ahead, the dry-season front stood on the horizon like a wall being built all day long, and somewhere inside that wall was Sainte-Rose, and a hill, and a promise, and Delphine—out there in the fleet's traffic with her decoy lights burning, buying hours she might already have paid for.

One tide. He had one tide, and a case full of names, and the fastest boat Teo's silence could buy.

He ran west into the building wind, and the sea, which had given him the door of names, began—gently, almost politely—to take the weather back.
