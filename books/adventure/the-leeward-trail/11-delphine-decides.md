## 11. Delphine Decides

Delphine found the camp at first light, when the tide was low enough to walk the coral shelf and the light came off the water in long copper blades. She had told herself she was coming to check on him. She had told herself a dozen things on the run out from Port Anse — that he'd simply moved his gear, that he'd gone ahead to the deep shoal without telling her because he was careless with other people's worry, that everything was fine.

The camp told her otherwise.

His tarp was down, dragged half into the water and weighted with stones, as if someone had tried to sink it and lost interest. His dive locker stood open and empty, the brass hasp pried back like a rib. His cooking fire had been drowned — not banked, drowned, a bucket of seawater thrown over coals still warm enough to hiss when she touched them. Someone had been here within the hour. Maybe less.

And there was blood on the coral.

Not much. A smear, really, dark against the pale bone of the reef, already drying to the color of rust. It led from the fire ring toward the water's edge and stopped where the tide had come up over the shelf. Whoever bled here had either walked into the sea or been carried out of it.

Delphine crouched over the stain for a long time without touching it. She was a careful woman; her mother had raised her on the principle that anything you touch, you own, and anything you own can be taken from you. So she didn't touch. She looked, and she read, the way she read water before a dive — current by current, sign by sign.

Boot prints in the wet sand near the landing. Three men, maybe four, heavy heels, fleet-issue soles — she knew the tread pattern because she'd sold salvage boots to Cormorant crewmen two seasons running and knew exactly what they left behind. The map case was gone from where Zephyrine kept it lashed under the tarp ridge. The wax-cloth wrapping Vex had used lay discarded, torn, its wax cracked along one fold. They hadn't been gentle with it. They hadn't needed to be. A man who bleeds will give you whatever is in his pockets.

She stood up slowly, and the fear arrived all at once, the way cold water arrives through a torn suit — not gradually but everywhere at once.

They had him. Or he had escaped them bleeding. And either way, the fleet had the map, which meant Marchetti had the map, which meant the race she had watched Zephyrine quietly lose his mind over for two weeks was no longer a race between him and the calendar. It was a race between him and Ivo Marchetti, and Marchetti did not lose races. Marchetti bought them, or drowned them, whichever was cheaper.

* * *

She went back to her boat and sat in the cockpit with her hands flat on the chart table and made herself think like a navigator instead of like a woman whose chest had turned to stone.

That was the discipline. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd taught it with charts instead of words: *when the sea goes wrong, you do not look at the sea. You look at your heading.* Panic was just bad navigation. Fear was a crosscurrent — real, dangerous, and survivable if you stopped fighting it sideways and started working with it.

So: facts.

Fact one. If they had Zephyrine aboard the Cormorant, he was being questioned, and questioning by Marchetti's people did not stop at conversation. Fact two. If they didn't have him — if he'd cut loose and run, bleeding, into the shoals — then what he needed more than anything in the world was time, and time was the one thing the fleet could take from him fastest, because the fleet owned every channel, every anchorage, every pair of eyes between Leeward Shoals and the open sea.

Fact three, and this was the fact she kept circling back to like a mooring buoy: none of it was her problem.

That was the sane reading. That was the reading any sensible woman would arrive at, sitting alone in her cockpit with the morning warming around her. Zephyrine Reyes had walked into this with his eyes open. He had lied to her about the locker, deflected her about the map, boarded the Cormorant under a false name without telling her, and vanished from her life for eleven days leaving nothing but a note that said *back by Thursday* — and it was now Sunday past Thursday. He had chosen his course. A good navigator respects another boat's heading even when she thinks it's suicide.

She said all of this to herself, out loud, in the empty cockpit, in the flat voice she used for reciting depth tables.

Then she looked east, toward the deep shoal, where the dry-season front was building on the horizon like a wall of bruised slate, and she thought about the night two years ago when she'd dove the sealed wreck illegally and seen the bodies that weren't storm victims, and how she'd never told anyone, and how Zephyrine had never asked, and how the two of them had built a partnership out of the things they agreed not to say — and how that silence had felt, until this morning, like trust.

It wasn't trust. She understood that now, standing in the wreckage of his camp. It was two people holding their breath together. And a partnership built on held breath drowns the moment one of them needs to inhale.

He had needed to inhale. He had needed help, and he hadn't asked, because asking meant telling, and telling meant Mayagüez, and Mayagüez was the ballast he couldn't cut loose. So he'd gone alone onto Marchetti's ship wearing a stranger's name, and now there was blood on the coral and boot prints with fleet treads, and Delphine Océane Charpentier was sitting in her cockpit doing arithmetic about whether he was worth dying for.

She hated him a little, right then. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in some log that would survive: *at 0640, Delphine hated him.* Because hating him was the only thing keeping the other feeling from capsizing her entirely.

* * *

By seven she had made her decision, and the decision frightened her less than the calm with which she made it.

She was going out into the fleet traffic. She was going to run decoy lights.

She knew the idea was insane the way you know a reef is there — not as an opinion but as a fact of the water. The Cormorant was anchored off Pointe Sable with six tenders and a dozen skiffs working the survey grid. Marchetti had posted quiet money on any diver asking about the deep shoal; Teo had told her that himself, drunk, apologetic, the way Teo told everyone everything eventually. The fleet was hunting one man across forty square miles of reef, and the man was wounded, and the front was coming, and she proposed to sail her thirty-one-foot sloop directly into the middle of the search pattern flashing lights like a stricken vessel and letting every lookout in the fleet decide she was the thing they were looking for.

It would buy hours. Not days. Hours. Enough for a bleeding man in a stolen skiff to put distance between himself and the net.

She provisioned anyway. That was the strange part, the part she would remember afterward: how ordinary it all felt. Water jugs filled. Diesel topped off. Flares checked — she took all twelve, including the three old ones that might misfire, because a misfiring flare was itself a kind of decoy. She pulled out her spare running lights, the ones wired for towing operations, and rigged them aft where they'd read from a distance as a second, smaller vessel. She put on her good foul-weather jacket, the orange one, high-visibility, the one that said *look at me* in every language the sea spoke.

If they stopped her, she had a story. She was a freelance salvager running a night tow contract, solo, slightly stupid, nothing to do with any diver. She'd rehearsed it twice and it sounded thin both times, and she decided that was fine. Thin stories were honest ones. Liars always sounded certain.

Before she cast off, she did one more thing. She went below and took the sealed envelope from under her chart drawer — the one she'd written three nights ago, after the confrontation on the dock, when she'd finally understood what he was planning and had lain awake doing the same arithmetic she was doing now. The envelope said, in her blunt hand: *For whoever finds my boat.* Inside was the location of her insurance papers, the name of her lawyer in Fort-de-France, and one line at the bottom that she had written and rewritten six times and finally settled on because it was the truest sentence she owned:

*If Zephyrine Reyes comes looking for me, tell him he owed me a Thursday.*

She wedged it under the chart table where a sober searcher would find it and a panicked one wouldn't bother.

* * *

She cleared the harbor at 0730 with the front's leading wind already on her neck, cool and mineral-smelling, smelling of the dry season's arrival. The sea was going gray-green and short-chopped, the water tightening the way it did before weather, and the fleet's masts stood up off Pointe Sable like a forest of bare trees.

She ran the first decoy light at 0800.

It was almost embarrassingly easy. She killed her mainsail, ran up the tow lights, and fired one of the old flares — the suspect one, and sure enough it stuttered, burned green-white for four seconds, and died, which from two miles away looked exactly like a distress signal from a boat losing power. Within ten minutes a tender broke from the survey grid and started toward her. Then a second. Then, from the Cormorant's anchorage, a fast launch with twin engines and too many silhouettes aboard.

Delphine stood at her helm in her orange jacket and let them come, and felt something in her chest that was terror and exultation braided so tightly she couldn't have told them apart with a knife.

*Come on,* she thought, at the launch, at the tenders, at the whole gray forest of the fleet. *Look at me. I'm right here. I'm the most interesting thing on your water.*

The first tender hailed her at half a mile. She answered in her best exhausted-contractor voice — engine trouble, tow line parted, requesting assistance, no I don't need a helicopter, no I'm not diving anywhere, yes I know the front's coming, thank you, thank you. The boarding party that eventually came aboard was two men, bored and salt-crusted, and one of them was young enough that his suspicion kept getting interrupted by his manners. She fed them coffee from her thermos and complained about diesel prices and let them "help" her re-rig the tow line, and while they were bent over the cleats she watched, over the rail, the fast launch from the Cormorant slow, circle, and turn away — called back, redirected, its search pattern broken.

Somewhere out in those forty square miles of reef, a bleeding man in a stolen skiff had just been given twenty minutes he wouldn't otherwise have had.

She could have wept. Instead she laughed at the young sailor's joke about her anchor light, and poured him more coffee, and thought: *this is what love looks like when it can't say its name. It looks like a heading. It looks like a woman choosing a course that might kill her, and steering it well.*

Her father would have called it madness. Her father had also once sailed into a hurricane to pull strangers off a dismasted ketch, and had come back with two of the five, and had never once called it anything but *the correct heading.* Delphine had spent her whole life deciding which of her parents' inheritances to carry. This morning, in the rising wind, with the fleet's eyes crawling all over her little boat, she chose her father's.

* * *

They let her go at noon, eventually — satisfied, or bored, or simply recalled, because the front was now a visible fact, a black-blue rampart marching up from the southeast, and every captain in the fleet was doing his own arithmetic about shelter. But the delay had cost the search something real. She'd counted. Four tenders had sat on her for nearly five hours. Two aircraft passes had circled *her*, not the shoals. Whatever net Marchetti had cast over the water that morning had a Delphine-shaped hole torn in the middle of it, and somewhere beyond that hole, she prayed, a skiff was running east with its throttle open.

She raised sail for the return and immediately understood the price.

The wind had backed and built. What had been fifteen knots at dawn was now twenty-five and gusting thirty, and the sea had organized itself into long steep gray walls with white tearing off their crests. Her boat handled it — she'd built the boat's handling herself over nine years, reef by reef, repair by repair — but the front was still two hours out and closing, and she was tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix, and her hands, when she looked at them on the wheel, were shaking.

Not from the cold.

Because she had done it. Because she had sailed into the teeth of the fleet and lied to its face and won five hours for a man who hadn't asked her to, and because winning had proven something she couldn't take back: that she was no longer a woman who could choose safety. The choice had been available to her at dawn, sitting in her cockpit with the chart table under her hands, and she had watched it sail away over the horizon without her, and now it was gone, and what remained was this — a woman in rising weather, committed, all sails set, steering toward a man who measured his life in miles because he was too afraid to measure it in years.

*You idiot,* she said aloud, to him, to the wind, to herself, and couldn't tell which of the three she meant most fondly.

She was eight miles from the shoals when she saw it.

At first she took it for a trick of the light — a smudge against the eastern water, low and fast, running inside the reef line where only a fool or a local would push a hull in this building sea. Then it resolved: a skiff, unregistered, running dark-gray, taking the swells quarter-on with a skill that made her chest ache, and even at this distance, even through spray and the front's first ragged rain, she knew the silhouette at the helm. You spend enough years watching a man handle a boat, you know his shoulders the way you know a signature.

Zephyrine. Alive. Moving. Running east ahead of the weather with the whole fleet behind him and the dry season closing like a door.

And then she saw what was behind him, and her relief turned to ice in her throat.

Out of the gray squall-line to the south, running parallel to the reef, came the fast launch from the Cormorant — the twin-engine one, the one with too many silhouettes. It had picked his trail up again. It was three miles back and gaining, and between it and the skiff lay ten miles of open reef-studded water and a front that would kill them both impartially if they stayed out in it.

Delphine looked at her compass. She looked at the launch. She looked at the skiff, small and stubborn and beautiful, running for its life.

There was a channel through the outer reef — the Sable Cut, narrow, vicious in a following sea, the kind of passage that punished hesitation and forgave nothing else. The launch was bigger than the skiff. Faster in open water. But in the Cut, size was a liability, and speed was a lie, and the only boats that survived it were the ones piloted by people who knew every rock personally.

She knew every rock personally.

Delphine put her helm over, squared her bow toward the Cut, and began, deliberately, loudly, with every light she had left, to lead the hunt into the one stretch of water where she could make them bleed for every fathom.

Behind her, the front came on like the end of the world. Ahead, the skiff vanished into the rain. And Delphine Charpentier, who had spent her whole life reading water, finally read her own heart, and found it had a heading after all.
