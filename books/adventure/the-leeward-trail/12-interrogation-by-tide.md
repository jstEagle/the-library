## 12. Interrogation by Tide

They came for him at slack water, when the sea forgets which way it's going.

Zephyrine had anchored the skiff in the lee of Îlet Chancel, a green thumbprint of an island where the mangroves grew right down into the water and the mosquitoes owned the night. He'd chosen it because it was the kind of place a man went to disappear, and he'd been wrong, because Marchetti's enforcers had known exactly where disappearing men went. The boat came around the point at dusk with its engines muffled, running dark, and by the time Zephyrine heard the wake slap against the mangrove roots there were already four of them in his cockpit.

He didn't reach for the knife under the thwart. He'd done that arithmetic years ago: four men, one blade, no witnesses. Instead he sat very still on the cooler box with Vex's coffee tin between his boots and watched the tallest one—a broad-shouldered man with a face like a dock fender and a scar splitting one eyebrow—look slowly around the skiff as if taking inventory.

"Zephyrine Reyes." The name came out flat, unimpressed, the way a customs officer reads a passport. "Mr. Marchetti would like a word."

"I'm a contract diver," Zephyrine said. "I work alone."

"You were a contract diver on the *Cormorant* for eleven minutes," the man said. "You've been asking about the deep shoal in every bar from Case-Pilote to Le Robert. And you're sitting on a bronze case that belongs to Mr. Marchetti."

The last part landed like a hook through the cheek. Zephyrine hadn't told anyone about the case. He hadn't shown it to anyone. Which meant they'd been watching him dive, or watching him surface, or—more likely—someone had sold him. Teo, maybe. Or the boy at the fuel dock who filled his tanks. It didn't matter which. What mattered was the arithmetic shifting under him again, the way it always shifted, the way the shoals themselves shifted after every storm.

The tall one held out a hand. "The tin."

Zephyrine looked at the coffee tin. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, was not the bronze case—the bronze case was lashed beneath the floorboards, under three feet of bilge water where no flashlight would find it—but Vex's ashes, and Vex's letters, and the wax-cloth map with Colette's duplicate page folded against it. Everything he had left of the promise.

"The tin," the man said again, and this time two of the others shifted their weight, and Zephyrine understood that the conversation about whether this would be simple was over.

He picked up the tin and handed it over.

* * *

They took him to the *Cormorant*.

Not roughly—they were professionals, and professionals don't bruise cargo—but thoroughly. His hands were zip-tied behind him, a hood went over his head that smelled of diesel and someone else's sweat, and he was lifted over the gunwale like a crate of engine parts. He counted steps by feel: seven across a steel deck, down a ladder, along a passage where the air changed from salt to machine oil, into a room where a chair waited and the door closed with the particular softness of expensive soundproofing.

When the hood came off, Ivo Marchetti was sitting across from him with a bottle of rum and two glasses, and the first thing Zephyrine felt—not fear, not yet, though fear was coming—was the strange insult of being offered a drink by a man who intended to take everything from him.

Marchetti was smaller than his reputation. A compact man in his sixties with silver hair combed straight back and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, wearing a linen shirt open at the collar, he might have been a retired banker on holiday. Only his hands gave him away. They were diver's hands, broad and scarred, and they lay absolutely motionless on the table while he studied Zephyrine the way a man studies a chart he's about to correct.

"You know," Marchetti said, pouring two fingers into each glass without asking, "when I saw your face on my tender ten days ago, I made a phone call. Do you know what I learned?"

Zephyrine said nothing.

"I learned that you are a ghost with excellent references. No bank account in your name. No lease. No boat registered anywhere in the Antilles. You pay cash. You work salvage under three different names depending on the year. And every single person who has ever dived with you describes you the same way." He leaned back. "*Careful.* That's what they say. Careful Zephyrine. Checks his gauges twice. Never exceeds his tables. A careful man." He smiled without warmth. "Careful men are the easiest men in the world to predict, Mr. Reyes. They always choose survival. So let us skip past the part where you pretend not to be afraid, and get to the part where we both make money."

He slid one glass across the table.

Zephyrine didn't touch it. His wrists ached inside the ties. Through the hull he could hear the fleet's generators, the distant clank of the dredge barge, the whole machinery of Marchetti's operation grinding away at the navy-sealed wreck like beetles through timber. Somewhere above them, Delphine was probably watching the anchorage, counting boats, doing her own arithmetic. He hoped she was far away. He prayed she was far away.

"You have something of mine," Marchetti said. "A bronze document case, colonial manufacture, recovered from a wreck in the deep shoal approximately—" he glanced at a clipboard on the table beside him, and Zephyrine understood with a cold drop in his stomach that they had been close enough to watch the dive itself— "thirty-six hours ago. I want it. In exchange, I am prepared to offer you the same arrangement I offered before, with one improvement." He tapped the clipboard. "The Mayagüez file. Not sealed. *Closed.* Expunged. There is a difference, and the difference is that a closed file cannot be reopened by anyone, including me. You could stop measuring your life in miles, Mr. Reyes. You could buy a boat with your own name on it. You could grow old somewhere with a harbor."

For a moment—and he hated himself for it, hated it the way a man hates a cramp that comes at depth—Zephyrine let himself see it. A harbor. A name. Growing old. Ten years of exile weighed against one bronze box, and the scales tipped so easily it was obscene.

Then he thought about what was in the box. Names. Families. A minister's fortune built on flesh and laundered through this very fleet, through these very generators he could hear chewing away overhead. Marchetti wasn't hunting treasure. Marchetti was hunting evidence, and the offer on the table wasn't freedom—it was the price of Zephyrine becoming complicit. A closed file was just a leash with a longer lead.

"No deal," Zephyrine said.

Marchetti sighed, genuinely regretful, the way a man sighs over weather. "I was afraid you'd say that. The trouble with careful men is that they're also stubborn men." He nodded toward the tall enforcer standing by the door. "Gaspard is going to ask you some questions now. Gaspard is not a patient man. When Gaspard is finished, if I still do not have my coordinates, we will go out to the deep shoal together, and Gaspard will show you how long a careful man can stay underwater without his gauges."

* * *

Gaspard worked methodically, which was worse than cruelty.

He started with the small bones of the hands—two fingers bent back until something gave, quick and businesslike, the way a man tests a lobster trap spring. Then he asked about the wreck site. Then he did the other hand. Between questions he never raised his voice, never hurried, occasionally checked his watch as though the evening had other appointments, and that casual glancing at the time did more damage than anything else, because it told Zephyrine that his pain was scheduled, budgeted, an item on an agenda.

Zephyrine gave them the coordinates.

He gave them up at the second question, bleeding from both hands onto the deck plates, and he gave them fluently, convincingly, with the tremor of genuine exhaustion in his voice, because he had spent the entire ride across the water composing them. That was the thing about rage—it burned hot for exactly as long as it took to become something else. By the time Gaspard touched him, the fire had cooled into arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *they will take the case anyway. They will search the skiff. So give them numbers that cost me nothing and cost them everything.*

The false coordinates were good ones. He'd built them from a real patch of reef eight miles northeast, a drowned coral shelf at forty meters where old anchors and ballast stones lay scattered enough to look promising on a sonar return. A site plausible enough to hold their attention. Deep enough to eat hours. Far enough that even if they found nothing, the round trip, the survey runs, the careful methodical dives that careful professional salvors would insist upon—all of it would burn daylight and tide.

And the tide was the whole equation. Six tides had opened this race. One tide remained ahead of him now, maybe less, and every hour Gaspard's people spent sweeping a dead reef was an hour purchased with eight fingers' worth of pain that he would have paid twice over.

Marchetti studied the numbers written in Zephyrine's own shaking hand, and Zephyrine watched him watch the paper, and knew the moment the director believed it—because Marchetti was a careful man too, and careful men trust details. The depth sounded right. The bearing matched the current patterns. The description of the landmark—a split rock, a coral head shaped like a mushroom—was specific in the way only a man who had actually been there would be specific.

"Northeast shelf," Marchetti murmured. "Of course. The current shear would hide a hull signature." He looked up, and for one unguarded second there was something almost like respect in his face. "You understand this changes nothing for you."

"I understand," Zephyrine said.

"They'll take the case regardless. Consider it a fee."

"I know."

"And the file stays open."

"I know that too."

Marchetti stood, buttoned his linen jacket, and paused at the door. "You're wondering why I don't simply kill you. It's a fair question. The answer is that dead men are cheap, Mr. Reyes, and useful men are expensive, and I have found over a long career that a man who has lost everything except a promise becomes very useful indeed—once you learn what the promise is." He smiled again, that banker's smile. "We'll talk again after the northeast shelf disappoints me. Gaspard will see you off my ship."

* * *

They put him back in the skiff two hours before dawn, at anchor off Pointe du Bout, with the bronze case gone from under the floorboards and his hands wrapped in gauze that smelled of iodine—someone aboard had splinted the broken fingers competently, because damaged divers can't work and Marchetti still thought he might be useful. They left him his tanks, his gear, half a tank of fuel, and the coffee tin, returned with the letters rifled but the ashes untouched, which told Zephyrine that Marchetti had read every page Vex ever wrote and understood none of them.

The skiff rocked. The fleet's lights strung away to the north like a fallen constellation, and beyond them, invisible, the true wreck sat in the dark holding its archive, and somewhere south of it, eight miles of empty water covered a reef that would soon be crawling with Marchetti's divers chasing a lie.

Zephyrine sat in the stern and did the only thing left to do. He counted.

One tide. That was the ledger now. The dry-season front was building on the horizon—he could smell it, that flat mineral smell the trade winds got before they died for the season—and when it arrived, the shoals would close like a book. One tide to get back to the deep shoal. One tide to dive the true hold, the one he'd plotted from Colette's duplicate page, the one where the full archive slept. One tide, minus the fuel he didn't have, minus the strength in two ruined hands, minus whatever decoy Delphine might or might not be running for him out there in the dark, because he hadn't seen her since the mangroves and he couldn't afford to hope.

Rage would have been easier. Rage was simple; it pointed in one direction and burned. But rage was a luxury, and luxuries sank men at depth. So he folded the rage up small and put it away where he kept the shame and the grief, in the flooded compartment below the waterline that every diver learns to carry, and what remained was cold and clean and numerical:

*Eight miles to the fuel dock. Teo owes him silence for another four days. Forty meters to the hold. Twenty minutes of bottom time on a single tank with these hands. Current turning at 0400.*

He started the engine.

Behind him the *Cormorant* blazed with light, and somewhere aboard it, in a soundproofed room, Ivo Marchetti was already asleep, certain that dawn would bring him to the northeast shelf and the end of his problem. Ahead, the channel ran black and smooth toward the deep shoal, and the tide, indifferent as God, had begun to turn.

Zephyrine turned the bow south and opened the throttle, and the skiff rose up on plane and ran—one tide, one chance, one promise—into the dark.
