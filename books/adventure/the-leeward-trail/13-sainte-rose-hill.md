## 13. Sainte-Rose Hill

The road to Sainte-Rose climbed the way grief does—gradually, then all at once. Zephyrine took it in a borrowed mule cart, because the skiff was too conspicuous and his legs, after two days of running the fleet's shadow through the channel islands, had begun to shake in a way he refused to name. The coffee tin sat on his knees, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from the sun as if the ashes inside were still deciding whether to be a man.

He could not afford this detour. Every hour the cart creaked uphill was an hour Marchetti spent tightening the net around the shoals, an hour the tide tables turned against him, an hour closer to the dry-season front stacking itself on the horizon like a debt coming due. He had done the arithmetic three times since dawn and three times it told him the same thing: go back, put your head down, dive.

But promises were not arithmetic. That was the whole trouble with them.

The tin shifted as the cart hit a rut, and he caught it without thinking, the way you catch a falling tool at depth—automatic, careful, already knowing what it costs to lose something small in the wrong place. He looked down at his own hands holding another man's death and thought: *this is what Vex bought with a map.* Not passage. Not rescue. Just this—a stranger's hands, steady enough, for one last mile of road.

* * *

The cemetery sat on the hill's shoulder like a hat set down carelessly. A low wall of coral stone, whitewashed and flaking, enclosed a scatter of graves that faced the sea—not out of sentiment, Zephyrine guessed, but because there was nothing else to face. The land fell away east toward the shoals, and from up here the water looked deceptively calm, a sheet of hammered pewter with the dry-season clouds beginning to bruise its far edge.

He came through the gate and stopped, uncertain for the first time in years. He knew how to enter a wreck. He did not know how to enter a place like this, where every stone was a sentence someone had finished.

There was no sexton, no priest, just an old woman sweeping sand off the paths with a bundle of palm fronds, and near the wall, a figure he had noticed from the road—a woman kneeling at a fresh-dug plot, laying flowers that were mostly stems, the kind you cut yourself when buying them would mean admitting the occasion.

Zephyrine stood at the gate a long moment. Then he walked in, because walking in was all that was left of the promise, and he had learned on the Leeward Trail that you finish what you carry or it finishes you.

She heard him before she saw him—the crunch of his boots on the shell path—and turned, and he saw Augustin Vex in her face the way you see a coastline in a chart: same jaw, same stubborn set of the mouth, softened by thirty years and a different weather. Her eyes went to the oilcloth bundle in his hands and her whole body changed, the way a hull changes when it takes water—no drama, just a settling, a new weight taken aboard.

"You're the diver," she said. Not a question.

"He said you'd know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I'm—my name is Zephyrine."

"I know what you are." She rose, brushing earth from her skirt, and looked at him with an appraisal that made him feel like a specimen pinned under glass. "He wrote me about you. Last letter. He said he'd found a diver who worked the Shoals like he'd been born down there, and that divers like that either die young or owe somebody. He didn't say which you were."

"Both," Zephyrine said. "Probably."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. She gestured at the open grave beside her, lined with rough planks, waiting. "I dug it myself yesterday. The gravedigger wanted paying and I wanted—" She stopped, and he watched her decide how much truth to spend. "I wanted to be the one who did it. He was my father. Somebody should do one thing for him with their own hands."

Zephyrine knelt at the edge of the plot. The coffee tin looked absurd there, small and red against the dark earth, and he found himself unable to simply tip it. Some part of him kept waiting for ceremony—for words, for a rite—to arrive and tell him what to do.

Colette solved it. She knelt opposite him and put both hands flat on the soil. "Say whatever you say," she told him. "It doesn't have to be good. He hated good. He used to say anything polished was hiding something."

So Zephyrine spoke into the wind off the shoals, and what came out surprised him, because he had planned nothing and meant all of it:

"He was dying when I found him, and he wasn't afraid, and I couldn't understand that until now. He traded me everything he had for a ride up this hill, and he paid first, and he never doubted I'd come. I've been trusted by exactly one person in ten years, and it was a dying prospector who owed me nothing." He paused. The wind filled the pause. "I broke the promise once already. I almost didn't make the turn off the coast road this morning. But here I am, and here he'll stay, and I'm glad—God help me, I'm actually glad—that some things can still be finished."

He opened the tin. The ashes went into the earth grey and fine, mixing with the coral soil, and Colette began filling the grave with her hands before he'd even finished pouring, working fast and unashamed, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her cheeks. He joined her without deciding to. The two of them packed the earth down together in the failing light, and when it was done she pressed her palms flat on the mound one final time, and stood, and became again the woman who had been appraising him at the gate.

"There," she said quietly. "Claimed."

The word went through him like a hook through soft tissue. He looked at the grave and understood, suddenly, what Chapter Eight of his own life had been trying to teach him out on the water with Vex's letters: that being claimed by somewhere was not the same as being caught. That a man could belong to a hill, or a woman, or a promise, and still run free.

He didn't believe it yet. But he wanted to, and wanting was new, and new things frightened him more than Marchetti ever could.

* * *

She fed him because refusing would have been rude and he was too tired to be rude. Her house sat below the cemetery, a low white cottage with a bread oven and a dog too old to bark, and inside it smelled of coffee and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where someone has been writing letters for years and never sending most of them.

Vex's letters were everywhere. Bundles tied with string on the shelf. A drawer left half-open on a drift of envelopes. Colette caught him looking and said, "He wrote every week. Whether he had news or not. Most weeks he didn't." She poured the coffee, thick and sweet the island way. "You want to know why he really gave you that map. Don't you."

"I want to know what I'm carrying," Zephyrine said. "Half a chart to a wreck nobody will name, in waters the navy sealed, hunted by a man who recognized my face from a file I thought was dead. Yes. I want to know."

Colette sat down across from him. For a long moment she studied him the way her father must have studied charts—looking not at the lines but at the spaces between them.

"My father spent forty years diving these shoals," she said. "Before the fleet came, before the navy, when it was just him and a hookah hose and men who paid in rum. And thirty years ago he found something he never stopped talking about and never once described. A wreck deeper than any of them. Colonial timbers. A hold full of iron boxes." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "He tried to raise one box, once, with two other men. One drowned. After that he wouldn't go back down. But he couldn't stop drawing it. Every winter, every rainy season, he'd sit at that table and draw the same chart over and over, better each time, and tear them up, and start again."

"The map he gave me was wax cloth. Careful work."

"That's the third version. There were others." She stood, went to the shelf, and began untying bundles with hands that shook only slightly. "Because my father was a careful man in every way except the ones that mattered, and one year—one year, after the drowning—he mailed me a piece of one. Just a page. No note. I asked him about it and he said, *keep it somewhere safe, girl, and if I ever stop writing, burn it.*"

She laid it on the table between them.

Zephyrine's breath stopped the way it does at ten fathoms when the regulator hiccups—one instant of pure animal silence.

It was a page of chartwork. Ink faded to sepia, the paper soft as cloth from handling. Bearings, soundings, a hand-drawn section of reef he knew intimately because he had been staring at its twin for six days—and across the top, in Vex's crabbed hand, a notation Zephyrine had not seen on the wax-cloth original, because the original's corner bearing that notation had been torn away or worn blank:

*TRUE HOLD — NOT THE STERN SECTION. THE NAVY MARKS THE WRONG SHIP.*

His eyes went down the page. Depth figures. A tidal correction table in Vex's handwriting, annotated with the obsessive precision of a man who had thirty years to check his sums. And at the bottom, a line that made the blood move strangely in Zephyrine's arms:

*They don't want cargo. They want the papers. Ask who pays the fleet and you'll have your answer.*

"He knew," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded far away. "Thirty years ago, he knew what they were sealing down there. He knew they'd sealed the wrong wreck—or let everyone think they had."

"He knew, and it killed him slowly, the way knowledge does." Colette sat back down. "He wouldn't dive it again. He wouldn't sell it. He wouldn't even tell me where it was, not straight out—he said a thing known is a thing owned, and he didn't want me owned by it. So he drew it, and redrew it, and mailed me one page like a man planting a seed he hoped would never sprout." She looked up. "And then he got old, and sick, and he met a diver on the shoals who worked the water like family, and he decided the seed had waited long enough."

Zephyrine stared at the page. The two halves of the chart lay in his mind now, clicking together like a lock accepting its key: Vex's bearings resolving against the Cormorant's master survey, and now this third element—the correction, the true hold, the admission that everything the fleet hunted was buried under the wrong coordinates. Marchetti had half a map and a navy seal and thirty years of institutional memory pointing at the stern section. Zephyrine had a dead man's obsession and a daughter's inheritance pointing at the truth.

"They took the bronze case from me," he said. "Marchetti's men. Two nights ago. I fed them false coordinates and they took the bait and the case, and I've been running on a one-tide lead ever since, telling myself I'd figure out the rest on the water." He laughed, short and bitter. "I wasn't going to figure out the rest. I was going to drown guessing."

"No," Colette said. "You were going to come here. He knew that too. Why do you think he made you promise the hill?" She reached across the table and tapped the page once, firmly, the way her father must have tapped a chart a hundred times. "Everything he did pointed here. The ashes, the letters, the page. He built you a road, diver, and every mile of it ends at this kitchen table. Grief shared is grief halved—that's what my mother used to say. He was betting the same was true of maps."

Outside, the light had gone the color of old brass, and the wind had swung south, and Zephyrine felt it in his chest the way sailors feel weather before instruments confirm it: the dry-season front, early, hungry, closing the window he'd been diving through. Two tides. Maybe less.

He should leave tonight. Every hour of arithmetic he owned said so.

Instead he spread the duplicate page flat, weighted its corners with salt cellars, and set Vex's compass—the dead man's brass compass, which he had carried since the flophouse and never once explained to himself—on the meridian line. The needle settled. The bearings resolved. And there it was, sudden and complete as a face surfacing from dark water: the true position of the hold, corrected, cross-checked, *confirmed*, sitting in water deep enough to kill him and shallow enough to reach.

Colette watched him work and said nothing for a long time. Then: "You're going back down."

"Tomorrow's morning tide."

"And Marchetti?"

"Marchetti thinks he has the map and the case and the coordinates." Zephyrine rolled the page, dry now, and slid it into the oilcloth beside the empty coffee tin—which he found he could not throw away, and did not examine why. "Let him think it. A man hunting the wrong ship is a man anchored to the wrong spot. I intend to leave him there."

Colette walked him out to the gate as the first stars came up over the shoals. At the wall she stopped, and took his hand, and held it a moment longer than strangers do.

"He called you family in the last letter," she said. "I didn't understand it until today. Now I do. You're the one who carries things home."

Zephyrine stood in the gate with the hill behind him and the sea ahead, holding a dead man's compass and a living woman's trust, and felt the strangest sensation of his exile: not lightness, exactly. Ballast, changing purpose. Weight that had been shame becoming fuel.

He went down the hill in the dark, and by the time he reached the coast road the wind had backed fully south, and far out over the Leeward Shoals the sky was burning with the dry season's first lightning—silent, patient, counting down.

Two tides. He ran.
