## 14. Two Tides Left

The duplicate page was warm from Colette's hands when he'd taken it, and even now, hours later, folded inside his shirt against his sternum, it seemed to hold that warmth like a coal refusing the sea.

Zephyrine worked by the skiff's single lamp in the lee of a nameless cay, the chart spread across the thwart and weighted at four corners with dive weights, a coffee tin of pencils beside his knee. The front was building to the east—a bruised shelf of cloud stacked on the horizon like cargo nobody would claim—and he could feel the pressure change in his bad ear, the one that had never cleared properly after Mayagüez. Two tides. Maybe two and a half if the front slowed, but men who planned for maybe drowned on maybe.

He smoothed Vex's original map flat, then Colette's page beside it, and for a long moment simply looked at them the way you look at two halves of a sentence until they make a word.

Vex had drawn his wreck from memory—bearings shot from shore, depths guessed with a lead line and a prospector's stubborn arithmetic. Good enough to find something. Not good enough to find *it*. Zephyrine had felt that wrongness for days without being able to name it, the way you feel a current pulling contrary to the surface chop. The bearings resolved against the Cormorant's master survey—he'd memorized enough of it in Marchetti's survey room to reconstruct the relevant quadrant—but when he laid the lines across the shoal chart, they didn't land on the navy-sealed wreck at all. They landed a mile and a half northeast of it, on a stretch of reef marked on no chart he'd ever seen except as an unremarkable scatter of coral heads.

And Colette's page explained why.

Her father had mailed it home six years ago, before his hands shook too badly to draw. It was the same survey quadrant—the same coastline, the same soundings in the same cramped hand—but with corrections. Vex had spent years comparing the fleet's master survey against what he found on the bottom, and he'd penciled his disagreements right onto the paper: a sounding struck through and amended. A reef head drawn where the survey showed open water. And in the margin, in letters so small Zephyrine had needed the lamp close as a kiss to read them: *They surveyed it wrong on purpose or they surveyed a different ship. Either way the seal is on the wrong grave.*

The first wreck—the one the navy had sealed, the one whose corroded locker had started all of this—was a decoy. Or worse: it was the ship someone wanted found, wanted sealed, wanted named and closed and forgotten, while the real archive sat quiet under a mile and a half of ordinary reef where no government would ever think to bolt a hatch.

Zephyrine sat back on the thwart and let that settle into him the way ballast settles.

*They sealed the wrong ship.*

Or the right ship, depending on which side of the truth you stood on. He thought of the hum he'd heard through the hull of the sealed wreck—that low, wrong vibration, like something mechanical still living down there behind the navy's plates. He'd told himself it was current through iron. Now he wondered if it was something simpler and more human: pumps. Ventilation. Someone keeping a tomb dry because the tomb mattered, because what was buried in it could still hang a man.

His pencil moved. That was the thing about fear and hope—they used the same hand.

He re-plotted Vex's corrected bearings against the survey quadrant he'd carried out of the Cormorant's survey room in his skull, checking each figure twice, then a third time because the third time was the one that caught liars. The lines crossed over the unnamed reef scatter, and where they crossed, Vex had drawn a small square with a cross inside it and written, in that same tiny hand: *the strongroom lies here, thirty-one fathoms, entry by the broken stern, mind the netting.*

Thirty-one fathoms. A hundred and eighty-six feet. Past the recreational limit, past the comfortable limit, into water where your air lasted half as long and your judgment lasted less. He'd made dives like that before—in another life, under another name, with a partner who trusted his numbers. His stomach turned over at the thought, not with fear exactly but with recognition, the way an old scar aches before rain.

He looked up at the horizon. The front had thickened since dusk; the first outrider clouds were moving now, unhurried, like scouts who knew the army behind them. Under the front, the sea would stand up in short steep seas, and the shoals would go white and blind, and nothing would dive there for weeks. When the dry season wind settled in, it settled in like a tenant.

Two tides. One dive. No second attempt.

* * *

Delphine came back an hour after midnight, running dark, her engine barely a murmur, and Zephyrine heard her before he saw her—not the engine but the particular way she shipped her oar-hook against the gunwale, three taps, their old signal from the days when they'd worked wrecks too close to other people's claims. Three taps meant clear. Two meant run. Tonight she gave him three, and then a fourth, which wasn't code at all, just Delphine saying *I'm tired and I'm here anyway.*

She came aboard the skiff without asking, which was also their old way, and crouched over the chart with her hands braced on her knees. She smelled of salt and diesel and the cheap clove candy she chewed instead of sleeping. In the lamplight her face was drawn and set, and there was a scrape along her forearm he hadn't seen her get.

"Traffic was thick off Pointe Corail," she said. "Fleet's running picket boats in pairs now. I counted six pairs between the point and the anchorage." She tapped the chart where the Cormorant's anchor light would be, a small red star fixed on the black water beyond the reef. "They're not fishing. They're waiting for you to come to them."

"They think I'm coming to the sealed wreck."

"They think you're coming somewhere." She straightened and looked at him, and the look went on a beat longer than comfort allowed. "You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one you make when you've stopped calculating and started believing." She said it without softness, but she sat down across from him, which was its own kind of softness. "Show me."

He showed her. He walked her through it the way he'd walked himself through it—Vex's original bearings, the survey quadrant, Colette's corrections, the crossing point on the unnamed reef. He watched her read the marginal note about the wrong grave, watched her lips move slightly over *they sealed the wrong ship*, watched her go very still in the way she went still when something large moved through her thinking.

"That's why the navy's plates," she said finally. "That's why the hum. They're maintaining it." Her finger came down on the decoy wreck's position. "Somebody paid to have this ship found and sealed and left alone, and somebody else is paying Marchetti to make sure nobody asks what's under the plates." She looked up. "And the real one is sitting under a mile and a half of nothing, where Vex found it twenty years ago and told no one but his daughter."

"Told no one," Zephyrine said. "Mailed one page home. Kept the rest in his head until his head started going." He touched the wax-cloth map, Vex's careful fading lines. "A dying man's insurance policy. He knew whoever buried the truth would come looking for anyone who knew where it slept. So he scattered himself—one piece ashore with a girl who loved him, one piece in a flophouse waiting for a diver desperate enough to say yes to a strange promise."

"And you said yes," Delphine said.

"I said yes."

"You always say you don't."

"I know what I always say." He almost laughed, and the laugh hurt on the way up. "Delphine—I need to say the next part plainly, because I've been arranging it in my head all night like it's a salvage plan and not what it actually is."

"Say it."

"I'm diving tomorrow's morning tide. Thirty-one fathoms on a single tank rig with a stage bottle, alone, on a site nobody has ever dived, with a front coming down on top of me. If I'm not back on this cay by slack before noon—" he made himself finish it, because unfinished sentences were how men died politely, "—then you take the skiff, you take the duplicate page, and you go to Fort-de-France without me. You find the journalist yourself. You don't come looking. You don't send anyone. You finish it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the flat, a fish jumped and slapped back down, and the sound was enormous in the silence she left.

"No," Delphine said.

"It's not a request—"

"I know it's not a request. That's why I'm saying no." She leaned forward, forearms on her knees, and her voice dropped into the register she used below decks, calm and absolute, the voice of a woman who had once held a flooded regulator in her teeth for ninety seconds to prove a point to him. "You don't get to hand me the ending and call it a plan. I ran decoy lights past six picket boats tonight. I let a patrol come within two hundred meters of my transom and I sat there with my engine off and my heart going like a bilge pump, and do you know what I was thinking? I was thinking *he's re-plotting right now, he's working, he's alive, and every hour I buy is an hour he gets.* That's the trade. That's the whole trade. You don't get to cancel my side of it by drowning."

"Then don't let me drown," Zephyrine said. "Be on the water when I surface. Be the boat I swim to."

She stared at him. Something moved behind her eyes—anger and arithmetic fighting for the same chair—and then the arithmetic won, because Delphine was, underneath everything, the best navigator he'd ever known, and navigators understood that some headings chose themselves.

"Morning tide," she said. "I'll be at the outer edge of the reef scatter, running dark, engine warm. If you surface anywhere inside a mile of me, I'll find you, because I've been finding you for six years and I'm better at it than you deserve." She jabbed a finger at the horizon, at the stacked cloud. "But when that front's leading edge hits the shoals, I'm gone, Zeph. I don't care what you've got in your hands. Gold, paper, the minister's own confession written in blood—you come up late, I leave, and I won't be sorry, because dead divers deliver nothing."

"Understood."

"And one more thing." She reached into her jacket and put something on the thwart between them: his own knife, the one the enforcers had missed when they took the bronze case, the one she must have lifted from his ransacked camp when she found the blood on the coral. She'd sharpened it. Of course she had. "You lost this. I found it. We're even for the map."

He picked it up and buckled it on, and the weight of it at his hip was the weight of being known, which was heavier than any case of bronze and harder to carry and worth it.

* * *

He didn't sleep. Sleep was for men with more than two tides.

Instead he rigged gear by lamplight with the deliberation of a priest laying out vestments: twin tanks for the descent, stage bottle slung for the return, cutting tool, slate, three lights because two was superstition and one was suicide. He checked each O-ring twice and lubricated them with the grease Teo had thrown in with the skiff, and while his hands worked he let his mind rehearse the dive the way he'd once rehearsed dives for money, back when his numbers were clean and his logs were honest.

Thirty-one fathoms. Bottom time, if he was disciplined, nineteen minutes. Nineteen minutes to find a broken stern in virgin reef, mind the netting Vex had warned of, locate a strongroom in a colonial hull that had been down for two centuries, crack it or cut it, and take what mattered. The manifest case he'd surrendered to Marchetti's enforcers had been bronze and heavy and had bought him nothing but a head start measured in one tide. Whatever was in the true hold, he'd take paper this time. Paper weighed nothing. Paper could be copied, split, scattered, sent in three directions from three ports so that no single seizure could kill it. He'd learned that much from losing the case: the fleet couldn't drown what was already ashore.

He thought about the phantom again—the one that lived at depth, the one with Tomás's voice, the one who waited in the blue past sixty feet and asked, in a tone of terrible patience, whether Zephyrine had checked his gauge or only his ambition. Ten years of diving and the phantom had never once let him pass without the question. Tomorrow, at a hundred and eighty-six feet, on a wreck full of the kind of truth that had ruined everyone who touched it, the question would be waiting.

Let it wait, he thought. This time I'll answer it on the way down.

Near dawn the wind backed a few points and freshened, and he stepped out of the skiff into ankle-deep water to look east. The front had come closer in the night. It no longer looked like weather; it looked like architecture—a wall built along the whole seam of the sky, gray-green and grained, with lightning flickering inside it the way thought flickers behind a closed face. The leading edge was still hours out, but the sea already knew. The swell had lengthened overnight, long low pulses rolling in from the open Atlantic, lifting the skiff at its anchor and setting it down again with a patience that felt like warning.

Delphine appeared beside him, barefoot in the shallows, and they stood together watching it come.

"My grandmother used to say the dry season front was the island closing its ledger," she said. "Everything unsettled gets settled. Everything hidden gets covered. Then it opens again in spring and you find out what survived."

"Did anything survive?"

"Us," she said. "Every year. Against all evidence."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Out past the reef, the sky behind the wall pulsed again, silent at this distance, and the light of it ran along the water toward them and broke at their feet.

"Hope hurts," he said, surprising himself. "I'd forgotten. Three years of wanting nothing—it's very peaceful. Like anesthesia. And now I want this—want it so bad my teeth ache—and I keep waiting for the peace to come back and it won't. It's gone. I did that. I opened the door myself."

Delphine looked at him sideways. "That's not hope hurting," she said. "That's you waking up. Anesthesia feels like peace right up until the surgery starts."

The eastern sky had gone the color of pewter, then the color of pearl, and the tide began to turn. He felt it before he saw it—the subtle reversal of the drift along the reef edge, the water hesitating, gathering itself, choosing a direction. Slack water. Twenty minutes of stillness, maybe thirty, before the flood ran hard and turned the deep shoal into a washing machine.

He finished dressing into his gear on the beach, Delphine helping him shoulder the twin rig without either of them speaking, the ritual of it older than words between them. At the waterline he stopped, and she stopped, and for a moment they just breathed, two people about to bet everything on a heading neither could see the end of.

"Colette gave me something else," he said. "I didn't tell you last night. I couldn't—there wasn't room in my head."

"There's room now."

"She told me what her father said at the end. About the promise. He said—" Zephyrine had to stop and start again, because the words were heavier than he'd priced them. "He said he didn't care if I found the wreck. He cared that someone finished what he started. He said a man's work should outlive his name, because names sink and work floats." He looked out at the gray water, at the reef scatter a mile and a half away where a wronged ship waited under two hundred years of quiet. "I've spent ten years keeping my name sunk. Maybe that was the wrong lesson off Mayagüez. Maybe the work is supposed to float."

Delphine put her hand flat against his chest, over the duplicate page folded there, over the heartbeat going fast and steady beneath it.

"Then float," she said.

He walked into the sea.

The water closed over his hood and the world went green and quiet, and below him the bottom fell away in ledges toward the deep shoal, and above him, far above, the storm wall stood along the horizon like a verdict not yet read aloud. He pulled himself down the anchor line he'd dropped at first light, checking his gauge at ten feet, at thirty, at fifty, answering the phantom's question before it could ask, and the deeper he went the sharper the hope got, until at a hundred feet it was no longer a feeling at all but a kind of navigation—a bearing, a heading, a fixed point he steered by through the cold and the blue—

And at a hundred and forty feet, hanging in the gloom where the reef scatter began, he saw the timbers.

Not coral heads. Never coral heads. A sternpost, huge and wormed and furred with two centuries of growth, lying exactly where a dying prospector's tiny handwriting had promised it would lie, with the torn ghost of netting still draped across it like a veil over a face.

The first wreck had been the decoy. The navy had sealed the wrong ship.

And somewhere below him, in the dark of the strongroom, the truth was waiting to be carried into the light—or to drown him trying.
