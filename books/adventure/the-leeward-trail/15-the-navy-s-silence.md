## 15. The Navy's Silence

The front had come up out of the southeast overnight like a debt collector, and the sea wore its color before the sky did—a bruised green-grey that Zephyrine had learned to read the way other men read a creditor's letters. Two tides. Maybe three if the weather lied to him kindly. He anchored the skiff in the lee of a coral head whose shape he'd memorized from Vex's duplicate page, and for a long moment he simply sat with his hands on the gunwale and let the numbers settle.

The first wreck—the navy-sealed wreck, the one every chart in the fleet pointed at—was forty fathoms off this position. The second was here, under his keel, in sixty feet of water that no survey had ever bothered to sound because no survey had ever been paid to look. Vex had found it by accident thirty years ago, chasing a runaway anchor, and had spent three decades keeping it the way a man keeps a wound: privately, painfully, and against all advice.

Zephyrine checked his gauges twice. Checked the duplicate page one more time, though he could have drawn it from memory now—Vex's hand was shaky but honest, bearings taken off stars and dead reckoning, depths marked in fathoms with a notation in old Creole French: *le silence commence ici.* The silence begins here.

He laughed once, without humor, and spat over the side.

"Fine," he told the water. "Let's see what you've been quiet about."

* * *

The descent was clean. That alone should have warned him.

No silt plume, no scattered debris field, no scatter of ballast stones marking a wreck that had died violently. Instead the bottom rose gently toward a shelf of coral, and set into the shelf like a coin pressed into wax was the hull of a ship that had gone down whole—or nearly so. Colonial lines, heavy timbers gone black as iron, copper sheathing still clinging in patches that glowed dull orange in his torchlight like embers refusing to die. A slaver. He knew it before his fingers touched the wood; the hull proportions told him, the way a coffin's dimensions tell a carpenter what it was built for.

Vex's page had marked an entry point: a breach aft where the sterncastle had torn open on the reef during the sinking, then been sealed by two centuries of coral growth—except along one seam, where the growth was younger, broken, recent. Vex had been inside. More than once, by the look of the scuffed stone.

Zephyrine worked through the gap sideways, torch held ahead of him, and felt the temperature drop as the ocean's noise fell away behind him. Inside, the water was older. Stiller. It moved only when he moved it, and it carried a taste of iron and something sweeter underneath, organic, patient—the taste of a place that had been holding its breath since before his grandfather was born.

His light swept across the orlop deck and stopped.

Not coin. Not plate. Not the romantic spillage of treasure that had funded every dream he'd ever had about getting out.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked with a care that no sinking ship ever managed, lashed and wedged and braced by hands that had come after the drowning. Lead-lined. Sealed with pitch. And stamped—this was the part that made his heart do something strange and slow behind his ribs—stamped with a crown and a serial number, the same stamp repeated down the stack like a rosary.

Government issue. Someone had put these here deliberately, after the wreck, in water deep enough to kill a man who lingered, and had walked away expecting the sea to keep their secret forever.

Zephyrine hung in the black water and understood, with the particular clarity that only comes at depth, that he was not the first person to know this hold existed. He was merely the first person in thirty years whom nobody had paid to forget it.

* * *

The first crate he opened cost him twenty minutes and most of his composure. The lead lining had done its work; inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had hardened to the texture of bark, were ledgers. Ships' manifests. Bills of lading. And beneath those, bundled in a lawyer's careful hand, correspondence—letters with seals he recognized from history books and one seal he recognized from the newspaper he'd read that very morning in Port Anse.

He worked by torchlight, gloved, turning pages with a reverence he usually reserved for nothing living.

The ship had been the *Sainte-Aurélie*, out of Nantes, 1789. Her manifest listed cargo in the language of the trade: *pièces d'Inde*, four hundred and eleven of them. Human beings, numbered like bales. But the sealed archive wasn't the slaver's own paperwork—that would have been damning enough and worth nothing. The archive was something else entirely. It was the paper trail of what happened *after*: the insurance claims, the salvage contracts, the compensation payments when abolition came and the owners demanded reimbursement for their lost property. Four hundred and eleven names, each one written out—because the compensation courts required names—and each one attached to a sum of money.

And that money had not evaporated. Page by page, letter by letter, Zephyrine watched it move. Into shipping. Into sugar. Into land. Into a bank house whose name changed twice over two centuries but whose signature hand never did. Into, in the final folder—the newest paper in the hold, deposited within living memory, its ink barely faded—a series of transfers that ran through a salvage company's books. A company whose name he knew the way he knew his own pulse.

Cormorant Marine Salvage. Ivo Marchetti's fleet. The money that had bought Marchetti's boats, his divers, his navy contacts, his quiet bounty on curious prospectors—it had all flowed down a channel dug in 1789 by men who owned human beings, and the channel had never once run dry.

And the last letters, the ones folded separately, tied with wire: instructions. Recent instructions. From a government minister whose portrait hung in every official building on the islands, directing that certain records be "secured below" and that the survey of the Leeward Shoals be "adjusted accordingly." Adjusted. The word sat in Zephyrine's mind like a fishhook.

They hadn't sealed the wrong wreck by accident. They hadn't even sealed the wrong wreck at all. The navy cordon on the first site wasn't a search—it was a fence. A distraction built of buoys and regulations, designed to keep every diver in the archipelago looking at empty water while the real archive slept sixty feet down in a hull full of ghosts.

Marchetti wasn't hunting treasure. Marchetti was the janitor. And Zephyrine had spent six tides breaking into the building he was paid to keep clean.

* * *

He should have taken one case and gone. That was the arithmetic he'd sworn by for ten years: take what you can carry, leave what can drown you. One bronze case of manifests would have been enough—enough proof, enough leverage, enough truth to burn whoever needed burning.

But his hands kept moving.

There was a compartment under the deck plating, revealed where Vex's crowbar work had pried a section loose decades ago, and inside it a strongbox—not gold, he saw immediately, not gold at all, but more paper, and above the paper, resting on top like a paperweight, a small brass plaque engraved with a family crest he'd seen that morning on the stern of the Cormorant's launch. The same crest. The same family. Two hundred years, and they still signed their secrets with the same mark.

Zephyrine filled his lift bag until it strained. Ledgers, the wired letters, the plaque. His gauge needle slid past the number he'd promised himself he wouldn't pass, and he made himself stop—not because of the depth, but because of what he'd learned at Mayagüez about the difference between courage and arithmetic. He'd falsified a log once to protect a record. He would not falsify a gauge to protect a grudge.

The ascent took everything he had. The front was closer now; the surface light had gone the color of pewter, and when he broke through, rain was already stippling the water around the skiff in grey needles. He hauled the bag aboard, collapsed beside it, and lay there breathing hard while the rain grew teeth.

Under his body, through the oilcloth, he could feel the edges of the papers. All those names. Four hundred and eleven people who had been counted as cargo, whose descendants were probably fishing these same shoals right now, diving for conch and lobster a mile from the hold where the proof of what was done to them lay sleeping under lead and pitch.

And above them, fat and respectable and photographed shaking hands with ministers, the family that had turned their suffering into a dynasty, and laundered the last of it through a salvage fleet that employed half the fishermen in Port Anse.

The prize was never gold. He'd known it somewhere in his body since the flophouse, since Vex's cracked voice saying *they don't want what's in her, boy, they want what she says.* But knowing a thing in the body and holding it in your hands were different weights entirely. This wasn't treasure. This was a bomb with a two-hundred-year fuse, and he was swimming it through open water in a storm.

* * *

He got the skiff moving before the squall's leading edge hit properly, running north-northwest with the duplicate page zipped inside his jacket and the lift bag lashed under the thwart where no casual eye would find it. The rain came sideways. Thunder walked across the water in long strides, and between the flashes he watched the horizon astern for masts.

He didn't see any. That frightened him more than masts would have.

Marchetti's fleet was too good to be caught out by weather this size. If the Cormorant wasn't visible, it was because she was somewhere deliberate—standing off, waiting, or already moving to cut the channels. And there was one other possibility, the one that put cold water in Zephyrine's stomach that had nothing to do with the rain: Marchetti might not need to find him at all. Not if Marchetti went looking for the people around him instead.

Delphine. Delphine, who had run decoy lights for him two nights ago, threading the fleet anchorage with her own boat lit up like a festival so that Marchetti's watchers would count the wrong wakes. Delphine, who had told him about the bodies in the sealed wreck and never asked him for anything except honesty he couldn't give. She'd gone back to her mooring off Pointe Corail to wait out the front. Alone. With a boat everyone in the fleet could identify on sight.

He thought about turning south. He thought about it for a long time, longer than the storm lasted, longer than the channel markers took to slide past. Every mile he ran toward her was a mile he ran away from Fort-de-France, away from the journalist, away from the one window in which the archive could still matter—if it surfaced after the dry season closed the shoals, after Marchetti had time to arrange things, it would surface into a world already shaped to swallow it.

But the arithmetic had changed. It had changed the moment he'd opened that compartment and seen the crest. Because Marchetti's leverage had always rested on one assumption: that the evidence stayed lost. Now it wasn't lost. It was in the bilge of a smuggler's skiff, riding a following sea toward a printing press. Which meant Marchetti's only remaining play was not to recover the papers—it was to make sure the man carrying them was never believed, never tried, never heard. Dead men's documents are just paper.

And the fastest way to find a man is through the woman who loves him.

Zephyrine stood at the tiller with the rain hammering his shoulders and made the choice the way he'd once made all his worst choices: quickly, and knowing he'd carry it either way. He came about. South. Toward Pointe Corail, toward Delphine, toward whatever the fleet had decided to do with the hours he'd bought them.

The thunder answered from directly overhead, one long roll like a door closing.

Behind him, far astern, a single white shape resolved out of the rain for just a moment—a tender, running dark, running fast, heading not for him but for the point. For the moorings. For her.

He opened the throttle all the way and prayed to a God he'd stopped believing in at Mayagüez that the sea would let him be early, just this once, just this one time, for someone who deserved it.
