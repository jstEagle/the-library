## 16. Cut Loose

Delphine found the blood at first light, and she knew before her fingers touched it that it was Zephyrine's.

The camp on the leeward cay was wrecked the way a man wrecks things when he's in a hurry and angry and afraid all at once—canvas slashed, the cook box overturned, rice scattered into the sand like a spill of small white bones. The map was gone from its oilskin sleeve. The sleeve itself lay half-buried where the tide had been working at it all night, patient as everything else the sea did.

She crouched over the coral shelf at the camp's edge and let her fingertips hover above the dark stain without touching. Blood dried on coral went the color of rust by morning. This hadn't finished drying.

Hours old, then. Maybe four. Maybe five.

She stood up slowly, and the arithmetic began, because Delphine's mind had always been a chart table: every fact a bearing, every bearing a line, every line crossing somewhere. Blood here. Map gone. Camp torn apart but nothing of value taken except the one thing worth taking. No body. No second set of tracks she could read, though the sand told its stories badly after wind.

They had come for him in the night. They had taken the map. They had not taken him—or he had not let them—and he'd left bleeding, which meant he'd left fast, which meant he'd left alive, because dead men didn't bleed their way out of camps.

Alive, and running, and now blind, because Vex's map was in someone else's hands.

* * *

She should have gone home. That was the sane arithmetic, the sum any honest friend would arrive at: the fleet had posted its quiet bounty, another prospector had already turned up drowned with his lungs full of shoal water, and the man she'd partnered with for six years—the man whose silences she'd learned to read like weather—was now the most hunted diver between Anse-Bertrand and the Saintes. Every hour she stayed in his orbit was an hour she spent inside the radius of whatever Ivo Marchetti wanted done.

Delphine stood in the ruined camp and gave the honest arithmetic exactly as much consideration as it deserved, which was none.

Because she knew something the fleet didn't know. She knew what Zephyrine carried that wasn't on any map. She knew about the promise—the coffee tin riding in his gear, the ashes, the hill at Sainte-Rose. She knew, because three nights ago he'd finally told her, standing in the shallows with the lantern between them like something they were both afraid to look at directly, that he meant to keep it. A dying prospector's bones on dry land. A promise made by a man who broke promises professionally, kept anyway.

And she knew the other thing too, the thing he still couldn't say out loud even to her: that whatever was sealed in that navy-marked hold, whatever Marchetti was really hunting down in the deep shoal, wasn't coin. Coin you could split. Coin you could run from. What Marchetti wanted buried, Zephyrine wanted raised—and Delphine had seen enough of the world to know that men who hunted evidence were more dangerous than men who hunted gold, and that the only way through them was faster than they were.

He needed hours. He needed the Cormorant looking south while he ran west. He needed eyes on the fleet that the fleet didn't know were watching.

So she went back to her boat, and she did the thing she'd been turning over in her mind since the night she'd confessed to him about the bodies she'd seen through the sealed hatch—the storm victims who hadn't drowned like storm victims drown. She lit her running lamps. All of them. Masthead, stern, the work lights she used for night salvage, strung along her rails so that from any distance her little boat burned bright against the darkening water like a signal fire afloat.

Then she pointed her bow straight through the middle of the fleet anchorage, slow and deliberate and utterly unafraid, and let every glass on the Cormorant find her.

* * *

It took them forty minutes. She'd guessed an hour; Marchetti's people were better than she'd credited.

The launch came alongside at full dusk, two boats actually, boxing her in with the casual geometry of men who had done this many times and never once been surprised. Floodlights snapped on and turned her deck white. She killed her engine herself, before they could demand it, and put her hands flat on the wheel where everyone could see them, and when the boarding officer came over the rail—a broad, careful man with a voice like wet gravel—he found her already smiling.

"Evening," she said. "Took you long enough."

That was the trick of it, or the beginning of the trick, and she'd worked it out in the hour of running lights: fear was a currency, and she could not afford to spend hers. If they saw terror, they'd strip her in ten minutes and put her ashore, or worse, and Zephyrine would have gained nothing. But if they saw a woman annoyed at being boarded—if they saw a partner furious at being abandoned, a woman with a grievance and a mouth—they would want to talk. Talking took time. Time was the only cargo she had left to sell, and she meant to sell every minute of it at the highest price the market would bear.

"You're Delphine Ocaña," the gravel-voiced man said. Not a question. Her name in their files. That landed colder than the floodlights.

"I'm the person whose boat you're standing on without permission."

"We have questions about your partner."

"My partner," she said, "left me three days ago with our best dive gear and no note. Whatever questions you have, he's the one who owes the answers. I'm just the fool holding the lease on his equipment."

The man studied her face for a long moment, and Delphine gave him the face she'd perfected over six years of negotiating with harbor masters and customs men and drunk prospectors: open, aggrieved, faintly bored. Inside, her heart was going like a pump in a flooded bilge. Outside, she was a woman with a complaint.

"Director Marchetti would like a word regardless," he said.

"Then Director Marchetti can buy me dinner like anyone else."

They took her aboard the Cormorant anyway, of course. She hadn't expected otherwise. But she walked up the boarding ladder on her own feet, under her own power, unbound, and that mattered—that was the first payment of time, banked.

* * *

Marchetti received her in the survey room, which told her more than anything he said.

She'd heard about this room the way harbor people hear about everything—rumor braided with fact—and it was worse than rumor. Charts everywhere, layered under lamplight, the whole Leeward Shoal system rendered in ink and pencil corrections going back decades. And pinned at the center, under a brass weight shaped like an anchor, a survey sheet with certain bearings circled in red, and beside it a photograph of a hand-drawn map on waxed cloth.

Vex's map. Half of it, anyway. It lay there under glass like a specimen, and Delphine felt the sight of it go through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

Marchetti was smaller than she'd imagined, and neater, a compact silver-haired man with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and the unhurried manner of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice because other people raised theirs for him. He poured two glasses of rum from a decanter and slid one across the chart table toward her, and the gesture was so precisely calibrated—hospitality as instrument—that she almost admired it.

"You know why you're here," he said.

"I know why you think I'm here." She left the rum untouched. "You think I know where Zephyrine Reyes is going."

"Do you?"

"If I did, do you imagine I'd tell a room full of strangers?" She let herself glance at the map under glass, deliberately, and then away, and watched him watch her do it. "You have his chart. You have his bearings. What you don't have is what the chart doesn't say, and neither do I, because he didn't trust me with it either. That's the kind of man you're chasing, Director. He doesn't trust anybody. It's his best quality."

Marchetti smiled thinly and removed his glasses, and looked at her with the mild attention of a man reading a document. "You dove the sealed wreck. Two years ago. Night dive, no permit, off your own boat." He tapped the red-circled bearings. "We have your track logged. You were down eleven minutes. You saw the hold."

The floor of her stomach opened, quietly, the way it does in dreams. So they'd known all along. All these years of silence between her and Zephyrine, all these years of her carrying that night alone—and the fleet had her name in a logbook the whole time, filed away like a splinter waiting for the skin to soften around it.

"I dove a wreck," she said. "It was sealed. I looked in a hatch. There was nothing worth the air I wasted."

"There were bodies."

"There were dead men. On a wreck, that's not news."

"They weren't storm casualties, Ms. Ocaña." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than shouting would have, because gentleness meant he didn't need leverage yet. "You know that. You've known it for two years, and you said nothing, and now you're sitting in my chart room telling me you saw nothing worth mentioning. Which means either you're lying to me, or you've spent two years lying to yourself about what you saw. I'm prepared to work with either condition."

Delphine held his gaze and thought about Zephyrine measuring miles instead of years, and about the drowned prospector whose lungs had filled with shoal water for seeing a map, and about the eleven minutes she'd spent in that black hold with her light finding faces that had died the way men die when other men decide they should.

"I'll make you a trade," she said.

Marchetti's eyebrows rose a fraction. Interest. Good. Interest was time.

"You want him. I want my boat and my name out of your logs. Tell me what you're offering for him, and I'll tell you whether it's insulting."

"And if I'd rather simply keep you aboard until he surfaces?"

"Then you'll be keeping me aboard when the dry season front closes the shoals, and you'll have spent your last diving window babysitting a woman who knows nothing, while he does whatever he's doing somewhere you aren't." She leaned forward and put both palms flat on his beautiful charts, and watched his jaw tighten at the fingerprints. "I'm the only lead you have that isn't a dead prospector, Director. Dead men are cheap. I'm expensive. Start bidding."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours.

That was the whole design of it, and she executed it the way she'd execute a salvage plan—staged, deliberate, each concession purchased at a price. She gave them the truth about nothing and the shape of truth about everything. Yes, he'd traded his gear to Teo for a skiff—everyone would learn that within a day anyway, and admitting it bought credibility. No, she didn't know his destination—but she described, in loving detail, the kind of water he favored, the depths he trusted, the habits of a decade, until Marchetti's officers were filling notebooks with a portrait of a diver that was accurate in every particular and useful in none. Yes, she'd help them—of course she'd help them, once terms were agreed—for a price that climbed each time Marchetti hesitated, and he hesitated often, because hesitation was the sound of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

By the third hour she could feel the ship's rhythm changing around her. Boots on the deck above moving faster. The winch crew mustering. Somewhere aft, an engine coming up to pressure. The negotiation was becoming a formality, and formality was a currency losing value by the minute.

Marchetti poured her a second rum she also didn't touch, and said, pleasantly, "Your partner falsified a depth log ten years ago. Off Mayagüez. A man died."

The room went very still around her, or she did.

"He's been running from that file ever since," Marchetti continued, folding his glasses closed, opening them again. "Which makes him predictable, Ms. Ocaña. Frightened men are predictable. And it makes you valuable, because a frightened man can be bought, and the person who delivers the offer is usually someone he trusts." He slid a folded paper across the chart table. Terms. Partnership. Amnesty. The Mayagüez file, closed and shredded, in exchange for the map and the manifest and Zephyrine's signature on a salvage contract that would own him forever. "Carry this to him. You'd be saving his life. Men in his position rarely get the chance to save anything."

Delphine looked at the paper and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that this was the moment the negotiation had been built toward—not her buying time anymore, but her being converted into a weapon. And she understood something else, something that arrived whole and warm and terrible in her chest: that Marchetti had misread her completely. He thought loyalty was a lever. He thought love was a joint he could apply pressure to until it bent.

He didn't know that she had already chosen her heading.

"I'll carry it," she said.

* * *

They kept her aboard through the evening—she'd expected that, had counted on it, because every hour the Cormorant sat at anchor with its divers resting and its officers arguing over her information was an hour Zephyrine spent unwatched. They gave her a cabin with a locked door and a porthole, and the porthole faced west, across the anchorage, toward the point beyond which the deep shoal waited under its closing season.

She sat on the bunk in the near-dark and listened to the ship breathe—generators, water against steel, the far-off voices of men who thought they were winning—and she pressed her palm flat against the cool brass rim of the porthole and watched the water.

Dusk thickened. The fleet's anchor lights came on one by one, a loose constellation of white and green. Searchlight beams swept the channel twice, methodical, sweeping nothing. In the survey room overhead she could hear chairs moving, voices rising and falling, Marchetti's officers building the net they believed was already closing.

And then, low against the darkening water, at the edge of where the anchorage lights reached and no farther—a shape.

Small. Unlit. Running without running lights, hull-down, the color of the dusk itself, sliding through the outer edge of the fleet's traffic the way smoke slides under a door. For three heartbeats she lost it behind a moored barge, and her breath stopped in her chest, and her hand pressed the brass hard enough to hurt. Then it emerged on the far side, still ghosting, still silent, threading the anchored fleet like a needle through cloth—

—and she knew the lines of that skiff the way she knew her own hands. Teo's boat. Zephyrine's borrowed hull, running past thirty ships and a hundred searchlights with the whole hunting fleet anchored fat and blind around him, because they were all looking at her, at her lit decoy run and her loud demands and her expensive, useless negotiations, and not one glass was turned toward the dark water where the actual man was passing.

He didn't look toward the Cormorant. Of course he didn't. He didn't know she was aboard. He thought she was safe ashore, or he hoped it, and he ran on westward toward the point and the deep shoal and the thing he'd sworn to raise, and Delphine watched him go with her forehead nearly touching the porthole glass and tears she refused to acknowledge running freely down her face.

Terror and pride, braided together so tightly she couldn't have separated them with a knife. Terror because he was alone out there, bleeding, hunted, diving limits no man should dive, carrying a dead man's promise and a living man's guilt into water that was about to close for the season. Pride because he was magnificent—because she had never in her life seen anything move through danger with such clean, unlit purpose, and because some part of that purpose had been bought with her hours, her lie, her night of burning lights, and she would spend a hundred more nights the same way without regret.

"Go," she whispered to the glass, to the fading shape, to the man who measured miles instead of years. "Go, you stubborn son of the sea. Go."

The skiff rounded the point and was gone, and the dry-season front stood on the western horizon like a wall being built by God, and somewhere below the water past that point lay the hold that held the truth, and somewhere above it Marchetti's divers were checking their equipment by lamplight, preparing to descend at dawn.

Delphine wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, efficiently, the way she did everything.

Then she picked up Marchetti's folded offer from where she'd kept it against her ribs all evening, and she unfolded it, and she read it again by the light of the porthole—not as a weapon to carry, but as a clock. Because the paper had told her something Marchetti didn't know he'd told her: the date the amnesty expired. The date they'd stop offering and start taking.

Two days. One tide less than Zephyrine had.

She folded the paper away, lay back on the narrow bunk, and began, silently and methodically, to plan how a locked woman steals a ship's smallest boat from under a fleet's nose—because the decoy had done its work, and the next heading was hers to choose, and Delphine Ocaña had never once in her life chosen a safe one.
