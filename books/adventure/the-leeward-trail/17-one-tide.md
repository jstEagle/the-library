## 17. One Tide

The wind died an hour before dawn, and in that dead calm Zephyrine heard the sea doing something he had never heard it do at Leeward Shoals: nothing.

No swell slapping the hull. No hiss of water over the reef flats. Just the creak of the skiff's timbers as they warmed with the first light, and somewhere below, faint as a pulse through a wall, the groan of coral expanding in the heat. He sat very still in the stern with a mug of cold coffee going colder in his hands and listened to the silence the way other men listened for gunfire.

The dry season front was visible now even to a man who refused to look at horizons. It stood along the eastern sky like a wall being built all night—a long gray rampart of cloud stacked in slow shelves, its base stained the yellow-brown of Sahara dust riding the trades. By tomorrow it would be here. By tomorrow the shoals would be a churn of white water and ten-foot confused seas, undivable, unfindable, closed for six months like a door slammed by God. Today was the last tide. The last slack water. The last window of glass-clear calm in which a man could go down forty fathoms and come back up again.

Behind him, four miles north where the deep channel bent around Pointe Corail, the fleet lay at anchor. He couldn't see the Cormorant from here—Delphine had chosen this anchorage well, tucked behind a nameless cay in the lee of a reef that had opened more hulls than any navy—but he could feel them the way you feel weather in an old wound. Somewhere on that tender, Ivo Marchetti was drinking his morning espresso and looking at the same chart Zephyrine had memorized, and somewhere on that tender were two dive boats with compressors already running, because Marchetti had not survived thirty years in salvage by sleeping late on the last day.

Zephyrine drank the coffee. It tasted of salt and charred grounds and the inside of a tin cup, which was to say it tasted exactly like every morning of the last three years, and he found he was grateful for that—for one more ordinary taste before the day stopped being ordinary.

He took out Vex's compass.

It was a cheap thing, brass-cased, the kind sold in port shops to tourists who wanted to feel like explorers, and half its markings had worn away under some earlier thumb. But Augustin Vex had carried it across forty years of shoals and wrecks and flophouse beds, and when he'd pressed it into Zephyrine's hand along with the wax-cloth map, dying and smelling of rum and camphor, he had said only: *So you don't lose your heading.* As if the compass were the important thing. As if a man who navigated by dead reckoning and starlight needed a tourist's trinket. Zephyrine understood now that Vex hadn't been giving him a tool. He'd been giving him a witness. Something that would be there, in the pocket, at the bottom of the sea, when no other living soul knew what he chose.

Zephyrine set the compass on his knee and let the needle settle, and thought about the two doors.

* * *

They were both down there. That was the thing that had kept him awake since Sainte-Rose, since Colette's duplicate page had shown him what the first wreck had been all along—a decoy, a shell game played across two centuries, the real archive hidden in the hold of the ship everyone believed was empty. Two compartments, forty feet apart in the dark, separated by a collapsed bulkhead and sixty years of silt and the bones of men whose names had been paid to be forgotten.

The captain's strongbox sat aft, under the quarterdeck, where captains always kept their wealth because captains trusted iron more than they trusted crews. Teo's smuggler gossip said the colonial manifests listed specie aboard—doubloons, pieces of eight, maybe a banker's chest of gold dust—and if even a tenth of it remained, it was enough. Enough to vanish so completely that no file, no inquiry, no Ivo Marchetti with his ten-year-old incident report could ever find him again. A new name on a new coast. A boat with no history. Years bought outright instead of stolen one tide at a time.

Wealth enough to disappear forever. That was the phrase he kept turning over, and every time he turned it, it showed him another face.

Because disappearing forever—that was the craft he'd been practicing for a decade. He was good at it. He had made invisibility into a discipline the way monks made prayer into one: hours, repetitions, the stripping away of everything unnecessary until nothing remained but function. And he knew, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own depth gauge, that the strongbox was not an exit. It was a deeper room in the same prison. A man with gold still has a past; he just has better walls around it. He would spend the rest of his life listening for boots behind him, counting tides in whatever harbor gold had carried him to, measuring miles, always miles, because miles were the unit of a man running.

The evidence locker sat forward, beneath the forepeak, sealed with a bronze case he had already touched once—the twin of the one Marchetti's enforcers had taken from him at gunpoint, the twin of the manifest pages Colette had given him from her father's mail. Names. Families. Shipments recorded in a clerk's careful hand because even slavers kept accounts. And threaded through those accounts, the thread he had followed down through chapter after chapter of Vex's research and his own dives: payments, launderings, a fortune built on flesh and washed clean through the salvage fleet itself, through companies whose letterheads he had seen nailed to the office wall aboard the Cormorant while he'd stood there in a borrowed diver's suit memorizing survey charts.

Truth that would burn him too. That was the honest arithmetic of it. The moment that evidence surfaced—not hidden in a journalist's drawer but *surfaced*, named, published—it would drag everything up with it. The minister. The fleet. And a diving accident off Mayagüez ten years ago, a falsified depth log, a partner named Emilio Serrano who went down because Zephyrine Reyes wanted a record, and an inquiry that had ended with a man walking south carrying nothing but a duffel bag and a shame so heavy he'd learned to stop feeling its weight the way you stop feeling a stone in your boot.

Marchetti's leverage lived in that file. Burn the truth, burn himself.

And yet.

He looked at the compass needle trembling toward north, and he thought about what Delphine had said to him on the night she told him about the bodies in the sealed wreck—the ones that weren't storm victims, the ones with their wrists wired, the ones the navy had sealed away with concrete and silence. She had said: *You can measure your life in miles or you can measure it in years, but either way it runs out, and the only question is what it ran toward.*

He had hated her a little for saying it. He hated her less now.

* * *

The sun cleared the cay at seven minutes past six, and with it came the tide.

He felt it before he saw it—the skiff swinging gently on its anchor, the current reversing, the water over the reef flats beginning to move the wrong way, landward, filling the lagoon like a lung drawing breath. Slack water would come in ninety minutes. Ninety minutes of dead calm between the tides, when the current through the channel would drop to nothing and the visibility below would open up like a cathedral door. After that, the ebb would run hard for five hours, and after the ebb, the front.

One tide. The whole race had come down to a single tide, and he sat in a dead-calm anchorage with a tin cup and a dead man's compass and tried to feel what he was supposed to feel, and found—nothing.

That was the strange part. He had expected terror. He had spent three nights imagining this morning as a wall of fear, and instead there was just this enormous quiet, as if the storm behind him and the storm ahead of him had agreed to leave one small circle of sea in peace. The eye of something. He'd heard survivors describe it once, fishermen out of Saint-Pierre who'd ridden out a hurricane in a pirogue: the sky goes strange and golden, the birds come out, the sea goes flat as poured lead, and you know—*you know*—that the other half of the storm is coming, and there is nothing left to do but sit in the stillness and decide who you will be when it arrives.

He decided to eat. Hunger was a discipline too; a diver who skipped breakfast made mistakes at depth, and mistakes at depth were how partners drowned. He ate hardtack and a tin of sardines and half a lime, chewing slowly, watching a frigatebird hang motionless a hundred feet up, riding the dead air, waiting for the wind like everything else.

At eight-fifteen he checked his gear for the fourth time. Twin tanks, charged. Slate and pencil. The cutting tool on his calf. The lift bag, folded flat. The waterproof sleeve holding Colette's duplicate page, its bearings re-plotted twice against Vex's original, agreeing to within a cable's length. His knife. And in the thigh pocket of his exposure suit, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing he had almost not brought: a second oilcloth packet, empty, sized for documents.

Room for one case. Maybe two, if he was greedy and fast, and greedy and fast were the two words carved on most divers' graves.

Forty fathoms to the wreck. Twenty minutes down if he pushed it, and pushing it was already written into the plan whether he admitted it or not, because the plan had never once included a safe ascent. He had run the numbers on his slate the night before, by lantern light, the way he used to run numbers for the salvage office in Mayagüez, and the numbers said what he'd known they'd say: bottom time of eleven minutes at depth, then a hanging ascent of nearly an hour on the line, decompressing in open water while the front built and the fleet's boats crossed the horizon. Eleven minutes to find a compartment, choose a door, breach it, and choose again.

Eleven minutes to become whoever he was going to be for the rest of his life.

* * *

The sound reached him at nine o'clock, and it was not the storm.

Engines. More than one. Not close—four, five miles off, to the northeast, beyond the cay—but carrying flat and clear across the windless water the way sound carries when the air has gone dead. He climbed to the skiff's bow and shaded his eyes and saw them: two white wakes unwinding across the blue like stitches, running parallel, methodical, a search pattern if he'd ever seen one. Dive boats from the fleet, sweeping the channel approaches. Behind them, far off, the Cormorant herself had raised steam; he could see her smoke, a dirty smudge standing straight up in the calm, a smudge that meant boilers lit and a director who had stopped waiting.

Marchetti wasn't searching anymore. Marchetti was converging. There was only one tide left and both of them knew it, and the fleet's boats were cutting the water between Zephyrine and the open sea with the patience of men closing a net around a known point.

Which meant Marchetti had plotted the true site too. Of course he had. The enforcers had taken the bronze case days ago; Marchetti had had Vex's bearings and his own master survey ever since. The only reason the net wasn't already drawn tight was Delphine—Delphine and her decoy lights, her stolen launch, her night of running false signals through the fleet's traffic to buy him these hours. He hadn't seen her since. He didn't let himself think about what that might mean. There would be time to be afraid for her later, or there would be no later, and either way the work was the same.

He looked at the wakes again, estimating speed and bearing, and did the arithmetic he was best at, the cold kind. They would sweep the outer channel for another hour, maybe two, covering the approaches before tightening onto the site. If he went down at slack water—ten o'clock, give or take—he would surface from his hanging ascent around noon, into water that held at least one fleet dive boat within visual range, and quite possibly two, and the smoke of the Cormorant growing on the northern horizon like a bad decision.

He could run. The thought arrived honestly, and he let it stand in the eye of the storm and be examined. He could weigh anchor right now, run south ahead of the front, keep his life, keep his exile, keep the file on Mayagüez closed and the leash intact and the miles stretching out ahead of him year after year until the years ran out. No one would ever know. Vex was ashore at Sainte-Rose under a proper stone; the promise was kept; the rest was treasure, and treasure was optional.

The frigatebird cried once, high and thin, and wheeled away east.

Zephyrine looked at the compass on his knee. The needle had settled at last, dead steady, pointing the way it pointed regardless of storms or fleets or the size of a man's fear. North-northwest. Toward the shoal. Toward the two doors.

*You can measure your life in miles or you can measure it in years,* Delphine had said. But standing in the stillness with the net closing and the wall of cloud building and one tide left in the world, Zephyrine understood that she had left out the third measurement, the one that actually mattered, the one Vex's cheap brass compass had been pointing at all along:

You could measure it in what you went down for.

He drained the last of the cold coffee, poured the remainder over the side as an offering to whatever governed last tides, and began to dress.

The tanks went on his back. The weight belt went around his waist, and he cinched it with the practiced tug of twenty thousand dives. He checked his air, checked his buddy line—there was no buddy; there had been no buddy since Mayagüez, and there would be none today, and the loneliness of that fit him like the old exposure suit fit him, snug and familiar and patched in places that would never be whole. He put Vex's compass in the thigh pocket, over his heart's left neighbor, and pressed the oilcloth flat against his ribs.

Then he sat on the gunwale with his fins in his hands and gave himself the last sixty seconds, the way you give a condemned man or a bridegroom, and in that minute he did not pray and did not plan. He listened to the silence. The sea lay flat as poured lead. The golden strangeness of the eye was all around him. And four miles north, the other half of the storm was coming on with its boilers lit.

He thought about Emilio Serrano, and for the first time in ten years the thought did not arrive as a stone in his boot. It arrived as a voice, calm, almost amused, the voice of a man who had waited a decade to ask a simple question from the bottom of the sea:

*Well, Zeph? Which door?*

Zephyrine Reyes slid off the gunwale, rolled into the water, and swam for the anchor line without looking back—because the answer, whatever it was, was forty fathoms down, and the tide had already begun to turn.
