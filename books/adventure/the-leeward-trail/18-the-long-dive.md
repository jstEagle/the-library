## 18. The Long Dive

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Zephyrine felt it before he saw it—a long exhale beneath the skiff, the sea drawing breath for the last time before the dry season front came down like a lid.

One tide. That was all that remained. He had counted them like coins for three years, and now he was down to his last one, and it was already spending itself against the hull while he checked his gauges a third time and a fourth, because checking was what his hands knew how to do when his mind wanted to run.

The strongbox chamber lay at forty-one meters. The evidence locker—Vex's duplicate page had been unambiguous about this, Colette's voice steady in his memory as she handed over the brittle paper—lay at fifty-three. Past recreational limits. Past the limits he'd written into his own salvage protocols, back when he still pretended to be a careful man. Bottom time at that depth: twelve minutes on air, maybe fifteen if he lied to himself about the margins, and every minute of lie would have to be paid back on the way up, decompression stops hanging off him like debts, while Marchetti's divers came down their lines with mixed gas and surface support and all the patience of men who were being paid by the hour.

They would be behind him. They might already be above him.

He looked west, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong—the dry season front, a wall of cloud stacked up like masonry, dragging its shadow across the water toward the shoals. When it arrived, the sea would stand up in whitecaps and no one would dive for days, and whatever was left undone would stay done forever.

Delphine's launch sat dark against the point where she'd anchored it, her decoy lights still burning out over the eastern channel where she'd left them, a little constellation of lies to keep the fleet's eyes pointed the wrong way. He hadn't seen her since dawn yesterday. He didn't know if she'd made it clear of the Cormorant's patrol boats. He let himself think about that for exactly three seconds, then sealed it away, because there was no version of this morning where he could afford to carry both her and Mayagüez, and one of them was already dead.

He spat in his mask, rubbed the glass, rinsed it. Pulled the regulator between his teeth and breathed, tasting the rubber-and-metal flatness of compressed air, the taste of every working day of his exile.

"Twelve minutes," he said aloud, to no one, to himself, to the water. "In and out."

The sea took him without comment.

* * *

The descent was a falling through colors: green going teal going blue going the particular bruised violet that lived below thirty meters, where the light gave up pretending and everything became silhouette. He pulled himself down the anchor line he'd set at slack tide two nights ago, hand over hand, watching his depth gauge climb through numbers he had spent ten years refusing to visit.

Thirty-five. Forty.

At forty-five meters the cold found him, a hand closing around his chest, and the nitrogen began its work—not drunk yet, not yet, but knocking, whispering at the edges of his thoughts the way it always did, making the deep feel less like a place and more like a mood. He had a rule for this. He'd always had rules. Rules were how a man who had killed someone with carelessness convinced himself he was now careful.

Forty-eight. The wreck's superstructure rose out of the gloom below him, the colonial slaver's bones furred with coral, railings like ribs, and beyond the broken quarterdeck the black rectangle of the hold hatch, the mouth Vex's map had promised led down to the captain's country.

And above him—far above, a pale smudge against the brightening surface—lights. Three of them, descending slow along lines dropped from boats he couldn't see. Marchetti's divers, coming down the wedding cake of their own schedule, forty minutes behind him or twenty or five; at this depth he couldn't trust his sense of time any more than he could trust his sense of mercy.

Zephyrine let go of the line and swam for the hatch.

* * *

Inside, the hold was a cathedral built by drowning.

His light swept across rows of collapsed shelving, copper fasteners gone green as old patina, the silt lying over everything like gray snow that had been falling for two hundred years. His bubbles went up ahead of him and vanished into the dark, and the dark swallowed them without echo, and the silence down here was total in the way only flooded iron can be silent—silence with weight, silence pressing on the eardrums like a confession waiting to be spoken.

Fifty-one meters. Fifty-three.

The evidence locker was where the duplicate page said it would be: an iron cabinet bolted to the frame of the aft bulkhead, its door swollen shut by two centuries of rust, its face stamped with letters he could read even through the coral crust. MANIFESTO. REGISTRO. And beneath, smaller, almost worn away: the name of a shipping house that still existed, that still had offices in Fort-de-France, whose founder's portrait hung in the minister's residence.

He got the pry bar under the door's lip and leaned into it, and the rust gave with a shriek he felt in his teeth more than heard, and the door swung open into a darkness that smelled—even through the regulator, even underwater—of old paper and older money.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, wax-sealed, wrapped in tarred canvas that had done its work. Names. The manifest case he'd lost to Marchetti's enforcers had been one bronze box; this was the whole archive, the full accounting, every family listed, every price paid, every fortune begun here in the dark and laundered upward into respectability until it came out the other side as ministries and salvage fleets and men like Ivo Marchetti who hunted evidence and called it treasure.

Twelve minutes. He was at nine.

Take it all? Impossible—the canvas bundles weighed like sin, and he had one lift bag and one pair of arms and a decompression ceiling already forming overhead like a debt collector's shadow. He took four ledgers, the ones the duplicate page had marked, wrapped them in the bag, clipped it to his harness. Then his light caught something else at the back of the locker, wedged upright: a strongbox, small, iron, its lock long since eaten through, its lid hanging open.

Empty.

No—not empty. A single object lay in the bottom, and when he lifted it out his glove came away smeared with corrosion, and he held it up to his beam and felt the nitrogen lean close and whisper: *you know what this is.*

A ship's bell, hand-sized, the kind they struck to mark the watches. Cast bronze. And stamped around its crown, in letters fine as stitching:

*MAYAGÜEZ.*

He stared at it and the hold tilted around him, or he tilted, or the nitrogen reached down through ten years and turned the world on its axis, because there was no bell from the *Mayagüez* in a colonial slaver's hold two hundred years old. There was no possible reason. Except that the sea kept what it was given, and somewhere above him, somewhere in the water column between here and the light, the *Mayagüez* had gone down too—or things from her had drifted, sunk, been swallowed by the same shoals, the same appetite—and the sea had filed it here, in the archive, with everything else that people had paid to lose.

Tomás's bell.

Tomás's watch bell, which had been hanging by the helm the morning the *Mayagüez* dove the wall off Puerto Rico, the morning Zephyrine falsified the depth log, the morning he wrote thirty-eight meters where the gauges said forty-six because forty-six would have meant scrubbing the record, would have meant admitting the error was his, would have meant Tomás diving the second tank alone on a bottom time that was already poisoned—

—and Tomás had come up without his regulator in his mouth, and the inquiry had asked questions, and Zephyrine had signed the log with a steady hand and flown south before the ink of his own lie had dried.

Nine minutes became eleven while he held the bell and the hold breathed around him.

He should have taken it. Some part of him understood that later, understood that leaving it was leaving the last piece of Tomás to the dark. But his hands wouldn't close around it. His hands had closed around a pen ten years ago instead of the truth, and they seemed to remember, and they refused.

He turned for the hatch, and the phantom was waiting for him in the doorway.

* * *

It was not a ghost. He knew that. Ghosts required a superstition he had never been able to afford; his disbelief was load-bearing, part of the architecture of the man he'd built over the corpse of the man he'd been. What stood silhouetted in the hatchway, backlit by the faint violet glow of the open water, was his own nitrogen-soaked brain wearing Tomás's shape—broad shoulders, the crooked way he'd cocked his head when he laughed, the dive knife with the taped grip that Zephyrine had bought him in Mayagüez harbor the week before.

But knowing a thing is a hallucination has never once stopped a hallucination.

*You're past your limit,* the shape said, and its voice came through the water the way voices do at depth, arriving inside the skull rather than the ear. *Eleven minutes. Twelve. You're standing in a flooded room fifty-three meters down talking to a dead man, Zeph. How does that fit in your protocols?*

"I don't have time," Zephyrine said, and heard his own voice boom absurdly inside the mask, huge and cracked.

*You never have time. That's the whole trick, isn't it? Miles, not years. Tides, not birthdays. Keep moving so you never have to stand still anywhere long enough for me to catch up.* The shape drifted aside, courteous, letting him pass, and somehow that was worse than if it had blocked the way. *Well. Here we are. Standing still. I've been waiting in every deep place you ever dove. You knew that, right? Every time you went past forty meters, some part of you hoped.*

"Shut up." His breathing was ragged now, the regulator rasping, and he made himself check his gauge—eighty bar, still enough, barely, if he moved—if he moved *now*—

*Say it.* The shape didn't move. Behind it, far above, the descending lights had grown brighter, closer, Marchetti's divers dropping down their lines with the patience of men lowering a net. *Ten years, Zeph. Ten years of carrying it as ballast, trimmed perfect, never cutting it loose because if you cut it loose you'd shoot for the surface and I'm the only thing keeping you down here where it's safe. Say it out loud. Into the mask. Nobody hears you down here but me and the silt.*

"There's nothing to say." He tried to swim past. The shape put out a hand—the taped-grip knife glinting—and though he knew, *knew*, that nothing solid stood in the water, his body stopped as if it had struck a wall, because the body believes what the deep tells it.

*You wrote thirty-eight,* the phantom said gently. *I read the log after. They showed me—no. No, I'm dead. I never read anything.* It laughed, and the laugh was Tomás's laugh, and Zephyrine's chest seized so hard around the regulator that for one full second he couldn't breathe at all. *You wrote thirty-eight meters because thirty-eight was survivable and forty-six was your fault. One number. One digit changed, and I went down with a bottom time that was already spent, and my second tank was half empty because you logged the fill wrong too, didn't you—*

"Yes." The word tore out of him, bubbling, enormous in the mask. "Yes. All right. Yes."

The hold went very quiet. Even his bubbles seemed to hang, listening.

*I falsified the log,* Zephyrine said, and each word cost him air he could not spare, and he spent it anyway, profligate, like a man burning his own house. *Both entries. The depth and the fill. Because the record was mine, my clean record, my perfect safety history, and I loved it more than—I didn't love it more than you, I didn't, but I acted like I did, and acting is the only truth there is. You went down on numbers I invented. You drowned on paper. And I signed it, and I flew south, and I buried the man who could do that, and I have been diving as that man's ghost for ten years, measuring miles because miles end and years don't—*

His vision was graying at the edges. Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, grief—all the same gray, all rising.

*There,* the phantom said, and its voice had changed, softened, become less than a voice, becoming just water and memory and the sound of his own heart. *That's all it was. That's the whole weight. Say it once and it's buoyant.*

"You're not real," Zephyrine whispered.

*No,* the shape agreed, already dissolving, already becoming silt-shadow and beam-light and nothing. *But the confession is. Take it up with you. It'll decompress better than you will.*

The lights above flared white, and a diver's fin broke the plane of the hatchway, and Zephyrine ran.

* * *

He went up the anchor line like a man climbing out of his own grave, and the decompression stops took their tolls one by one—fifteen meters, three minutes, hanging in the blue while his body screamed at him to rise; twelve meters; nine—and at six meters, in the long final stop where the sea holds you like a creditor, he saw the first of Marchetti's divers descend past him.

The man paused. Visor to visor, six meters apart in the brightening water, close enough that Zephyrine could see the fleet's insignia stenciled on the tank, the slate strapped to the man's wrist with coordinates chalked on it. The diver looked at him, at the lift bag heavy with ledgers on his harness, and made no move at all—because at six meters on a decompression stop, attacking a man meant killing yourself too, and the arithmetic of the deep applies to everyone equally.

The diver raised one hand, slowly, and pointed up. Then pointed at Zephyrine. Then drew a finger across his throat, unhurried, almost kind.

Then he continued down toward the hold, toward the empty locker, toward the bell that waited in the dark with Tomás's name on it, and Zephyrine watched him go and felt nothing he could name except lighter—ten pounds lighter, a hundred, the exact weight of one sentence spoken aloud after ten years.

Above him the surface burned silver, and beyond it the dry season front had come down at last, a gray wall marching across the shoals, and the first cold wind of it rippled the water as his head broke through into the rain.

Delphine's launch was not at its anchorage.

Instead, running straight at him through the building sea with her engines wide open and her deck guns of rain streaming off her bow, came a boat flying the salvage fleet's colors—and standing at her rail, unmistakable even at distance, hatless in the storm, was Ivo Marchetti.
