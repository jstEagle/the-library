## 19. Scuttled

The evidence locker was a strongbox's ugly sister.

Zephyrine found it aft of the flooded hold, bolted to the frame beneath the captain's cabin, an iron cabinet the size of a sea chest with a brass plate that had once read something official and now read nothing at all—corrosion had eaten the letters the way the sea ate everything it was given time enough to keep. The lock had surrendered decades ago. The door hung open like a jaw.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, sealed with pitch that still smelled faintly of pine when he cut it with his knife, were the papers.

He had imagined gold on the way down. He had imagined it again in the dark of the hold, when the phantom of Tomás Vidal stood at his shoulder wearing the face of a drowned man and Zephyrine had spoken the truth of Mayagüez aloud into his mask for the first time in ten years—*I falsified the log, I cut your line short to save my record, I let you die so I could be clean*—and the phantom had not forgiven him, because phantoms don't, but it had stepped aside, which was more than he deserved.

Now there were no phantoms. There was only the work.

He pulled the first bundle free and held it into the beam of his lamp. Paper should not have survived forty years underwater, but paper sealed in pitch is a different animal than paper left to the water, and whoever had packed this locker had done it with the care of a man hiding something he expected to come back for. The top sheet was a manifest. Not cargo—cargo meant coin, crates, tonnage. This was a manifest of *people*. Names in a clerk's careful hand, ages, origins, prices. A colonial-era slaver's ledger, complete as a census.

Below it, more bundles. Correspondence. Letters of credit drawn on houses whose names Zephyrine recognized from the modern world—the same names stamped on the salvage fleet's tendering documents, the same names Marchetti answered to. A minister's fortune, built here in this hold, laundered through a century and surfaced again as respectability. And beneath all of it, the thing that made his hands go still: a signed order, dated within living memory, authorizing the *sealing* of this wreck by naval survey. Someone had paid to close this hole in the water and keep it closed. The navy hadn't sealed the wrong ship by accident. They'd been sent to bury the evidence, and they'd buried the decoy instead, and someone had been paying Marchetti ever since to make sure no one dug twice.

The lamp flickered. Above him, faint through thirty feet of green water and the broken deck plating, he heard it: engines. The Cormorant's boats. And under the engines, a sound he knew from the survey room aboard the tender—the flat mechanical knock of a compressor charging dive lines.

Marchetti's divers were coming down.

* * *

There was no time to be careful, so he was fast instead.

He stripped the oilcloth bundles from the locker and stuffed them into the waterproof satchel strapped to his chest—six bundles, seven, eight, until the satchel strained against its buckles and the last bundle, the thinnest one, the one with the minister's signature, went in against his sternum where he could feel it press with every breath. He checked the seals twice. He had watched men drown for want of checking seals.

Then he turned to the strongbox.

It sat against the far bulkhead where the fleet's survey charts said it would sit, iron-banded, brass-cornered, worth more than everything Zephyrine had earned in ten years of salvage diving. Wealth enough to vanish forever. Wealth enough to buy a new name in a new port where no one had heard of Mayagüez or Leeward Shoals or a man named ox-alpha who measured his life in miles. He put his palm flat against the cold lid and felt the sea's patience in the metal.

*I could take you,* he told it. *I could take you and the papers both.*

But the satchel was already heavy, and the passage behind him was already narrow, and above him the compressors knocked their patient rhythm, and somewhere in his chest a colder arithmetic was running numbers it had learned in ten years of exile: a man carrying gold swims slow. A man carrying gold surfaces into searchlights. A man carrying gold has something to lose, and Marchetti's whole leverage rested on men having things to lose.

The papers were different. The papers were a fire, and fires don't weigh anything until they burn you.

He took out the cutting charge.

The fleet had planted its own charges along the wreck's spine two seasons ago—shaped charges meant to drop the remaining deck structure and seal the hold under collapsed plating, the final burial. Marchetti's divers had wired them and run the firing line up to a buoy, waiting for the dry season's end and the last quiet week to finish the job. But wiring is wiring, and Zephyrine had spent three years working these shoals, and he had found the firing line in the dark the way you find anything you've made yourself need to find: by refusing to come up without it.

He clipped his own charge to the strongbox chamber's doorframe—not the box itself; the frame, the load-bearing rib of iron that held the passage roof above the strongbox—and set the fuse for ninety seconds. Then he swam to the junction where the flooded hold met the escape scuttle, the crawl-space Delphine's charts had shown him, the way out that wasn't a way out until you committed to it, and he looked back once at the strongbox gleaming in his lamplight like a closed eye.

"Sleep," he said, and did not know if he meant the box or the dead or himself.

Then he kicked for the scuttle, pulling the firing line after him, and gave it everything.

* * *

Ninety seconds is a long time underwater when you are counting.

He counted in strokes. Twenty strokes to clear the hold. Thirty to reach the scuttle's mouth, a gap barely wide enough for his shoulders with the satchel dragging at his chest like a second heart. Forty, fifty, wriggling through, the wreck's ribs pressing his back, the satchel snagging on a rivet and tearing free with a sound he felt rather than heard—a small rip, a small loss, a corner of tarred canvas left behind like a tip left on a table.

At sixty strokes the charge went off.

The sea did not roar; it *punched*. The pressure wave came through the water and through his body at once, a fist closing around his lungs, his mask pressing against his face, and behind him the passage he had crawled through folded shut like a book closing. Dust and silt bloomed outward in a rolling brown cloud, and through it, dimly, he saw the strongbox chamber's roof come down in a single grinding sheet of iron and coral and forty years of silence, burying the strongbox, burying the gold, burying the last road back.

Marchetti's divers, descending the lines above, would find rubble. Would find a wreck that had finally, permanently, stopped talking.

Zephyrine swam.

* * *

The launch was where Delphine had left it, tucked in the lee of the outer reef a quarter mile east, riding low and quiet with her engine hood cracked and her running lights doused. He had argued with her about it at dawn—*you shouldn't be anywhere near this water*—and she had looked at him the way she looked at bad weather coming and said, "Then swim faster," and taken her own boat north to run decoy lights through the fleet anchorage, buying him hours with a heading that might kill her.

That had been hours ago. He had no way of knowing what those hours had bought, or what they had cost.

He broke the surface beside the launch's transom and hung there for a moment, breathing, letting the night air hit his face, the satchel a dead weight trying to pull him back down toward the wreck he had just made into a grave. Behind him, to the west, the Cormorant's lights burned in a ragged arc across the water, and he could hear voices carrying over the swell—divers surfacing, radios crackling, the particular tone of men reporting failure. Somewhere in that arc, Delphine's decoy lights had gone dark an hour past, and he refused to think about what that meant, and thought about nothing else.

He hauled himself over the transom.

The launch rocked, settled. He lay on the deck plates with the satchel still strapped to his chest and stared up at a sky rinsed clean by the passing squall, stars scattered thick as salt, and he waited for the fear to arrive. It didn't. That was the strange part. Ten years of fear—fear of files, fear of names, fear of being known—and now, with the evidence of a minister's crimes pressed against his sternum and a salvage fleet hunting the water he floated on, there was only a vast and ringing quiet, like the inside of a bell after the strike.

He had buried the strongbox. He had buried the passage. He had buried, somewhere in the brown cloud behind him, the last version of himself that wanted gold enough to drown for it.

What was left felt, for the first time in ten years, light enough to float.

* * *

He got the engine going on the third pull, keeping the revs low, steering east-northeast away from the fleet's arc and toward the open channel where the dry season's front was building on the horizon—a long black shelf of cloud with lightning walking inside it, the weather that would close the shoals for six months and make tonight the last tide anyone could work this water. Marchetti's leverage had died with the wreck. But Marchetti himself was still alive, still armed, still commanding a dozen boats between Zephyrine and Sainte-Rose, and a man who has just lost his treasure becomes a very simple creature: a man who wants revenge.

The radio on Delphine's console hissed. He almost didn't touch it. Then a voice came through the static, thin and distorted and unmistakably hers, flattened by distance and interference into something calm and procedural, the voice she used for reading coordinates:

*"—repeat, all fleet units, the diver's skiff was sighted heading south-southeast off Pointe Corail, repeat, south-southeast. Recommend units converge—"*

South-southeast. Away from the channel. Away from the front. Away from every heading that made sense.

She was alive. She was lying to the fleet. She was spending herself to bend their search pattern wrong, and every boat that turned south was a boat that wasn't looking northeast, and Zephyrine sat in the dark of her stolen launch with his hand pressed flat against the radio as if he could answer her through it, and did not dare transmit, and did not turn the dial, and listened until her voice dissolved into static and the fleet's boats began, one by one, to swing south.

He turned the bow northeast, toward Sainte-Rose, toward the hill cemetery, toward Colette Vex and the duplicate page that had shown him the true hold, toward the journalist in Fort-de-France who would turn eight bundles of pitch-sealed paper into a fire no one could put out.

The satchel rode against his chest. The front walked closer across the water, lightning counting down inside it like a fuse.

One tide left. One night. Everything he had taken from the deep was worthless until it reached the light—and the whole world between here and daylight was full of men paid to stop him.

He opened the throttle.
