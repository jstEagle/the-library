## 21. Miles Into Years

The dry season had come and gone the way grief comes and goes—not all at once, but in stages, each one taking something with it that you only noticed later by its absence.

Zephyrine stood at the tiller of Delphine's launch, the one she'd stolen back from the fleet's anchorage with a borrowed key and a straight face, and watched Leeward Shoals rise out of a green morning sea. The water over the shoals was the color of bottle glass held up to the sun, and the reef showed itself in long dark seams, like handwriting on a page he'd spent six tides learning to read and a lifetime learning to fear.

He wasn't diving today. That was the whole point.

He'd said it aloud to himself twice on the run out from Port Anse, once as instruction and once as prayer: *Not diving. Looking.* His gear sat in its bag at his feet, zipped shut, and he kept his hand resting on the bag's mouth the way a man rests his hand on a dog's collar—not because the dog will bolt, but because the habit of holding on outlives the need for it.

Delphine sat forward on the bow thwart, barefoot, her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth she'd worn through every bad day of the past season. She didn't ask why they were going. She'd stopped asking him why about most things months ago, which was either trust or surrender, and he'd decided—without evidence, without proof, the way he decided everything now—that it was trust.

The shoals came up slow. The navy buoy was gone; someone had cut it loose weeks after the Cormorant limped north with her divers empty-handed and her director's name in every paper from Fort-de-France to Bridgetown. Where the seal had been there was just water again, ordinary water, holding nothing anyone official wanted.

But underneath it, forty feet down and half-buried in sand the color of old bone, lay what was left of the hold. He knew its shape now the way he knew the shape of his own hands. The charges Marchetti's men had set to seal it had done their work too well—the passage behind him had come down in a roar of coral and ballast stone while he swam through blackness with the manifest case strapped to his chest, counting his heartbeats because he no longer trusted his gauges, counting them because Vex had taught him that a man who counts something stays alive longer than a man who counts on something.

He brought the launch in over the outer seam and dropped anchor in the lee of the reef, where the swell broke soft and white and the bottom showed clear at twelve feet.

"There," Delphine said, pointing.

He followed her finger. Below the hull, in the sandy channel between two coral heads, the wreck's shadow lay like a sleeping animal. From up here it was nothing—a darker patch, a suggestion, a place where the light went down and didn't quite come back the same. Ten years ago he would have been over the side already, mask on, breath held, already halfway to proving something to somebody who wasn't watching.

He stayed in the boat.

* * *

They ate breakfast on the thwart—hard bread, salt fish, mango so ripe it ran down their wrists—and neither of them talked much, because there was nothing left that needed saying and both of them had learned, separately and painfully, the difference between silence that hides and silence that holds.

It was Delphine who broke it, eventually, the way she always did, with something practical.

"The minister resigned Tuesday," she said. "My cousin saw it in the Gazette. Photograph and everything. Standing on the steps of the Palais with his wife behind him looking like a woman who'd known for years."

"I heard."

"Marchetti hasn't been heard from since. Some say Barbados. Some say farther." She turned a piece of bread in her fingers, not eating it. "Teo says there's a price on information now instead of a price on divers. Says the whole fleet's coming apart like wet rope."

"Good," Zephyrine said, and found that he meant it without heat, which surprised him. For months the name Marchetti had lived in his chest like a swallowed hook. Now it was just a name. Somewhere a man was packing suitcases in a hurry, and Zephyrine Reyes was eating salt fish in a boat, and the world had sorted itself out without requiring anything further from him.

"You're not even curious where he went?"

"No."

Delphine looked at him for a long moment. "That's new," she said. "You used to be curious about everything. It's how you got into trouble. Curiosity and a depth gauge you didn't respect."

"That was another man."

"Was it?" She said it gently, without challenge, the way she asked all her real questions. "Because I dove with that man. I've eaten breakfast with this one. And I'm telling you, from where I sit, the distance between them is about four feet of open water."

He didn't answer. He looked down through the green glass at the shadow of the hold, and thought about the dark, and thought about how he'd spoken into his mask at sixty feet with his partner's ghost beside him—said the truth of Mayagüez out loud, Étienne, I falsified the log, I left you down there to protect my record, I have carried it ten years and it has carried me further—and how the words had gone up through the water in bubbles and burst somewhere near the surface, unseen, unheard, and how he had surfaced anyway, alive, lighter by exactly the weight of the thing he'd put down.

Some confessions need a witness. His hadn't. It had needed only water, which takes everything and tells no one.

* * *

In the afternoon, when the light came slantwise and the reef threw its longest shadows, he opened the salvage bag—not for the gear but for what was wrapped in a shirt at the bottom of it.

Vex's compass.

Brass case gone green at the seams, glass cracked across the lower quadrant, needle still true. Augustin Vex had carried it through thirty years of shoals and flophouses and half-mapped wrecks, and had pressed it into Zephyrine's hands in a room that smelled of camphor and dying, along with a wax-cloth map and a promise about ashes and a hill at Sainte-Rose. *A man should end up claimed by somewhere,* Vex had told him. *Even if it's only six feet of it.*

Zephyrine had buried the ashes on the hill in the rain, with Colette standing beside him under a black umbrella she refused to open, and the promise had turned out to be the one cargo on the whole Leeward Trail that couldn't be scuttled, sold, or traded. He'd tried, God knew. He'd offered it up twice as collateral—one tide to Marchetti's enforcers, once to his own exhaustion—and both times it had come back to him like ballast, like the thing that kept him upright in heavy weather.

Now he held the compass flat on his palm and let the needle find north, then east, then settle wherever it pleased, swinging lazy in the small movements of his hand.

"What are you doing?" Delphine asked.

"Deciding something."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was, he suspected, the actual skill that had made her a salvager—everyone else thought it was lungs or nerve, but it was waiting, knowing how long a thing could be left alone before it needed you.

"He'd have hated this spot," Zephyrine said. "Too shallow. No wreck worth the name. He liked his water deep and his odds bad." He turned the compass over. The maker's mark was worn to a ghost. "But he also said a map isn't a promise until somebody walks it. Or swims it. So."

He stripped off his shirt and shoes, took the compass in one fist, and went over the side.

The water took him the way it always had—cool hand, closed door—and he kicked down through the bright layers into the dimmer ones, ears clearing, the reef rising to meet him. Twelve feet. Twenty. The shadow of the hold resolved out of the general dark: the broken spine of the keel, the scatter of ballast stone, the collapsed mouth of the passage where the charges had brought the coral down like a jaw closing. Sand had drifted over most of it. In a year the sea would have it all. In five, a diver would swim here and see nothing but reef, and that seemed right to him. The sea kept what it kept and gave back what it gave back, and it had never once owed him an explanation.

He found a shelf of dead coral above the collapsed passage, flat as a table, and set the compass on it. The brass caught what light remained and threw it back dull and green. The needle swung, wavered, settled—pointing, as far as he could tell, at nothing in particular, which was to say at everything, which was to say home, depending on what a man meant by the word.

*You're claimed,* he told Vex silently, the words forming in the closed space behind the regulator's mouthpiece. *Sainte-Rose hill. Six feet of it. And your daughter writes to me now, did you know that? Short letters. She signs them C. and asks nothing, which is how I know she's yours.*

The needle trembled and held.

He hung there a moment longer in the green half-light, breathing slow, watching his own exhaust rise in silver ropes toward a surface he could actually see. It occurred to him—he noticed it the way you notice a sound stopping—that he was not afraid. Forty feet down, alone, over a sealed wreck, with the whole drowned history of the trail beneath him, and his chest was quiet. His hands were quiet. Somewhere in the last year, without ceremony, without his permission, the water had stopped being the place where men went to disappear and become the place where he simply went.

He ascended slowly, honoring every stop, and came up into a sky so blue it looked freshly painted.

* * *

Delphine had the launch turned into the evening breeze when he climbed aboard, and they ran south with the sun going down ahead of them like a coin dropping into a pocket. The shoals fell away astern. He didn't look back. That, too, was new.

They anchored that night in the lee of a cay he'd have skipped past a year ago—no shelter worth the name, no reason to stop—but Delphine wanted to cook on the beach and he found he wanted whatever she wanted, which was another thing that had happened to him without his permission. They built a small fire of driftwood and ate grilled fish and drank rum cut with coconut water, and the stars came out in the reckless profusion they only manage over open water, thick as spilled grain.

Delphine sat with her knees drawn up, firelight on her face, and studied him across the flames with the expression she wore when she was about to say the thing she'd been carrying since morning.

"So," she said. "The season's over. The fleet's dead. The file on Mayagüez is closed—or as good as. You're a free man, Zephyrine Reyes, if such a thing exists." She turned the rum bottle in her hands. "What are you going to measure now?"

He looked at her.

"You know what I mean," she said. "I've watched you do it for three years. Miles. Tides. Bottom time. Air remaining. Heartbeats between one safe place and the next. You measure everything except the thing other people measure, and I used to think it was because you were running. But you're not running anymore. So." She shrugged, and the gesture was almost shy, which on Delphine was as rare as calm water on the open Atlantic. "What is it? What's the unit?"

He was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Out past the reef line, the sea breathed against the sand in long, patient strokes, the way it had before him and would after, indifferent and generous in the same motion.

"I don't know yet," he said.

And then he waited for the old feeling to arrive—the cold drop in the stomach, the sense of a man standing on a dock with no ship and no heading, the exile opening under his feet like a hatch. It had always come before. Not-knowing had been the enemy his whole adult life; he had filled every unknown hour with arithmetic, tides and depths and distances, because a man who is calculating cannot simultaneously be drowning.

It didn't come.

Instead there was something else, sitting in his chest where the dread used to berth. Lighter. Looser. Almost warm. He turned it over mentally, prodded it, tested it against everything he knew about himself, and could not make it be fear no matter how he handled it.

"Not-knowing," he said finally, and heard his own voice come out steady, almost wondering. "For the first time it doesn't feel like exile. It feels like—" He stopped. Started again. "You know what it feels like? It feels like the minute after you surface. When you've got the wreck behind you and the boat ahead of you and nobody's shooting at you, and for about ten seconds the whole ocean belongs to nobody at all."

Delphine smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way her smiles did, rare and total, like weather breaking.

"Ten seconds," she said. "That's a small unit of measurement, diver."

"It's the only honest one I've got." He reached over and took the rum bottle, poured two fingers into her cup and refilled it, and found his hand didn't shake. "Maybe I'll get better at it. Maybe it stretches. Vex measured his life in promises at the end. Said they were the only cargo that appreciates." He looked into the fire. "I thought he was being sentimental. Dying men get a pass on sentiment. But he might've been onto something."

"And your next promise?" Delphine asked. "Got one lined up?"

He started to say no. The word was actually in his mouth, shaped and ready, the reflex of ten years—because promises were leashes, because promises were how men like Marchetti got their hooks in, because the last great promise he'd made had ended with a man named Étienne on the bottom off Mayagüez—

But what came out instead, quietly, surprising him more than it seemed to surprise her, was:

"Colette wrote that the cemetery wall at Sainte-Rose is coming down. Storm damage. The parish has no money." He turned the cup in his hands. "I sent something. Anonymous. Signed it 'the diver.' But walls take more than money. They take hands. And I've been thinking—" He paused, and laughed shortly at himself, at the absurdity of it, the ex-salvage-diver, the man who'd made invisibility a craft, planning a return to a hillside village where everyone would remember Augustin Vex and wonder who the stranger was. "I've been thinking maybe a man ought to be claimed by somewhere too. Even if it's only six feet of wall he helped rebuild."

Delphine said nothing for a moment. Then she leaned across the fire, took his chin in her rough hand, and turned his face toward hers the way she'd turn a compass needle.

"Sainte-Rose," she said. "Dry season's ending. Roads'll be open inside the month."

"You don't have to—"

"I know what I don't have to do." She released his chin and sat back, and the firelight made her eyes bright and unreadable. "I'm telling you my heading, Zeph. You can sail with it or not."

He looked at her across the small fire, this woman who had run decoy lights through a hostile fleet for him, who had lied to enforcers with a smile, who had never once asked him to be anyone other than what he was and had somehow, impossibly, loved him anyway—and he understood that the answer to her question had already been given, days ago, in the space between one tide and the next, in the wordless agreement of two people who kept anchoring in the same coves.

"With it," he said. "Obviously."

She nodded once, satisfied, and looked away toward the water, and the conversation was over, because with Delphine the important things were always settled briefly and the unimportant ones argued for hours.

Later, when she'd rolled into her blanket and her breathing had gone slow and even, Zephyrine sat alone by the coals and let the night happen to him. The stars wheeled. The sea kept its long patience. And in his shirt pocket, against his heart, was a letter he'd read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth—Colette's latest, three lines long, arriving that morning with the supply boat.

*The mason says the wall will hold if the footing is redone before the rains. My father always said you were a man who finished things. The hill is beautiful this time of year. There are flowers growing wild along the east path—I have pressed one inside, though I don't know why, except that he would have.*

He drew the letter out now, and unfolded it, and shook it gently over his palm. A small dried flower fell into his hand, purple and papery-thin, a bloom from the wild growth along the east path of a hillside cemetery he had visited exactly once, in the rain, keeping a promise to a man whose bones were finally where they belonged.

Zephyrine held it up to the last of the firelight and thought: *a man who finishes things.*

There was a wall at Sainte-Rose with bad footing. There was a road that opened inside the month. There was a woman asleep behind him who had already chosen her heading, and a flower in his hand from a hill he'd begun, without noticing the exact tide of it, to think of as his.

He folded the letter carefully around the flower and put it back over his heart, and for the first time in ten years, Zephyrine Reyes went to sleep without counting anything at all.
