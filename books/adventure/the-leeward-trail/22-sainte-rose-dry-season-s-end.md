## 22. Sainte-Rose, Dry Season's End

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except the postmistress, Madame Aurelle, who believed that all important correspondence arrived on Tuesdays because the Lord rested on Sundays and the devil took Mondays off.

Colette Vex was on her knees in the churchyard when the bicycle bell rang at the gate — that tin, cheerful sound she had come to associate with bad news, because in Sainte-Rose the only people who rode bicycles with any urgency were the postmistress and the doctor, and neither of them ever came up the hill for good reasons.

She sat back on her heels and looked at her hands. She had been clearing the wild basil from her father's grave, or rather from the place where his grave would be, because the mason was three months behind on the headstone and the plot was marked only by a wooden cross that Colette had painted white herself, twice, the second time in the rain because she couldn't bear the thought of the rain washing the first coat thin and her father lying under something half-finished. He had been a man who finished things. Half a map, she had learned. Even his maps were only ever half a map. But the half he'd mailed her, folded into an envelope with a stamp from Port Anse and a note that said *keep this where the sea can't reach it, ma chérie* — that half had been whole enough.

The bell rang again.

"Mademoiselle Vex!" Madame Aurelle stood at the gate with her bicycle propped against the wall, holding an envelope out at arm's length as though it might be warm. "From Fort-de-France. No return address. I checked it for powder. Your father always said I was paranoid, God rest him."

"My father said you were the only honest woman on the island," Colette said, and took the letter.

She did not open it in front of the postmistress. This was a discipline she had learned in the months since the diver came down from the hill with her father's ashes in a coffee tin and a face like a man who had been shown something he couldn't un-see. In Sainte-Rose, grief was communal property, and Colette had learned to ration hers.

* * *

She opened it at her kitchen table with a paring knife, because she had learned that much from her father too — that anything worth having was worth opening carefully, and that a man who tore open envelopes was a man who tore open other things.

The paper inside was good stock, heavy, the kind that held ink without bleeding. There was no letter. There was a bank draft, drawn on a Fort-de-France institution she'd have to look up, for an amount that made her set the paper down and press her palm flat against the table to be sure her hand wasn't shaking. Enough to rebuild the cemetery wall. Not enough to be obscene. The exact amount, she realized after a moment, that Père Lucien had quoted her in the spring, when she'd asked him what it would cost to repair the eastern wall before the next hurricane season took it down entirely — the wall her grandfather had helped lay, the wall that held back the hillside, the wall where the names of the old families were carved into the coping stones, some of them names that had been carved there by men who had bought and sold other men.

And beneath the draft, folded once, was a flower.

A pressed flower. Frangipani, white gone to cream, its petals thin as communion wafers, flattened with the kind of patience that takes weeks — pressed between pages, weighted, turned, pressed again. Someone had done this properly. Someone had done this the way her mother used to do it, laying blossoms in the big dictionary with the cracked spine, and Colette had not thought of her mother's dictionary in four years and the flower cracked something open in her chest that she had been keeping shut with both hands.

There was no signature. There was no note. There was only, at the bottom of the page where a signature would go, two words in a hand that was careful and unlovely and pressed down hard enough to emboss the paper:

*the diver*

Colette sat for a long time with the flower in her palm.

* * *

She had thought about him more than she admitted to anyone, which was to say she had thought about him the way you think about weather — constantly, and without any belief that thinking would change it.

He had come up the hill in the last week of the wet season, on foot, in boots that had been resoled at least twice, carrying a coffee tin wrapped in a flour sack. She had been on the porch of the little house below the cemetery, the house she'd grown up in and fled at nineteen and come back to at thirty-one because there was no one else to bury her father properly and she had discovered, in the space of one telegram from Port Anse, that *properly* was the only way she knew how to love anyone.

He had stood at the gate the way a man stands at a gate when he is not sure he will be let through, and he had said, "You're Colette Vex," not as a question but as a man confirming a bearing.

"And you're the one who found him."

"Found him." He had turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "He found me. I just — carried him the last part."

She had made him come inside, because that was what you did in Sainte-Rose, and because she wanted to look at his hands. Her father had always said you could read a man's whole ledger in his hands. The diver's hands were scarred in the particular way that salt water scars working skin — not the clean white lines of knives but the puckered, sea-cured marks of coral and line and rust, a map of small disasters. She had thought: *these are hands that have been forgiven nothing.*

He had given her the tin and told her, in a voice that was flat and rehearsed, where he had found her father — a flophouse in Port Anse, a bed, a bargain — and Colette had listened and understood that he was telling her the truth and also telling her nothing, the way her father used to tell her nothing, the way sailors and prospectors and men who lived by tides all told nothing: fluently, with great precision.

"He made you promise," she had said.

He hadn't answered, which was an answer.

"To bring him here. To this hill."

"He wanted to be claimed by somewhere," the diver had said, and then had looked startled, as if the sentence had come out of him without permission, and she had watched him decide not to take it back.

They had buried Augustin Vex the next morning, in the plot beside her mother, with Père Lucien saying the words and half the village climbing the hill because her father had bought drinks for half the village over forty years and debts of that kind get paid at funerals. The diver had stood at the back, at the wall, where the coping stones were broken and the hillside showed through, and when it was over he had shaken her hand — those sea-cured hands, brief and formal — and said, "He asked me to tell you he was sorry about the years. That's all he said. Sorry about the years." And then he had walked down the hill and out of her life, and she had watched him go and thought: *that man is carrying something heavier than ashes, and he will carry it until he puts it down or it puts him down.*

She had not expected to hear from him. She had told herself she didn't want to. Both of these were lies, and she had made her peace with them the way you make peace with the trade winds — not by stopping them but by learning to build in their direction.

* * *

Now she sat at her kitchen table with the draft and the flower and the two embossed words, and she did what her father had taught her to do with anything that didn't make sense: she laid it out flat and looked at it until it confessed.

The draft was dated eleven days ago. Eleven days was, she calculated, roughly the time it would take a letter to travel from Fort-de-France by post and a man to travel from anywhere at all by boat, if the man were the kind of man who posted letters from cities he'd already left. The flower was frangipani. Frangipani grew everywhere on the island — it grew in her own yard, it grew wild along the coast road — but it grew *thick* along the hill above Sainte-Rose, on the leeward slope, where the trees the old families had planted still bloomed white every dry season over the graves they'd bought and the graves they hadn't. A flower from this hill. Pressed with care. Which meant he had come back here, or had never entirely left, or had carried a blossom from her father's hill in his pocket through whatever he had gone on to do, pressing it between pages in some room she couldn't picture, in some port she'd never seen.

The money was for the wall. Not for her. Not for the headstone — the mason had been paid months ago, in cash Colette had scraped together and would not have accepted help with, because there are debts a daughter keeps. The wall. The wall that held the hill together. The wall that kept the sea's weather off the graves and the graves' silence off the sea.

He had understood it, she thought. That was the thing that made her throat tighten. He had understood that the wall wasn't masonry. Her father had spent his last years writing letters about that wall — she had them in a shoebox, letters to her from Port Anse and Cayenne and once, improbably, from a hospital in Bridgetown, all of them circling back to the same theme: *a man needs to be claimed by somewhere, ma chérie, and if no one claims you, you claim a piece of ground and hold it, and the ground will hold you back.* Augustin Vex had prospected half the Caribbean for gold and come home with a philosophy instead, and Colette had spent her twenties thinking it was a poor trade, and now, holding a pressed flower from the hill, she thought: *no. It was the whole cargo.*

The diver had buried her father on this hill because he had promised. And now he was paying to keep the hill itself, because — she turned the thought over, carefully, the way you turn over something that might be alive — because the promise hadn't ended at the grave. Because a man who had spent his life measuring miles had learned, somewhere in the last year, in the water or out of it, that some things are measured in years, and years are kept the way you keep a wall: a stone at a time, over seasons, without anyone watching.

* * *

She walked back up to the cemetery in the late afternoon, when the light came long and gold across the water below and the frangipani on the leeward slope threw its scent all the way up the path.

The eastern wall was worse than she remembered. Two of the coping stones had fallen since the spring, and behind them the hillside showed its red clay ribs, and the rain had begun its patient excavation of the grave of a family named Delorme whose last descendant had died in 1961 and whose stone read only *AT REST, PLEASE.* Her father had always laughed at that stone. *Please,* he'd say. *Asking permission even in death. That's a Sainte-Rose man for you.*

Colette stood at the broken section and held the flower.

Below the hill, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, the dry season sea, the sea that looked harmless and wasn't, the sea that had taken her father's living and given him back a map in halves. Somewhere out there past the point were shoals she had never seen and did not want to see — the Leeward Shoals, the sealed wreck, the second wreck, all of it the geography of a story the diver had never told her and she had never asked for, because she had recognized in his face the same thing she saw in her father's letters: a man with one chapter he would not read aloud. She had given him the duplicate page of the map that spring morning on this hill, and he had taken it in both hands like something consecrated, and he had said, "I'll bring it back," and she had said, "Don't. Bring back whatever it was for."

She still didn't know what it had been for. In the autumn there had been rumors — there were always rumors, but these had come with names attached, a minister's name, a fleet's name, a newspaper in Fort-de-France printing things it had never dared print before, and then the minister resigning in a statement so brief it was practically a confession, and then nothing, silence, the particular silence of an island deciding not to look too closely at a gift. Colette had read the newspaper accounts three times and understood perhaps a tenth, and the tenth she understood was this: someone had found a truth at the bottom of the sea, and someone had carried it ashore, and the carrying had cost exactly what it should.

She had wondered, for a while, whether the diver had drowned doing it. It was the kind of thing she thought about now, since her father's death — the specific weight of a man lost to water, the way a village absorbs it, the way the sea keeps what it takes without apology. But then, in December, a letter had come to Père Lucien with money for the church roof, unsigned, and in February a crate of good tools had arrived at the mason's yard with no bill of lading, and Colette had begun to keep a private ledger, in her father's honor, of kindnesses with no return address. The diver was in that ledger. She was almost sure of it. Some men cannot be thanked and so you thank the world on their behalf and let the world sort out the forwarding.

She knelt at the broken wall and set the frangipani blossom into the gap between the fallen coping stones, white against the red clay, and she said, out loud, because her father had believed the dead were bad at reading but good at listening:

"He kept it, Papa. Whatever you gave him. He kept it."

The wind came up the hill the way it always did at that hour, warm and salted, and it moved the blossom once, gently, like a hand adjusting something, and was still.

* * *

She paid the mason the next week.

They worked through February, Colette hauling water and the mason, old Félicité, setting stone, and by the end of the month the eastern wall stood whole again, its new coping paler than the old but joined to it, which was, Colette thought, how everything true got built — not matching, but joined. She had the names recut where the rain had softened them, all of them, even the Delormes, even the ones the old families would rather have forgotten, because her father had taught her that a wall that only holds the stones you like isn't a wall, it's a performance.

She wrote to no one about the draft. But she kept the paper it came on, and the two embossed words at the bottom, and once — only once, on an evening in March when the first dry-season stars were out and the sea below the hill had gone to hammered silver — she took out a pen and a sheet of good paper and addressed an envelope to *The Diver, Leeward Shoals,* and then sat looking at it for a long time before she laughed at herself, softly, alone in the kitchen, and put it away. There was no address for a man like that. There was only the trail he left, which was made of mended roofs and paid masons and pressed flowers, and which continued, she understood now, without him — the way a trail continues after the walker has passed, visible only to whoever comes behind, saying only what every trail says: *someone was here, someone was going somewhere, follow if you choose.*

She did not follow. She had her hill, and her wall, and her father's letters in their shoebox, and the ledger of unsigned kindnesses that she was fairly certain would keep growing, because men who measure their lives in miles eventually, if they're lucky, meet someone who taught them to measure in years — and her father, dead eight months and asking permission from under a painted wooden cross, had apparently gone on teaching even from the grave.

On the last evening of the dry season, she walked up the hill one more time and found, wedged into the mortar of the new coping stone where she had set it, the frangipani blossom — dried now, gone to parchment, but whole. All five petals. Held fast by the wall it had helped to build.

And beneath it, half-hidden in the shadow of the stone, a second flower. Fresh. Pressed flat between two small boards tied with twine, cream-white, picked from the leeward slope within the day.

Whoever he was, wherever he was, he had been here. Recently. He had climbed her father's hill in the dark or the early light, and he had seen that the first flower had held, and he had left another — a man checking his work, a man tending something, a man who had once measured everything in tides now measuring, she was certain of it, in seasons.

Colette stood a long time in the gold light with the fresh blossom in her hands.

Then she went down the hill to get the dictionary with the cracked spine, because there was room in it for one more flower, and the dry season was ending, and the sea beyond the point was already turning — and somewhere out along the leeward trail, she knew, without knowing how she knew, a boat was moving, and a man was counting not miles, not tides, but years, and finding, for the first time, that there were enough.
