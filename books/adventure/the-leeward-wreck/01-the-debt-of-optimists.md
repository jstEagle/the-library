## 1. The Debt of Optimists

The letters came on the Thursday boat, as they always did, tucked into the same canvas mail sack that carried the village's bread flour and its small, patient disappointments. Tamsin Brightwater watched from the window of his rented room above the chandlery as old Hollis Ferris sorted the post on the quay, and he counted—without meaning to, the way a man counts waves in a rising sea—the pale rectangles with the Vane Syndicate's crest stamped in the corner. Four this week. Last week there had been three.

He had learned to read them at a distance. The first ones, months ago, had been thick: formal notices, itemized schedules of what he owed, the language of solicitors who believed in their own politeness. Then they had grown thinner, terser, until now they were single pages that said very little, which was worse. A thin letter meant they no longer needed to explain themselves. A thin letter meant the explaining was over.

Hollis looked up from the sack, found Tamsin's face in the chandlery window, and did the thing he always did—touched his cap brim without quite lifting it, a gesture that managed to be both greeting and apology. He set four envelopes on top of the stack for the Greyhaven rooms and went back to his sorting. Even the postman here had learned the choreography of avoidance. It was, Tamsin thought, almost a kindness. Almost.

He went down anyway. That was the discipline of it, the daily penance: you went down, you took the letters, you stood on the wet cobbles with the gulls screaming their idiot commentary overhead, and you let them sit in your hand unopened until your fingers went cold. Running, he had discovered, was not one grand act. It was a hundred small ones, repeated until they wore a groove in you, and then it was simply the shape your life fell into, like water finding a channel.

* * *

Greyhaven sat at the end of the road the way a sentence sits at the end of an argument that has run out of breath—a scatter of slate roofs and tarred sheds around a harbor that emptied twice a day into miles of glistening mudflat. In summer the tourists came for the puffins and the light. In October they left, and the village folded itself back into its working silence, and men who owed money could hide in it the way a coin hides in a pocket full of change.

Tamsin had been hiding for eleven months. He knew the number precisely because he kept it the way other men kept a wound count—not out of pride but because forgetting felt like the first step toward believing the debt wasn't real, and the debt was the only real thing left about him.

His room above the chandlery smelled of hemp rope and paraffin and, underneath it all, the salt rot of the walls themselves, which wept in damp weather. His gear occupied most of the floor: two expedition duffels gone soft with mildew, a storm kite rolled in its bag like something dead, the aluminum frame of a weather mast he'd built himself in another life, in another country, when he had still been the kind of man whose name appeared in expedition bulletins. *Brightwater's run,* they used to call it—that forty-mile stretch of open water where the storms came ashore with a violence that made other chasers look like hobbyists. He had chased weather the way some men chase God. And for a while, the weather had answered.

Then the *Meridian* expedition. Then the loss. Then Vane.

He sat on the edge of the bed with the four envelopes squared on his knee and opened none of them. Outside, the light was doing something wrong. He'd noticed it since morning—the sky too high and too clear, a hard blue like polished steel, and the barometer in the chandlery below climbing all day when it should have been falling, climbing the way it does before it breaks. Old fishermen called it the liar's sky. Tamsin called it by its proper name, because naming weather was the last professional habit he had left: a veering wind ahead of a deepening low, pressure recovering briefly before the secondary system came through. There would be a gale tonight. Possibly worse.

He found he was almost glad.

* * *

Mrs. Penhale ran the Salt & Anchor, the only public house that stayed open past September, and she poured him his pint without asking and said nothing, which was her form of conversation. Tamsin took his usual corner table, the one with sightlines to both doors, because eleven months of running teaches you habits you can't drink away.

"You'll want to batten something down tonight," Mrs. Penhale said, passing back through with a tray. "Barometer's been queer all day."

"It'll come round from the southeast," Tamsin said. "Hard, and early. Before midnight."

She gave him the look she always gave him—the look that said she knew perfectly well who he was, or at least what he was good for, and considered it rude to mention either. "You'd know." And she was gone again, and he was alone with his pint and the low murmur of the four regulars arguing about herring quotas, and the feeling that had become so familiar he'd stopped noticing it except at moments like this, when the ordinary life of the village brushed against him and showed him the outline of what he'd become: a man present in a place without belonging to it, known without being seen.

That was the thing about debt nobody warned you. They told you about the interest rates, the collectors, the ruin. Nobody told you how it thinned you. How every handshake became a calculation—*does this person know, could this person be paid to know*—until you were doing arithmetic in your sleep. How you started choosing tables by the exits. How the name *Tamsin Brightwater*, once printed in journals and spoken with a certain envious weight at conferences, became a thing you flinched at hearing, so that you trained yourself to answer to nothing, to be a lodger, a customer, a man at the corner table. You didn't lose your name all at once. You misplaced it, letter by letter, the way you misplace keys, and one day you reached for it and it wasn't where you'd left it.

The Vane Syndicate had financed the *Meridian*. Three ships' worth of instrumentation, a support crew, a season of charter time—all of it signed against Tamsin's certainty that the coming winter would produce a record storm system off the outer banks, a system worth documenting, worth selling, worth the syndicate's investment many times over. He had been right about the storm. He had been catastrophically wrong about everything else. The *Meridian* had gone down with two of her crew, and the data—eleven months of it, the finest storm records ever captured—had gone down with her, sealed in flooded housings at the bottom of the sea, and the insurance had been voided on a technicality Tamsin still couldn't read without his jaw tightening, and the debt had come home to roost on him personally, every pound of it, compounding quarterly like something alive.

Aldous Vane himself had written only once. One page, on heavy cream paper, no threats at all, just a kind of courteous arithmetic: *We are patient men, Mr. Brightwater. Patience is not the same as forgiveness.* Tamsin had burned that letter in the chandlery stove and regretted it immediately, because burning it hadn't destroyed anything except the evidence of how much it had frightened him.

* * *

The gale arrived at half past ten, exactly as promised, and it arrived like a door being kicked open.

Tamsin stood at his window and watched it take the harbor apart. The wind came off the sea in long, flat gusts that didn't howl so much as shove—a physical, muscular pressure against the glass, against the walls, against anything a man might have thought was fixed. Rain came horizontal. Somewhere down the quay a boat cover tore loose and flapped away into the dark like a wounded bird, and the moored vessels plunged and snubbed at their lines, and the whole village seemed to lean eastward together, holding its breath.

He should have been afraid. Instead he felt the old current move under his skin, the one that had made him a storm chaser in the first place—the animal recognition, deeper than thought, that this was the biggest living thing in the world and it was here, now, and he was watching it through a window like a man watching his own funeral procession go by.

By midnight the southeast gale had done its work on the harbor, and by two it had turned and begun to do its work on him.

It found the chandlery's weak side—the seaward wall, the old sash windows with their rotten putty—and it peeled the protection off the yard where Tamsin kept the gear too large for his room. He heard it happen even through the roar: a crack of timber, a long metallic shriek. When he got down the stairs with a lantern, braced against wind that fought him for every step, he found the yard gate torn from its hinges and his life's remaining equipment scattered across the cobbles like the aftermath of a wreck.

The weather mast—his mast, the one he'd welded himself, the one piece of the *Meridian* project that had survived—had been thrown against the stone wall and lay bent double, its aluminum spars snapped clean, pointing at the sky like a broken finger accusing heaven. The storm kite bag had burst; the silk was streaming out across the yard, filling with rain, tangling in the fence wire, and he grabbed at it and the wind took it and for one absurd moment Tamsin Brightwater stood wrestling a kite in a gale like a boy fighting his own shadow, and then the fabric ripped along its length with a sound like laughter and was gone over the roofline into the dark.

He stood in the flooding yard, soaked through in seconds, and let the lantern gutter, and understood with a clarity that felt almost gentle: there was nothing left to protect. The gear had been the excuse. As long as the gear existed—as long as he was *repairing* it, *inventorying* it, telling himself he'd need it when he started again—he had a reason to stay in Greyhaven, and staying in Greyhaven was just running with better scenery. Now the wind had taken even that. The syndicate's letters were closing in from one direction and the weather from the other, and between them they had squeezed out the last of his hiding places.

He gathered what was gatherable—wet rope, a case of instruments that might or might not have survived, the bent mast that was now scrap metal and he knew it—and carried it inside, and sat dripping on the floor of his room among the ruins of his profession, and laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it.

The four envelopes lay on the table where he'd left them, dry and patient. He picked up the topmost one at last, because after tonight it hardly mattered, and slit it with his thumbnail.

One page. Thinner than the rest. The Vane crest, the date, and beneath it a single line in the new clerk's efficient hand:

*We are given to understand you may be found in Greyhaven.*

They knew. Not where he was—they'd known that for months, probably; the letters proved that—but that he was nearly finished. That the hiding was over whether he chose it or not. We are given to understand. As if he were a rumor, a sighting, a storm system being tracked toward landfall.

Tamsin read the line three times. Then he set the letter down, and looked out the black window at the dying gale, and felt something shift in his chest that wasn't fear and wasn't relief but was adjacent to both—the feeling of a man who has run out of road and discovers, strangely, that his feet have stopped hurting.

In the morning, the tide would be out further than anyone in Greyhaven had seen it in years. The gale and the spring tide together, the sea pulled back like a curtain. He didn't know why the thought lodged in him the way it did. He only knew that he would be on the flats to see what it uncovered.
