## 2. What the Tide Gives Back

The tide that morning was a thing out of almanac legend. Tamsin heard it before he understood it—a long, wet exhalation from the direction of the point, as though the sea itself had leaned back in its chair. He stepped out of Mrs. Calloway's rented room with his tea going cold in one hand and watched the water fall away from Greyhaven's harbor like a curtain drawn by a careless hand.

By seven the fishing boats sat aground on mud the color of old pewter, listing at angles their owners would spend all day cursing about. By eight the sea had retreated past the second sandbar, past the kelp beds, past anything Tamsin had seen it do in the four months he'd been hiding in this village pretending to be nobody.

*An extreme spring tide,* the harbormaster said to anyone who'd listen, tapping the tide table pinned beside his window like a man confirming a prophecy. *Equinox plus a blow offshore. Won't see its like again for eighteen years.*

Tamsin didn't go down to the harbor with the rest of them. He went the other way—north along the cliff path, past the ruined lime kiln, to where the coast bent inward and the rock went black and folded, and where for four months he had noticed, without letting himself notice, that the sea spent more time inside the land than any map suggested.

He told himself he was walking for his health. He told himself a great many things these days; lying to himself had become a kind of breathing.

* * *

The cave mouth wasn't supposed to exist. The charts he'd bought secondhand in Torquay marked nothing there but a hazard symbol and a depth figure, and the villagers spoke of the stretch only to warn children away from it—the Drowned Stair, they called it, though none of them could say why. But the sea had pulled back forty yards beyond its usual line, and where the water usually broke white against the base of the cliffs there was now a corridor of glistening stone, ribbed and green-slimed, descending into shadow.

Tamsin stood at the top of the path for a long time, wind pulling at his jacket, and felt the old sickness rise in him—that specific hunger of a man who has been broke too long. It was the hunger that had financed his last expedition, that had signed his name to Vane Syndicate paper, that had put him on a boat in the Southern Ocean chasing a storm system every sane meteorologist said couldn't be ridden. The hunger had cost him everything except itself.

"Look," he said aloud, to no one, "but don't touch."

His boots found the first step anyway.

* * *

Inside, the cave breathed. That was the only word for it—the swell entered some deep chamber below and pushed air up through fissures, so that the whole passage sighed and shuddered around him, and the sound was so much like lungs that twice he stopped and pressed his hand against the wet rock just to feel something solid.

Daylight followed him down the corridor in a thinning wedge. Where it failed, he switched on his headlamp, and the beam swung across walls furred with barnacle and lit a scatter of things that stopped his breath: timber. Shaped timber. Curved planking, black with age but whole, running back into the dark in a line too straight and too deliberate to be driftwood.

He knew the stories. Everyone on this coast knew the stories. The *Leeward*, merchant brigantine, lost with all hands in the winter of '31 carrying a cargo manifest that had launched a hundred salvage claims and settled none of them. A ship whose papers were worth more than her hull ever was, because somewhere in those pages was the proof of who really owned what—who had insured her, who had underwritten her, who had quietly moved her cargo onto another vessel's books the week before she sailed. The Corvane family had claimed salvage rights for three generations and been denied three generations' worth of times, on grounds no one could produce in writing. The wreck itself had never been found. Officially, the *Leeward* had gone down far offshore, in deep water, unrecoverable.

Officially.

Tamsin's lamp found the hull's curve rising above him like the ribcage of something enormous, and he stood beneath it with seawater seeping into his boots and forgot, entirely, to be afraid.

She hadn't sunk here. She'd been *put* here. The cave floor was graded, leveled in places, and the ship rested keel-down on a bed of shingle that someone had arranged—or that the sea, over ninety years, had arranged for her, tucking her in like a body in a crypt. Her masts were gone but her deck structures remained, cabin house intact, hatches seated, rigging turned to soft black lace that dissolved when his light touched it too directly. The cold and the dark and the anoxic silt had done what the open sea never does: they had refused to let her finish dying.

"Remarkable preservation," he murmured, and heard how it sounded—the expedition voice, the grant-application voice, the voice of a man cataloguing instead of feeling. He let it drop. What he actually wanted to say was something simpler and less professional, something like *hello* or *I'm sorry* or *who left you here*.

* * *

The chest sat in the afterhold, wedged beneath a fallen spar, iron-banded, its wood gone the color of strong tea. A seal across the lid had cracked but not broken—wax the shade of dried blood, stamped with an anchor-and-crown device Tamsin didn't recognize and then, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the unstable footing, did. He'd seen it on letterhead. He'd seen it on the contract he'd signed two years ago, the one with the repayment schedule shaped like a noose.

Vane.

No—not Vane. Older than Vane. The same anchor-and-crown, but the wax was brittle and the stamp was period, and the realization arrived in his stomach cold and complete: the Syndicate hadn't grown out of nothing. It had grown out of *this*. Out of ships like this one. Out of losses like this one, arranged or endured, papered over or preserved. His creditors' fortune might be sitting six feet from his hand, sealed in wax, older than anyone alive who could explain it.

He should have walked away. Every instinct he'd sharpened in four months of hiding said walk away, know nothing, owe nothing further. But his hands were already working the lid, and the bands gave with a groan that the cave swallowed whole, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that still held a ghost of its waterproofing, lay paper.

Dozens of documents. Ledgers with spines intact. A logbook whose cover bore the *Leeward*'s name in faded gilt. And on top, flat and separate as if placed there last, a single folded sheet sealed with the same anchor-and-crown.

Tamsin's fingers hovered. The cave sighed around him. Somewhere below, the sea was already turning—he could hear it in the changed pitch of the swell, a note lower and more insistent, the tide beginning its long climb home.

He took the top sheet. Only the top sheet. He told himself it was prudence—one document, deniable, while the chest and the wreck stayed exactly where they were, evidence undisturbed, a discovery he could report rather than rob. He told himself he'd bring it back. He was aware, distantly, of what a man sounds like when he tells himself things, because he had been listening to himself tell himself things for four months, and the voice never changes, and it is always reasonable, and it is always lying.

The seal cracked between his fingers like a knuckle.

* * *

He read it by headlamp, standing knee-deep in the first returning water, because he could not wait and because some part of him already knew he wouldn't get a second chance.

It was not a cargo list. It was a rider—an addendum to the *Leeward*'s insurance policy, executed three days before she sailed. And the names on it were not merchants. They were investors. Men who had taken positions on the *Leeward*'s loss the way gamblers take positions on a race, men who stood to collect enormously if she never reached port—and one clause, initialled in a cramped and furious hand, that shifted liability for any salvage claim onto a named third party.

Corvane. E. Corvane. Salvage rights assigned, contingent, revocable—revocable at the insurers' sole discretion, in perpetuity, to heirs and assigns.

They hadn't just stolen from the family. They'd written the theft into the policy before the ship ever left harbor, and then they'd lost the ship on purpose, or close enough to purpose that the difference was a matter of weather, and the proof of it had lain in the dark for ninety years waiting for a tide extreme enough to open the door.

Tamsin read it twice. Three times. The water climbed his thighs. His hands shook, and not from cold.

Because there, at the foot of the rider, among the investor signatures—small, careful, a clerk's hand rather than a principal's—was a witness attestation. And the witness's name was Brightwater.

His grandfather's name. His grandfather, who had died comfortable, respected, whose debts Tamsin had always believed were his own stupid, self-made inheritance. Whose signature, apparently, had helped seal a fraud that had beggared another family for three generations.

The wave came without warning—swell finding the corridor, sudden and waist-high and shockingly cold, knocking him against the hull. The chest was submerged. The remaining documents bloomed and darkened in the black water like flowers closing. He grabbed for them and got nothing but oilcloth and foam, and the sea said *no* in the oldest voice there is, and he understood that the choice had been made for him: he had one document, and the document had him.

He fought his way up the corridor with the paper pressed inside his jacket, against his chest, like a heart he'd stolen. Behind him the cave filled, sealing itself, keeping the *Leeward* and everything else he'd seen—and everything he hadn't—for another eighteen years, or forever.

On the cliff path, soaked and gasping, he finally let himself think the thought whole:

This clears my debt. This ends the Syndicate. This ruins me, or saves me, or both—and either way, the moment I show it to anyone, they will come for it, and they will come through me, and I have nowhere left to run.

Above Greyhaven, on the road from the ferry landing, a black car he didn't recognize was just beginning its descent toward the village.

He saw it and felt the dread settle over the awe like a lid over a chest.

Someone else knew the tide had given something back.
