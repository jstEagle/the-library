## 3. The Granddaughter of the Leeward

Maren Corvane had flown the Greyhaven run so many times she could do it blind, and some mornings, in the fog that came off the water like breath on glass, she nearly did. The Cessna knew the route better than she did—knew the dip where the current bent the wind at Kettle Point, knew the way the cliffs threw back an echo of the engine noise like a hand raised in greeting. Forty minutes over the strait, twelve minutes up the fjord, wheels down on the gravel bar the village optimistically called an airstrip. She'd been flying it since she was nineteen, ferrying mail and mutton and the occasional stubborn tourist, and in all those years the one thing Greyhaven had never given her was a reason to stay grounded.

Until the tide went out farther than it had any right to.

She'd heard about the cave the way news traveled in a place like this: sideways, half-heard, garnished with exaggeration. Old Piet's boy swore he'd seen stone arches down past the Gullet that no chart showed. The Henderson sisters claimed the low spring tide had peeled back the headland like a lid off a pot. Maren filed it away with the rest of the village mythology—right up until she noticed the stranger.

He'd arrived three weeks ago on the coastal packet, a tall man with salt-and-pepper stubble and the particular way of standing that told her everything: weight off his left hip, favoring an old injury, eyes always moving. He'd taken the room above the chandlery and paid cash, which in Greyhaven was less a sign of wealth than of someone who didn't want to be found. He kept to himself. He walked the tideline at dawn. And this morning, when she taxied out past the point for her first run, she'd seen him crouched at the base of the headland below the Gullet, doing something with his hands that looked very much like prying at rock.

She almost let it go. That was the Greyhaven way—you saw what you saw, you said nothing, you flew your route. But something about the set of his shoulders snagged in her like a fishhook. She'd spent her whole life around people who looked at the sea and saw money in it. Her grandfather had been one of them, once. It had killed him.

* * *

The Leeward had gone down in the winter of '61, running the inside passage against every warning, carrying a cargo manifest that had never been recovered and eleven souls who were never coming home. Elias Corvane was the youngest man aboard—twenty-six, second mate, newly married, with a salvage claim staked in his name on a stretch of coast the company wanted. The official inquiry called it weather. The family had called it theft for sixty years, quietly, at kitchen tables, over photographs of a young man in a wool coat squinting into a camera that would outlive him. Maren had grown up on the story the way other children grew up on lullabies. The sea took him. The company took everything else.

So when she finished her last run that evening and saw lamplight leaking from under the chandlery door well past midnight, she didn't knock. She let herself in the way locals did, uninvited and unapologetic, and stood in the doorway watching Tamsin Brightwater spread charts across a workbench like a man laying out cards he intended to cheat with.

"You're trespassing," he said, without looking up.

"So are you." She nodded at the charts. "That's a hydrographic survey of the Gullet. Restricted issue. You'd need harbor authority clearance to have that."

"I borrowed it."

"From whom?"

"From a drawer that wasn't locked." He finally looked up, and she got her first close look at his eyes—grey, tired, and sharp as a gaff hook. "You're the pilot. Corvane."

"Maren Corvane. And you're the man who's been walking my coastline every dawn for three weeks measuring things with his eyes." She crossed the room and put her palm flat on the largest chart, right over the headland. "What's down there?"

For a long moment he said nothing. She watched him weigh her—the way men like him weighed everything, assets against liabilities—and she felt the old familiar heat rise in her chest, the one that came whenever someone decided what she was worth before she'd said a word.

"I'll tell you what I tell everyone," he said at last. "Nothing. I'm a surveyor. Contract work."

"Liar," she said pleasantly. "Surveyors don't pay cash. Surveyors don't flinch when the mail boat comes in. And surveyors don't—" she tapped the chart, twice, hard, "—circle the same quarter-mile of coastline forty times like a man circling a wound."

His jaw tightened. Outside, the harbor slapped against pilings, patient as a debt collector. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped its charm entirely, and somehow that was worse. "You wouldn't want to know what's down there, Ms. Corvane. Trust me. Knowing is expensive."

"My grandfather died on the Leeward," she said.

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them. She watched them land—watched the stillness come over him, the recalibration, the flicker behind his eyes that was not sympathy but arithmetic. She hated him a little for it. She hated herself more for having said it anyway, for spending her family's grief like coin because it was the only currency this stranger seemed to accept.

"The Leeward," he repeated slowly.

"Went down in '61. Eleven dead. My grandfather among them. His salvage claim on this coast was never honored—buried in an inquiry that took four months to blame the weather and thirty years to lose the paperwork." She pulled the chart out from under his hand and folded it, not gently. "So yes, Mr. Brightwater. I want to know what's down there. I've wanted to know my whole life. And if you don't tell me, I'll tell the harbormaster what you've borrowed, and the harbormaster will tell whoever's asking about you, and then we'll all find out together."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the street a dog started up and thought better of it.

"You've done this before," he said quietly. Not a question.

"I've grown up doing it." She set the folded chart on the bench between them like a blade laid hilt-first. "Every Corvane for two generations has chased that wreck through archives and registries and lawyers' offices, and every one of us has come home with nothing but the story. So either you tell me what you found in that cave, or you tell me why I shouldn't fly to the mainland tomorrow and sell your location to the highest bidder. Because believe me—I've had offers of interest in that stretch of coast before, and I remember every face."

Something shifted in him then. Not softening—she doubted the man softened—but a kind of surrender to the inevitable, the look of a gambler pushing his last chips to the center of the table. He reached into his coat and drew out a document, creased once, salt-stained at one corner, and laid it beside the chart without letting go of it.

"I didn't find the cave," he said. "The cave found me. Extreme spring tide, three nights ago. The headland opened up like it was waiting." His thumb moved across the paper's edge, unconscious, protective. "There's a ship down there, Ms. Corvane. Intact. Preserved in cold black water like something in a jar. And there's a strongbox in her hold with the company seal still bright on the lock."

Her heart was doing something unprofessional. "The manifest."

"Maybe. Maybe just ballast and bilge records. I couldn't open it—no tools, and the tide was coming back." He met her eyes at last, and whatever he saw in hers made him look away first. "I took one page. Loose paper, wedged in the frame of the strongbox. It's a partial cargo list. And it has names on it that shouldn't be there."

"What names?"

"Investors. Backers of the voyage." He paused, and she heard the cost of the next words before he spoke them. "One of them might be mine."

The room went very small. Maren became aware of her own breathing, of the tick of the cooling lamp, of sixty years of kitchen-table grief rearranging itself around a single sentence. *Might be mine.* She should have asked whose. She should have demanded the page. Instead she heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized:

"Show me."

He turned the paper toward her. The ink had faded to the color of dried kelp, but the letters held—columns of figures, a date, and a list of names subscribed beneath a company heading she knew from her grandfather's photographs. Her eye ran down it and stopped, snagged, on a line near the bottom. *E. Corvane — 40 shares.*

"That's him," she whispered. "That's my grandfather's stake. Forty shares. He bought into the voyage with his claim as collateral—that's what my grandmother always said, that he bet the coast itself on that crossing." Her finger trembled above the ink, not quite touching. "And when the ship went down, they didn't just take his life. They took the proof he ever owned anything."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its edges. "If this page is real, it means the inquiry was fixed. It means the company knew who held claims and buried them. It means—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"It means my family was robbed," Maren finished. "Say it. I've said it my whole life. It costs less than you think."

"And it means I'm holding the only evidence that exists." He drew the page back toward himself—not greedily, she noted, but the way a man holds something that has begun to burn. "Which means everyone who helped bury it will want it buried again. Starting with me. There are people looking for me, Ms. Corvane. They financed my last expedition, and I owe them more than I can pay, and they are very good at finding things. If they learn what's in that cave—"

"They'll take it. Like the company took it. Like everyone takes from us." She straightened. Something had settled in her chest, hard and clear as ballast. "Then here's how this goes, Mr. Brightwater. You don't hand that page to anyone. Not to me, not yet. But you don't run with it either, because you can't outrun them forever and you can't open that strongbox alone. You need a pilot. You need someone who knows this coast and these mountains and every back channel between here and the capital. And I need—" she exhaled, "—I need the truth about my grandfather more than I need to keep hating strangers."

He studied her for a long moment. "This is a bad alliance," he said. "You understand that. Everyone in it gets hurt."

"Everyone in my family already got hurt. Sixty years ago." She picked up his folded chart and held it out to him, handle of the blade returned. "The question isn't whether this ends badly. The question is whether it ends honestly. Can you promise me that much?"

He took the chart. His hand, she noticed, was not steady. "I can promise I'll try."

"Not good enough." She was at the door before he answered, and she made herself turn back, made herself meet those grey hooked eyes one last time. "But it's a start. Dawn tomorrow. Bring the page. We'll see what the tide gives back next."

Outside, the fog had come all the way up the street, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea was turning over in its sleep, keeping—she now knew—its oldest secret one tide longer. Behind her, in the lit window of the chandlery, the stranger stood motionless over his charts, and Maren Corvane walked home through the dark with her grandfather's name burning a hole through sixty years of silence, knowing only this: whatever came next, it would come fast, and it would not come alone.
