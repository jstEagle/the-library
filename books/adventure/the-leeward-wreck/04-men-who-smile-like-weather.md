## 4. Men Who Smile Like Weather

They came on a morning so clear it felt like an accusation.

Tamsin saw the car first—a long black thing with mud on its wheel arches that didn't match Greyhaven's grey clay, idling at the top of the harbor road where the lane narrowed and no one with any sense would leave a vehicle. It sat there the way certain weather sits offshore: patient, gathering, in no hurry at all because it already knew how the day would end.

He was mending nets for Old Piet, three pence an hour and all the herring he could stomach, when the second car arrived. This one was silver, immaculate, and out of it stepped a man who looked like he'd been poured into his coat rather than dressed in it. Tall. Silver at the temples, arranged as deliberately as everything else about him. He stood a moment surveying the harbor—the leaning boats, the drying racks, the gulls screaming over the gutting tables—with the expression of a man appraising property.

Tamsin's hands went still inside the net.

He knew that face from prospectuses and courtroom sketches and the margins of his own nightmares: Aurel Vane. Not the Syndicate itself—Vane was too senior to chase debts personally—but its public face, its smile, the part of the machine designed to be seen. Beside him, younger, broader, wearing a fisherman's oilskin over what was clearly not a fisherman's clothes, came a man Tamsin knew even better. Corren Hask. The Syndicate's recovery agent. The man who had once shaken Tamsin's hand at the signing of the Meridian expedition contract and said, *We believe in optimists, Mr. Brightwater,* while his associate photographed every page of Tamsin's collateral.

Hask's eyes found him across the harbor the way water finds a crack.

"Mr. Brightwater." Vane crossed the cobbles with his hand already extended, voice pitched to carry warmth without effort. "Aurel Vane. I don't imagine you're pleased to see me, but I hope you'll allow me to buy you breakfast. The innkeeper tells me there's smoked mackerel this morning that would make a grown man weep."

"I'm working," Tamsin said.

"So I see." Vane glanced at the net, at Tamsin's rope-burned fingers, and something flickered behind the charm—not quite pity; pity would have been kinder. Recognition, maybe. One professional assessing another's decline. "Nets. From storm chaser to net-mender in eighteen months. There's a parable in that somewhere, though I'd never be so rude as to tell it."

*You just did,* Tamsin thought, but he set down the netting needle because Piet had drifted off toward the fuel shed and because some animal part of him understood that running now, in front of the whole harbor, would cost more than standing. "Say what you came to say."

"What I came to say," Vane said pleasantly, "is best said sitting down. Over eggs. With coffee that isn't made from grounds boiled twice." He smiled, and it was a good smile, a warm smile, the kind of smile that had signed away half of Tamsin's life two years ago. "Ten minutes, Mr. Brightwater. That's all I ask. And then, if you want me gone, I'll be gone. You have my word."

Everyone in Greyhaven knew what the Vane Syndicate's word was worth. Everyone except, apparently, the man giving it.

* * *

The inn smelled of woodsmoke and fried onions, and the only table far enough from the window was the one by the hearth, which suited Vane fine—he chose it himself, gesturing Tamsin into the chair facing the door, a courtesy that was also a message: *you can watch them come in; we want you to.* Hask took the third chair without being asked and ordered nothing. He sat with his back to the wall and his hands folded and watched Tamsin the way a man watches a tide gauge.

Vane ordered for both of them without consulting the menu. Of course he did.

"You've been difficult to find," Vane said, when the mackerel arrived and the girl had gone. "Not impossible. Difficult. There's a difference, and the difference interests us. A man who's merely hiding wants to stay hidden. A man who's *difficult*—" he buttered his toast with small precise strokes, "—wants something. He's waiting for something. He has a reason to be exactly here, in exactly this village, doing exactly this work, and pretending it's poverty."

"The reason is called rent."

"The reason," Vane said, "is called Greyhaven. Population four hundred and eleven. One road in, one road out, closed by snow four months of the year. No bank branch. No courthouse within sixty miles. And—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before naming the organ, "—a spring tide last week that dropped lower than any tide in nine years. Low enough, our hydrographer tells us, to expose sea caves along the north headland that haven't seen daylight since before the war."

Tamsin kept his face still. He'd had practice. Two years of dodging letters teaches a man to keep his face still even while his pulse climbs into his throat.

"Caves," he said.

"Caves." Vane ate a bite of mackerel, unhurried. "And one of them, according to a very old chart we acquired—acquired is such a useful word—contains wreckage consistent with a three-masted barque lost in '31. You'll know the name better than I do, I suspect. You always did have a weakness for famous disasters." He looked up, and the warmth in his eyes was the warmth of a lamp over an operating table. "*Leeward*, Mr. Brightwater."

Outside, a gull hit the window glass hard enough to leave a smear, and neither of them flinched.

"There's no wreck," Tamsin said. "There are stories. Fishermen tell stories the way other men tell lies—for sport."

"Mm." Vane dabbed his mouth with the napkin. "Then you won't mind showing me. Tomorrow's tide will be nearly as low. We could walk out together, you and I, and confirm that there's nothing there, and I'll write you a receipt for your time and be on my way, and you can go back to your nets in peace." He spread his hands, all reasonableness. "That's the generous reading of the situation. Here's the accurate one."

He nodded once, slightly, and Hask reached into his coat and laid a folder on the table. Not slid—laid, square, deliberate, like a card played face-up.

Inside, when Tamsin opened it against his better judgment, were photographs. The cave mouth, taken from the water at slack tide. The tunnel. And then—his stomach dropped through the floor of himself—the inner chamber, shot with a long lens and a powerful light: black ribs of hull rising out of green water, the curve of a rail, and there, unmistakable, the stern transom with letters still legible under the silt film.

L — W — R — D.

Someone had been in the cave. Someone had photographed it days ago, before the tide closed it again, and sold the pictures, and Tamsin understood with cold clarity exactly who: the same someone who'd have sold anything else that wasn't nailed down. He thought of Maren and felt the guilt arrive early, like a debt coming due ahead of schedule.

"We know you found it first," Vane said quietly. "The footprints in the silt were yours—we matched the boot. The broken coral fan on the ledge. The disturbed chest. And one document missing from a manifest case that was sealed in 1931 and photographed intact in 1987 by a marine surveyor who never reported it, because reporting it would have meant sharing it." He laced his fingers. "You took a page, Mr. Brightwater. We'd very much like to see it."

"And if I say there's nothing?"

"Then you're a poor liar and a poorer businessman, and I'll be forced to conclude you intend to profit from that document alone, or with partners." Vane's voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse. "And then the arithmetic changes. Because here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, and I want you to understand that it is genuinely generous, given what you owe."

He leaned forward. Up close, the charm had seams in it, hairline fractures around the eyes, and Tamsin realized with a strange floating calm that Aurel Vane was tired. That this mattered to the Syndicate. That whatever was in that manifest was worth sending a senior partner to the end of a dirt road in the rain.

"The Meridian debt stands at forty-one thousand, six hundred marks, plus interest accruing daily. Your equipment was seized. Your license is suspended pending review. Your name appears on a list that follows you to every port, every lender, every charter office on the coast." Vane ticked each item off softly, like a man counting someone else's ribs. "In exchange for the complete manifest—all of it, every page, surrendered to the Syndicate unconditionally—I will forgive the debt in full. Restore your standing. Pay you a finder's fee of five thousand marks, cash, today. You walk out of this inn a free man. No letters. No Hask. No list. You could charter a boat tomorrow. You could chase storms again. You could be *Tamsin Brightwater* again, instead of whatever it is you've become."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind the bar a radio murmured shipping news. And Tamsin sat in the warmth of it and felt the offer move through him like liquor—sweet, immediate, dissolving. Forty-one thousand marks. Eighteen months of running, of nets and herring and sleeping with his boots by the door. Gone. Five thousand marks besides. His name, clean. His life, returned.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Not the threat—the ease. He could feel the shape of the yes already forming in his mouth, the relief of it, the way surrender always feels like flight until you notice you're the cargo.

"And if I refuse?"

Vane sighed, and for a moment the smile went out of him entirely, and what remained was worse than any threat: simple, administrative patience. A man looking at a line item.

"Then the debt stands, and grows, and we pursue it by every lawful means, and some means whose lawfulness we'd debate. Then the cave gets a proper salvage operation—ours—and whatever's in that chest becomes Syndicate property through channels so clean they'll shine. Then your friend the ferry pilot learns what her grandfather's ship is worth to us, and learns it from lawyers, in winter, when she has no leverage at all." He rose, buttoned his coat, and left a fold of notes beside his untouched plate—too many notes, deliberately. "You have until the tide turns tomorrow evening. Think of it not as selling the dead, Mr. Brightwater. Think of it as buying yourself back."

At the door he paused, silhouetted against the bright cold street, and turned the charm back on like a lamp.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I read your book. The one about the Meridian. The ending was honest. Most men wouldn't have written themselves that badly." A beat. "I wonder if you still can."

Then he was gone, and Hask with him, and the silver car pulled away up the harbor road past the black one that never moved, and Tamsin sat alone with two breakfasts going cold and a fold of money he couldn't touch and couldn't quite push away.

* * *

He walked the north shore until dark, and the document rode against his ribs the whole way like a coal.

He'd read it a dozen times since the cave. A single page from the Leeward's manifest, water-stained, the ink ghosted but legible: a bill of lading for salvage rights assigned to one E. Corvane, witnessed and stamped, dated eleven days before the ship sailed for the last time. Proof. The kind of proof that doesn't age. In his coat pocket it weighed nothing; in his head it weighed everything, because he'd begun to understand what it was worth to the Syndicate—not the paper, but the silence it could buy, the claim it could bury, the family it could keep cheated for another fifty years.

Forty-one thousand marks against one dead man's granddaughter.

He stood on the shingle below the headland and watched the sea work at the rocks, patient as a creditor, and tried to find the version of himself who could take Vane's deal and live in it afterward. He couldn't locate the man. But he could feel how close he lived to him—how thin the wall was between the person he was and the person the debt had been building, year by year, payment by missed payment. Running had made him hollow. Selling the page would finish the job.

Behind him, gravel crunched.

Maren came down the path with her collar up and her jaw set, and he knew before she spoke that she'd heard—that the inn had ears, that Greyhaven had one nervous system and everyone felt everything. She stopped an arm's length away, wind pulling at her hair, and looked at him with an expression he'd learn to dread and depend on in equal measure: the look of a woman deciding whether you're weather or wreckage.

"They offered to forgive your debt," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And you told them—"

"I haven't told them anything yet." He turned to face her fully, the document pressing cold against his chest. "But they gave me until tomorrow's tide. So we need to talk tonight—all of it, the page, the journal, everything—because tomorrow evening, either I'm a free man, or we're both in this past the point of rescue."

Above the headland, unseen, the black car's headlights came on, sweeping the shore road once, slowly, like a searchlight finding its range.
