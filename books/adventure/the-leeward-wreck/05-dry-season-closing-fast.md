## 5. Dry Season, Closing Fast

Maren Corvane had grown up reading weather the way other children learned to read street signs. Her father taught her before he taught her letters: the color of the sea at dawn, the way gulls sat sullen on the breakwater instead of riding the thermals, the smell of kelp rotting in a wind that had swung too far south. *The sky always tells you first,* he'd say. *People are the ones who lie.*

So when she stood on the headland above Greyhaven on the last morning of September and saw the first white caps on the pass—thin as spilled flour along the shoulders of Mount Verrane—she did not need anyone to tell her what it meant. The dry season was closing like a door. And they were still on the wrong side of it.

She found Tamsin Brightwater in the boatyard, under the upturned hull of a dory that hadn't floated since before either of them was born, surrounded by the wreckage of his old life. He'd laid his storm-chasing gear out on a tarp with the grim precision of a man taking inventory of himself: cracked anemometer housing, a laptop with a salt-swollen keyboard, three hard drives he kept wrapped in oilcloth like relics. He looked smaller than he had in the cave. Running wore a man down to his load-bearing walls.

"The pass," she said, without greeting.

He glanced up. "How long?"

"Three weeks. Maybe less if the jet stream dips early. After that, Verrane closes until April." She crouched beside the tarp, studying the gear without touching it. "And the Syndicate's men are already counting our days for us. The one who calls himself Mr. Aldous has been asking at the fuel dock about charter rates north."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. "They're not leaving either."

"They're not *crossing*. They're waiting to see which way we run." She straightened. "Which means we stop running and start moving. On our schedule, not theirs."

* * *

They planned in her grandfather's house, in the room that had been Josiah Corvane's study, where the light came through salt-frosted glass and fell across forty years of charts pinned to the walls. Maren had spent her childhood in this room listening to stories about the Leeward—the ship that went down with her grandfather aboard, the salvage rights her family had signed away in grief and bad faith to men whose signatures now lived in the very chest Tamsin had pulled from the sea. The manifest lay between them on the desk, still smelling faintly of brine and iron, and beside it, Josiah's journal, its spine soft as old leather gloves.

Tamsin spread a chart of the interior across everything else, weighting the corners with coffee cups.

"We can't drive," he said. "Every road out of Greyhaven funnels through two checkpoints, and Aldous's people will be sitting at both like spiders."

"So we fly."

He looked at her. "You said yourself your plane won't make it over Verrane fully loaded."

"It won't." She traced the coastline north with one finger. "But it doesn't have to. I take us up the sound to Harrow's Strip—a gravel shelf an old friend keeps, four hundred meters, enough for a bush plane with a good pilot and no cargo worth mentioning. From Harrow's, we go overland. Two days' hike to the Kestrel Col, then down the east side to Corvane Station. My family's old survey outpost. Nobody's watched it in twenty years because nobody remembers it exists."

"And the Syndicate?"

"Will know we've flown somewhere. They'll guess north. But there are eleven strips along this coast and they'll have to check them one at a time." She met his eyes. "It buys us a day. Maybe two. It's not much."

"It's more than we had this morning." He leaned over the chart, and she watched him work—the quick, hungry intelligence of him, the way his mind moved through terrain like water finding a channel. This was the man the Syndicate had once trusted with their money, she realized. Not a fool. A fool wouldn't frighten them.

"There's a problem," he said quietly. "Your plane."

"What about it?"

"You said *borrowed*." He tapped the chart. "Borrowed from whom? Because if the Syndicate is leaning on this town—and they are; I've seen the way the harbormaster won't meet my eyes anymore—then every favor you call in is a debt someone else has to carry. I've spent two years learning exactly how that feels. I won't do it to your friends."

Maren was silent for a moment. It was the first thing he'd said that sounded like a man who had actually changed, and she distrusted herself for noticing.

"Denna Oakes," she said finally. "She runs the mail run to the lighthouse stations. She owes my family more than she'll ever admit—my grandfather's charts saved her father's boat twice. She'll fly us to Harrow's and swear blind to anyone who asks that we never set foot in her plane." A thin smile. "Besides, she's been wanting an excuse to spite the Syndicate since they tried to buy her moorage lease. Some debts are pleasant to pay."

* * *

That evening they emptied Josiah's journal onto the kitchen table and read it by lamplight while rain worked patiently at the windows.

Maren had known the journal existed her whole life, but she had never read past the first pages—it had felt too much like eavesdropping on a dead man's prayers. Now she read it as evidence, and evidence demanded a colder eye than grief allowed. Page by page, Josiah Corvane assembled the Leeward's last voyage in his careful surveyor's hand: cargo manifests copied in duplicate, coordinates logged to the second decimal, and then, in the final third, something else entirely. Names. Dates. Payments received and payments *promised*, in a column headed with a mark Maren recognized from the manifest chest—the coiled serpent of the Vane house, drawn small and neat, like a signature on a poison pen letter.

"He knew them," Tamsin said. His voice had gone strange. "Before the wreck. Your grandfather was doing business with the Vanes."

"Not business." Maren turned a page with a conservator's care. "*Work*. Look—he logged soundings along the whole leeward coast for eight months before the ship sailed. He was mapping the approaches. And here—" she tapped an entry dated six weeks before the sinking—"he writes that the cargo manifest he was given didn't match what was loaded. That he raised it with the owners' agent and was told to log what he was given or lose his berth."

Tamsin had gone very still. In the lamplight his face had the look of a man watching a tide come in over ground he thought was solid.

"Maren. If your grandfather documented a falsified manifest before the Leeward ever left port—"

"Then the wreck wasn't just a wreck. It was a scheme that got wet." She closed the journal gently, as if the truth inside might bruise. "And the salvage settlement my family signed was hush money dressed up as charity. The Vanes didn't cheat us of rights. They bought our silence about their own crime, and my grandfather signed it because he was dying and his daughter needed the money, and shame is cheaper than lawyers."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain thickened toward the gale they both could feel gathering offshore, the season turning its great wheel.

"This goes beyond your debt," Maren said. "And beyond my family. If we get this over the mountains—to a court, to a journalist, to anyone the Vanes can't buy—"

"It buries them." Tamsin said it flatly, but she heard what moved underneath the flatness. "And they know it. Which is why Aldous is in town buying charm by the gallon." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. When he lowered them, he looked older. "There's something you should understand about me, Maren. When the pressure gets high enough, I have historically made the choice that keeps me breathing. I'm not proud of the record. But you should know it before you stake your grandfather's name on me."

"I do know it," she said. "I watched you pocket a document in a flooding cave instead of swimming for your life. Whatever you are, Tamsin, you're not only a runner." She stood, gathering the journal into its oilcloth. "We leave in four days. Denna flies at first light Thursday. Pack for cold, pack for altitude, and pack nothing you'd hate to lose—because on Verrane, we lose things."

* * *

On the third night, unable to sleep, Maren walked down to the harbor and found the watchers exactly where she expected them: the fair-haired man called Pell, smoking beneath the fish shed lamps, pretending an interest in the tide tables nailed to the post. He tipped his hat to her with a smile like mild weather. She smiled back the way her father had taught her—pleasantly, emptily, giving nothing away—and walked the length of the quay with her heart hammering pleasantly, emptily, all the way through.

Let him report that she was calm. Let them waste a day wondering what calm meant.

At the end of the pier she stopped and looked north, where the clouds had torn open briefly around Verrane's black shoulders. The snowline glowed faintly in the starlight, lower than it had been that morning. Three weeks, she had told Tamsin. Looking at it now, she amended the estimate downward and told no one, least of all herself, a comfortable number.

Behind her, boots on planking. She turned, hand already closing around the flare gun in her coat pocket—and it was Tamsin, breathless, holding a folded paper like it had burned him on the way over.

"Aldous found me," he said. "An hour ago. At the inn." He held out the paper. "He didn't threaten anything. That's what scared me. He just gave me this and said the offer stands until the pass closes."

Maren unfolded it under the harbor lamp. It was a single line, typed, on Syndicate letterhead:

*The debt dies with the document. So does everything else.*

Below it, in ink, a date. Four days out. The morning they were meant to fly.

"They know," Tamsin said quietly. "Not where we're going—they can't. But they know we're going, and they know when. Someone talked, Maren. Someone close."

Above the dark water, far to the north, the mountain waited with its new white collar, patient as a creditor.
