## 6. The Long Ascent

The mountains did not announce themselves. They arrived the way weather arrives in Greyhaven—first as a suggestion on the horizon, then as a fact you could no longer argue with.

Tamsin felt them before he saw them, a change in the quality of the air, a thinning of the salt, and by mid-morning of the second day the road had given up pretending to be a road and become a track, and the track had become a rumor of a track, and they were walking on stone that had never cared what anyone thought of it.

Maren walked ahead, as she had since dawn, her pack riding high on her shoulders like something that belonged there. She moved the way her plane flew, he was beginning to understand: economically, without wasted motion, with an attention so total it looked like indifference. She checked the ground ahead of her feet every third step, not out of fear but out of habit, the way a sailor checks the sky.

Behind them, the valley fell away in folds of green and grey, the sea a hammered sheet at its far end, Greyhaven invisible already, which felt like a kind of mercy.

"You're favoring your left," Maren said, without turning around.

"I'm not."

"You've been doing it since the river crossing." She stopped, finally, and looked back at him, and her eyes went where his leg was, not where his face was pretending to be. "Which knee?"

"The one I have," Tamsin said.

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the things about her that unsettled him most—that she could stand in silence for as long as it took for a lie to die of natural causes.

"Old fracture," he admitted. "Femur. It set crooked because I set it myself, which is exactly the sort of decision a man makes when he's twenty-four and certain he's immortal." He rolled the joint experimentally. It answered with a deep, grinding ache, familiar as an old debt. "It's fine on flat ground. It complains on scree."

"There's four miles of scree above the treeline," Maren said. "And then the pass."

"Then we'd best make complaints while we can still afford them."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if she ever decided to build there—and she turned and walked on, and Tamsin followed, and told himself the tightness in his chest was only the altitude.

* * *

They made the treeline by late afternoon, pines giving way to krummholz giving way to nothing at all, just wind-scoured rock and lichen the color of old bruises. The air up here tasted of iron and distance. Below them the world was still green; above, the peaks wore their snow like men who had put on formal dress early, expecting company.

Maren called a halt at a shelf of granite sheltered from the worst of the wind, and they ate cold food in the particular companionship of people who are too tired to talk. Tamsin's knee had gone from complaining to filing formal grievances. He stretched it out on the stone and watched the light drain westward off the ridgelines, gold going to copper going to ash.

"We're being followed," Maren said.

He didn't look up. Looking up was how you told someone you'd been hoping they were wrong. "Since when?"

"This morning. Maybe yesterday. There's a man in a grey coat who keeps being where a man in a grey coat shouldn't be." She turned her tin cup slowly in her hands. "Back at the ford, he was downstream of us. On the switchbacks, he was above us. Now—" she tipped her chin toward a spur of rock half a mile back along their route, dark against the darkening sky, "—I'd bet the Corvane name he's on that shoulder right now, watching our fire or lack of one."

"A tracker."

"A Syndicate tracker. They don't hire the other kind." She said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than if she'd said it afraid. "My grandfather used to say the Vanes never send soldiers. They send accountants with good boots. Men who follow you the way interest follows a loan—quietly, patiently, always gaining."

Tamsin stared into the middle distance, doing arithmetic he hated. One man, alone, moving fast over ground he clearly knew. Not trying to catch them. Not yet. Herding them, maybe. Or simply confirming—watching where they went, waiting for the pass to close behind them, waiting for the mountain to do the Syndicate's work for free.

"He could have taken us at the ford," Tamsin said. "Two of us, loaded down, one narrow crossing. If Vane wanted the manifest badly enough to spend a tracker, why not take it there?"

"Because taking it isn't the same as owning it," Maren said. "You know that better than anyone. A stolen manifest gets contested. A surrendered one doesn't." She looked at him then, directly, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it heavier. "They don't want to rob you, Tamsin. They want you to hand it over and thank them for the privilege. That's how they got my family. That's how they get everyone."

The wind came up the face of the mountain and went through his jacket like it was reading his mail. He thought about the document sewn into the lining of his own pack—the manifest, the names, the weight of it no greater than a folded shirt and no lighter than a coffin lid. He thought about how many times in the last two days he had imagined setting it down. Just leaving it somewhere. Letting the mountain have it. Letting the whole thing drown again the way it had drowned once before.

He hadn't told Maren about those imaginings. He suspected she'd had them too. He suspected that was the real treaty between them—not trust, exactly, but the mutual recognition of two people carrying things they'd both considered dropping.

"We move at first light," he said. "We put the pass behind us before he can close the gap."

Maren nodded once, and banked the fire down to nothing, and the mountain closed over them like water.

* * *

The scree began an hour after dawn and did not end.

It was the kind of ground that punished certainty—a whole slope of loose stone, grey and clattering, shifting underfoot with every step, the mountain quietly dismantling itself below the snowline and daring anything with legs to cross it. Maren took it in long diagonal lines, reading the slope the way she read weather, finding the firm seams in the chaos. Tamsin followed in her exact footprints, which worked beautifully for forty minutes.

Then his knee ended the arrangement.

It happened without ceremony. One step planted, the next step begun, and the joint simply declined—a hot wire of pain from hip to heel, the leg folding like wet paper, and suddenly he was sliding, arms windmilling, stones hissing away downslope beneath him in a long silver cascade toward a drop he couldn't see the bottom of.

He heard Maren shout his name. He heard the mountain answer with more sliding stone. And then he heard nothing at all, because the world had narrowed to the single animal problem of not dying.

His hand found a boulder. His fingers closed on it and held, screaming tendons, boots scrabbling for purchase against stone that offered none, his whole body strung out on the slope like laundry in a gale. Below him the scree ran out into air. Above him, silence, and then movement—Maren coming down toward him in controlled slides, fast and precise, no hesitation anywhere in her, the way she did everything.

"Rope," she said, when she was close enough. Not a question. Her voice was level, but her eyes weren't; he'd seen that look before, on people pulling drowning swimmers from Greyhaven's harbor. Fear, worn as focus.

"I have it—" he started.

"You have a rope or you have pride. Choose fast."

He chose the rope. She anchored it to the boulder with two wraps and a hitch done blind and perfect, and fed it down to him, and talked him through the last ten feet in a voice gone quiet and steady as a compass needle—*left foot there, no, there, weight on the heel, now let me take it*—and then her hands were on his forearm and his collar, hauling him up onto solid ground, and the two of them lay on the stone breathing hard while the scree finished its argument without them.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The wind filled in the silence with its own opinions.

"That," Tamsin managed finally, "was nearly very embarrassing."

"Shut up," Maren said, but her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he understood that she was not angry. She was frightened, and the fright was only now allowed to arrive, after the emergency had cleared customs. She sat up and looked at his knee, which had swollen inside his trouser leg into something the mountain could feel smug about. "Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Truthfully."

"No," he said. "But yes anyway."

She stared at him for a moment, and then she laughed—one short, sharp, involuntary note, gone as soon as it came, but real. Then she bound the knee with a strip of her own shirt and half the tape in her kit, wrapped it tight enough to make his eyes water, and got under his arm on the bad side without asking permission.

"It's four miles to the pass shelter," she said. "We'll do it slow. We'll do it together. And tomorrow—" she paused, adjusting his weight against her, finding the balance of it, "—tomorrow we'll figure out what we do about the man in the grey coat."

"How do you know he's still following?"

"Because I checked the slope while you were busy auditioning for the sea," she said. "He's on the ridge above us. He watched the whole thing." Her jaw tightened. "He knows your knee is ruined, Tamsin. He knows exactly how slow we are now. Whatever he's waiting for—he just learned he has time."

They climbed. The sun climbed with them, indifferent and bright. And Tamsin, leaning on a woman whose grandfather his own blood had helped ruin, carrying a document that could free him or bury them both, felt the truth of his situation settle over him with the clarity of altitude:

He had spent six years running so that no one would ever hold him up again. And now he could not take another step unless she did.

Above them, at the lip of the pass, something glinted—briefly, deliberately, like a signal or a salute. The man in the grey coat, letting them see him.

Letting them know the race was still on, and that he liked their odds exactly as they were.
