## 7. Snowline

The snow came the way Maren's grandfather had always said it would—sideways, and without apology.

She'd been watching the sky all afternoon, reading it the way other people read faces. The clouds had been stacking themselves over the western peaks since noon, gray going to slate going to a bruised purple that meant business, and by the time they reached the shelter—a low stone hut wedged into the shoulder of the pass, its roof sagging under decades of moss—the first flakes were already coming down fat and slow, like the mountain was testing them before committing.

"Inside," she said, and Tamsin didn't argue.

That alone told her how bad he felt. His ankle had been swelling since the scree field below them, wrapped in a strip of his own shirt over his boot, and he'd climbed the last hour in a silence that wasn't stoicism so much as arithmetic—he was calculating what each step cost him and deciding not to say. She'd watched him do it. She'd let him.

The shelter was one room, stone-floored, with an iron stove that some long-dead survey crew had bolted to the wall and a stack of split pine that someone, mercifully, had left dry. Maren got a fire going while Tamsin lowered himself onto the bench along the far wall, easing the boot off with a hiss he tried to swallow. The ankle beneath was purple-black and tight as a drumhead.

"You should have told me at the scree."

"And you'd have done what?" He managed something like a smile. "Carry me?"

"I'd have turned us back."

"To where?" He nodded at the window, where the snow was thickening by the minute, erasing the trail they'd come up on. "There's no back anymore, Maren. There's only through."

She wanted to argue. Instead she fed the stove another log and watched the flames take it, because he was right, and being right made her angrier than being wrong would have.

They ate tinned fish and hard bread and drank tea from her battered thermos, and for a little while the storm outside was just weather—loud, enormous, but only weather. Tamsin talked about nothing. About the fishing boats in Greyhaven, about the way gulls argued over the harbor like creditors at a door, about anything except the canvas-wrapped bundle strapped to his pack, which sat between them like a third person who never spoke but was always listening.

The manifest. Her grandfather's journal beside it in her own bag, its pages soft as cloth from sixty years of handling. Elias Corvane had written in it every day of the Leeward's last voyage, right up until the entries stopped mid-sentence, and Maren had read those final pages so many times she could recite them: *Vane says the weather will hold. Vane is wrong about everything except money.*

The snow kept falling. And somewhere inside the falling snow, Maren knew, was a man named Corliss Hale.

* * *

She'd seen him first two days ago, or thought she had—a figure on the ridgeline behind them, patient as a fence post, gone when she looked again. She hadn't told Tamsin then. Tonight, with the fire down to coals and Tamsin half-asleep against the wall, she finally did.

"There's been someone following us."

He came awake the way sailors do—all at once, no grogginess anywhere in him. "Since when?"

"I think since the ferry landing. I saw him again this morning on the ridge. Tall. Gray coat. Walks like he's got all the time in the world."

"Hale." Tamsin said the name flatly, the way you'd name a diagnosis. "Corliss Hale. The Syndicate doesn't send anyone else twice."

"You know him."

"I financed my last expedition through men who smile like weather, Maren. Hale is the part after the smiling." He looked at the door—at the wind hammering it, at the snow sealing them into their stone room—and she watched him do the arithmetic again, the same silent tallying he'd been doing since the scree. "He won't come up in this tonight. Even Hale isn't stupid enough to climb in whiteout. But tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow we're gone before light."

"Maren." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "If he catches us on the open pass, in this, with my ankle—we don't outrun him. We don't outclimb him. We get cornered somewhere worse than this, and he takes the manifest off your grandfather's bones, and everything Elias wrote dies in a snowdrift."

"So what's your plan?"

And here was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing she would turn over and over in the weeks to come, looking for the moment the shape of it changed: he hesitated. Just barely. A hitch in him, small as a skipped heartbeat, and then he covered it with that crooked, exhausted smile and said, "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

She slept. That was the unforgivable part, maybe—that she trusted him enough to sleep, curled against her pack with her grandfather's journal under her cheek like a child with a stuffed animal, while across the room Tamsin Brightwater sat by the dying fire doing sums with a man's life.

* * *

She woke to voices.

For one disoriented second she thought the storm had gotten inside, that the wind itself had learned to speak—but no. The door stood open six inches, packed around with blue dawn light and blowing snow, and in that gap stood a tall man in a gray coat dusted white, snow melting off his shoulders onto the stone floor, and he was talking to Tamsin in a low, reasonable voice, the voice of a bank manager discussing terms.

"—and I'm sympathetic, genuinely. But Mr. Brightwater, you must see how this looks. You owe my employers a very great deal, and here you are, halfway up a mountain, carrying something that belongs to them."

"It doesn't belong to them." Tamsin's voice was steady. Too steady. She knew that steadiness; she'd heard it in pilots who were about to put a plane down in weather they couldn't survive. "It belongs to the dead, and to their families. It's evidence, Hale. Of fraud. Your employers falsified the Leeward's salvage claim sixty years ago and let Elias Corvane's family carry the loss. The manifest proves it."

Hale stepped fully inside then, stamping the snow from his boots with unhurried courtesy, and Maren's hand closed around the journal in her bag. The man was exactly as she'd imagined and somehow worse—unremarkable, almost kind-looking, with the eyes of someone who had long ago stopped seeing people as anything but line items.

"The Syndicate's position on historical documents is well known," Hale said mildly. "Possession is nine-tenths. As for the tenth—" his gaze slid to her, and she made herself meet it, "—Miss Corvane. Your family has my condolences. And my offer."

"There's an offer?" Her voice came out colder than she'd intended.

"There is always an offer. That's rather the Syndicate's whole character." He unbuttoned his coat and sat, uninvited, on the bench opposite Tamsin, arranging himself like a man settling in for a long negotiation he already knew the end of. "The manifest comes to us. In exchange, Mr. Brightwater's debt is voided—not forgiven, voided, there's a legal distinction that matters to people who care about such things—and Miss Corvane walks down the mountain untouched, with her grandfather's journal, and whatever story she cares to tell. The Syndicate is generous with grief. It costs nothing."

"And if we refuse?"

Hale spread his hands, taking in the storm, the mountains, the smallness of the shelter, the smallness of them. "Then I take the manifest, and I'm afraid the arithmetic becomes less pleasant. Two people, one injured, three days from anywhere, in weather that kills experienced climbers every year. Accidents happen in the passes, Miss Corvane. They happen constantly. It's practically a tradition."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Maren had ever heard. Louder than the wind. She looked at Tamsin—looked at him the way you look at a chart when the instruments have failed, searching for true north—and found his face carefully blank, which frightened her more than any expression could have.

"Tamsin," she said quietly. "Tell him."

He looked at her. Held her eyes. And smiled—that same crooked, exhausted smile from last night, the one she'd started, God help her, to trust.

"Here's my counteroffer," he said to Hale. "You leave now, alone. We keep walking. And when we reach the valley, the manifest goes to the provincial records office, the newspapers, and the Corvane family solicitor, all within the hour. Your employers can spend the next decade explaining to investigators why their tracker was chasing two civilians through a blizzard."

Something flickered in Hale's face—not anger. Interest. The interest of a man watching a card player make a bet he hasn't seen before.

"That's bold," Hale said. "Bold men usually want something. What do you want, Mr. Brightwater? Truly?"

"I want you to understand that you've lost."

"Do I?" Hale rose, buttoning his coat, and for a moment the kindness drained out of his face entirely and left something old and patient underneath, something that had waited out storms longer than this one. "Then I'll leave you to your victory. The weather should clear by morning. I'll be at the col below the eastern ridge—it's the only way through, you know. You'll come to me eventually. Everyone does."

He paused at the door, snow swirling in around him, and looked back once.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Brightwater—I believe you meant that. Which is what makes tomorrow so unfortunate."

Then he was gone, swallowed white, and the door banged shut, and the silence came back.

Maren let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for two days. "You did it. You actually—" She laughed, shaky. "You stared him down."

Tamsin didn't answer. He was looking at the fire, at the coals, at nothing, and his hands were flat on his knees and very still.

"Tamsin?"

"We need to leave," he said. "Now. Before first light. Before the storm breaks."

"Now? In the dark, in this—"

"He's right about the col, Maren. It's the only way through, and he knows it, and he'll be waiting." He was already moving, pulling his boot on over the swollen ankle without wincing, or wincing where she couldn't see. "But there's another way. The north face. Old miners' route—it's steep, it's dangerous, and nobody waits on it because nobody sane takes it. We go down the north face in the dark and we're past him before he knows we've moved."

She stared at him. "You know a route off the north face of a mountain you've never climbed?"

"I studied the charts for three weeks before I ever came to Greyhaven." He met her eyes then, and his were steady, and honest, and full of something she couldn't name yet, something sitting just below the surface like a rock under fast water. "I planned for every version of this. Trust me."

And she did. That was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry like a stone: she looked at him in the coal-light with the storm screaming at the door and the man in the gray coat waiting below, and she trusted him completely.

"All right," she said. "The north face. Show me."

He smiled, and began packing the gear, and did not look at her again.

Because there was no north-face route. He'd invented it in the space between Hale's question and his answer, whole cloth, the way he invented everything—smoothly, convincingly, with steady eyes. The real plan had assembled itself in his head during the standoff, cold and complete: give Hale the route to the col, confirm the eastern passage, buy Maren's safety with the only coin he had left that wasn't the manifest. *We'll come through the col,* he would tell Hale at first light, alone, while she believed herself descending the north face toward the valley. *Let her go, and I'll walk into your hands.*

He owed the Syndicate a debt. He was about to pay it with himself.

Outside, the snow went on falling, softening the world, burying tracks, keeping secrets—even, for a few hours more, from the woman lacing her boots beside him, humming her grandfather's old songs, ready to follow him anywhere.
