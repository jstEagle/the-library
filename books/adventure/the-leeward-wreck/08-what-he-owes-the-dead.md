## 8. What He Owes the Dead

The bothy smelled of peat smoke and old rain, and Tamsin sat alone in it for the first time in nine days.

Maren had gone down to the glen before dawn to check the ferry strip—to see whether the Syndicate's watchers had moved off the road, whether the weather window she'd been praying for was real or a rumor the sky told to fools. She'd left him the fire, the last of the oatcakes, and instructions not to open the chest without her.

He had opened it.

Not all the way. Not out of greed—though he could no longer pretend greed wasn't in him, curled like a hook under the breastbone—but because he needed to know what he was carrying over those mountains. A man should know the weight of what breaks his back. He told himself that, sitting cross-legged on the cold flagstones with the manifest chest between his knees, its salt-stained iron bands flaking rust onto his trousers. He told himself it was prudence. It was, at least partly, prudence.

The cave had given them three hours on that first day, and they'd spent two of them hauling the chest up through the flooding dark, Maren swearing at the tide like it owed her money, Tamsin's shoulder screaming where the old injury tore itself fresh against the rock. They'd cached it here, in this shepherd's bothy below the snowline, wrapped in oilcloth, and neither of them had touched it since. There had been reasons not to. Good ones. Once you read a thing, you can't unread it; once you know what you hold, you become someone who holds it.

He worked the lock with a flat stone and a patience he didn't feel. The lid came up with a groan of protesting hinges and a breath of air from another century—brine and paper and something faintly sweet, like wax candles long burned down.

Inside, the documents lay in ordered rows, dry as autumn leaves. The Leeward had been a well-run ship before she died; her papers were the papers of a careful man. Bills of lading. Insurance certificates bearing the seal of an underwriter whose name Tamsin recognized from his grandfather's study, from the brass plaque above the mantel. Letters of instruction. And there, bound in oiled leather gone stiff as bark, the master manifest—the list of every soul and every stake aboard her when she went down off the Corvane coast forty years ago.

He lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking.

* * *

Outside, the wind had begun again, working at the bothy's one shuttered window with fingers made of grit. Inside, Tamsin Brightwater read by firelight, and the firelight did him no kindnesses.

The first pages were cargo: timber, salt fish, machine parts, a piano crated for a house that would never hear it played. The second section was crew. He found Elias Corvane's name where Maren had promised it would be—third mate, age thirty-one—and felt a strange grief for a man he'd never met, a grief that belonged properly to someone else and sat wrong in his chest, borrowed goods.

The third section was investors.

He almost skipped it. It was the dullest part, surely—names and sums, the arithmetic of other men's ambitions. But his eye caught on a familiar formation of letters, the way you catch your own name in a stranger's conversation across a crowded room, and stopped.

*BRIGHTWATER, E.*

He read it twice. Three times. The letters didn't rearrange themselves. The sum beside them didn't shrink. Four hundred shares in the Leeward Shipping Concern, subscribed by Edmond Brightwater of Harrowgate, gentleman investor, dated eleven months before the wreck.

His grandfather's name. His grandfather's money. His grandfather's signature, reproduced in facsimile in the margin, in a hand Tamsin knew better than his own—that confident forward slant, the way the terminal strokes hooked downward like talons. He had spent his childhood being told he wrote too carelessly, that a Brightwater's hand should look like *that*. He had spent his adulthood signing loan documents in a hand that looked exactly like that, and hating himself a little each time the pen touched paper.

Edmond Brightwater had helped buy the Leeward. Had helped load her, insure her, send her out. And when she went down—with Elias Corvane aboard, with the salvage rights that should have passed to the Corvane family tangled deliberately, corruptly, into the Concern's own charter—Edmond Brightwater had been one of the men who profited from the loss.

Tamsin set the manifest down on his knees. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Everything else was shaking.

* * *

He thought he understood debt. He had built a life inside it the way a barnacle builds inside its shell—gradually, then completely, until the shell was the shape of the living thing and there was no separating them. He knew debt the way sailors know weather: intimately, superstitiously, with a private vocabulary of dread. He knew what he owed the Vane Syndicate to the last coin, because he'd written the number on the back of his hand once, drunk, so he couldn't forget it in the morning. He hadn't forgotten it any morning since.

But he had always believed the story he told about himself: that he was a gambler who'd lost, an optimist who'd bet on a storm season that never came, a fool with expensive taste in expeditions. That the debt was his own creation, his own fault, his own weight to carry. There was a grim dignity in that version. Even shame, if it's earned, is a kind of property.

Now the manifest lay open on his knees and the story came apart in his hands like wet paper.

Because here was the thing he saw slowly, the way you see the true shape of a coastline only when the fog lifts: the Vane Syndicate had bought his debt two years ago from a consortium of regional lenders—bought it cheap, aggressively, against all sense, chasing a storm chaser with no assets worth seizing. Everyone had said it was strange. Tamsin had said it himself, more than once, in the bad hours. *Why me? I'm not worth the paper I owe on.* He'd assumed appetite. Predators don't need reasons.

But predators do have reasons. And Edmond Brightwater had been a founding subscriber of the Concern, and the Concern had become the Concern Holdings Group, and the Concern Holdings Group had been absorbed, decades ago, in a merger whose paperwork Tamsin now realized he had never read—

—into Vane.

The fire popped. He didn't move.

It wasn't coincidence. It had never been coincidence. The Syndicate hadn't chased him because he owed them; they had chased him because he was *theirs*, an inheritance, a receivable with legs, the grandson of a man whose fortune had been built on a drowned ship and a cheated family. They had financed his expedition the way you water a crop. Every letter he'd dodged in Greyhaven, every charming menace in a good coat—they weren't collecting a debt. They were managing an asset that had wandered off the ledger. Him.

And the manifest in his hands—the manifest that proved the fraud, that restored the Corvanes' rights, that could clear his debts or bury him—was, in the oldest and coldest sense, already his. Paid for. Inherited. Bought with his grandfather's money, which had been made from this ship, this lie, this drowned man whose granddaughter was at this moment walking down through the glen to check the road for watchers, trusting him.

Tamsin laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry note with nothing behind it, and it frightened the small part of him still watching from a distance.

* * *

He got up because sitting still had become unbearable, and paced the bothy's twelve feet end to end, end to end, while the wind worried the shutters. Grief came first, oddly—not for himself but for the boy he'd been, the one who'd idolized Edmond Brightwater, who'd kept the old man's compass on his belt through every storm he'd ever chased, who'd signed his loans in that taloned hand thinking he was honoring a legacy. The compass suddenly weighed a pound of lead on his hip. He took it off and set it on the windowsill, face down, and couldn't tell you why except that he couldn't look at it.

Then fury, which was worse, because fury wanted somewhere to go and the man it belonged to was forty years dead and couldn't be reached by anything Tamsin had. You can't shout at a grave. You can't make a dead man account for what he took. The anger just circled the bothy with him, pacing, pacing, looking for a door.

And underneath both, quiet and terrible, the honest look—the first one he'd allowed himself in years.

Here is what he saw: a man who had spent a decade running from creditors, telling himself he was free-spirited, unencumbered, allergic to ledgers, when in truth he had simply inherited the family business. Edmond Brightwater had made his money from other people's losses and called it investment. Tamsin had made his living from storms and called it science. Both of them had stood at a distance from the wreckage, taking notes, taking profit, taking care never to be standing where the damage landed. He thought of the villages he'd flown over during the bad seasons, the roofs he'd photographed for insurers, the people watching his plane pass and wondering whose side of the sky it was on. He had always answered *nobody's*. He had never once believed it until now, and now the belief curdled, because the manifest proved there had been a side all along, and he'd been born on it.

Maren's grandfather had died carrying cargo for Edmond Brightwater's dividend. Maren had grown up on stories of a theft no one would name. And Tamsin had walked into Greyhaven with his grandfather's compass and his grandfather's handwriting and his grandfather's creditors, and let her teach him the tides, and said nothing, because he hadn't known—and the not-knowing was the last thing he'd have to answer for, and the easiest.

He went back to the chest. He didn't close it. He sat down in front of it like a man keeping vigil, because that was what it was now, whatever else it was: a wake. Forty years late, but a wake.

The document he'd taken from the cave that first day—the single page he'd pocketed before the flood, the insurance certificate he'd shown Maren to win her wary trust—he drew it out now and laid it beside the manifest, aligning the edges, and saw what he should have seen weeks ago: the Concern's charter clause, the salvage-rights rider, the language that had quietly converted a family's birthright into a company's asset. The whole architecture of the theft, in his hands, in his grandfather's ink.

He could burn it. The thought arrived honestly and he let it stand in the room. Burn the manifest, keep the page, trade the page to Vane for his freedom, walk away clean the way Brightwaters had always walked away clean. No one would ever know. The mountain would keep it the way the sea had kept the ship.

The wind gusted hard against the north wall, and somewhere below in the glen, faint under the noise, he heard it: an engine. Not Maren's ferry—a heavier sound, a road engine climbing the track toward the bothy, the kind of sound the Syndicate's watchers made.

Tamsin closed the manifest, wrapped it in oilcloth, and stood with his back to the door and his heart going like a driven bird, understanding at last that he had run out of sea between himself and everything he owed.

Headlights swept the bothy's shuttered window and stopped.
