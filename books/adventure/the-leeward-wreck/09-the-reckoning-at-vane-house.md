## 9. The Reckoning at Vane House

Maren had flown through storms that peeled paint off her wings, but nothing had ever frightened her like the quiet of Vane House at dusk.

It sat above the harbor town of Corrow Bay like a gull on a fish head—white clapboard, green shutters, a widow's walk with an iron railing gone to rust-bloom. Pretty from the water. From the gravel drive, where she stood with wet boots and a satchel heavy as sin, it looked like what it was: a counting house dressed as a home. The Syndicate did its business in parlors. That was how they'd survived three generations while every honest salvage firm on the coast went under.

She had left Tamsin asleep at the trail shelter four hours before dawn, his breath fogging the window, the manifest's oilcloth wrapping still warm from his chest. She had taken the journal too—her grandfather's journal—and the route notes he'd sold to the tracker named Hesk, because Maren Corvane had read enough ledgers in her life to know that men who smile like weather always keep two sets of books.

The betrayal had not surprised her. That was the worst part. She'd lain awake in the shelter listening to him breathe and understood, somewhere below thought, that a man who had spent nine years running would always choose the door over the room. He'd traded their route for her safety, and he hadn't even had the courage to let it be a lie she never caught. He'd left the note. *Forgive me. This is the only way I know.* As if that were an explanation instead of a confession.

So she had done what Corvanes do. She'd gotten up first.

* * *

The woman who opened the door was not what Maren expected. She was small, silver-haired, wearing a cardigan the color of old rope, and she looked at Maren the way one looks at weather coming off the sea—assessing, unimpressed, already calculating the damage.

"You're the Corvane girl," she said. "Edith Vane."

"I know who you are." Maren held up the satchel. "I'm here about your grandfather's ship."

Something moved behind Edith Vane's eyes—a flicker, quickly banked. "My grandfather built a shipping concern. He lost vessels, as all men in that trade do."

"He lost the *Leeward*," Maren said. "And then he lost the truth about it. I have both back now."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the cypress along the drive. Then Edith stepped aside. "You'll take tea," she said, in a voice that made clear it was not a question, "and you will tell me exactly how much trouble my family is in."

They were in the parlor—the good parlor, all dark wood and brass instruments and framed charts of coasts the Vanes had bled dry—when the cars came up the drive. Maren heard them first, gravel crunching under tires too expensive for this road, and watched Edith's teacup pause halfway to her lips.

"That will be my son," Edith said, "and Mr. Hesk, and whatever lawyers they've scraped together since the tracker reported in. You understand, Miss Corvane, that you've just walked into the lion's den and sat down in its chair."

"I didn't walk into it," Maren said. "I flew over it, landed behind it, and locked the gate. The manifest isn't coming down the mountain tonight. It's already here."

Aldous Vane entered like a front rolling in off the water—tall, gray at the temples, smiling the way his grandfather must have smiled when he told the Corvanes their salvage claim had lapsed. Behind him came Hesk, mountain mud still on his boots, and a thin man with a lawyer's pallor carrying a leather case.

"Miss Corvane." Aldous's voice was warm as bathwater. "What a remarkable journey you've had. We were so worried about you both up there in the early snows. Tamsin Brightwater especially—he's had such bad luck, hasn't he? Such debts. Such desperate need of friends."

"Here's what happens next," Maren said, and set the satchel on the table between them. She did not open it. "Inside is the Leeward's original manifest, sealed, countersigned by the harbormaster at Greyhaven, plus forty years of correspondence your family buried—the letters proving the Corvane salvage claim was never voided, only purchased out from under us by a clerk your grandfather paid. There are also copies. Three sets. One goes to the Maritime Court at Port Ellery tomorrow morning whether I'm breathing or not. One went to a journalist in Greyhaven who owes my family more than I can say. And one is in this room."

Hesk shifted. Aldous's smile stayed exactly where it was, which told Maren everything.

"So here is the arithmetic," she went on, standing now, because her grandfather had died in cold water and she would not discuss him sitting down. "You can take this satchel, and lose the other two copies, and spend the next decade explaining to investigators why the Vanes kidnapped a document they claimed meant nothing. Or you can honor the contract your own firm wrote—the one you offered Tamsin Brightwater in Greyhaven, the one promising debt forgiveness in exchange for the manifest. Except the manifest doesn't come to you. It goes to the court. And in exchange, the Vanes withdraw every claim against Tamsin Brightwater, restore the Corvane salvage rights in full, and sign the restoration before I leave this house."

"You're a ferry pilot," Aldous said softly. "You fly mail and tourists. Do you truly believe you can navigate something like this?"

"I fly in weather other pilots ground themselves for," Maren said. "This is just more weather."

* * *

Tamsin arrived an hour after full dark, and Maren heard him before she saw him—boots on gravel, uneven, favoring the knee she'd strapped on the scree, and beneath that the particular silence of a man walking toward a thing he cannot undo.

He came into the parlor soaked past the shoulders, gray-faced, and stopped when he saw her. Whatever speech he'd rehearsed on the road died there. Good, she thought. Let him stand in it.

"Maren." His voice cracked on it. "I can explain—"

"You sold our route to buy my safety without asking me if my safety was worth more than my choice." She kept her voice level, because the room was listening, and because if she let it shake she might never get it back. "That's the explanation. Everything else is decoration."

He took it like a blow taken on purpose—chin down, absorbing. Then he looked at Aldous Vane, and something in his face changed. Maren had seen him afraid on the mountain, afraid in the cave, afraid in his sleep. This was different. This was a man setting down a weight so heavy he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

"The contract," Tamsin said. "Read me the clause."

The lawyer opened his case. Aldous said, "There's no need—"

"Read it." Tamsin's voice filled the parlor the way wind fills a sail. "Clause four. Debt forgiveness contingent on delivery of the Leeward manifest to the Vane Syndicate, quote, *in whatever condition and custody it is found*, end quote. You wrote it vague on purpose. Vague enough that a desperate man would sign it thinking he was handing you paper, when really—" He turned to Maren. "When really it means whoever holds the manifest sets the terms. She does. Not you."

Edith Vane, in her chair by the fire, made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"And clause seven," Tamsin went on, stepping forward, gravel-voiced, relentless. "*All obligations arising from prior agreements between the parties are extinguished upon execution.* Extinguished. Every debt. Including ones inherited." He pulled a folded page from inside his jacket—water-stained, torn from the manifest's margin, the one document he'd pocketed in the cave before the tide came for them all. "Page eleven of the investor registry. Read the name in the third column."

The lawyer read it. Maren watched his face go the color of the ledger paper.

"Brightwater," the lawyer said. "Elias Brightwater. Investor of record, 1961."

"My grandfather helped finance the Leeward's last voyage," Tamsin said, and now his voice did shake, and he let it. "Which means my debt wasn't earned. It was *assigned*. Your syndicate has been collecting on a fraud for nine years—collecting from me for a loss your own firm engineered when it bought out the Corvane claim and scuttled the salvage. The manifest proves it. The correspondence proves it. And I am done being the collateral."

He crossed the room, took the satchel from the table, and placed it in Maren's hands. In front of all of them. Deliberately, the way a man lays a card down when he has already won or already lost and no longer cares which.

"It's yours," he said to her. "It was always going to be yours. I just needed to stop long enough to see it."

Maren looked at him—at the wreck of him, soaked and limping and lighter than she'd ever seen him—and felt the anger in her chest do something complicated, folding down into something that wasn't forgiveness yet but had its address.

"Then we're agreed," she said, turning to the room. "The manifest goes to the Maritime Court at Port Ellery, first ferry. The Corvane salvage rights are restored. Tamsin Brightwater walks out of this house owing no one. Sign it."

Aldous Vane looked at his mother. Edith Vane looked at the fire, and then at the framed chart on the wall—the Leeward's last route, ink faded to ghost-gray—and said, quietly, "Sign it, Aldous. Grandfather's ghosts have waited long enough, and I find I'm tired of paying rent on them."

The pen scratched. Outside, the wind turned, and the first smell of snow came down off the mountains.

Maren tucked the signed restoration into her jacket, beside her grandfather's journal, and felt fifty years of silence lift off her family like fog burning off water. Then she looked at Tamsin, standing empty-handed in the middle of his enemies' parlor, poorer than he'd walked in and visibly, unmistakably free.

"There's a survey boat at Greyhaven in three weeks," she said. "We'll need someone who knows the cave. Someone who can hold his breath and hold his nerve." She shouldered the satchel. "Be at the dock at dawn. And Tamsin—you're flying with me this time. I don't trust you on the ground."

He almost smiled. Almost.

Behind them, unnoticed by everyone but Maren, Edith Vane rose from her chair and stood a long moment before the faded chart of the Leeward's final route, her hand resting on the glass as if on a shoulder—and when she turned, there was a second, older document in her hands, yellowed and folded, that she held out toward them both across the dying light of the fire.
