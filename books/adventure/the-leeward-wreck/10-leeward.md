## 10. Leeward

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Investigations, Tamsin had learned, moved at the pace of paperwork, and paperwork loved Tuesdays.

He read it standing in the doorway of Maren's boathouse, the wind coming off the harbour in long grey pushes, gulls wheeling and complaining overhead as if they'd been personally inconvenienced by the news. The Vane Syndicate was under formal inquiry. Assets frozen. Three names he knew—Halloway among them, the man who smiled like weather—summoned to give evidence before the maritime commission in the capital. There was a paragraph near the bottom, dense with legal phrasing, that amounted to this: *the debts contracted under fraudulent instruments are void.*

His debt. His ruinous, years-deep, shape-his-whole-life debt. Void.

Tamsin read it twice, then a third time, because the words kept sliding off him like rain off oilskin. He had spent so long carrying the weight that its absence didn't feel like freedom. It felt like vertigo—the lurch of a man who has leaned his shoulder against a door for years and suddenly finds it open.

*You are no longer obligated,* the letter said.

No longer obligated. He turned the phrase over like a coin from a foreign country. For six years he had been a man defined by what he owed. It had decided where he slept, what he ate, which towns he could afford to be seen in, how fast he walked past post offices. Running hadn't just been something he did. It had been the architecture of him. And now a stranger with a stamp had dismantled it in an afternoon.

He folded the letter along its creases, carefully, the way you handle something you don't yet trust yourself with, and put it in his jacket pocket over his heart, which was doing something complicated.

Maren was out on the slipway, kneeling over the survey crates with her sleeves rolled, her hands black to the wrist with pitch. She worked the way her grandfather must have worked, Tamsin thought—like the job would get done because she was doing it, and no other reason required. He watched her for a while from the doorway. She knew he was there. She always knew. But she let him have his moment of watching, the way you let a cat sit in a patch of sun without commenting on it.

"Read it out loud," she said finally, not looking up. "I want to hear your voice when you say it."

"It's void," Tamsin said. "All of it. The whole—" He stopped. Started again. "The Syndicate's finished, Maren. Halloway's giving evidence in three weeks. They froze everything."

She sat back on her heels and looked at him then, and there was pitch on her cheekbone and sawdust in her hair and she was grinning like a woman who had won a bar fight she hadn't even thrown a punch in. "Say the other part."

"They're restoring the salvage rights." He cleared his throat. "To the Corvane family."

"To my family." Her voice went quiet in a way that was louder than shouting. "My grandfather signed that manifest with his own hand, and they took it from him, and they took it from my mother, and they told us all our lives that we were people who lost things. That we were the family the sea beat." She looked out at the water, at the grey line where the cave lay drowned under forty feet of cold Atlantic patience. "We weren't beaten. We were robbed. There's a difference, and now there's paper that says so."

Tamsin came down the slipway and sat beside her on the salt-bleached planks, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because some silences aren't empty. They're full up.

* * *

He was penniless. He wanted to be precise about that, even in the privacy of his own head, because precision was the one honest thing he had left. The inquiry had frozen the Syndicate's assets, which meant the settlement owed to him—if anything was ever owed to anyone—was somewhere in a ledger behind a court seal. His gear was mostly wrecked, his savings were a rumour, and the last of his money had gone to a ferryman named Odell who had asked no questions and charged accordingly.

And he found, to his genuine astonishment, that he did not mind.

This was new. This was the strangest weather he had ever stood in. For years, poverty had been the wolf at his heels; now it was just a fact, sitting quietly across the table, asking nothing of him. He woke in the room above the chandlery that Maren had bullied the landlord into renting him against next season's work, and he drank tea that tasted like tar and freedom, and he walked down to the harbour, and everyone in Greyhaven looked at him—not through him, not past him, but *at* him—and nodded.

That was the part that undid him, some mornings. Being seen. Not as the debtor, not as the stranger, not as the man who kept his collar up and his eyes down. Just as Tamsin Brightwater, who had carried a chest out of a drowning cave and given it away. Old Mrs. Penhale, who ran the bakery, had started leaving the end-cut of the seed loaf aside for him without being asked. Children pointed him out to each other on the quay. A fisherman named Brannock, a man of perhaps eleven words per calendar month, had clapped him on the shoulder outside the Anchor & Chain and said, "Proper done," which Tamsin understood, by local reckoning, to be roughly equivalent to a knighthood.

Visibility, it turned out, was not something that happened to you. It was something you chose, daily, like getting up. Like telling the truth again after the first time hurt.

* * *

On the last Friday of the month, Maren spread the survey plans across the floor of the boathouse and weighted the corners with wrenches and a mug of cold coffee, and told him what she intended to do.

"The commission's granted preliminary access," she said. "Six weeks of fair tides in September, maybe eight if the autumn holds. I'm running a proper survey—full documentation, photogrammetry, the hull, the cargo hold, everything. The Leeward goes into the record under my grandfather's name, where it should have gone sixty years ago." She tapped the plan with a finger still stained faintly brown. "I need a diver. Someone who can work cold, dark, low visibility, someone who doesn't panic when the tide turns early. I need someone stubborn."

"You've got me," Tamsin said, before she'd even asked properly, because some offers you make fast, before the coward in you can file an objection.

"I haven't asked yet."

"I know. I'm pre-committing. It's a technique I learned from creditors."

She laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she'd only started letting him hear in the last month—and then she went quiet, and looked at him with that directness of hers that always made him feel like a window being cleaned.

"Why?" she said. "You could walk. The debt's void. You could take whatever's left of your gear and go chase storms anywhere in the world, and nobody here would think worse of you. Nobody would blame you. So why stay?"

Tamsin looked at the plans a long time before answering, because the true answer was embarrassing, and he had spent enough of his life lying to spend one more day doing it.

"Because I spent six years being a man who runs," he said. "And I got good at it. And being good at it nearly killed me, and it definitely killed everything else. And then I found that ship, and I found that chest, and for about nine days I held the fortune that would have bought me back my life—and then I gave it away, and it was the first thing I'd ever done that I wasn't ashamed of." He paused. "I don't want to go back to being invisible. I like being this man. I'd like to find out if he holds."

"He'll hold," Maren said, simply, as if reporting a tide table. Then she slid the dive roster across the floor toward him. "Sign here."

He signed. His hand shook slightly. It was the best signature of his life.

* * *

They went out to the cave on the neap tide, the two of them in Maren's boat with the engine idling low, the morning so clear that the far mountains stood up sharp against the sky like the ridge-line of a drawn blade. The entrance showed at slack water: a black seam in the cliff base, breathing. You could see the swell move in and out of it, slow, rhythmic, patient—the cave inhaling and exhaling on a schedule older than any of the names written on the manifest.

Maren cut the engine and they drifted.

"First dive is Thursday," she said. "I want to start with the stern section, where the hull's intact. Grandfather's journal says the crew quarters were aft. If there's anything personal left—letters, effects—I want those families to have them. There were eleven men aboard. Eleven families were told their men died as paupers on a bankrupt ship. We're going to tell them the truth instead."

"Eleven," Tamsin said. He had read the manifest so many times he sometimes dreamed in its columns. "One of them was an investor. Elias Brightwater."

"Your grandfather."

"My grandfather." Saying it out loud still landed strangely, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was in his own chest. "He put two hundred pounds into the Leeward and lost it, and the loss broke him, and the shame of the loss went down the family like a stain, and sixty years later his grandson was borrowing money from the same syndicate that sank him, because shame is a thing that teaches itself downward." He shook his head slowly. "It was never a debt I earned, Maren. It was a debt I inherited. And I paid it forward for six years before I even knew whose name was on the original note."

"And now?"

"Now it ends with me." He said it steadily, and found, checking himself the way you check a knot, that it was true. "Not the money. The pattern. The hiding. That's what I'm actually paying off. The rest is arithmetic."

Maren reached over and gripped his forearm once, hard, diver to diver, and let go.

The tide was beginning to turn. They both felt it before they saw it—the change in the boat's drift, the subtle stiffening of the water around the cliff base, the cave's breath going longer and deeper. In an hour the entrance would be sealed, and the Leeward would close her eye and keep her secrets another night, holding her dead and her truth in the dark and the cold, waiting, the way she had waited sixty years, for people willing to come honestly.

Tamsin looked at the black seam until the swell swallowed it, and felt no dread. Only the peculiar calm of a man who has stopped running and discovered that the ground, all along, had been holding him up.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday," Maren agreed, and started the engine, and turned the bow for home.
