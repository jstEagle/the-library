## 1. Miles, Not Years

The odometer read 214,887 when the storm finally stood up on the horizon, and Ines Vasquez thought, not for the first time, that she and the truck were aging at roughly the same rate.

She'd been following this supercell since it was a rumor on radar — a smudge of red over the Nebraska line that had spent four hours deciding whether to become something. Now it had decided. The anvil spread overhead like a ceiling in a house nobody lived in, and beneath it the updraft base went green-black and hungry, rotating with the slow confidence of a thing that knew exactly what it was.

"Okay," she said to the windshield, because talking to herself was cheaper than a spotter network. "Okay. Show me."

The plains out here were flat as a promise from a loan officer — wheat stubble, fence lines, gravel roads running straight to the edge of the world. She took the truck off the highway onto county road 14, gravel popping under the tires like applause, and let the mesocyclone come to her. This was the part she loved, the part she'd never admit to loving: the waiting, the arithmetic of wind, the moment when all the numbers she kept in her head — dewpoints, shear, CAPE, the distance between her bumper and the hail core — resolved into one clean certainty.

*There.* The wall cloud dropped its skirt two miles north, and beneath it the funnel began, tentative as a finger testing bathwater.

Ines pulled over, killed the engine, and got out into air that tasted like iron and cut grass. The tornado touched down white and thin, danced across a fallow field doing no damage to anything but the soil, and lifted again like it had only been stretching.

"Rope-out at 4:47 p.m.," she said, typing it into her log. "Duration ninety seconds. Nobody hurt. Beautiful."

That was the whole job, some days. Ninety seconds of beauty, logged and uploaded for a research consortium that paid her four hundred dollars a deployment, which was not nothing, but was also not the eleven thousand dollars she owed the Delgado brothers.

Her phone buzzed against the dashboard. She looked at the screen — *DELGADO, R.* — and did what she always did: turned it face-down and drove away.

Not forever. Just for now. Just until the adrenaline wore off and the guilt caught up, somewhere around mile eighty.

* * *

The guilt usually caught up around mile eighty, and today was no exception.

She was three counties east, chasing the sunset instead of the storm, when the phone started buzzing again in the cupholder. Face-down, buzzing, insistent, like a wasp in a jar. Raul Delgado. Then, thirty seconds later, Marco Delgado. Then Raul again. They alternated. It was almost musical.

Ines pulled into a gas station outside Wasta, bought a coffee she didn't want because you couldn't loiter without buying something, and sat in the cab watching the light go amber over the buttes.

The voicemails, when she finally listened, were cheerful. That was the terrible thing about the Delgados — they were never angry. Anger would have been easier. Anger you could fight.

*"Ines! Raul here. Hey, listen, no pressure, just thinking about you. Marco says hi. We've been doing some numbers on your account and honestly? The news is better than we expected. Call me back."*

Then Marco's: *"Inesita! You missed Raul's call, he'll be crushed. Listen, we found your little weather channel thing online — the tornado video, very impressive, very you. Anyway. No pressure. But also, pressure. You know how it is."*

She did know how it was. How it was, specifically, was this: eighteen months ago she'd borrowed nine thousand dollars from the friendliest men in three states to replace a transmission, then another two to cover a season of deployments that hadn't paid out, and the interest had been reproducing in the dark of her account like rabbits. The Delgados ran what they called a lending cooperative, which was a phrase that should have been a warning sign all by itself, and they smiled the entire time. They brought pastries to meetings. They remembered her mother's name. They called her *Inesita* in voices warm as fresh bread, and every month the number got bigger, and every month their smiles stayed exactly the same size, which was somehow worse than if the smiles had grown.

She dialed Raul back because not dialing back only made them drive farther to find her, and they always found her, and the driving made them irritable in a way the phone never did.

"Inesita!" Raul answered on the first ring, delighted. "I knew you'd call. Marco owes me ten bucks."

"You bet on whether I'd call?"

"I bet you'd call by seven. He said eight. So really you cost him money and saved me some, which makes you basically a charity." A pause, warm and awful. "So. The numbers."

"The numbers are the same as last month, Raul."

"They're actually a little better than last month, believe it or not. Marco refinanced your late fee into the principal, which sounds bad but is actually — "

"It sounds bad because it's bad."

"See, this is why I like you. You're honest. Honesty is rare in this business." Papers rustled on his end; she could picture his office, the wood paneling, the framed photo of the brothers shaking hands with a minor league baseball mascot. "Here's my thinking. You're good at storms. Storms are predictable to you, right? So be predictable to us. One payment. Any size. Just so we can tell ourselves a story where this ends well."

Ines watched a pronghorn cross the road a quarter mile out, unhurried, sovereign, answering to nobody. "I'll have something after the next deployment."

"That's what you said after the last one."

"And I meant it both times."

"That's what scares us, Inesita." And he laughed, genuinely, and hung up before she could find anything in that sentence to hold onto.

* * *

The diner was called the Chuckwagon, and it had a plastic cow out front wearing a saddle made of painted tires, and Ines chose it for dinner because it was forty miles from anywhere and she was tired of being findable.

It didn't work. Nothing worked. At 9:40 p.m., while she was working through a plate of chicken-fried steak the size of a hubcap, a silver Suburban eased into the lot and parked beside her truck with the precision of a couple pulling into adjacent spots at a funeral.

Marco came in first — the younger one, round-faced, reading glasses pushed up into curly hair, dressed like a substitute teacher who wanted to be liked. Raul followed, silver-haired, tan, in a golf shirt, radiating the contentment of a man whose worst problem had never once been his own.

"Well, look who's eating vegetables," Marco said, eyeing her plate, sliding into the booth across from her uninvited. Raul took the window seat. They flanked her like bookends, if bookends ordered pie.

"How did you even find me?"

"Inesita." Raul pressed a hand to his chest, wounded. "We're not stalking you. We're *invested* in you. There's a difference. Stalkers don't bring gifts." He nodded at Marco, who produced a bakery box from nowhere and set it on the table between the napkin dispenser and her coffee. Inside: six pan dulce, still faintly warm.

"You drove two hours to bring me sweet bread and a threat."

"A *timeline*," Raul corrected. "The bread is just hospitality." He opened a leather folder — leather, always leather, she'd never seen either brother touch paper that wasn't leather-bound — and laid out a single printed page, squared carefully to the table's edge. "So here's where we are. Eleven thousand, four hundred and change. We've carried you eighteen months. We've never once raised our voices. Frankly, we deserve sainthood."

"You deserve an audit."

"Ha! Marco, write that down, she's funny." Marco did not write it down. "Here's the generous part. Two weeks. One final extension, interest frozen — frozen, Ines, that's us losing money on purpose, out of respect — and then we start collecting however we see fit. And before you ask: yes, the truck counts. Yes, the equipment counts. We know what the radar unit's worth. Marco priced it."

"It's used," Marco said gently, apologetically, pushing his glasses down onto his nose to look at her over them. "But it's good used."

Ines looked at the page. The number at the bottom had been highlighted in yellow, which was somehow more menacing than red. Two weeks. Fourteen days. She had four hundred dollars coming from the consortium, maybe six hundred from a stock footage sale if the editor ever returned her calls, and a checking account that contained, at last count, eighty-one dollars and a sense of humor.

"I don't have it," she said simply. "You know I don't have it. Freezing the interest on money I can't pay isn't generosity, it's theater."

Raul leaned back and laced his fingers over his stomach, considering her the way a man considers a horse he's decided not to buy yet. "Okay. Honest. Good. Then let's skip to the honest solution." He tapped the folder. "We get offers. All the time. Odd jobs, escort work, recovery work — people need things found, driven, delivered. You have a truck, a skill set, and a face that looks trustworthy when you're lying, which is rarer than you'd think. Anything comes through our office, you take it, fee goes against your balance at full value. Dollar for dollar. No interest on earned credit. That's not theater, Inesita. That's math."

"What kind of jobs?"

"Honest ones. Mostly." Both brothers smiled at the same time, identically, and Ines felt the smile pass through her like weather.

She should have said no. She knew that the way you know a cloud is going to rotate — some deep barometric instinct, older than reasoning. Taking work from the Delgados meant the debt stopped being a number and became a leash. Every job would buy a little slack and cost a little length, and the slack and the length would cancel out forever, and she'd spend her life at seventy miles an hour towing their interests behind her.

But the pan dulce was warm, and the steak was half-eaten, and the truck in the parking lot was technically theirs already in every way that mattered, and fourteen days was fourteen days.

"Fine," she said. "Anything legal."

"Legal-ish," Raul said, and when she stared at him he spread his hands, all innocence. "*Mostly* legal. I'm being transparent. Transparency is a gift, Inesita. Ask anyone."

Marco slid a pen across the table. She signed a single sheet — no carbon copy, no second signature, terms described aloud rather than written, which she noted and hated and did anyway — and the brothers beamed at her like proud uncles.

"There," Raul said, closing the folder. "Doesn't that feel better? A plan. Everybody loves a plan." He stood, dropped a twenty on the table — "for the lady's dinner, we're not animals" — and paused, struck by a thought, turning back with the expression of a man about to mention a lottery ticket he'd forgotten he bought.

"Oh — actually, funny timing. There *is* something. An old fella came by the office last week, prospector type, wanted a driver up into the mountains. Paid in cash, talked too much. We told him we don't do mountain runs anymore." Raul shrugged. "Probably nothing. But if a man like that asks around again, well. You're our girl now."

He said it lightly, tossing it off, already walking toward the door. Marco followed, boxless, the pan dulce left behind as tribute.

"Our girl," Ines repeated to the empty booth, and ate a piece of sweet bread, and it was delicious, and she hated that it was delicious.

* * *

She slept in the truck at a rest stop with the windows cracked and the radar app running on low brightness, out of habit more than hope. Sometime after midnight a line of thunderstorms marched through from the west, and she lay in the dark listening to rain hammer the roof and feeling, briefly, the old clean peace of it — weather passing over, asking nothing, owing nothing.

Then her phone lit up. Unknown number. Local prefix, mountain country, two area codes away.

She almost didn't answer. But the Delgados' words were sitting in her chest like swallowed stones — *if a man like that asks around again* — and something in her, some instinct she'd learned to trust more than any forecast, made her thumb move.

"Vasquez."

The voice on the other end was old and dry and cracked like drought ground, and it said, without preamble:

"You the storm chaser? The one the Delgado boys keep on a string?" A cough, long and wet. "Good. I need a driver. And I need somebody who ain't scared of weather, 'cause the weather where I'm going don't wait for anybody."

Ines sat up in the dark, rain drumming, heart doing something complicated.

"Where are you going?"
