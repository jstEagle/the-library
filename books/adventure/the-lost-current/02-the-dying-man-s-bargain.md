## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The voice on the phone belonged to a man named Elias Crane, and he was camped — if you could call it that — forty miles east of her, on the shoulder of a road that wasn't on most maps.

"Forty miles," Ines repeated, looking at the sky, which had finally stopped trying to drown the plains and settled into a sullen gray drizzle. "In this?"

"In this," Crane agreed, and coughed again, that long wet sound like a boot coming out of deep mud. "You'll want to leave now, girl. I ain't got all day."

"You don't know that. You might have weeks."

"I know my own engine better than anybody," he said, with the flat certainty of a man who'd already looked under the hood. "Forty miles. Headlights on. There's a fork past the old grain elevator — take the left, even though it looks wrong. It's always been the right one."

He hung up before she could ask what he meant by *the weather where I'm going*, which was probably deliberate. Men who were dying tended to ration their sentences like water in a drought.

Ines sat in the truck cab for a full minute, watching rain crawl down the windshield, doing the arithmetic she always did: fuel, hours, risk, payoff. The arithmetic came back ugly. It usually did. But the Delgados' stones were still sitting in her chest, and forty miles east felt, for once, like a direction instead of an escape.

She turned the key.

* * *

The left fork past the grain elevator did look wrong. It looked like a rumor of a road, two ruts and a prayer, climbing into scrub country where the rain had turned the clay to something between grease and glue. Ines drove it with both hands and half her attention on the sky, because the clouds out here had organized themselves into something with opinions, and she'd spent enough years reading them to know when they were thinking about violence.

She found him just as the light went copper.

His camp was a tarp strung between his truck and a fence post that had given up on being straight decades ago. A bedroll underneath. A camp stove. A wooden crate serving as a table, and on the crate: a lantern, a tin cup, and a map.

Not a printed map. A hand-drawn one, ink on what looked like butcher paper, covered in a cramped, furious handwriting and elevation lines drawn freehand but drawn *right* — she could tell even from ten feet away that whoever made it had walked every contour himself.

The man under the tarp was seventy if he was a day, lean as fence wire, with a beard gone the color of dust and eyes that burned like coals someone kept forgetting to put out. He was propped against his truck tire, and he watched her climb out of her own truck with an expression she recognized, because she wore it herself sometimes in truck-stop mirrors: the look of a person assessing whether a stranger is a problem or a tool.

"Vasquez," he said. "You drive fast."

"You told me to hurry."

"I told you to come quick. Hurrying's different. Hurrying kills people." He waved at the crate. "Sit. I got coffee. It's terrible. Sit anyway."

She sat. The coffee was, indeed, terrible — burnt and bitter and somehow also weak, which she hadn't known was possible — and while she drank it Elias Crane studied her the way she studied cloud formations, patiently, looking for the thing underneath.

"The Delgado boys," he said finally. "They lend you money?"

The question landed like a dropped wrench. "How do you—"

"'Cause they lent me money too. Twenty years back." He smiled, and it was mostly teeth. "Cheerful fellas, ain't they? Always smiling. That's how you know. Anybody who smiles that much about money is collecting interest on something you can't see yet." He took the cup from her hands, drank, grimaced at his own coffee as if surprised by its badness. "I paid mine off. Took me nineteen years and everything I ever found. But I paid it. So I know exactly what those stones in your chest feel like."

Ines set the cup down very carefully. "What do you want, Mr. Crane?"

"What everybody wants. To not be forgotten." He said it lightly, but his hand, when he reached for the map, shook like a leaf in a gust. "I'm going to show you something, and then I'm going to ask you for one thing, and you're going to say no, and then I'm going to talk until you say yes. Fair warning."

"That's not fair warning, that's a hostage situation."

"Good. You're smart. Smart's rare out here." He unfolded the map.

* * *

It took him an hour to explain, and he explained it the way old men tell stories — in loops, in digressions, doubling back, losing the thread, finding it again somewhere unexpected. But the shape of it, eventually, was this:

There was a river in the mountains south of Puerto Cielo that went into the ground and didn't come out for twenty miles. Every map agreed on that much. What no map agreed on was where it came back out carrying gold.

"I've traced it four times," Crane said. His eyes had gone bright, feverish, lit from some furnace inside him that fifty years hadn't cooled. "Four expeditions. Lost two partners, one marriage, and every dollar I ever dug out of the ground. And the fourth time—" his voice dropped, went almost reverent, "—the fourth time I stood on the bank where it comes back up, and I panned that water, and Ines Vasquez, I am telling you, the pan came up *yellow*. Not flecks. Yellow."

"And you didn't dig?"

"I started to." Something crossed his face then, a shadow moving over the coal-fire eyes. "And then I found things in the dirt that weren't gold. Things that told me why nobody lives there anymore. Why the maps go quiet. Why the government records for that valley are—" he tapped his temple, "—all up here, and nowhere else."

"What kind of things?"

"Foundations. A church bell. A schoolhouse slate with a kid's name still chalked on it." He looked at her steadily. "A whole town, girl. Under that water, under that gravel. Nobody drowned it on purpose, near as I can tell, and nobody remembers it on paper. But the river does. The river never forgets anything. That's where your gold comes from. It's washing out of a graveyard."

Ines sat with that. Around them the drizzle had thickened, and somewhere north thunder muttered like an old man disagreeing with the radio.

"So don't dig," she said slowly. "Just... tell somebody."

"Tell who?" He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and the cough went on too long. When it stopped he was grayer. "Who listens to a prospector with a story about a town that isn't on any map? No. There's one person alive who has a right to that valley, and that's my daughter. Marisol. She lives in Puerto Cielo. Runs a hostel there, or a boarding house, or whatever failing business she's failing at this year." A pause. His voice roughened. "Her mother took her away when she was six. Told her I chose the mountains over them. Wasn't true. Wasn't not true, either. That's the trouble with leaving — everybody gets to write their own version."

He folded the map along its worn creases and held it out to her.

"One promise. You carry this to Marisol Crane, in Puerto Cielo, and you put it in her hands, and you tell her —" he stopped. Started again. "You tell her I was sorry. Not for the gold. For the years."

"Mr. Crane—"

"Elias."

"Elias. I'm a storm chaser. I chase weather across flat country for camera crews and insurance adjusters. I don't do mountains. I don't do errands. I especially don't do promises, because the last three people who held paper over my head taught me what paper costs." She stood up. "Find another driver."

"There is no other driver." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "There's me, dying on a fence line, and there's you, and there's forty miles of bad road behind you and a pair of smiling brothers ahead of you. You think I picked you random? I asked around. The Delgado boys keep a list of everybody who owes 'em, and everybody who might be useful, and you're on both pages." He pushed the map toward her again. "Take it. Because if you don't, the next person who finds me won't be delivering anything. They'll be *collecting*."

The wind picked up. The tarp snapped like a flag. And Ines Vasquez, who had spent ten years running toward storms because they were the only honest thing in her life — storms never pretended to be anything but what they were — looked down at the map in the dying man's hands and felt the exact moment her reluctance began hardening into something else.

"How far is Puerto Cielo?" she asked, which was not yes.

But they both knew it was.

* * *

He died a little after four in the morning.

She'd stayed — partly because the road was impassable in the dark, she told herself, and partly because a man alone on a fence line shouldn't die listening to nothing but wind. He talked most of the night, in fragments, drifting: about a wife named Rosa who made bread that could raise the dead; about a mule named General who hated him personally; about a daughter who used to draw maps of the backyard and bury "treasure" in coffee cans, and how he'd taught her contour lines before she could ride a bike.

"She was better at it than me," he murmured once, near the end. "Six years old. Better eye than me. Never told her." A long silence. "Never told her much of anything."

At some point before dawn the talking stopped, and the breathing changed, and Ines sat up from the passenger seat of his truck and knew, the way she knew a storm had turned, without anyone having to say it.

By first light she'd done what needed doing — the county sheriff's office was a two-hour drive; she called it in, gave the coordinates, answered the questions in a flat voice. The deputy who took the call said someone would come out. Ines folded Elias Crane's hands over the map, which he'd insisted on holding to the last, and stood in the gray morning feeling the strange weight of being the only person left on earth who knew what a dead man had known.

That was when she heard the engines.

Two vehicles, coming up the left fork past the grain elevator — the fork that looked wrong and was right. The first was a black SUV riding too clean for this road. The second was a pickup with chrome that caught the early sun like a grin.

Raul Delgado climbed out of the pickup smiling, the way he always smiled, and Ines understood with cold clarity that Elias Crane had been right about one more thing: the next person to find him would have been collecting.

"Well, hell," Raul said pleasantly, taking off his hat to the tarp, the bedroll, the stillness underneath it. "Old man beat us here after all." His smile widened as his eyes found hers, warm as a handshake, patient as interest accruing. "Morning, Ines. Funny thing — we've been looking everywhere for Mr. Crane."

Behind him, his brother Marco leaned against the SUV and smiled too.

"And now," Raul said, "it looks like you found him first."
