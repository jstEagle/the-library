## 3. Cheerful Creditors

The Delgado brothers arrived at the Broken Spoke Diner the way weather arrives on the high plains — you felt the pressure change long before anything visible happened, and by then it was too late to drive anywhere.

Ines saw them first in the window's reflection, which was a mercy, because it gave her time to finish her coffee and arrange her face into something that wasn't outright dread. Raúl came in first, as always, all six-foot-two of him folded into a cheerful wave. Emilio followed, smaller, quicker, already smiling at the waitress like they were old friends. They were wearing matching windbreakers. That was the thing about the Delgados that nobody warned you about: they dressed for the occasion. Every occasion. Today's occasion, apparently, was optimism.

"Ines!" Raúl boomed, sliding into the booth across from her without asking, because asking would imply she had a choice. "You look wonderful. Rested. Prosperous, even."

"I'm eating pie," Ines said. "Pie is not prosperity."

"Pie is *hope*," said Emilio, sitting beside his brother with the smoothness of a man who'd rehearsed this entrance in a mirror. He flagged down the waitress. "Three forks. And whatever she's having — she has good taste, I can always tell."

"You've never tasted anything I've ordered in my life."

"Which is why I trust it." He beamed.

The waitress brought the pie and three forks, and for a moment there was nothing but the small domestic sounds of a diner at mid-morning: the coffee pot ticking, a radio murmuring weather from somewhere near the ceiling, the grill hissing. Ines ate her pie. The brothers watched her eat her pie with the patient attention of men watching a horse they intended to buy.

"So," Raúl said finally, leaning forward, forearms on the table, windbreaker creaking. "We hear you've come into an opportunity."

"We hear lots of things," Emilio added. "It's our gift. People tell us things. We're very easy to talk to."

"That's true," Raúl said warmly. "People find us approachable."

Ines set down her fork. "Who told you?"

"A little bird," said Emilio.

"A dying little bird," said Raúl, and both of them smiled wider, and Ines understood that the map — Elias Crane's map, folded now inside her jacket against her ribs like a second heartbeat — was no longer a secret. She didn't ask how they knew. Asking how the Delgados knew things was like asking a river how it got downhill. It simply did, and arguing about it wasted breath.

"Here's the beautiful part," Emilio said, producing a folder from nowhere, glossy and thick, the kind of folder that cost more to print than Ines's last three tanks of gas combined. "We're not here to collect. Not today. Today we're here to *invest*."

"Partner," Raúl corrected gently. "We prefer partner. Investor sounds cold."

"Cold," Emilio agreed, shuddering theatrically. "We are not cold people, Ines. Look at us." He spread his hands, presenting himself and his brother like a matched set of kitchen appliances. "We are warm people. We believe in you. We have *always* believed in you. Even when your truck broke down outside Alvarado and you needed four thousand dollars by Friday — we believed. Did we ever once raise our voices?"

"No," Ines admitted. "You brought flan."

"We brought flan," Raúl confirmed, with real nostalgia. "And we'll bring flan again. Because here is what we think: you're going into those mountains after something valuable, and you need equipment, fuel, permits-adjacent assistance, and a person who knows how to handle situations. We provide all of that. You provide the map. Sixty-forty, our favor, and your debt —" he made a gesture like wiping crumbs off a table — "*gone*. Clean slate. Fresh start. Everybody happy."

"And if I say no?"

Emilio laughed. It was a lovely laugh, genuinely warm, and it frightened her more than any threat could have. "Then we'd be *sad*, Ines. And when we're sad, we get worried. And when we're worried, we start thinking about other ways to help you. And our other ways are never as pleasant as this way." He tapped the folder. "This is the pleasant way. This is the flan way."

Raúl slid the folder across the table. Inside were satellite printouts of the mountain range, a route marked in confident red ink, and a photograph of a helicopter. The helicopter had a ribbon on it, like a gift.

"Take tonight," Raúl said, standing, buttoning his windbreaker against a chill only he could feel. "Sleep on it. Dream about it. But dream *warmly*." He leaned down, and for one terrible moment Ines thought he might kiss her forehead. Instead he straightened her coffee cup so the handle faced her hand. "Little kindnesses," he explained. "That's who we are."

They left money for the whole pie, plus the waitress's tip, plus the tip of the man at the counter who hadn't spoken to anyone. The bell over the door chimed behind them, bright as a dinner bell.

Ines sat very still for a long time, looking at the folder, at the red route drawn through mountains she hadn't yet entered, at the ribboned helicopter. Then she took out her phone and called Bo.

* * *

Bo Okonkwo ran the only garage between the highway and the foothills, a cathedral of rusted parts where every vehicle went to be judged and most were found wanting. He was under a pickup when she arrived, just his boots sticking out, and he didn't come out until he'd finished whatever sacred business he was conducting with the transmission.

"You look terrible," he said, emerging, wiping his hands on a rag that had possibly once been a shirt.

"Thank you. I need the truck ready by dawn."

"Dawn." He looked at the sky, which was doing its evening performance, all amber and apology. "Dawn tomorrow?"

"Dawn tomorrow. Full tanks, spare everything, and don't file the trip plan with anybody."

Bo studied her the way he studied engines — patiently, listening for the knock. "Delgados?"

"How'd you know?"

"They bought pie at the Spoke this morning and tipped the cook forty dollars. Nobody tips the cook forty dollars unless they're softening up a mark." He tossed the rag into a bin. "I'm coming."

"You're not coming."

"I'm coming. Your front left shock is held together by prayer and my personal welding, and prayer doesn't do mountain roads. Also—" he held up a hand before she could argue— "you'll say no, and I'll follow you anyway, and then we'll have wasted a tank of gas. Bo knows roads, Ines. Bo also knows when a friend is about to do something stupid alone instead of stupid with company, and the second one has better odds."

Ines opened her mouth. Closed it. The map pressed warm against her ribs, or maybe that was just her heart doing overtime.

"Load the winch cable," she said finally. "And Bo? If two men in matching windbreakers ask you where I've gone—"

"I'll tell them you went north."

She stared at him.

"To throw them off," he said, wounded. "Obviously north. South is where the mountains are. Any idiot can see the mountains. I'd say north, they'd go north, they'd be angry in the wrong direction. It's called tactics."

"It's called lying badly."

"Lying *generously*," Bo said. "There's a difference. Now go home and sleep, because I'm not driving behind a woman who yawns on switchbacks."

* * *

She slept two hours, packed one bag, and stood for a long moment over the map before folding it into a waterproof sleeve and zipping it into her jacket. Elias Crane's handwriting crawled across it — elevations, a river's vanishing point, a single annotation in a shaking hand: *it comes back carrying what the mountain hides.* She had promised a dying man she'd deliver this to his daughter. She had not promised the Delgados anything yet, and she intended to keep it that way, at least until Puerto Cielo, at least until Marisol Crane had seen it first.

At 4:40 a.m., the streets of town were empty except for a dog with strong opinions and nowhere to be. Bo was already at the garage, loading the last of the gear, and by 5:15 they rolled out under a sky still deciding whether to exist.

"North?" Bo asked.

"South. Straight through. No stops."

"Tactics," Bo sighed, and turned south.

For ninety minutes the road belonged to them — frost steaming off fence wire, the truck's heater finding its voice, Bo narrating the mechanical history of every ranch gate they passed. Then, somewhere past the second ridge, Bo went quiet in a way that meant trouble, because Bo was never quiet unless his instruments or his instincts had flagged something.

"Don't turn around," he said.

"What is it?"

"Vehicle. Been holding us since the pass. Quarter mile back, gray SUV, rental plates, keeps its distance like it's being paid by the hour." He glanced at the mirror. "Not the Delgados. Delgados drive chrome. This is fleet vehicle energy. Government energy, or the thing companies rent when they want to pretend they're not a company."

Ines kept her eyes on the road and let the paranoia move through her, cold and specific. The Delgados she understood — they were weather, loud and predictable, all thunder and flan. But a quiet tail with rental plates was a different animal entirely. She thought of Halloran — no, she didn't know that name yet; what she thought of was the survey crew rumor Bo had mentioned at the diner, some outfit buying up mineral rights across three counties with paperwork nobody could produce twice.

"Take the next fork," she said. "The rough one."

"The rough one eats shocks."

"The rough one tells us if they're serious."

Bo grinned despite himself and took the fork. The truck bucked and complained. Behind them, without hesitation, without lights, the gray SUV turned too — smooth, unhurried, keeping its quarter mile like a debt keeping its interest.

"They're serious," Bo said.

"They're serious." Ines felt something shift in her chest — fear, yes, but fear settling into a shape, the way loose cargo settles once you hit the highway. Purpose. She had a map, a promise, a mechanic, and a head start measured in minutes. Ahead lay the range, the snows, the monsoon, a daughter who didn't know her father was dead, and a river that vanished underground carrying gold it had no business carrying.

Behind her, patient as arithmetic, the gray SUV followed.

"Bo," she said, "how do you feel about driving after dark?"

"I feel like we're going to anyway."

"Good answer. Step on it."
