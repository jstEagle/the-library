## 4. Marisol

The road into Puerto Cielo had been eating Bo's suspension for two hours before the town appeared, and when it did, Ines felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched.

It was a town built on a shelf of the mountain like a plate balanced by a careless waiter — flat-roofed houses the color of dried chiles, a church with a bell tower leaning just enough to be charming rather than alarming, and above it all the range going up and up into cloud. Water ran in stone channels along both sides of the street, quick and cold and loud, as if the whole place were whispering to itself.

"Hostel's at the end," Bo said, reading from the scrap where Elias Crane had scrawled his daughter's name. *Marisol — Casa del Río.* "Man gave you directions to a hostel instead of an address. That's either trust or shame."

"With Elias Crane it was probably both."

They found the Casa del Río by its sign, which hung from one chain and rotated slowly in the wind like something deciding whether to give up. The building behind it was long and low, whitewashed once upon a time, now weathered to the color of old bone. A garden fought a losing war against gravel out front. A hand-painted board listed rates that made Ines wince — not because they were high, but because they were so low they bordered on charity.

Inside smelled of woodsmoke and coffee and, faintly, of paint. A woman stood on a ladder in the common room, rolling white over a wall that had been some ambitious blue in a previous decade. She was perhaps forty, dark-haired, sleeves pushed up, a smear of paint across one cheekbone like a dueling scar.

"We're closed," she said without turning around. "Renovations."

"The sign says open."

"The sign is a liar." She came down the ladder, and Ines watched her take them in — Ines's dust-caked jacket, Bo's grease-stained hands, the particular way travelers who've driven too far stand, half-leaning on invisible furniture. "You want beds or food?"

"Neither." Ines held out the map case. "I have something for Marisol Crane. From her father."

The roller stopped mid-stroke.

For three full seconds the woman didn't move. Then she set the roller down with exaggerated care, the way you put down a thing you'd rather throw, and wiped her hands on a rag that was already beyond saving.

"I'm Marisol," she said. "And if this is about money he owed someone, I'll tell you the same thing I told the last two men. Whatever Elias Crane promised you, he wasn't authorized to promise it. He hasn't been authorized to promise anything since 1998."

* * *

*He wrote to me every year,* Marisol thought, watching the storm-chaser woman unroll the paper on her kitchen table. *One letter, every year, on my birthday. Eleven lines, always eleven, as if he'd counted them out and refused to go over budget.*

She'd stopped opening them four years ago. They sat in a shoebox under her bed, forty-one letters deep, a sediment core of a man's excuses.

The map on her table was not one of those letters. It was better than those letters, which was somehow worse. It was drawn in ink gone brown with age, on paper soft as cloth, and it was beautiful — river lines laid down with a draftsman's patience, elevations noted in a small careful hand, annotations in Spanish and English both. It was the work of years. Of decades, maybe.

Of a man who could do *this*, and couldn't manage twelve lines in December.

"And he gave this to a stranger," Marisol said. "A woman he met at a campsite. But not to me."

"He said you'd know why," Ines offered.

"Oh, I know why." She laughed, and heard how ugly it came out. "Because I would have told him what to do with it. Because I'm the one person whose face he'd have to watch while he explained himself. Strangers are easy. Strangers get the legend. Family gets the bill."

The tall one — Bo — shifted by the door, uncomfortable, the way men got when women argued in front of them, as though disagreement were weather they hadn't dressed for. Marisol almost liked him for it.

"There's a problem," Ines said slowly. "Elias mentioned a partner. Someone who might come looking."

"He found you first, then."

Ines went very still. "What?"

"A man came through in the spring." Marisol crossed to the dresser and took out the other map — the one she'd kept meaning to burn and never quite managed, because burning things was permanent and she'd spent her whole life leaving doors open. She laid it beside her father's. "Said his name was Reyes. Said he'd prospected with Elias for twenty years. Very polished. Very sorry about the estrangement — said it was Elias's stubbornness, not mine, that kept us apart. Which, credit where due, was true."

The two maps lay side by side. Same river. Same mountains. Different everything else.

Her father's map showed the river vanishing into the earth below a shoulder of gray rock and reappearing twenty miles north. The stranger's map showed the same disappearance — but the source of the gold was marked in the south, near a place called, in faded pencil, *Agua Perdida.*

"They don't agree," Ines said.

"No. And one of them is wrong, and one of them is bait." Marisol tapped the stranger's map. "This one came with a very generous offer. He wanted me to fund an expedition. Half up front. I told him I ran a hostel with four working radiators and he recalibrated his offer downward twice before I showed him the door."

"You didn't believe him?"

"I believed he believed himself." She looked at the storm-chaser — Vasquez, she'd said her name was, with eyes that kept flicking to the window like the sky owed her money. "That's the dangerous kind. Here's what I know about my father, Ms. Vasquez. He could find anything except his way home. Every scheme was going to fix us. Every season was the season. My mother waited eleven years for the season that paid off, and died waiting, and he sent flowers to the funeral with a note saying he'd make it up to us." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced level for thirty years. "So you'll forgive me if the inheritance I don't want is a piece of paper promising treasure from a man who never once delivered groceries."

* * *

Ines walked out into the street because the room had gotten too small for the conversation, and because she recognized the look on Marisol Crane's face. She'd seen it in her own mirror more mornings than she'd admit — the expression of someone doing arithmetic on a relationship and coming up short every time.

Bo fell into step beside her. "Well?"

"She's got the other half of the picture and no reason to share it with us."

"That's not what I asked."

Ines leaned on the low stone wall where the water channel rushed past, cold enough to ache if you touched it. "Elias picked me because I was nobody. Because I'd deliver the thing without asking questions, and he could die thinking he'd done right without ever having to hear the answer." She turned the map case over in her hands. "And I took it anyway. Because I need the reward, or the salvage rights, or whatever's attached, bad enough that I told myself delivering it was the same as earning it."

Bo said nothing. That was Bo's gift; he let silence do the heavy lifting.

"She thinks we're after the gold," Ines went on. "And the worst part is she's right. I keep telling myself there's a difference between needing money and wanting it, but the Delgados don't care about the difference, and neither does Halloran's survey crew, and neither will anyone else who comes up that mountain."

"So what's the play?"

Ines looked back at the hostel. Through the window she could see Marisol at the table, not moving, staring at the two maps like a woman reading her own autopsy.

"The play is she comes with us," Ines said. "Or nothing moves."

Getting her to agree took another hour, two cups of terrible coffee, and one argument that knocked a picture frame off the wall. What finally did it — Ines suspected — wasn't logic. It was the moment Ines said, quietly: "He died holding your name. Not the map's. Yours. Whatever else he lied about, I don't think that was one of them."

Marisol had looked away then, jaw tight, and started giving orders about supplies in a voice that pretended it wasn't surrendering.

* * *

*He died holding my name,* Marisol thought that night, lying awake in the good bed she always gave guests and never used herself. *Eleven lines a year, and a dying man's last word, and two maps that disagree, and a stranger in my kitchen who looks at mountains the way I look at ledgers.*

Below the window, the channel water ran loud and constant, the sound she'd fallen asleep to her whole life, the sound her mother had grown up with, somewhere else, in a valley Marisol had never been allowed to ask about.

She got up, took the stranger's map from the table, and held it over the stove.

The paper trembled in her hand. She thought of her mother's one rule, spoken only once, the night before she died: *If anyone ever asks you about Agua Perdida, you say you've never heard of it.*

In the morning, Tomás arrived at the hostel door with his mule, three days early, and a look on his face that meant the weather had turned against them.

"We leave today," he said, "or we don't leave at all."
