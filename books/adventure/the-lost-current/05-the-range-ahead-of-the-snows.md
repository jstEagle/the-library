## 5. The Range Ahead of the Snows

The mountains did not announce themselves. They simply arrived one morning beyond the windshield of Bo's truck, blue-gray and patient, the way a bill arrives — something you knew was coming and had hoped to outrun anyway.

Ines stood at the edge of the last gas station before the foothills, watching snowline flicker on the highest ridges like a signal only she could read. Her phone was open to the satellite loop she'd been refreshing since dawn. The cold front sat offshore, fat and white and wrong for the season, spinning in the wrong direction at the wrong time.

"Early," she said.

Bo emerged from the cab with two coffees and the expression of a man who had heard that word too many times this week. "Define early."

"Three weeks early. Maybe four." She took her coffee. "Which means we're not racing the Delgados anymore. We're racing winter, and winter doesn't negotiate."

Behind them, Marisol climbed out of the truck's back seat with Tomás already standing beside it, as if he'd never been sitting at all. He was a compact man somewhere between fifty and forever, with a face weathered into topography and a way of looking at Ines that suggested he'd already decided exactly how much of what she said he would believe. So far: roughly forty percent, and generous of him.

"The northern pass closes first," Tomás said. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in two days, so Ines gave it full weight.

"How long do we have?"

He studied the peaks the way she studied radar loops — reading texture, reading light. "If the front holds its course? Six days through the pass. Five if the wind turns."

"And if we're slow?"

Tomás looked at her then, fully, and Ines understood she'd asked the wrong question. There were no *ifs* in his grammar. Only *whens*.

* * *

They made the trailhead by noon, and Ines felt the old familiar click inside her chest — the sound of a plan becoming motion. It was the feeling she loved best in the world, better than any summit or storm or finish line: the moment when everything ahead of you is still possible and nothing behind you has caught up yet.

Bo loaded the last pack onto his shoulders and groaned theatrically. "I want it noted for the record that I am a mechanic. I fix things. I am not built for carrying things up mountains."

"Noted," Marisol said dryly, adjusting her own pack with an ease that surprised Ines. The hostel keeper's softness, it turned out, stopped at the tree line. On the trail she moved like someone who'd grown up in these hills — because, Ines remembered, she half had. Elias Crane had come down from the mountains often enough to leave a daughter who walked them in her sleep.

"You've done this before," Ines said, falling into step beside her.

"I grew up hearing about it." Marisol's voice went flat in a particular way Ines was learning to recognize — the sound of a woman handling her father's memory with tongs. "Every story he told me ended with a mountain. None of them ended with him staying."

Ines let that sit. There wasn't much else to do with it.

By mid-afternoon they were above the pines, on switchbacks cut into granite, and the world had narrowed to breath and bootfall and the enormous indifferent sky. Ines checked the satellite feed whenever she could steal a bar of signal. The front hadn't turned. It also hadn't slowed.

At dusk they camped in a hollow below a ridge, and Tomás built a fire small enough to hide from the valley below. Ines lay awake listening to the wind change pitch over the rocks, cataloguing it the way other people counted sheep. Southwest, steady, cold at the edges. Good news, technically. Bad news traveled from the north.

She was almost asleep when Bo whispered, "Ines. Headlights."

Far below, two points of light crawled along the road they'd left that morning. They stopped at the trailhead. They stayed stopped.

"Delgados," she said.

"Or Halloran."

"Halloran took the southern pass. Tomás said there's no other road." She watched the lights a while longer. Two vehicles now. The second one's beams swept the parking area in slow, methodical arcs, like a man searching a room. "That's the Delgados. And they brought friends."

Bo exhaled. "How many friends does cheerful buy?"

"More than we can afford." She rolled onto her back. "Sleep fast, Bo. Tomorrow we climb like it matters."

"It already matters."

"No," Ines said quietly, watching the stars wheel over the ridge. "*Now* it matters."

* * *

The third day, the mountain tried to kill them.

It happened the way these things always happen — not dramatically at first, but with a sound. A deep, grinding groan from the slope above the pass, like the earth clearing its throat before saying something unforgivable.

Tomás heard it first. He didn't shout. He simply stopped walking, held up one hand, and said, "Off the trail. Now." And because his forty percent had somehow become a hundred, they moved.

The rockslide came thirty seconds later.

It wasn't huge, by the standards of mountains. It didn't need to be. A few tons of granite sheared loose and came down across the switchbacks like a dropped wall, dust boiling out in a brown cloud that swallowed the trail, the sun, and the last fifty meters of their route. When the air cleared, the path ahead was gone. Not blocked — *gone*, buried under slabs the size of trucks, stacked at angles that mocked the idea of climbing.

Marisol coughed dust and stared. "Is there another way around?"

"There is a way," Tomás said. "Through the slide itself. Slowly."

"Or the southern pass," Bo offered, and immediately regretted it, because everyone knew what waited on the southern pass: Halloran, permits that didn't exist, and a man whose smile was the most expensive thing about him.

Tomás was already moving toward the rubble, testing stone with his boot, reading the pile the way Ines read clouds. "Some of these will hold. Some will not. The trick is knowing which."

"That's the whole trick?" Bo said weakly.

"That is always the whole trick."

They were twenty minutes into picking their way across — Ines third in line, hands on cold rock, heart doing arithmetic — when she heard voices from below.

Not echoes. Voices.

She edged to the lip of the slide and looked down. Four figures stood at the base of the rubble where the trail resumed: two she recognized instantly, because cheerfulness like theirs carried. Raúl and Emilio Delgado, dressed for the mountains the way men dress when someone else packed for them, flanked by two hired shoulders with necks like bridge cables.

"Ines!" Raúl called up, waving, genuinely delighted, which was the worst part. "What luck! What *fortune*! You see, the mountain wants us to work together!"

"The mountain just tried to bury us," Ines called back.

"All the more reason!" Emilio spread his arms, beaming. "Four of us cannot cross safely. Eight of us can! Rope, hands, shared risk — this is partnership, Ines. This is what we have been offering all along!"

Beside her, Bo muttered, "I hate that he's right."

"So do I," Ines said. "Hold that thought. It's all we have."

She looked at Marisol, who was watching the Delgados with an expression Ines couldn't fully read — anger, yes, but underneath it something older and sadder, the look of a woman recognizing her father's world arriving to claim her. Then she looked at Tomás, who gave a single shrug that meant, in his language, *the mountain does not care who pays your debts.*

"We cross together," Ines called down. "Our rope, our order, our pace. Anyone who argues climbs alone."

Raúl's smile widened like a door opening onto a room she didn't want to see. "Ines! You drive such a hard bargain. It's why we like you!"

* * *

The crossing took four hours and cost Ines every scrap of calm she owned.

The Delgado muscle turned out to be competent — silent, sure-footed men who moved rock like it owed them money — and that competence frightened her more than incompetence would have. Cheerful creditors were one thing. Cheerful creditors with skills were another entirely.

Twice the pile shifted under them, settling with sounds like distant artillery, and twice Tomás froze them all with a raised hand until the mountain finished its sentence. At the worst point, Marisol's footing gave way and one of the hired men — the younger one, with a scarred jaw — caught her arm without a word and held her against the rock until she found it again. Marisol thanked him. He looked briefly, enormously embarrassed, and said nothing.

When they finally stood on the far side, dusty and shaking and alive, the alliance dissolved the instant their boots touched clean trail. The Delgados' men stepped back. Raúl clapped his hands together like a man concluding a pleasant business lunch.

"Well! That was bonding, no?" He fell into step beside Ines, uninvited. "You lead, we follow. Everyone reaches the river. Everyone shares. Simple."

"Nothing about this is simple, Raúl."

"Everything is simple," he said warmly. "Money is simple. Mountains are simple. People only make them complicated." He glanced ahead, up the trail toward the high country, and for just a moment the cheerfulness thinned and Ines saw the thing underneath it — sharp, patient, and very tired of waiting. "The front is coming, Ines. Even we can read the sky. Let us not be foolish together."

He dropped back to his brother, and Ines walked on with her pulse loud in her ears.

Above them, the pass narrowed into a notch of gray stone. Above that, the peaks wore their new snow like a warning. And far to the south, invisible but perfectly clear in her mind's eye, Halloran's crew was climbing the gentle pass with good gear and forged papers, gaining whatever the southern route gained.

Six days, Tomás had said. Maybe five.

Ines checked the sky, checked her watch, and did the only math that mattered anymore.

"Tomás," she said. "How fast can we move if we stop being careful?"

He considered this with visible distaste. "Faster than is wise."

"Good," Ines said. "Let's be unwise."

Behind them, low on the flank of the mountain, a sound rolled down the valley — long, metallic, wrong. Bo stopped dead. "Tell me that was thunder."

It wasn't thunder. It came again, rhythmic and deliberate, echoing off the walls of the pass: the unmistakable report of machinery. Of drilling.

From the southern pass — the one Halloran's crew was supposed to be three days away from — the mountain was already being opened.
