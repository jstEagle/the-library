## 6. What the River Remembers

Marisol had learned to read Tomás the way other people read weather. A certain set to his shoulders meant rain. A particular silence, deeper than his usual silence — which was already oceanic — meant something worse than rain.

Tonight it was the worse silence.

They'd made camp in a fold of the mountain where a spring-fed creek chattered over stones, and Bo had produced, from some impossible pocket of his person, a bag of dried mango and half a bottle of something amber that he claimed was "medicinal." Ines was sketching tomorrow's route in her notebook by headlamp. And Tomás sat apart from the fire, not quite outside its light but not quite in it, turning his cup slowly in his hands like he was waiting for it to tell him something.

Marisol went and sat beside him. She didn't ask. Asking was how you lost him for three days.

For a long while neither of them said anything, which was their native language, and then Tomás looked at the creek and said, quietly, in Spanish, "You know why they call this range the Ribs?"

"I assumed someone thought it looked like ribs."

"Everyone assumes that." He almost smiled. "It's because of what's under them. Hollow places. Caves, old mines, and—" he paused, and Marisol felt the pause like a hand on her arm— "one valley that isn't on any map anymore."

The fire popped. Ines glanced over but had the good manners, or the good instincts, to keep drawing lines on her page.

"Agua Perdida," Marisol said.

Tomás turned his cup again. "You know the name."

"It was on the back of my father's map. Tiny letters, like an afterthought. *A. Perdida.* I thought it was a joke. Lost Water."

"It is not a joke," Tomás said. "It is the opposite of a joke."

* * *

He told it slowly, the way old men tell things they've carried too long — not because they want to put them down but because their arms are tired.

Fifty years ago, he said, there had been a town in that valley. Not a famous town. A working one. Two hundred families, maybe more, a church with a bell cast in Puebla, orchards, a school where his own mother had taught arithmetic before she married and moved down-mountain. The river ran through it bright and shallow, and every spring it ran gold — literally gold, flakes of it catching the sun, and the townspeople panned enough each year to buy shoes and roof tin and, once, gloriously, a piano.

"Then the engineers came," Tomás said. "Not many. Quiet men with quiet papers. They said the valley would be flooded for a hydroelectric project. There were meetings. There were signatures. My grandfather signed one, God forgive him, because the alternative was men with rifles explaining the alternative."

"But the dam was never built," Marisol said. "I'd have heard of it."

"The dam was built," Tomás said. "That is what no one understands. It was built small, and badly, and by a company that folded before the paperwork reached any capital. The reservoir filled anyway — water does not wait for bureaucracy. The valley drowned in eleven days. The bell is still down there. Some say you can hear it when the water is low and the wind is right." He shrugged, as if embarrassed by his own people's stories. "The company died. The records burned, or were burned. The government of that decade preferred to forget, and so officially Agua Perdida never existed. Officially, no one was moved. Officially, no one drowned."

"Did anyone drown?"

Tomás was quiet long enough that the creek got loud. "Three who refused to leave. An old woman, a drunk man, and a boy who went back for a dog." He drank. "The survivors were scattered north and south with a little money and a great deal of advice about keeping quiet. Most of them kept it. What else do you do? You are poor, and the thing that took your town cannot even be sued, because it never happened."

Marisol found she was gripping her own knees. "And the gold."

"And the gold." Tomás nodded toward the dark, toward the range beyond it, toward the river they hadn't reached yet. "Your father's river disappears into the mountain and comes out twenty miles later carrying dust. Everyone who has ever chased it has assumed there is a vein somewhere inside, some secret mother lode bleeding into the current. But I have walked this range my whole life, and I will tell you what I believe." He set the cup down. "There is no vein. The river runs through the drowned valley. It runs through the streets of Agua Perdida itself. And every flood season, it scours a little more of that town away — the tailings piles, the sluice boxes, the pans and shovels left behind, the ground itself, which was always faintly golden. That is your lost current. It is not a treasure. It is a graveyard, washing downhill."

No one spoke. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Ines had stopped drawing. Her pencil hovered above the notebook, and Marisol watched her look at her own route lines as if they'd rearranged themselves into something uglier.

"So Crane knew," Ines said at last. "Elias knew this."

"He knew," Tomás said. "Or he suspected, and spent forty years refusing to suspect. Men can do that. Build a whole cathedral out of one stone they refuse to look at."

* * *

Later, when Bo had gone to check the tie-downs and Ines had rolled into her bag pretending — unconvincingly — to sleep, Marisol stayed up. She did it deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to learn its shape.

Her mother had been born in 1958. Marisol did the arithmetic twice, sitting by the coals, and both times it came out the same: her mother had been four years old when the valley flooded. Four years old. Old enough to remember a church bell. Old enough to have been carried out of a drowning town on someone's hip, and young enough that no one had ever told her what she was remembering, so she'd grown up thinking it was a dream she had, a recurring dream about water rising in a street, about a piano floating, about a dog barking on a roof.

Marisol had heard about that dream her whole childhood. Her mother described it the way you describe weather in another country — distant, puzzling, slightly embarrassing. *"Isn't that strange? Always the same one."*

And Elias Crane had known. He must have known. He'd married a woman from the scattered families — or lived with her, or loved her; the marriage was one of the foggy regions of his legend — and he had known where her recurring dream came from, and he had said nothing. Worse: he had spent decades chasing the gold that leaked out of her drowned hometown, walking the banks of the river that was erasing it flake by flake, calling it his *prospect*, his *future*, his *great chance*. He had turned her mother's grief into a lottery ticket.

Marisol stood up too fast and had to stand there until the world steadied.

She wanted to scream at him, which was difficult, since he was dead. She settled for kicking a pinecone with tremendous violence, which helped less than advertised.

"You're awake," Ines said from her bag. Not moving. Just saying it, into the dark, in the flat tone of a woman who had spent years sleeping in cars near strangers and had learned exactly how much attention to pay.

"No, I'm not."

"Fair." A pause. Then, carefully: "Your mom. Was she from there?"

Marisol looked at her. The firelight caught the edge of Ines's face — the part of her that was always calculating, always measuring distance and time and odds — and beneath it, unexpectedly, something that looked almost like gentleness, worn thin from use.

"She was four," Marisol said. "When it flooded. She dreamed about it her whole life and thought it was just a dream." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "My father drew that map. He wrote *A. Perdida* on the back in little letters like a footnote. Like the town was a landmark. Like it wasn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Like two hundred families were a scenic feature."

Ines sat up slowly, elbows on knees. For once she didn't have a plan, and Marisol could see her deciding whether to fake one.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," Ines said finally. "If Tomás is right, there's no fortune up there. There's a valley full of dead houses and a river stripping them bare. Which means everyone racing us — the Delgados, Halloran, all of it — they're racing toward nothing. Or worse than nothing."

"Worse," Marisol said. "They're racing toward a slope that's been sitting underwater-adjacent for fifty years, and nobody's checked on it lately."

Ines was quiet. Marisol watched the exact moment the storm-chaser brain kicked in — saw it arrive behind her eyes like weather.

"That's actually what scares me," Ines said. "Not the nothing. The *geology*. A drowned valley, saturated ground, monsoon season coming early..." She shook her head. "Tomorrow we move faster."

"We should turn around," Marisol said. "Deliver nothing. Bury the whole stupid map."

But even as she said it, she heard the lie in her own voice, and she could hear Ines hearing it. Because if there was no gold — if the whole cursed inheritance was a fiction built on a drowned town — then the only thing left worth delivering was the truth. And the truth was uphill.

"Your father," Ines said quietly, "knew all this. And he still sent you the map."

"He sent me a treasure hunt," Marisol said. "He sent me his life's work wrapped in a bow. He never once sent me an apology."

The coals ticked. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the trees with a sound like water rising in a street.

"Maybe," Ines said, "the apology is at the source."

Marisol didn't answer. But she lay back down facing the mountain instead of away from it, and she slept badly, and she dreamed — for the first time in her life — her mother's dream. Water in a street. A piano, turning slowly. A dog barking on a roof that was already an island.

She woke before dawn with the word *monsoon* stuck in her chest like a fishhook, and found Ines already up, staring at the sky in the east, where the stars over the lowlands had gone out one cloud at a time.

"Ines," Marisol said. "What's that."

Ines didn't turn around. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of a woman reading instruments only she could see.

"That," she said, "is the season arriving six weeks early."
