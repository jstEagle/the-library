## 7. Halloran's Offer

The high camp sat in a bowl of stone below the last stand of pines, where the trail bent west toward the pass and the air had gone thin enough to make even Bo's jokes wheeze a little on the way out. They'd made good time — better than Ines had dared hope — and for one evening it almost felt like an adventure instead of a debt with scenery.

Tomás built the fire small and neat, the way he did everything. Marisol heated water without being asked. Bo lay flat on his back reading the sky like a menu.

"Front coming," he said. "Two days, maybe three. That cloud there's got ambitions."

"Everything has ambitions," Marisol said. "That's the problem with this mountain."

Ines was checking her gear by headlamp when she heard the vehicle. Not on the trail — that was impossible; the trail had ended six miles back — but somewhere below, engine noise climbing through the trees like a rumor. She went still, hand on the tent pole, and listened the way she'd learned to listen to storms: not for what was there, but for what was about to be.

Headlights swung through the trunks. A white SUV, absurdly clean, rolled into their camp as if it had been invited, and stopped with the precision of a man who parallel-parked his conscience every night.

Halloran stepped out in a fleece jacket that probably cost more than Ines's truck.

"Well," said Bo, sitting up. "He found us. On a mountain. With no road."

"I'm resourceful," Halloran said pleasantly. "Mr. Okafor, Ms. Vasquez. Señorita Crane. Tomás." He nodded to each of them individually, which somehow made it worse. "I hope you don't mind. I've been wanting to have a civilized conversation before this stops being possible."

"Civilized," Marisol repeated. "From a man driving a car up a goat path."

"The car is rented." He smiled like this settled something. "May I sit? I brought coffee. Real coffee. I noticed you've been drinking whatever's in that bag of yours, Ms. Vasquez, and frankly it concerned me."

* * *

Here was the thing about temptation: it never arrived looking like temptation. It arrived looking like relief.

Halloran spread a tablet on a camp table and showed them satellite imagery of the range, the river, the twenty-mile gap where it vanished underground. He spoke softly, so the mountain wouldn't overhear. He knew things about the lost current that Ines hadn't told anyone — the flow rates, the silt color, the old survey markers from fifty years ago that no official record acknowledged.

"You've been chasing weather your whole life," he said to Ines, over the coffee, which was, infuriatingly, excellent. "You know how systems work. Pressure builds, pressure releases. This valley is a system too. There's something down there feeding that river gold, and my employers want to know what. You sell me the map — Crane's map, and the daughter's copy if she's feeling generous — and you walk away with enough money to never take another call from the Delgado brothers again."

Ines felt the number land in her chest like a dropped anchor. She thought of the spreadsheet she kept in her head at 3 a.m., the interest compounding like a storm cell, the cheerful voicemails — *Ines! Just checking in, amigo!* — that were somehow more terrifying than shouting would have been. One signature. One map. Debt erased. Truck paid off. Maybe even a new truck, with tires that didn't sing hymns at highway speed.

"That's a lot of zeros," she said carefully.

"It's a fair price for paper."

"It's not the paper I'm selling."

"No," Halloran agreed, and his eyes were steady and reasonable and utterly without warmth. "It's Elias Crane's life work. And possibly his daughter's inheritance. But inheritances are heavy, Ms. Vasquez. I'm offering to help carry them."

Marisol stood up abruptly and walked to the edge of the firelight. Tomás followed her with his eyes but didn't move, which Ines was learning was his way of saying everything.

Bo leaned close to Ines and lowered his voice. "Ask him about permits."

"What?"

"His crew's been running surveys all summer. Ask him what permits they're flying under." Bo wasn't whispering to be dramatic. He was whispering because he'd gone very serious, and Bo serious was rarer than snow in July. "I talked to a ranger at the last town. Twice. Nobody's got a filing for a corporate survey in this range. Not state, not federal, nothing. His paperwork doesn't exist, Ines. Which means either he's lying about who sent him, or whoever sent him knows exactly what they're doing is illegal."

Ines looked across the fire. Halloran was watching her with patient, courteous attention, the way a banker watches a signature line dry.

"Your permits," she said. "What agency issued them?"

Something flickered behind the pleasantness — half a heartbeat, no more. "A mineral exploration license. Regional office."

"Which regional office?"

"You can verify everything after we conclude our business."

"That's a no," Bo said cheerfully. "That's a whole no with a bow on it."

Halloran laughed, genuinely, and Ines hated how likable he managed to be while being, apparently, a fraud. "You're a careful woman, Ms. Vasquez. I respect that. The offer stands until morning. Sleep on it. Rich people sleep better, but poor decisions sleep fine too." He gathered his tablet, left the rest of the coffee as a parting gift — a bribe in beverage form — and drove back down into the dark, headlights strobing between the trees like a lighthouse with somewhere else to be.

* * *

They argued for an hour after he left, which meant Marisol argued, Bo argued, Tomás said four sentences that outweighed everyone else's forty, and Ines mostly listened to the war inside herself.

"He's offering us a way out," Bo admitted. "And I hate that part of me wants it. But Ines — if his permits are fake, then whatever's down that river is something somebody wants badly enough to break laws for. And when people want something that badly, the people holding the map stop being partners and start being loose ends."

Marisol came back to the fire with wet eyes she pretended were cold-wind eyes. "My father lied to me my whole life," she said. "Every story, every postcard, every promise to come home. If I sell his map, I'm agreeing that the only true thing he ever gave me was its price." She looked at Ines. "You promised him. I heard you say it, that first day, when you thought I couldn't hear. You promised to deliver it. Not to appraise it."

"I remember what I promised," Ines said quietly.

"Do you?" Marisol's voice cracked, anger and grief tangled together like fishing line. "Because everyone who ever loved that man remembers what he promised, and look how that went."

Tomás added wood to the fire and said, in his way, "The mountain decides who arrives. Not the money."

Which was either profound or a comment on the trail conditions, and with Tomás it was usually both.

Ines lay awake in her tent doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. The debt. The interest. The Delgados' voices, bright as slot machines. Against that: a dead man's hand-drawn map, a stranger's grief, a mechanic who'd left his shop with a duffel bag and unconditional loyalty, and a word she'd given a dying man because at the time it had cost nothing.

Promises were cheap until they weren't. That was the whole trick of them.

She fell asleep deciding — actually deciding, she thought — and dreamed of numbers turning into rivers.

* * *

She woke at 3 a.m., which was professional habit, to a sound that was not wind.

Not footsteps, exactly. Fabric. The particular soft drag of a zipper opened slowly by someone who understood zippers the way she understood storms. She lay motionless in her sleeping bag and listened to someone go through her pack outside the tent — methodical, unhurried, checking pockets she herself had forgotten she had.

They found the map tube. She heard the cap come off, heard paper breathe in the night air, heard it go back. Then the main compartment, the side pouches, the false bottom under the tire gauge where she kept cash — that got a long, thoughtful pause, and she felt oddly insulted that they took none of it.

Then the sounds moved toward Bo's tent, and Ines moved too.

She came out of her tent low and fast, the way she'd practiced a thousand times for storm intercepts, and caught a shape crouched at Bo's gear — slim, gloved, efficient. It froze. She froze. For one absurd moment they regarded each other across the fire pit like two animals surprised at the same watering hole.

"Evening," Ines said.

The shape bolted. She got a handful of sleeve — expensive fabric, technical weave — and then nothing but empty air and the sound of boots finding the trail downhill at a pace that suggested practice. No vehicle started. Whoever it was simply dissolved into the mountain, which was worse.

Bo emerged rubbing his eyes. "Was that—"

"Somebody going through our stuff."

"Anything taken?"

"No. Everything put back wrong, though. They wanted to know what we have, not to have it." Ines knelt at her own pack, re-zipping what had been re-zipped. Her hands were steadier than she expected. "They were cataloguing us."

Marisol stood at the edge of the firelight in her coat, pale. "Halloran's people?"

"Halloran makes offers," Ines said. "This felt different. Quieter." She looked up the trail, north, where the pass waited, and then south, where the darkness had swallowed the visitor. Two directions, two kinds of danger, and somewhere behind them both the Delgados grinding uphill with hired muscle and cheerful voices.

Tomás appeared beside her without having seemed to arrive. He studied the disturbed ground, the boot prints heading south, and said only: "Tomorrow we climb early. Before light."

Ines looked at the map tube in her hands — the dead man's careful ink, the river that vanished and returned, the fortune everyone was suddenly willing to cheat each other for. She'd held it for weeks thinking it was worth money.

Standing in the cold with her camp turned over and her friends' faces lit by dying coals, she understood for the first time that it might be worth something else entirely. Worth blood, maybe. Worth more than any of them had bargained for.

"Pack everything," she said. "We leave now."

Above the pass, unseen, the first clouds of the early front were already sliding over the ridge like a hand closing.
