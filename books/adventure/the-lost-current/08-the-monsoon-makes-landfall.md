## 8. The Monsoon Makes Landfall

The rain came in the night like an uninvited guest who'd been waiting in the truck.

Ines woke to a sound she knew from a hundred storm seasons — not the polite patter of mountain drizzle but the flat, drumming insistence of real water arriving all at once. She was out of her tent before her boots were laced, standing in the dark with her face tipped up, letting it hit her, and what she felt wasn't dread. It was recognition. Somewhere over the coast, three hundred miles away and weeks ahead of schedule, the monsoon had made landfall, and everything below six thousand feet was about to become a rumor.

"Bo," she called into the dark. "Bo, wake up."

"I'm awake." His voice came from inside his sleeping bag, muffled and deeply unconvinced. "I'm having a dream where it's dry."

"It's here. The monsoon. It's early."

A long pause. Then the zipper, and Bo's head emerging into the rain like a skeptical turtle. He let it fall on his face for a moment, considering. "Huh," he said finally. "Well. I did say we should've left Tuesday."

"You said we should've left Tuesday *last* Tuesday."

"And haven't I been right this whole time?" He crawled out and began striking the camp with the unhurried competence of a man who had accepted his circumstances somewhere around the second week of the expedition. "Tomás will want to move to the high route before the lower passes go under. Marisol will want to argue with him about it. You'll want to drive too fast. It's a good system. Everybody gets to be themselves."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. Because underneath the familiar rhythm of it — Bo grumbling, Tomás silent, Marisol stubborn as a fence post — there was the other thing, the thing that had been following them up the mountain for days now: two sets of headlights behind them, and neither one friendly.

The Delgados had hired muscle in San Rafaelito. Four men and a white pickup with a winch, cheerful as a birthday party. And Halloran's survey crew had taken the southern pass with their satellite phones and their permits that didn't exist, which meant everyone in this valley wanted the same river, and the river was running out of patience faster than any of them.

"We beat them to the bridge," Ines said, folding her tent with hands that wouldn't quite stay steady. "We cross first, we cut the far anchors if we have to—"

"If we have to," Bo repeated. "Sure. And when do we decide we have to?"

"When they're close enough that we can see their faces."

"That's a terrible plan, Ines."

"I know," she said. "Get the truck."

* * *

They reached the rope bridge at midmorning, in rain so heavy the gorge itself seemed to be exhaling. The bridge was older than any of them — two cables gone gray and furred with moss, planks spaced like bad teeth, swaying over a drop that the fog filled in helpfully, so you couldn't see the bottom, only imagine it, which was worse. The river below had already turned the color of coffee with too much cream, hauling whole trees along like matchsticks.

Tomás crossed first with the packs, moving with the flat economy of a man who had done this since childhood and saw no reason to dramatize it. Then Marisol, white-knuckled, eyes fixed on nothing, talking to herself in Spanish too fast for Ines to follow. Then Bo, halfway across, when the white pickup came around the bend.

"Ines!" Marisol's voice cracked across the gorge. "Truck!"

Ines turned. Raul Delgado stepped out of the passenger side into the rain, and even at forty yards, even soaked through, he was smiling. That was the thing about the Delgados that had frightened her from the beginning — not the muscle, not the money, but the smile, the genuine warmth of it, the way Raul looked at you like you were the best news he'd had all day while his brother calculated what you were worth.

"There she is!" Raul called, arms wide, embracing the weather. "Ines! My friend! What a morning, huh? The river is high, the road is gone — God is telling us something!"

"He's telling you to turn around!" she shouted back.

Raul laughed, delighted. Behind him, the four hired men got out of the truck bed, and they weren't laughing. One of them carried a coil of new rope and a machete, and Ines understood with a cold, settling clarity exactly how this was going to go. They would cross behind them. All of them. And on the far side there would be a conversation, and the conversation would end with someone else holding the map.

Bo was still mid-bridge, sixty feet out over the void, and he'd stopped. He looked back at Ines, then at the Delgados' men, then at her again, and Ines watched him do the arithmetic — the same slow, decent arithmetic he always did — and she saw him arrive at the answer before she could shout it.

"No," she said. "Bo, no—"

He grinned at her, that big easy grin, and cut the near anchor line.

Not the far one. The near one. The cable sang, whipped, and the whole bridge swung out over the gorge like a pendulum, and Bo rode it — actually rode it, legs pumping, hand over hand toward the far side while the structure twisted beneath him — and Ines understood: he hadn't cut it to drop the bridge. He'd cut it to swing himself clear, to get to the far bank before the Delgados' men could start across, to hold the landing.

It almost worked.

It worked until the third plank from the far side, rotted through by fifty years of monsoons, gave way under his left boot. Ines heard the crack even over the rain. Bo dropped, caught the cable with both hands, and hung there over the brown roaring water, swinging, and the bridge began — slowly, groaningly, plank by plank surrendering — to come apart.

What happened next happened fast and happened badly. Two of the hired men started onto the remains of the bridge anyway, because they were paid to, because Raul was shouting, because nobody in this entire expedition had ever once done the sensible thing. The added weight finished what the rot had begun. The far cable parted at the anchorage with a sound like a rifle shot, and the bridge folded, and Bo fell — twelve feet, fifteen — onto the talus slope at the river's edge, rolling, stopping against a boulder, and did not get up.

Ines was down the switchback trail before she remembered deciding to be. She went past Marisol, past Tomás, past reason, sliding the last twenty feet on her heels, and knelt in the mud beside him with the rain hammering her back.

"Hey. Hey, hey, hey. Look at me."

Bo's face was gray under the tan. His right leg was bent wrong below the knee, and his breath came in short, careful sips, the way people breathe when breathing has become negotiable. But his eyes found hers, and he smiled again, weaker now.

"Told you," he said. "Should've left Tuesday."

"Shut up. Shut up, you idiot, you beautiful idiot." Her hands were moving without her — jacket off, rolled, under the knee; splint from the pack Tomás dropped beside her without a word. "Can you move your toes?"

He wiggled them. Relief hit her so hard she nearly sat down in the river.

Above them, on the rim, the Delgado brothers stood at the broken stump of the bridge, looking down at the gap where their claim used to be. There was no crossing now. Not today, not tomorrow, maybe not for a week. The monsoon owned the gorge. And whatever else the Delgados were — cheerful, ruthless, patient as interest compounding — they could not fly.

Raul called down something. Ines didn't hear it over the water. She didn't need to. It would have been warm. It would have been generous. It would have been about next time.

She turned back to Bo, and to the map burning a hole in her jacket, and to the source waiting upstream — and she made herself stop shaking.

"Tomás," she said. "How far to the source from here?"

He considered the rain, the river, the ruined leg, the season arriving all at once. "Two days," he said. "One, if the weather is kind." He looked up at the sky, which was doing its level worst. "It will not be kind."

* * *

They left Bo at the old shepherd's hut above the gorge with Marisol — over her furious objection, over his mild one — splinted, dosed with the good painkillers from the kit, propped by the stove with a rifle across his lap and instructions to shoot anyone cheerful. Tomás and Ines went on alone, up the narrowing valley, into the teeth of it.

They climbed through the night and most of the next day, and the whole time the mountain talked to her. That was the thing Ines had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't chased storms: weather wasn't noise. It was speech. The barometric pressure dropping was a sentence. The smell of the rain changing — mineral, metallic, the smell of water that had touched deep rock — was a paragraph. And the sound coming off the slopes above them now, a low subterranean mutter beneath the rain, like the mountain clearing its throat, was a warning delivered in the politest possible terms: *I am full. Do not test me.*

Near dusk on the second day, wet to the soul and half deafened by the roar of the swollen tributaries, they came around a shoulder of granite and Ines stopped walking.

There it was. The lost current. The river Elias Crane had drawn in faded ink, the river that vanished underground for twenty miles and came back carrying gold.

Except it wasn't gold.

The river emerged from the base of the slope in a churning spring, and in its shallows, yes, there was brightness — flecks and glints catching the last gray light, enough to make a dying prospector's heart sing and five grown adults chase each other across a mountain range. Ines crouched at the edge and scooped a handful, and the glitter settled in her palm, and she rubbed it between her fingers, and it flaked. It crumbled. Mica. Pyrite. Fool's gold, and not even good fool's gold — just the mountain shedding its skin.

But that wasn't the horror either. The horror was what she saw when she stood up and looked at the slope the spring poured out of.

Fifty years ago, they said, a town had drowned here. Agua Perdida. A dam that officially never existed, a valley officially never flooded, survivors officially never scattered. And now Ines stood at the throat of that drowned valley and read it the way she read a supercell — the saturated hillsides sagging under their own weight, the seeps bleeding from every fold of ground, the old dam's bones still buried in the gorge downstream, holding back fifty years of silt and water and forgetting. Above the spring, the slope had begun to creep. She could see it — a scar of raw earth opening like a wound, trees leaning at angles trees don't lean, the ground ticking softly as it moved.

When the rains peaked — three days, maybe four — that slope was going to let go. And when it did, it would take the buried dam with it, and everything the valley had swallowed — the town, the secrets, the silence — would come down the gorge at the speed of a freight train, through Puerto Cielo, through the flood zone where a herder family was living right now, tonight, in the old drowned fields because the grass was good and no one had told them why it was free.

Tomás stood beside her, and she heard him breathe out slowly, once, the longest sentence she'd ever gotten from him.

"The gold," Ines said. Her voice sounded strange to her, small under all that water. "There never was any gold."

"No," Tomás said. "There was never any gold."

And the mountain, overhead, muttered — and shifted — and Ines was already turning downhill, already running, because she had spent her whole life learning to outrun storms, and this one had a head start.

Behind her, the spring ran bright and glittering, giving up its beautiful lie to anyone who cared to look.
