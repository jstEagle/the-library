## 9. The Weight of Water

Ines had spent eleven years learning to read the sky, and in all that time she had never once been wrong about the sound a landscape makes when it decides to die.

It wasn't thunder. Thunder came from above, rolled and grand, all theater. This was lower — a groan from inside the mountain itself, deep in its wet bones, like an old house settling except the house was four thousand tons of saturated hillside and the settling was going to be permanent. She stood at the lip of the high valley with rain hammering her hood and felt the sound come up through her boots, through her knees, into the place where instinct lived, and every alarm she owned went off at once.

The slope above Agua Perdida was moving. Not sliding yet. *Breathing.* The whole face of it bulged and eased, bulged and eased, like something enormous inhaling before it spoke.

She had maybe two hours. Maybe less. Maybe forty minutes if the rain kept up at this rate.

"Bo," she said into the radio, and her voice came out flat and calm, which surprised her, because underneath the calm was a scream with a head start. "Get down to the lower camp. Now. Tell Tomás we're evacuating everyone out of the flood zone. Everyone. I don't care what they say about their goats."

Static, then Bo's voice, wind-shredded: "The *flood zone*? Ines, there's nothing down there but—"

"There's a family," she said. "Herders. Tomás knows them. And there's fifty years of water sitting behind a slope that just started to fail. Move."

* * *

She ran the ridge line back toward the lower camp the way she used to run ahead of hail cores on the plains — not fast, exactly, but *efficient*, every step chosen, because a twisted ankle here was a death sentence with paperwork. The rain came sideways off the peaks. Below her, the old drowned valley lay green and innocent in the gray light, the river running bright over stones that had been someone's church foundation once, and she thought, with a bitterness that tasted like copper: *they named it well. Agua Perdida. Lost Water. Nobody ever asked where the town went.*

Tomás met her at the tree line, and she didn't have to explain. He'd heard the mountain too. His face did something complicated — grief and fury and a terrible kind of recognition — and then it did nothing at all, which was how she'd learned to know he meant business.

"The Quispe family," he said. "Three generations. They summer their herd in the valley floor because the grass is sweetest where the river floods." He said the last word like he was spitting out a stone. "They won't want to go."

"They'll want to go," Ines said, "when you tell them. You're the one they'll believe."

"And you?"

"I'm going to do what I do." She pulled her pack tight. "I'm going to watch the mountain and tell them how long they have."

* * *

The Quispes' camp sat on a bench of land above the river bend — two canvas tents, a corral, a cookfire drowning steadily under the rain. An old woman was pulling her grandchildren's things together already; mothers know. But the grandfather, Aurelio, stood in front of his animals with his arms crossed and the particular stubbornness of a man who has survived everything so far by refusing to be hurried.

"The herd can't travel in this," he said. "The trail washes out. We lose everything."

"You lose everything either way," Ines said, "if you stay. Sir — Aurelio — I chase storms for a living. Eleven years. I've watched houses disappear in ninety seconds. That slope up there—" she pointed, and made herself keep her voice level, teacher-calm, the voice she used with panicky spotters — "that slope is holding back more water than your river has carried in fifty years. When it lets go, it will not be a flood. It will be a wall. It will move faster than any animal you own."

He looked at the mountain. The mountain groaned again, louder this time, close enough now that even the children stopped moving.

Marisol appeared out of the rain at that moment, soaked through, hair plastered flat, and Ines saw her take in the scene — the old man, the tents, the corral, the children — and make a decision with her whole body. She walked straight past the argument and picked up the smallest child.

"We're leaving," Marisol said, in Spanish, gently, to the old woman. "All of us. My mother was born down in that valley, under that water. I'm not letting this one take anyone else." Then, to Aurelio, without softness: "You can drive the herd up the east bench yourself or you can watch us carry your grandchildren out and explain later why you stayed. Choose fast."

Ines could have kissed her. Instead she checked her watch and turned to the mountain, because the mountain was the only thing that mattered now, and reading it was the only useful thing she knew how to do.

Here is what eleven years of storm-chasing teaches you, though nobody pays you for it: disasters are not sudden. They are patient. A supercell doesn't drop a tornado the moment it looks angry; it organizes, it tightens, it tells you in rotation and inflow and the color of the light exactly when it means to act. Mountains were no different. The slope above the valley was telling her everything. The bulge was still breathing — good, that meant structure held. But the seeps had changed. That morning there'd been three springs trickling clear water out of the toe of the slide. Now there were seven, and they ran brown, thick as milk coffee, carrying clay from deep inside the failure plane. Brown water meant the slope was eating itself. Clear water meant time. Brown water meant the clock had started.

She counted seeps. She timed the groans — nine minutes apart, then eight, then six and a half. She watched a crack near the crown widen by the length of her hand in twenty minutes. And she built the forecast the way she built every forecast, cold and specific, because fear was useless but arithmetic was armor.

"Tomás," she called. "How far to safe ground? Really safe?"

"East bench, past the granite spine. Forty minutes with the herd, if the trail holds."

"Then we leave in fifteen minutes," she said. "Not sooner — the low trail is still passable and we need every minute for the animals. Not later. At the top of the hour, whatever isn't loaded stays."

Aurelio stared at her. "You can't know that."

"No," she agreed. "Nobody can know anything. But I've guessed weather for a living for eleven years, and I'm guessing we have until roughly four o'clock, plus or minus half an hour, and I would bet my truck on it, and my truck is the only thing I've ever loved without complications."

That got a strangled laugh out of Bo, who arrived mud to the eyebrows with Tomás's mules, and even the ghost of a smile out of Aurelio, which was the point. Scared people freeze. Laughing people move.

* * *

Halloran found them at three-forty.

He came up the west trail with two of his crew, and even drenched he managed to look pressed, which Ines decided was probably a personality trait rather than an achievement. His eyes went past her to the loading, the mules, the children wrapped in oilcloth, and his jaw tightened with something that wasn't quite concern.

"Ines. Good, you're all together. Listen — the source site. If we pull core samples tonight, before—"

"Before the mountain kills you," Ines finished. "Yes. Great plan. Take it up with the mountain."

"This find is worth millions—"

"It's worth *nothing*," Marisol snapped, stepping between them with a child still on her hip. "There's no gold seam, Halloran. There's a drowned town and a slope full of its graveyard, and you're standing in the blast zone arguing about souvenirs."

For one second — Ines saw it, filed it away — Halloran's polish cracked and something calculating showed through, doing math about permits and liability and whether three witnesses on a mountainside mattered. Then he smiled his clean smile and said, "My crew is equipped. We'll take our chances at the site."

"Your crew will die at the site," Ines said quietly. "And I don't say that to frighten you. I say it because I've read that slope for six hours and I am very, very good at my job. Four o'clock, Halloran. Maybe four-thirty. Every minute you spend drilling is a minute your people spend downhill of eight thousand tons of moving earth." She let that sit. "You're the one who told me your permits were real. So prove you're legitimate: help us load these goats."

And here was the strange thing, the thing she'd think about later with something like respect: he did. Whatever else Halloran was, he was not stupid, and the mountain groaned again — five minutes apart now, and longer, deeper, a note that vibrated in the chest — and calculation finished its work. His crew took the lead ropes. For twenty minutes the surveyor and the storm chaser and the herder and the guide hauled a small civilization uphill through horizontal rain, and nobody mentioned maps or money or gold.

At 3:58, Ines looked at the crown of the slope and saw the crack had become a scarp, raw earth showing like a wound, and brown water was pulsing from the toe in time with some vast interior heartbeat.

"Go," she said. "Go *now*. Everybody east. Don't look back, don't stop for anything, and when you reach the granite spine, keep climbing."

* * *

They made the spine at 4:19.

Ines knew because she was watching her watch when the mountain finally spoke — not a groan anymore but a roar, a sound like a freight train the size of God coming down a valley, and she turned despite herself, because eleven years of habit demanded she witness it, and saw the whole face of the slope let go at once.

It did not flow. It *flew* — a black-brown avalanche of hillside, trees, and fifty years of held-back water, slamming into the valley floor and erasing the river bend, the sweet grass, the corral, everything below the old flood line in under a minute. The wave of displaced water came after it, gray and enormous, chasing the debris down the canyon, and the lost current — the famous, fabled, gold-bearing lost current — ran red-brown and boiling, choked with silt, burying its secret deeper than any map could follow.

Beside her, Marisol was crying without noise, and Tomás put his hand on her shoulder and said something in Quechua, low, like a prayer or an apology. Aurelio counted his grandchildren twice, then a third time, and each time the number came out right, and each time he laughed like a man being born.

Bo leaned against Ines, dripping, shaking slightly. "Your forecast," he said. "Off by nineteen minutes."

"Within my margin," she said, and discovered she was shaking too, and laughing, and crying, all three at once, which she supposed was what it felt like when everything you'd ever learned finally meant something.

Then she looked down the east trail and stopped laughing.

Because coming up it, slow and deliberate, mud-spattered and unmistakable, was the Delgados' truck — and riding in the bed, white-faced and silent, were the two brothers themselves, who had been somewhere below the valley floor when the mountain let go, and had driven out of the debris fan alive by a margin Ines did not want to calculate.

Rafa Delgado climbed down, looked at the ruined valley, looked at Ines, and said, in a voice with all the cheerfulness gone out of it:

"We need to talk about your debt."
