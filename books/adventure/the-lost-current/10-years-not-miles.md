## 10. Years, Not Miles

The mountain did not roar the way Ines had always imagined mountains would when they finally gave up. It groaned — a long, wet, structural complaint, like a house settling underwater — and then the slope above Agua Perdida simply let go.

She was still running when it happened, one arm around a herder's boy named Nico, Marisol ahead of her with the grandmother's hand in hers, Tomás bringing up the rear with the family's two goats on leads and an expression of profound personal offense at being made to hurry. The ground shuddered under their boots. Behind them, the hillside that had held the drowned town for fifty years slid down into the valley in a slow brown avalanche, taking the old dam's bones, the seeping adit, the whole secret architecture of the lost current, and folding it into itself like a letter no one would ever read again.

The river came out of it red.

Not with blood — with silt, decades of it, the ground-up remains of Agua Perdida's streets and doorframes and church bell, all of it moving downstream in a single thick pulse. Ines stopped at the tree line, set Nico down, and watched the water run the color of old brick until the sun went off it.

"Is it gone?" Nico asked. He was seven and had already survived more than most people managed in a lifetime, so he asked it the way other children asked about dinner.

"The gold?" Ines said. "Yeah, kid. It's gone."

"Good," said Nico, which surprised her until she thought about it, and then it didn't surprise her at all.

* * *

They came down out of the mountains over three days, because the monsoon had eaten the passes and there was no longer any reason to hurry. That was the strange part, Ines kept thinking. For two months every hour had been a currency, spent and budgeted and stolen from sleep, and now time was just weather again — something you moved through at the speed your body preferred.

Halloran they found at the trailhead, sitting in the back of his own survey truck between two very polite officers of the environmental ministry, who had been waiting there since morning. His forged permits had surfaced the way forged things eventually do — not through any cleverness of Ines's or Bo's, but because a junior surveyor named Okafor had gotten tired of watching his boss lie to people's faces and had made four phone calls from a satellite phone in the rain.

"You could have taken the offer," Halloran said when Ines passed him. He didn't sound bitter. He sounded genuinely puzzled, a man holding a map of a country that turned out not to exist. "Everyone walks away rich."

"Everyone doesn't," Ines said. "That was always the problem with your math."

He considered that. Then he nodded, almost respectfully, and let them put him in the truck. Later she heard he'd cooperated fully, given up names, permits, the whole chain of paper, as if some part of him had been waiting years for permission to stop.

* * *

The Delgados were harder to account for, because the Delgados had never existed anywhere but in paperwork and charm. The rope bridge went into the gorge with half their hired muscle's equipment, and what remained of their claim died with it — you cannot foreclose on a river, and you certainly cannot foreclose on a river that has buried its own source under forty feet of new earth. Ines sent them a letter, certified, enclosing photographs of the failed slope and a statement she'd signed before a notary in Puerto Cielo: *the collateral is gone; the debt stands; I will pay it in full.*

Rafael Delgado called her personally, which she took as a compliment.

"You're going to pay us," he said. "In full. With what?"

"With money," Ines said. "It turns out I have a skill set. Storm data sells. Insurance companies, agricultural co-ops, the ministry — everybody wants to know where the weather is going to be before it gets there. I've got eight years of readings nobody ever thought to monetize because I was too busy outrunning my own odometer."

A pause on the line. She could hear him recalculating, the cheerful machinery whirring.

"And if it isn't enough?"

"It'll be enough. It'll take me eighteen months, and you'll hate every month of it because interest is how you love people, but it'll be enough." She looked out the window of Marisol's hostel at the plaza, where Bo was arguing cheerfully with a man about a water pump. "One condition. You never come near this town again."

Another pause. Then Rafael laughed — a real laugh, rueful, almost human. "You know," he said, "Elias Crane owed us too. Did you know that? Small debt. He used to send us postcards promising payment. We framed the last one." A breath. "Eighteen months, Vasquez. Don't make us come find you."

"I'll be easy to find," Ines said, and hung up, and discovered her hands were steady.

* * *

The hostel had eleven rooms, a roof that thought about leaking whenever it rained, and a common room with chairs that had been repaired so many times they were mostly history rather than furniture. Ines stayed a season, then another season pretending it was still the first one.

She fixed the roof with Bo, who treated carpentry as a philosophical discipline ("a nail is just a bolt that gave up"). She learned the stove, which had moods. She learned which guests lied about having reservations and which ones lied about everything else, and how Marisol could tell the difference from across a room by the way a person set down their bag.

Marisol herself was quieter after the mountain, but it was a different quiet than before — not the silence of someone refusing an inheritance, but of someone who had finally opened it. She'd taken Elias's map, the original, the one drawn in a dying man's shaking hand, and had it framed. It hung behind the front desk, next to a photograph of Agua Perdida before the water: a street, a bell tower, a woman with a market basket caught mid-stride, forever walking toward something.

"That's my grandmother," Marisol said once, catching Ines studying it. "The one we carried out. She lived to ninety-one and never once went back to look at the valley. I used to think she was afraid of it." She straightened the frame a millimeter. "She wasn't afraid. She just didn't need to see it twice. Some things you carry better than you visit."

Ines thought about the burned map — hers, the copy, fed page by page into the hostel stove the night they came down from the mountain while Bo narrated solemnly like a funeral for a small paper nation. She'd expected to feel the loss. Instead she'd felt lighter, the way you do after setting down luggage you forgot you were carrying.

* * *

Winter came early to Puerto Cielo, the way it comes everywhere now, earlier than the old almanacs promised. The town put up its festival lights anyway. On the night they lit them, the whole plaza smelled of cinnamon and woodsmoke, and Nico — whose family had been resettled in the blue house above the bakery — taught Ines a card game that was either very simple or profoundly deep depending on who was winning.

"You're staying," Nico said. It wasn't a question. Children that age don't ask questions; they issue findings.

"For the winter," Ines said.

"You unpacked everything."

"I did."

"Nico told me," Marisol said, arriving with two cups of something hot and faintly alcoholic, "that you put your books on a shelf. Not in the box. On the shelf."

"They were getting bent."

"Mm." Marisol handed her a cup and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which in Puerto Cielo in December was less a gesture than a survival strategy. "Bo says the truck's due for its timing belt. Says you keep postponing it."

"I know what he says."

"Do you? Because he also says you've been staring at the eastern pass every morning like it owes you an apology, and I've been letting you have your privacy about it, but it's been six weeks, Ines, and the pass isn't going to apologize. It's a pass."

Ines looked into her cup. The truth was that every morning, coffee in hand, she stood at the east window and checked the sky — not for storms anymore, but the way you check on an old injury. Somewhere past that pass was the highway, and the highway was the life she'd had: seventy miles an hour, odometer for a heartbeat, a woman who could tell you exactly how far she'd driven in any given year and not one true thing about whether she'd been happy in it.

"I keep thinking I should go," she said. "Chase season starts in April. There's a whole plains out there."

"There is."

"And I'm good at it. I'm — I was the best there was at reading a sky."

"You are," Marisol agreed. "And the sky will still be there in April. And in May. And every year after. That's the thing about skies, Ines. They're not the ones that leave."

Down in the plaza, the lights came on all at once, the whole town gasping on cue the way towns do, and Nico shouted something triumphant about his cards, and Bo's laugh rolled up from somewhere near the pump he was definitely not fixing at this hour.

Ines sat with the cup warming her hands and did the arithmetic she'd avoided her whole adult life. Eighteen months to clear the debt. Two seasons, maybe three, of helping Marisol get the hostel right. A shelf with her books on it. A timing belt she would actually replace. A card game with a seven-year-old who considered her, apparently, a permanent feature of the landscape.

Years, she thought. Not miles. It was such a small unit of measurement, and it terrified her more than any supercell ever had, and she found — testing it the way she'd test a shifting wind — that she wanted it anyway.

"All right," she said to Marisol, to the window, to the pass and whatever waited beyond it. "April. But I'm coming back before the snows."

Marisol smiled into her cup. "I know," she said. "Your books are on the shelf."

Below them the lights glowed, and the first flakes of the year came down soft over the plaza, unhurried, falling at exactly the speed they chose — and Ines Vasquez, who had once measured her whole life in tenths of a mile, sat still and let the evening take as long as it wanted.
