## 1. Fuel and Rumors

The rain had stopped an hour before Orsen Blackwood dropped out of the cloud deck, but the strip at Two Rivers was still drowning in it—mud so deep and brown it looked like something the river had coughed up and then regretted. He brought the Cessna in low over the treeline, flaps down, watching the wind sock hang limp and sodden from its pole like a drowned man's arm. No crosswind. Small mercies.

The tires hit standing water at the hundred-meter mark and threw up twin rooster tails of brown spray that arced over the struts. The plane slewed left, caught, straightened. Orsen rode the rudder with the flat patience of a man who had done this four hundred times in weather worse than this, and taxied to the far end of the strip where the grass grew tall enough to hide the wheel ruts.

Two Rivers wasn't a town so much as an argument between a river and a road, with the road losing. A fuel cache under corrugated tin. A trading post whose porch sagged in the middle like a tired back. A dozen houses on stilts, their paint long since surrendered to the damp. And mud everywhere—mud on the boardwalks, mud in the boot prints, mud ground into the grain of every plank in town until the whole place seemed built out of it.

He cut the engine and sat for a moment, hands loose on the yoke, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the drip of water off the propeller. This was the part he liked—the silence after shutdown, when the plane stopped being a machine and became a small dry room floating in all that wet green country. For thirty seconds he could pretend he was nowhere in particular, which was the closest thing to home he'd had in eleven years.

Then he unlatched the door and the smell came in: river rot, woodsmoke, diesel, and underneath it all the green ferment of the interior in monsoon season. He climbed down into the mud and didn't bother looking for the boards.

"Blackwood." Old Teodor was already crossing the strip, moving with the careful rolling gait of a man who'd broken both hips in separate decades and trusted neither since. He ran the trading post and, unofficially, everything else within forty kilometers. "You're late."

"I'm early," Orsen said. "You expected me Thursday."

"Thursday I expected you Thursday." Teodor reached the plane and put a hand on the strut, not for support but the way other men touched horses. "You got the insulin?"

"Cold box is in the back. Six vials, packed in gel ice. Get it inside inside twenty minutes or you're selling expensive water."

Teodor grunted, which from him was gratitude. Orsen hauled the cold box out of the cargo bay and pressed it into the old man's arms, then went back for the rest: flour and rifle cartridges for the Kovalenko family upriver, a crate of schoolbooks someone in the capital had donated and someone else had marked up fifty percent, mail bundled in oilcloth, and a cardboard box of spare parts for the mission's generator that he carried free because Sister Agnieszka had once pulled a fishhook out of his palm without asking how he'd gotten it.

That was the arrangement. Medicine, mail, cartridges, gossip. He took payment in cash when there was any, in smoked fish and stories when there wasn't, and in the currency he valued most: news of where the mining company's men had been seen, and where they hadn't.

"You heard anything?" he asked, while they stacked crates under the trading post's lean-to.

"Heard plenty." Teodor spat off the porch edge, a long brown arc into the mud. "Company survey crew came through three weeks back. Not the usual kind. Usual kind measures dirt. These ones measured people."

Orsen kept his eyes on the crate he was lashing down. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning they had photographs. Asking after names. Who flies in here regular, who buys how much fuel, who's got family in the capital, who owes money to whom." Teodor shrugged elaborately, a gesture that cost him visibly. "I told them what I tell everyone. I'm an old man. I sell flour. I don't remember faces."

"But you remember theirs."

"Oh, sure." The old man's eyes were quick and dark under the brim of his hat. "There was one, big fellow, quiet, never took off his gloves. And a younger one did all the talking, smiley, called everyone *friend*. They paid cash for information and wrote down who took it." He paused. "Nobody took it. But everybody remembered who they asked about."

"Who'd they ask about?"

Teodor looked at him for a long moment, and Orsen felt the question land somewhere below his ribs, where dread kept its permanent residence.

"A bush pilot," Teodor said. "Flies alone. Lands at strips nobody else uses. Never stays two nights anywhere."

* * *

The rule was simple, and like most rules that keep a man alive, it had been learned expensively: never stay two nights anywhere. One night made you a guest. Two nights made you a fixture, and fixtures could be found by anyone willing to ask two people instead of one. In eleven years of flying the interior, Orsen had slept in trapping cabins, mission infirmaries, the back of his own plane with a tarp over the windows, and once, memorably, in a hay barn with a bull who had opinions about visitors. He had never once woken up in the same bed twice in a row, and he intended to die having kept that record.

It wasn't paranoia if they were actually following you. That distinction had taken him years to accept. For the first few he'd told himself he was being jumpy, that no one cared about a mediocre pilot with a forged second name and a past he kept locked in a box smaller than the one he carried his charts in. Then he'd seen a man at a fuel stop in Corben's Landing look at him a half-second too long, and a week later that same man had been waiting outside a hotel in the capital with a photograph, and Orsen had understood that the box he'd buried was not as deep as he'd believed.

So: one night. Fuel, food, gossip, gone. He knew every strip in the interior the way a priest knows psalms, and he knew which ones had sightlines, which ones had back doors, which ones had owners who asked questions and owners who were grateful not to have to.

At dusk he ate fried fish and rice at the Kovalenkos' kitchen table because refusing would have been rude and rudeness in the interior had a longer memory than kindness. Marta Kovalenko fed him until he had to physically slow his fork, and her husband Pavel talked about the price of marten pelts and the state of the river bar at Kilometer Forty, and their granddaughter—a solemn girl of maybe nine—stared at Orsen the whole meal with the unblinking frankness of children and dogs, the only creatures in the world who hadn't learned to lie about noticing things.

"You sleep here?" Pavel asked, clearing plates.

"One night. Flying out at first light."

"Where?"

"North." It wasn't a lie exactly. North covered a lot of country.

Pavel nodded and didn't push, because that was the etiquette of the interior: you didn't ask a man his business, you asked him if he'd eaten, and if he said yes, you let him be. Orsen washed the dishes without being asked, which bought him more goodwill than money ever had, and spread his sleeping bag on the porch because sleeping indoors made him feel cornered, and listened to the rain start again on the tin roof, and lay awake doing arithmetic.

Three weeks ago, company men asking after a pilot who never stayed two nights. Which meant either the description was general enough to be useless—and it wasn't—or someone had given them a pattern, and patterns meant records, and records meant somebody upstream had started pulling threads. He turned the thought over the way he'd turn a stone looking for scorpions, found nothing actionable, and finally slept in the shallow, light-breathing way of a man who keeps one ear open even in dreams.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Orsen was at the plane before the mist had lifted off the river, preflighting by touch and habit, draining water from the fuel sumps, checking the tie-downs he hadn't used because he never trusted overnight weather enough to leave a plane untied even for twelve hours. The mist lay on the strip like gauze. Somewhere across the river a bird was making a sound like a rusty hinge complaining.

He was wiping the dipstick when the truck came.

He heard it before he saw it—the particular knock of a bad universal joint carrying through wet air—and by the time it rolled out of the mist onto the strip he had catalogued it without deciding to: heavy four-by-four, company plates, two men inside, clean enough to be new, wrong enough to be here. His hands kept working. His heart rate did not.

The passenger door opened and a man stepped out into the mud with the careful delicacy of someone who considered mud beneath him. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a canvas jacket that had never been near actual canvas work, and he smiled the way salesmen smile—warmly, immediately, and at full wattage, before any information had been exchanged.

"Pilot Blackwood?" The voice was pleasant, modulated, the voice of a man who'd been to seminars about his voice.

"Depends who's asking."

"Halvard Renner, Northern Consolidated Minerals." A card appeared between two fingers as if by sleight of hand. Orsen didn't take it. Renner set it on the strut anyway, weighting it against the wind with one fingertip. "I've heard good things about you. Reliable. Careful. Willing to go places other pilots won't."

"Other pilots have sense."

Renner laughed, delighted, as though Orsen had said something charming rather than true. Behind him, the driver stayed in the truck, engine idling, and Orsen noted that the driver's eyes never left him—not the plane, him—and that the driver wore gloves despite the mild morning.

"Here's my situation." Renner strolled along the fuselage, trailing a finger along the fabric like a man appraising furniture. "We've got a survey party needs positioning, far interior, east fork of the Sable. Strip's marginal, weather's marginal, and our contract outfit won't take it. Word is you will."

"Word's wrong today. I've got med runs booked through the month."

"I can pay your month in a single charter." Renner named a figure. It was, genuinely, too much money—three times the going rate, maybe four, the kind of number designed to short-circuit a man's judgment and make his mouth say yes before his brain finished the sentence. "Cash. Half now."

Everything in Orsen went quiet, the way the forest goes quiet when something predatory moves through it. Too much money was never payment; it was a purchase. You didn't pay a man four times market rate to fly a routine positioning run. You paid it because the run wasn't routine, or because you needed leverage, or because you wanted his name on a manifest, his plane on a flight plan, his habits entered into a ledger where men with gloves could find them.

"East fork of the Sable," Orsen said mildly. "Nothing out there but a dead camp and mosquitoes."

Renner's smile held perfectly steady, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts under ice. "Survey work takes us to some lonely places."

"Mm." Orsen closed the cowling latch and latched it, deliberately, giving his hands something honest to do. "Appreciate the offer. Can't take it."

"Half now, half on delivery. And a bonus if you're discreet about the flight plan."

"There it is," Orsen said.

"Sorry?"

"The bonus for discretion. That's the part that tells me what this actually is." He turned and looked at Renner directly for the first time, and let the man see his face fully, which was itself a calculated risk and a deliberate message: *you are looking at a man who has nothing to hide, and also you are not looking at anything at all.* Eleven years of practice lived behind those eyes. "I don't file false plans. I don't take ghost charters. And I don't do business with men who pay four times rate and then want it kept quiet. Whatever you're surveying on the Sable, you can hire someone who doesn't ask questions."

"And if I find him?"

"Then he'll deserve whatever happens to him."

For a moment the pleasant mask thinned, and Orsen glimpsed what was under it—not anger exactly, but recalculation, the rapid silent arithmetic of a man deciding whether this pilot was an obstacle or an asset, and what tools applied to each. Then the smile reassembled itself, seamless as ever.

"Well." Renner retrieved his card from the strut and tucked it away. "If you change your mind. Any Northern Consolidated office can reach me." He paused at the truck door, one hand on the frame, and glanced back with what might have been genuine curiosity. "You know, you remind me of someone. Years ago. Different name, though."

"Common complaint," Orsen said.

The truck ground away through the mud, and Orsen stood in its wake breathing the wet air and feeling the familiar cold settle into its usual seat behind his sternum. *Different name, though.* Coincidence. The interior was full of coincidences. He'd built his whole life on the assumption that they were only coincidences, and the assumption had held for eleven years, mostly, and he told himself it would hold today.

He flew out at mid-morning, north as promised, climbing through the breaking mist with a load of flour and cartridges and a head full of arithmetic. Below him the rivers braided silver through the green, and the dead camp on the Sable lay somewhere under that canopy with whatever it held, and a dying prospector he hadn't yet met was breathing his way toward a bargain that would crack Orsen's life open like an egg.

But that was ahead of him. For now there was only the drone of the engine, the compass needle swinging to north-northwest, and the rule, worn smooth as a river stone from eleven years of handling:

Never stay two nights anywhere.

He was already planning tomorrow's landing.
