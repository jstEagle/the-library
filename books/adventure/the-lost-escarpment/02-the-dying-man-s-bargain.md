## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The rain had followed Orsen Blackwood north for three days, a gray curtain that never quite fell and never quite lifted, so that the whole world seemed to exist inside the drum of his own engine noise. He flew low because he always flew low—low enough to read the river like handwriting, to see where the current braided and where it strangled itself against gravel bars—and because up high, in the clean cold altitudes, a man became visible. Visible to radar, to weather, to anyone who might be looking. Orsen had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not being looked at, and the first rule of it was to stay under the ceiling of ordinary attention.

He was running medicine again: a crate of penicillin for the nursing station at Two Rivers, insulin packed in dry ice for a diabetic trapper whose name Orsen knew only as Old Semyon, and a small sealed box of morphine tablets that he carried without asking questions, because the questions in this country were always worse than the answers. The cargo was lashed behind the second seat of his de Havilland Beaver, an aircraft older than he was and more honest than most men he'd met. Her name was *Kestrel*—a coincidence he'd never examined too closely, painted on her tail by some previous owner whose reasons were their own.

The strip at Two Rivers was a mud scar between black spruce, and by the time he set down, the rain had thickened into something with intent behind it. He taxied to the bank of drums someone had chained to a stump, unloaded fast, and was back in the air inside nine minutes. Nine minutes. He counted them the way other men counted money.

It wasn't that he disliked people. It was that people, given time, began to ask things. Where are you from. What did you do before. Why does a man with your hands fly a bush plane instead of doing whatever those hands were trained for. Orsen's hands were careful hands, precise, with old scarring across the left knuckles that he explained, when forced to explain anything, as the result of a machinery accident. It was even true. It simply wasn't the whole truth, and the whole truth was a country he had emigrated from and could never return to.

So he kept moving. Never two nights in the same place—that was the rule, carved into him deeper than any commandment—and the rule had kept him alive through years that would have buried a more settled man.

Which is why, when he saw smoke rising from the trapping cabin on the shore of Marsh Lake, he almost didn't land.

* * *

Almost.

The cabin sat where the map on his knee chart said it should—a squarish dot labeled MARSH, E., which told him nothing except that some surveyor decades ago had bothered to record a name. Smoke curled from the stovepipe in a thin, wrong way, straight up and listless, as if no one inside had the strength to feed the fire properly. There was a canoe pulled up on the shingle, half-full of rainwater. There was a fish rack with nothing on it, in a season when it should have been heavy.

Orsen circled once. Then, against the grain of every instinct he owned, he set the Beaver down on the long lake and taxied to within wading distance of the beach.

He told himself it was the smoke. A dead man's fire left untended could burn a whole forest down, and forest fires were bad for everyone, including invisible men. He told himself he'd look in, douse the stove, leave a tin of matches on the table, and be gone before anyone could thank him. Thanks were dangerous. Thanks started conversations.

The door wasn't locked. In this country doors rarely were; locks only announced that you had something worth stealing.

Inside, the smell hit him first—woodsmoke and paraffin and beneath both something sweetish and rotten, the smell of blood that has been sitting in a body too long before coming up. The cabin was one room, dark, the single window fogged with condensation. A cot against the far wall. A table with a tin plate, a fork, a cup gone cold. And on the cot, propped half upright against a rolled blanket, an old man watching the door with eyes that were still sharp even though everything else about him had begun the process of leaving.

"You're the pilot," the man said. His voice was a ruin, wet gravel dragged over wet gravel. "Blackwood. I've heard your engine over the lake for two years. You never land."

"I'm landing now," Orsen said. "Your fire needs wood."

"My fire needs nothing." The man coughed then, a deep, catastrophic sound, and pressed a rag to his mouth, and when he took it away there was fresh red on it, bright as paint. He folded the rag over with the practiced indifference of a man who had been doing this for weeks. "Lungs," he said. "Doctor in Fort Nelson told me last spring I had maybe a year. Doctors are optimists."

Orsen stood just inside the doorway, rain dripping off his jacket onto the floorboards. Every part of him was already leaving—the hands itching for the yoke, the mind calculating fuel and light and the weather window closing over the pass. But his feet stayed planted, and he hated them a little for it.

"You're Marsh," he said. "Elias Marsh."

"Was." The old man smiled, and the smile cost him something. "Sit down, pilot. You're dripping."

"I shouldn't stay."

"Nobody should stay anywhere." Marsh's eyes glittered. "That's what they say about you at Two Rivers. The pilot who won't sleep twice under the same roof. I've been waiting two years for you to break your rule, and now here you are, breaking it for a dying man. That tells me something about you, whether you know it or not."

"It tells you my engine needed a reason to stop."

Marsh laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on so long Orsen crossed the room despite himself and held the tin cup of water to the old man's lips. The water came back up pink. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the shake roof like static, like applause for something terrible.

When Marsh could speak again, he said, "How much do you know about the mining companies? The big ones. Corvus Consolidated, and the ones behind it."

"I know they pay cash for quiet."

"That's the least of what they pay for." Marsh reached under the cot, slowly, wincing, and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord. He laid it on the blanket between them like a man laying down a card he'd been holding for thirty years. "I need you to take something to Kestrel Landing. To my daughter."

"I don't carry passengers' errands."

"This isn't an errand." Marsh's hand rested on the bundle. His fingers were enormous, a workingman's fingers gone skeletal, knuckles like knots in driftwood. "This is a debt. Mine. Thirty years old, and I'm the last one alive who can pay it."

Orsen looked at the bundle and did not touch it. Touching it would mean something. Everything in this room meant something; the air was thick with meaning the way the window was thick with condensation, and he wanted none of it. He wanted to be back in the air, alone, nameless, carrying insulin and rumors and nothing that bled.

"I'll radio the nursing station," he said. "They can send someone—"

"The nursing station will call Fort Nelson. Fort Nelson will call the company, because half the men on that payroll owe Corvus their licenses or their silence. And Corvus—" Marsh stopped, breathed, mastered himself with visible effort. "Corvus has been looking for what's in this bundle for thirty years. If it goes through official hands, it burns. Do you understand me, pilot? It doesn't get filed. It gets burned, and everyone connected to it gets quietly ruined, and the names in it stay dead twice."

"What names?"

Marsh studied him for a long moment. The rain filled the silence. Somewhere under the cot, water dripped into a pan with a slow, patient rhythm, tick... tick... tick, like a clock counting down something.

"You're a careful man," Marsh said finally. "I can see it. Careful men make the best couriers and the worst heroes, and I'm not asking you to be a hero. I'm asking you to be a courier. One flight. Kestrel Landing is ninety miles southeast. You're going there anyway—I can tell by the way your cargo is stowed, you're running the circuit. Find my daughter. Wren Marsh. She works the fish dock, lives above the net shed. Give her this. Tell her—" and here the old man's voice cracked open like river ice going out, "—tell her it isn't gold I'm leaving her. Tell her it's the truth, and I'm sorry it weighs more than gold ever would."

"I don't want it."

"No," Marsh agreed. "You don't. Nobody sane would." He pushed himself higher against the blanket, and the effort left him gray-lipped and gasping, but his eyes never left Orsen's face. "But you landed. You broke your rule for a stranger's chimney smoke. So either you're not as closed as you pretend, or you're so tired of running that part of you wants a reason to stop. Either way, you're the man I've got. There's nobody else. Believe me, pilot, I have spent thirty years making sure there was nobody else, and now I am dying faster than I planned, and the only soul in five hundred miles I trust is a bush pilot who won't stay two nights anywhere."

"Why trust me?"

"Because you refused the charter." Marsh's smile was thin and knowing. "Word travels on the rivers faster than boats. A Corvus man offered you four hundred dollars at Two Rivers to fly a survey party into the upper gorge, and you turned it down without blinking. Four hundred dollars. Men have killed each other over less than that up here, and you looked at it like it was dog dirt. A man who turns down four hundred dollars blind is a man with a price that isn't money. Those are rare. Those are the only ones I can use."

Orsen said nothing. The rain hammered. The drip under the cot kept its slow time.

"I'm not asking you to promise me," Marsh said. "Promises to dying men are cheap; we collect them like stamps. I'm asking you to promise her. Look her in the eye and give her what's hers. That's all. Ninety miles. One night on the ground at worst, and then she's somebody else's problem and you're back to being nobody, which I understand is your life's ambition."

Every instinct Orsen owned was screaming the old litany: *don't touch it, don't carry it, don't know it.* Carrying things made you a target. Knowing things made you a witness. Witnesses had histories dug up around them like graves. He had built his entire existence on the principle that the safest place in the world was the middle of nowhere, occupied by no one.

And yet.

He thought of the fish rack with nothing on it. The canoe full of rainwater. The straight, listless smoke. A man alone in a cabin, folding bloody rags with practiced indifference, waiting two years for an engine sound to change its mind.

"How long?" Orsen asked, and immediately regretted the question, because it was the question of a man already involved.

"Days," Marsh said simply. "Maybe hours. The blood's in both lungs now. I can feel it rising like water in a bilge." He unwound the cord with trembling fingers and opened the oilcloth a corner, just enough for Orsen to see the edge of something flat and stiff and old—a map, hand-drawn, its margins dense with tiny writing. "Thirty years I've kept this. Buried it, moved it, slept on top of it. There are men who would burn this country down to its bedrock to keep what's on this map from seeing daylight. And there's a girl in Kestrel Landing who draws coastlines in her sleep because her father taught her the shapes of places he never said he'd been. She deserves to know why."

Orsen looked at the map. Just the edge of it. A surveyor's stamp, half-visible, faded brown: letters he couldn't read and wouldn't ask about. Asking was involvement. Involvement was death by inches.

He held out his hand anyway.

"Give me the bundle," he said. "I'll deliver it. That's all I'm promising—one delivery, face to face, and then I'm gone. I don't want the story. I don't want the history. I want my anonymity back by Thursday."

Marsh laughed until he coughed, and coughed until he wept, and Orsen couldn't tell which was which. The old man wrapped the oilcloth tight and pressed the bundle into Orsen's hands with both of his own, gripping hard, a drowning man transferring his weight to solid ground.

"Wren Marsh," he said. "Above the net shed, Kestrel Landing. Tell her the coordinates are hers. They always were." He sagged back, breath whistling. "And pilot—when she asks what kind of man her father was, don't lie to her. Whatever else you do in your careful life, don't lie to her."

"I don't know what kind of man you were."

"No," Marsh said, closing his eyes. "But you will. That's the trouble with the truth. It makes couriers into witnesses. I'd apologize, but I'm past the reach of apology." His eyes opened one last time, fierce and clear. "Go now, before the weather closes the pass. Go, and don't look back at this cabin, because if you look back you'll come back tomorrow with a shovel, and I don't want digging. I want delivering."

Orsen stood. The bundle weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed everything. He walked to the door, and his hand was on the latch, and he did not look back—he made himself not look back—but at the threshold he heard the old man speak once more, so quietly the rain nearly took it:

"Thank you, Mr. Blackwood. Whoever you really are."

The words followed him out into the rain, across the shingle, up into the cockpit, and stayed with him all the way down the lake, all the way up through the murk, all the way into the gathering dark of the pass.

* * *

He flew southeast with the map lashed behind the seat where the morphine had been, and the whole way he argued with himself.

It was a mistake. It was charity, and charity was just involvement wearing a kinder coat. By Thursday he'd be a name in someone's ledger. Corvus would hear that Blackwood flew to Kestrel Landing the day Elias Marsh died, and Corvus employed patient men, and patient men with ledgers were the one predator an invisible man could not outrun, because they didn't chase you—they waited for you to land.

He should turn around. He should put down on some anonymous gravel bar, bury the thing, fly away, let the river keep its secrets the way it kept its drowned.

He did none of these things. Instead he climbed through the murk into the brief cold clarity above the cloud deck, where the peaks stood up like islands, and for a few minutes the world was simple: engine, horizon, fuel, light. He checked his watch. Ninety miles. At this heading, with this wind, he'd set down on Kestrel Landing's dock water before full dark.

One night. Maybe two, if the weather turned, and the weather was turning—he could feel it in the barometer's slow fall, in the bruised color of the southern sky. Monsoon season was coming early this year; the old-timers had been saying so since June, and the old-timers were usually right about weather and usually wrong about everything else.

Two nights, then. The rule bent, not broken. He'd find the daughter, hand off the bundle, and be airborne before she finished reading whatever was written in those margins. He would not ask her questions. He would not learn the story. He would remain, in every way that mattered, a man who had merely carried a package.

Below him now the country changed—the spruce giving way to the great sedge flats, the river widening, the first lights of Kestrel Landing pricking through the dusk like sparks off a grinder. The fish dock showed as a pale finger into the dark water. Above it, one window glowed warm yellow in a building that had to be the net shed.

Orsen throttled back and began his descent, and found, to his considerable irritation, that his heart was beating faster than the descent alone could account for.

Somewhere below, a woman was about to learn her father was dead. And Orsen Blackwood—who had spent eleven years practicing the art of not being present for anything—was the one who had to tell her.

The *Kestrel* kissed the water at the mouth of the harbor just as the first true rain of the monsoon came slanting out of the south, fat drops ticking against the windshield like fingers asking to be let in.
