## 3. Two Nights Too Many

The smell woke him before the light did.

Orsen Blackwood had slept in a hundred bunkhouses across the interior, and his body had learned to sort their noises the way a doctor sorts heartbeats—the settling groan of green lumber, the tick of a stove cooling, the small industrious sounds of mice in the walls. This was none of those. This was a low, wet crackle underneath everything, like something enormous chewing slowly, and beneath it a hiss that rose and fell with the wind.

He was out of bed and at the window in three steps, boots unlaced, shirt half-buttoned. Below, past the lean-to where he'd stowed his gear, the fuel cache burned.

It wasn't a dramatic fire. That was what made it wrong. Nobody screamed, nobody ran with buckets. The company's drums—forty-four gallons apiece, aviation gas for the strip that fed half the upper river—stood in their gravel pit like a row of soldiers, and the two nearest were burning with a steady orange patience, flames licking down their sides and pooling on the ground in slow blue tongues. The rest stood untouched, heat-shimmered, waiting their turn. Someone had started it carefully. Someone wanted it to spread at its own speed, so there would be time to watch who came running.

Orsen stood very still in the dark window and let himself understand it.

They hadn't come for the fuel.

* * *

He dressed without the lamp, by feel and by the gray seep of dawn through the boards, and packed the way he'd packed a thousand times: everything that mattered into one duffel, everything that could burn left to burn. The map rode against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside his jacket, where Elias Marsh's trembling hands had pressed it two nights ago. Two nights. He'd broken his own rule for a dying man, stayed a second night at the Kettle River strip because Elias's breathing had gone shallow around midnight and Orsen couldn't make himself walk out into that cold and leave him alone with it.

Two nights too many. Now the whole town knew the stranger who'd visited the trapper's cabin had lingered.

He went out the back, into air that tasted of kerosene and river mud. The strip lay east, four hundred meters of graded muck between black spruce, and his Cub sat at the far end where he'd parked her nose into the wind, tied down against nothing in particular because this town had never given him reason to be careful. He crossed the open ground at an easy walk, deliberately, because a man who runs is a man who has something to run from, and the eyes he could feel on his back belonged to people who'd been paid to notice.

The fire drew a handful of figures anyway—a woman in a quilted coat, two deckhands off the barge, the storekeeper in his long johns—all standing at a respectful distance, watching the drums the way you'd watch a sleeping dog. Nobody moved to fight it. There was no water worth using and everyone understood, in the wordless way of the interior, that some fires are messages and you don't answer them.

"Whole cache'll go," the storekeeper said as Orsen passed. Not accusing. Just stating weather.

"Looks like it."

"Started from the middle drum. Funny thing." The storekeeper scratched under his cap. "Middle drum was near full. Rest were half-empty or less."

Orsen met his eyes. The man's face was open, tired, frightened in the ordinary way of a man whose livelihood is standing in a gravel pit catching fire—but underneath it, a question. *You know something about this, pilot?*

"Company man came through yesterday," Orsen said. "Offered me money for a charter north. I turned him down."

"I heard. Three hundred." The storekeeper whistled softly. "That's real money."

"It was a strange charter."

"Funny thing about that too." The storekeeper looked back at the fire. "Fella asked more questions than he answered. Asked who'd been up to Marsh's cabin. Asked when you come in, when you planned to go out. Wrote things in a little book." He paused. "I told him you only ever stay one night. Everybody knows that about you."

Something tightened behind Orsen's ribs. "And then I stayed two."

"And then you stayed two." The storekeeper didn't smile. "So when the fella comes back asking why, what do I tell him?"

Orsen weighed it. He could lie—he was good at lying, had built a life out of it, aliases like spare shirts folded in a drawer—but this man hauled his fuel and sold him coffee and had never once asked Orsen's business, which in the interior was the deepest form of respect. He owed him better than a lie.

"Tell him the truth," Orsen said. "I stayed because a sick man asked me to. Tell him whatever else you want. But if anyone asks—you never saw which way I went out."

The storekeeper nodded once, slow, and turned back to his burning livelihood without another word, and Orsen understood that he'd just been given something it would cost the man to give.

* * *

The Cub started on the fourth swing of the prop, coughed, caught. Orsen ran her up with the throttle cracked just enough, checking mags while his eyes worked the tree line. The fog was coming off the river in ropes, thickening as the cold water breathed against the warming morning—it would burn off by nine, but right now it was a gift, a gray wool blanket pulled over the valley, and he intended to be inside it before it lifted.

He taxied to the end of the strip with his head on a swivel. At the midpoint he passed the lean-to where the company man had waited yesterday, hat brim low, boots too clean for the country, and Orsen made himself look at the spot and name what he felt. Not fear exactly. Fear was a tool; he'd used it plenty, used it to stay alive through years he didn't talk about. What he felt now was the specific vertigo of a man whose camouflage has failed—who has spent a decade being no one, forgettable as weather, and has woken to find himself a name in someone's little book.

*I refused three hundred dollars,* he thought, swinging the Cub into the wind. *In this country, that's a confession.*

He opened the throttle and she rolled, tail lifting, wheels hammering the soft grade, and he pulled her off at half the strip's length because he wasn't going to use all of it, wasn't going to give whoever might be timing him a clean picture of his load. The fog took her at eighty feet. One breath the trees were below him, black spires in gray milk; the next there was nothing but his instruments and the memory of the river's bend, and the sound of his own engine filling the world.

He flew the way he'd learned to fly in years he kept locked: low, slow, by feel, following the ghost of the water. Fog flying kills pilots who trust their eyes, and it kills faster the ones who trust their instruments alone in country like this, where a canyon wall can live at the end of a heading. You flew it with your hands and your gut and thirty years of the river written into your spine, and you accepted that somewhere ahead was a rock you couldn't see, and you made your peace with that every single second.

Behind him, faint under the engine, he heard it: another engine.

Not close. Not yet. A heavier sound, a radial, deep-chested—the kind of motor that hangs on a Beaver or an Otter, something with range and muscle, something that doesn't belong to a man hauling fish or freight. It came and went in the fog layers, sometimes ahead-left, sometimes swallowed entirely, and Orsen told himself it could be anything. Morning traffic. A mine shuttle. The interior was full of radials.

He told himself that for eleven minutes, and then the other plane came through the fog layer above him, close enough that he saw its shape for two seconds—high wing, cargo doors, no markings he could read—and dove.

Not a dramatic dive. He eased the nose down, traded altitude for the river's skin, leveled off with his wheels kissing the mist off the current, and put a bend of the gorge between them. His mouth had gone dry. His hands were steady. Both facts registered with distant interest, the way they always did, the pilot and the observer in him riding side by side.

They weren't searching for the map. They were searching for *him*. For the last man known to have visited Elias Marsh, the dying prospector with his lungs full of blood and his head full of something the company wanted buried so badly it would burn a town's winter fuel to send a message. And the message wasn't really for the town. The message was: *we can reach you anywhere, we can take away what keeps you alive, and we will do it slowly enough that everyone watches and no one helps.*

Orsen had seen that arithmetic before. In another life, under another name, he'd been on the wrong side of it once, and he'd spent ten years making sure he'd never be on either side again. Invisible. Uninvolved. Never two nights anywhere, never a debt unpaid, never a promise big enough to hang a man.

And now here he was, skimming a fog-bound river with a dead man's map against his chest and a hunter's engine in his ears, because he'd made a promise to a man with maybe a week left in him, and the promise had a daughter at the end of it, in Kestrel Landing, who didn't even know her father was dying.

He should turn south. He knew that with total clarity. Put the Cub down at a strip forty miles off the route, hide her in the trees, wait out the hunt, deliver the map in a month by a roadless detour. Every instinct he owned, every rule that had kept him breathing since the life he'd buried, said the same thing: *disappear. Be weather again.*

His thumb found the oilcloth packet through his jacket, pressed it flat against his sternum.

Elias Marsh had gripped his wrist with both hands, those last hours, with a strength that surprised them both, and said: *They'll come for it. Not for gold—for the names. You understand? Not the gold. The names.* And Orsen, who had spent a decade ensuring that no one anywhere had any claim on him, had heard himself say, *I'll get it to her.*

Below, the river bent hard left around a bluff, and beyond the bluff the fog was thinning earlier than it should, sun bleeding through in yellow smears. Which meant the other pilot could see too. The radial sound swelled again, closer now, confident, herding—not chasing, herding, cutting angles, pushing him toward the open water downstream where a plane could be followed and a landing could be forced.

Paranoia, Orsen thought, was just pattern recognition with bad publicity. He'd earned every ounce of his.

He made the decision the way he made all his best ones: fast, ugly, and committed. He hauled the Cub up and left, into the thickest rope of fog still clinging to the gorge wall, and turned upstream instead of down—back toward the fire, back toward the hunters' expectations inverted, because no fleeing man flies toward the crime scene. He hugged the cliff so close he could see the lichen streaming past his struts, engine at climb power, airspeed bleeding, gambling the mountain wouldn't reach out and swat him for the insolence.

The radial roared somewhere behind and below, hunting empty river.

At the top of the gorge the fog opened onto blue sky and he was through, alone, shaking slightly now that it was safe to shake, the whole valley laid out below him green and indifferent and beautiful. Kestrel Landing lay six hours southeast, down rivers he knew and ridges he didn't, and somewhere behind him men with a little book and a deep-chested airplane were learning that their quarry had gone to ground.

Let them learn it, he thought. Let them chew on it.

He set a heading, checked his fuel twice, and did the thing he had not done in ten years: he began to plan for a second night in the same place. Because the rules had changed. Because a dying man had handed him a weight and called it a map, and the weight wasn't going to get lighter by being carried alone.

Kestrel Landing, he thought. A fish dock. A daughter named Wren who drew coordinates in her sleep.

He flew southeast into the climbing sun, low over the river, and for the first time in a decade, Orsen Blackwood was not hiding from where he was going.
