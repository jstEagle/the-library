## 4. Wren

The herring came in at dawn, the way they always did, silvering the water off Kestrel Landing until the whole bay looked like a coin flipped face-up. Wren Marsh stood hip-deep in the day's first catch, her rubber apron black with scales, her hands moving through the bins on their own authority while her mind went somewhere else entirely—somewhere it always went when the work turned mindless, which was to say somewhere north and high and green, a plateau she had never seen but could have drawn blindfolded.

"Marsh! You dreaming or working?"

"Dreaming pays better," she said, and old Tam Voss laughed his wet laugh and threw another net's worth of fish across the table at her, and that was that. That was the whole of conversation on the Kestrel Landing dock before seven in the morning. It was one of the things she loved about the place, though she'd have bitten her tongue bloody before admitting it. Nobody asked you questions here. Nobody wanted your history. The town was too small for secrets and too tired for curiosity, and so everyone kept their own counsel like a knife they didn't want anyone else to hold.

She'd been in Kestrel Landing four years now. Long enough that the dock boss stopped asking where she'd come from. Long enough that the woman who ran the boarding house had stopped asking why she never wrote letters, never received them either, never once used the mail. Four years of gutting fish and hauling crates and sleeping in a room above the fuel shed that smelled of diesel and creosote, and in all that time she had sent exactly nothing to Elias Marsh, and he had sent exactly nothing to her, and between those two silences lay twenty years of everything else.

*He chose the mountains,* she thought, wrenching the head off a herring with more force than the fish required. *He chose them over me, and then he chose them over my mother, and then my mother died, and he still chose them.*

The thing nobody understood about anger was how much of it was just grief wearing armor.

"Wren." Tam again, jerking his chin toward the strip at the edge of town, where the gravel runway ran down to meet the tide flats. "Plane coming in."

"I see it."

"You want to take the run out? Fuel drum needs ferrying."

"I'm gutting fish, Tam."

"And I'm asking if you want to gut them later instead."

She looked up. The plane was still a speck against the low cloud, a single-engine something dropping out of the murk the way planes did here—no tower, no radio chatter worth the name, just a pilot reading the wind off the water and committing. She almost said no. She said no most mornings. But something in the way the plane flew bothered her, some wrongness in its line, too low, too deliberate, hunting the strip rather than simply arriving at it—and besides, the morning was gray and cold and her hands were freezing anyway, and any excuse to move was an excuse she took.

"Fine," she said, and stripped the apron, and went.

* * *

The plane was a de Havilland Beaver, or had been once; now it was a patchwork of riveted aluminum and faded paint, the kind of aircraft that looked less built than survived. It came down the strip in a long flat slide and rolled to a stop near the fuel cache with its engine ticking, and the man who climbed out of it moved like nobody Wren had ever seen move—which is to say, like someone trying very hard not to be seen by anyone at all.

He was tall, lean in the way of men who forgot meals, with dark hair going gray at the temples and eyes that did a strange thing: they swept the dock, the tree line, the windows of the cannery office, cataloguing everything, and then deliberately refused to look at her. He checked the strip twice before he even untied the cargo straps. When he finally spoke to Tam, his voice was quiet and roughened, like a file dragged over wood.

"Insulin for the clinic. Cold chain, so we should get it inside."

"You're the runner from upriver," Tam said. "Blackwood, isn't it?"

Something flickered across the man's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Orsen," he said. "Just Orsen works fine."

Wren stood back with her hands in her pockets and watched him work. There was a competence in him that she recognized and distrusted in equal measure, the same economy of motion her father had, the same way of touching machinery as if it might betray him. And there was something else, something under the competence, a coiled watchfulness that made her think of animals in traps—not the ones already caught, but the ones who knew the trap was there and were deciding whether the cheese was worth it.

Tam signed for the insulin. The pilot helped carry the cooler to the clinic himself, refusing help, declining coffee, turning down every small hospitality the town offered with a politeness so complete it was practically hostile. Wren had grown up around bush pilots. They were loud men, hungry men, men who told stories about weather and women and engine failures with equal relish. This one wanted nothing except to be gone, and that, more than anything, made her look at him twice.

He was walking back to his plane when she spoke.

"You came up from the south?"

He stopped. Didn't turn all the way. "I came from wherever the freight needed carrying."

"The south," she said. "Past the divide. Past the Burnt Country."

Now he turned, and up close his eyes were a pale gray-green, the color of shallow water over slate, and they held hers with an effort that seemed to cost him something. "Why do you ask?"

"Because you've been flying since dawn and you haven't once looked relaxed, and because you keep checking the tree line like it owes you money, and because—" she jerked her chin at the plane "—you've got mud on your struts from strips that don't exist on any map I've ever bought."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, quietly: "Who are you?"

"Nobody," she said. "Dock worker. Ask Tam."

"That's not what I meant."

"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose it was."

He looked at her a moment longer, and whatever he saw in her face made him glance away first, which she noted with a small, cold satisfaction. Then he climbed into his plane, and the engine caught, and she stepped back off the strip as the propeller wash flattened the puddles, and she watched the Beaver lift off into the murk and vanish south, and she stood there in the drizzle feeling like she'd just missed a step in the dark.

She went back to the fish.

* * *

He came back three days later.

She knew it before she saw it—the sound of the engine arriving out of the southwest, ragged, running rough, a sound like a man coughing blood. She was on the dock at first light, and she heard it, and something in her chest went tight and cold, because she had grown up with that sound, had fallen asleep to it as a child in camps where the plane was the only thread tying them to the world, and she knew what a sick engine sounded like the way sailors know what a sick sky looks like.

The Beaver came over the tree line trailing a thin gray ribbon of smoke, wobbled once, corrected, and put itself down on the strip with a landing that was less flying than falling with style. It rolled nearly to the tide flats before it stopped. The engine died. For a long moment nothing moved.

Then the pilot got out, and Wren saw immediately that something was wrong with him beyond the machine. He moved stiffly, favoring his left side, and his face had the gray, sweat-sheened look of a man in pain who has decided pain is not on today's schedule.

"Engine's cooked," he said to Tam, without preamble. "Cylinder's scored. I need parts, and I need a hangar, and I need both yesterday."

"We've got a shed," Tam said. "Parts are another matter. Mail plane comes Thursday."

"Thursday." The word came out flat. "That's four days."

"Three, counting today."

The pilot closed his eyes briefly, and Wren watched him do a calculation behind them, some arithmetic of risk and distance that she couldn't see the numbers of. When he opened them, they found hers across the dock, and this time he didn't look away.

"You," he said. "You asked me about the south."

"I ask everyone about the south."

"Do you." He came closer, and up close she could see the exhaustion sitting on him like a coat he couldn't take off. "Then maybe you can answer me something. Does the name Marsh mean anything in this town?"

The world went very quiet and very large.

There are moments that arrive pre-broken, Wren thought later, moments you recognize only afterward, when you understand that the person you were before them and the person you became after them are two different people separated by a single sentence. She stood on the dock with fish blood drying on her sleeves and heard her own name spoken by a stranger, attached to a stranger's question, and felt the ground tilt beneath the boards.

"Why?" she said. Her voice came out steady. She would remember being proud of that later, and ashamed of the pride.

"Because I carried a message here," he said. "From a man named Elias Marsh. And the message is you."

* * *

They talked in the shed, among the nets and the rusted drums, because she refused to go anywhere private with him and he seemed to understand that refusal without offense. He sat on an upturned crate with his injured side braced against the wall, and he took a package from inside his jacket—a oilcloth bundle, tied with cord, soft-edged from handling—and laid it on the crate between them without letting go of it.

"Elias Marsh," he said. "Trapping cabin, up past the divide. I found him ten days ago. Lungs full of blood. He was dying when I landed and dying when I left, and he knew it, and he wasn't afraid of it—he was afraid of something else. Afraid this wouldn't reach you."

Wren looked at the bundle and did not touch it.

"He's dead," she said. Not a question.

"Not when I left him. But he was coughing red, and he'd been coughing red a long time, and he'd stopped pretending otherwise. He asked me to bring you this. He wouldn't tell me what's in it. Just that it had to reach his daughter in Kestrel Landing, and that I shouldn't open it, and that I shouldn't let anyone else open it either." The pilot's jaw worked. "I've spent the last week being followed because of it. Company men burned a fuel cache looking for it. So whatever it is, it's worth something to somebody, and none of those somebodies are good."

"You should have thrown it in a river."

"I considered it." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile. "He made me promise."

"People make promises to strangers all the time. It doesn't obligate the stranger."

"It does," he said, "if the stranger says yes."

And there it was, Wren thought. The whole man, in one sentence. A man who had said yes, and hated himself for it, and done it anyway. She knew that architecture intimately. She had lived inside it for twenty years.

She reached out and took the bundle.

It was lighter than she expected. The oilcloth was cracked with age and rewrapped with newer cloth, layers upon layers, the way you wrap something you have opened and rewrapped many times. Her fingers knew the gesture before her mind caught up—she had seen her father wrap maps exactly this way, always against the damp, always twice, always with the knot facing up.

She untied it.

Inside was a map. Hand-drawn, on paper gone amber and soft as cloth, covered in her father's handwriting—that narrow, slanted, impatient script she hadn't seen in two decades and recognized in half a second, the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Rivers in blue ink gone brown. Contours in pencil, layered and reworked, thirty years of revisions pressed into the same sheet. And in the margins, dense as scripture, notes in every color: elevations, bearings, distances, corrections, arguments with himself, arguments with the map itself.

Her eyes went to the center of it, because her eyes had always gone to the center of it, and there it was. The plateau. The escarpment. Drawn in her father's hand with a surveyor's precision and labeled in letters pressed so hard they'd torn the paper:

THE LOST ESCARPMENT.

Below it, smaller, in different ink, added years apart like rings in a tree: coordinates. Bearings. A stamp from a survey camp she'd never heard of, dated the year she was born. And along the eastern rim, in red, in handwriting gone shaky and urgent:

*They were here. All of them. Count the graves.*

Wren sat down on a coil of rope, because standing had stopped being an option.

Because she knew these coordinates. Not the way you know a fact. The way you know your own hands. As a child, before the silence, before her mother's illness and the slow unraveling of everything, she had sat on her father's knee in a dozen drafty cabins while he drew this country over and over—always the same country, always the same plateau, always the same obsessive annotations—and she had drawn it too, on butcher paper, on birch bark, in the condensation of cabin windows. She had drawn it hundreds of times. She had drawn it after she stopped speaking to him. She had drawn it last winter, alone, in a boarding house room above a fuel shed, without knowing why, the way you scratch an itch you've forgotten the origin of.

"Miss?" The pilot's voice, careful. "Are you all right?"

"He's really drawing it still," she said. Her own voice sounded far away. "Thirty years. He's been drawing the same map for thirty years."

"There were others in the cabin. Dozens. Same map, drawn over and over, each one more detailed than the last. Like a man trying to get something exactly right before—" He stopped. "Before running out of time."

She turned the map over. On the back, in the shaky red hand, was a letter. Short. Addressed to her. Dated six weeks ago.

*Wren —*

*If you're reading this, then the map found you, which means I'm dead or close enough, and which means you finally have what I could never give you in person: the truth about the escarpment. Not gold. I know that's what the company thinks, and I let them think it, because gold they only guard. Truth they burn.*

*There was a camp on that plateau. Officially it never existed. Unofficially it held men who dug and dredged and died, and when the ore played out, the company erased every record and every witness it could reach. I was young. I carried freight in and out. I told myself I didn't know what I knew. Thirty years I've been proving to myself that I did.*

*The graves are real. The ledgers are real. Everything is in a vault on the east rim, under the collapsed barracks. Names. Orders. Photographs. Enough to name every man they buried without a marker.*

*I'm sorry I wasn't brave enough to come to you myself. I'm sorry I chose the mountain over you, every time, right up to the end. I told myself it was for the truth. It was also because I couldn't look at you and be the man I am.*

*Count the graves, Wren. Say the names.*

*— Your father*

The shed was silent except for rain starting on the tin roof, a sound like static, like distant applause, like nothing at all. Wren read the letter twice. Three times. Somewhere in the middle of the third reading she realized her cheeks were wet and had been for some time, and that the pilot was watching her with an expression she couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular discomfort of a man witnessing a grief he feels partially responsible for delivering.

"He named me," she said finally. "In the letter. He actually—" She stopped, started again. "Twenty years of silence and the first thing he does is apologize."

"How did you leave things? If you don't mind my asking."

"How did we leave things." She laughed, and it came out broken. "We left things the way glaciers leave things. Slowly, and then all at once. My mother got sick when I was sixteen. He was up here. He was always up here. She died waiting for him to come down, and he came down for the funeral and stayed three days, and I told him if he ever contacted me again I'd burn whatever he sent. So he stopped sending things. And I told myself I was glad." She folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle something that has already survived you. "Twenty years I told myself I was glad."

"And now?"

"Now I'm holding his map in a fish shed and I can hear the rain." She looked up at the pilot. "What's your name? Your real one."

A pause. Something moved behind his eyes, some internal negotiation conducted and concluded in the space of a breath. "Orsen," he said. "That's the one I use."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he said quietly. "It wasn't." And he didn't answer it, and she found, strangely, that she respected the silence more than she'd have respected any lie.

She looked down at the map again, at the red ink along the eastern rim. *Count the graves.* Her father's last instruction to her, delivered posthumously through a stranger with a broken airplane and a haunted face. Twenty years of silence, and his final words to her were a job.

Grief hit her then, properly, the way weather hits—no buildup, just a wall of it, and she sat on the rope coil in the fish shed and let it come, shoulders shaking, the map clutched against her chest with both arms like something that might be taken from her. She cried for her mother, who had waited. She cried for the girl she'd been, who had drawn the plateau in window fog. She cried for the old man in the trapping cabin, coughing blood into a tin cup, wrapping a map in oilcloth with hands that must have known they were wrapping his own epitaph. And underneath all of it, beneath the grief like bedrock beneath topsoil, something else was settling into place, something with weight and edges and a terrible clarity.

Because she had drawn those coordinates her whole life. Because the map was already in her hands, in her memory, in her blood. Because her father had spent thirty years becoming the map, and now the map had come home to the one person who could read it fluently.

The inheritance wasn't paper. It never had been.

When the storm of it passed, she wiped her face on her sleeve, and stood, and folded the map along its ancient creases with hands that had stopped shaking.

"He said company men burned a fuel cache looking for this," she said.

"They did. Looking for him, really. The map was the excuse."

"Then they'll come here."

"Eventually. Yes."

"Then we don't wait for eventually." She looked at the broken Beaver in the yard, at the gray sky pressing down on the bay, at the strip that was the town's one thin thread to the world. "How long for parts?"

"Thursday. Maybe."

"Can it be fixed faster?"

"With the right cylinder, in a day. Without it, it's scrap."

"Then here's what happens," Wren said, and heard her father's stubbornness come out of her mouth in her own voice, and did not flinch from it. "You fix your plane, or we find another way, and meanwhile I read this map the way I was raised to read it. Every margin note. Every correction. Thirty years of my father's obsession, and I'm going to learn it by heart before anyone takes it from us. Because they will try to take it. Men who burn fuel caches don't stop at fuel caches."

The pilot studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to settle something in him—or unsettle it; with him it was hard to tell the difference.

"You're his daughter," he said.

"I'm worse," Wren said. "I'm his daughter with nothing left to lose."

Outside, the rain thickened against the roof, and far to the south, past the divide, past the Burnt Country, a man was dying or dead in a cabin full of maps, and the company that had erased a camp was already counting strips and docks and faces, working its way north. Wren tucked the oilcloth bundle inside her jacket, against her heart, where it fit as if it had always been carried there.

"Come on," she said to the pilot. "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. First rule of my father's map: you never let it out of arm's reach, and you never sleep twice in the same place."

Something flickered across his face at that—surprise, and under the surprise, unmistakably, relief.

"Where'd you learn that rule?" he asked.

"Same place you learned yours," Wren said, and pushed open the shed door into the rain.
