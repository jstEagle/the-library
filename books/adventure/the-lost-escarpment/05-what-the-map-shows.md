## 5. What the Map Shows

Wren Marsh had learned to read her father's silences the way other children learned to read weather. A silence with his jaw set meant money trouble. A silence with his eyes on the middle distance meant he was somewhere else entirely—somewhere north and high, in a country of stone and ice that existed for him more vividly than the room he sat in. She had spent eleven years learning to stop caring where that place was.

She cared now.

The map lay open on the table between them, weighted at the corners with a tin cup, a spool of fishing line, and the flat iron she used to press her work shirts. The pilot—Blackwood, he'd said, offering nothing else, no first name, no history, just the name like a coin tossed on a counter—sat across from her with his hands wrapped around a mug of coffee he hadn't touched. He had the stillness of a man who had practiced being unnoticed until it became his native language. But he watched her read the map the way a man watches someone else open a letter addressed to him.

The paper was soft as cloth from thirty years of folding and unfolding. Elias Marsh had drawn it himself, or redrawn it—she could see the palimpsest beneath the ink, older lines ghosting up through newer ones, corrections layered over corrections, a coastline argued with across decades. The rivers were drawn with the confidence of a man who had paddled them. The mountains were drawn with the frustration of a man who had only ever seen them from below.

And there, in the upper left quadrant, past the last named river, past the last surveyed anything: a plateau rendered in fine cross-hatching, its rim broken by a single notch, and beside it, in her father's small, fierce hand:

*THE LOST ESCARPMENT.*

Below that, smaller, almost apologetic: *or what they want you to think is lost.*

"You knew," Blackwood said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I knew he was obsessed." Wren turned the map ninety degrees, then back. "I didn't know he'd finished it. I thought—" Her voice caught on something. She swallowed it down. "I thought it was the thing that took him away from us. I never thought it was something he could actually hold."

She traced the route with one finger. From Kestrel Landing upriver, through the gorge, past a bend marked *dead water—portage left*, into country she had drawn herself, over and over, in the margins of school notebooks, on the backs of fish receipts, in the fog on windows when she was small enough not to know she should have outgrown it. Coordinates. Her father used to recite them like prayers at night, thinking she was asleep: latitude, longitude, elevation, the bearing of the notch in the escarpment wall from the mouth of the gorge. She had never known what they pointed to. She had only known they mattered more to him than she did, and she had hated them for it, and she had memorized every one.

*Sixty-one forty-one north. One-three-seven fifty-two west. Rim elevation nine thousand feet. The notch bears two-sixty from the gorge mouth.*

She could say them now, in this room, to this stranger, and she did, quietly, watching his face change.

"That's the plateau," he said.

"That's the plateau." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Thirty years of listening through a cabin wall, and I could navigate there blind."

* * *

Orsen Blackwood had made a career out of knowing exactly how much to know. Too little and you died on a strip in fog you should have waited out. Too much and you ended up like Elias Marsh, coughing your lungs out in a trapping cabin while men with company paychecks combed the river towns looking for what you carried. The sweet spot was narrow and he had lived in it for eleven years, and now here he sat in a fish dock shack that smelled of diesel and brine, watching a woman discover that her whole childhood had been a map she didn't know she was reading.

He wanted to leave. That was the honest truth of it, the truth he kept below the waterline where even he didn't have to look at it directly. Every instinct he owned said: deliver the map, deliver the message, get back in the plane, go be nobody somewhere else. The promise had been to a dying man, and dying men's promises were heavy but finite—you carried them so far and then you set them down and walked away lighter. That was the arrangement. That was how it worked.

Except Elias Marsh hadn't just given him a map. He'd given him a story, and stories didn't set down easy.

"Look at the stamp," Wren said.

She turned the map toward him and put her finger on the lower right corner, and Orsen leaned in, and felt the floor of the morning shift under him.

It was a surveyor's stamp—a rubber-stamp impression, ink long faded to the brown of old blood, pressed into the corner the way such stamps were pressed into every official document in the territory. Most of them he could have ignored. This one he couldn't, because he knew the format, and the format was wrong. The territorial survey office stamped with a double ring and a crown. This was a single ring, and inside it, letters he had to tilt the map toward the window light to read:

*NORTHSTAR SURVEY DIVISION — CAMP K-7 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL*

"You know what that is?" Wren asked.

"No." And then, because lying to her suddenly seemed both pointless and dangerous: "I know what it isn't. There's no Camp K-7. Not in the registry, not on any chart I've ever flown with. Northstar Survey Division doesn't exist either. The mining consortium runs its own surveys, but they don't stamp things—they don't *want* things stamped. Stamps are records. Records are evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

Orsen looked at the map again. At the plateau. At the notch. At the small annotation near the gorge that he had taken, on the flight in, for a note about current speed: *do not land, do not linger, they watch the water.* He had thought *they* meant the river guides. Sitting here, with the impossible stamp under the window light, *they* had begun to mean something else.

"I fly medicine into places the government forgot and companies pretend aren't there," he said carefully. "You hear things. Fragments. Ten years ago there was a rumor about a survey camp up past the headwaters that wasn't on any manifest. Men who went in under contract and didn't come out on the payroll. A camp that got struck from the books so clean that even the people who remembered working there started to doubt themselves." He paused. "I always figured it was the kind of story bush pilots tell each other to make the nights shorter."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at its stamp on a map drawn by a man who spent three decades getting close enough to steal an impression of it."

Wren sat back. Outside, the dock creaked under the morning traffic—crates of ice, the slap of herring, someone swearing cheerfully at an outboard motor. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where maps were maps and stamps meant paperwork and dead men stayed dead without requiring witnesses.

"He talked about it," she said. "Near the end. Not at first—at first he just held my hand and told me he was sorry, which I thought was about leaving, which it was, but it was also about everything else." Her hands flattened on the table, steadying herself against it. "The last day, when the blood was really bad, he got clear-eyed. Scary clear. He said, *Wrennie, I need you to know it wasn't gold.* I said, I know, Papa, the escarpment, the map. He said no. He said everyone always thinks it's gold, that's why nobody's ever believed him, because a man who chases a lost plateau must be chasing gold, and gold makes a man ridiculous, and ridiculous men don't get believed."

Her voice had gone very level, the way voices go when the alternative is breaking.

"He said there was a camp up there. Nineteen sixty-one, maybe sixty-two—he was never sure of the year because the company burned the records and the men who survived were paid to forget. Laborers. Local men, mostly, brought in under short contracts to cut a supply trail and run a winter survey. And when the survey found what the company didn't want found—or when the money ran out, he was never sure which came first—the camp didn't close. It was *erased*. The men were going to be witnesses. So the company made sure there weren't any witnesses."

"How many?" Orsen asked. His coffee had gone cold in his hands. He drank it anyway.

"Twenty-two." She said it without hesitation, and he understood that she had heard the number many times, had probably been falling asleep to it since before she could count that high. "Twenty-two men went into Camp K-7 and four came out, and the four who came out signed papers and took money and disappeared, and one of the four was a boy. Fourteen years old. He'd gone in as his father's helper." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "My father spent thirty years trying to find that boy. Or find anyone. A ledger, a photograph, a grave, a name—anything that could turn twenty-two missing men from a rumor into a crime. Because that was the whole trick, he said. You can't prosecute a rumor. The company understood that better than God does. They didn't hide the bodies deep. They hid the *fact* of the bodies, and a fact that isn't written down anywhere might as well never have happened."

* * *

The tide turned mid-morning and the light through the salt-fogged window went from grey to a thin, washed silver, and neither of them moved to light the lamp. Wren spread the map fully open, all four quadrants, and the two of them bent over it together like surgeons over a patient, and what the map showed, shown whole, was not a treasure map at all. It was a prosecution.

Elias Marsh had annotated it the way a lawyer builds a case. Here, a river bar marked *interviewed J.T., 1974, saw trucks, would not repeat on tape.* There, a ridge line marked *aerial photo 1963 shows structures — photo withheld, see correspondence.* Along the gorge, in handwriting that deteriorated with age but never with conviction: distances, bearings, the depth of snowpack by month, the weeks when the glacier tongue was passable and the weeks when it ate people. Thirty years of a life folded into a single sheet of paper, and every mark on it pointed at the same coordinates, the same notch, the same plateau where the company had once kept a camp that officially never existed.

"He wasn't mapping a route," Wren said softly. "He was mapping testimony."

Orsen said nothing. He was doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The fuel cache burn at the strip town. The company man with too much money for a simple charter. The questions about Elias Marsh asked too casually by men whose boots were too clean for the mud they stood in. He had told himself those were loose threads. They weren't threads. They were a net, and the net had a shape, and the shape was this map.

"They'll come here," he said. "For this. Maybe not today. But the old man traded it to me instead of burning it, and burning it was the smart move, which means either he was too sick to think straight or he decided the truth was worth more than his daughter's safety." He met her eyes. "I'm sorry. That's a hard thing to say to you. But you need to hear it, because I promised him I'd bring you this map, and I'm starting to understand that the promise doesn't end when the map changes hands."

"My father," Wren said, "spent thirty years being careful and it bought him a lung full of blood in a freezing cabin, alone, trading his life's work to a stranger because he ran out of time to trust anybody." She began, methodically, to copy the map—not tracing it, that would take tools she didn't have, but transcribing the annotations onto the backs of fish receipts, her hand moving fast and sure. "So we're not going to be careful. We're going to be fast."

"*We*," Orsen repeated.

"You promised to carry it to me. Carrying done." She didn't look up from her writing. "What you do next is your own business, pilot. But you flew that thing through a burned fuel cache and company men with questions, and you're sitting in my shack drinking my bad coffee, and a man who wanted to walk away would already be walking." Now she looked up, and there was something in her face that he recognized from her father—the same stubborn gravity, the same refusal to look away from hard country. "Besides. You know what the stamp means. You knew before I showed you. Whatever you were running from when you landed here, it's smaller than what's running toward us."

Orsen looked at the map. At the cross-hatched plateau. At the stamp from a camp that had never existed, pressed into paper like a bruise pressed into skin. Eleven years of invisibility sat in his chest like ballast, and for the first time he could remember, the weight of it felt less like safety than like drowning.

"The monsoon fronts are stacking up over the coast," he said finally. "Nothing flies north for days. Maybe a week."

"So we go by water."

"The river above the gorge isn't water. It's a meat grinder with a current."

"Then we'll need someone who knows it." Wren folded her transcriptions into an oilskin pouch and tucked it inside her shirt, against her skin, the way her father had taught her to carry things that mattered. "There's a drifter who's been hanging around the dock for two days. Says he's run the upper river. Says he's been to the escarpment."

Orsen's instincts, eleven years well-honed, raised every hackle he owned. "Nobody's been to the escarpment."

"That's what I told him." She smiled, and it was her father's smile exactly—thin, sharp, and aimed at something far away. "He said, *that's what everybody says.*"

Outside, down the dock, a man was laughing at something the fish buyers had said, a warm, carrying laugh, the laugh of a man who wanted very much to be liked. Orsen heard it through the wall and did not like it at all.
