## 6. The Stranger at the Dock

Kestrel Landing smelled of fish scales and diesel, and Orsen had been in it for three days, which was two days too many by his own arithmetic.

He told himself the delay was weather. It was partly true—a low ceiling had parked itself over the coast like a lid on a pot, and the strip east of town was socked in from dawn until well past noon. But the truth he didn't say aloud, even to himself, was that Wren Marsh had a way of looking at him that made leaving feel like a theft. She'd spent two evenings at the dockside boarding house with her father's map spread across a table scarred by fifty years of fish knives, tracing lines with a fingertip worn blunt from rope work, and every time she looked up at him there was something in her face that made his rule—never stay, never be known, never owe anyone anything—feel less like wisdom and more like cowardice wearing a sensible coat.

The map had changed hands again. Elias's daughter kept it now, folded into an oilcloth pouch she wore slung across her body like a bandolier, and she slept with it under her pillow, and she talked about it the way other people talked about a dead parent—which, in a sense, she was.

"The surveyor's stamp," she said for the fourth time since breakfast, tapping the faded violet mark in the map's lower corner. "Carrow & Finch, Territorial Survey Division, Camp Sixteen. There is no Camp Sixteen, Orsen. I've been through every public record in the provincial archive twice. The Division's camp registry jumps from Fifteen to Seventeen like the number never existed."

"Companies burn numbers all the time," Orsen said. He was watching the harbor through the boarding house window, where the fishing fleet came and went and strangers came and went among them. "Doesn't mean there's gold up there. Doesn't mean there's anything but old bones and older lies."

"You read the margin notes." Her voice was quiet, but it had a keel to it, ballast laid down early. "You read what my father wrote about the graves."

He had. *Rows*, Elias Marsh had written in a hand gone shaky toward the end. *Not scattered like plague dead. Rows. Someone counted them. Someone kept a ledger.* And below that, in fresher ink: *The company didn't kill them all. The company named them afterward, and then unnamed them. That's the crime. That's the thing worth carrying.*

Orsen turned from the window. "We need a boat," he said, because it was easier than agreeing with her.

"We need a boat," she agreed, and something passed between them then—not trust, not yet, but the recognition of two people carrying the same weight up the same hill—and Orsen felt the familiar dread shift in his chest, rearranging itself into something that might, in poorer light, have been resolve.

* * *

They found their boat before they found their boatman.

It was a freighter canoe, thirty feet if it was an inch, lapstrake hull gone soft at the seams, sitting on the mud below the fish dock like a dog too old to follow the pack. The owner, a Kestrel Landing Finn named Aksel Rautio with forearms like dock pilings, wanted four hundred dollars for it and swore on his mother's grave it had run the upper river every season since before the war.

"It leaks," Orsen said.

"Everything leaks," Rautio said. "Question is whether you can bail faster than she drinks. This one, you can. Mostly."

Wren ran her hand along the gunwale the way she might have checked a horse's leg, and Orsen watched her do it and understood that between the two of them, the boat was already half hers. He haggled Rautio down to three hundred and twenty-five, shook on it, and was counting out bills from the inner pocket where he kept the money that mattered when a voice behind him said:

"I'll cover the difference."

Orsen turned slowly, the way he always turned when a stranger spoke to him—with his weight settled, his hands loose, his eyes doing the work his face refused to do.

The man on the dock was perhaps thirty-five, lean in the way of someone who'd been hungry more often than fed, with dark hair going gray at the temples long before its time and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his expression, as though it had been sent ahead as an ambassador. He wore a canvas jacket the color of wet river stone, patched at both elbows, and he stood with the particular ease of a man who had learned to make himself welcome anywhere because nowhere had ever truly taken him in.

"Callum Reeve," he said, extending a hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake designed to leave no impression. "I couldn't help overhearing. You're buying a boat."

"You've got good ears," Orsen said.

"And you're headed upriver." Callum's gaze moved past Orsen's shoulder to Wren, and lingered there with a warmth that seemed genuine and therefore was more suspicious than any coldness would have been. "And you're Wren Marsh. Your father was Elias Marsh. I'm sorry—I knew of him. Everyone who ever worked the interior knew of Elias Marsh."

Wren's chin came up a fraction. "You knew my father?"

"I met him once. Years ago." Something flickered behind the charm, some door closing quickly in a lit hallway. "He wouldn't remember me. I was nobody worth remembering."

"Then how do you know his name?"

"Because a man who spends thirty years asking questions about Camp Sixteen gets remembered by everyone, even the people he never met." Callum smiled again, and this time the smile didn't quite reach whatever lived behind his eyes. "Especially those people."

Orsen put himself half a step sideways, so that he stood between the stranger and Wren without making a production of it. It was an old habit, older than his name. "We're not hiring crew," he said.

"I'm not offering to be crew." Callum reached into his jacket and produced a roll of bills held with a rubber band, thick enough to make Rautio's eyebrows climb. "I'm offering to be a partner. Three hundred dollars now, same again when we make the escarpment. Plus gear—you'll want gear. Rope, resin, spare canvas, a proper first aid kit, tinned food enough that you're not eating your boots by day six. I know where to get all of it in this town, and I know which prices are lies."

"Why?" Orsen asked. One word, flat as the horizon.

"Because I've been where you're going." Callum said it simply, without theater, and that simplicity was the most theatrical thing about him. "Once. A long time ago. When I was young enough that the memory should have burned off by now, and hasn't."

"The escarpment?"

"Below it. The gorge. The rapids." He pointed upriver, toward the wall of cloud where the interior hid itself. "There's a stretch below the escarpment where the river drops ninety feet in two miles through a gorge no wider than a wagon road. Every chart marks it as unnavigable. Every chart is wrong—but only just wrong, and only if you know the line. There are three ledges you can run and one you can't, and the one you can't looks exactly like the third one you can, right up until the moment it kills you. I've run it. I can take you through it."

Orsen looked at Wren. Wren looked at the river. Neither of them said anything, and the silence stretched long enough that gulls settled back onto the pilings they'd startled from.

"You said you were young," Wren said finally. "How young?"

Callum's smile stayed fixed, but Orsen saw the effort it took, the way a man keeps a door shut by leaning on it. "Younger than I'd like to say in public," he said. "Ask me again somewhere private and I might answer differently."

* * *

They let him buy them dinner at the Anchor Line, the only place in Kestrel Landing that served food a person hadn't personally caught, and over plates of fried char and potatoes cooked in old grease, Callum Reeve performed the act of being an open book.

He was, he said, from downcoast originally—orphaned early, knocked around the interior camps as a boy doing whatever work boys could do in places where men outnumbered women ten to one and the law was a rumor with a badge. He'd run boats, flown as a mechanic's helper, trapped one bad winter above the Chute country, and generally survived by being useful to people who needed a useful stranger. He'd heard of the Lost Escarpment the way everyone in the interior had heard of it—as a story prospectors told each other over stoves, half boast and half warning—and he'd seen it once, from below, years back, on a supply run that had gone wrong in ways he declined to specify.

"And the money?" Orsen asked. "A drifter doesn't carry seven hundred dollars in a rubber band."

"A drifter who's spent eight months working the cannery at Port Anselm does." Callum shrugged. "I don't drink much. I don't gamble. I've been saving for something, I just hadn't figured out what. Then yesterday I hear a bush pilot and Elias Marsh's daughter are buying a leaky canoe to go looking for the place every sane man avoids, and I think: there it is. That's what the money's for."

"What did you think the money was for before?" Wren asked.

"For getting away from something," Callum said, and for one unguarded instant the charm dropped entirely and what was underneath was tired, so tired, a weariness that looked geological, like something that had accumulated in strata. Then the smile reassembled itself, plank by plank, and he was the charming drifter again. "But maybe it's for getting toward something instead. Maybe that's the better use."

Orsen walked back to the boarding house alone after midnight, taking the long way along the waterfront because the long way let him see who followed him, and nobody did, and somehow that bothered him more than being followed would have.

He stood at the rail of the empty fish dock and did the arithmetic he always did, the cold ledger of risk. A stranger with money and a ready-made history. A stranger who knew Elias Marsh's name before anyone introduced him. A stranger whose story had holes in it shaped exactly like the questions Wren hadn't thought to ask yet. In Orsen's experience—and his experience was long, and purchased at a price he didn't discuss—people who volunteered themselves this completely wanted something, and what they wanted was rarely printed on the label.

But.

But the gorge was real. He'd heard of it too, the Drop below the escarpment, the water that ate boats. Without Callum they'd be portaging around ninety feet of falling river with a loaded freighter canoe and monsoon coming, and portage routes through that country were the kind of knowledge that got men killed for lacking it. And the gear—the resin, the canvas, the food—was real, stacked in Rautio's shed as of that afternoon, paid for with real money that Callum hadn't blinked at spending.

Necessity, Orsen thought, is just distrust that's lost the argument.

He heard footsteps on the dock planks and didn't turn, because turning was information, and information was currency.

"She likes you," Callum said, arriving at the rail beside him. "The Marsh girl. She trusts you. That's rarer than you think, and worth more."

"Don't talk about her."

"No offense meant."

"None taken. Don't talk about her anyway."

Callum laughed softly, genuinely, and leaned on the rail beside him, and for a while they watched the black water slide beneath the pilings, carrying the reflected lights of Kestrel Landing out to sea like coins spilled from a pocket.

"I'm going to tell you something," Callum said at last, "and you can believe none of it if you want. But you should hear it." He was quiet a moment, gathering it, or arranging it—Orsen couldn't tell which, and that inability was itself alarming. "When I was nine years old, I lived at a camp in the interior that wasn't on any map. My mother worked the cook shack. There were maybe sixty of us. Men mostly, some families. And one autumn the company men came up from the coast, and by spring there weren't sixty of us anymore, and the ones who left didn't leave in any way I could explain to a magistrate, and the ones who stayed—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The ones who stayed are in rows on a plateau, and somebody counted them, and somebody wrote it down, and I have spent twenty-five years trying to decide whether knowing that makes me a witness or just the last piece of evidence nobody's collected."

The dock creaked. Far out, a foghorn spoke once into the dark and got no answer.

"If that's true," Orsen said carefully, "then why come to us? Why not go yourself? You clearly know the way."

"Because I've tried to go myself." Callum turned and looked at him, and in the low light from the shore lamps his eyes were very still. "Twice. Both times I got close and both times I turned back, and I'll tell you why, pilot, because you already know the answer better than I do. Some doors you can't open alone. You need someone standing next to you who wants the truth more than they're afraid of it. I've been afraid of it for twenty-five years. Your girl's father wasn't afraid of anything, and look what it bought him—a cabin full of his own blood, dying alone, waiting for a stranger to fly his ghost home." He pushed off from the rail. "Three hundred dollars says I'm useful. Same again says I'm honest. And if I'm neither—if I'm exactly what your instincts are telling you I am—then you'll find out upriver, where it won't matter, because either way you need me to run that gorge, and we all know it."

He walked away down the dock, unhurried, and Orsen watched him go and hated how well the man had read him.

* * *

In the morning the ceiling had lifted and the strip east of town was clear, and Orsen stood in the doorway of Wren's room at the boarding house while she packed the oilcloth pouch deep inside her duffel, and he gave her the only honesty he had.

"I don't trust him," he said.

"I know."

"He knows things he shouldn't know. His story has more versions than a hymnal. And he looked at your father's map today—he asked to see it, and you showed him, and I watched his hands while he looked. His hands were calm. Nobody's hands are that calm looking at a map that's gotten people killed."

Wren zipped the duffel. She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke, her voice had her father's stubbornness in it, the same note Orsen had heard in Elias Marsh's rasp in that trapping cabin, blood in his lungs and certainty in his voice.

"My father trusted no one for thirty years," she said, "and it got him a grave with no name on it and a daughter who wouldn't answer his letters. I'm not saying trust Callum. I'm saying we can't afford his price in suspicion, either. If he betrays us, we deal with it upriver. If we refuse him, we drown in the gorge, and the map dies with us, and sixty people stay unnamed forever." She shouldered the bag and looked at him, and her eyes were gray as the sea off the dock. "So we take him. And we watch him. And we never—*never*—tell him everything."

Orsen held her gaze for a long moment. Somewhere below, out on the water, an engine turned over, and gulls rose screaming off the pilings.

"That," he said finally, "is the first plan I've heard in this whole business that sounds like mine."

They went down together, out into the bright hard morning, and Callum was waiting at the canoe with the gear already loaded and coffee boiling on a spirit stove, cheerful as Sunday, and the river lay open to the north like a road with no bridges.

Orsen took the stern. Wren took the bow. Callum sat amidships, between them, exactly where a wise man would put a stranger he didn't trust.

They cast off on the morning tide, and the current took them, and it was only after the town had shrunk to a smudge behind them that Orsen noticed the small boat standing off the breakwater, engine idling, two men aboard who were not fishermen—who had not been fishing, because their nets were dry and coiled and their binoculars were trained, steadily, on the canoe.

He said nothing. Not yet. He only began, quietly, to count the miles.
