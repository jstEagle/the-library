## 7. Downriver

The storm front came in from the west like something with a grudge.

Orsen stood on the dock at Kestrel Landing and watched it build all morning—a bruise-colored wall stacked up over the coastal range, swallowing the peaks one by one, lightning flickering inside it like a bad filament. By noon the barometer had fallen so far the fuel drums were sweating. By one o'clock the strip radio was broadcasting nothing but weather advisories in a loop, a woman's recorded voice repeating *severe convective activity, all VFR traffic grounded* until Orsen wanted to put his fist through the speaker.

His Cub sat tied down at the end of the strip under a tarp he'd weighted with rocks, and every hour he walked out and checked the lines again, not because they needed checking but because standing next to the airplane was the only thing that made him feel like himself. The Cub was the last honest thing in his life. It went where he pointed it and asked for nothing but fuel and respect, and in eleven years of flying the interior it had never once lied to him.

People lied constantly. Maps lied. Weather lied. And now there was Wren Marsh, sitting on the dock with her father's map spread open under an oilcloth, tracing coordinates she'd apparently been drawing since she was a child, looking up at him with eyes the same gray-green as Elias Marsh's and saying things like *we can be there in nine days if we leave tomorrow.*

Nine days by river. Because the sky was closed.

"You keep looking at that plane like it's going to fix this," Wren said without looking up.

"I keep looking at that plane because it's the only way in that doesn't involve drowning."

"The river doesn't drown people who respect it."

"The river drowns everybody eventually," Orsen said. "It's just a question of scheduling."

She almost smiled. It was the first time he'd seen anything like humor cross her face since he'd landed with her father's map three days ago, and it changed her whole face—loosened something around her jaw that had been clenched since the moment she'd read the margin notes in Elias's cramped, failing hand. Grief had been living in her body like a tenant who paid no rent. For half a second, it moved out.

Then Callum came down the dock with the news.

He moved like a man who'd never once hurried in his life, which Orsen had learned to distrust on principle. Hurry was honest; it betrayed you. Calm was a costume. Callum wore his calm like a tailored coat, hands in pockets, that easy grin already loaded.

"Bad news and worse news," he said, dropping onto a bollard. "Bad news is the front's parked. Met man at the trading post says it's a monsoon pattern, first of the season, could sit on the range for two weeks. Worse news is I made some inquiries about our friend Harker."

Orsen felt it in his chest before he heard it—the cold drop, the old familiar dread settling back into its seat. "And?"

"And there's a floatplane charter out of Meridian that filed a flight plan yesterday morning. Three souls aboard, company registration. Filed for Kestrel Landing, diverted to the logging strip at Two Rivers when the weather closed." Callum shrugged, spreading his hands. "Could be anybody. Could be surveyors."

"Surveyors don't file false passenger manifests."

"Exactly my thought." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. That was the thing about Callum that kept Orsen off balance: the grin was real, and so was the fear underneath it, and he seemed genuinely unsure which one was driving. "So. Sky's closed, road's nonexistent, and the company knows roughly where we are. Which leaves—" he jerked his chin toward the river, wide and brown and fast below the dock, carrying whole trees along on its current like the water didn't even notice them, "—the wet route."

The Kestrel River ran north out of the interior lakes, through the gorge country, past the drowned valleys, and emptied into the estuary two hundred miles downstream. Going upstream wasn't possible—not against that current, not with that volume. But the river didn't go to the escarpment. The river went *around* it, and the map showed what Elias Marsh had shown only once, in pencil, in a margin note Wren had memorized before Orsen ever met her: a tributary, the Serrat, that branched off the main channel ninety miles up and died in the gorge below the escarpment itself. Ninety miles upriver through rapids that freight companies wouldn't run in high water.

Which this very much was.

"There's a man at the fish camp," Callum said, "who has a freighter canoe he'll part with. Thirty-six feet, cedar and canvas, outboard and a sweep oar. He wants four hundred for it."

"Because it leaks," Orsen said.

Callum's grin widened. "Everything leaks. Question is whether it leaks slower than you bail."

* * *

They bought the canoe at dusk, in rain that had started soft and would not stay soft.

The fish camp sat on pilings at the river's edge, a scatter of sheds and drying racks and nets hung like the ghosts of enormous birds. The owner was a broad, silent man named Teodor who looked at the money, then at Orsen, then at the sky, and said only: "High water. You want to take her up the Serrat in high water, you'll want to know she takes water over the bow in the standing waves. Bail or die. Simple arithmetic." He spat over the side. "Also the canvas has a soft spot midships, port side, about the size of a dinner plate. I put a patch on it in spring. Patch is tired."

"Anything else?" Wren asked.

"She floats," Teodor said, as if this were the entire sales pitch, and maybe it was.

They loaded her by lantern light while the rain thickened from a whisper to a statement. Orsen packed the way he packed an aircraft—weight forward, center low, everything waterproofed twice, the map and Elias's letters sealed in oilskin inside the case that rode between his feet where his body could protect it. Food for ten days compressed into sacks. A coil of rope. The outboard, a fifteen-horse two-stroke that Teodor swore ran and that Orsen intended to test within the first hundred yards, because men who swore things about engines were men who hadn't tried them lately.

Wren worked beside him without being told what to do, handing gear across the gunwale, stowing it, cinching it down. She'd grown up on water—he'd guessed it from her hands, the calluses in the wrong places for a town girl—and watching her load a boat was like watching someone speak their first language. She knew where things wanted to go.

"Your father ever run this river?" Orsen asked.

"Every year of my childhood." She tied off a sack with a hitch that took half a second. "He'd take me up as far as the falls, camping, prospecting. He called it 'going to see the neighbors.' I thought he meant actual people." Her voice stayed level. "It took me until I was nineteen to understand there were no neighbors. There was just whatever he was looking for up there, and why he needed to look alone."

"Do you know why now?"

She looked at him across the loaded canoe, rain running off her hood, and for a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I know he wrote *they have names* forty times in those margins. Whatever's on that plateau, he spent thirty years learning the names of dead people nobody would admit existed." She picked up the last sack. "That's not madness. That's a debt. He was paying it the only way left to him."

Orsen said nothing. He thought about debts. He had one of his own, older than hers, carried so long it had worn smooth, and he recognized the shape of hers immediately—the way it fit the hands, the way you stopped noticing the weight until you tried to set it down.

Callum appeared out of the dark with the last of the fuel, whistling something tuneless, and the moment broke.

* * *

They cast off at first light, and the river took them the way a strong man takes your arm—firmly, without malice, entirely without interest in your opinion.

The first day was the easy day, though Orsen wouldn't understand until later how easy it had been. They ran the lower channels with the outboard at half throttle, threading gravel bars and dodging logjams, the current doing most of the work while the rain settled in overhead like a tenant arranging furniture. The forest came down to the banks in walls of black spruce and alder, dripping, steaming, alive with the small sounds of things sheltering. Mist stood in the cutbanks. Somewhere inland thunder rolled with a patience that felt deliberate.

By the second morning they reached the confluence, and the character of the water changed.

The Serrat came in from the northwest through a gap in the hills, and even from a mile downstream Orsen could hear it—a low, continuous roar, like the river clearing its throat. Where the main channel had been brown and heavy, the Serrat was green-gray and fast, braided around islands, and the standing waves at its mouth stood up a foot higher than anywhere around them, folding and unfolding in the wind.

Teodor's arithmetic began here.

They camped that night at the mouth and scouted by daylight, which was the only sane way to do it. Orsen walked the bank with Wren while Callum climbed a bluff with binoculars and read the water, and when he came down he was quiet in a new way—not performing quiet, actually quiet.

"It's runnable," he said. "Right-hand channel, then hard left above the rock garden, then you're committed through the narrows. After that it eases for six miles before the next drop." He looked at Orsen. "You've run whitewater?"

"Freighter canoes, loaded, in spring breakup. Twice." Both times had nearly killed him, which he did not say. "I can read water. What I can't do is read *this* water. So you call the line and I drive."

Callum nodded slowly, and something passed across his face that Orsen couldn't name—something almost like relief, or gratitude, or the expression of a man setting down a weight he'd been pretending was weightless. "Okay," he said softly. "Okay. Then here's how we do it."

* * *

They ran the first rapids at noon on the third day, in the first real rain of the monsoon.

It did not begin gently. One minute the mist was drifting; the next the sky simply opened, a gray cataract falling out of a cloud deck so low it dragged in the treetops, and the river answered it instantly, rising as they watched, the standing waves growing teeth. Wren stood in the bow with a paddle, Callum amidships calling the line, and Orsen took the sweep oar at the stern with both hands wrapped around wood gone slick with rain, and for the next twenty minutes there was nothing in the world but water.

It was, he would later admit, the closest thing to joy he had felt in eleven years.

Not safety—there was no safety, the canoe wallowed and shipped water over the bow in the standing waves exactly as Teodor had promised, and the bilge rose faster than pride allowed, and twice the hull struck something beneath the surface that rang through the cedar like a bell. Not control either, because control on that water was a polite fiction, a negotiation conducted at speed with a counterparty that outweighed you by a factor of ten thousand. It was something purer than either. It was *clarity*. In the rapids there was no past, no buried name, no company men filing false manifests two ridges away. There was the line Callum shouted, the stroke Wren dug, the weight of the river pressing on the hull, and the next five seconds. Five seconds, forever, five seconds at a time.

"LEFT!" Callum roared. "HARD LEFT—ROCK GARDEN—NOW NOW NOW!"

Orsen threw his whole body against the sweep. The canoe slewed, wallowed, the bow swinging through the current seam, and the rock garden rose up on their right—a field of granite fangs with the water peeling off them in white ropes—and then they were through, into the narrows, committed, the banks closing to fifty feet and the river accelerating as if squeezed through a funnel. Spray hit them sideways. Wren was bailing and paddling both, working the bailer one-handed between strokes, and Orsen saw her glance back at him once, just once, and what was in her face wasn't fear.

It was the same thing that was in his.

For twenty minutes they were three people welded into a single instrument, and none of them was hiding from anything, and the rain hammered down like applause.

Then the narrows spat them out into a long green pool, the water slackening, the roar fading behind them to a mutter, and the world reassembled itself—mist, banks, the drumming rain, the ordinary weight of everything they were carrying besides gear.

Wren started laughing. It burst out of her sideways, breathless, half disbelief, and she sat down hard in the bilge water and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, and Callum grinned his tailor's grin and said something about how that was nothing, wait until the gorge, and Orsen found that his own hands were shaking on the sweep oar and that he too was smiling, helplessly, like a man caught at something.

"That," Wren said, "was—"

"Don't name it," Orsen said. "Rivers punish naming."

But the exhilaration lasted exactly as long as the pool did.

Because at the head of the pool, where the channel bent and the mist thinned for a moment, Orsen saw the smoke.

One thread of it, thin and blue-gray, rising from behind a point bar on the outside of the bend—too steady to be a deadfall fire, too contained to be a lightning strike. A campfire, banked and tended, in a stretch of river where nobody had any reason to be, ninety miles from the nearest road, on the first day of a monsoon.

He didn't say anything. He let the canoe drift, casual, and watched the smoke through the rain until the bend took it away.

But he counted, after that. Counted the hours to the next camp, and lay awake in the tent listening to the rain on the fly and the river talking to itself, and somewhere past midnight, upstream, faint under the water-noise, he heard it: the far-off stutter of an outboard engine, running at night, running against the current, coming on.

He lay in the dark and did the arithmetic he hated. A boat behind them. Smoke ahead of them. And somewhere south, grounded like his own plane, a floatplane full of company men waiting for weather, patient as stone, because Harker—the name Callum had given them, the quiet methodical man from the trading post gossip—Harker did not need good weather. Harker only needed everyone else to run out of options.

The rain fell. The river rose. And Orsen Blackwood lay awake between the sleeping forms of Wren and Callum, listening to an engine he couldn't see, and understood with perfect clarity that the rapids had been the easy part—that the river was not trying to kill them at all.

The river was just the road. Something else was following it.
