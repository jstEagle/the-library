## 8. Company Men

The trading post at Two Rivers announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and creosote and the sweet rot of a season's worth of salmon heads composting behind the drying racks. Wren smelled it from a quarter mile upriver and felt her shoulders drop a half inch, the way they always did near anything that could be called civilization. Then she remembered what civilization had done to her father, and the shoulders climbed right back.

The post sat on a gravel bar where the river split around a humped island of grey stone, a scatter of buildings that might have been dropped there by a careless hand: a long low store with a false front painted the color of old milk, a fuel dock sagging on pilings, a cluster of wall tents, and a church that had given up on being a church and was now a warehouse for cases of condensed milk and shotgun shells. A radio mast leaned against the store like a drunk against a lamppost. Somewhere a generator thuttered, and somewhere else a dog was making a long complaint of itself.

They came in with the current, the three of them in the freighter canoe that Orsen had haggled down to half its asking price and then spent an evening patching with resin and a prayer. Wren sat amidships with the map case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, and she had not once, in four days of river, taken it off to sleep. Callum rode the stern seat behind Orsen's paddle, and he rode it the way he did everything—loosely, gracefully, as though the river were a thing he'd personally invited along.

"Two Rivers," Callum said, shading his eyes. "Last dry socks before the gorge. We should eat something that didn't come out of a foil packet, and we should not enjoy it too loudly."

Orsen didn't answer. He'd been quiet all morning in a way that Wren was learning to read. Not his natural quiet—that was a settled thing, a lake at dawn—but the other kind, the quiet of a man listening for something under the water. He'd been doing it since the checkpoint at the mouth of the Blackwater, where a company skiff had sat anchored midstream with a tarp over its cargo and two men aboard who had watched them pass without waving. Nobody watched river traffic without waving. That was the whole grammar of the river: you waved, because you might need each other someday, because the water didn't care whose side you were on.

They hadn't waved.

Orsen swung the canoe toward the fuel dock, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done. Fuel, yes. Food, yes. But a trading post was also a place where a man could be seen, and being seen was the one thing they could not afford. Her father had spent thirty years being not-seen, and it had killed him anyway, alone in a trapping cabin with his lungs filling like a bilge. She kept her hand on the map case and thought: *not for nothing*.

The dock boards were soft with rot. Wren stepped up and felt the whole structure give a sigh, and a boy of maybe ten materialized from behind a stack of net floats and looked at the canoe with the frank hunger of a child counting outboard motors.

"You selling the canoe?" the boy asked.

"Not today."

"Tomorrow?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

He grinned and vanished the way children do, and Wren turned to find Orsen standing at the head of the dock, not moving toward the store, his head tipped slightly as if reading weather. She followed his eyes. Parked beside the store, half in the shade of the false front, was a grey aluminum skiff with a company registration number stenciled on the transom. It sat there the way a spider sits—patient, decorative, wrong.

"Orsen," she said quietly.

"I see it."

"Is that—"

"That's a company boat." He said it the way you'd say *that's a rattlesnake*. Not loud. Just final.

Callum came up the dock behind them, and Wren watched his face do something interesting: a flicker, there and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. She'd been watching Callum's face for four days now, collecting these flickers the way her father had collected rock samples, and she was beginning to understand that Callum's face was a map of its own, and that she was not yet fluent in it.

"Company boat," Callum said lightly. "Half the river runs on company fuel. Doesn't mean anything."

"It means somebody's paying for fuel at a post this far up," Orsen said. "That's not a patrol. That's a posting."

They stood there a moment, three people on a rotten dock with a river running under them, and Wren felt the old familiar feeling rise in her chest—the one from the fish dock in Kestrel Landing, the feeling of being a small person in a large machine's path. She had spent her whole life being small in that way, hauling nets for a company cannery at wages the company set, buying from the company store at prices the company set, and she had told herself it was different from what the mining company did to the interior because at least nobody got shot. She wasn't sure anymore that the difference was as wide as she'd needed it to be.

"We could push on," she said. "Run the gorge today. We've got light till eight."

"You don't run the gorge tired and hungry," Orsen said. "And you don't run it with a leaking hull. We eat. We take on fuel. We keep our heads down and we're gone inside an hour." He looked at each of them in turn, and Wren saw him doing it again—counting, weighing, the bush pilot's habit of calculating load and balance even when the load was people. "One hour. Nobody talks to anybody. Nobody looks at the skiff. We're a medicine run. That's all we've ever been."

* * *

The store was dim after the river's glare, lit by two windows and a hanging lantern, and it smelled of kerosene, burlap, dried fish, and the particular sweetness of a hundred years of spilled sugar. The man behind the counter was old and wide and had a face like a river rock, and he watched them come in without any expression at all, which told Wren everything: he'd already been talked to.

Orsen bought fuel, rice, tea, a coil of rope, and a tin of resin with the flat competence of a man who had done this in a hundred posts. Wren bought salt, matches, and, after a moment's argument with herself, a paper bag of lemon drops, because her father had always carried lemon drops on the trail and she had decided somewhere on the river that she would carry them now, and that this was not sentiment, it was logistics. Callum bought nothing. Callum talked.

He talked to the storekeeper about the fishing. He talked to a trapper mending a net on the porch about the price of marten. He talked to two old women shelling peas about a cousin of theirs he claimed—warmly, convincingly, impossibly—to have known in a town Wren was fairly sure he'd never seen. And all the while, some part of him, Wren was certain, was doing what she was doing: reading the room, counting the exits, finding the company men.

Because there were company men. Two of them, seated at a table by the window with coffee they weren't drinking, playing cards they weren't playing. They wore canvas and rubber boots like everyone else on the river, but they wore them new, and they sat facing the door, and when Orsen walked in, the taller of them looked up with the unhurried attention of a man checking a trapline.

Wren felt it go through her like cold water: *they're looking for us*. Not for a rumor. For us, specifically, by face.

She turned her back to them and studied a shelf of canned goods with the focus of a woman choosing between peas and beans, and she listened. The store was full of small sounds—the generator outside, the dog, the creak of the floor, the storekeeper's knife opening a bale of twine—and under all of it, the low murmur of the card table.

"—came through the Blackwater yesterday," the tall one was saying, to no one, in the tone of a man remarking on weather. "Canoe. Three aboard."

"River's full of canoes," the other said.

"This one had a woman aboard." A pause, the soft click of a card laid down. "And a pilot."

The word *pilot* landed in Wren's stomach like a stone. She kept her eyes on the peas. Beside her, Orsen had gone very still in the way of a man making himself a piece of furniture, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, why he had lived so long as a ghost. It wasn't just caution. It was a discipline, a whole architecture of invisibility, and she was watching him build it around them now in real time—his shoulders rounding, his voice when he asked the storekeeper for the rope pitched half a tone duller, his eyes never once flicking toward the window.

Callum, on the other hand, was doing the opposite. Callum was drifting toward the card table like a leaf toward a drain, and Wren's stomach dropped again, because she could see what he was going to do, and she could see that it was brilliant, and she could see that she hated it.

"Well now," Callum said, pulling out the fourth chair without being invited, "somebody's got the ace of hearts and it isn't either of you."

The company men looked at him. The tall one's face didn't change, but the other one's did—a twitch at the mouth, the involuntary response of a card player to a stranger who has just read his hand. Callum laughed, easy and warm, and asked to buy in, and said he'd been skunked at every post from the delta up and was owed a winning hand by the universe, and inside two minutes he had them playing five-card draw for matches and the room had reorganized itself around him, the way rooms did. Wren watched it happen with a feeling she couldn't name—admiration and dread braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which was which.

Because this was the thing about Callum: he was either the best man on the river or the worst, and every day the evidence for both piled up evenly, like snowfall.

Orsen finished his buying and touched Wren's elbow, the smallest pressure, and they moved toward the door together, casual as Sunday. The tall company man didn't look up from his cards. The other one looked up and looked at Orsen a half second too long, and Wren saw Orsen receive that look and file it somewhere behind his eyes, and she knew they had maybe minutes, not hours.

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter. The monsoon clouds had been building all day over the western ridges, a wall of bruised purple with a grey skirt of rain beneath it, and the air had the charged, held-breath quality of weather deciding. Orsen loaded the canoe with quick, economical movements, and Wren stowed the supplies, and Callum came out of the store whistling something cheerful, and for one whole minute Wren let herself believe the hour had worked.

Then she went back for the lemon drops.

She'd left them on the counter—deliberately, she'd tell herself later, though at the time it felt like carelessness—and she was already through the door when she stopped, because Callum had come back too, saying he'd forgotten his knife, and the store was empty now except for the storekeeper, who had stepped out to the porch to look at the weather, and Callum was standing at the counter, and he was not looking for a knife.

He was looking at the tall company man, who had come in through the back, silent as a cat on the boards, and who was holding out his hand.

Wren stood in the doorway with the grey light behind her and the dim store in front of her, and she watched Callum take a folded piece of paper from inside his jacket—watched him do it smoothly, without hurry, without even a glance toward the door—and she watched him set it, not into the man's hand, but onto the counter beside it, under the shadow of the lantern, in the manner of a man leaving payment for a debt.

The tall man took it. He didn't open it. He just looked at Callum for a long moment, and Callum said something—she couldn't hear the words, only the shape of them, easy, apologetic, a shrug made of sound—and the man nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods at a transaction concluded.

Then Callum picked up his knife from the counter—he'd laid it there himself, she realized, he'd laid it there five minutes ago in plain sight, a prop waiting for its cue—and he turned, and he saw her.

For one heartbeat his face did nothing at all, and that nothing told her more than any flicker ever had. Then it reassembled itself, warm and rueful, and he said, "You forgot your sweets," and he held the paper bag of lemon drops out to her like a gift, like an offering, like a man handing a woman a small yellow sun, and Wren took it because her hand was already reaching and there was nothing else in the world for it to do.

* * *

They were a mile upriver before she told Orsen, and she told him in a voice pitched under the sound of the water, her mouth close to his ear while he paddled and Callum sat in the bow watching the banks with binoculars he'd bought in Kestrel Landing with money he'd never explained.

"A note. He passed a note to the company man. Folded paper, hand to counter, under the lantern. I watched it."

Orsen's paddle didn't break rhythm. That was the frightening thing, how little it broke. "You're sure it was a note."

"I'm sure it was paper and I'm sure it was passed and I'm sure of the look on his face after." She had replayed it the whole mile, turning it over like a stone, looking for the innocent side. There wasn't one. There was only the fact that Callum had known the company men were in the store, had seated himself at their table, had bought an hour of their attention with cards and charm—and then had paid them something in private. "Orsen. He's been ahead of us the whole time. Every post, every checkpoint, every place the company's been waiting—Callum's known. He's been steering us. Or steering them."

The river ran fast here, braided and brown with the first flush of the rain far upstream, and the canoe hissed along in the current like something alive. Ahead, the gorge walls had begun to rise on either side, dark granite going black under the storm light, and the first drops came down fat and cold and sparse, spotting the water around them like the river weeping upward.

Orsen was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was flat and careful, a man walking on ice he already knew was thin.

"Options," he said. "One: we turn him off the boat at the next gravel bar and take our chances in the gorge without him. Two: we say nothing, watch him, and let him lead us somewhere we can see coming. Three—" he paused, and Wren heard the cost of it in the pause, "—we ask him."

"Nobody asks," Wren said. "You ask a man like that a question, he answers with a story, and his stories are all true and none of them are true, and meanwhile the real answer is in his jacket next to his knife."

Another silence. The rain was finding its rhythm now, that monsoon drumming, and the gorge had swallowed the sky down to a ribbon of grey. Wren pulled her hood up and kept her hand on the map case, and she thought about her father's margin notes, the tight patient handwriting, thirty years of a man watching a company that erased people, and she thought: *this is what it's like, being watched back. This is what he knew every day of his life.*

"Orsen." Callum's voice from the bow, cheerful, carrying easily over the water. "Rapids a mile on, maybe less. I'd say we camp above them, but I'd also say the company skiff left Two Rivers five minutes after we did, so maybe we don't camp anywhere."

Wren looked at Orsen. Orsen looked at her. And in that look she saw him arrive at the same arithmetic she had: that Callum had just told them the truth as a way of making the lie irrelevant, that a man with nothing to hide doesn't hand you your own danger as a gift.

Or did he. Or was the skiff a fiction, a pressure applied to keep them moving exactly where he wanted, fast, tired, into the gorge where a canoe full of evidence could have an accident that no one would ever investigate, because on this river the law was a rumor and the company wrote all the rumors?

The rain thickened. The walls closed. Ahead, the river's murmur deepened into something with teeth in it, and Wren tightened the strap of the map case across her chest and thought about the note under the lantern, the folded paper, the small weight of it, and how the heaviest things on any boat are the things you can't see.

"Run it or camp?" Orsen asked her, and she heard in his voice that he was asking her something larger than rapids.

Wren looked at the white water ahead, and at Callum's back in the bow, straight and easy and pointing the way, and she made the only choice there was to make.

"Run it," she said. "And Orsen—tonight, when he sleeps, we read his jacket."
